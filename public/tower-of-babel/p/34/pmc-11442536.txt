Phosphorus dynamics in volcanic soils of Weizhou Island, China: implications for environmental and agricultural applications
Authors: Ran Bi, Wei Fu, Xuanni Fu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe dynamics of phosphorus are intricately governed by geological and ecological processes. Examining phosphorus dynamics in volcanic islands can enhance our comprehension of its behavior within such unique geological systems. However, research on phosphorus dynamics in volcanic islands remains limited. We investigated the phosphorus content of volcaniclastic rocks and basalt soils from Weizhou Island, China, to understand the influencing factors on phosphorus dynamics. The results indicate that in the volcaniclastic profile, phosphorus concentrates at 20–40 cm (17 mg/kg), decreases at 40–60 cm (11.9 mg/kg), and increases at 80–200 cm up to 46.4 mg/kg proximate to the bedrock, for the basalt profile, phosphorus content increases from the surface (80.2 mg/kg) towards the bedrock (83.9 mg/kg). The differences in phosphorus distribution between volcaniclastic rocks and basalts reflect the influence of parent material, rock weathering degree, carbonate content, topographic elevation, sea level changes, and geological activities. A strong positive correlation (R = 0.96907) between total and available phosphorus has been observed, suggesting that total phosphorus content effectively predicts available phosphorus content. Volcaniclastic rocks in wharves and high-elevation areas show low total phosphorus, while forest land with dense vegetation and neutral to alkaline soil supports higher total phosphorus due to enhanced bioavailability for plant absorption and utilization. Overall, the basalt soil of the volcanic island Weizhou Island demonstrates superior long-term fertility compared to the volcaniclastic soil. Despite its low total phosphorus content, it mainly exists in a highly bioavailable form, facilitating plant absorption, which is crucial for enhancing agricultural yields and ecosystem restoration on volcanic islands.Keywords: Volcanic Island, Phosphorus, Volcaniclastic rock, Basalt, Spatial differentiation

Introduction

Volcanic activities lead to the formation of volcanic islands, and their distinctive geological background and natural conditions result in significant differences in the distribution and characteristics of soil elements compared to other regions (Fabricio Neta et al., 2018; Rad et al., 2007). Research on the geochemical characteristics of soil phosphorus elements in volcanic islands is instrumental in uncovering the causes and mechanisms underlying soil fertility. Additionally, it provides a scientific basis and technical support for the protection and management of the ecological environment of these islands.

Phosphorus is an indispensable nutrient for plant growth and development, critical for processes such as root growth, flower bud differentiation, and fruit development. Its presence facilitates these essential physiological functions in plants (Chu et al., 2021; Lambers, 2022). Total phosphorus represents the overall quantity of phosphorus in the soil, serving as a crucial indicator for assessing its fertility status and providing valuable insights into phosphorus levels (Moser et al., 1959). Available phosphorus is the quantity of phosphorus in soil that is accessible and can be effectively absorbed and utilized by plants. Higher levels of available phosphorus content in the soil facilitate improved absorption of this essential nutrient by plants, thereby promoting their growth and development (Zhang et al., 2014). Adequate available phosphorus indicates a sufficient phosphorus supply, preventing stunted growth or reduced yield in plants. Moreover, high available phosphorus content enhances soil structure, increases crop nutrient absorption and utilization efficiency, and boosts overall soil fertility.

The relationship between total phosphorus (TP) and available phosphorus (AP) is crucial for predicting the dynamic changes in plant communities across different soil environments and understanding the factors affecting ecosystem stability and resilience. A high total phosphorus content does not necessarily indicate a high availability of phosphorus. For instance, research conducted in China has demonstrated that the proportion of available phosphorus varies significantly among different soil types. Even though some areas may have higher total phosphorus reserves, the density of available phosphorus can differ markedly among these soil types (Zhang et al., 2005). Similarly, studies on tropical Ultisols (Linquist et al., 1997) have shown that, despite high levels of total phosphorus, the availability of phosphorus for plant uptake may still be insufficient following fertilization, underscoring the critical role of available phosphorus in soil management. These findings highlight that total phosphorus content alone does not directly correlate with available phosphorus, emphasizing the need for a comprehensive understanding of available phosphorus dynamics.

The phosphorus cycle on volcanic islands has garnered attention in scientific research. For instance, Porder and Ramachandran (2013) investigated the influence of parent rock type on soil phosphorus content, particularly across different soil age gradients. Their study found that the type of parent rock significantly affects the total phosphorus content in the soil, a finding with global implications. For example, soils derived from phosphorus-rich rocks typically exhibit higher phosphorus concentrations when young. In contrast, older soils may show lower total phosphorus levels, a phenomenon closely related to the physical and chemical changes occurring during soil formation.

Organophosphorus compounds, a form of soil phosphorus, can be converted into inorganic phosphorus by microorganisms, thus contributing to the pool of available phosphorus. Turner and Engelbrecht (2011) utilized NaOH-EDTA extraction and 31P nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy to comprehensively evaluate the composition and properties of organophosphorus in 19 different soil samples from tropical rainforests in Panama. Their study revealed significant variability in organic phosphorus concentrations among the soils, with phosphoric acid monoesters being the predominant form. The proportion and form of organophosphorus varied significantly across different soil types and chemical environments. This variability indicates that, although organophosphorus constitutes a substantial portion of total soil phosphorus (approximately 26% on average), its content and form can differ markedly depending on the soil environment. This has important implications for plant phosphorus acquisition and nutrient cycling within ecosystems.

An investigation into Andisols formed on volcanic ash along the Sunda arc crossing Java Island, Indonesia (Van Ranst et al., 2004), revealed that various parent rock materials have a significant influence on the mineralogical and chemical properties of the soil. Changes in the content of active aluminum (Al) and iron (Fe) compounds are particularly noteworthy, as they enhance soil reactivity and the capacity to adsorb fluoride and phosphate. Moving from the east to the west of the island, there is a gradual increase in the soil’s ability to fix and retain phosphorus, underscoring the profound impact of geological variations on soil chemistry.

Mage and Porder (2013) investigated phosphorus (P) availability in the terrestrial ecosystem of the Luquillo Mountains in Puerto Rico and its primary drivers. The study examined the effects of two main parent materials (quartz diorite and volcaniclastic rocks), three topographic locations (ridge, slope, and valley), and elevation changes on phosphorus content and other indicators of phosphorus status, through soil sampling within the subwatershed. The results revealed that soils derived from volcaniclastic parent material contain significantly higher phosphorus levels compared to those from quartz diorite parent material, with increased proportions of insoluble phosphorus and higher relative phosphorus loss. Topographic location significantly affects the soluble phosphorus pool, with valley soils exhibiting notably higher phosphorus concentrations than ridge soils. The comprehensive analysis indicates that different environmental factors have varying degrees of influence on phosphorus status within the ecosystem, highlighting the significant roles of parent material type and topographic location in these variations.

On volcanic islands, the phosphorus cycle and soil age are closely related to climate and profoundly impact ecosystem nutrient status. Research by Peter Vitousek and Oliver Chadwick focuses on the Hawaiian Islands, where geological differences have led to unique biogeochemical processes and soil development characteristics. Along the age gradient of the islands, phosphorus sources in the soil shift significantly from young to old islands (Vitousek, 2006; Vitousek et al., 2019). Their study also highlights the impact of wet and dry cycles on soil phosphorus dynamics. Experiments in the Hawaiian Islands demonstrate that repeated wet and dry cycles enhance inorganic phosphorus exchange while reducing the efficiency of biological phosphorus cycles (Chadwick et al., 2007; Helfenstein et al., 2021). The total concentration of rock-derived nutrients varies in the soil fraction less than 2 mm across vegetated areas. Soil nutrient loss is primarily due to leaching under high precipitation conditions and is driven by plant cycling and wind erosion under low precipitation conditions. Conversely, nutrients are best preserved in topsoil with moderate precipitation, which supports abundant plant growth without contributing significantly beyond evapotranspiration. Vitousek and Chadwick (2013) also discuss soil development thresholds and processes, identifying different thresholds and process domains of soil development on islands with varying precipitation gradients and geological ages in the Hawaiian Islands.

The study of spring waters in the Taupo Volcanic Zone, North Island, New Zealand (Timperley, 1983), reveals that phosphorus and other elements primarily originate from the reaction processes within rhyolitic volcanic limestone aquifers. These reactions are significantly influenced by acidic conditions resulting from dissolved carbon dioxide in groundwater. However, the low concentration of calcium (Ca) in the water restricts the formation of hydroxyapatite. Thermodynamic analysis indicates that phosphorus can exist in water at high concentrations without exceeding the mineral’s solubility. Additionally, the deposition and dissolution of volcanic ash in aquatic environments play a crucial role in element release. Experimental findings (Jones & Gislason, 2008) demonstrate that the flux of elements released from unhydrated volcanic ash varies significantly depending on environmental conditions, particularly pH and water solubility, including abundant nutrients such as iron (Fe), silicon (Si), and phosphorus.

In addition, studies of phosphorus distribution and adsorption properties in heavily fertilized soils in northern Iran revealed significant differences in phosphorus content and chemical forms (Emadi et al., 2009). These variations reflect diverse phosphorus supply capacities among the soils. Sandy clay soils exhibit high maximum phosphorus buffer capacity and adsorption isotherms, attributed to their richness in iron (Fe), aluminum (Al), and clay particles. In contrast, sand shows low levels of iron (Fe), aluminum (Al), loam, and clay, highlighting the pivotal role of soil properties in phosphorus adsorption and release. Particularly noteworthy is the significant influence of soil texture and organic matter content on phosphorus behavior (Larsen, 1967; Mahdi & Mouhamad, 2018).

The study of volcanic islands, particularly concerning the unique properties of volcaniclastic soils, provides a theoretical foundation for examining similar volcanic environments such as Weizhou Island. This research holds practical significance for soil management and ecosystem restoration. The texture and organic matter content of volcanic island soils play crucial roles in the adsorption and release of phosphorus. Minerals such as aluminum (Al) and iron (Fe) compounds, which are common in these soils, significantly influence phosphorus reactivity and adsorption, thereby affecting its geochemical behavior.

Furthermore, soil development on volcanic islands is closely linked to the age and climatic conditions of the islands. The source and cycling of phosphorus in the soil change markedly with variations in age and precipitation. Additionally, volcanic activities impact groundwater acidity, which in turn affects phosphorus solubility. These soil characteristics, prevalent in volcanic islands, significantly influence the phosphorus cycle.

Similarly, Weizhou Island, as a subtropical volcanic island, experiences seasonal variations in precipitation and soil moisture. The presence of comparable minerals in its soil results in similar effects on phosphorus availability and biological cycling. Understanding the mechanisms of phosphorus cycling and soil development on Weizhou Island is highly relevant for comprehending phosphorus dynamics and ecosystem nutrient states in similar environments, providing valuable insights for research on analogous volcanic islands.

The distribution and cycling of phosphorus elements in volcanic island soils exhibit complex and diverse characteristics. Geological variances, hydrogeological processes, volcanic ash properties, and soil physicochemical attributes profoundly influence the adsorption, release, and bioavailability of phosphorus. These factors are intricately linked to the geological structure, climatic conditions, land use patterns, and human activities on volcanic islands. Investigating the phosphorus distribution and dynamics in volcanic island soils enhances our understanding of the geological evolution and soil formation processes specific to these environments. Such insights are crucial not only for safeguarding volcanic island ecosystems but also for providing theoretical underpinnings for effective environmental management.

Geological and geographical setting

Weizhou Island, located in Guangxi, is the youngest volcanic island in China. It is nearly circular, with a total area of approximately 25 km2 and a maximum elevation of 79 m. It has a subtropical monsoon climate and is one of the regions in Guangxi with the most abundant heat and rainfall (Cai et al., 2021a, b; Cao et al., 2016a, b; Wu et al., 2021). Weizhou Island is formed by volcanic eruptions and accumulation. Over 95% of its strata consist of volcanic rocks, and the surface is covered with weathered purple and red basalt (Yang et al., 2020). Its geological background and location map are shown in Fig. 1.

Weizhou Island’s volcanic materials and sediments weathered in a moist environment, forming a strongly corrosion-resistant latosol. The natural soil on the island is slightly acidic. However, after years of accumulation of marine sediments rich in coral and shellfish debris, the soil gradually became alkaline, with a pH of more than 8.0 and calcification (Shi and Tang, 2017). These geological processes and the evolution of soil characteristics provide unique conditions for the ecological environment and vegetation of Weizhou Island, and also inject vitality into the rich natural landscape and biodiversity of the island.

The land use of Weizhou Island (Fig. 2) indicates that most of the island is cropland. Cultivated land is primarily distributed in the northeast, garden land occupies central and southern areas, and forest land is mainly found in the northern and southern coastal regions. The densely populated areas are predominantly concentrated in the western corner of Weizhou Island.

Sampling and analytical methods

The sampling locations are depicted in Fig. 1. The surface soil sampling encompassed all the surface soil sample points (0–20 cm) across Weizhou Island. A grid method (500 m × 500 m) was employed to collect 104 soil samples, while 16 representative fresh rock samples were collected from the same location on the island. Sampling was conducted at profiles W01 and W02. The weathering crust of the W01 profile is located in Wucaitan, and the lithology is volcaniclastic rock. The W02 sampling site is situated behind the radar station, on the southeast side of the Daling intersection, and features a basalt weathering crust profile. Sampling was conducted systematically, progressing from fresh parent rock through semi-weathered and highly weathered strata to fully weathered strata, as illustrated in Figs. 3, 4. A total of 12 samples were collected from the W01 profile (W01-1 to W01-12) and 12 samples from the W02 profile (W02-1 to W02-12).

All soil samples collected from the W01 to W02 profiles, as well as from the surface of the sampling area, were pre-treated and naturally dried at room temperature to remove impurities such as plant roots and large gravel. Under contamination-free conditions, the samples were cleaned with Milli-Q water, dried, crushed at room temperature, passed through a 200-mesh nylon screen, and stored in sealed bags within a drying vessel. For subsequent analysis, pH was determined using the glass electrode method. The content of soil organic matter was measured by the potassium dichromate-sulfuric acid oxidation method. Major elements, including total phosphorus content, were analyzed using X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (XRF). We utilize the XRF ZSX PrimusII instrument to accurately determine principal elements. This instrument measures the intensity and energy of X-ray fluorescence in the sample using principles of spectral excitation and analysis, enabling precise qualitative and quantitative analysis of element content and type. The technical specifications of the XRF ZSX PrimusII instrument guarantee reliable experimental results (Table 1).

To ensure the accuracy of XRF analysis results, soil and rock samples were thoroughly digested prior to total element analysis. We employed an acid digestion method to guarantee complete dissolution of all elements in the samples. HF, HNO3, and HCl were added in a 1:2:2 volume ratio to the digestion vessel, followed by the addition of the pre-treated sample powder. The mixture was subjected to heat digestion on a heating plate at 150 °C for 4 h to ensure complete dissolution of all elements. After digestion, the sample solution was allowed to cool naturally and was then diluted to 50 mL with deionized water for XRF analysis.

To verify the effectiveness of the digestion process, we performed comparative analyses with reference materials to confirm that the results of the digested samples were consistent with those of the reference materials. The analysis of the digested samples was conducted in duplicate to ensure result consistency and reliability. These digestion procedures ensure that all elements in the sample are fully dissolved, providing a solid basis for accurate XRF analysis.

The Mehlich 3 soil test method was employed in this study (Pierzynski, 2000). The Mehlich 3 extractor consists of a mixture of NH4NO3, CH3COOH, HNO3 which effectively extracts phosphorus from both acidic and alkaline soils. This method is widely applicable across various soil types, particularly those with low phosphorus availability. The Mehlich 3 method is especially effective for soils with high mineral content and strong phosphorus fixation capacity, and it is particularly suitable for volcaniclastic and basaltic soils (Pierzynski, 2000). Therefore, we selected this method to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the test results.

In this study, the Mehlich 3 extract was prepared according to the method described by Mehlich (1984). This extract is widely used for analyzing soil available phosphorus. To prepare the reserve solution, 1200 mL of distilled water was added to a 2 L capacity cylinder. Then, 277.8 g of NH4F was added and stirred until completely dissolved. Next, 146.1 g of ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) was added, and stirring continued until the EDTA was fully dissolved. The solution was then adjusted to a final volume of 2 L and stored in a plastic container for later use.

To prepare the Mehlich 3 extract, 8 L of distilled water was added to a 10 L container. In this container, 200 g of NH4NO3 was dissolved. Subsequently, 40 mL of the reserve solution was added, and the mixture was stirred thoroughly. Then, 115 mL of CH3COOH (99.5%, 17.4 M) and 8.2 mL of HNO3 (68–70%, 15.5 M) were added. The final solution was adjusted to a pH of 2.5 ± 0.1.

The 2.0 g soil samples were accurately weighed, placed in a 50 mL conical flask, and their volumes and weights were recorded. Then, 20 mL of Mehlich 3 extract was added to the conical flask. The mixture was incubated at 24–27 °C and agitated at a rate exceeding 200 oscillations per minute for 5 min. To remove impurities, decolorization was performed by adding 200 mg of activated carbon (DARCO G60), followed by mixing and filtering. The extract was then filtered using Whatman No. 42 filter paper, and the process was repeated until the filtrate ran clear. Finally, the available phosphorus content was determined using a QT-YF02 soil nutrient analyzer, with calibration performed using a blank and standard solutions prepared with Mehlich 3 extract.

In this study, loss on ignition (LOI) was used to assess the carbonate content in the samples. Accurately weigh 5.0 g of each soil or rock sample, ensuring that the samples are homogeneous and free of visible impurities. Dry the samples in an oven at 105 °C for 24 h to remove moisture. After drying, remove the samples, cool them to room temperature, and measure their dry mass (DW1). Next, roast the dried samples in a high-temperature furnace preheated to 1000 °C–1050 °C for 2 h to remove volatile substances, including carbonates and organic matter. Transfer the roasted samples to a desiccator, cool them to room temperature, and accurately weigh and record their roasted masses (DW2) (Santisteban et al., 2004).

The sample analysis was conducted by the Guangxi Key Laboratory of Concealed Metal Mineral Exploration at Guilin University of Technology. This laboratory has established a robust quality control and quality assurance protocol through many similar studies over the years (Wu et al., 2015; Yuan et al., 2023).

Using SPSS 21.0 and Origin 2021, we conducted descriptive statistical analysis, correlation analysis, and multiple linear regression analysis on the gathered soil sample data. All geostatistical analyses were performed using ArcGIS (version 10.2), ensuring meticulous and reliable exploration and interpretation of the data.

The Chemical Index of Alteration (CIA) was used to describe the characteristics of soil weathering and the degree of rock weathering at different stages. This index reflects the migration and enrichment ratios of chemical elements in various minerals and cements within the weathering crust, representing the overall characteristics of natural chemical weathering in the weathering crust. Generally, the lower the content of SiO2 and the higher the contents of Fe2O3 and Al2O3, the greater the degree of soil weathering (Nesbitt & Young, 1982).

LOI (loss on ignition) refers to the percentage of mass loss from a sample during high-temperature roasting, primarily due to the decomposition of carbonates and the volatilization of organic matter (Hoogsteen et al., 2015; Santisteban et al., 2004). During this process, carbonates decompose into carbon dioxide (CO2) and other volatile substances, leading to a reduction in sample mass. This method estimates the volatile substances in the sample by measuring the mass lost at high temperatures, thereby reflecting the content of carbonates and organic matter. A higher LOI value typically indicates a greater content of organic matter and carbonates.

The comprehensive evaluation index requires dimensionless standardization (Su et al., 2015). Because these indicators differ in meaning and dimension and are not directly comparable, their values need to be standardized within the range of 0–1. The standardized calculation formula Si is used to obtain the value of indicator xi after standardization, where xi represents the value of the indicator before standardization, xmax and xmin represent the highest and lowest values of the original data. The calculated value of each indicator will then fall within the range of 0–1.

Geostatistical interpolation is an important method for analyse the spatial variability of surface materials such as soil. By using measured data from the same area and combining it with geostatistical principles, the unknown regions can be predicted to obtain a spatial continuity layer. To ensure the continuity and accuracy of the data, the Ordinary Kriging method (Cressie, 1988) was adopted in this study to construct the spatial variation map. The Spatial Dependency Index (SDI) (Cambardella et al., 1994) was used to verify spatial autocorrelation. This index is used to determine the range of spatial correlations between samples and calculate the degree of correlation based on the relationship between the nugget effect (Co) and the sill (Co + C) (Bogunovic et al., 2017).

Statistical indicators provide an objective assessment of the accuracy of the spatial interpolation model. By using cross-validation techniques, the accuracy of the spatial interpolation theoretical model is evaluated, and statistical indicators of the prediction process are obtained: coefficient of determination (R2), mean error (ME), and root mean square error (RMSE).

Characteristics of total phosphorus level distribution

The acidity and alkalinity (pH) of the soil affect the availability of phosphorus (Cai et al., 2021a, b; Najafi & Jalali, 2016). In acidic soil, interactions reduce the availability of phosphorus, resulting in low total phosphorus content. Soil type and characteristics are also important factors affecting phosphorus content (Djodjic et al., 2004; Han et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2022). Different types of soil have different physical, chemical, and biological properties that affect phosphorus cycling and bioavailability. In addition, soil microbial activity, soil erosion and loss, vegetation cover and ecosystem restoration, soil organic matter content, soil permeability and water conditions, land use history and human activities, as well as topography and geomorphic features, also impact the relationship between soil total phosphorus content in Weizhou Island (Table 2).

Physicochemical Properties and Total Phosphorus Content of Surface Soil in the Study Area

According to the distribution map of surface soil total phosphorus content and land use types in Weizhou Island (Fig. 2, Fig. 5), low values of total phosphorus content are found in the wharfs and tails of Weizhou Island in the west, where the lithology is volcaniclastic rock. In contrast, forest areas show high values of total phosphorus.

Distribution of total phosphorus (TP) in surface soil of Weizhou Island

Porder and Ramachandran (2013) demonstrated that rock type significantly influences total phosphorus content. Silica-rich, iron-poor rocks exhibit lower total phosphorus levels, whereas iron-rich, silica-poor rocks have higher phosphorus content. Specifically, basalts contain substantially more phosphorus compared to other rock types, while the phosphorus content in metamorphic rocks is similar to that of their original igneous or sedimentary precursors. The observed low total phosphorus content in the volcaniclastic rock regions of Weizhou Island aligns with Porder and Ramachandran’s findings, further confirming the substantial effect of rock type on total phosphorus content.

As a relatively natural ecosystem, the high content of total phosphorus in forest soil is attributed to the combined effects of various factors (Bates & Baker, 1960; Turner & Engelbrecht, 2011). Forest land typically has a high organic matter content, which promotes the fixation and release of phosphorus, resulting in relatively high total phosphorus content in the soil. The forest ecosystem contains abundant microorganisms and plant root systems, which participate in the phosphorus cycling process in the soil. These factors promote the effective absorption and utilization of phosphorus, leading to high total phosphorus content. Forest soil typically has a slightly acidic to neutral pH (Lie et al., 2023; Spielvogel et al., 2008; Ste-Marie & Paré, 1999). This suitable pH value enhances the biological availability of phosphorus and facilitates its absorption and utilization by plants, leading to a relatively high total phosphorus content. Forest areas are densely vegetated, with trees, shrubs, and herbs contributing to the accumulation of total phosphorus in the soil by absorbing nutrients, including phosphorus, from the soil. Additionally, forest land typically exhibits better soil permeability and moisture conditions, which promote phosphorus migration and circulation, resulting in a relatively high total phosphorus content.

Controlling effect of soil parent rock on soil total phosphorus

According to the soil layer and corresponding total phosphorus content data of volcaniclastic rock profile W01 provided (Fig. 3), it can be seen that the total phosphorus content in the volcaniclastic rock profile presents irregular fluctuations with the increase of depth. During the process from the deep layer to the surface layer, the total phosphorus content decreased first and then increased. At a depth of 80 cm, the total phosphorus content increased. In the soil layer between 180 and 200 cm, which is close to the bedrock, the total phosphorus content reached its highest value in the entire W01 profile (46.4 mg/kg). The soil’s total phosphorus content exhibited a complex and non-linear trend, decreasing initially and then increasing from the deep layer to the surface layer. This pattern can be attributed to various complex geochemical and ecological processes. The relatively high total phosphorus content in the surface soil is mainly influenced by biological activities, including the release and accumulation of phosphorus resulting from organic matter decomposition and the action of plant roots (Ducousso-Détrez et al., 2022; Luo et al., 2022). Deep soil is affected by erosion and leaching, resulting in a decrease in total phosphorus content. Plant roots are mainly distributed in the surface soil, absorbing phosphorus in the soil and leaving a high total phosphorus content in the soil. Hydrogeological factors such as groundwater flow and changes in the groundwater table also affect the distribution of soil total phosphorus content.

In the basalt W02 profile (Fig. 4), the total phosphorus content fluctuates to a certain extent, and there is no obvious linear trend. Instead, the total phosphorus content increases from the deep layer to the middle layer, then decreases from the middle layer to the surface layer. The total phosphorus content in the uppermost layer is basically the same as that in the deep layer. From the deep layer of the profile to the surface layer, the total phosphorus content in adjacent soil layers changes irregularly and fluctuates greatly. In the deep layer (165–180 cm) of basalt bedrock, the total phosphorus content is 83.9 mg/kg, which does not show a significant decreasing trend. This indicates that the effect of basalt bedrock on the total phosphorus content is not very significant. The variation in total phosphorus content in soil is affected by many complex factors. The diversity of environmental factors, including precipitation, temperature, soil type, and vegetation cover, leads to the irregular changes in total phosphorus content in different soil layers (Arenberg & Arai, 2019; Ilyas & Masih, 2018). The cycling of phosphorus in soil is jointly regulated by biological, chemical, and physical processes, such as the decomposition of organic matter, uptake by plant roots, and microbial activities, which further increase the uncertainty and fluctuation of total phosphorus content (Ding et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2021). Geological factors also affect the distribution of soil total phosphorus content (Liu et al., 2021; Zhang & Wang, 2020), especially in the basalt bedrock area, the weathering degree of bedrock and the supply of phosphorus elements have a significant effect on the spatial distribution of total phosphorus content.

The content of total phosphorus in deep soil is higher, which is affected by the accumulation of rich phosphate minerals or phosphorus elements in deep soil. As the soil profile moves towards the surface, the total phosphorus content gradually decreases due to the transfer and loss of phosphorus elements, such as water erosion and soil particle migration. It is worth noting that the total phosphorus content of the surface soil is basically the same as that of the deep soil, because the surface soil receives new phosphorus input from plant residues and organic matter decomposition, and the surface soil gradually tends to balance phosphorus content after long-term agricultural management, fertilization and plant absorption. Therefore, higher total phosphorus content in deep soil is expected to promote crop growth and development, but the reduction of phosphorus in middle to surface layer requires appropriate fertilization measures to maintain soil fertility and meet crop phosphorus demand.

The total phosphorus content of volcaniclastic bedrock is 46.4 mg/kg, while that of basalt bedrock is 83.9 mg/kg, which is different due to the comprehensive influence of various factors. The composition of the rock types is different. Volcaniclastic rock is rich in silicon and potassium, while basalt is rich in iron (Fe) and magnesium, which directly affects the level of total phosphorus content in the rock. Different sedimentary environments also affect the phosphorus content in rocks (Yalcin et al., 2022; Planavsky et al., 2023). Volcaniclastic rocks are formed in the process of volcanic eruption and volcaniclastic accumulation, while basalt is formed by the cooling and solidification of volcanic magma flow. These different sedimentary environments play an important role in the formation of total phosphorus content. Later geological processes, such as metamorphism and hydrothermal activities experienced by rocks after their formation, also affect the total phosphorus content (Janicka et al., 2022; Nishigaki et al., 2018). These processes change the chemical composition and mineral composition of the rocks, thus affecting the level of total phosphorus content.

The total phosphorus content of basalt bedrock is significantly higher than that of volcaniclastic rock, indicating that basalt soil has more abundant phosphorus supply capacity and higher fertility level. This difference will directly affect soil fertility and plant growth and development. In contrast, volcaniclastic soils require additional phosphate supplementation or other soil treatments to improve fertility, meet plant phosphorus requirements, and promote healthy growth.

In the profiles of volcaniclastic rocks and basalts, the variation trend of total phosphorus content reflects the geological characteristics and formation processes of different soil types. In the basalt profile, the increase in total phosphorus content from the deep to the middle layer is attributed to the decomposition of organic matter and the release of phosphorus from mineral weathering (Babechuk et al., 2014; Dontsova et al., 2014). Conversely, the decrease from the middle to the surface layer is associated with plant root absorption and nutrient loss (Dontsova et al., 2020). The total phosphorus content in the surface layer is similar to that in the deep layer due to the input of new phosphorus. In contrast, the volcaniclastic rock profile exhibits a trend of decreasing total phosphorus content followed by an increase, influenced by processes of soil erosion and deposition. The increases observed near the 80 cm depth are associated with organic enrichment or phosphorus input, while the higher levels found in the bedrock strata reflect variations in geological composition and phosphorus sources.

Control effect of soil parent rock on soil available phosphorus

In the study of soil phosphorus, total phosphorus content and available phosphorus content are two important indicators. The relationship between them is crucial for evaluating soil nutrient status and formulating appropriate fertilization strategies. Changes in total phosphorus content in soil directly impact the availability of phosphorus, thereby affecting crop growth and yield (Ahmad et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2022). Studying the correlation between total phosphorus content and available phosphorus content can help better understand the dynamic changes of phosphorus in soil.

Porder and Ramachandran (2013) demonstrated that different rock types significantly affect soil phosphorus content and availability, with phosphorus availability being closely related to rock type. Figure 6 shows a strong positive correlation between total phosphorus and available phosphorus in the volcaniclastic rock and basalt profiles. Pearson’s R (0.969) approaches 1, indicating a high linear correlation between the two, and the R2 value (0.939) is also high, Total phosphorus content effectively explains variations in available phosphorus in soil, consistent with the conclusions of Porder and Ramachandran, and further supports the significant influence of rock type on soil phosphorus availability. In these two profiles, the total phosphorus content exhibits a strong predictive ability for available phosphorus content, providing valuable insights for assessing soil fertility and guiding fertilization management.

According to the analysis data of soil samples from the volcaniclastic rock profile (Table 3), the contents of total phosphorus and available phosphorus exhibit a certain pattern of variation across different depth ranges. Distribution of total phosphorus (TP) and available phosphorus (AP) in basalt and volcaniclastic rock profiles in Weizhou island

The content of available phosphorus in profile W01 of volcaniclastic rock is higher in the depth range of 180–200 cm, and the content of available phosphorus shows a trend of decreasing first and then increasing during the process from deep to surface. The available phosphorus content increases near 80 cm depth and is highest in deeper bedrock formations. Specifically, the available phosphorus content fluctuated greatly in the depth range of 20–80 cm and remained at a relatively high level, especially about 20 mg/kg at the depth of 20–40 cm and 80–180 cm. However, in the range of 40–80 cm depth, the available phosphorus content decreased slightly, from 23.1 mg/kg to 13.4 mg/kg. In the range of 80–200 cm depth, the available phosphorus content showed an overall increasing trend, and reached the highest value of 23.8 mg/kg at the depth of 180–200 cm. This change trend indicates that there are rich phosphorus resources in volcaniclastic rocks and rich phosphorus resources available to plants in soil, which are the result of the comprehensive effects of phosphate minerals contained in volcaniclastic rocks, weathering degree and soil formation process.

In contrast, the data from the basalt W02 profile (Table 3) show that the total phosphorus content increases from the deep layer (165–180 cm: 83.900 mg/kg) to the middle layer (120–135 cm: 105.300 mg/kg) and decreases from the middle layer (120–135 cm: 105.300 mg/kg) to the surface layer (45–60 cm: 68.600 mg/kg). The total phosphorus content in the most superficial layer (15–30 cm: 80.200 mg/kg) and in the deep layer (165–180 cm: 83.900 mg/kg) is essentially the same. In the depth range of 105–135 cm, the total phosphorus content in the soil was higher, reflecting the enrichment of phosphorus in this depth range. In the depth range of 120–150 cm, the total phosphorus content in the soil was significantly higher than in other depth sections, reaching its peak value of 105.300 mg/kg in the 120–135 cm range. Conversely, the total phosphorus content in the 45–60 cm soil layer was relatively low, at 68.600 mg/kg.

The content of available phosphorus in the basalt W02 profile soil increases gradually from the deep layer (165–180 cm: 23.200 mg/kg) to the middle layer (120–135 cm: 23.700 mg/kg). From the middle layer (120–135 cm: 23.700 mg/kg) to the surface layer (45–60 cm: 16.000 mg/kg), the available phosphorus content shows a decreasing trend. It is worth noting that the available phosphorus content in the surface layer (15–30 cm: 22.900 mg/kg) and the deep layer (165–180 cm: 23.200 mg/kg) is basically the same, which is similar to the change trend of total phosphorus content. The reason for the increase in available phosphorus content from the deep (165–180 cm: 23.200 mg/kg) to the middle layer (120–135 cm: 23.700 mg/kg) is likely due to the migration of phosphorus from deep soil organic matter degradation to the middle layer, leading to an increase in middle layer phosphorus content. The decrease in available phosphorus content from the middle layer (120–135 cm: 23.700 mg/kg) to the surface layer (45–60 cm: 16.000 mg/kg) is due to the higher activity of phosphorus in the surface soil, which is more easily absorbed and utilized by plants, resulting in relatively low available phosphorus content in the surface layer. The obvious enrichment of available phosphorus in the depth range of 105–135 cm may be influenced by the mineral composition and the history of fertilization in this depth range. This phenomenon of enrichment reflects the accumulation of soil phosphorus in this depth profile. The fluctuation trend of available phosphorus content was influenced by soil environmental factors, such as precipitation, aeration, and microbial activity (Arenberg & Arai, 2019; Ilyas & Masih, 2018).

Based on the analysis of the two sets of data, the distribution of available phosphorus content in soil in the profiles of volcaniclastic rock and basalt shows different characteristics. Basalt and volcaniclastic rocks exhibit specific differences in phosphorus content. Although the total phosphorus content of basalt is higher than that of volcaniclastic rock, the available phosphorus content of basalt is similar to that of volcaniclastic rock. This observation is consistent with the phosphorus concentration variations in different rock types reported by Porder and Ramachandran (2013). Turner and Engelbrecht (2011) also found similar variations in available phosphorus across different soils. The mineral composition and chemical properties of rocks determine the rate and form of phosphorus release, which subsequently influences the phosphorus status in the soil.

Basalt and volcaniclastic rocks are influenced by distinct geological conditions during their formation, leading to differences in total phosphorus content (de Aquino et al., 2020; Xiang et al., 2015). Basalt undergoes more mineral enrichment and metamorphism, resulting in relatively high total phosphorus content. Furthermore, phosphorus in basalt exists in an insoluble form (Pasek et al., 2022; Walton et al., 2023), limiting its availability in soil and affecting the rate of phosphorus release, resulting in slower phosphorus release in basalt. Soil environmental characteristics such as pH and texture also impact the effective absorption and utilization of phosphorus. On the other hand, volcaniclastic rocks undergo varying degrees of weathering and loss, resulting in lower total phosphorus content. Additionally, geological characteristics also influence the availability of phosphorus in the soil, leading to similar available phosphorus content. Environmental conditions such as precipitation, temperature, and soil texture also affect the distribution and availability of phosphorus in soil.

Discussions

Correlation analysis of soil physicochemical properties and phosphorus elements

There are obvious differences in phosphorus content and soil properties across different types of soil profiles. Geological characteristics affect the availability of phosphorus in soil (Nishigaki, et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2022), while environmental conditions such as precipitation, temperature, and soil texture also influence the distribution and availability of phosphorus in soil (Cao et al., 2016a, b; Condron et al., 1990; Hou et al., 2018). There is a complex correlation between total phosphorus content, available phosphorus content, pH, OM, and chemical weathering index in soil, which is influenced by the unique geological origin and mineral composition of the parent rock.

According to Table 3, Table 4, and Table 5, the total phosphorus content in the volcaniclastic rock profile of W01 shows a fluctuating trend at different soil depths, but increases significantly to 46.4 mg/kg at 180–200 cm, which is related to the distribution of phosphate minerals in the deep soil layer of the volcaniclastic rock. The available phosphorus content throughout the W01 profile is relatively stable with a small variation range, ranging from 9.3 mg/kg to 10 mg/kg, indicating that the available phosphorus in the soil of volcaniclastic rocks is affected by the adsorption and release of minerals and maintains a relatively constant level. The soil pH value decreases gradually with increasing soil depth, from 7.15 to 5.68. This change is influenced by the mineral composition in the soil, resulting in an increase in soil acidity. The organic matter content decreases significantly with increasing depth, from 2.765 to 1.324%, reflecting the high decomposition rate of organic matter in the deep soil of volcaniclastic rocks, leading to a decrease in organic matter content. The soil chemical weathering index increases from 69.0 to 88.0 with increasing depth, reflecting changes in mineral composition and the influence of geochemical weathering processes on the soil.

Compared with W01, the basalt profile of W02 shows significantly higher total and available phosphorus content (Table 3). This difference is attributed to the higher organic matter content in the soil of the W02 profile. Phosphorus released by the decomposition of organic matter increases the soil phosphorus content, and organic matter can improve the soil structure and promote ion exchange, which is conducive to the adsorption and storage of phosphorus (Rittmann et al., 2011; Zhao et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2018). Additionally, the alkaline soil conditions and low iron (Fe) and aluminum (Al) oxide content of the W02 profile also contribute to improving the availability of phosphorus in the soil (Table 4).

A comprehensive analysis of the soil volcaniclastic W01 profile and basalt W02 profile data shows that there are some differences in total and available phosphorus contents between the two. In particular, the total and available phosphorus contents of the soil volcaniclastic rock profile are relatively low, but abnormally high contents appear in some samples from deep soil layers, especially in the depth range of 180–200 cm. The basalt profile shows a wide range of total and available phosphorus contents, especially in shallow soil layers. The relatively high content of phosphorus in the soil, especially the high value of the CIA parameter, suggests a high abundance of aluminum (Al) and other elements in the soil (Fan et al., 2019; Schroeder et al., 2004; Zheng, 2010).

The scatterplots depicting the relationships between soil total phosphorus (TP), available phosphorus (AP), soil pH, organic matter (OM), and the chemical index of alteration (CIA) for the W01 and W02 profiles (Fig. 7 and Fig. 8) reveal that pH and OM have a more significant impact on TP and AP in the W01 volcaniclastic profile compared to the W02 basalt profile. Conversely, the influence of CIA on TP and AP is stronger in the W02 basalt profile than in the W01 profile. Basalts are rich in phosphorus, and as minerals decompose and dissolve during weathering, a large amount of phosphorus is released, resulting in an increase in total and available phosphorus content (Delmelle et al., 2015; Porder & Ramachandran, 2013; Saputro et al., 2022). Soil pH and organic matter content also affect the adsorption and desorption of phosphorus. Lower pH values and higher organic matter content contribute to the desorption of phosphorus, making it more easily absorbed by plants (Debicka et al., 2016; Ghodszad et al., 2022; Spohn et al., 2022). In contrast, the phosphorus content in volcaniclastic rocks are relatively low, soil pH and organic matter content regulate the total phosphorus content by affecting the process of phosphorus dissolution and adsorption, the content of available phosphorus is mainly affected by soil weathering, which leads to the decomposition of minerals and the release of available phosphorus (Gillman et al., 2002; Anda et al., 2015; Lewis et al., 2021).

LOI (loss on ignition) represents the mass loss of a sample when heated at high temperatures, which typically includes the loss of water, carbonates, and organic matter (Heiri et al., 2001; Wang et al., 2011). In soil and rock analysis, high LOI values are often associated with higher carbonate content. The CaO (calcium oxide) content can also reflect the presence of carbonates (Gupta & Fan, 2002; Wang et al., 2018). Carbonate minerals, such as calcite and dolomite, are relatively stable during weathering and contribute to the formation of CaO in the soil. Consequently, higher CaO content usually indicates a higher presence of carbonate minerals in the soil over the long term. By analyzing LOI data and CaO content, the carbonate content in the sample can be inferred, which helps further understand the influence of carbonates on phosphorus availability in the soil (Gupta & Fan, 2002; Heiri et al., 2001; Wang et al., 2011).

As shown in Table 4, the LOI values in the W01 volcaniclastic rock profile range from 7.650 to 13.526 across different depths, while in the W02 basalt profile, LOI values range from 5.988 to 14.875 at various depths. Overall, the LOI values in the W02 basalt profile tend to be higher than those in the W01 volcaniclastic rock profile. Higher LOI values generally indicate a higher carbonate content. Specifically, in the W02 basalt profile, LOI values are notably elevated at the 90–180 cm depth range, from 12.874 to 14.875. This depth range in the W02 basalt profile also shows a higher CaO content, ranging from 6.532 to 10.763, which corresponds to the increased LOI values and suggests a greater carbonate presence in these layers. Conversely, the high LOI layer (90–180 cm) in the W02 basalt profile exhibits a low CIA value (34.300 to 53.600), indicating that the carbonate content may inhibit the weathering process. In contrast, the LOI values in the W01 volcaniclastic rock profile show relatively less variation and lower values (7.650 to 13.526), consistent with the lower CaO content in this profile (0.168 to 0.251), suggesting a smaller carbonate content.

The bioavailability of phosphorus is generally low in soils with high carbonate content (Helyar and Anderson, 1974; Prakash et al., 2017; Geng et al., 2022). Carbonate minerals (such as calcite and dolomite) can react with phosphorus in soil to form insoluble phosphate mineral complexes, thereby reducing phosphorus availability (Prakash et al., 2017). For instance, carbonates can react with phosphates to form calcium phosphate minerals (such as hydroxyapatite) (Tunesi et al., 1999), which have low solubility in water and thus limit phosphorus bioavailability. Additionally, high carbonate content raises soil pH, making the soil more alkaline, which further affects phosphorus solubility (Arnesen et al., 2007). While high pH may enhance phosphorus dissolution in some environments, phosphorus is released more slowly under high carbonate conditions. This slow release leads to phosphorus being fixed in insoluble minerals, thereby reducing its availability in the soil (Helyar and Anderson, 1974).

Furthermore, carbonate minerals are relatively stable during chemical weathering, which inhibits weathering processes. The presence of carbonate affects the chemical behavior of phosphorus, further decreasing its availability. The fixation of phosphorus in insoluble minerals during weathering reduces the amount of available phosphorus (Prakash et al., 2017). Consequently, in soils with high carbonate content, phosphorus availability is influenced by multiple factors, resulting in low phosphorus bioavailability, which adversely affects plant growth and development.

Relationship between topography, environmental changes, and soil phosphorus

As shown in Fig. 9, most sample sites with low phosphorus values are located in the high-elevation areas of Weizhou Island. These areas are characterized by volcaniclastic rocks, which lack phosphorus-rich minerals. Consequently, the phosphorus source in the soil is limited, and the release of phosphorus during long-term weathering is constrained. The slower rate of soil weathering and the dryness at high elevations further reduce the biological availability of phosphorus, resulting in low phosphorus content. Additionally, the geological conditions at high elevations exacerbate phosphorus loss due to increased weathering and erosion. Higher precipitation and water flow rates at these elevations lead to significant phosphorus removal through erosion, which reduces soil phosphorus accumulation and limits plant growth and biomass. This, in turn, further diminishes phosphorus input and the accumulation of soil organic matter. The presence of low magnesium calcite and relatively high strontium content in the diagenetic environment of the beach rocks on Weizhou Island (Liu et al., 2020) also suggests that seawater scour plays a significant role in the diagenetic process.

Spatial Distribution of Low Phosphorus Content Points on the Elevation Terrain of Weizhou Island

Climate factors and wind-driven waves shape landforms, affecting the distribution of low phosphorus values in high-elevation regions. On Weizhou Island, which experiences a subtropical monsoon climate, the erosion effects of wind waves and typhoons are particularly significant along the southeast to southwest coast (Mo, 1989), the development of microlandforms, such as marine erosion cliffs and platforms, has significantly inhibited the release of phosphorus from soil and sediments during weathering in these areas. Additionally, changes in wind direction and wave action on the island have caused material from the southern shore to be transported to the northern regions. Consequently, the accumulation of sand berms and coral reef deposits in the north has resulted in relatively high phosphorus concentrations in these areas. However, the high-elevation areas are primarily composed of old volcanic rocks, which weather relatively slowly, resulting in low phosphorus release and soil phosphorus concentrations.

The distribution of low phosphorus values on Weizhou Island is closely linked to historical sea level changes and geological uplift. Liu et al. (2020) demonstrated that at the end of the Pleistocene, the sea level in the Beibu Gulf was significantly lower than it is today, with Weizhou Island being land at that time. As sea levels rose, the terrigenous debris in island sediments gradually diminished, while marine sedimentary components increased, leading to a decrease in phosphorus content. During the Late Holocene, sea levels around Weizhou Island were approximately 0.91 to 1.16 m higher than present levels. This rise in sea level intensified seawater erosion, resulting in phosphorus loss and lower phosphorus values in beach rocks. Additionally, rising sea levels exacerbated saline intrusion, which affected soil salinization and negatively impacted the bioavailability and deposition of phosphorus. The increased frequency of erosion and soil loss associated with higher sea levels further reduced phosphorus accumulation at high elevations.

Weizhou Island is located on the Weizhou Bulge at the western margin of the northern part of Leiqiong Depression in the Himalayan stage subsidence zone (Hou et al., 1987; Mo, 1989), the ongoing uplift facilitates the preservation of ancient sea caves and sand embankments. In this tectonic setting, sediments at high elevations undergo prolonged weathering processes, resulting in a further decline in phosphorus content. Furthermore, Liu et al. (2020) showed that 14C absolute dating of the beach rocks indicates an island uplift rate of approximately 1.6 to 2.1 mm/year. This long-term, stable uplift rate exacerbates the dilution of phosphorus content at elevated elevations.

The future sea-level rise may impact the phosphorus content in Weizhou Island. Tang et al. (2013) reported calculation results from the current climate model indicating an expected sea-level rise of 30 cm, which could potentially mitigate the island’s vulnerability to future sea-level changes. The rising sea levels could alter the island’s erosion patterns and sediment distribution, subsequently influencing phosphorus release and sedimentation. Specifically, as sea levels increase, coastal erosion may intensify, leading to the release of phosphorus from terrestrial sediments into the ocean. However, projections suggesting a limited short-term impact of sea-level rise on islands imply that any consequent changes may not significantly affect long-term phosphorus levels.

Evaluation of soil nutrient levels in Weizhou Island

The contents of total phosphorus (TP), available phosphorus (AP), and organic matter (OM) in the soil of Weizhou Island (Table 3) were standardized according to the soil nutrient grade standard for Weizhou Island (Table 5) to assess the soil nutrient status.

As shown in Fig. 10, the standardized values for organic matter (OM) content in the W01 volcaniclastic rock profile are predominantly 3 and 4, where a value of 3 represents high and medium organic matter content, and a value of 4 represents medium organic matter. This suggests that the W01 profile has favorable conditions for organic matter, beneficial for soil health and plant growth. However, total phosphorus (TP) content varies significantly, ranging from 10.500 mg/kg to 46.400 mg/kg, with a standardized value of 6 indicating extremely low phosphorus content. This variability reflects the uneven distribution of phosphorus resources in the soil. The standardized value for available phosphorus (AP) is primarily 4, indicating a medium level of available phosphorus, with content ranging from 7.400 mg/kg to 10.000 mg/kg. Despite fluctuations in total phosphorus, the actual phosphorus resources available to plants remain relatively stable, though overall levels are still low. In summary, while the W01 volcaniclastic rock profile shows high organic matter content, phosphorus resources are unevenly distributed and largely unavailable to plants. Although total phosphorus (TP) content varies widely and is considered “extremely low” in some areas, the available phosphorus (AP) remains relatively stable and medium, potentially limiting phosphorus supply to plants.

The soil organic matter (OM) content in the W02 basalt profile ranges from 1.223 to 3.067, with standardized values predominantly 3 and 4. A value of 3 corresponds to high and medium organic matter content, while a value of 4 indicates medium content. This suggests that the soil organic matter in the W02 profile is generally lower compared to the W01 profile, which may impact soil structure and plant growth. The total phosphorus (TP) content in the W02 profile ranges from 68.600 mg/kg to 105.300 mg/kg, which is significantly higher than in the W01 profile. Despite the high total phosphorus, there is still potential for improvement to enhance phosphorus availability and utilization.

Although the total phosphorus content is low in the soil nutrient rating, the high available phosphorus content means that there are some suitable factors for plant growth in the soil of Weizhou Island, which have a better supporting role for plant growth. Available phosphorus is necessary for plant growth, and its sufficiency helps plants absorb the nutrients they need and promote growth. This study provided some reference for soil management and agricultural production in Weizhou Island, and corresponding fertilization and control measures could be taken according to the available phosphorus content in soil to better support the growth and development of plants.

Conclusions

In a subtropical volcanic island environment, significant differences exist in the distribution and bioavailability of phosphorus between volcaniclastic and basaltic soils. The total phosphorus (TP) and available phosphorus (AP) contents in basaltic soil are higher than in volcaniclastic soil, primarily due to the release of phosphate minerals during weathering. However, the high carbonate content in basaltic soil inhibits further weathering, resulting in phosphorus being present as insoluble calcium phosphate minerals and thereby reducing its biological availability. Additionally, the presence of carbonate increases soil pH, further limiting phosphorus dissolution and uptake by plants.

In contrast, the phosphorus content in volcaniclastic soils is more significantly influenced by soil pH and organic matter content. In these soils, both total phosphorus and available phosphorus are primarily controlled by the degree of soil weathering and environmental conditions. The formation of high-elevation areas with low phosphorus values is closely related to geological background and climatic conditions. Limited phosphorus sources in the soil, combined with precipitation and water flow, exacerbate phosphorus erosion and loss. Additionally, historical sea level changes have impacted phosphorus distribution, with marine erosion and saltwater intrusion reducing phosphorus accumulation in terrestrial sediments.

In general, the total phosphorus (TP) content in volcaniclastic soil is very low and unevenly distributed. Although the available phosphorus (AP) content is moderate, it is insufficient to effectively address phosphorus nutrient deficiencies. In contrast, phosphorus resources in basaltic soil are more abundant, but the availability of total phosphorus may be limited. Basaltic soil tends to have better long-term fertility compared to volcaniclastic soil, and its higher phosphorus content enhances soil fertility potential. Despite the low total phosphorus content of volcanic island soils, the high available phosphorus content indicates that phosphorus in these soils is in a form readily absorbed by plants. This finding highlights the potential advantages of volcanic island soils in phosphorus resource management and suggests that soil improvement and agricultural practices should focus on increasing phosphorus content in volcaniclastic soils while fully utilizing the fertility potential of basaltic soils.
