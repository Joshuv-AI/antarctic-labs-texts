Actin cytoskeleton protection by the formin-mediated safety valve
Authors: Fernando R. Valencia, Sergey V. Plotnikov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mechanical forces shape cellular form and function by regulating key cellular processes; however, when dysregulated, they contribute to disease. Excessive forces can be detrimental to cells, damaging cytoskeleton, deforming nuclei, and even rupturing the cell itself. To counteract these effects, cells deploy protective mechanisms that enhance mechanical resilience. Emerging evidence highlights a novel strategy for rapid tension release via force-dependent actin polymerization mediated by formin-family proteins such as Dia1. Acting as a mechanical “safety valve”, Dia1 buffers otherwise damaging stress and promotes zyxin-mediated repair, preserving cytoskeletal architecture, safeguarding the nucleus, and maintaining cellular integrity. Loss of Dia1 disrupts signaling cascades that converge on key mechanotransduction processes governing cell fate and disease progression. In this review, we explore recent advances in force-dependent actin polymerization and its role in cytoskeletal protection, nuclear homeostasis, and cellular adaptation to mechanical forces.

Introduction: sensing, resisting and thriving under mechanical force

Mechanical forces, ever-present and constantly changing, have the capacity to challenge, shape, and define cellular behaviours from the onset of life to death. The forces regulate key biological processes — governing cell fate commitment [1,2], metabolic activity [3] and migration [4] — ultimately shaping tissue development and driving disease. For example, during embryogenesis, the precise spatiotemporal regulation of mechanical interactions drives fundamental morphogenic events, including cell ingression [5], extrusion [6,7] and tissue invagination [8], ensuring proper germ layer formation and enabling proper organ development [9,10]. However, mechanical forces can also be detrimental. Excessive forces, whether exerted on or generated within the cell, can have devastating effects on cell and tissue physiology — damaging stress fibers [11], causing nuclear rupture and DNA leakage [12], or even rupturing entire cells and wounding tissue [13,14]. To counteract these harmful effects, cells deploy multiple protective mechanisms that enhance their mechanical resilience. For instance, mechanical stimuli can trigger the recruitment and assembly of intermediate filaments in the perinuclear region (e.g. keratins), which act as a protective shield that absorbs and dissipates forces [15]. Similarly, the recruitment and assembly of intermediate filaments at the inner nuclear membrane (e.g. laminA/C) reinforce nuclear structural integrity, increasing nuclear stiffness and mechanically integrating chromatin with force-bearing structures [16,17]. The mechanical engagement of chromatin induces epigenetic and architectural changes, including post-translational modifications of histones [16]. The dynamic nature of this structural reorganization enables the nucleus to act as a mechanical sink and also to alter gene expression, providing both immediate resilience and long-term cellular adaptation to mechanical forces.

Another defense system that enhances cell resilience is associated with the actin cytoskeleton, which plays a crucial role in absorbing, generating, and redistributing forces to maintain nuclear and cellular integrity [18]. As a generator and buffer of mechanical force, the actin cytoskeleton itself experiences stress and must be actively reinforced [19]. This is achieved through diverse molecular pathways. For example, forces applied to the cell can activate the small GTPase RhoA, which promotes the assembly of stress fibers and myosin contractility [20,21]. Concurrently, forces alter the coupling of stress fibers with the extracellular matrix through focal adhesions by clustering integrins and unfolding mechanically gated cryptic domains within talin, which recruits vinculin, reinforcing the engagement between actin and integrins, and priming the actin cytoskeleton for mechanical challenges [22,23]. Additionally, mechanical forces couple stress fibers to the nucleus, further integrating cytoskeletal and nuclear mechanics [19]. From this perspective, the mechanisms safeguarding the cell’s integrity function as mechanical reinforcements: while intermediate filaments increase nuclear stiffness, the actin cytoskeleton acts as a primary integrator of mechanical forces, transmitting signals from adhesions to the nucleus while simultaneously strengthening under force. These force-driven adaptations enhance the load-bearing capacity of adhesions and the cytoskeleton, ultimately increasing the cell’s overall mechanical resilience (Figure 1a).

However, mechanical reinforcement is not always sufficient to cope with uncontrolled mechanical forces. In dynamically changing circumstances, particularly during rapid deformation cycles such as stretch-relax events, cells experience forces that can exceed a critical magnitude or rate. In response to these noxious forces, cells utilize an alternative mechanism, known as fluidization, which enables them to survive [24]. Unlike reinforcement, which strengthens force-bearing cellular structures to resist overload, fluidization, typically triggered by the release of preexisting tension, rapidly dissipates mechanical energy through the mass disassembly or severing of the actin cytoskeleton, followed by the assembly of a de novo cytoskeletal network [24,25]. From this lens, cell’s responses to forces can be viewed as an energy landscape, with mechanical reinforcement represented as distinct energy minima capable of tolerating forces proportional to their level of mechanical reinforcement. In contrast, fluidization occurs when the cell encounters an extreme, inescapable mechanical stimulus that surpasses its adaptive capacity, forcing it to shift into a more compliant state (Figure 1b).

While fluidization has traditionally been considered as the major mechanism associated with energy dissipation, recent findings suggest that cells can also dissipate mechanical tension through the modulation of formin-mediated actin polymerization [26]. Local mechanical activation of formins relieves tension by extending contractile structures, much like a fisherman letting out additional fishing line to prevent it from snapping under strain. Given the ubiquity of the actin cytoskeleton across biological scales and diverse organisms, these findings suggest a novel mechanism of rapid formin-mediated tension release as a conserved strategy that enables cells to dissipate mechanical force, preserve cytoskeletal integrity, and prevent force-induced damage at both cellular and tissue-wide levels [27]. Here, we review recent advances in the force- dependent regulation of actin cytoskeleton dynamics, exploring how mechanical cues modulate actin polymerization and contribute to cytoskeletal integrity. The mechanical regulation of the actin cytoskeleton - to cap or not to cap

Among cells, the primary functional unit of force generation is an ensemble of myosin motors, organized into myosin filaments, which pull on actin filaments. This actomyosin network transmits forces throughout the whole cell to actin-anchoring sites, such as cadherin cell—cell junctions, integrin cell—matrix adhesions, and cytoskeleton-nuclear links [19,30]. By acting at and near anchorage sites, these forces can initiate a diverse range of mechanosensitive cellular responses, among them, the activation of actin polymerizing factors [31,32].

The concept of mechanically regulated actin polymerization was first proposed by Kozlov and Bershadsky, who used mathematical modeling to suggest that formins alter their actin polymerization activity in response to mechanical inputs [33]. This concept has since been extended beyond the formin family and applied to the regulation of a wide range of force-generating actin structures. The simplest example of a mechanically controlled actin structure is an array of actin barbed ends polymerizing against the plasma membrane. The mechanical behaviour of such a structure is explained by the Brownian ratchet model: thermal fluctuations of actin filaments create transient gaps between the membrane and filament tips, allowing actin monomers to insert and thereby generate protrusive force. The same principle also governs actin filament capping, as capping protein requires a similarly sized transient opening to access barbed ends and terminate filament growth [34]. Therefore, filament elongation and capping operate as two coupled Brownian ratchets, one driving filament extension, the other limiting it, together forming a balanced and adaptive network under force. An increase in compression minimizes transient openings, excluding capping protein, leading to more free barbed ends and effectively increasing the density of the actin network. In conjunction with the reduced capping, the barbed ends sequester WH2 domains and reduce the ability of nucleation-promoting factors to activate Arp2/3, thereby suppressing new filament nucleation. This feedback, termed barbed end interference, highlights a broader principle: mechanical forces do reshape actin dynamics by balancing growth and capping, thereby governing higher-order network architecture.

As direct regulators of actin dynamics, formins are a family of multi-domain proteins that play an instrumental role in remodeling the actin cytoskeleton across a wide range of organisms, from unicellular yeast to multicellular plants and humans [35]. In addition to actin polymerization activity, the multi-domain composition of formins enables them to associate with a variety of actin structures, including filopodia [36], stress fibers [21], andtransmembrane actin-associated nuclear (TAN) lines [37], as well as non-actin-based structures, such as microtubules [38], mitochondria [39], and the nucleus [40]. However, the mechanosensitivity of formins has been primarily associated with their actin polymerization activity [26,33,41–49].

Formin-driven actin polymerization relies primarily on two structural domains: the Formin Homology 1 (FH1) and Formin Homology 2 (FH2) domains. The highly conserved FH2 domain, found in all formins, undergoes homotypic dimerization, forming a ring-like structure that binds to the barbed end of an actin filament [50]. In contrast, the FH1 domain facilitates interactions with a pool of profilin-bound G-actin through its profilin-actin binding site(s) [51,52]. The coordinated action of these two domains enables formins to recruit and deliver actin to the growing filament, thereby promoting actin polymerization. As a new actin monomer is added to the barbed end, the FH2 domain dissociates from the filament and translocates to the newly added monomer. This cyclic process of dissociation and reattachment enables the processive movement of the formin dimer with the growing barbed end. The dynamic coordination between the domains classifies formins as processive cappers [44].

The idea of processive capping gives the impression that any mechanical force pulling on the FH2 domain would weaken its interaction with the barbed end, thereby reducing its capping effect, and increasing the probability for G-actin to incorporate into the filament, thus enhancing formin’s actin polymerization activity. This hypothesis was first supported by computational modeling [33] and later confirmed by single-molecule experiments using tension applied through magnetic tweezers [45], optical traps [42,49], and microfluidics [42,43,47]. Notably, a seminal study from the Romet-Lemonne/Jégou group demonstrated that actin polymerization increases when the FH2 domain, anchored to the coverslip, is exposed to mechanical force while the FH1 remains unexposed. Pulling force applied to the FH2 dimer at the barbed end shifts the conformational equilibrium, favoring an open conformation [43]. Together, these data establish the FH2 domain as a critical mechanosensitive element of formins, converting mechanical force into actin polymerization (Figure 2a).

While the mechanical regulation of formins through the FH2 domain accounts for many experimental observations, such as the increased rate of actin polymerization under tension, the discovery of the negatively mechanosensitive formin Cdc12 suggests an alternative regulatory mechanism of force-dependent actin polymerization. Unlike many formins that enhance their activity under force, Cdc12 reduces its actin polymerization activity in response to force [46], an effect that is unlikely to be mediated by changes in the FH2 domain’s capping activity. Using protein engineering it was demonstrated that replacing the FH1 domain of Cdc12 with the mechanically insensitive FH1 domain of Dia2 yields a mechanically insensitive Cdc12 chimera [46]. These data demonstrate that the mechanosensitivity of formins is, at least in part, mediated by the FH1 domain, which regulates the delivery of G-actin to the barbed end through its interaction with profilin-bound actin. Although the exact mechanism of FH1 domain mechanosensing remains elusive, several studies suggest that mechanical tension may induce conformational changes or expose cryptic profilin binding sites, thereby altering the supply of actin monomers competent for polymerization [46,53].Indeed, computational simulation has predicted that extension of the disordered FH1 domain by tension reduces its conformation entropy, thereby increasing monomer capture efficiency [54]. Given that some formins possess up to thirty putative profilin-binding sites [35], this mechanism mediated by the FH1 domain could provide a graded response to mechanical tension, progressively upregulating actin polymerization in response to increasing force (Figure 2b).

Although much of our understanding of formin mechanical regulation has come from in vitro studies, recent work has demonstrated that force-dependent actin polymerization occurs in cells, playing a crucial role in maintaining cytoskeletal integrity. This process is largely orchestrated by the mechanosensitive formin Dia1, which governs actin polymerization at force-bearing focal adhesions [21,26]. Mechanical tension generated by the actomyosin cytoskeleton increases mDia1’s activity, thus increasing actin polymerization precisely at sites under mechanical load. When contractility is reduced, such as through the suppression of myosin, actin polymerization is reduced to the levels observed in Dia1 knockdown cells (Figure 2C). Similar formin- and myosin-dependent actin incorporation, albeit not confined to focal adhesions but also observed along stress fibers, was recently reported by Nishimura et al. [55]. Such discrepancy is likely attributed to the difference in the imaging setup for actin photoconversion — while photoconversion by wide field fluorescent microscopy allows actin diffusion across the cell and unbiased incorporation in actin structures, photoconversion through TIRF illumination favors labeling of the most membrane proximal structures, including focal adhesions. Interestingly, this study showed that under externally applied force, actin incorporation into stress fibers becomes formin-independent and is likely mediated by zyxin-VASP complexes recruited to stress fiber strain sites [11,56,57].

Despite its intrinsic capacity for rapid actin elongation [58], Dia1’s activity at cellular context is reduced likely due to a competition for profilin—actin with other actin nucleators (e.g., other formins, ARP2/3, ENA/WASP), actin-sequestering cofactors (e.g., thymosin), and spatial constraints, highlighting the complex, highly regulated nature of actin assembly [59,60]. Moreover, the rate of Dia1-mediated actin polymerization varies significantly across the cell, and even focal adhesions in proximity exhibit vastly different polymerization rates, suggesting that focal adhesions act as autonomous mechanosensitive units. Together, this demonstrates that actin polymerization at focal adhesions is likely governed locally by mechanical force and largely independently of cell-wide biochemical signaling.

Balancing act: actin polymerization controls tension on the cytoskeleton and facilitates stress fiber repair

Precise spatiotemporal regulation of actin polymerization allows the assembly of actin networks with diverse mechanochemical properties [61,62], but how does force-dependent activation of Dia1 at focal adhesions affect cytoskeletal mechanics? Previous studies have shown that Dia1 depletion significantly reduces the number of force-generating stress fibers per cell [21]. Despite this pronounced effect on actin organization, Dia1 knockdown causes only a moderate decrease in the phosphorylation state of myosin light chain [63], suggesting that contractile forces generated by the cytoskeleton of Dia1 depleted cells are transmitted through a limited number of stress fibers, thereby increasing tension on individual fibers. Laser nano-surgery experiments and FRET imaging confirmed this, demonstrating that suppression of Dia1 elevates prestresses on stress fibers and increases focal adhesion tension [26]. These findings provide experimental evidence supporting mathematical modeling predictions [56], that force-sensitive actin polymerization dynamically buffers mechanical tension on the actin cytoskeleton.

Mechanical forces are a potent regulator of cytoskeletal organization, but excessive forces can compromise the integrity of the actin cytoskeleton [11,64]. The critical importance of cytoskeleton integrity is evident from the fact that cells harbor mechanisms to repair damaged actin structures. The LIM domain containing protein zyxin plays a central role in this repair process. Upon mechanical strain, zyxin is recruited to the damaged sites of stress fibers, facilitating repair by recruiting the actin nucleating factor VASP and the cross-linker α-actinin [65]. Consistent with the increased tension observed upon Dia1 suppression, cells depleted for Dia1 exhibit a two-fold increase in the frequency of zyxin recruitment to stress fiber strain site, indicating heightened mechanical stress and structural damage (Figure 3) [26]. Supporting this, recent findings have shown that cells treated with pan-formin inhibitor SMIFH2 exhibit a significant increase in actin incorporation along the length of stress fibers, suggesting the formation of multiple barbed ends likely due to stress fiber damage [55]. Consistent with this, inhibiting myosin contractility markedly decreases actin incorporation in formin-inhibited cells, suggesting the mechanical nature of stress fiber damage [55]. These data support the idea that force-dependent actin polymerization mediated by Dia1 may act as a safety valve dynamically releasing excessive tension on stress fibers.

Besides protecting stress fibers from mechanical damage, Dia1 is likely to collaborate with zyxin in facilitating the repair of stress fibers. Depletion of Dia1 has been shown to suppress zyxin-mediated stress fiber repair [26]. Although Dia1 has never been detected in the stress fiber strain sites and is unlikely to mediate actin polymerization at damaged sites directly, it likely supports the repair system from a distance. Given its role in regulating cytoskeletal tension, Dia1 may not only prevent the formation of strained sites but also extend their lifetime, allowing for efficient zyxin recruitment. Indeed, modelling predicted that mechanical tension on actin filaments induces cracks that serve as binding sites for zyxin [66]. The stability of these cracks is highly sensitive to mechanical load, with greater tension dramatically shortening their lifespan. By alleviating tension, Dia1 could stabilize these cracks, enabling the recruitment of zyxin and facilitating the repair of actin filaments. In the absence of Dia1, crack lifetime can be too short for efficient repair. Similarly, Dia1-mediated tension release could facilitate the formation of zyxin bridges that link damaged actin filaments [65]. These findings position Dia1 not only as a safety valve that regulates mechanical tension but also as a critical component of the cytoskeleton repair machinery, essential for effective zyxin-mediated repair.

Shifting mechanical demand: implications of Dia1 safety valve in mechanotransduction and cell resilience

The organization of the actin cytoskeleton, governed by tightly regulated polymerization, plays a pivotal role in how cells interpret and respond to mechanical cues. Mechanical forces originating from the extracellular environment or generated internally are transmitted across the cytoskeleton, where they are converted into biochemical signals in a process known as mechanotransduction. These signals can influence gene expression and guide cell fate decisions. In turn, the cytoskeleton reorganizes in response to these signals, modulating intracellular tension and reinforcing structural integrity to adapt to ongoing mechanical stress [67]. Within this framework, the Dia1 mechanical safety valve helps preserve cytoskeletal homeostasis by sustaining the integrity of the actin cytoskeleton under load and facilitating stress fiber repair. Thus, disruption of Dia1 impairs the buffering capacity, resulting in reduced stress fiber abundance [26,62,68]. In cells lacking a functional Dia1-mediated safety valve, a reduction in stress fibers weakens the mechanical coupling between the cytoskeleton and the extracellular matrix, as evidenced by a loss of focal adhesions [21,26]. This breakdown in adhesion not only attenuates integrin-dependent signaling pathways, such as focal adhesion kinase activity [63], but may also impair microtubule-dependent signaling by reducing the interaction between microtubules and focal adhesions [69,70]. Reduced microtubule targeting to adhesions alters the recruitment of the Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factor GEF-H1 to microtubules, leading to elevated RhoA activation [71]. The upregulation of RhoA likely increases stress fiber contractility, establishing a positive feedback loop that exacerbates cytoskeletal damage and signal distortion. These cellular maladies appear to culminate in tissue-scale defects; indeed, Dia1 depletion has been shown to perturb adherens and tight junctions in epithelial cells [72,73], suggesting that its regulatory role extends beyond controlling stress fiber tension at focal adhesions and encompasses a broad range of morphogenic events.

A decrease in stress fiber content, observed in cells with impaired Dia1 activity, can also shift the balance between filamentous and globular actin, which in turn regulates mechanosensitive gene expression. In particular, myocardin-related transcription factors (MRTFs), which bind to G-actin in the cytoplasm, are sequestered and prevented from translocating to the nucleus when G-actin levels are elevated [74]. Thus, impaired Dia1 activity is expected to enhance MRTF sequestration in the cytoplasm and suppress their transcriptional activity, disrupting a broad spectrum of mechanically sensitive differentiation programs. Supporting this, Dia1 suppression has been shown to inhibit smooth muscle and myofibroblast differentiation by sequestering MRTFs in the cytoplasm, thereby downregulating MRTF-dependent gene expression [75]. These findings highlight a broader role for Dia1 in integrating mechanical signals into cell fate decisions by controlling of actin polymerization.

An alternative mechanism by which the Dia1 safety valve regulates cell behaviour is through its control of cytoskeletal tension and its downstream impact on nuclear mechanotransduction (Figure 3). Mechanical forces generated by actomyosin contractility are transmitted across the cytoskeleton to the nucleus via the LINC (Linker of Nucleoskeleton and Cytoskeleton) complex, which couples the perinuclear actin cytoskeleton to the nuclear lamina. This mechanical linkage enables cytoskeletal forces to deform the nucleus, influencing chromatin architecture, histone modification patterns, and the spatial organization of transcriptionally active or repressive regions. Additionally, nuclear pore complexes (NPCs) are sensitive to mechanical stress, and increased nuclear tension can enhance the transport efficiency of transcription factors such as YAP/TAZ and MRTF-A [76]. In Dia1-depleted cells, where stress fibers exhibit elevated baseline tension due to defective actin polymerization and repair, these excessive forces are predicted to exaggerate these nuclear responses, mimicking cellular exposure to a stiff extracellular matrix or external mechanical input. While the precise molecular cascade linking Dia1 to nuclear mechanosensing remains unclear, recent evidence indicates that loss of Dia1 in invasive cancer cells compromises nuclear envelope integrity, leading to aberrant nuclear deformation, increased double-strand DNA breaks, and defects in nucleocytoplasmic trafficking of regulatory proteins [77]. These findings suggest that Dia1 acts not only as a cytoskeletal tension buffer but also as a gatekeeper of nuclear homeostasis under mechanical stress. Together, these findings suggest that the Dia1-mediated safety valve is critical for preserving mechanotransduction and sustaining cytoskeletal homeostasis through coordinated crosstalk between different cytoskeleton networks, adhesion complexes, and the nucleus, ultimately supporting tissue mechanics and morphogenesis.

Reframing the actin cytoskeleton: implications from adaptations to disease

The Dia1-mediated safety valve not only buffers mechanical stress but also integrates with other mechanosensitive pathways, ensuring cell mechanical stability through coordinated force dissipation and cytoskeletal remodeling. Conceptualizing the cell as an energy landscape, where each mechanosensitive pathway represents a distinct energy minimum, highlights how mechanical activation of Dia1 patterns these minima, i. e., the range of cytoskeletal tension at which each pathway operates, allowing cells to adapt seamlessly to a broad spectrum of mechanical demands.

Given the well-established role of mechanosensitive processes in regulating key cellular functions, dysregulated mechanical activation of Dia1 is likely to drive pathological outcomes. For example, while fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation depends on mechanical forces, excessive fibroblast activation can lead to fibrotic pathology instead of healthy tissue repair [78]. In this context, the mechano-safety valve may not only protect myofibroblasts from aberrant force accumulation but also sustain their hypertensive states, a feature that drives fibrosis. Similarly, it remains to be determined, whether the mechanical control of actin polymerization mediated by Dia1 contributes to cancer invasion by altering the activity of signaling pathways, e.g., Rac1, which is not directly considered mechanosensitive but is known to regulate the mechano-responsiveness of migrating cells [79].

A comprehensive investigation of the role of mechanically controlled actin polymerization requires the field to develop a mechanically insensitive Dia1 variant. Beyond serving as a valuable tool for studying the mechanics of actin polymerization, such a variant would provide critical insights into the fundamental mechanisms governing the mechanical regulation of protein activity. Moving forward, this tool will advance our understanding of the actin cytoskeleton as an active mechanosensitive system that determines where and how mechanical forces are distributed, ultimately shaping cellular behavior, mechano-adaptation, and disease probabilities.
