WaSTE: a multicentre audit of waste segregation practices in elective knee and hip arthroplasty
Authors: Rohan Prakash, Alan J Hilley, William Fishley, Ahmed A H H Nasser, WaSTE Study Collaborative, Deborah M Eastwood, Mike R Reed, Yuvraj Agrawal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsArthroplasty generates the most waste among the sub-specialties of orthopaedic surgery. Infectious waste generates ten times more CO2 than recycled waste, and over 90% of it is misallocated. This multicentre study aimed to quantify waste from primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) and total hip arthroplasty (THA) across UK NHS trusts, guiding strategies for waste reduction and improved segregation.MethodsData were collected prospectively from ten UK centres over six months between October 2023 and April 2024, covering at least ten arthroplasty cases per centre. Waste was categorized and weighed across six streams (infectious, offensive, recycling, general, sharps, and linen). Data on scrubbed personnel, drapes, implant brand, and intraoperative complications were also collected.ResultsData from 222 procedures (92 THAs, 130 TKAs) revealed significant differences in total waste production across centres (p < 0.001). THAs produced a mean of 14.8 kg waste, while TKAs generated 13.6 kg. Only 5/10 trusts utilized the offensive waste stream. Implant brand, number of scrubbed personnel, and reusable drapes were predictors of increased waste production on unadjusted univariate analysis; multivariable linear regression analysis showed local centre practice was the only significant predictor of waste production.ConclusionLocal waste management practices were the strongest predictors of waste production, underscoring need for standardization, education, and adherence to best practices. The use of offensive waste streams eliminated infectious waste, supporting a shift towards less carbon-intensive disposal. Adherence to established national guidelines is required to reduce the quantity of infectious waste and reduce the carbon footprint of arthroplasty surgery. Implementing offensive waste streams can reduce carbon intensity significantly. Addressing centre-specific policies, adhering to national guidance, and industry collaboration are essential for minimizing arthroplasty waste.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(4):499–506.Keywords: Waste, Sustainability, Hip, Knee, Arthroplasty, hip and knee arthroplasty, knee, Total knee arthroplasties (TKAs), total hip arthroplasties (THAs), arthroplasty and surgery, carbon, orthopaedic surgery, univariate analyses, intraoperative complications, linear regression analysis

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasties (TKAs) and total hip arthroplasties (THAs) are among the most frequently performed procedures in orthopaedic surgery, with over 225,000 cases annually in the UK, contributing significantly to healthcare waste production.1,2 With the NHS aiming to be net zero by 2045, the need to reduce the carbon footprint of orthopaedic surgery is pressing, and arthroplasty has been identified as the subspecialty that generates the largest quantity of waste.3 It has previously been estimated that knee and hip arthroplasty cases generate in excess of 3 million kg of waste annually in the UK alone, with approximately 70% of this being discarded as infectious waste.2,4 Previous work has highlighted that up to 90% of infectious waste is incorrectly segregated and disposal of infectious waste is known to generate up to ten times more carbon dioxide than general waste disposal with significantly increased costs for the hospital.5-8 Our pilot data showed that simple educational interventions can significantly reduce the infectious waste generated by 3.52 kg for TKA cases, and 2.09 kg in THA cases, which, if extrapolated nationally in the UK, would equate to between 70 and 132 gasoline/petrol-powered passenger vehicles driven for a year.4,9

In order to effect large-scale change on a national level, it is necessary to understand the different waste management practices across multiple trusts to more effectively target practices to reduce the carbon footprint and waste generation from TKA and THA cases. Our multicentre study aims to quantify waste generation in TKA and THA across multiple trusts in the UK. We hypothesized that there may be significant heterogeneity in waste management practices between trusts and thus CO2 production. We aimed to identify predictors of increased waste production, including assessing the effect of different implants, reusable compared with disposable drapes, and number of team members scrubbed. This information will be valuable in providing recommendations nationally to reduce the environmental impact of arthroplasty.

Methods

This was a prospective, multicentre audit conducted across ten UK NHS trusts between October 2023 and April 2024. The study was supported by the National Joint Registry (NJR)10 and British Orthopaedic Association (BOA),11 along with Northern and Midlands trainee collaboratives, CORNET12 and BON,13 utilizing existing trainee networks to promote the study and recruit centres. Upon registration, an orthopaedic trainee (or an equivalent-level doctor) was designated as the local lead, with an optional second collaborator appointed to assist in data collection. A named consultant supervisor was required at every site. The study was registered as a service evaluation in each centre, and all collaborators were responsible for complying with local trust protocols and information governance procedures for data handling.

A protocol was sent to each centre, along with a standardized digital weighing device (TOWEAR Portable Digital Weight Scale). Data were collected using a standardized Excel 2010 (Microsoft, USA) spreadsheet. All data entered into the database were anonymized and contained no patient identifiable information.

Each team was responsible for identifying appropriate cases and each centre was required to contribute data for both primary TKA and THA cases. Exclusion criteria included complex primary procedures (requiring specialized implants), revisions, robotic or unicompartmental arthroplasties, and trauma cases.

Waste was categorized into general, infectious, offensive, recycling, sharps, and linens, and was weighed at the end of each case. Waste from the anaesthetic room and anatomical waste were excluded. Additional data on scrubbed personnel, drapes and gowns (reusable or single-use), implant brand, use of cement, and intraoperative complications were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using mean (SD) where normally distributed and median (IQR) where skewed. Categorical data were presented as counts and percentages. The distribution of total waste was assessed which demonstrated non-normality for both THA and TKA procedures. Outliers were winsorised using 1.5× IQR limits to reduce undue influence while preserving sample size. Differences between hospitals and between subgroups were analyzed using Kruskal-Wallis or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables and presented with p-values.

For each procedure type (THA and TKA), the primary outcome was total waste weight (kg). Unadjusted univariate linear regression was undertaken for each candidate predictor. Variables with p < 0.25 on univariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model.

For regression modelling, after log-transformation, total waste for THA and TKA approximated normality. All prespecified covariates were included: hospital site, implant type, implant brand, and scrubbed team size. Number of scrubbed personnel was modelled as a binary variable (three vs ≥ four scrubbed). Categorical predictors with > two levels were dummy-coded, with reference categories selected a priori based on frequency. Small brand groups were combined into an ‘Other’ category (Brand 5) for descriptive analysis; sparse categories causing perfect collinearity (tolerance = 0.000) were excluded during regression. Model fit was assessed using R² and adjusted R². standardized (β) and unstandardized (B) coefficients with 95% CIs were reported. All analyses were conducted using SPSS v. 28 (IBM, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

There was variation between centres regarding the use of different waste streams, with only 8/10 centres utilizing recycling streams, 5/10 centres using offensive waste at the start of the study (with one centre switching during the study), and 5/10 centres initially using infectious waste, later reducing to 4/10.

THA model: After adjustment, hospital site was the primary independent predictor of log-transformed waste. Using Hospital 4 as the reference category, Hospitals 5, 8, and 9 generated significantly less waste (B = -0.258,–0.241, and -0.433, respectively; p = 0.014, p = 0.006, and p < 0.001, respectively). These sites also showed the largest standardized effects indicating significant influence on reductions in waste relative to the reference hospital. Other hospitals did not differ significantly from Hospital 4.

Implant brand contributed only modestly to the adjusted model. Brand 3 was associated with increased waste compared with the reference brand 1 (B = 0.275, p = 0.044; β = 0.274), whereas other brands were not significant. Implant type and number of scrubbed personnel were not independently associated with increased waste after adjustment.

TKA model: Hospital site was the major determinant of waste production. Taking Hospital 5 as the reference, Hospitals 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, and 9 produced significantly greater waste (B = 4.13, 5.39, 1.71, 7.06, 8.87, and 3.17, respectively; all p ≤ 0.017). Hospitals 1, 8, and 10 did not differ significantly from the reference.

After adjusting for hospital, neither implant brand nor scrubbed team size was independently associated with total waste. The coefficients for implant brands (Brand 2, Brand 4, Brand 5) and Scrubbed 3 were small and non-significant (all p > 0.45; β ≤ 0.14). This suggests that the apparent brand and scrubbed effects seen in univariate analyses were largely explained by hospital practice.

The models adjusted R2s (THA R2 = 0.72, and TKA R2 = 0.55) explain approximately 72% and 55% of the variance. Model residuals were approximately normally distributed and showed no concerning patterns on residual-versus-fitted plots. All included variables showed acceptable multicollinearity (VIF 1.21 to 8.92). Variables with VIF > 5 warranted further exploration and were included where clinically appropriate. Only Brand 1 was excluded from both final models because they occurred exclusively in single hospitals, resulting in perfect collinearity with hospital site. No intraoperative complications were recorded.

Discussion

The burden of misallocated waste

One of the most striking findings of this study was the variation in waste management practices across NHS trusts. Despite national waste management guidelines,14 significant differences exist in how waste is being segregated and disposed of. The wide variation in infectious waste generation between trusts support previous findings that incorrect classification of non-infectious waste as infectious waste is a widespread issue, leading to unnecessary incineration and increased CO₂ emissions.15

Infectious waste disposal may generate up to five times more CO₂ than offensive waste disposal due to the requirement for high temperature incineration of infectious waste.7 Yet, our study found that only half of the participating NHS trusts used an offensive waste stream (tiger-stripe bags), suggesting that many hospitals continue to incinerate non-infectious waste unnecessarily. This is most likely due to misconceptions regarding which waste stream should be used for waste generated in theatre. According to national guidelines, the infectious waste stream (orange bags in NHS hospitals) should only be used for cases where there is concern for infectious agents, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), COVID-19, or carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE), otherwise the offensive waste stream (tiger-stripe bags) and general waste should be used.16 For items including gowns, gloves, and drapes that are contaminated with patient bodily fluids which are not infectious, guidance suggests utilizing the offensive waste stream. For patients considered to be possibly infectious, the infectious waste stream would be appropriate, however, this makes up a very small proportion of patients undergoing planned elective orthopaedic surgery. Implementing standardized offensive waste policies could significantly reduce carbon intensity and cut disposal costs. In our study half the trusts used offensive rather than infectious waste streams. Extrapolating this nationally using the latest NJR figures, assuming that only half of the trusts in the UK are utilizing the offensive waste streams, if the remaining trusts were to switch from the infectious waste stream the potential reduction in CO2 production could be equivalent to 900 economy flights from London to New York each year (approximately 880 tonnes of CO2).1,7,17

The role of implant packaging and industry collaboration

Not only did infectious waste generation differ between trusts, but the total waste produced varied widely. Multivariate regression analysis highlighted that the choice of brand was not a significant contributing factor. Reducing total waste production is a priority in waste management, and a drive from industry is necessary to reduce bulky packaging and reduce individual plastic wrapping where possible.18

An analysis of waste items following hip and knee arthroplasty cases highlighted that the vast majority of items are not labelled as recyclable; incentivization for suppliers to use biodegradable or recyclable packaging is an important step towards reducing the carbon footprint from arthroplasty procedures.2 Streamlining the instrument trays can also reduce the impact of additional sterilization and transport requirements, and has already proven to be effective in other sub-specialties to reduce cost and the environmental impact of surgery.19,20 Our findings highlight the need for dialogue between healthcare providers and industry partners to reduce unnecessary packaging waste in arthroplasty.

Optimizing team-size to reduce waste

The number of scrubbed personnel for both TKA and THA procedures were not found to be independent predictors of increased waste. Larger surgical teams will inevitably result in higher disposable PPE usage leading to greater waste generation. As the need to reduce the environmental impact of surgery becomes ever greater, careful consideration should be given to which team members are required to scrub for a case, and which members could participate or observe unscrubbed although the training of resident surgeons must remain a high priority and our data highlights that the most important factor in optimizing waste management is local hospital policy, rather than the number of personnel scrubbed.

Re-usable drapes

Given that reusable drapes are laundered and reused, it is important to note they should not be treated as waste in the same way as other streams. When linen laundry was discounted, waste produced with the use of reusable drapes was significantly reduced. Multiple studies have demonstrated the potential benefits of reusable drapes in reducing CO2 emissions and therefore the effect of reusable drapes on total waste should not be considered a reason to favour single-use drapes per se.21,22

Cost

While waste disposal costs vary depending on individual and local arrangements, based on our data and approximate figures from Royal Orthopaedic Hospital NHS Foundation Trust, switching from infectious to offensive waste streams would save approximately £1.38 per THA case and £1.22 per TKA case.

Our study did not assess waste generated from anaesthesia, which is an important area for future study. There was considerable variation in the numbers contributed by each centre, and a greater number of centres with greater overall numbers would make the findings more generalizable. Case selection within each centre was not conducted using randomization or consecutive cases, and thus a risk for selection bias exists.

In conclusion, our data suggest that in the UK, TKA and THA generate significant amounts of waste, approximately 13 kg to 15 kg per case on average. There is huge variation in waste management practices across different trusts, and local hospital policies on waste segregation are a major determinant of waste misallocation. Using the offensive waste stream (tiger-stripe bags) could eliminate all unnecessary infectious waste. In our study only half of the centres were utilizing the offensive waste stream (despite this being national guidance), which should give reassurance to remaining centres of the feasibility of implementing this waste stream in theatres. Eight out of ten trusts recycled waste in our study; industry collaboration is essential to reducing packaging waste and using more recyclable packaging materials. Through educational interventions and standardizing waste management policies, NHS trusts can substantially reduce the environmental impact of arthroplasty and surgery as a whole, while maintaining efficiency and patient safety, and reducing costs in the process.

Take home message

- Adherence to national guidelines is required to reduce the quantity of infectious waste and thus reduce the carbon footprint of arthroplasty surgery.
