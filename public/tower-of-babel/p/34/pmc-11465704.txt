Trend analysis, gender-specific patterns, and age dynamics of childhood and adolescent obesity: insights from the Tehran lipid and glucose study
Authors: Behnaz Abiri, Amirhossein Ramezani Ahmadi, Ali Valizadeh, Mojtaba Akbari, Farhad Hosseinpanah, Mohammad Nikoohemmat, Majid Valizadeh, Mohammadreza Vafa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood and adolescent obesity pose significant challenges to global health, with escalating prevalence and associated short- and long-term health consequences. This longitudinal study leveraged data from the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS) to investigate the trends of obesity among Tehranian children and adolescents over a 21-year period.MethodsUtilizing data from TLGS phases I to VII (1999–2021), we included 3845 participants aged 3–18 years at the beginning of phase I. Anthropometric measures, including height and weight, were collected, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated. Childhood obesity (2–19 years) was defined as BMI-for-age > 2SD based on World Health Organization (WHO) standards, and adult obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 30. Descriptive statistics, trend analysis, prevalence calculations, odds ratios, and interaction analyses were employed for data interpretation.ResultsThe mean BMI increased from 18.46 ± 4.37 kg/m² at Phase I to 26.36 ± 5.03 kg/m² at Phase VII. Boys exhibited a greater increase in BMI than girls, and age at study entry influenced BMI trajectories. The prevalence of obesity rose from 6.4% at Phase I to 21.5% at Phase VII, with a more pronounced increase in boys. Odds ratios for obesity steadily increased across phases, indicating a growing risk. Interaction analyses revealed age-specific dynamics, with older participants demonstrating lower odds ratios initially but higher odds ratios in later phases.ConclusionOur study shows a worrisome increase in childhood obesity among Tehranian children and adolescents over 21 years. Sex- and age-specific trends emphasize the necessity for targeted interventions, informing policymakers, healthcare practitioners, and educators regarding public health strategies and interventions against the obesity epidemic.Keywords: Childhood obesity, Tehran lipid and glucose study, Longitudinal study, Prevalence, Trends

Introduction

Despite being a concern for several decades, the problem of excess weight in childhood and adolescence continues to be a crucial issue in global health [1, 2]. In 2016, estimates indicated that more than 40 million children under the age of 5 years and more than 330 million children and adolescents aged 5–19 years were overweight or obese [3].

Childhood overweight or obesity affects individuals in both the short-and long-term. In the short term, it is associated with psychological challenges, including depression and anxiety, as well as physical complications, such as asthma, inflammation, liver problems, and musculoskeletal issues.

Metabolically, there is an increased risk of cardiovascular issues, including high blood pressure, dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes [4]. The long-term consequences of childhood obesity include an elevated risk of cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cancers, and musculoskeletal disorders in adulthood, potentially resulting in disability and premature death [4]. Treating obesity in adulthood poses a significant challenge, as evidence suggests that approximately three-quarters of overweight or obese children continue to maintain this condition into adulthood [4]. The enduring overweight status and the limited effectiveness of existing treatments underscore the importance of preventing overweight and obesity from the earliest stages of life.

In Iran, as in numerous other nations, the increasing occurrence of obesity among young individuals is a matter of concern. Research conducted in Iran indicates an increase in the prevalence of overweight and obesity over the past decade, escalating from 4.8% and 10.1–6% and 14%, respectively [5, 6]. Additionally, a systematic review and meta-analysis highlighted an increasing trend of excess weight among young Iranian children [7]. However, these reports lack longitudinal data to monitor obesity trends in specific populations over time. After 6.6 years of follow-up in the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (phases 1–3), childhood obesity showed a rising trend [8]. Over a decade, this trend persisted, especially among younger children [9].

In this longitudinal investigation, utilizing updated data spanning phases I to VII of the TLGS, our objective was to assess the prevalence and trend of obesity over 21 years in a cohort of children and adolescents from Tehran. By analyzing TLGS data, our aim is to offer valuable insights into urban childhood obesity in Tehran, considering its status as the largest city in Iran and the observed high prevalence of obesity. This study will inform policymakers, healthcare practitioners, and educators regarding interventions against the obesity epidemic.

Methods and materials

Study population

This longitudinal, population-based study utilized data from the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS), a cohort study designed to assess the prevalence of non-communicable disease risk factors and their outcomes among the urban population of Tehran. In the baseline measurements, 15,005 individuals aged ≥ 3 years were selected through multistage cluster random sampling methods during phase I (1999–2001). Subsequent assessments occurred at approximately 3-year intervals in various phases: phase II (2002–2005), phase III (2006–2008), phase IV (2009–2011), phase V (2012–2015), phase VI (2016–2019), and phase VII (2020–2022). Information regarding this study has been published previously [10]. For this study, 3,845 individuals aged 3–18 years at the commencement of phase I, who were measured at least once again during phases II–VII, were selected.

Participants were selected based on rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria that considered various factors, including health status. The inclusion criteria encompassed individuals aged 3–18 years at the commencement of Phase I, who were measured at least once again during Phases II–VII. We performed a comprehensive assessment of participant information to gain a thorough understanding of the participants’ overall health and well-being. Moreover, we emphasized the inclusion of individuals in good health and excluded those with any pre-existing health conditions. This exclusion was necessary to ensure that the observed trends in obesity prevalence were not influenced by underlying health issues that could disproportionately affect obesity rates. By excluding those with pre-existing health conditions, we aimed to provide a more accurate depiction of the trend in obesity prevalence over time. However, participants were excluded from the study if they had incomplete or missing data. The final analysis comprised 3,845 participants. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Research Institute for Endocrine Sciences (RIES) at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (code IR.SBMU.ENDOCRINE.REC.1402.040). All participants provided written informed consent, with parental or legal guardian consent obtained for participants under the age of 18, prior to participating in the study.

Measurements and definitions

Experienced interviewers utilized pre-tested questionnaires to gather demographic information.

Trained personnel also performed the anthropometric assessments. Participants’ weight was measured with minimal clothing and without shoes using a digital electronic weighing scale (Seca 707, range 0·1–150 kg; Hanover, MD, USA), recorded to the nearest 100 g. Height was measured in the standing position using a tape meter following standard protocols. BMI was computed by dividing the weight in kilograms by the square of the height in meters (kg/m²). Childhood obesity (2–19 year) and sever obesity for each sex was defined as BMI-for-age > 2SD and > 3SD, respectively, based on the standard tables of the World Health Organization (WHO) [11], and adult obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 30.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means (SD), while categorical covariates were expressed as frequencies (%). Interaction tests were conducted to assess the time trends in obesity prevalence, with analyses stratified by age and gender subgroups. The prevalence of obesity was considered as dependent variable, the GEE (Generalized Estimating Equation) model with auto-regressive working correlation structure, through log-link function with binomial errors, was used to investigate marginal means and P-values and estimate the OR of responses and 95% confidence intervals. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS for Windows, ver. 27.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA); the significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Figure 2 illustrates the trend of BMI over a 20-year follow-up in the total population and subgroups. The mean BMI was 18.46 ± 4.37 kg/m2 at Phase I and 26.36 ± 5.03 kg/m2 at Phase VII. It was observed that boys experienced a greater increase in mean BMI from Phase I to Phase VII compared to girls (boys: from 18.38 ± 4.26 to 27.25 ± 5.01; girls: from 18.53 ± 4.47 to 25.48 ± 4.90). Regarding age groups, the difference in BMI between those who entered at age ≤ 11 years and those who entered at age > 11 years decreased over time (age ≤ 11 years: from 18.46 ± 4.37 to 26.36 ± 5.03; age > 11 years: from 16.25 ± 3.38 to 25.76 ± 5.05).

Regarding the interaction between time and age group, those aged > 11 years had a lower OR for obesity in Phases I to VI and a higher OR for obesity in Phases V to VII compared to those aged ≤ 11 years (P-interaction < 0.001). Although girls had a lower OR for obesity compared to boys over the seven phases of the study, the interaction of obesity, time, and sex was not statistically significant (P-interaction = 0.198).

Discussion

Our longitudinal study, spanning 21 years and utilizing data from phases I to VII of the TLGS, sheds light on the prevalence and trends of childhood obesity among Tehranian children and adolescents. The observed increase in obesity prevalence from 6.4% at Phase I to 21.5% at Phase VII underscores the urgency of addressing this public health concern. Gender differences in the rise of mean BMI suggest potential gender-specific factors influencing weight trajectories, emphasizing the need for nuanced interventions. While boys exhibited a higher prevalence of obesity, the interaction analysis suggests multifaceted dynamics requiring further investigation. Age at entry into the study cohort played a significant role, emphasizing the need for age-specific interventions.

In previous analyses of TLGS, after 6.6 years of follow-up (phases 1–3), childhood obesity showed a rising trend [8]. Over a decade, this trend persisted (total prevalence of obesity in children increased from 5.5 to 9.4% from phase I to IV), especially among younger children [9].

Over the past four decades, there has been a global increase in obesity among individuals of all age groups, which is similar to the trend observed in adults [12]. However, there seems to be a more accelerated increase in obesity rates among individuals aged 5–19 years compared to younger children, experiencing an eight-fold increase between 1975 and 2016. This is in contrast to an approximately two-fold increase in obesity rates among children aged 2–4 years between 1980 and 2015, although the metrics used are not directly comparable. Variability exists in both the levels and trends of obesity prevalence among regions and countries, which is influenced by the stage of the global obesity epidemic. Specifically, there has been a notable leveling off of trends, particularly among individuals with a high socioeconomic status in high-income countries [13].

The most recent assessments of overweight trends among children under the age of 5 years were collaboratively released by UNICEF, WHO, and the World Bank in April 2019 [14]. Globally, the prevalence of overweight has witnessed a modest increase, increasing from 4.8% in 1990 to 5.9% in 2018. However, estimates for United Nations regions characterized by low- and middle-income status exhibited diverse and heterogeneous trends. Estimates for high-income regions have not been published. Additionally, the NCD-RisC maintains the most extensive global database on obesity among children and adolescents aged 5–19 years [15]. The latest estimates, released in 2017, were derived from 2416 measured data sources [12]. These findings demonstrate that from 1975 to 2016, the prevalence of obesity increased from 0.7 to 5.6% in girls and from 0.9 to 7.8% in boys. However, the global increase in obesity has masked the heterogeneous trends at the national level.

The trend in childhood obesity has leveled off in developed countries in Europe and the United States [16–20]. In the United States, analyses of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data, utilizing cross-sectional data from a representative sample of children and adolescents, revealed an escalating trend in obesity prevalence. Specifically, from 1971 to 1974 to 1988–1994, there was an increase in the prevalence of obesity in youth aged 6–11 and 12–19 years (from 4.0% and 6.1–11.3% and 10.5%, respectively). This trend persisted until 1999–2000, with prevalence of 15.3% and 15.5% [21]. The overall prevalence of youth obesity was 16.3% in the period 2003–2006 [22] and increased to 17.0% in 2013–2014 [23]. Unlike our findings, in the USA, the overall prevalence of obesity was similar between girls and boys among youth aged 2–19 years. The differences between our findings and those of the USA can be attributed to variations in sample populations, including differences in socioeconomic background, utilization of preventive strategies, and the diversity of ethnicity in the NHANES study.

Consistent with our results, the prevalence of overweight among older children has experienced a substantial increase in Brazil (from 1975 to 1997) and China (from 1991 to 1997) based on nationally representative data. This shift reflects a change in the burden of nutritional issues from energy imbalance deficiency to excess weight gain [24]. Furthermore, these results are corroborated by recent studies that indicate an upward trend in childhood obesity in China [25] and India [26]. In Asia, the prevalence of overweight/obesity among Japanese schoolchildren declined from 2003 to 2012 [27]. In Korea, the prevalence of childhood overweight and obesity has remained relatively stable from 2001 to 2012 in both boys and girls [28].

The observed gender difference in our study, with boys exhibiting a higher prevalence of obesity, may be attributed to genetic, environmental, and dietary factors [29]. These findings emphasize the need for tailored interventions addressing gender-specific dynamics.

Research conducted on children and adolescents suggests that individuals with very high body mass indices (based on various definitions) are at an increased risk of elevated blood pressure, abnormal glucose levels, and abnormal lipids [30–32]. Furthermore, studies have indicated low health-related quality-of-life levels in children and adolescents with extreme obesity [33], and instances of weight-based victimization in children and adolescents with obesity [34]. Therefore, the ongoing monitoring and adaptation of strategies are essential to address the evolving patterns observed throughout the study period.

A notable strength of our study is that it is the first in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region to investigate the trend of obesity among children and adolescents using a longitudinal cohort study design, with 21 years of follow-up. In terms of anthropometric data, we employed trained personnel for data collection, as opposed to relying on self-reported measures, which could potentially lead to underestimation of obesity prevalence. Additionally, we employed GEE analysis for statistical modeling, representing an advanced method for handling missing data. However, limitations include reliance on BMI as the sole indicator, a single-center focus limiting generalizability, potential confounding factors not fully addressed, the lack of comprehensive sociodemographic information in the dataset utilized for analysis, and limited socio-cultural context. Acknowledging these strengths and limitations is crucial for a nuanced interpretation of the study’s insights and informs directions for future research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our 21-year longitudinal study, utilizing data from the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study, reveals a significant increase in childhood obesity among Tehranian children and adolescents, particularly in boys. The rise in mean BMI underscores the urgency of tailored interventions. Gender-specific and age-related patterns highlight the need for targeted strategies, emphasizing the global challenge of childhood obesity. Our findings will inform policymakers, healthcare practitioners, and educators regarding interventions against the obesity epidemic.
