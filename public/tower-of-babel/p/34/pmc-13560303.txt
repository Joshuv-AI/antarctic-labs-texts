Capturing goal content from free text in weight loss: an exploratory pilot on feasibility, reliability, and early outcomes
Authors: Tomomi Nagasawa, Tsuyoshi Okuhara, Yuriko Nishiie, Hiroko Okada, Takahiro Kiuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveUnderstanding patients’ self-articulated weight-loss goals may support tailored weight-management interventions; however, reproducible methods to capture and classify free-text goal content are limited. This pilot study aimed to develop and preliminarily evaluate a laddering-informed free-text elicitation and classification protocol, and to explore its associations with motivational quality and short-term weight change.ResultsAdults with a body mass index ≥ 25 kg/m² enrolled in a 3-month digital behavioral weight-management program completed two free-text prompts. Responses were coded by two trained raters, using rules mapped to the Aspiration Index (AI) and the Goal Content for Weight Maintenance Scale (GCWMS). Inter-rater agreement was excellent (Cohen’s κ = 0.91 for both frameworks). The protocol yielded analyzable data. AI showed better operational fit than GCWMS, with fewer unclassified (“Other”) responses (2.1% vs. 19.1%). Differences in motivational quality across goal-content categories were small (partial η² = 0.005–0.032). Introduction

Obesity is a major public health concern associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and reduced life expectancy [1]. Lifestyle-based weight-management programs targeting diet and physical activity are effective for weight loss [2], yet outcomes vary substantially across individuals. Qualitative evidence suggests that individual motivation plays an important role in initiating and maintaining weight-loss behaviors [3].

Goal Content Theory distinguishes intrinsic goals (e.g., health, personal growth, relationships) from extrinsic goals (e.g., image) and posits that goal content shapes motivational quality and behavior [4]. Intrinsic goal content predicts healthy eating behaviors [5–7] and is associated with higher exercise participation than appearance-driven motives [8–11]. Goal content also influences the quality of motivation [12, 13], indicating goal content as a modifiable target for health interventions. However, most prior studies rely on structured questionnaires rather than participants’ own descriptions of their goals.

In weight-management practice, participants’ self-articulated goals are often elicited to inform motivational interviewing and action planning [14]. Despite this, reproducible procedures to extract and classify goal content from free-text responses remain limited. The Aspiration Index (AI) is a widely used framework for classifying life goals [4, 15], whereas the Goal Content for Weight Maintenance Scale (GCWMS) was developed as a weight-specific measure [16]. Their applicability to participant-generated free-text weight-loss goals has rarely been examined.

The data analyzed in this study were obtained within a real-world digital weight-management program in which participants were asked to describe their reasons for wanting to lose weight. The free-text questions were refined through pre-testing for research purposes and then implemented within the program to enable systematic analysis of goal content. Laddering is a qualitative technique that elicits higher-order goals by repeatedly asking why an initial goal is important [9, 17–19]. Because laddering captures superordinate reasons underlying behavior, it may be well suited for eliciting goal content from free text in health behavior contexts. However, its feasibility and reliability for systematically operationalizing weight-loss goal content using established frameworks have not been formally evaluated.

Accordingly, this pilot study aimed to develop and preliminarily evaluate a reproducible, laddering-informed protocol for operationalizing and classifying self-articulated weight-loss goal content from free-text responses, and to explore associations between extracted goal content, motivational quality, and short-term weight change.

Methods

Design

This exploratory pilot used a short-term longitudinal observational design, conducted in advance of a planned 6- and 12-month follow-up study. Goal content and motivational quality were assessed at program entry, and weight change was assessed prospectively over the 3-month program period. As a pilot, analyses focused on feasibility, reliability, and exploratory associations. This exploratory pilot study was not preregistered.

Participants and procedure

Eligible participants were adults with body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m² who enrolled between March 2024 and March 2025 in a 3-month weight-loss program provided by Issin Co., Ltd. Exclusion criteria were prior bariatric surgery; current pharmacotherapy for weight loss; ≥10-kg weight change in the past three months; ongoing treatment for serious diseases such as cancer; current medical treatment including dietary/exercise counseling for any illness; pregnancy or within one year postpartum; and enrollment in the program for purposes other than weight loss. A total of 47 participants were included.

At program sign-up, participants viewed an online study information sheet embedded in the program’s web questionnaire and provided informed consent by responding to consent items. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Graduate School of Medicine/Faculty of Medicine, The University of Tokyo (IRB No. 2023377NI).

Free-text elicitation of goal content

Goal content was elicited using a laddering-informed approach [9, 17–19]. Participants responded to two free-text prompts newly developed for this study: (1) “Why do you want to lose weight?” and (2) “Why is what you wrote above important to you? What do you want to achieve through it?” (see Supplementary Table 1) The two-item format was finalized following pre-testing to ensure sufficient response volume and diversity while minimizing reliance on examples (see Supplementary Table 2). This structure was designed to elicit both initial reasons and superordinate goals underlying weight-loss intentions.

Coding and classification of goal content

Free-text responses were transcribed verbatim and independently classified using both AI and GCWMS for the same responses. AI was selected as a framework aligned with Goal Content Theory, and GCWMS as a weight-specific framework, allowing comparison between theory-based and domain-specific approaches. A coding guideline was developed based on the original scale development papers [4, 15, 16] and refined through pilot coding (see Supplementary Table 3).

Coding was performed for responses to both prompts. For the first prompt, multiple categories were permitted and used only for descriptive summaries. For the second prompt, a single category was assigned in principle and used for inferential analyses. This approach was adopted because the laddering-informed second prompt was designed to identify the most salient superordinate meaning underlying participants’ goals, and mutually exclusive categories were required for the exploratory quantitative analyses. When responses included multiple or ambiguous goals, the primary goal was determined using the predefined rules: (i) means–end relation (end as a primary), and (ii) consistency with the context of the first prompts. Responses that could not be classified under either framework were coded as Other.

Both coders had graduate-level training in health communication and prior familiarity with Self-Determination Theory–related concepts. Two coders independently classified all responses after one hour of training using the coding guide. Inter-rater agreement for the second prompt was assessed using Cohen’s κ, and initial disagreements occurred in 3 of 47 cases (6.4%) and were resolved by discussion based on predefined coding rules until consensus was reached. Consensus codes were used for subsequent analyses.

Measures

Motivation quality

Motivational quality was operationalized at program entry using the Japanese version of the Treatment Self-Regulation Questionnaire (TSRQ), adapted to the weight-loss context [20]. TSRQ assesses the degree of autonomous versus controlled regulation regarding why individuals engage in health-related behavior. The scale comprised 15 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = not at all true to 7 = very true) and yielded four subscales: Autonomous, Introjected, External, and Amotivation. Mean scores were computed for each subscale. Internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) was acceptable for Autonomous (0.78), Introjected (0.87), and External (0.78), and lower for Amotivation (0.46), consistent with prior validation work.

Weight change

Weight change was calculated as the difference between the mean weight during the first program week and the 12th week. Daily body weight was measured using the program’s “smart bath-mat” scale and averaged over a 7-day window at each time point [21, 22]. Positive values indicate weight loss.

Covariates

Covariates included age, sex, and baseline BMI, all of which have been associated with weight-loss outcomes in prior research [23–26]. Baseline BMI was computed from self-reported height and mean body weight during the first program week. Program engagement was included as an additional covariate in secondary analysis and was operationalized as the summed count of logged actions and health-coach sessions recorded by the program system [27, 28]. Logged actions reflected the completion of predefined behavioral goals set within the program, such as walking for 30 min per day.

Addressing bias

Anonymity was emphasized in the web survey, mitigating social desirability bias inherent in self-administered data. Classification bias was addressed by having two independent coders conduct goal-content coding and quantifying inter-rater reliability. All analytic decisions, including handling of ambiguous responses and exclusion rules, were pre-specified.

Sample size

As a pilot study, sample size was not determined by a priori power calculation. All eligible participants who enrolled during the target period and provided consent were included in evaluating feasibility, coding reliability, and preliminary association patterns to inform the planned longitudinal study.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 21.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive analyses used all registered cases (n = 47). For association analyses, AI category was utilized, and one case classified as Other was excluded due to insufficient cell size, resulting in an analytic sample of n = 46. Given the small and imbalanced subgroup sizes, analyses were intended to be hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons, and inference was not based solely on p-values. Results were interpreted primarily using effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals (CIs); bootstrap CIs were used where appropriate.

Feasibility was evaluated by response length and the distribution of extracted goal-content categories. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s κ with bootstrapped 95% CIs, and framework fit was compared descriptively based on the proportion of responses classified as Other. Because AI yielded fewer Other classifications, AI categories were used for subsequent association analyses. Motivational subscale scores were summarized across AI goal-content categories. Associations with short-term weight change were explored using hierarchical multiple regression, adjusting for covariates, autonomous motivation, and program engagement. A sensitivity analysis including the participant classified as Other in the AI framework yielded substantively unchanged results; therefore, the primary analysis excluding this case is reported.

Feasibility of free-text goal elicitation

Adequate free-text responses were obtained from the two-step laddering-informed prompts. Mean response length was 40.5 characters (SD = 34.6) for Item 1 and 24.6 characters (SD = 13.0) for Item 2, indicating sufficient volume for goal extraction. Extracted goal content spanned multiple categories under both classification frameworks, with health- and image-related goals most frequently reported (Table 2).

Reliability and framework fit

Inter-rater reliability was excellent for both frameworks (Cohen’s κ = 0.91 for AI and GCWMS). However, the proportion of responses classified as Other differed substantially: 2.1% under AI versus 19.1% under GCWMS. Cases coded as Other in GCWMS typically concerned goals related to family relationships or a sense of fulfillment in life. Given the lower proportion of unclassified responses, AI categories were used for subsequent association analyses.

Associations with motivational quality

Differences in TSRQ motivational subscale scores across AI goal-content categories were small, with no statistically significant between-category differences observed across subscales (all p > .70; partial η² =0.005–0.032). Given the pilot nature of the study and limited cell sizes, these findings are reported descriptively, and detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table 4. Descriptive distributions of key variables are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Associations with short-term weight change

In the hierarchical regression (Table 3), goal-content categories showed limited direct association with 3-month weight change. In the final model, standardized coefficients for goal-content categories ranged from β = −0.29 to 0.13, with wide confidence intervals. Adding autonomous motivation and program engagement substantially improved model fit. Engagement was positively associated with weight loss (β = 0.51, p = .002), whereas autonomous motivation showed a negative association (β = −0.36, p = .02).

Discussion

The two-step, laddering-informed prompts yielded sufficient free-text data for analysis in a real-world program. Beyond feasibility testing, this study contributes a preliminary operationalization framework for capturing and classifying self-articulated goal content from brief free-text responses. Coding reliability was excellent for both frameworks; however, AI showed better operational fit to free-text responses than GCWMS, as indicated by a substantially lower proportion of unclassified (Other) responses. This likely reflects the laddering structure, which elicits superordinate meanings that align more naturally with AI’s broader life-goal categories than with the more specific and behavior-focused categories of GCWMS [4]. Thus, the observed difference may reflect compatibility between the elicitation method and the level of abstraction of the framework, rather than superiority of one framework over the other. This approach may be applicable to other behavior change contexts where capturing self-articulated goals is relevant.

Differences in motivational subtypes across goal-content categories were small, consistent with the conceptual distinction between goal content (what individuals aim for) and motivational quality (why they engage). In line with prior theory, the same goal content may be regulated autonomously or in a controlled manner depending on internalization and value congruence [29, 30].

Direct associations between goal content and short-term weight change were limited, whereas program engagement was positively associated with weight loss. Autonomous motivation showed a negative association over the 3-month period. Given the short observation window, this finding should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory and may reflect sample-specific factors, restricted variance, or statistical artifacts, in addition to the possible time-dependent motivational dynamics, whereby autonomous regulation may contribute more to sustained behavioral adaptation than to immediate quantitative outcomes [31–33].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample and cell imbalance introduce uncertainty into estimates of group differences and associations. Second, the 3-month window may not capture the temporal dynamics of motivation and behavior change. Third, self-selection may overrepresent highly motivated individuals. In addition, assigning a single category to Item 2 responses may have reduced the complexity of overlapping goals; future studies may benefit from multi-label approaches. In the 6- and 12-month longitudinal phases, recruitment channels will be addressed via covariates or stratification to assess generalizability. While the present study examined associations between goal content and short-term weight change, goal-directed behavior cannot be fully explained by motivational factors alone. Future research should examine how goal content interacts with planning and self-regulatory processes in the translation of intentions into action, a challenge highlighted by the intention–behavior gap literature [34–36].

Conclusion

This pilot provides initial evidence that a two-step, laddering-informed protocol can reproducibly operationalize and classify self-articulated weight-loss goal content from free-text responses. The AI appeared better suited to participants’ spontaneous goal narratives than GCWMS. This approach may provide a practical framework for studying self-articulated goal content in behavioral weight-management research. Future follow-up analyses should clarify how self-articulated goal content relates to motivational processes and behavioral outcomes over time, and whether this approach may support tailored behavioral weight-management interventions.
