Effects of probiotics and synbiotics on body weight management in individuals with overweight or obesity: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Joungbo Ko, Jisun Chun, Choongsung Yoo, Drew E. Gonzalez, Youngil Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
[Purpose]This meta-analysis examined whether short-term probiotic/synbiotic supplementation reduces body weight, BMI, fat mass, body fat percentage, and lean body mass in adults with overweight or obesity.[Methods]Electronic databases were searched for RCTs of probiotic or synbiotic interventions versus placebo. Study quality was assessed using the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias tool. Standardized mean differences (Hedges’ g; RevMan 5.4) were pooled using a random-effects model on post-intervention endpoint values.[Results]Ten RCTs (633 participants) were included. No significant differences were observed in body weight (9 studies, n = 592; SMD = -0.44; 95% CI: -0.92 to 0.04; I2 = 87%) or lean body mass (5 studies, n = 342; SMD = -0.14; 95% CI: −0.53 to 0.25; I2 = 69%). A significant reduction in BMI was identified (10 studies, n = 633; SMD = -0.50; 95% CI: -0.91 to -0.09; I2 = 84%), and a small but significant effect on fat mass/body fat percentage was observed (8 studies, n = 479; SMD = -0.34; 95% CI: -0.62 to -0.06; I2 = 57%). In sensitivity analyses excluding a study with pre-randomization baseline imbalance, the BMI effect attenuated to borderline significance (SMD = -0.45; p = 0.048) and the fat mass/body fat percentage effect lost statistical significance (SMD = -0.26; p = 0.060).[Conclusion]Short-term (8-12 weeks) probiotic/synbiotic supplementation may yield modest benefits on BMI and adiposity-related outcomes; however, sensitivity analyses indicated these effects were not robust to single-study exclusion. Body weight and lean body mass were not significantly affected. Use of post-intervention endpoint values (change-from-baseline scores were not consistently available) may have biased pooled estimates. Findings should be interpreted as preliminary.

INTRODUCTION

Obesity, an intricate and multifaceted condition characterized by excessive body fat, poses substantial health risks, including cardiovascular diseases [1], diabetes [2], and cancer [3]. Internationally recognized as a critical public health issue, obesity contributes to increased mortality and disability rates among the elderly [4]. The urgency of this challenge is underscored by the dramatic increase in obesity prevalence in recent decades [5]. For instance, global data from 2022 indicated that 43% of adults were overweight and 16% were living with obesity, while the condition also affected 160 million children and adolescents [6].

A body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2 is considered overweight, while a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 is defined as obesity [6]. However, BMI calculations do not differentiate between fat and muscle mass [7], posing challenges in accurately diagnosing obesity, especially in individuals with a high proportion of lean body mass [8]. While maintaining a negative energy balance is essential for combating obesity [9,10], preserving muscle mass is equally crucial when constituting approximately 20-30% of the total weight loss [11,12].

Although the causes of obesity are multifactorial, including genetics, metabolic rate, and environment, growing evidence has emerged that microorganisms, especially in gut microbiota, which rely on the host’s food for survival and relay communications between the host and environment, contribute to metabolic distress and thus can be an origin of metabolic diseases. For instance, dysbiosis (gut microbial imbalance) between beneficial and harmful bacteria communities among about 10 trillion symbiotic microbes in the gut potentiates systemic metabolic dysregulation associated with obesity [13,14]. Given the fact that healthy active gut microbiomes produce a significant amount of physiologically beneficial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids, vitamins, and antioxidants, while unhealthy microbiomes generate toxic metabolites and pro-inflammatory cytokines, it seems probable that maintaining a beneficial microflora is critical to health.

Recent studies have shown that the defective microflora in obese people contributes to gaining fat mass and losing lean body mass [15,16], while probiotics, defined as live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host, may reinstate a healthier microflora composition [17], suggesting that supplementation with probiotics may serve as a potential strategy to mitigate the prevalence of obesity-related metabolic disturbances. For example, previous studies have demonstrated that probiotics improve lipid profiles [18] and increase the release of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and peptide YY (PYY) hormones [19,20], which are known to play a critical role in curbing appetite [21,22]. In animal studies, a notable increase in muscle mass was observed in mice following the administration of probiotics [23,24]. Moreover, probiotics administration has been associated with attenuation of pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and preventing muscle atrophy [25,26].

Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have evaluated the effects of probiotics on weight loss and BMI [27-29]; however, no systematic review or meta-analysis has yet investigated whether probiotics affect LBM. Understanding the impact of probiotics on LBM is particularly important, as preserving or enhancing LBM during weight loss interventions can help maintain metabolic rate, physical function, and overall health outcomes, preventing issues like sarcopenic obesity where muscle loss exacerbates fat accumulation. Therefore, this study represents a novel contribution by conducting the first systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the impact of probiotics on body weight, BMI, fat mass, fat percentage, and LBM in individuals with overweight and obesity, thereby filling a critical gap in the literature and providing evidence-based insights into probiotics’ role in optimizing body composition for obesity management.

METHODS

Design of Study

We conducted this systematic review to synthesize and analyze quantitatively and qualitatively the studies investigating the effects of probiotics on weight management in individuals with overweight or obesity. This review is registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, No. CRD42024513347) and adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.

The Methodology for Identifying and Selecting Studies

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were established based on the PICOSD framework (Participants, Interventions, Comparisons, Outcomes, and Study Design), guiding the search strategy and the selection of studies.

Inclusion Criteria

1) Participants

Participants were overweight (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) or obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2).

2) Intervention

The interventions comprised the administration of probiotics and synbiotics.

3) Comparison

Comparisons included a control group, specifically serving as a placebo group.

4) Outcomes

Scales representing body weight management, such as body weight, BMI, fat mass, percent body fat, and lean body mass, were included.

5) Study Design

This study included only randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Exclusion Criteria

Studies published before 2014, those not written in English, individuals with a BMI less than 25 kg/m2, and animal studies were excluded. The 2014 lower boundary was established because probiotic strain characterization standards and body composition measurement protocols, particularly DXA-based LBM and fat mass assessment, became more consistently applied from this period onward. We acknowledge that this restriction, along with the English-language restriction and exclusion of grey literature, may have introduced selection and language bias; these are discussed in the limitations section.

Literature Search Strategy

This study was initiated after a search for studies in February 2024 and was registered in PROSPERO in March 2024. Experienced meta-analysis researchers utilized the following keywords in their search strategy: ‘(randomized controlled trial) AND (obesity OR overweight) AND (weight loss OR weight management) AND (probiotics OR synbiotic).’ The search included several international electronic databases: Web of Science, Cumulated Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE), and Excerpta Medica Database (Embase). Grey literature, such as conference proceedings and theses, was not systematically searched in this review, focusing instead on peer-reviewed publications from major databases.

Data Extraction and Study Inclusion

Studies identified in the aforementioned electronic databases were exported to Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington, USA), and duplicates were removed. Experienced meta-analysis researchers employed a systematic procedure for study selection: First, titles and abstracts were screened based on the inclusion criteria; second, the study design and data in the full texts were examined; third, a consultation was conducted on which studies to include or exclude; and fourth, data were extracted from the studies that met all the criteria.

Quality Assessment

Since only RCTs were ultimately included, all studies were assessed using the seven-item Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) tool developed by the Cochrane Bias Methods Group [30]. Ratings (low, unclear, or high) were assigned by two experienced meta-analysis researchers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. A formal inter-rater reliability test (e.g., Cohen’s kappa) was not applied; this represents a limitation that future reviews should address.

Strategy for Data Synthesis

RevMan 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK) was used for all analyses. A random-effects model based on the DerSimonian and Laird method [54] was applied to all pooled analyses. Effect sizes were expressed as SMDs calculated using the Hedges’ g approach as implemented in RevMan 5.4. Post-intervention endpoint values (group means and SDs at follow-up) were used across all analyses; change-from-baseline scores were not uniformly reported and could not serve as the primary metric without additional imputation assumptions. Cohen’s SMD criteria were applied: < 0.4 small; 0.4-0.7 moderate; > 0.7 large. Heterogeneity was assessed via Cochran’s Q test and I2 (I2 > 75% = high; < 40% = low) [31]. Fat mass (kg) and body fat percentage (%) were combined into a single pooled outcome using SMD to facilitate comparison across differently scaled measures; lean body mass, fat-free mass, and appendicular muscle mass-related variables were similarly pooled as a single LBM outcome. The specific measure entered into the meta-analysis for each study is specified in Table 1. Publication bias assessment via a funnel plot was pre-specified but not performed, as all outcome analyses included 10 or fewer studies, below the minimum threshold for reliable funnel plot interpretation per Cochrane guidance [43]. Given the limited number of studies per outcome (5-10), formal subgroup analyses by intervention type, strain, sex, duration, or co-intervention were not statistically feasible; the distribution of studies across proposed subgroup categories is described in the discussion.

RESULTS

Literature Search and Characteristics of the Included Randomized Clinical Trials

The search identified 210 studies. After removing 98 duplicates, 112 remained. After title/abstract screening (74 excluded) and full-text review (28 excluded), 10 studies were included (Figure 1) [33-42].

Methodological Quality Assessment

RoB assessments: random sequence generation (low: 10); allocation concealment (low: 5; unclear: 5); blinding of participants and personnel (low: 1; unclear: 9); blinding of outcome assessment (low: 1; unclear: 9); incomplete outcome data (low: 9; high: 1); selective reporting (low: 10); other biases (low: 1; unclear: 9) (Figure 2). The predominantly unclear rating for blinding of outcome assessment (9 studies) introduces uncertainty regarding outcome ascertainment and should be considered when interpreting the pooled estimates. Formal sensitivity analyses excluding high- or unclear-risk studies were not performed, as doing so would render meta-analysis infeasible for most outcomes.

Effects of Probiotics and synbiotics on Body Weight Management

The final review analyzed ten studies involving 633 overweight or obese participants. Interventions consisted of probiotics and synbiotics, with treatment durations ranging from 6 to 12 weeks. Outcome measures assessed body weight management using various assessment tools (Table 1). The findings showed a mixed pattern across outcomes: significant pooled effects were observed for BMI and fat mass/body fat percentage, whereas no significant effects were observed for body weight or lean body mass. As described in the subsequent subsections, the BMI and fat mass findings were sensitive to the exclusion of Kim et al., with BMI attenuating to borderline significance and fat mass losing statistical significance.

Effects of Probiotics and Synbiotics on Body Weight

In a meta-analysis of 9 studies including 592 individuals, no significant difference in body weight was identified. Results from the random-effects model: SMD = -0.44; 95% CI: -0.92 to 0.04; heterogeneity (χ2 = 63.06, df = 8, p < 0.001, I2 = 87%); Z = 1.81. Notable individual effects: Jung (SMD = -0.71), Kim (SMD = -3.10), Lim (SMD = -0.46). Kim et al. had substantial pre-randomization differences in body weight between groups, which likely contributed to the high heterogeneity for this outcome. Sensitivity analysis excluding Kim et al. (2018): 8 studies, n = 532; SMD = -0.19 (95% CI: -0.41 to 0.03); χ2 = 11.18, df = 7, I2 = 37.4%; Z = -1.72 (non-significant). Heterogeneity decreased substantially from 87.7% to 37.4%, confirming that Kim et al. was the primary driver of heterogeneity. The pooled effect moved toward the null, indicating that Kim et al.’s extreme SMD (-3.10), attributable at least in part to pre-randomization baseline imbalance, had inflated the original pooled estimate.

Effects of Probiotics and Synbiotics on BMI

In a meta-analysis of 10 studies involving 633 individuals, a significant reduction in BMI was observed. SMD = -0.50; 95% CI: -0.91 to -0.09; heterogeneity (χ2 = 56.00, df = 9, p < 0.001, I2 = 84%); Z = 2.37, moderate effect (Figure 4). Large individual effects: Higashikawa (-2.39), Jung (-1.32), Kim (-0.96). Sensitivity analysis excluding Kim et al. (2018): 9 studies, n = 573; SMD = -0.45 (95% CI: -0.90 to -0.004); χ2 = 54.80, df = 8, I2 = 85%; Z = 1.98, p = 0.048. The pooled effect attenuated and approached the threshold of statistical significance, with the upper confidence limit narrowly excluding zero. Heterogeneity was largely unchanged from the original analysis, indicating that variability in BMI outcomes across studies was not primarily driven by Kim et al.

Effects of Probiotics and Synbiotics on Fat Mass and Body Fat Percentage

In a meta-analysis of 8 studies involving 479 participants, a small but significant effect was observed. SMD = -0.34; 95% CI: -0.62 to -0.06; heterogeneity (χ2 = 16.11, df = 7, p = 0.024, I2 = 57%); Z = 2.38 (Figure 5). Large individual effects: Higashikawa (-1.18), Kim (-0.90). Sensitivity analysis excluding Kim et al. (2018): 7 studies, n = 419; SMD = -0.26 (95% CI: -0.53 to 0.01); χ2 = 11.61, df = 6, I2 = 48%; Z = 1.88, p = 0.060. The pooled effect lost statistical significance after exclusion of Kim et al., and heterogeneity decreased from I2 = 58% to I2 = 48%, indicating that Kim et al. contributed materially to both the magnitude and the heterogeneity of the original pooled estimate.

Effects of Probiotics and Synbiotics on LBM

In a meta-analysis of 5 studies with 342 participants, no significant difference in LBM was identified. SMD = -0.14; 95% CI: -0.53 to 0.25; heterogeneity (χ2 = 12.83, df = 4, p = 0.012, I2 = 69%); Z = 0.72 (Figure 6). Kim et al. (SMD = -0.81) showed a significant individual effect; the other four studies yielded non-significant findings. Baseline LBM in the Kim et al. placebo group was approximately 3.6-5.3 kg higher than in the probiotic groups, which likely influenced the endpoint-based pooled estimate for this study. Sensitivity analysis excluding Kim et al.: 4 studies, n = 282; SMD = 0.00 (95% CI: -0.32 to 0.33); I2 = 46.0%; Z = 0.02 (non-significant). The pooled LBM effect was essentially zero after removing Kim et al., indicating that the baseline LBM imbalance in this study was the primary driver of the original pooled LBM SMD.

Publication Bias

Publication bias assessment via a funnel plot was pre-specified but not performed, as all outcome analyses included 10 or fewer studies, below the threshold for reliable funnel plot interpretation per Cochrane guidance [43].

DISCUSSION

Summary of Evidence

The present meta-analysis examined the impact of probiotic and synbiotic supplementation across 10 RCTs involving 633 individuals with overweight or obesity. Significant reductions in BMI (SMD = -0.50; moderate effect) and fat mass/body fat percentage (SMD = -0.34; small effect) were observed. Body weight and LBM were not significantly affected. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, given the high heterogeneity for BMI (I2 = 84%) and body weight (I2 = 87%), the mixed nature of the interventions, and the presence of co-interventions in several trials. As confirmed by the sensitivity analyses, Kim et al. was the primary driver of heterogeneity for both body weight and LBM outcomes, and its exclusion moved the pooled body weight effect toward the null.

Impact of Probiotic and Synbiotic Supplementation on LBM and Body Weight

Probiotic supplementation has been considered as one potential countermeasure to sarcopenia [44]. A recent meta-analysis of 24 RCTs assessed the impact of probiotic supplementation on muscle mass, total lean mass, and muscular strength among adults (both healthy and unhealthy) and reported no effect on lean body mass (SMD = -0.03, 95% confidence interval: -0.1 to 0.13, I2 = 0%, p = 0.69) [45]. Consistent with the study, the present meta-analysis did not find a positive effect on lean body mass or body weight following probiotic and synbiotic administration. The near-identical effect sizes for body weight (SMD = -0.44) and BMI (SMD = -0.50) indicate that the marginal difference in statistical significance does not reflect a true biological divergence. The body weight analysis comprised 9 studies (592 participants), while the BMI comprised 10 studies (633 participants); the slightly larger BMI dataset yielded marginally tighter confidence intervals. Kim et al. [35]’s substantial pre-randomization body weight imbalance inflated within-study variance for body weight, widening its pooled CI. The body weight CI (-0.92 to 0.04) crosses zero by only 0.04 standardized units, likely reflecting marginal statistical power rather than a null finding. Since adult height does not change over 8-12 weeks, any true change in BMI must proportionally reflect a change in body weight; the near-identical SMDs confirm the biological consistency of these outcomes.

VARIABILITY IN INTERVENTION CHARACTERISTICS

A fundamental consideration when interpreting the pooled estimates is the substantial heterogeneity in the interventions. Probiotic strains ranged from single-strain preparations (e.g., Lactobacillus sakei [33], Pediococcus pentosaceus LP28 [42], L. gasseri BNR17 [35], L. rhamnosus GG [39]) to multi-strain combinations (e.g., L. curvatus HY7601 + L. plantarum KY103241, Lee et al. [36]) and synbiotic formulations (Hadi et al. [34], Kong et al. [37], Batu et al. [38]). Dosages ranged from approximately 5×108 to 1011 CFU per day; durations from 6 to 12 weeks. Because probiotic mechanisms are strain-specific, involving differential effects on short-chain fatty acid production, bile acid transformation, and gut barrier integrity, pooling these heterogeneous interventions attenuates strain-level effects and inflates between-study variance, which likely accounts for much of the I2 observed for body weight and BMI. Three of the ten studies used synbiotic formulations. Because synbiotics combine a probiotic with a prebiotic substrate, they may act through partially distinct mechanisms; the prebiotic component may selectively promote survival and activity of the co-administered probiotic. Among the three synbiotic studies, two (Hadi et al. [34], Kong et al. [37]) reported significant reductions in body weight, while Batu et al. [38] found no significant change after six weeks. Pooling synbiotics with probiotic-only interventions introduces conceptual heterogeneity beyond that captured by I2 statistics; future meta-analyses should evaluate these as separate categories.

Among the seven probiotic-only studies, individual standardized mean differences in the meta-analysis reached statistical significance for body weight in three of the six contributing studies (Jung et al., Kim et al., Lim et al.); for BMI in three of seven (Higashikawa et al., Jung et al., Kim et al.); for fat mass/body fat percentage in two of six (Higashikawa et al., Kim et al.); and for lean body mass in one of four (Kim et al.). Among the three synbiotic studies (Hadi et al., Kong et al., Batu et al.), no individual SMD in the meta-analysis reached statistical significance for any outcome. We note that Hadi et al. and Kong et al. reported significant within-study reductions in body weight in their original publications using change-score analyses; these were not detected in our endpoint-based SMD calculation. For transparency, Mohammadi-Sartang et al. was retained in the probiotic-only category despite the inclusion of 3 g of inulin (~1% of the yogurt by weight), as inulin represented a minor component of a primarily probiotic-focused fortified yogurt and the comparator group also received probiotic yogurt; reclassification does not change the qualitative pattern of findings, since Mohammadi-Sartang’s individual SMDs were non-significant for all four outcomes. The pooled estimates therefore reflect a heterogeneous mixture of intervention types, and any aggregate signal must be interpreted as reflecting the combined intervention category rather than a probiotic- or synbiotic-specific effect.

Impact of Probiotic and Synbiotic Supplementation on BMI

Several of the best weight-loss practices require creating an energy deficit [9], necessitating adherence to either hypocaloric diets or increased energy expenditure via exercise. However, these practices can be challenging for overweight and obese individuals [46]. In this regard, probiotics have been suggested as a potential additive or advantage in weight loss. To evaluate the efficacy of probiotics in weight loss, BMI has been a commonly used tool for assessing overweight and obesity [47]. However, researchers and clinical professionals are increasingly concerned about BMI as an obesity index because it does not differentiate fat mass from lean body mass [48-50]. Nevertheless, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis has maintained that BMI remains a helpful obesity screening tool in clinical practice and can provide meaningful initial information to the individual [51].

Our results indicate a tentative statistical signal for BMI reduction following 8 to 12 weeks of probiotic/synbiotic supplementation. This signal should not be interpreted as evidence of a biologically distinct effect on BMI relative to body weight, as the underlying effect sizes were nearly identical (SMD = -0.50 vs -0.44); the difference in statistical significance reflects differences in sample size and confidence interval width rather than a true biological divergence. As shown in our sensitivity analysis, the BMI effect was attenuated and approached the threshold of significance when Kim et al. was excluded, further indicating that this finding should be interpreted with caution. Consistent with our observation, Zhang et al. showed that at least eight weeks of probiotic supplementation may be needed to induce reductions in anthropometric and body composition parameters and BMI among those classified as obese or overweight [52]. Our meta-analysis identified only three of the ten RCTs [35,41,42] showing a favorable effect of probiotic supplementation on BMI. For instance, Higashikawa and colleagues [42] assessed the anti-obesity effect of Pediococcus pentosaceus LP28 among 62 overweight individuals in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled fashion and noted that the probiotic group experienced a near-significant mean difference from placebo for BMI (mean, [95CIs]; -0.33 kg/m2 [-0.74, 0.08]) following a 12-week intervention. Furthermore, the probiotic group had a lower BMI than the placebo and heat-killed LP28 groups (p = 0.03) [42]. Additionally, Jung et al. [41] demonstrated that supplementation with Lactobacillus curvatus HY7601 for 12 weeks resulted in statistically significant reductions (p = 0.001) in BMI for the probiotic group (-0.24 ± 0.09 kg/m2) compared to the placebo group (-0.14 ± 0.07 kg/m2). Kim et al. [35] assessed the impact of the probiotic Lactobacillus gasseri BNR17 (109 CFU/day) for 12 weeks among obese adults in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled fashion and noted that the placebo group experienced a slight increase in BMI from baseline values (+0.15 kg/m2) while the probiotic group did not (-0.05 kg/m2). These studies support the conclusions of Zhang and colleagues’ meta-analysis that a longer supplementation duration may be needed to induce a reduction in BMI following probiotic ingestion.

Not all studies reported a favorable effect; however, it still appears promising that probiotic/synbiotic supplementation may have a positive effect on BMI. For example, although no statistically significant changes in BMI between the treatment and placebo groups were noted (p = 0.061) after eight weeks of probiotic administration, the probiotic treatment resulted in less of an increase in BMI (0.38 ± 0.67 kg/m2) compared to the placebo group (0.75 ± 0.52 kg/m2) 36. In addition, Mohammadi-Sartang et al. noted a trend for reductions (-1.8 ± 1.16) (p = 0.283) in BMI among a treatment group who ingested yogurt-containing probiotics for 10 weeks [40]. In line with our meta-analysis results, these studies show the potential of probiotics/synbiotics on BMI mitigation.

Impact of Probiotic and Synbiotic Supplementation on Fat Mass and Fat Percentage

Assessing body composition is critical for any weight loss intervention, and many researchers believe parameters such as fat mass, lean mass, and fat percentage provide more objective and convincing information about the efficacy of the weight loss intervention than BMI alone. Overall, our results indicate a small effect of probiotics/synbiotics on fat mass and fat percentage with a medium level of heterogeneity. Our results parallel those of Borgeraas et al. in that short-term supplementation (e.g., 8-12 weeks) with probiotics led to reduced fat mass and fat percentage with relatively small effects [53].

Interestingly, while not all studies demonstrated small favorable effects of probiotic/synbiotic supplementation, several important conclusions can be made from the studies included in our meta-analysis. First, the supplementation duration likely needs to be at least 8-12 weeks, with the longer durations inducing more favorable outcomes. For example, Batu et al. assessed the impact of six weeks of synbiotic supplementation among 61 sedentary, obese females and demonstrated no difference between the treatment and control groups (p = 0.722) for body fat percentage [38]. The authors indicated that the six-week administration may have been too short to induce favorable changes in fat percentage, among other anthropometric and body composition parameters. Second, probiotic administration may attenuate fat accumulation or blunt increases in fat mass, even in the absence of calorie restriction or exercise-induced energy deficits. For example, Kim et al. [35] noted, albeit without reaching statistical significance (p = 0.187), that the group ingesting probiotics did not experience changes in body fat percentage while the placebo group did (+2.0%). Additionally, some studies did not find statistically significant differences between the placebo and probiotic groups; however, several of these studies still reported potentially favorable outcomes. For instance, Higashikawa et al. [42] demonstrated a trend for reduction in fat mass among the probiotic group (mean, [95% CIs]; -0.58 [-1.29, 0.14], p = 0.075). Lastly, it may be best advised to administer probiotics in conjunction with whole foods. Supporting this concept, Mohammadi-Sartang et al. [40] demonstrated that probiotic-fortified yogurt ingestion for ten weeks led to a statistically significant reduction in fat mass (-3.4 ± 4.7 kg, p < 0.01) compared to the non-probiotic group (p = 0.026, d = -1.69), whose results appear to be the strongest among the studies included in our meta-analysis. Co-interventions involving dietary restriction or exercise were present in several included studies [36,39,40], which may have contributed to body composition changes independently of the probiotic/synbiotic intervention.

Taken together, short-term duration probiotic supplementation (8-12 weeks) seems advantageous in reducing fat mass and fat percentage among overweight and obese persons. The mechanisms proposed to underlie these effects, including modulation of gut microbiota composition, attenuation of systemic inflammation, improvements in insulin sensitivity, and upregulation of GLP-1 and PYY signaling, are biologically plausible and supported by the broader mechanistic literature. However, the present meta-analysis did not directly measure any of these pathways, and the included studies differed substantially in strain composition and delivery mode. The proposed mechanisms, therefore, represent hypotheses consistent with preclinical and mechanistic data, rather than conclusions supported by the present analysis. Given this heterogeneity, a unified mechanistic interpretation is not warranted.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTION

This meta-analysis is, to our knowledge, the first to include LBM as a primary outcome in an analysis of probiotic/synbiotic effects on body composition. Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the restriction to studies published from 2014 onward and to English-language publications, combined with the exclusion of grey literature, may have introduced selection and language bias. Second, three of the ten included studies used synbiotic formulations; pooling these with probiotic-only studies introduces conceptual heterogeneity beyond that captured by I2 statistics alone. Third, fat mass and body fat percentage were pooled as a single adiposity outcome, and lean body mass, fat-free mass, and muscle mass were pooled as a single LBM outcome; although the use of SMD permits comparison across differently scaled measures, these are not equivalent constructs, and their combination adds a further layer of conceptual heterogeneity. Fourth, post-intervention endpoint values were used across all analyses. In the presence of pre-randomization group imbalances, most notably in Kim et al., this approach may bias pooled effect estimates. As demonstrated by our sensitivity analyses, excluding Kim et al. reduced I2 for body weight from 87.7% to 37.4% and moved the pooled estimate substantially toward the null (from SMD -0.44 to -0.19), confirming that change-from-baseline scores or covariate-adjusted estimates would have been preferable for this study. Fifth, the limited number of studies per outcome (5-10) precluded formal subgroup analyses. The distribution of studies across proposed subgroup categories consistently fell below the threshold for a meaningful between-subgroup comparison. Sixth, co-interventions (dietary restriction, exercise) were present in several trials, potentially masking the isolated probiotic/synbiotic effect. Seventh, variations in age and sex distribution among the study groups may have introduced additional bias. Eighth, no formal inter-rater reliability test was applied during data extraction. Finally, 10 studies involving 633 participants were included; the small aggregate sample and high heterogeneity limit the precision and generalizability of the pooled estimates.

CONCLUSION

The current evidence suggests a tentative statistical signal of modest magnitude for short-term (≤ 12 weeks) probiotic/synbiotic supplementation on BMI and adiposity-related outcomes; however, sensitivity analyses showed that these effects were not robust to the exclusion of a single study with pre-randomization baseline imbalance. Body weight and lean body mass were not significantly affected. Importantly, our reliance on post-intervention endpoint values, in the absence of consistent change-from-baseline data, renders the pooled estimates vulnerable to bias when pre-randomization imbalances exist among included studies. The sensitivity analyses confirmed that this vulnerability affected the pooled estimates across all four outcomes, with body weight and lean body mass attenuating substantially toward the null, fat mass/body fat percentage losing statistical significance, and BMI attenuating to borderline significance. Given the small number of synbiotic studies (n = 3) and the conceptual heterogeneity introduced by their pooling with probiotic-only interventions, the present findings should be regarded as preliminary evidence pertaining to the combined probiotic/synbiotic intervention category, rather than as evidence supporting either intervention type individually. Despite these limitations, this is, to our knowledge, the first meta-analysis to systematically evaluate lean body mass as a primary outcome of probiotic/synbiotic supplementation, and our sensitivity analyses provide a transparent framework for interpreting the robustness of pooled estimates. Future studies should report change-from-baseline data with adequate randomization checks to enable more robust meta-analytic synthesis.
