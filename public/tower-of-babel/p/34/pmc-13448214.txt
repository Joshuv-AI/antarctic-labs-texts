Comprehensive reliability-quality integration framework for enhancing gear performance in laser powder bed fusion additive manufacturing process
Authors: Vikram Kumar Gupta, Sanjay Kumar Chaturvedi, Rajiv Nandan Rai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study introduces a comprehensive framework that integrates reliability assurance and quality control in the Laser Powder Bed Fusion (L-PBF) gear manufacturing process via the Reliability-Quality-Reliability (RQR) chain model. The framework highlights the reciprocal link among manufacturing system dependability, process quality, and product reliability, hence enhancing gear performance and operating efficiency. The examination of L-PBF gears indicated that internal flaws, including absence of fusion, balling, keyhole porosity, and residual stress, significantly compromise structural integrity, while exterior issues such as surface wear, hardness variation, and geometric deviation exacerbate reliability deterioration. Experimental reliability graphs shown a significant decrease from about unity to below 0.1 during extended operation when both fault types were analyzed. The assessment of essential quality attributes verified the general stability of the process, with slight variations in wear resistance and geometric precision. Process parameters, including laser power, scan speed, and layer thickness, were recognized as critical factors affecting microstructural integrity, fatigue life, and wear reliability, while post-processing treatments like HIP markedly enhanced performance. The suggested RQR-based performance matrix offers a systematic framework for iterative process optimization and system-wide improvements, facilitating defect reduction, enhanced fatigue and wear reliability, and sustainable, high-quality manufacturing of L-PBF-manufactured gears. The framework is demonstrated using independent peer-reviewed datasets and should be interpreted as a methodological and benchmark validation rather than experimental post-process validation. Future work will focus on independent experimental verification of the proposed framework.

Introduction

Additive manufacturing (AM) has emerged as a revolutionary approach to modern manufacturing, enabling complex and innovative designs with fewer restrictions. AM encompasses several stages that transition from a virtual CAD model to the creation of a physical component. The extent and manner in which AM is utilized vary depending on the product. For smaller and less complex items, AM might primarily serve to produce visualization models. In contrast, larger and more intricate products with significant engineering requirements often incorporate AM across multiple stages and iterations throughout the development process1. AM systems are more productive and adaptable in high-yield production than subtractive and formative manufacturing. However, it is common knowledge that the AM is a highly complex process that combines material science, mechanics, optics, and electronics knowledge with numerous applications in aerospace, healthcare, and manufacturing2. AM encompasses three primary methods: wire arc additive manufacturing, ,fused deposition modelling and powder bed fusion, which employs lasers or other heat sources to melt and fuse metal powder layer by layer based on 3D model data3. Selective Laser Sintering (SLS), developed at the University of Texas at Austin, USA, was the first powder bed fusion process to be commercialized. Subsequently, Laser Powder Bed Fusion (L-PBF) emerged as one of the first commercialized AM processes. The L-PBF technique shares several fundamental characteristics, including the use of one or more thermal sources to fuse powder particles, a mechanism for controlling fusion within specific regions of each layer, and systems for depositing and smoothing powder layers, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Due to these features, L-PBF is widely adopted globally, supporting a diverse range of materials, such as polymers, metals, ceramics, and composites. It is increasingly utilized for direct digital manufacturing of end-use products, as the material properties are comparable to many engineering-grade polymers, metals, and ceramics3.Fig. 1Metal additive manufacturing process4.

Metal additive manufacturing process4.

Industries such as aerospace, automotive, healthcare, and defences, which rely on lightweight, high-performance designs, stand to benefit significantly from AM technologies. Notable examples include ducting, airflow systems, and vent components manufactured using AM. A key advantage of AM lies in reducing the number of components in a finished product, which simplifies supply chains. The reliability of AM products must match or exceed that of conventionally manufactured components to realize the full potential of this technology. While traditional manufacturing processes have well-established frameworks for reliability assessment, AM technologies are still in the process of developing comprehensive frameworks to address variability and ensure consistent performance. The variability inherent in AM processes directly impacts product reliability, making it essential to identify, analyze, and mitigate these risks throughout the product development lifecycle5. Obviously, despite its transformative potential, the widespread adoption of AM for industrial applications is hindered by concerns about process repeatability, achieving consistent quality and reliability, especially in the production of functional components like gears using L-PBF6.

L-PBF additive manufacturing process design framework

The design of additive manufacturing phase involves incorporating design-for-additive principles to maximize the efficiency of the L-PBF process. This includes topology optimization to reduce weight while maintaining mechanical performance as well as optimizing the gear geometry to reduce distortion and improve dimensional accuracy. The gear teeth, for example, may be designed with specific considerations to minimize thermal distortion during printing, which can significantly impact the final part quality and functionality. The central focus of the L-PBF gear manufacturing process is to meet stringent system performance and reliability goals. These goals are addressed by emphasizing several interconnected phases, each crucial to the reliability of the final gear product. Material selection and process parameters

The choice of materials in the L-PBF process plays a crucial role, as it significantly influences the mechanical properties, microstructure, and overall performance of the final product. The materials utilized in the L-PBF process need to exhibit excellent thermal properties, including high thermal conductivity, to guarantee uniform melting and solidification throughout the layer-by-layer fabrication process. Typical materials utilized in the L-PBF process encompass a range of metals and alloys, including titanium alloys like Ti-6Al-4 V, aluminum alloys such as AlSi10Mg, nickel-based superalloys like Inconel 718, and stainless steel, specifically 316L7.Metal AM relies heavily on process parameter selection, which affects microstructure and defect development. Finding the “optimal” processing regimes for various materials to produce defect-free manufacturing has been a focus of study over the last decade8. As per the investigation, a detailed examination of the optimization strategies such as surrogate modelling and design of experiment should be employed to find the best AM process parameters for the process9. Process parameters can be classified into three categories, as shown in Fig. 3.Fig. 3L-PBF process parameters classification.

L-PBF process parameters classification.

It involves defining key process parameters, including laser power, scan speed, hatch distance, and layer thickness, to control the energy density applied during printing. These parameters are optimized to minimize the residual stresses, porosity, and other defects that can arise during the build process, which are critical for the mechanical reliability of the gear10. Essential post-processing operations to consider during the design phase encompass powder removal, support removal, build plate detachment, heat treatments, inspections, final machining, cleaning, surface polishing or enhancements, and joining methods such as welding or brazing. Post-processing contributes to the customization of material properties, including the optimization of hardness and corrosion resistance, while also addressing internal porosity that could act as initiation sites for fatigue cracks, depending on the alloy composition and application requirements for gear functionality11.

Quality engineering and reliability assessment

The L-PBF technique is significantly advanced, but fundamental challenges remain, particularly in achieving high-quality parts for mass production. These challenges arise because the process is controlled by several critical variables, which significantly impact production quality and can lead to defects in L-PBF-produced parts such as gears as shown in Fig. 4. This involves continuous process monitoring, where in-situ sensors are used to monitor temperature, melt pool dimensions, and laser power among other parameters during the build process to ensure consistency, and detect anomalies in real time. Quality management practices are also implemented that includes detailed inspections of the finished parts, such as dimensional analysis, surface roughness measurement, and gear profile accuracy assessments using tools like a dual flank roll tester to compare the manufactured gear against a master profile. Additionally, continuous improvement initiatives such as statistical control chart and design of experiment are employed to refine the L-PBF process parameters based on quality feedback, ensuring consistent part production over time12.Fig. 4L-PBF defects and process parameter relation13.

L-PBF defects and process parameter relation13.

Reliability Assessment is critical in evaluating whether the manufactured gears can reliably perform under intended service conditions. This involves comprehensive testing both Non-Destructive Testing (NDT) and Destructive Testing (DT)14. NDT methods, such as X-ray computed tomography (CT) and ultrasonic testing, are employed to detect internal defects, such as cracks or pores, without causing damage to the part. DT methods, including tensile testing, fatigue testing, and microhardness evaluation, are used to understand the mechanical properties and failure mechanisms of the gears15. The Failure Mechanism analysis involves investigating the types of failures that could occur in the manufactured gears, such as pitting, wear, and fatigue, and understanding their root causes. Risk Analysis is conducted alongside to quantify the likelihood and consequences of these potential failures, leading to strategies that mitigate risks through adjustments in process parameters, material choices, or design modifications16. Furthermore, Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) evaluates the environmental impacts of the entire production process, from raw material selection to end-of-life disposal, to ensure that the manufacturing of L-PBF gears is sustainable. Degradation Analysis is an important part of this phase, where the long-term behaviour of the gears, such as wear resistance, corrosion resistance, and creep, is studied to predict their service life17.

The Laser Powder Bed Fusion (L-PBF) gear manufacturing process encompasses several interrelated mechanisms that affect system performance and dependability18. Common faults encompass incomplete melting and inadequate layer overlap, resulting in porosity19; balling, characterized by the formation of spherical droplets in molten tracks under low energy density due to insufficient wettability; and keyhole holes20, which arise from excessive evaporation during the melting process21. Residual stresses result from abrupt temperature gradients, leading to fractures, warping, and dimensional errors22, whereas wear and fatigue behavior are influenced by microstructure, hardness, and processing conditions23. A Physics of Failure (PoF) framework as shown in Fig. 5 is utilized to guarantee dependability, classifying deterioration into thermal, geometric, fatigue24, and static domains throughout pre- and post-processing phases23. Pre-processing challenges (e.g., inadequate fusion, keyholing, warping, surface tension) influence thermal conductivity and dimensional accuracy13, whereas post-processing defects (e.g., fatigue fractures, root cracks, wear, surface irregularities25) diminish longevity and functionality26. Complementary Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) assesses material efficiency and energy consumption, whereas PoF focuses on fatigue, wear, and interactions of residual stress. Methodology for design of additive manufacturing using reliability-quality-reliability chain modelling

Reliability, which inherently requires repeatability, shows similar indicators such as fatigue life and geometry variations between AM and traditional techniques like casting and composites. However, reproducibility remains a challenge for AM5. To reliably design for AM, it is essential to first understand the inherent variability of the technology, enabling effective monitoring, compensation, control, and design with this variability in mind. The conceptual RQR (Reliability Quality Reliability) chain model illustrates how forward RQ and QR effects explain production reliability degradation, while backward RQ and QR feedbacks establish a reliability-focused quality control framework. To support decision-makers in assessing the impact of various quality control and reliability assurance measures, a reliability-oriented quality control framework is introduced5. This framework integrates these measures to systematically address production reliability degradation in manufacturing processes, as shown in Fig. 6.Fig. 6Reliability-quality-reliability chain modelling for gear27.

Reliability-quality-reliability chain modelling for gear27.

The defects illustrated in Fig. 6 can be categorized into two types: Exterior Defects I and Exterior Defects II. Exterior Defects I must be identified and addressed during the manufacturing process, as their presence in the usage phase would result in product quality issues, such as out-of-tolerance conditions and geometric distortions. On the other hand, Exterior Defects II impact production reliability by contributing to cumulative dimensional variations, even when individual variations remain within tolerance limits34. These defects are challenging to detect and have low visibility during manufacturing. Interior defects, in contrast, are difficult to identify during production and typically become apparent over time, leading to product failures during use, such as dry solder joints or potential cracks in soldering. Notably, Exterior Defects II can be regarded as a unique subset of interior defects, as they often serve as root causes of reliability degradation. There is an interactive relationship between exterior and interior defects, allowing them to transform under specific conditions. For instance, environmental stresses can gradually cause interior defects, such as potential cracks, to evolve into Exterior Defects I, as depicted in Fig. 6.

Additionally, Exterior Defects II can lead to alignment issues, which may contribute to the formation of new interior defects, such as residual stress, during the usage phase. The primary causes of production reliability degradation include manufacturing defects, process quality variations, and system reliability degradation mentioned in Eqs. (1) and (2). Manufacturing defects stem from the severity of process quality variations, which in turn are influenced by system reliability degradation. To prevent reliability degradation, manufacturing enterprises should enhance their quality control systems used to detect defects during production through the following three steps, namely, (i) Optimizing Reliability Testing Time. (ii) Quality Loss Environmental and (iii) Stress Screening and Burn-in Testing. However, conventional testing time optimization often overlooks quality deviation losses, leading to incomplete strategies. By incorporating hidden quality deviation losses into testing time optimization, enterprises can develop proactive and cost-effective defect prevention strategies27. Promoting Reliability-Oriented Quality Control in identifying key quality characteristics (KQCs) that negatively affect production reliability via the forward QR effect is crucial. Based on the type and significance of these KQCs, appropriate process control charts and capability analysis techniques should be implemented to monitor quality variations5. Special attention is needed for censored KQCs, which remain largely unmonitored and require further investigation to improve control measures.

This study examines the research deficiency in the integration of reliability assurance and quality control in L-PBF gear manufacture. Current research addresses these elements separately; this study integrates them via the RQR chain architecture, creating reciprocal connections across process quality, system reliability, and product performance to provide thorough defect-reliability modelling.

Gear manufacturing from L-PBF process: a case study

Gears play a critical role in transmitting momentum and mechanical power between shafts, ensuring smooth operation throughout the lifespan of the machinery they are part of. However, when operating under adverse conditions, a natural gear damage becomes unavoidable35. Early detection of gear damage is crucial to avoiding undesirable consequences, such as downtime caused by machinery failures and associated financial losses. The American Gear Manufacturers Association (AGMA) has standardized the terminology and classification of various gear defects to aid in their identification and management36.The American Gear Manufacturing Association (AGMA) recently emphasized the need to investigate all aspects of AM steel gear production. While static and fatigue properties of AM materials have been extensively studied, reliable data for designing critical components, such as tooth root bending properties remain untouched or scarce. The design of components like gears and shafts relies on standardized analytical codes, which require reliable resistance data typically provided in standards. However, due to the novelty of AM technologies, such resistance values are not yet available in standard codes, and only limited data on wear behavior exist in the literature23.

The present study utilizes independent, peer-reviewed experimental datasets reported by Tezel et al.23 and Concli et al.36 to demonstrate the applicability of the proposed RQR framework. The Authors also tested the two major failure mode of gear tooth bending fatigue and wear behaviour using standard test rig. Thus, this study employs the data from these studies to find the RQR chain from different AM manufacturing phases. It is important to clarify that no new experimental measurements were conducted within this study. Instead, the selected datasets provide validated and widely accepted benchmark cases for wear and fatigue performance of L-PBF gears. Therefore, the results presented in this work should be interpreted as a methodological demonstration and benchmark validation of the RQR framework rather than direct experimental validation of process improvement. This approach ensures reproducibility and allows the framework to be tested across realistic industrial scenarios while avoiding bias from limited in-house datasets.

This study demonstrates the applicability of the RQR framework using benchmark datasets and literature-based industrial cases. The framework is employed as a decision-support model to analyze the interaction between manufacturing system reliability, process quality, and product reliability in L-PBF gear manufacturing. The integrated benchmark validation demonstrates that the proposed RQR framework can quantitatively reproduce the key fatigue, wear, and reliability degradation trends reported in the literature. The model captures the steeper degradation associated with combined internal and external defects, the beneficial effect of improved inspection, and the reliability enhancement induced by post-processing treatments such as HIP and polishing. The low prediction error and strong benchmark agreement indicate that the framework is numerically stable and physically consistent. Therefore, the proposed architecture is suitable for reliability-oriented process evaluation, defect mitigation analysis, and performance prediction in L-PBF gear manufacturing. Framework generalizability and limitations

This study demonstrates and benchmarks the proposed framework using independent, peer-reviewed datasets, supported by quantitative robustness and sensitivity analyses that confirm consistent reliability behavior under parameter variation. The results should be interpreted as methodological and benchmark validation rather than experimental confirmation, with future work directed toward independent experimental verification. The framework operates based on key quality characteristics (KQCs), defect populations, and degradation parameters, enabling its application to complex geometries-including thin-walled structures, lattice architectures, and cantilever-rich components-through recalibration of geometry-dependent defect sensitivity coefficients.

Applicability to complex geometries The RQR framework is defect-mechanism-driven and independent of specific geometry, allowing application to complex structures by incorporating geometry-specific KQCs such as distortion, residual stress indicators, and crack sensitivity metrics. These parameters account for variations in thermal gradients and structural behavior.

Adaptability across materials The framework is structurally material-agnostic but parameter-dependent, enabling extension across different alloys through recalibration of defect populations and reliability parameters. Variations in thermophysical properties (e.g., thermal conductivity, absorptivity, melting range, and thermal expansion) influence melt-pool dynamics, defect formation, and Weibull parameters (η, β), which are integrated without altering the model structure.

Extension to other AM processes Although developed for L-PBF, the framework can be extended to other AM processes such as EBM and WAAM by incorporating process-specific defect mechanisms and recalibrating defect–reliability relationships while maintaining the system–process–product linkage.

Effectiveness and quantitative defect mitigation The framework accounts for multiple defect types-including lack of fusion, keyhole porosity, balling, residual stress, deformation, and cracks-by representing them as measurable KQCs and integrating them into coupled interior and exterior reliability models. While it does not eliminate defects physically, it provides quantitative mitigation capability by predicting reliability improvements through defect reduction, inspection enhancement, and process optimization. Sensitivity analyses further validate its effectiveness by quantifying the impact of defect intensity, detection probability, and failure kinetics on reliability metrics such a useful life and degradation trends.

Conclusion

This study proposed a Reliability–Quality–Reliability (RQR) chain-based framework to systematically connect material behavior, process parameters, defect formation, and reliability performance in Laser Powder Bed Fusion (L-PBF) Inconel 718 spur gears. The framework enables quantitative propagation of process-induced defects such as porosity, residual stress, and geometric distortion into time-dependent and cycle-dependent reliability predictions, thereby bridging the gap between manufacturing conditions and functional performance. The results indicate that defect evolution is strongly governed by process parameters, particularly laser power, scan speed, and thermal conditions, which influence energy density and melt pool stability. Quantitatively, reliability decreased to approximately 0.2 at 4000 min when only internal defects were considered, whereas the combined presence of internal and surface defects further reduced reliability to about 0.1, approaching near-zero levels at extended durations. This demonstrates that surface-driven degradation mechanisms amplify the reliability loss beyond that caused by internal defects alone. Evaluation of key quality characteristics shows that while mechanical properties remained within acceptable ranges hardness 319 BHN, tensile strength 399 MPa, critical deviations were observed in wear 4.9744 mm versus design tolerance 4.79± 0.394 mm and geometric accuracy 10.702 mm versus nominal 10± 0.5 mm. These deviations are directly attributable to thermally induced residual stresses and process parameter–dependent melt pool instabilities, highlighting that dimensional fidelity and surface integrity are the dominant contributors to reliability degradation in L-PBF gears.

Fatigue and wear analyses further emphasize the role of post-processing in mitigating defect effects. For instance, HIP-treated components exhibited improved fatigue strength (420 MPa at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${10}^{5}$$\end{document} cycles) compared to as-printed (380 MPa) and heat-treated (340 MPa) conditions, while wear reliability in unprocessed components decreased sharply with increasing operational severity. These findings confirm that both process optimization and post-processing strategies are critical levers for enhancing reliability.

The primary novelty of this work lies in the development of an integrated, defect-informed reliability modelling framework that establishes forward–backward traceability between process parameters, defect formation, and product performance. Unlike conventional approaches that treat these aspects independently, the proposed RQR framework enables simultaneous prediction, diagnosis, and comparative evaluation of manufacturing strategies within a unified structure. The present results are based on benchmark datasets and should be interpreted as validation of the modelling approach. Future work will focus on experimental calibration, incorporation of in-situ monitoring data, and development of predictive optimization tools for industrial-scale implementation.
