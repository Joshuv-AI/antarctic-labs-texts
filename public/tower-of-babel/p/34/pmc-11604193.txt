Prediction of the maximum oxygen consumption (VO2max) for young Saudi females using exercise parameters
Authors: Lubna I. Al-Asoom, Marwah M. Almakhaita
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Maximum oxygen consumption (VO2max), an essential indicator of integrated function of the cardiovascular, respiratory, and muscular systems, is an excellent predictor of all-cause morbidity and mortality. Direct measurement is a sophisticated procedure which for some individuals is difficult to perform. The use of international formulae leads to overestimation or underestimation of values. Therefore, the present study aimed to generate a best-fit VO2max prediction formula for young Saudi females.MATERIALS AND METHODS:One hundred and two randomly selected young healthy Saudi females (19–25 years) with normal body mass index (18.5–24.99 kg m−2) and sedentary lifestyle underwent maximum cardiopulmonary exercise testing on cycle ergometer when using breath-by-breath analyzing system. Hemodynamic and ventilatory parameters were assessed before and during the maximum exercise. Significantly correlated exercise and nonexercise parameters enterd in multiple linear regression analysis to find the best-fit model, and used to generate a VO2max prediction formula.RESULTS:The mean measured VO2max was 1449.1 ± 233.92 ml min−1. The VO2max prediction formula was 1304.193 ± 17.902 × body weight + 43.857 × time until exhaustion − 6.83 × maximum heart rate. The mean calculated VO2max was 1449.14 ± 143.97 ml min−1. No statistically significant difference was observed between the measured and calculated VO2max. The Bland–Altman test for the limit of agreement was performed and showed good agreement between the two values with a minute proportional bias. The standard error of the estimate was 156.34, which is approximately 10.6% of the mean measured VO2max. Reliability analysis yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.709.CONCLUSION:This study presents a reliable and valid VO2max prediction formula for young Saudi females. This nationally generated formula is far more representative of the VO2max value than international formulae. We recommend the implementation of this formula and an investigation of its validity in other Saudi population groups.

Introduction

Maximum oxygen consumption (VO2max) is the volume of O2 used by the body during maximum high-intensity exercise. It can also be defined as the product of cardiac output and arteriovenous difference of O2 (a-vO2) at maximum exercise.[1] VO2max is the best indicator for the integrated function of all systems including cardiovascular, respiratory, muscular, blood, and neuroendocrine systems.[2] It has been found that VO2max showed a strong prognostic correlation with all-cause morbidity and mortality. It is the best predictor of future cardiovascular health and related diseases.[3] Therefore, the assessment of VO2max is essential in the evaluation of the health of the population and the estimation of future risk. However, the measurement of VO2max requires sophisticated equipment and trained personnel. The gold standard of VO2max measurement is cardiopulmonary exercise testing, which involves the performance of maximum exercise of a specified protocol with concurrent breath-by-breath analysis of the expired gases.[4] Multiple studies have aimed at predicting VO2max based on the most associated pretest values such as age, sex, body weight (BW), height, and body mass index (BMI). These methods provide some reasonable estimation, yet had a high degree of bias (either overestimation or underestimation).[5] As a result, other maximum exercise parameters have been used to generate predicting formulae for VO2max such as the duration of exercise (time until exhaustion) and maximum heart rate (MHR). Multiple well-known international formulae for the prediction of VO2max have been implemented using the software of the exercise testing setup. However, none of these formulae is legitimate for all populations. Among these international prediction formulae are Wesserman, Jones and Hansen which depends on preexercise data.[6] An attempt for the validation of these formulae was done in Saudi Arabia for young Saudi females, and a significant statistical difference was found between the product of these formulae and the corresponding measured VO2max using Bruce cycle ergometer protocol.[7] Variability of VO2max between different populations is vast, as several factors such as age, sex, lifestyle, genetic and environmental factors have an impact. Information on exercise capacity in Saudi Arabia, specifically for women, is still limited. An earlier study on young Saudi females reported a relatively lower VO2max in this population than the international values. The author attributed this low VO2max to the low physical activity within the culture and predicted a correlation with future cardiovascular risk.[8] Another study demonstrated the existence of specific mutations and SNPs in Saudi females that is associated with low VO2max and related to limitation in cardiac function as a result of altered cardiac muscle development or changes in heart rate (HR).[9] Furthermore, low VO2max in young Saudi females was found to be attributed to a common mutation in the hemoglobin and specifically in α globin (3.7 α-globin deletion).[10] Thus, the population of concern in this study is unique in regard to the genetic and environmental background and the implementation of the international formulae that result in invalid estimates. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to generate a VO2max prediction formula for young Saudi females that implements exercise parameters to generate the best fit and most precise formula.

Materials and Methods

In this cross-sectional study, 102 healthy young Saudi women were recruited using a random sampling technique during the period March 2018–January 2019. The included participants were in the age range of 19–25 years, with normal BMI (18.50–24.99 kg m−2) and a sedentary lifestyle. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) vide Letter No. IRB-PGS-2017-01-219 dated 21/12/2017, and written informed consent was taken from all participants in the study.

The targeted sample size was calculated by G*power 3.1 statistical power analysis software using (Heinrich-Heine-University Dusseldorf, Germany) the following values: Effect size: (0.39), power of the test: 95%, mean VO2max value and the standard deviation (SD) of the null hypothesis H0 are (33.7 ± 10.97 ml min−1 kg−1) which were extrapolated from a previous Saudi study, while the mean VO2max value of the alternative hypothesis was H1 (40 ml min−1 kg-1).[8] Each invited participant was interviewed, and her medical history was recorded to ensure her health status. Height was assessed by a stadiometer and expressed in centimeters (Seca, Hamburg, Germany; Model 217), and body mass was assessed by a digital weight scale and expressed in kilograms (Model 8813306100327; Seca, Hamburg, Germany). BMI was calculated and expressed as weight (kg)/height (m2). Normal BMI was considered in the range of 18.5 ≤ BMI ≤25 according to Asian values.[11] Sedentary lifestyle was checked by the International Physical Activity Questionnaire.[12] An appointment was scheduled for each subject for cardiopulmonary exercise testing. The participants were instructed to avoid caffeic drinks 1 day before the procedure, but to be well hydrated with water. Maximal oxygen consumption was assessed using a COSMED system for cardiopulmonary exercise testing (Quark CPET; COSMED, Rome, Italy; Model C02900-01-04). The detailed procedure was performed as described earlier by Almakhaita et al.[7] This procedure started with an assessment of resting HR and blood pressure after 5 min rest. Then, all subjects performed a maximal exercise test on a bicycle ergometer after a 1-min incremental protocol with instant breath-by-breath analysis. Determination of work rate increment was based on a previously published formula.[13] This incremental protocol included a warm-up period of 3 min-cycling without resistance followed by a stage of 20-watt increment, then an increase in the resistance by 15 Watt every minute by software control. The test was terminated when the subject is limited by depleting symptoms despite verbal encouragement. The cycling frequency was maintained during the test at 50 revolutions per min (rpm). Finally, participants were asked to continue cycling for 3 min without resistance during the recovery period. Time to exhaustion (T) was recorded. HR and blood pressure were monitored every 2 min. Maximum VO2 was determined by the plateau of oxygen consumption when two of the following criteria are achieved: A respiratory exchange ratio ≥1.1, a plateau of oxygen consumption despite an increase in workload, achievement of the age-predicted maximal HR (220–age), and rated perceived exertion (RPE) ≥17.[14] Subjects were excluded from the study if they did not reach VO2max.

Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used to perform statistical analyses. Physical and exercise data were presented as mean ± SD. Pearson’s correlation was performed between all physical, hemodynamic, and ventilatory data at rest and during maximum exercise. All data that showed a significant correlation with the measured VO2max were entered into a linear regression model to generate the best-fit prediction of VO2max. When the best-fit model for VO2max was identified, including only significantly related data, a prediction equation for VO2max was determined. The predicted VO2max values were obtained. A paired t-test was used to compare the measured and calculated VO2max. The limit of agreement was then examined following the Bland–Altman test, and the Cronbach’s reliability coefficient was calculated.

Using the Pearson’s correlation test, the measured VO2max was significantly correlated with age, weight, BMI, time until exhaustion (T), and MHR [Table 4]. These parameters were then entered into the linear regression model. The model was significant with an F = 19.924, P < 0.001 with R = 0616, adjusted R2 change of 36.0%, and standard error of the estimate equal to 187.157 [Table 5]. The model yielded three parameters that showed consistent significant correlation. Based on the correlation coefficients and the constant value [Table 5], we generated the prediction formula as follows: VO2max = 1304.193 ± 17.902 × BW + 43.857 × T–6.83 × MHR (where BW is the body weight, T is the time until exhaustion, and MHR is the MHR).

The formula was applied and the calculated VO2max for all subjects was generated. A paired t-test was used to compare the measured VO2max and the calculated value, and the test showed an insignificant difference between the two values (t = −002, one-sided P = 0.499, two-sided P = 0.998).

The Bland–Altman test was then applied to study the limit of agreement. First, the mean difference between the values of measured VO2max, the corresponding calculated VO2max, and the mean of the means of these two values were obtained.

A one-sample t-test for the difference between the measured and calculated VO2max was performed to test the accuracy of either null hypothesis H°, which states that the difference between the measured and calculated VO2max is equal to zero, or the alternative hypothesis Ha, which states that the difference between the measured and calculated is different from zero. The test showed insignificant results, with P values: one-sided = 0.499 and two-sided = 0.998. Therefore, the null hypothesis is true.

The Bland–Altman test of agreement was structured, as shown in Figure 1. It is a scatter plot of the mean of the measured and calculated VO2max on the X-axis and the difference between these two values on the Y-axis. Three horizontal lines were drawn: the middle line corresponds to the mean of the difference, and the two other lines represent the confidence interval of the difference. Most of the data were scattered around the middle line and within the limits of the confidence interval and the outliers were very limited. This indicates good agreement between the two types of measurements.

Bland–Altman test of agreement. The red line represents the mean of the difference between the measured and calculated VO2max values, and the two green lines represent the 95% confidence interval. Note that 53.1% of the data are above the mean difference and 46.9% below the mean difference

Furthermore, to examine the presence of any proportional bias, a linear regression test was run between the difference between the two values, that is, the measured and the calculated VO2max and the mean of the means of these two values. The test was significant with P < 0.001, R = 0.539, adjusted R2 = 0.284, and standard error of the estimate = 156.34. The test indicated the presence of a proportional bias. However, the ratio of the standard error of the estimate to the mean value of the measured VO2max was 10.6%.

To check internal validation, Cronbach’s alpha was determined and found to be 0.709. This value was reasonable and showed good internal validation.

Discussion

Maximum oxygen consumption is a significant physiological parameter that reflects the integrative functions of multiple body systems. It reflects the health status of cardiovascular, respiratory, and muscular systems. A reduction in VO2max in certain individuals can indicate early signs of defects in any of these systems. Furthermore, determination of the VO2max level in certain populations and an estimation of the range of this value can help the health sector to predict and prevent future risks of cardiovascular, metabolic, and respiratory diseases. There is a dearth of data on maximum oxygen consumption (VO2max) in Saudi Arabia. Besides, there is no reference data for the Saudi population. Furthermore, relevant studies have focused on male individuals and specifically male athletes. Our aim in the current study, therefore, was to generate the best prediction model for VO2max in healthy young Saudi females to facilitate and encourage assessment of this valuable indicator. Moreover, the Saudi government has announced a specific focus on Vision 2030 for female health and its involvement in sports.[15]

The current generated model was carefully selected to reflect the most accurate value of VO2max and included exercise and nonexercise variables, which were time until exhaustion, MHR, and BW. The calculated VO2max values were not statistically different from the measured values, as reflected by the nonsignificant paired t-test. In addition, the one-sample t-test for the mean difference between these two values was not significant. However, the limit of agreement plot and analysis revealed the existence of some proportional bias, particularly for very low and very high values. This proportional bias requires further investigation to test its clinical significance and whether it affects the interpretation of a large segment of this population. Nevertheless, the newly obtained prediction model may be the best for this population. No other VO2max prediction equation is currently available for young Saudi females. An earlier attempt to validate VO2max prediction formulae was performed by Almakhaita et al. This group attempted to validate three international formulae: Wasserman, Jones, and Hansen: Jones equation: VO2 (l/min−1) = −2.26 + 0.025 × height + 0.01 × body mass − 0.018 × age; Hansen/Wasserman equations: VO2 (l/min−1) = 0.001 × height × (14.783–0.11 × age) + 0.006 × body mass (actual-ideal); and Wasserman equation: VO2 (l/min−1) = (body mass + 42.8) × (22.78–0.17 × age)/1000. However, paired t-test for the comparison of the measured VO2max and the predicted using these formulae showed significant difference and overestimation of VO2max.[7] The reliance on international prediction formulae for the estimation of VO2max for different populations failed, revealing unreliable data by multiple trials performed in different populations since VO2max is a population specific variable that is affected by multiple factors such as genetics, environment, lifestyle, and geographical location. In the same context, a study performed in Korea compared the actual measured and predicted VO2max using multiple formulae, including the international formulae of Wasserman, Jones, and Hansen and the regional Chinese formula. The results showed that the Chinese formula yielded the most reliable VO2max data for Korean subjects, while the other formulae showed significant discrepancies. This observation was justified by the authors because of the great similarity in the two populations, i.e., the Chinese and Korean population.[16] Similarly, Ahmadian et al., examined the validity of VO2max prediction using Jones and Wasserman equations for the military on active duty. However, the predicted values were significantly different from the values measured perhaps as a result of the difference in the physical activity and fitness between the active duty military and the sample used for the generation of these formulae.[17]

In Brazil, Almeida et al., compared and validated the use of international equations (Wasserman and Jones) with the Brazilian formula. The comparison showed that the locally generated formula closely correlated with the actual measured VO2max while the values in the international formulae were significantly different from both the measured and calculated values by The Brazilian equation.[18] The Brazilian equation showed a standard error of the estimate of 4.32 ml min−1 kg−1, which accounts for approximately 14.8% of the mean value of the measured VO2max, while our formula showed a standard error of the estimate equal to 156.034, which is approximately 10.8% of the mean value of the actual measured VO2max. Furthermore, the distribution of residues in our data showed almost similar homogenous distribution above and below the mean difference in Bland–Altman plot to that of the Brazilian study (53.1% above and 46.9% below the mean difference line, 50.2% above, and 49.8% below the mean difference line) for our study and the Brazilian study respectively.

Conclusion

A population-specific prediction formula for VO2max is necessary for the facilitation of the assessment of this parameter. Reliance on international formulae for this purpose has proven to be unreliable. We generated a VO2max prediction formula for young Saudi females with a normal BMI and sedentary lifestyle. The product of this formula showed good agreement with the measured values despite a minute proportional error. A clinical assessment of the effect of proportional error requires further assessment to facilitate the dissemination of this formula, particularly with the urgent need to assess VO2max in the current population in Saudi Arabia.

In this study, we aimed to target a specific population of young Saudi females with a normal BMI and sedentary lifestyle to generate a highly precise prediction formula. However, generalization of the use of this prediction formula to other population groups, such as older women, those with higher BMI, or male subjects, might not be applicable and requires further validation.
