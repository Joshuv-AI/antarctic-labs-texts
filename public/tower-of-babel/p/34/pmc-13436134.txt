Association between triglyceride levels and upper urinary tract calcium stones recurrence
Authors: Jiawen Yang, Jun Cai, Yinan Li, Jiangwei Du, Dong Wang, Minchao Qin, Xianlin Xu, Kai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTriglyceride (TG) is a known risk factor for kidney stones, but its association with stone recurrence remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between serum TG levels and recurrence of upper urinary tract calcium stones.MethodPatients with upper urinary tract calcium stones hospitalized between May 2021 and August 2024 were retrospectively enrolled. Multivariable logistic regression was applied to evaluate the association between TG and stone recurrence after adjusting for potential confounders. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was used to explore dose–response relationships. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed to assess result robustness.ResultsA total of 303 patients were included, with a mean age of 46.44 ± 13.04 years and a mean TG level of 1.83 ± 1.43 mmol/L. The overall recurrence rate was 45.54%. Recurrence rates varied across TG quartiles (Q1–Q4: 35.06%, 44.00%, 40.00%, and 63.16%), with the highest rate observed in Q4. Higher TG levels were significantly associated with increased stone recurrence risk. Compared with the lowest quartile, the highest TG quartile showed a 2.98-fold increased risk (95% CI: 1.42–6.39). RCS analysis demonstrated a linear dose–response relationship. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings.ConclusionElevated triglyceride levels were associated with a higher risk of calcium stone recurrence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05083-w.

Introduction

Kidney stone disease (KSD), as a common urinary system disorder, has a worldwide prevalence ranging from 1% to 13% [1]. Surgical management is effective for removing existing stones (symptomatic treatment) but does not prevent recurrence of the disease [2]. Its annual recurrence rate is between 10% and 20%, and the recurrence rate within 5 years is about 50%, imposing a substantial burden on society and patients [3]. Therefore, preventing stone recurrence is essential to mitigate both the clinical burden on patients and the economic burden on healthcare systems.

Nephrolithiasis can cause a spectrum of sequelae, from acute renal colic and hematuria to chronic complications like obstructive uropathy and renal impairment. There are several types of kidney stones and the most common stone types are calcium-based, primarily calcium oxalate (CaOx) and calcium phosphate (CaP) [4]. Obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome are recognized as key risk factors for nephrolithiasis [5]. Inci et al., have reported that elevated body mass index (BMI), hypercholesterolemia, and hyperlipidemia may be associated with the formation of different types of urinary stones [6]. Dyslipidemia is also recognized as an independent risk factor for nephrolithiasis in recent years [7]. TG, as a central component of dyslipidemia, is a well-established risk factor for several major health conditions, including cardiovascular disease and acute pancreatitis [8]. In addition, another study confirmed that high serum TG is a dose-dependent risk factor for T2DM [9]. Meanwhile, accumulating evidence in recent years has also suggested that hypertriglyceridemia and related metabolic parameters are associated with the risk and recurrence of kidney stones [10–14]. Kang et al. reported that hypertriglyceridemia was associated with an increased risk of stone recurrence in patients with urolithiasis [10]. Subsequently, Hung et al. demonstrated that dyslipidemia increased the risk of incident kidney stone disease in a large Taiwanese population [11]. A single-center case-control study by Feng et al. further confirmed the association between metabolic syndrome components and urinary stone recurrence [14]. However, most of these studies included all stone types without restricting to specific compositions. Given that calcium-containing stones account for approximately 80% of urinary stones and have distinct metabolic features, the present study specifically focuses on the association between triglyceride levels and calcium stone recurrence in a Chinese cohort.

In our previous meta-analysis, no significant association was observed between hyperlipidemia and kidney stone recurrence [15]. However, we speculate that this null finding may be attributable to the lack of stratification by stone composition in the included studies. Therefore, we conducted this study to explore the correlation between TG and calcium stone recurrence, thereby potentially providing a novel perspective for preventing calcium stone recurrence.

Methods

Study population

We initially identified 399 patients from the electronic medical record of Sir Run Run Hospital Nanjing Medical University, covering the period from May 2021 to August 2024. All included patients had undergone surgical stone removal at the hospital and their clinical data were collected by reviewing medical records. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by Sir Run Run Hospital Nanjing Medical University Ethics Committee (2022-SR-042). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants. From the initial 399 patients, we excluded 1 patient aged < 18 years old, 68 patients having non-calcium kidney stones, and 27 patients without TG measurements. Consequently, 303 patients were included in our analysis (Fig. 1).

Exposure and outcome definitions

Serum TG level was designed as the exposure variable in this study and was measured from fasting blood samples collected at the time of initial hospital admission for the index stone event, prior to any surgical intervention. The recurrence of kidney calcium stones was treated as an outcome variable. Based on the medical record system, stone recurrence was defined as a case in which, following successful initial stone expulsion therapy, the patient underwent a subsequent stone removal procedure for a radiologically confirmed new stone (e.g., on KUB or CT) at least six months later.

Stone classification definition

Kidney stones were classified according to the guidelines from the European Association of Urology and the Mayo Clinic stone analysis practice [16]. Infrared spectroscopy was employed for stone composition analysis, with all results being reviewed and confirmed by a staff urologist at the Sir Run Run Hospital Nanjing Medical University. The specific criteria were as follows: CaOx stones were defined when any form of CaOx (whewellite and/or weddellite) constituted ≥ 50% of the stone’s composition. CaP stones were defined when apatite, brushite (calcium hydrogen phosphate dihydrate), or amorphous CaP constituted the majority (≥ 50%) of the stone. Uric acid (UA) stones were defined when UA or UA dihydrate accounted for ≥ 50% of the stone composition. Infection stones (Struvite) were defined by the presence of ≥ 10% struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate), ammonium acid urate, or mono-sodium urate monohydrate. Cystine stones were defined as those in which any detectable amount of cystine was present.

Covariates

The covariates included in the present study contained age, gender, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, family history of kidney stones, fasting blood glucose (FBG), serum creatinine, serum UA, total cholesterol, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), urine specific gravity, hydroureter, hydronephrosis, maximum stone size, stone density, number of stones, stone composition, stone location, and stone classification. Hypertension was defined by a documented history, active treatment, or measured blood pressure values of ≥ 140 mmHg systolic or ≥ 90 mmHg diastolic [17]. Diabetes was defined based on either a documented diagnosis, current use of glucose-lowering medication, or a fasting blood glucose level of ≥ 7.0 mmol/L [18]. All laboratory tests for the enrolled participants were performed uniformly at the central laboratory of the Clinical Laboratory department in our hospital, including a complete blood count, hepatic and renal function panels, and a urinalysis. All laboratory reports were reviewed and verified by certified clinical laboratory physicians prior to finalization. The eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine equation [19]. Specifically, hypertriglyceridemia was defined as a serum triglyceride level > 1.7 mmol/L [20, 21]. Stone characteristics, including size, location, and number, were assessed based on preoperative imaging findings from computed tomography (CT). Based on the predominant crystalline component, calcium stones were classified as CaOx stones and CaP stones. Meanwhile, calcium stones were further subclassified into pure calcium stones (composed of a single type of calcium salt) and mixed calcium stones (containing multiple crystalline components).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations, and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. TG were categorized into quartiles (Q1-Q4) for intergroup comparisons, with the lowest quartile (Q1) serving as the reference group. Multivariable logistic regression models were performed to assess the association between TG and the risk of calcium stone recurrence. Model 1 was unadjusted for any covariates. Model 2 was adjusted for gender, age and BMI. Model 3 additionally included variables that showed a statistically significant association with stone recurrence in baseline characteristics (P < 0.05), specifically: serum uric acid, hypertension, total cholesterol, and stone location. Violin plots were employed to compare the distribution of TG among various clinical groups. Subsequently, we employed RCS analysis with 4 knots to assess the dose-response relationship between TG levels and calcium stone recurrence risk, while adjusting for covariates included in the multivariable models. Subgroup and interaction analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of the association between TG level and calcium stone recurrence across different subgroups. To confirm our main findings, we performed a series of sensitivity analyses by respectively excluding the following groups of patients: (1) those with extreme triglyceride values (defined as the top and bottom 5% of the distribution); (2) those with hypercalcemia; (3) those with hyperuricemia; and (4) those with renal failure. This stepwise approach allowed us to examine whether the observed association remained stable after removing these potential confounding subsets. Data analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.4.1). A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. The study finally included 303 participants, with a mean ± SD age of 46.44 ± 13.04 years, 71.29% were male. The mean TG level was 1.83 mmol/L, with a SD of 1.43 mmol/L. The interquartile ranges for TG were 0.50–1.03 mmol/L (Q1), 1.03–1.42 mmol/L (Q2), 1.42–2.13 mmol/L (Q3), and 2.13–11.74 mmol/L (Q4). The overall calcium stone recurrence rate was 45.54%. When analyzed by TG quartile, the calcium stone recurrence rate varied across quartiles, with the highest rate (63.16%) observed in Q4, although rates in Q2 (44.00%) and Q3 (40.00%) were similar. Among the variables analyzed, statistically significant differences were found in gender, BMI, serum UA, total cholesterol, triglycerides, hypertriglyceridemia, hypertension, and stone location (P < 0.05).

Violin plots illustrated a significant difference in the distribution of serum TG level between patients with initial stone episodes and those with recurrence (P < 0.01, Fig. 2). This statistical distinction indicated that elevated serum TG level may associate with calcium stone recurrence. In contrast, serum TG level distributions showed no significant differences across other demographic or clinical subgroups (Fig. 2).

The association between TG and the risk of calcium stone recurrence

Logistic regression models were conducted to further identify the association between serum TG level and the risk of calcium stone recurrence. As shown in Table 2, a positive association was observed across models. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), every unit increase in TG was associated with a 29% higher risk of calcium stone recurrence (OR = 1.29; 95% CI: 1.05–1.63; P = 0.022). This positive association remained statistically significant when serum TG levels were analyzed as quartiles. Compared with individuals in the lowest TG quartile (Q1), those in the highest quartile (Q4) had a 2.98-fold higher risk of calcium stone recurrence (OR = 2.98, 95% CI: 1.42–6.39, P = 0.004). We also found a similar relationship between hypertriglyceridemia and kidney stone recurrence, with patients having a 77% higher risk compared to those with normal serum TG levels (OR = 1.77; 95% CI: 1.05–2.99; P = 0.031).

Subgroup analysis

To assess the consistency of the observed association, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by age, gender, BMI, diabetes, and hypertension(Fig. 4). We found an elevated calcium stone recurrence risk associated with higher serum TG levels across most subgroups. Each unit increase in serum TG was significantly associated with a 36% increase in the risk of calcium stone recurrence in participants aged under 60 years (OR: 1.36, 95% CI: 1.07–1.73). Consistent results were obtained across the subgroups of normal weight individuals, males, and non-hypertensive patients. In the interaction test, we found a significant effect modification by hypertension on the association between serum TG and calcium stone recurrence (P for interaction = 0.046). However, the interaction between TG and hypertension status was not significant after FDR correction (corrected P = 0.230).

Sensitivity analysis

We performed a series of sensitivity analyses based on the fully adjusted model to evaluate the robustness of our findings (Table S1). Under different exclusion criteria, these analyses sequentially excluded: (1) individuals with extreme triglyceride values (top and bottom 5%); (2) patients with hypercalcemia; (3) patients with hyperuricemia; and (4) patients with renal failure. Across all of these analyses, the positive association between elevated TG level and calcium stone recurrence risk persisted robustly, indicating that higher serum TG level may serve as a significant and independent risk factor for calcium stone recurrence.

Discussion

In this retrospective study of clinical patients presenting with calcium kidney stones, we enrolled 303 participants. The analysis revealed that patients with higher TG levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of calcium stone recurrence. After adjusting for gender, age, BMI, serum UA, hypertension, total cholesterol, and stone location, patients in the highest triglyceride quartile (Q4) were found to have a 198% higher risk of calcium stone recurrence compared to those in the lowest quartile (Q1). This positive association remained consistent across subgroups stratified by gender, BMI, diabetes, and hypertension, and was consistently validated through sensitivity analyses. Further analysis using RCS curve indicated a linear relationship, where the risk of calcium stone recurrence increased steadily with higher TG levels.

TG, as a core component of dyslipidemia and metabolic syndrome, plays a pathophysiological role in a spectrum of diseases [22, 23]. In the large Bezafibrate Infarction Prevention trial (N > 15,000), elevated serum TG level was independently linked to increased all-cause mortality [24]. Similarly, a large Danish cohort study enrolled 7,587 women and 6,394 men demonstrated that elevated TG levels were an independent predictor of myocardial infarction and ischemic heart disease [25]. The risk of acute pancreatitis, a severe and often fatal disease, exhibits a dose-dependent relationship with the degree of hypertriglyceridemia [20]. Increasingly, accumulating evidence supports a role for dyslipidemia in KSD, potentially mediated through altered lipoprotein metabolism and systemic inflammation [7, 10]. Kidney stone formation is influenced by multiple factors such as age, gender, and diet, the stones are fundamentally composed of crystals and organic matrix, the latter of which contains appreciable levels of lipids [26, 27]. Specifically, uric acid and calcium oxalate stones are characterized by a higher content of lipids such as cholesterol and phospholipids [28]. Studies have indicated that these lipids play a crucial role in crystal nucleation [28, 29]. Dyslipidemia is associated with higher rates of stone recurrence and multiple stone formation and TG is an integral component of dyslipidemia [30]. A 7-year prospective cohort study in an Asian population demonstrated that hypertriglyceridemia was associated with an increased risk of kidney stone formation [31]. Individuals with hypertriglyceridemia often associated with obesity, tend to consume higher amounts of calcium and salt but less water, leading to increased urinary calcium excretion. Furthermore, patients with hypertriglyceridemia exhibit elevated uric acid and oxalate levels along with lower urine pH, which may collectively promote the formation of calcium stones [7]. Hypertriglyceridemia is frequently implicated as a component of the insulin resistance (IR) and IR contributes to lithogenesis by reducing urinary citrate excretion, thereby favoring the nucleation and growth of calcium stones [32].

This study reveals a positive association between serum TG levels and calcium stone recurrence. In the subgroup analysis, we found a positive association in normal-weight individuals, suggesting that the mechanism by which TG promotes calcium stone recurrence may be independent of the classic obesity-insulin resistance pathway. These findings underscore the necessity of individualized risk assessment and prevention.

While this study has several strengths, including its clinically derived patient data, comprehensive adjustment to account for potential confounders, and rigorous sensitivity analyses that reinforce the robustness of the findings, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the definition of stone recurrence in this study was based on subsequent surgical intervention for radiologically confirmed new stones, which inevitably introduced detection bias, as asymptomatic recurrences, spontaneously passed stones, or cases managed conservatively were not included. Consequently, the reported recurrence rate may underestimate the true incidence. Furthermore, as patients with higher triglyceride levels are more likely to experience symptoms or undergo surgical procedures, the observed association between triglyceride levels and recurrence could be overestimated or biased. Second, despite comprehensive adjustment for known confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured or unknown factors may still influence the observed associations. Our study did not collect data on post-surgical preventive interventions, including thiazide diuretics, potassium citrate, dietary counseling, or fluid intake. These factors are known to influence stone recurrence risk and may also correlate with serum triglyceride levels. In addition, we excluded 24.06% of initially identified patients, primarily due to non-calcium stones or missing TG data. As shown in Table S2, the recurrence rate was 45.54% in the included group and 46.88% in the excluded group, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.912). However, the possibility of selection bias cannot be fully excluded. Patients with missing TG data or non-calcium stones may differ in unmeasured factors that could influence the observed association between TG levels and calcium stone recurrence. Therefore, residual confounding due to these unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Third, all the population in this study was Asian and the generalizability of our findings may be limited. In addition, as a single-center study, our findings are susceptible to potential selection bias and require validation in future multi-center studies across different regions. In our study, the median follow-up duration was 19 months (IQR: 13–25 months), with a range of 9 to 48 months. No significant difference in follow-up duration was observed between patients without recurrence and those with recurrence [20 (13–26) vs. 18 (13–23) months, P = 0.131]. However, we cannot fully exclude the possibility of observation-time bias. While a time-to-event analysis would have been more appropriate, we chose this parsimonious approach given the exploratory nature and sample size limitations. Furthermore, serum TG levels were measured only once at the time of the first stone event, with no follow-up measurements. Because our study relied on a single baseline measurement and did not account for variable observation time, we cannot establish a temporal or causal relationship between TG levels and stone recurrence. Future prospective studies should employ standardized follow-up protocols and time-to-event analyses to confirm the temporal relationship.

Conclusion

In this retrospective, single-center study, elevated triglyceride levels were associated with an increased risk of calcium stone recurrence. The finding suggests a potential link between triglyceride levels and recurrence. However, future prospective studies are still required to further clarify the causal role.
