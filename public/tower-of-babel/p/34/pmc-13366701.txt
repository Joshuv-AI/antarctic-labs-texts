Biofortification with selenium and melatonin improves seed yield and quality of Phaseolus vulgaris L. under drought stress by enhancing physiological and antioxidant responses
Authors: Zohre Amini, Mohammad Mehdi Rahimi, Mehdi Hosseinifarahi, Seyed Mashaallah Hosseini, Abolfath Moradi, Moslem Abdipour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBeans are one of the main crops in Eqlid County, northern Fars Province, where they were cultivated more than 18,000 hectares. To investigate the effect of melatonin (Mt) and selenium (Se) on biofortification, seed yield, and yield components of common bean under drought stress, a two-year experiment was conducted using a split-plot factorial arrangement within a randomized complete block design with three replications at the Agricultural and Natural Resources Research Station of Eqlid County in northern Fars Province. The main factor included three levels of drought stress (T1: no stress, T2: irrigation after 80 mm evaporation from a Class A pan as moderate stress, and T3: irrigation after 100 mm evaporation from a Class A pan as severe stress). The first sub-factor comprised three levels of Mt (Mt1 = 0, Mt2 = 100, Mt3 = 200 µM), and the second sub-factor involved foliar application of Se as sodium selenite at two levels (Se1 = 0, Se2 = 5 mg/L). Recorded traits included seed yield, number of pods per plant, 100-seed weight, protein, proline, superoxide dismutase, catalase, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, total phenol, and selenium concentration in seeds.ResultsResults showed that seed yield (T1 = 2591.1 > T2 = 1850 > T3 = 877.8 kg/ha), pod number/plant (T1 = 9.33 > T2 = 7.21 > T3 = 6.54) and 100-seed weight (T1 = 38 > T2 = 35.7 > T3 = 33.9 g) declined under waer deficit stress but improved with increasing Mt and Se concentrations (T1 = 4023.3, T2 = 3513.3, T3 = 1837.8 kg/ha, T1 = 14.8, T2 = 14.3, T3 = 12 and T1 = 45.7, T2 = 43.9, T3 = 40.9 g). Additionally, antioxidant enzymes activity (Superoxide dismutase and Catalase) increased in stressed plants with higher Mt and Se levels compared to control (by 179 and 58% for T1, 248 and 69% for T2), indicating an enhanced defense response. Although the highest seed yield and yield components were obtained under water deficit stress conditions, considering drought-induced yield reduction, the application of 200 µM Mt and 5 mg/L Se may be cost-effective.ConclusionOverall, the results suggest that the use of Mt and Se could serve as an effective strategy in managing water deficit stress and improving crop performance in agricultural environments with challenges.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08965-1.Keywords: Biofortification, Common bean, Drought stress, Melatonin, Selenium

Introduction

Common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) is an annual, self-pollinating plant that serves as a staple food and provides a significant source of protein in the human diet [1]. This species is highly sensitive to soil and water conditions, such that even brief periods of stress can impair its biological and economic performance. Given the nutritional value of beans in human diets and their economic importance through export, it is essential to identify and introduce drought-tolerant strategies capable of withstanding extended periods of water scarcity, thereby allowing for water conservation while achieving acceptable yields [2].

Among the various methods to mitigate the effects of drought stress, i.e. increase drought tolerance in plants, exogenous plant growth regulators have been identified as a promising approach. For example, the use of methionine (Mt) has been known to be an effective solution in these conditions. Mt as a plant growth regulator exists in almost all living organisms and can mitigate the effects of abiotic stresses such as temperature, salinity, drought, and aging by affecting various plant physiological processes [3].

Studies show that it improves photosynthetic efficiency in plants under water deficit stress, protects PSII proteins against oxidative damage and regulates the levels of senescence-related proteins[4]. Chloroplasts are the main source of free radicals in plants and require strong protection against oxidative stress. It has been shown that Mt biosynthesis in plants may occur in chloroplasts. Previous studies indicate that the application of exogenous Mt can induce the biosynthesis of endogenous Mt in chloroplasts [5]. In wheat, the influence of the exogenous application of Mt showed that it promoted the growth and yield under water deficit conditions by regulating the antioxidant enzymes, reactive oxygen species, and ions [6]. The results of this study suggest a positive association between Mt-induced improved growth attributes and drought tolerance. It also documented that Mt-induced regulation of growth, secondary metabolites and oxidative defense system in sunflower (Helianthus annuus L.) plants subjected to water deficit stress[7]. Overall, foliar applied Mt was effective to improve drought stress tolerance of sunflower plants and reduce relative membrane permeability (RMP), malondialdehyde (MDA) and H2O2 contents under water deficit conditions. In canola (Brassica napus L.) plants, supplementation of Mt mitigates drought stress through the modulation of physio-biochemical attributes and key antioxidant enzymes, and oxidative stress management[8]. These researchers concluded that the foliar applied Mt can improve the drought tolerance of canola plants in terms of growth, seed yield, and oxidative defense system.

Selenium (Se) is an essential trace element for humans and animals, playing a vital role in several biological processes. Although its role in plants is not yet fully understood, research indicates that it is involved in plant physiology and metabolism. Se plays an important antioxidant role in both plants and humans, making it an essential trace mineral in the human body. Additionally, it is crucial to the metabolism of plant cells Unlike animals and humans, Se is not essential for plants, but at low concentrations, it is beneficial for plant growth and stress tolerance. Furthermore, Se has been shown to increase the content of proline, carotenoids, and chlorophyll in plants while reducing the production of ROS, such as hydroxyl radicals [9]. Foliage applied Se on wheat showed that drought stress impaired the wheat productivity while foliar application of Se (25 or 50 mM) considerably improved wheat yield and WUE of wheat due to notable expansion in gas exchange traits and anti-oxidant potential of wheat subjected to drought stress[10]. In tomatoes, foliar application of Se enhances drought tolerance by modulating the antioxidative system and restoring photosynthesis[11]. These findings offer valuable insights into the mechanisms governing Se-induced drought tolerance in tomatoes. Se application in rice seedlings has also been associated with favorable results and improved antioxidant response and photosynthesis in fragrant rice (Oryza sativa L.) seedlings during drought stress[12].

The interaction between Se and Mt has been well-documented for other abiotic and biotic stresses, suggesting a powerful partnership that could plausibly extend to drought conditions. For example, in tomato plants, a combined treatment of Se and Mt was 74.05% effective in controlling postharvest gray mold disease, a result significantly better than either treatment alone[13]. This synergy boosted antioxidant enzymes and defense-related gene expression. Research on cadmium stress in tomatoes found that Se's protective effect depends on Mt. When Mt biosynthesis was inhibited, Se's ability to alleviate cadmium toxicity was compromised[14]. Se application also can increase the endogenous levels of Mt in plants by upregulating key biosynthesis genes (like T5H and COMT), as shown in cucumbers under cold stress[15].

No published studies were found that specifically test Mt and Se combined application for drought tolerance in crops. Given their synergy in other stresses, co-application for drought is a highly promising but untested hypothesis. Given the lack of direct studies on drought, our question points to a valuable research gap. Therfore, this study aimed to evaluate the individual and interactive effects of Mt and Se on seed yield, yield components, antioxidant defense and seed Se enrichment of common bean under water deficit stress.

Materials and methods

To investigate the effects of Se and Mt on biofortification, yield, and yield components of common beans under drought stress, a two-year field experiment was implemented at the research field in Eqlid County, northern Fars Province. The study followed a split-factorial arrangement based on a randomized complete block design, with three replications (Fig S1). The main factor included three levels of drought stress: (T1) no stress, (T2) irrigation after 80 mm of evaporation from the Class A evaporation pan (moderate stress), and (T3) irrigation after 100 mm of evaporation (severe stress). Water deficit stress was applied after the seedlings were fully established and from the time of the appearance of the third three bean leaflets (stage V4) to the maturity stage based on 80 and 100 mm evaporation from the surface of the class A evaporation pan.

In Eqlid County, the pre-flowering stage of bean cultivation coincides with the final irrigation of wheat, resulting in temporary water scarcity in the fields. The first sub-factor included three levels of Mt (Mt1 = 0, Mt2 = 100, Mt3 = 200 µM), while the second sub-factor involved foliar application of Se from sodium selenite (Na2SeO3) at two levels (Se1 = 0, Se2 = 5 mg/L). Seeds of the common bean variety ‘Saleh’ (Saleh as local variety is one of the most productive and prolific varieties of the Cranberry class; a variety with a growth of 85 to 95 cm, a 90-day period and an average yield of 3.35 tons per hectare. Due to its grain shape, excellent ripening and high marketability, it has been among the main choices of professional farmers for years) were sourced from the Agricultural and Natural Resources Research Station in Eqlid.

Each plot (2m × 5 m = 10m2) consisted of four planting rows, 5 m in length, with 50 cm of spacing between plots and 2 m between blocks (Fig S1). Before plowing, soil samples were collected from multiple points at a depth of 0 to 30 cm to create a composite sample, which was sent to the laboratory for analysis of physical and chemical properties (Table 1). The land was disked, leveled using a land planer, and shaped into furrows and ridges with a furrower.

Measured characteristics

Number of pods per plant

The number of pods per plant was counted on five plants, and the average was recorded for each plot.

100-seed weight

After final harvest, 100 seeds from each plot were collected and weighed.

Seed yield

The yield was determined by weighing seeds collected from an area of 1 square meter from each plot and extrapolating this value to a hectare.

Se content in bean seeds

Se was measured using atomic absorption spectroscopy with hydride generation using sodium borohydride [16].

Protein content

Protein content in powdered plant samples was measured using the Kjeldahl method. The total nitrogen percentage was calculated using the following formula:

%TN = (T − B) S × N × 141,000 × 100%TN = S(T − B)​ × N × 100,014​ × 100.

where TN represents total nitrogen percentage, T is the acid consumed for titration of the sample in mm, B is the acid consumed for the blank, S is the sample weight (g), and N is the normality of sulfuric acid (5%). To determine total protein in each sample, the nitrogen value obtained from the Kjeldahl method was multiplied by a factor of 5.11[17]. The results were then reported in mg/g of sample weight.

Antioxidant enzymes

Catalase (CAT)

Catalase enzyme activity was measured at 25 °C using a spectrophotometer (160-Shimadzu UV) by the following method [18]. The reaction mixture consisted of potassium phosphate buffer with pH 7.0 (3000 µL), hydrogen peroxide 5 µL of 3.41 M, and enzyme extract (100 µL). Enzyme activity was expressed as µmol min⁻1 mg−1.

Superoxide Dismutase (SOD)

SOD activity was measured photometrically according to the method described by [19]. The reaction buffer consisted of phosphate buffer with pH 7.8 (100 mM), methionine (12 mM), nitro blue tetrazolium (75 µM), EDTA (micromolar), and Triton X-100 (0.025%). Each well was filled with 290 µL of the main buffer. Then, 5 µL of 2 µM riboflavin buffer was added to the reaction mixture, and the device was calibrated at 560 nm. For each sample measurement, 10 µL of protein extract was used. This reaction was determined based on the reduction of nitro blue tetrazolium and the ability of the SOD enzyme to inhibit this reaction. Enzyme activity was expressed in units per minute per milligram of protein (U min⁻1 mg−1).

Proline content

Leaf proline content was determined using the method described by [20]. Initially, 0.2 g of fresh leaf sample was ground in 10 mL of 3% sulfosalicylic acid. The resulting extract was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. 2 mL of the clear extract was transferred to sealed test tubes, and 2 mL of ninhydrin reagent and 2 mL of glacial acetic acid were added to each tube. After sealing the tubes, they were placed in a water bath at 100 °C for one hour. Once cooled, 4 mL of toluene was added to each tube. The proline concentration was determined using a spectrophotometer at 520 nm and based on a standard curve and reported in milligrams per gram of fresh tissue (mg g−1 FW).

Total phenolic compounds

Phenolic compounds were quantified using the method described by Y Velioglu, G Mazza, L Gao and B Oomah [21]. A 0.1-g sample was extracted with 5 mL of 80% methanol containing 1% hydrochloric acid and shaken for 2 h at room temperature to ensure complete extraction. The mixture was then centrifuged at 3000 g, and the supernatant was used to determine total phenolic compounds. To 100 µL of the supernatant, 750 µL of Folin reagent was added, and the resulting mixture was kept at room temperature for 5 min. Subsequently, 750 µL of 6% sodium carbonate was added. After 90 min, the absorbance of each sample was read at 725 nm. The concentration of total phenolic compounds was calculated using a standard curve of gallic acid and reported in milligrams per gram of fresh weight (mg g−1 FW).

Phenylalanine ammonialyase (PAL) activity

PAL activity was assessed based on the amount of cinnamic acid produced [22]. For this purpose, 300 mg of sample tissue was ground with 6.5 mL of 50 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 8.8) containing 15 mM β-mercaptoethanol. The extracted mixture was then centrifuged at 50,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used for enzyme activity measurement. In this assay, phenylalanine was used as the enzymatic substrate, and PAL activity was estimated based on the rate of cinnamic acid formation. In a test tube, 1 mL of extraction buffer, 0.5 mL of 10 mM L-phenylalanine, 0.4 mL of distilled water, and 1 mL of enzyme extract were mixed and incubated at 37 °C for one hour. The reaction was terminated by adding 0.5 mL of 6 M hydrochloric acid. The resulting product was extracted with 15 mL of ethyl acetate, which was then evaporated. The residue was dissolved in 3 mL of 0.05 M sodium hydroxide, and the concentration of cinnamic acid was measured by reading the absorbance at 290 nm, using an extinction coefficient of 9500 M⁻1 cm⁻1. One unit of PAL activity was defined as the production of one micromole of cinnamic acid per minute. The specific activity of the enzyme was reported as units per milligram of protein (U mg−1 protein).

Total chlorophyll content

Chlorophyll was extracted from the flag leaf using acetone, following a modified version of the method described by [23]. The procedure is as follows:

Sample preparation

0.5 g of frozen flag leaf, ground in liquid nitrogen, was mixed with 0.5 g of magnesium sulfate and 10 mL of 80% acetone in a mortar. This mixture was ground thoroughly.

Filtration and centrifugation

The resulting extract was filtered through Whatman filter paper into test tubes. The samples were then centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 2 min to obtain a homogeneous extract.

Dim lighting and temperature

Throughout the extraction and measurement, all operations were conducted under dim light at 4 °C to prevent chlorophyll degradation.

Spectrophotometric measurement

The absorbance was measured using a spectrophotometer (Varian 300 Scan, USA) at 645 nm and 663 nm. The concentrations of chlorophyll a, b, and total chlorophyll were calculated using the following formulas:

Chlorophyll a content (mg/g FW)

Chlorophyll a (mg/g) = (W × 1000)V × (Absorbance at 645 nm × 69.2 − Absorbance at 663 nm × 7.121000)Chlorophyll a (mg/g) = V(W × 1000)​ × (1000Absorbance at 645 nm × 69.2 − Absorbance at 663 nm × 7.12​).

Chlorophyll b content (mg/g FW)

Chlorophyll b (mg/g) = (W × 1000)V × (Absorbance at 663 nm × 69.4 − Absorbance at 645 nm × 9.221000)Chlorophyll b (mg/g) = V(W × 1000)​ × (1000Absorbance at 663 nm × 69.4 − Absorbance at 645 nm × 9.22​).

Total chlorophyll content (mg/g FW)

Total Chlorophyll (mg/g) = (W × 1000)V × (Absorbance at 663 nm × 8.02 + Absorbance at 645 nm × 9.201000)Total Chlorophyll (mg/g) = V(W × 1000)​ × (1000Absorbance at 663 nm × 8.02 + Absorbance at 645 nm × 9.20​).

In these equations, VV is the final volume of the extracted sample, and WW is the fresh weight of the sample [24].

Statistical analysis

Data normality and homogeneity of variances were checked, and data were analyzed based on combined analysis of variance. Duncan's multiple range test (MRT) was used to compare the significance of differences between means. Duncan's test uses varying significance levels based on the rank range between means. It prioritizes finding potential differences over strict control of overall false positives. The pre-specified risk level for a Type I error per comparison was α = 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed with SAS software v.9.4 [25].

Results

Seed yield and its components

The results of the combined ANOVA for seed yield, number of pods per plant, and 100-seed weight are shown in Table 2. Drought stress significantly (P ≤ 0.05) affected seed yield and its components in bean plants (Table 2). However, the application of Se and/or Mt alleviated the effects of drought stress and improved these variables under stress conditions. Among the different levels of Mt, the 200 µM concentration produced the highest yield, suggesting a positive effect of Mt on seed yield under stress (Table 3). Additionally, among sodium selenite treatments, the highest yield was observed at a concentration of 5 mg/L, which may be attributed to the role of this element in enhancing metabolic activities and improving plant resilience (Table 4). The highest seed yield was obtained under non-stress conditions (Tables 3 and 4), highlighting the importance of optimal conditions for maximizing seed production. Under non-stress conditions, an average increase of 55.3% in seed yield was observed when plants were supplemented with 5 mg/L of Se and 200 μM of Mt compared to the control (Table 5).

A similar trend was observed for the number of pods per plant and 100-seed weight (Table 2). Treatments containing Mt and/or Se resulted in a greater number of pods and higher 100-seed weight compared to the control, likely due to the important roles of these compounds in improving growth and mitigating the adverse effects of drought stress (Tables 3 and 4). These findings highlight the importance of providing suitable growth conditions and the positive nutritional impact of these compounds on seed quality. As shown in Table 5, the highest values for both the number of pods per plant and 100-seed weight were recorded in plants supplemented with 5 mg/L of Se and 200 μM of Mt.

Seed protein

The findings indicated that elevated drought stress levels led to a decrease in seed protein content (Table 2), which may have contributed to reductions in various growth parameters. Notably, exogenous application of Se or Mt significantly enhanced seed protein under both drought and non-drought conditions (Tables 3 and 4). We found that the application of 200 µM of Mt and 5 mg/L of Se under non-stress conditions enhanced seed protein content with the maximum increase of 20.7% compared to the control (Table 5). Similarly, under moderate and severe drought stress, Se and Mt significantly increased seed protein by 36.3% and 22.1%, respectively.

Proline

Proline was significantly affected by drought stress (Table 2), with levels increasing under more severe stress conditions (Table 5). It was also affected by Mt and Se application, so 49.3% and 47.2% increases were observed with the application of 200 µM Mt and 5 mg/L Se, respectively, compared to the control (Tables 3 and 4). The highest proline content (2.37 mg g−1 FW) was recorded with the combined application of 200 µM Mt and 5 mg/L Se under severe drought stress. It was about 96.6% higher than that of the control (Table 5).

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) enzyme activities

The results of combined ANOVA for SOD and CAT enzyme activities are presented in Table 6. The SOD and CAT enzymes were stimulated by drought stress, with mean values reaching 0.3416 U min−1 mg−1 and 0.5135 µ mol min−1 mg−1, respectively, compared to the control, whose mean SOD and CAT activities were 0.1377 U min−1 mg−1 and 0.3546 µ mol min−1 mg−1, respectively (Tables 7 and 8). Regarding the response of SOD and CAT enzymes to Se and Mt application, their activities increased at higher Se and Mt rates (Table 9). As shown in Tables 10 and 11, a greater increase in enzyme activity was observed with Se and/or Mt application under drought stress conditions compared to non-drought stress conditions.

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) and total phenols

Our results (Table 6) showed that PAL and total phenols were significantly affected by drought stress. As shown in Tables 10 and 11, PAL significantly decreased by drought stress compared to the control, while total phenol increased. This increase, especially under drought stress, was higher compared to non-stress conditions. The increase in total phenol in drought stress indicates that the plant attempts to alleviate stress by enhancing phenolic compounds. We also observed a significant increase in PAL and total phenol by Se or Mt application under non-stress and stress conditions (Tables 10 and 11). As expected, the increase in PAL and total phenols with Se or Mt application was greater under stress conditions than under non-stress conditions. However, the highest PAL and total phenols were found when Se and Mt were used together. These results support the existence of a synergistic effect for the simultaneous application of Se and Mt (Table 7).

Total chlorophyll

Total chlorophyll was influenced by drought stress, Mt, Se, and their interaction (Table 6). It significantly decreased under drought stress compared to normal conditions, whereas Mt and Se alleviated the adverse effects of drought stress (Tables 10 and 11). However, chlorophyll levels significantly increased by the application of Mt and Se, especially under drought stress conditions (Tables 10 and 11). The highest total chlorophyll content (2.4533 mg g−1 FW) was obtained from unstressed plants treated with 200 µM Mt and 5 mg/L sodium selenite (Table 7).The reduction of chlorophyll contents and inhibition of photosynthesis are among the most significant effects of drought on plants.

Seed selenium

As shown in Table 6, total seed selenium was affected by water deficit stress and significantly decreased compared to normal conditions (Tale 5 and 7). We also observed an interaction among drought stress, Mt and Se treatments. The seed selenium content increased significantly with the application of sodium selenite (5 mg/L) compared to the control (Table 11). However, we obtained the highest seed selenium content (0.4555 mg/kg DW) from the application of 5 mg/L−1 sodium selenite and 0 µM Mt under non-stress conditions, which did not show a significant difference compared to the application of 200 µM Mt and 5 mg/L sodium selenite under non-stress conditions (Table 7). The increase in seed selenium content was not only related to non-stress conditions but also to the application of Se, so that a three-fold increase in seed selenium content was observed with the application of 5 mg/L Se under drought stress conditions (Table 7).

Discussion

Mt acts as a powerful antioxidant, helps regulate the plant's stress hormones, and protects the reproductive processes that are essential for forming seeds. It also reduces stress-induced degradation of chlorophyll and improves photosynthetic activity [26]. As an antioxidant, Se enhances stress resistance and improves photosynthesis and nutrient uptake, indirectly boosting seed yield [27]. Similarly, under moderate and severe water deficit stress treatments (T2 and T3), we experienced a 20.2% and 18.8% increase in total chlorophyll content with the application of 5 mg/L Se compared to untreated plant. These results may indicate the effective role of Se in increasing seed yield due to the maintenance of chlorophyll content under water deficit stress conditions. Studies have reported higher seed yields in soybean [28], wheat [29], and rapeseed [30] when Se was applied at optimal doses. Mt regulates plant hormones (e.g., auxins and gibberellins) and promotes reproductive growth by enhancing flower retention, pollination efficiency, and seed development. Research on soybean [31], wheat [32] and maize [33] has demonstrated higher seed yields under drought stress, attributed to improved antioxidant activity. The significant increase in flowering period and seed filling in plants treated with Se, as phenological traits affecting seed yield, in this study can be attributed to the application of the above element, which led to an increase in seed yield (data not shown).

Se may increase pod number by improving flower fertility and reducing abortion. The significant increase in the number of pods in plants treated with Se under moderate and severe stress conditions (T1 = 21% and T2 = 22%) compared to untreated plants could be due to the role of this element in reducing pod abortion. In mungbean [34] and lentil [35], Se supplementation increased pod number under stress conditions. Mt reduces flower drop by modulating abscisic acid (ABA) and ethylene levels [36], and also promotes cell division in developing pods. Studies on mungbean [37] and soybean [38] have reported increased pod numbers following Mt fertilization. As shown in Table 3, the application of 200 Mm Mt resulted in a 67% and 52% increase in pod number under moderate and severe water deficit stress conditions compared to untreated plants, respectively. This increase could be due to the role of Mt as a regulator of the ABA and ethylene. It has been documented that Se enhances carbohydrate and protein translocation to seeds, thereby improving seed weight [39]. In wheat [40] and oilseed crops [41], optimal Se application increased seed weight under drought stress. Mt contributes to seed filling by enhancing sucrose metabolism and nutrient mobilization, and it can reduce oxidative damage during seed maturation [42]. Several studies have reported increased seed weight in soybean [31] and maize [43] following Mt application.

As shown in Table 5, a synergistic effect was observed when Se and Mt were applied together. Both compounds are bioactive agents known to positively influence plant growth, stress tolerance, and yield parameters across various crops. It seems that Mt as a cellular protector and growth regulator and Se as a stress mitigator and physiological booster could increase the plant's resilience under water stress conditions and have led to the retention of a greater number of pods as well as an increase in the length of the seed filling period, which ultimately has led to an increase in 100-seed weight and seed yield. Therefore, their combined application may offer additive benefits, particularly under drought stress conditions. However, to date, no studies have specifically investigated the effect of their simultaneous application on the above traits under drought stress.

Se acts as a cofactor for antioxidant enzymes (e.g., glutathione peroxidase), reducing oxidative damage to proteins [44]. It also lowers lipid peroxidation (MDA content), thereby protecting cellular structures involved in protein synthesis [45]. Se can increase the activity of nitrate reductase (NR) and glutamine synthetase (GS), promoting amino acid production [46]. It helps maintain protein synthesis even under water deficit conditions. Incorporation of Se into selenoproteins enhances their stability under stress [44]. Mt directly neutralizes ROS (e.g., H₂O₂ and O₂⁻), preventing protein oxidation and degradation. It enhances the expression of genes involved in protein biosynthesis (e.g., ribosomal proteins) [47]. Mt modulates ABA signaling, improving drought tolerance without suppressing growth. It also enhances root vitality, facilitating nitrogen uptake for amino acid and protein synthesis [48]. We found a synergistic effect between Se and Mt in increasing seed protein when applied together (Table 5). Changes in total chlorophyll content and hormone levels with simultaneous application of Se and Mt, especially under stress conditions, were significant and increased, which could explain the increase in seed protein content with simultaneous application of the above elements (Table 5). Similar synergistic effects have been previously reported in studies on melon and tomato plants [13, 49]. Considering the role of Mt in enhancing nitrogen metabolism [48], the role of Se in promoting sulfur assimilation and amino acid synthesis [46], their combined capacity to reduce oxidative stress [50], the improvement of chlorophyll content by Mt [51], and the protection of chloroplasts by Se [52], the observed synergistic improvement of seed protein in beans plant is logical.

Proline accumulates in all plant organs during water deficit stress and appears most prominently in leaves. It begins to increase rapidly once leaf water potential is lost under stress conditions. Any factor that reduces water content brings about the accumulation of this amino acid [53]. The most common pathway for proline synthesis in plants is glutamate. During water deficit stress, more glutamate is converted to proline. Reduced proline degradation and disruption of the protein synthesis process also contribute to increasing proline at low water potential [54]. Some researchers have stated that proline, as a compatible osmolyte, can accumulate in high concentrations within cells without damaging macromolecules [55]. Proline accumulation in plants during water deficit plays a role in carbon and nitrogen storage, which is important in post-stress stages [56].

Mt is an excellent molecule that improves plant growth under stress conditions by stimulating antioxidant enzymes. The proline content in plants treated with Mt under water stress showed a significant increase compared to the untreated plants. These findings suggest that Mt promotes the accumulation of osmotic solutes, thereby minimizing water loss and protecting cell membranes under drought stress [57]. Specific metabolic processes occur in plants in response to drought stress, increasing the net concentration of cellular solutes and enhancing leaf turgor. Many of these compounds including proline play essential roles in osmosis, membrane stabilization, and macromolecular protection. Proline, in particular, helps cells retain hydration and resist drought stress [58].

Soybean seeds treated with Mt can induce higher levels of antioxidants, which help eliminate excess ROS. Additionally, the rise in osmotic regulators (soluble proteins and proline) can improve the balance of cellular osmotic potential [59].

Some studies have shown that Se supplementation increases proline levels under drought, enhancing osmotic adjustment and stress tolerance [11, 60]. The results of a study [61] showed that the application of Se to wheat plants increased proline content under drought stress, thereby improving antioxidant defense. Several mechanisms have been proposed to account for the role of Se in increasing proline levels under stress conditions: stimulation of proline biosynthesis enzymes (e.g., Δ1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthetase, P5CS) [62]; reduction of proline degradation (inhibition of proline dehydrogenase, ProDH) [63]; and improvement of nitrogen metabolism, providing more glutamate (a proline precursor) [46]. The significant improvement in proline with the application of Se and Mt, especially under water stress conditions and when these two elements were used together, indicates the existence of their positive synergistic effects (Table 5). Improvement in proline due to Se and Mt application when accompanied by increases in traits such as chlorophyll, seed yield, and yield components could be an approach to reduce the effects of stress in bean plants.

As drought stress increases, oxygen free radicals accumulate, prompting the plant to enhance antioxidant enzyme activity to counteract them [64]. Therefore, elevated levels of these enzymes can indicate plant tolerance to drought stress [65].

Se upregulates the synthesis of SOD and CAT, which scavenge ROS like superoxide radicals (O₂⁻) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), respectively. By reducing ROS, Se helps maintain chloroplast integrity and stomatal regulation, indirectly supporting SOD and CAT activity [27].

Mt neutralizes ROS (e.g., OH, H₂O₂), thereby reducing oxidative stress and lowering the burden on SOD and CAT. It also enhances the expression of genes encoding SOD (Cu/Zn-SOD, Mn-SOD) and CAT [66], and interacts with redox signaling pathways (e.g., NO, Ca2⁺, and MAPK) to activate these enzymes. By preserving chlorophyll and Rubisco activity, Mt reduces ROS generation, indirectly supporting SOD/CAT function [67]. The highest SOD and CAT activities were 0.3833 U min−1 mg−1 and 0.5522 µ mol min−1 mg−1, achieved with Se and Mt treatments at rates of 200 µM and 5 mg/L under severe drought stress (Table 7). These results suggest a positive synergy when Se and Mt are applied simultaneously, enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity to cope with drought stress. Although no study was found on the combined application of Se and Mt to increase drought stress tolerance, numerous reports have documented increased tolerance through individual application of these elements by increasing the activity of SOD and CAT activity. Exogenous application of Mt in maize seedlings has been shown to mitigate drought-induced damage and enhance drought tolerance by activating antioxidant defense systems and eliminating ROS [5].

In wheat, Mt notably increased drought tolerance by enhancing antioxidant capacity, reducing endogenous ROS levels, decreasing membrane damage, thickening epidermal cells, ensuring intact granal thylakoids, and improving chloroplast and leaf structure, alongside higher photosynthetic rates and maximum efficiency [68]. Other researchers [69] also verified that Se application enhanced proline accumulation, increased the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as ascorbate peroxidase (APX), guaiacol peroxidase (GPX), superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione reductase, and elevated antioxidant enzyme levels [69].

PAL is a key enzyme in phenylpropanoid metabolism, converting phenylalanine to cinnamic acid, a precursor for phenolics [70]. At low doses, Se can enhance PAL activity, thereby increasing phenylalanine utilization and phenolic compound synthesis [71]. Mt is a potent antioxidant that upregulates PAL under stress (e.g., drought, heavy metals), which contributes to boosting phenylalanine metabolism [72]. We also found a significant positive correlation between changes in SOD, CAT, PAL, and total phenol following the combined use of Se and Mt (Fig. 1). The effect of increasing the latter enzymes emphasizes the effective role of Se and Mt in increasing water stress tolerance in bean plants. Phenolic compounds are secondary metabolites with antioxidant properties, synthesized via the phenylpropanoid pathway [73]. Se can stimulate phenol production by activating antioxidant pathways (e.g., via PAL) [71]. Mt enhances phenol accumulation by upregulating PAL and other enzymes in the phenylpropanoid pathway [74]. It also mitigates oxidative stress, indirectly promoting phenol synthesis [75].

We observed a positive synergistic effect on PAL and total phenol when Se and Mt were applied simultaneously (Table 5). This positive synergistic effect once again emphasizes the importance of the combined application of these two elements in improving water stress tolerance in bean plants.The interaction of Se with natural antioxidants has been extensively described in many scientific studies in which the enrichment of a plant with this micronutrient translates into elevated levels of polyphenols, chlorophyll, and antioxidant capacity [76]. Both compounds (Se and Mt) may upregulate PAL more effectively together than individually, increasing phenolic content [49].

Decrease in leaf chlorophyll content is a critical factor contributing to the reduction of photosynthetic rates under drought conditions [77]. It has been documented that the increase in chlorophyll content in leaves can be attributed to the effective role of Mt in protecting chloroplasts and delaying leaf senescence, which reduces chlorophyll degradation [78]. In mungbean [79], Se treatment reduced the negative effects of drought stress by increasing chlorophyll accumulation, indicating a positive regulation of photosynthesis. Similarly, evidence of increased chlorophyll after Se treatment has been observed in sorghum [80] and wheat [81]. An increase in chlorophyll following Se application in drought-sensitive edamame (green soybean) suggests that Se may minimize chloroplast damage, thereby helping to maintain photosynthetic pigment levels under abiotic stress conditions [69].

Mt protects chlorophyll by altering the expression of a protein involved in chlorophyll degradation during the leaf senescence process. Additionally, the effects of Mt as an ROS scavenger and antioxidant activator contribute to membrane stability, which stabilizes chloroplasts and prevents chlorophyll degradation [82]. The effect of Mt on photosynthesis and the activity of enzymes such as RuBisCO can positively influence primary net production, ultimately leading to enhanced plant growth [83]. In the present study, the highest total chlorophyll in both stress and non-stress conditions was associated with the simultaneous application of Se and Mt. These results indicate the positive synergistic effects of Se and Mt in improving total chlorophyll under these conditions.

The use of sodium selenate has been reported to increase the Se content in rice [84], Iranian mustard [85], spinach [86], and potato [87].

There is a growing interest in organic selenium dietary supplements. Organic sources are absorbed more effectively than inorganic selenium and have lower toxicity, making them more suitable for use as dietary supplements. Se acts as an antioxidant at low concentrations and can stimulate plant growth while improving tolerance to oxidative stress [88].

A bean plant with high proline levels is better equipped to survive a mid-season drought or heatwave [89]. It will experience less flower and pod drop, maintain its photosynthetic capacity, and have the resources to recommence growth and seed filling once conditions improve, leading to a higher final yield. Chlorophyll content is a direct indicator of a plant's photosynthetic capacity. With more chlorophyll, the plant can fix more atmospheric CO₂ into sugars (photosynthesis) [90]. The increase in seed yield in beans due to higher total phenol content is not a direct relationship but rather a cascade of indirect, beneficial effects. Think of phenols as the plant's multi-purpose toolkit; using these tools more effectively leads to a healthier, more productive plant [91]. Interestingly, there was a positive and highly significant correlation between bean seed yield and seed protein content (Fig. 1). The positive correlation of seed yield and seed protein content in the bean plant" describes a highly desirable but complex relationship[92]. For a long time, plant breeders operated under the assumption of a negative correlation between yield and protein content, believing the plant couldn't efficiently do both at a high level (often called a "dilution effect" or a trade-off). However, recent research has shown that a positive correlation is possible and can be achieved, which is great news for improving food security and nutritional quality.

The correlation of SOD and CAT enzyme with seed yield was also positive and highly significant. These enzymes are positively correlated with seed yield, especially under stressful conditions. They don't directly produce yield, but they protect the plant's metabolic machinery, allowing it to reach its full yield potential. Research often shows that drought-tolerant bean varieties exhibit a sharper and more sustained increase in SOD and CAT activity under water stress compared to sensitive varieties[93]. The tolerant varieties maintain better yield under stress, directly linking high enzyme activity to yield stability. The positive and significant relationship between the enzyme PAL and bean seed yield is also noteworthy. PAL sits at the top of a pathway that simultaneously strengthens the plant, defends it, and ultimately, allows it to invest more successfully in its reproductive output—the seeds we harvest [94]. We also observed a positive and highly significant correlation between total chlorophyll and other traits (except seed selenium). Although seed selenium showed a positive correlation with other traits, this correlation was not significant. The "positive correlation" means that the general trend of your data points slopes upwards. In other words, as seed selenium content increases, seed yield tends to also increase [29]. The "non-significant" part is the crucial caveat. It means that this upward trend you see is not strong enough to rule out the possibility that it happened by random chance. It's a starting point for more research, not a definitive answer.

Conclusion

Our results provide insights into understanding the effects of Mt and Se on the biofortification, yield, and yield components of common bean under drought stress conditions. Based on the results, with an increase in the severity of drought, seed yield and yield components decreased, but with the increase in Mt and Se levels, these traits improved. Additionally, antioxidant enzymes increased in the plants under stress conditions with the increase in Mt and Se levels, as a response to cope with stress conditions. Although the highest yield and yield components were achieved under non-stress irrigation conditions, considering the drought conditions and yield reduction, it can be cost-effective to apply Mt at a rate of 200 µM and Se at a rate of 5 mg/L.

Overall, the results suggest that the use of Mt and Se could serve as an effective strategy in managing stress and improving crop performance in agricultural environments with challenges. This information can offer practical applications for farmers aiming to enhance yields and improve crop quality under adverse conditions. Further studies should focus on molecular and biochemical mechanisms through which Se and Mt are directly or indirectly effective in alleviating the drought stress effects. It is also recommended that the present study be conducted in multi-location conditions to confirm the results.
