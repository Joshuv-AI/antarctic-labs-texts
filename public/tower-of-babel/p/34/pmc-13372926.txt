Simple endpoint PCR assay using portable devices for Escherichia coli quantification in water samples
Authors: Masataka Aoki, Atsushi Manaka, Yoshiyuki Shibata, Yoshitaka Ebie, Kazuaki Syutsubo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuantitative detection of Escherichia coli in water is essential for assessing fecal contamination because of its potential adverse effects on human health. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based techniques enable rapid detection, facilitating timely decision-making and public health interventions. However, conventional quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) assays generally require expensive, non-portable equipment and are often inaccessible in resource-limited settings. Therefore, this study evaluated a simple endpoint PCR combined with SYBR Green I (SGI)-based colorimetric deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) detection (PCR–SGI) assay for E. coli quantification. In this assay, the E. coli 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid gene was amplified by PCR using a portable thermal cycler and conventional PCR primers. Subsequently, the amplicon concentration level was estimated from SGI-induced hue changes using tablet-based image analysis with a custom sample chamber developed for stable hue data acquisition. The preliminary assay detection limit, determined using E. coli genomic DNA, was 2.47 genome copies per reaction. The assay, combined with a modified DNA extraction protocol using a commercial kit and portable devices (vortex mixer and portable centrifuge), detected E. coli in domestic wastewater and in lake and river water spiked with domestic wastewater. The E. coli concentrations obtained using the assay correlated strongly with those obtained by conventional qPCR (Pearson’s correlation coefficient [r] = 0.973) and colony-forming unit (CFU) counts (r = 0.917) in samples with positive PCR results (CFU range: 102–106 CFU 100 mL−1). Introduction

Anthropogenic fecal contamination of water resources poses a major public health risk. As pathogen monitoring is costly and complex, fecal contamination is typically evaluated by detecting fecal indicator bacteria, such as Escherichia coli, which inhabit the gastrointestinal tracts of humans and other warm-blooded animals (Jang et al., 2017). Climate change is expected to increase the frequency of extreme weather events, including floods, which are associated with fecal contamination in water resources (Holcomb & Stewart, 2020). Therefore, rapid quantitative methods for E. coli detection are critical for timely decision-making and public health mitigation during or immediately after such events. However, commonly used cultivation-based methods for E. coli, such as membrane filtration, most probable number, and plate counting methods, require extended incubation periods (> 18 h) (Price & Wildeboer, 2017; Tambi et al., 2023).

Rapid and simple alternatives for detecting E. coli in water have been developed using β-D-glucuronidase (GUS), a biomarker enzyme produced by most E. coli strains. Several GUS detection-based methods have been successfully applied to detect E. coli in river water (RW) (Briciu-Burghina et al., 2019; Satoh et al., 2021; Wildeboer et al., 2010), drinking water (Hesari et al., 2016), and wastewater (Satoh et al., 2020). These methods have a response time of ≤ 2–3 h (Satoh et al., 2021), enabling rapid E. coli detection. However, they entail higher costs and require specialized equipment such as a fluorescence (bio)sensor and a temperature-controlled fluorescence microplate reader, making them less accessible in resource-limited settings.

Nucleic acid amplification-based quantification methods enable the detection of specific deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) or ribonucleic acid (RNA) sequences in target microorganisms through sequence-specific amplification (Price & Wildeboer, 2017; Tambi et al., 2023). Consequently, these methods bypass the need for culturing and can identify microorganisms within hours. Among these methods, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assays are the most widely used. During these assays, target nucleic acid amplification is monitored in real time using fluorescence from DNA-intercalating dyes or fluorescent DNA probes (Valasek & Repa, 2005). This enables qPCR assays to obtain reliable quantification data on target nucleic acids. Several qPCR assays targeting E. coli have been established to effectively quantify their concentration in environmental water samples (Maheux et al., 2009; Noble et al., 2010; Saleem et al., 2024; Tomioka et al., 2021; Walker et al., 2017). However, conventional qPCR assays require expensive real-time PCR machines, which are often unavailable in resource-limited settings. Additionally, samples must typically be transported to a central laboratory because most real-time PCR machines are not portable, which delays decision-making and the deployment of mitigation strategies.

Competitive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is an endpoint PCR-based method that allows targeted nucleic acids to be quantified using a conventional thermal cycler without real-time monitoring (Zentilin & Giacca, 2007). Several low-cost, portable thermal cyclers have become commercially available, including the miniPCR thermal cycler (Marx, 2015). The miniPCR mini16X thermal cycler (Amplyus, Cambridge, MA, USA), equipped with 16 reaction wells, is priced at 835 USD (www.minipcr.com; accessed on December 19, 2025). Accordingly, the application of competitive PCR with a portable thermal cycler enables the quantification of target nucleic acids in resource-limited, non-central laboratory settings. However, competitive PCR still requires post-PCR gel electrophoresis and image analysis for quantification (Zentilin & Giacca, 2007), which limits its speed and overall convenience.

SYBR Green I (SGI) is an asymmetric cyanine dye that binds preferentially to double-stranded DNA (Bruijns et al., 2016). When DNA is mixed with SGI, an immediate color change from light orange to bright green occurs, which is directly observable under ultraviolet or ambient light. SGI- or other DNA-intercalating dye-based detection can be used to detect certain pathogenic E. coli strains (e.g., O157 and O157:H7) in foods by naked-eye observation following specific DNA amplification (Azinheiro et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2010). However, naked-eye observation is limited by low accuracy, as it relies on subjective interpretation of subtle color changes and fails to provide reliable quantitative results.

Digital image colorimetry using a smart device (e.g., smartphone and tablet) represents a promising alternative for rapid, direct quantitative analysis in analytical and bioanalytical chemistry (Fan et al., 2021; Pohanka, 2020). This technique may offer a cost-effective means of quantifying color changes in PCR solutions induced by DNA-intercalating dyes while ensuring reliable quantitative measurements. However, to the best of our knowledge, no simple method has been evaluated for the quantitative detection of E. coli in water samples that integrates PCR amplification using a portable thermal cycler with colorimetric analysis via a smart device.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate a simple endpoint PCR combined with SGI-based colorimetric DNA detection (PCR–SGI) assay to quantitatively detect E. coli in water samples. The assay amplifies the E. coli 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene using the miniPCR mini16X thermal cycler with conventional E. coli-specific PCR primers. The resulting PCR mixture was combined with SGI solution and subsequently analyzed by colorimetric image analysis. To ensure consistent image capture at a fixed position and illumination using a tablet’s front image sensor, we designed a custom sample chamber and assessed its performance. Additionally, we evaluated portable DNA extraction protocols to support the application of PCR–SGI assays in resource-limited settings.

Materials and methods

Water sample collection and preparation

To evaluate the applicability of the E. coli-specific endpoint PCR–SGI assay to water samples, domestic wastewater (DWW) and artificially DWW-contaminated freshwater samples were used. A summary of the water samples is provided in Table 1. This study used seven influent DWW samples from seven different DWW treatment facilities (n = 1 per facility). Six of the DWW samples (sample IDs: DWW-1 to DWW-6) were collected in November 2021 from DWW treatment facilities in Tokushima, Japan; one sample, designated DWW-7, was sourced from a rural community wastewater treatment facility in Ibaraki, Japan (collection date: November 2024). The treatment facilities in Tokushima included two small-scale, on-site DWW treatment systems (called Johkasou), one rural community wastewater treatment facility, and three community plants. Details of the facilities, sampling procedures, membrane filtration for sample concentration, sample preservation, and water quality characteristics have been presented previously (Aoki et al., 2023).

To prepare artificially DWW-contaminated environmental water samples, fresh lake water (LW) and RW were collected in Ibaraki, Japan. These newly collected freshwater samples, together with the DWW-7 sample, were stored at 4 °C until use. These water samples were passed through a 600-µm stainless steel sieve to remove large debris. Subsequently, the DWW-7 sample was mixed with fresh LW or RW at volume ratios of 1:99, 1:499, 1:1,249, 1:3,499, and 1:5,999. Each dilution series was prepared once per sample type, without replicates. The resulting mixtures were filtered through 25-mm-diameter, 0.22-µm-pore-size Millipore Express PLUS polyethersulfone (PES) membrane filters (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) to concentrate the samples. Filtration volumes were set to 10, 30, and 100 mL for the DWW-7, LW, and RW samples, respectively, based on volumes that allowed smooth filtration without membrane clogging. The resulting filters were stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction. Concentrations of total dichromate-based chemical oxygen demand and total suspended solids, as well as conductivity and pH measurements for the newly collected samples, along with the analytical methods, are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

DNA extraction

All microbial DNA extracts derived from water samples were prepared using the Extrap Soil DNA Kit Plus ver. 2 (BioDynamics Laboratory Inc., Tokyo, Japan). In this extraction kit, microbial cells are disrupted by bead beating, and the released DNA is subjected to protein removal followed by purification with magnetic beads and a magnetic separator (Magtrap; BioDynamics Laboratory Inc.).

Supplementary Fig. S1 presents schematic workflows of the standard protocol (S), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the modified protocols (M1 and M2) for DNA extraction from membrane filter-concentrated water samples. The standard and modified protocols differ in microbial cell disruption and centrifugation steps. In protocol S, cells were disrupted using a FastPrep-24 5G instrument (MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA, USA) at 6.0 m s−1 for 45 s, the most stringent conditions recommended by the manufacturer. Centrifugation in protocol S was performed using an MX-300 high-speed refrigerated microcentrifuge (TOMY SEIKO Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at 14,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. In modified protocols M1 and M2, cells were disrupted by vigorous mixing using a Vortex-Genie 2 mixer (Scientific Industries, Inc., Bohemia, NY, USA) fitted with a 24-tube adapter (catalog no. 13000-V1-24; QIAGEN N.V., Venlo, Netherlands) at maximum speed for 5 and 15 min, respectively. Centrifugation in M1 and M2 was performed using a Multi Spin MA-1 portable centrifuge (TOMY SEIKO Co., Ltd.). Its steady-state speed is 6,000 rpm (± 20%), generating centrifugal forces of 1,260–2,840 and 1,310–2,960 × g for 1.5- and 2-mL tubes, respectively.

The extracted DNA bound to the magnetic beads was eluted in 100 µL of nuclease-free water (catalog no. 318-90105; NIPPON GENE CO., LTD., Tokyo, Japan) at 65 °C for 5 min using a portable heat block (MyBL-10; AS ONE Corporation, Osaka, Japan), with vortex mixing every 2 min. DNA extraction required approximately 30, 35, and 45 min when processing a single sample using protocols S (as specified in BioDynamics Laboratory Inc., 2023), M1, and M2, respectively. Extracted DNA concentrations were determined using a Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit with a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Qubit assay; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) and/or a NanoDrop Lite Plus spectrophotometer (NanoDrop assay; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). DNA purity was evaluated based on the absorbance ratio at 260 and 280 nm (A260/A280 ratio) measured using the NanoDrop assay.

The performance of the modified DNA extraction protocols was evaluated using a whole-cell mock community and the DWW-7 sample. The mock community was purchased from the Biological Resource Center at the National Institute of Technology and Evaluation in Chiba, Japan (NBRC; Human Microbial Cell Cocktail Cell-Mock-003, lot no. 230502NC) (NBRC, 2023; Tourlousse et al., 2022). This mock community comprised 18 NBRC bacterial strains of human origin at a concentration of 2.00 × 1010 cells 500 μL−1, in which all strains were included at equal cell abundances. The strains are listed in Supplementary Table S2. For DNA extraction comparisons, 40-µL aliquots of the mock community were used. Membrane filtration was not applied to the mock community because it contained sufficient cells (1.60 × 109 cells per 40 µL aliquot) to evaluate DNA extraction performance. The DWW-7 sample was used as a representative water sample for evaluating DNA extraction performance in a membrane filter-concentrated water sample.

The modified DNA extraction protocol M1 was applied to DWW samples (DWW-1 to DWW-7) and artificially contaminated LW and RW samples (Table 1) to evaluate the performance of the endpoint PCR–SGI assay. For comparison of extraction protocols, the DWW-7 sample and a whole-cell mock community were processed using the standard protocol S and the modified protocols M1 and M2.

E. coli genomic DNA standard preparation

To prepare a quantitative standard for PCR assays, an E. coli genomic DNA standard with a known copy number was prepared. In this study, an E. coli MG1655 genomic DNA standard (MBD0013; Merck KGaA) was employed. The concentration of a high-concentration E. coli genomic DNA stock solution was verified by the Qubit assay. The DNA standard concentration (ng µL−1) was then converted to genome copies (GC) µL−1 using the genome size of E. coli MG1655 (4,641,652 bp; accession number U00096), a molar mass of 650 g mol−1 per bp, and Avogadro’s constant (6.02214076 × 1023 mol−1). Serial dilutions of the genomic DNA standard were prepared using a 5 ng µL−1 salmon sperm DNA solution. This solution was prepared by diluting a 10 mg mL−1 sheared salmon sperm DNA solution (Biodynamics Laboratory, Inc.) with nuclease-free water (catalog no. 318-90105; NIPPON GENE CO., LTD.), and its double-stranded DNA concentration was verified by using the Qubit assay. Note that the 5 ng µL−1 salmon sperm DNA solution was also used as a negative control in PCR assays and as a diluent for DNA extracts from water samples.

SGI-based DNA detection and colorimetric image analysis

For colorimetric DNA detection, 50 µL of linear double-stranded lambda bacteriophage DNA (lambda DNA) solution or PCR reaction mixture prepared by endpoint PCR, as described later, was mixed with 4 µL of 675 × SGI solution by brief vortexing in a 0.2-mL PCR tube. The 675 × SGI solution was prepared by diluting the 10,000 × SGI Nucleic Acid Gel Stain (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) with molecular biology-grade dimethyl sulfoxide (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan). The lambda DNA solutions were prepared at concentrations of 0–70.0 ng µL−1 in 10.0 ng µL−1 increments and used as the standard DNA solutions. These solutions were obtained by diluting a 100 ng µL−1 lambda DNA solution (provided as a Qubit 1 × dsDNA BR Assay Standard #2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) with Tris-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.5; Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA).

For colorimetric image analysis, a PCR tube containing the colored solution was placed in a custom 3D-printed sample chamber (Form 2 resin, Formlabs, Inc., Somerville, MA, USA) positioned above the front-facing camera of a tablet (T0855/GAS; NEC Personal Computers, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) (Fig. 1). During image capture, the tablet screen serves as the light source, eliminating the need for any supplementary lighting apparatus. This feature is a distinctive characteristic of the sample chamber, contributing to its notably simple design. The tablet screen brightness was set to maximum. To minimize screen-light absorption within the sample chamber and eliminate ambient light interference, the chamber interior was painted white, whereas the exterior and cover were painted black. The sample chamber was attached to the tablet using black double-sided adhesive tape.

Experimental setup for hue data acquisition using a tablet-based system. a Custom sample chamber and placement of a 0.2-mL polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tube within the chamber. b Image showing the tablet during image acquisition and hue data analysis

In general, digital images captured by image sensors in smart devices are processed and stored in the Red–Green–Blue (RGB) color space, where colors are represented as additive combinations of red, green, and blue components. For quantitative evaluation of color change, the acquired RGB values were converted to the hue–saturation–value (HSV) color space, which separates color information into hue, saturation (chroma), and value (brightness) (Smith, 1978). Changes in sample color were quantified and analyzed using the hue component. The in-house image software used in this study was a modified version of a tool previously developed for simple colorimetric quantification of hypochlorous acid in water (Manaka et al., 2023). The software captures subject images and converts RGB values into HSV values, enabling objective quantitative evaluation of color changes in DNA-containing solutions. All images were captured with a 0.2-s exposure time and an International Organization for Standardization (ISO) sensitivity (also referred to as ISO speed) of 100, as defined by ISO 12232:2006 (ISO, 2006). Details of hue value calculation from captured images using the software are provided in Supplementary Text S1.

Unless otherwise stated, image acquisition was performed in a laboratory under moderate illuminance of 500 ± 100 lx. The reported illuminance values were measured immediately before capture using a light meter (TM-209M; Tenmars Electronics Co., Ltd., Taipei, Taiwan). In an experiment evaluating the colorimetric image analysis system using standard lambda DNA solutions, images were captured in a dark room at four illuminance levels, adjusted within an imaging box (40 × 40 × 57 cm) equipped with ceiling-mounted LED lights.

Endpoint PCR–SGI assay

To amplify the E. coli 16S rRNA gene and enable quantification using the endpoint PCR–SGI assay, PCR was performed using a MiniPCR mini16X thermal cycler (Amplyus). Each 50-μL PCR reaction mixture contained 10 μL of template DNA, 0.8 μM each of the ECA75F (5′-GGAAGAAGCTTGCTTTGCTGAC-3′) and ECR619R (5′-AGCCCGGGGATTTCACATCTGACTTA-3′) primers (Sabat et al., 2000), and dye-free 1 × KOD One PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO CO., LTD., Osaka, Japan). The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 98 °C for 20 s, followed by 40 amplification cycles (98 °C for 10 s, 63 °C for 5 s, and 68 °C for 1 s), and a final extension step at 68 °C for 5 s. The PCR conditions were empirically optimized using the E. coli MG1655 genomic DNA standard. The total time required for PCR amplification using the portable thermal cycler under these conditions was approximately 50 min.

In the endpoint PCR–SGI assay, a standard curve was generated by plotting the log10-transformed template DNA copy number against the hue of the PCR mixture after the addition of 4 µL of the 675 × SGI solution. The curve was constructed using threefold serial dilutions of the E. coli MG1655 genomic DNA standard, ranging from 60.0 to 0.247 GC µL−1, corresponding to 600–2.47 GC per reaction. As the endpoint PCR–SGI assay was designed for simplified quantification, calibration curves with a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.90 or higher were considered acceptable for water sample analysis. A standard curve was generated for each endpoint PCR–SGI assay during water sample analysis. The R2 values of the standard curves were 0.919–0.942.

The endpoint PCR–SGI assay was applied to DNA extracts prepared from the water samples (Table 1). Note that E. coli genomic DNA concentrations in DWW-derived DNA extracts were expected to be high and could potentially exceed the applied calibration curve range. Therefore, 64- or eight-fold diluted DNA templates were used in this assay. For the DWW-1 sample, in which the diluted DNA template failed to produce a positive signal, the undiluted DNA template was also analyzed. E. coli concentrations in the artificially contaminated LW and RW samples were expected to be lower than those in the DWW samples. However, to reduce potential PCR inhibition, both undiluted and eightfold diluted DNA extracts were analyzed. All diluted DNA extracts were prepared using the 5 ng μL−1 salmon sperm DNA solution. Reported quantitative data for each sample were obtained from a single technical replicate. Although technical replicates can improve analytical precision, this study employed a single-replicate design for water sample analysis to better reflect realistic field and resource-limited conditions. In such contexts, incorporating technical replicates would compromise the time efficiency and operational convenience of the assay. The 5 ng μL−1 salmon sperm DNA solution was included in each PCR run as a negative control.

qPCR assay

As a reference method for the endpoint PCR–SGI assay, an E. coli-specific qPCR assay was used to quantify E. coli in the water samples (Table 1). The qPCR assay was performed using a LightCycler 96 system (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). Each PCR reaction mixture (total volume of 20 μL) contained 4 μL of template DNA, 0.25 μM each of the ECA75F and ECR619R primers (Sabat et al., 2000), and 1 × FastStart Essential DNA Green Master (Roche Diagnostics GmbH). The thermal cycle for the real-time PCR included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 amplification cycles (95 °C for 10 s, 68 °C for 10 s, and 72 °C for 25 s). Post-amplification melting curve analysis was performed to confirm assay specificity. The PCR conditions were empirically optimized using the E. coli MG1655 genomic DNA standard. Real-time PCR data were analyzed using LightCycler 96 Application Software v.1.1 (Roche Diagnostics International Ltd., Rotkreuz, Switzerland). Absolute quantification was performed using a standard curve generated from eight-fold serial dilutions of the E. coli MG1655 genomic DNA standard, ranging from 2.00 × 104 to 6.10 × 10−1 GC μL−1, corresponding to 8.00 × 104–2.44 GC per reaction. All standard curves had R2 values of ≥ 0.999. PCR efficiency (E), calculated from the slopes of the standard curves using Eq. (1), was 0.870–0.879:

DNA extracts were diluted using the 5 ng μL−1 salmon sperm DNA solution, applying the same dilution factors as in the endpoint PCR–SGI assay. All samples were amplified in duplicate, and the quantification cycle (Cq) values were used to calculate the reported mean E. coli genomic DNA concentration. The 5 ng μL−1 salmon sperm DNA solution was included as a negative control in each real-time PCR run.

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing analysis

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing analysis was employed to evaluate taxon-specific differences in DNA extraction performance between the standard and modified protocols. 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequence libraries were prepared using a two-step tailed PCR method. The 27Fmod and 338R primer pair targeting hypervariable V1–V2 regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene (Kim et al., 2013) was used in the first-step PCR. Detailed procedures for preparing 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequence libraries are described in Supplementary Text S2. Amplicon sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene was performed on a NextSeq 1000 System using NextSeq 1000/2000 P1 Reagents (600 cycles; Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Primer sequences at the 5′ ends of the raw 16S rRNA gene amplicon reads were trimmed using Cutadapt v.4.4 (Martin, 2011). These trimmed reads were then processed using mothur v.1.48.4 (Schloss et al., 2009) with the SILVA reference database release 138.2 (Quast et al., 2013). Mothur analysis followed the MiSeq Standard Operating Procedures (https://mothur.org/wiki/miseq_sop/) (Kozich et al., 2013), with modifications for analyzing the hypervariable regions V1–V2. The mothur commands and options used are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. Sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using a 97% sequence similarity threshold. The 16S rRNA gene sequences of the 18 bacterial strains in the cell mock community can be classified into 17 genus-level groups using mothur analysis (Supplementary Table S2). Based on this classification, the strain-level 16S rRNA gene composition of DNA extracts from the cell mock community was estimated.

Plate counts of E. coli

As a reference culture-based E. coli quantification for the endpoint PCR–SGI assay, plate counting was performed. Colony-forming units (CFUs) of culturable E. coli in the newly collected water samples were counted using CompactDry EC plates (Shimadzu Diagnostics Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For artificially contaminated LW and RW samples in which low E. coli CFU levels (< 10 CFU mL−1) were expected, membrane filtration was performed using the 25 mm-diameter, 0.22-µm-pore-size Millipore Express PLUS PES membrane filters (Merck KGaA) to concentrate E. coli. The DWW-7 sample was diluted 100-fold with sterile saline solution (0.85% [wt./vol.] sodium chloride), and the diluted sample was used for plate counting. The reported CFU counts represent the mean values obtained from technical duplicates. Previously obtained E. coli CFU count data from six DWW samples (DWW-1 to DWW-6) collected in Tokushima, Japan (Aoki et al., 2023) were referenced in this study without additional measurements. Aoki et al. (2023) also used CompactDry EC plates for plate counting.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the stats package in R v.4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024). The Friedman test was performed using the friedman.test function to assess differences in paired data across three or more related groups. Because the Friedman test does not require the assumption of normality, it was selected for analysis of the present dataset, which had a limited sample size (n = 4 or 5 technical replicates per group). R2 values for the generated standard curves were calculated using the lm function. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) and the corresponding p-value were calculated using the cor.test function. This test was applied to evaluate the linear relationship between endpoint PCR–SGI assay data and qPCR or CFU count data for E. coli, after log10 transformation of all datasets. Given the approximately linear relationship between the variables and the absence of extreme outliers, Pearson’s correlation was considered appropriate. To maintain a conservative approach considering the relatively limited sample size, statistical significance was set at p < 0.01 for all tests.

Results and discussion

Performance of the tablet-based colorimetric image analysis system

The color of the lambda DNA solution after mixing with the SGI solution (final SGI concentration: 50 ×) shifted from light orange toward bright green as the DNA concentration increased. Figure 2 shows the hue values of the DNA solution obtained using the tablet-based colorimetric image analysis system. No significant statistical differences in hue data were observed among the same examined DNA concentrations at four different illuminance levels (p > 0.01, Friedman test), which included the recommended ranges for various room types (50–1,200 lx; Archtoolbox, 2021). In the HSV color space, hue is independent of value (brightness) and saturation and is generally less sensitive to variations in ambient light. However, to ensure stable and reliable colorimetric measurements, images should be captured under controlled conditions, including a fixed imaging angle, consistent distance from the sample, stable illumination, and standardized camera settings (Balbach et al., 2021). Our findings indicate that hue values can be reliably obtained with the tablet-based system under various room lighting conditions. Additionally, at the same illuminance level, the hue values of all lambda DNA solutions at concentrations ≥ 20.0 ng μL−1 exceeded blank mean (0 ng μL−1) plus three standard deviations (SD), indicating reliable DNA identification at ≥ 20.0 ng μL−1. This DNA detection performance is useful for identifying DNA templates that may show false-positive reactions without PCR amplification, as discussed later for water sample analysis.

Standard curves generated from lambda DNA solutions ranging from 20.0 to 70.0 ng μL−1 exhibited high linearity (R2 = 0.978–0.995; Supplementary Fig. S2), demonstrating that the system can be used for DNA quantification. These results support the applicability of the endpoint PCR–SGI assay, which is based on estimating PCR amplicon concentration levels at the endpoint.

Notably, this study used the Qubit assay to prepare a high-concentration E. coli genomic DNA standard stock for generating standard curves. However, the results suggest that the tablet-based system can be used to prepare high-concentration DNA standard stocks with known concentrations from E. coli cultures in resource-limited settings where sophisticated DNA quantification systems (e.g., a Qubit fluorometer) are unavailable.

Performance of the endpoint PCR–SGI assay evaluated using pure DNA extracts and reagent cost

We evaluated the sensitivity of the E. coli 16S rRNA gene-targeted endpoint PCR–SGI assay. After applying the assay, the hue value of the negative control was 19.2 ± 1.1 (mean ± SD, n = 5 technical replicates). Hue values obtained from E. coli genomic DNA (ranging from 600 to 2.47 GC per reaction) were all higher than the negative control mean plus three times its SD. These data showed that E. coli genomic DNA could be reliably detected and distinguished from background noise, even at a low template copy number approaching a single GC. Accordingly, the preliminary limit of detection (LoD) for the assay was 2.47 GC per reaction. This LoD is comparable to those reported for various E. coli-specific qPCR assays (i.e., 1–10 GC per reaction) (Maheux et al., 2009; Saleem et al., 2024; Walker et al., 2017). Additionally, a linear relationship was observed between log10-transformed template DNA copy numbers and the corresponding hue values (R2 = 0.983; Fig. 3). These results demonstrate that the endpoint PCR–SGI assay can quantify E. coli genomic DNA copy numbers in DNA extracts.

The quantitative performance of the assay was further evaluated based on hue variability across different E. coli genomic DNA copy numbers (Supplementary Table S4). The assay exhibited stable precision across the tested DNA copy number range, with relative standard deviations (RSDs) remaining below 10% between 600 and 7.41 GC per reaction (1.3–5.8%). Notably, the RSD was limited to 12.5% even at the lowest template copy number tested (2.47 GC per reaction). Considering that the endpoint PCR–SGI assay was developed as a simple quantitative PCR method, acceptable precision was maintained even under low-template E. coli genomic DNA conditions.

The total reagent cost of the endpoint PCR–SGI assay (excluding PCR primers) is approximately 267 JPY per reaction (Supplementary Table S5), which is approximately 3 times higher than that of the qPCR assay kit used in this study (approximately 91 JPY per reaction). However, the PCR–SGI assay does not require a costly real-time PCR machine; therefore, the initial investment is substantially lower, representing a practical advantage of the PCR–SGI assay.

Evaluation of DNA extraction protocols using portable devices

General DNA extraction protocols capable of efficiently recovering genomic DNA from diverse bacteria require costly benchtop equipment, limiting rapid detection and quantification of fecal indicator bacteria and pathogens in field or resource-limited settings. Although the primary aim of this study is to evaluate a simple E. coli quantification assay, monitoring E. coli alongside other fecal indicators or specific pathogens provides a more reliable measure of water quality. Thus, we also evaluated DNA extraction protocols using portable devices to support future development of quantitative assays targeting various indicator/pathogenic bacteria.

Numerous commercially available DNA extraction kits have been developed for efficient recovery of genomic DNA from a broad range of bacteria. One such kit, the Extrap Soil DNA Kit Plus ver. 2, has been successfully used to extract DNA from DWW and environmental water for detecting and quantifying potentially pathogenic bacteria and E. coli (Aoki et al., 2023, 2024; Tomioka et al., 2021). The manufacturer’s standard protocol recommends using a high-speed cell disruptor for intense cell disruption (4–6 m s−1 or 4,200–6,800 rpm for 30–45 s). In contrast, the manufacturer has confirmed that 15-min microbial cell disruption using a standard vortex mixer yields bacterial DNA quantities nearly identical to those obtained with a high-speed disruptor in soil matrices (BioDynamics Laboratory Inc., 2023). However, to the best of our knowledge, the applicability of vortex mixer-based extraction protocols to bacteria from the human microbiome and DWW has not been evaluated.

Figure 4 illustrates the extracted DNA yields from the mock community and DWW sample DWW-7. These extracts were prepared using the standard protocol S and modified protocols M1 and M2, which employ portable devices, such as a vortex mixer and a portable centrifuge. No significant differences in extracted DNA yields were observed among the protocols for either the mock community or the DWW sample (p > 0.01, Friedman test). The A260/A280 ratio of DNA extracts from the mock community and DWW sample was approximately 1.8 (Supplementary Fig. S3), which is generally accepted as pure DNA (Wilfinger et al., 1997). No significant differences in A260/A280 ratios were observed among the extraction protocols (p > 0.01, Friedman test).

Collectively, our results indicate that the modified DNA extraction protocols using portable devices can extract DNA from various bacteria associated with the human microbiome and DWW, with performance comparable to that of the standard protocol, which employs expensive, less portable devices.

Quantitative detection of E. coli in water samples by the endpoint PCR–SGI assay

The E. coli genomic DNA concentrations in the DWW samples and in artificially contaminated LW and RW samples were evaluated using the endpoint PCR–SGI assay. In this experiment, DNA extracts were prepared using the modified protocol M1 because it offers a time-saving advantage over M2, while showing comparable DNA extraction performance (see above). Considering the above-mentioned preliminary assay LoD value, a sample was considered positive if its hue exceeded the 2.47 GC of E. coli genomic DNA amplified in the same PCR run.

Positive amplification was detected in all DWW samples and artificially contaminated RW samples. The minimum CFU count in the artificially contaminated RW samples that tested positive by the endpoint PCR–SGI assay was 230 CFU 100 ml−1, corresponding to the sample with a 1:5,999 DWW-to-RW mixing ratio. This E. coli CFU count value was comparable to the LOD of GUS activity-based microplate reader assays for RW (430 MPN 100 ml−1) (Satoh et al., 2021) and treated municipal wastewater (2,200 MPN 100 ml−1) (Satoh et al., 2020), as well as a GUS activity-based handheld fluorescence detector assay applied to RW (700 CFU 100 ml−1) (Wildeboer et al., 2010). Notably, the quantification value for the undiluted DNA extract prepared from the 1:5,999 DWW-to-RW mixture sample was 14.0 GC per reaction. This value was within the same order of magnitude as the preliminary assay LoD (2.47 GC per reaction) determined using the E. coli genomic DNA standard.

The E. coli CFU count of 230 CFU 100 mL−1 in the artificially contaminated RW sample satisfies the E. coli criterion for Class A RW under the Japanese environmental water quality standards (≤ 300 CFU 100 mL−1), which is the second-highest classification within a six-tier system ranging from Class AA to Class E (Ministry of the Environment, Japan, 2025). Class A RW is designated for water supply (Class 2, the second‑highest category, referring to source waters that can be treated using conventional purification processes such as sedimentation and filtration), fisheries use (Class 1, the highest category, encompassing waters designated for oligosaprobic fisheries, such as masu salmon and charr, as well as other fisheries organisms categorized under fisheries Classes 2 and 3), bathing, and other uses classified as Class B or lower. In addition, the E. coli CFU count of 230 CFU 100 mL−1 was markedly lower than those estimated in combined sewer overflow (e.g., 105 CFU 100 mL−1) and combined sewer overflow-impacted Japanese RW (e.g., 104 CFU 100 mL−1) (Poopipattana et al., 2021). Therefore, in RW samples that allow effective filtration of 100 mL and reliable PCR amplification, the assay is likely to provide sufficient performance for the quantitative detection of fecal pollution associated with extreme weather events.

In contrast, artificially contaminated LW samples exhibited a positive response only at 13,400 CFU 100 mL−1 (DWW-to-LW mixing volume ratio of 1:99). This outcome may be attributable to the limited filtration volume for contaminated LW samples (i.e., 30 mL) because of membrane clogging, as well as the presence of PCR inhibitors, such as humic substances (Schrader et al., 2012), in the DNA extracts. To reduce potential PCR inhibition, eightfold-diluted DNA extracts from the contaminated LW samples were also tested in the endpoint PCR–SGI assay. However, no positive response was obtained from contaminated LW samples at DWW:LW mixing volume ratios of 1:499, 1:1,249, 1:3,499, and 1:5,999.

Similar results were obtained with the qPCR assay. Except for the 1:99 DWW-to-LW mixing sample, the mean Cq values for both undiluted and eight-fold diluted DNA extracts from all LW samples exceeded the threshold Cq value (approximately 34.6), which corresponded to the minimum copy number of the qPCR standard curve (2.44 GC per reaction). Consequently, E. coli was quantifiable only in the 1:99 DWW-to-LW mixing sample. Notably, the qPCR analysis of the 1:99 DWW-to-LW sample yielded a 3.8-fold higher E. coli concentration when using the eight-fold diluted DNA extract than when using the undiluted DNA extract, whereas the contaminated RW samples showed similar concentrations regardless of DNA extract dilution (Supplementary Table S7). These qPCR data suggest that the contaminated LW-derived DNA extracts contain higher levels of PCR inhibitors than those derived from contaminated RW. Although the DNA extraction kit used in this study is expected to produce high purification efficiency (Tsuchiya et al., 2015; BioDynamics Laboratory Inc., 2023), appropriate PCR enzyme selection and/or additional inhibitor-removal procedures may be required for water samples containing substantial PCR inhibitors. Furthermore, the development of simple and field-deployable E. coli concentration methods that do not rely on conventional membrane filtration is crucial for improving detection limits in less-filterable water samples.

The CFU count of the LW sample positive by the endpoint PCR–SGI assay (i.e., 13,400 CFU 100 mL−1) exceeds Japanese environmental water quality standards in lakes (≤ 20–1,000 CFU 100 mL−1; Ministry of the Environment, Japan, 2025). However, similar high E. coli CFU levels were observed in nearshore Lake Michigan following sanitary sewer overflow and combined sewer overflow events (104 to nearly 105 CFU 100 mL−1) (McLellan et al., 2007). Therefore, even for challenging LW samples affected by limited filtration volumes and PCR inhibitors, the current protocol is expected to detect severe fecal pollution associated with extreme weather events.

Note that false positives may occur in the new assay if the extracted DNA concentration is too high. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S5, no significant hue change occurs in the absence of PCR amplification within the 0–200 ng per reaction template DNA range. Additionally, we confirmed that template DNA amount was not significantly correlated with hue values obtained from the endpoint PCR–SGI assay (r = 0.46, p > 0.01; Supplementary Fig. S6). Thus, we concluded that no false positives due to high template DNA concentrations occurred in this study. For practical applications, the hue of a DNA extract should be equal to or lower than that of a 20.0 ng μL−1 lambda DNA solution (200 ng per reaction). Note that 20.0 ng μL−1 lambda DNA can be reliably detected by the colorimetric image analysis, as described above.

Figure 6 illustrates the strong correlation between log10-transformed E. coli GC concentrations in positive samples, measured by the endpoint PCR–SGI assay, and results from the conventional qPCR assay (r = 0.973, p < 0.001) or log10-transformed E. coli CFU counts (r = 0.917, p < 0.001). These results suggest that the endpoint PCR–SGI assay can estimate the level of E. coli contamination in water samples. However, qPCR yielded values more than two-fold higher than those obtained by the endpoint PCR–SGI assay in 10 of 13 positive samples. This discrepancy may result from differences in PCR efficiency between the PCR enzymes used to analyze the samples. Similarly, in 11 of 13 samples, E. coli CFU counts were more than twice as high as those obtained from the endpoint PCR–SGI assay. Theoretically, CFU counts should match or be lower than GC concentrations, as each viable cell generally contains one chromosomal DNA molecule, while DNA from non-viable cells in samples may also contribute to GC concentrations. The higher CFU counts may have resulted from DNA loss during DNA extraction and reduced PCR efficiencies in the analyzed samples compared with the genomic DNA standard. Absolute differences have been observed among E. coli quantification approaches (Noble et al., 2010; Saleem et al., 2024; Walker et al., 2017). However, the strong correlations suggest that the endpoint PCR–SGI assay could be useful for the relative assessment of fecal contamination in environmental waters.

In the endpoint PCR–SGI assay, PCR amplicon concentration-dependent color development occurs immediately upon SGI dye addition, and the hue can be recorded within a few tens of seconds per sample. Therefore, when analyzing a moderate number of samples (e.g., ≤ 16, which can be amplified simultaneously using the portable thermal cycler), the total analysis time is similar to that of conventional qPCR assays. According to Noble and Weisberg (2005), rapid methods for detecting indicator bacteria in recreational waters yield results within 4 h, including sample preparation. The endpoint PCR–SGI assay is highly likely to meet this criterion when a limited number of water samples are processed, as it delivers results within this timeframe even when accounting for steps such as sample filtration, DNA extraction, PCR mixture preparation, PCR amplification, and colorimetric analysis.

We also acknowledge certain aspects of this study that have not been fully examined. A strong correlation between quantitative data from endpoint PCR–SGI and conventional qPCR assays was confirmed. However, the number of water samples analyzed was relatively limited. Therefore, further validation using a more diverse range of water samples is required to confirm assay robustness. Additionally, the endpoint PCR–SGI assay detects DNA from non-viable E. coli cells, although this represents an inherent limitation of DNA-based methods and is not unique to the endpoint PCR–SGI assay. Sample pretreatment with a DNA‐binding viability dye (Codony et al., 2020) may be necessary to more accurately evaluate fecal contamination levels in water samples containing a high proportion of non-viable E. coli cells.

Conclusions

We evaluated the endpoint PCR–SGI assay, a simple PCR-based method for E. coli quantification that can be performed using affordable, portable devices. The modified DNA extraction protocols, which require neither costly nor non-portable equipment, performed comparably to the standard DNA extraction protocol. The preliminary LoD for the assay, determined using an E. coli genomic DNA standard, was 2.47 GC per reaction. When combined with one of the modified DNA extraction protocols, the assay produced data that correlated strongly with those obtained by qPCR and culture-based methods in samples with positive PCR results. This finding confirmed the assay’s reliable quantitative detection performance across water samples containing various E. coli concentrations, including levels expected during extreme weather events (CFU range: 102–106 CFU 100 mL−1). Although the endpoint PCR–SGI assay cannot completely replace established culture-based E. coli quantification, it may serve as a complementary tool for water quality management and public health protection, particularly in applications requiring rapid response, such as water pollution events associated with extreme weather. Further method optimization and validation using diverse water samples, including challenging samples affected by limited filtration volumes and PCR inhibitors, as well as the incorporation of viability dye pretreatment, are required to evaluate and enhance the robustness of the endpoint PCR–SGI assay.
