Assessing the impact of phage therapy on growth performance, microbiome and phage specific immune response in chickens
Authors: I. Nazir, D. Perez, S.J.R. Vargas, S. ur Rehman, D. Arora, M. Chandra, J. Thimmapuram, P. Ebner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacteriophages could be an effective alternative to antibiotics in preventing or controlling disease outbreaks in the poultry industry. This study evaluated the effects of Salmonella Gallinarum and Avian Pathogenic E. coli (APEC) phage treatments, previously shown to significantly reduce targeted bacteria in challenged chickens, on chicken growth performance, immune response, and gut microbiome. To measure the impact of phage treatment on growth and immune response, 7-day old (d) chickens were fed diets supplemented with Salmonella (1.0–2.0 × 108 PFU/mL) or APEC (107 PFU/mL) phages at 1mL/kg of feed. Growth parameters including average daily gain, average weight gain, average daily feed intake, and feed conversion ratio were calculated. Blood samples were collected at d21, d28, and d35 to detect antiphage antibodies (IgM, IgG, and IgA). Chickens receiving either Salmonella Gallinarum or APEC phages showed significantly higher ADG and AWG (p ≤ 0.05) compared to untreated controls, with no significant differences in detection of phage antibodies across groups. For microbiome analysis, chickens were challenged with either APEC or Salmonella Gallinarum and administered phages via gavage. Cecal contents were collected before and after phage treatments and microbial communities were characterized by 16 S rRNA sequencing. Differential abundance analysis (DESeq2, edgeR) revealed increased levels of beneficial taxa including Lachnospiraceae, Actualibacteriaceae, and Ruminococcaceae in birds treated with Salmonella phages, and Butyricoccaceae and Lachnospiraceae in APEC phage-treated birds (q ≤ 0.05). These results indicate phage treatment may enhance growth performance and promote a favorable gut microbial profile without inducing measurable B-cell mediated antibody responses, supporting their potential as antibiotic alternatives in poultry production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45338-y.

Introduction

Antibiotics play an important role in veterinary medicine, serving multiple purposes. In food animal production, they are commonly used to prevent and treat bacterial infections. Traditionally, antibiotics have also been used in food animal production for growth performance, namely, to improve feed efficiencies. This third use, however, has significantly declined as the US, the European Union, and other countries have discontinued the use of most antibiotics for growth performance. As a result, these nations have experienced a notable decrease in antimicrobial sales for food-producing animals1–4.

Efforts to further reduce antibiotic use in food animal production continue, however, driven by concerns over the emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria5. Toward this end, numerous groups have focused significant research attention on the development of alternatives to antibiotics, specifically treatments that reduce bacterial infections in animals without a concomitant risk to human health. The use of bacteriophages is among such potential alternatives. Bacteriophages (phages) are viruses specific to bacteria1,6,7. The viruses, which are often highly-specific to individual strains or subtypes of bacteria, have antibacterial properties in that they have the capacity to infect bacterial cells, propagate, induce lysis of the bacterial cell, and be released to infect other bacterial cells8.

Numerous groups have explored the use of bacteriophages to prevent or treat bacterial infections in food animals8–11. When aggregated, these data indicate that phage treatments reduce bacterial concentrations of targeted pathogens in both pigs12 and broiler chickens13. Most of these studies used comparisons of concentrations of targeted bacteria (e.g., MPN or CFU/g or CFU/mL of a sample) between phage-treated and untreated animals as the main measure of the efficacy of the treatment.

As potential antibacterials, phages likely have other impacts on the host including gut microbiome composition, growth performance, or immune response14,15. As such, greater adoption of phage based antibacterials in food animal production will require more comprehensive examination of some of these potential effects to ensure phages do not compromise animal growth. For instance, the intestinal microbiome is a vital component of the gastrointestinal tract (GIT), playing a key role in shaping and maintaining intestinal morphology, immune responses, nutrient digestion, and pathogen defense16. Many of the bacteria that inhabit the GIT prevent the growth of pathogenic bacteria and produce essential metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and vitamins for the host17. Disruptions of the gut microbiome can lead to opportunistic infections (e.g., Clostridium difficile), which are sometimes seen with antibiotic therapy18,19. Although phages have an even narrower spectrum than the most selective antibiotics, the potential effects of administering high concentrations of phages on the host gut microbiome deserve further study.

While phages interact with the immune system when administered therapeutically to humans or animals20, the factors determining immunogenicity and specific responses affecting phage pharmacokinetics are also not well understood. Some studies have found that oral administration of phages did not elicit a phage specific immune response (e.g., anti-phage IgM, IgG, and IgA); most of those studies, however, did not explore continuous exposure to high doses of phages21–23. Others have found that phages vary in their immunogenicity, and the host humoral response depends on many factors such as dose and route of administration, host immunity, and phage protein structure20,22. A clearer understanding of the humoral immune response to phage administration could assist in predicting when phage treatment would be most effective and when host-immune responses may lead to treatment failure.

Finally, it is important to note that financial solvency in food animal production is a function of the efficiency of the animal and how well the animal utilizes expensive inputs such as feed. Indeed, the use of antibiotics for performance is an effort to maximize that efficiency as animals administered antibiotics in the feed often have greater average daily gains (ADG) and better feed conversion ratios (FCR)24,25. Thus, it is critical to understand the impact of phage treatment on growth of the animal as any decrease in efficiency could negate the positive impact of reducing pathogenic bacteria.

Salmonella Gallinarum is the causative agent of fowl typhoid, a severe septicemic infection in poultry with more than 80% mortality in broilers26. Avian Pathogenic E. coli (APEC) causes colibacillosis also resulting in high mortality rates in poultry27. Both diseases remain prevalent in Pakistani poultry and pose serious economic threats and reduce consumers’ accessibility to high-quality protein. Our previous studies identified novel polyphage (“cocktails”) treatments that significantly reduced growth of Salmonella Gallinarum and APEC in poultry. The study described here aimed to assess the impact of the same phage cocktails on the chicken gut microbiome, humoral immune response, and growth performance, where deleterious effects resulting from phage treatment could reduce the adoption of the technology. We hypothesized that the lytic or inhibitive effect of phages on pathogenic or harmful bacteria may promote the growth of beneficial bacteria and this combined mechanistic effect could improve the overall growth performance of chickens. Taken together, these results advance our understanding of the efficacy and appropriateness of targeting livestock and poultry pathogens with phages.

Results

Impact of phage treatment on chicken growth/performance

Body weights of chickens were recorded individually on day (d)7, d14, d21, d28 and d35, and aggregated feed intake (FI) was recorded daily for each treatment group. There were no significant differences observed in feed intake (FI), average daily gain (ADG), average weight gain (AWG), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) across treatment groups during the initial phase of the experiment from d7-d14 (Table 1; phage treated chickens received initials phage doses on d7-d14). Likewise, there were no differences in performance metrics across treatment groups from d7-d14 or d15-d21.

Following the second administration of phage treatments (d21-d25), ADG from d22 to d28 of chickens treated with APEC phages (ADG = 76.3 g) was significantly higher (p ≤ 0.05) than that of untreated chickens (ADG = 64.0 g) and chickens receiving only sodium alginate (ADG = 65.1 g) in their diet. ADG in chickens treated with Salmonella phages (ADG value = 72.1 g) was not significantly different (p > 0.05) from any of these treatments during this period.

Over the entire experiment period from d7-d35, however, ADG and AWG in chickens treated with the APEC phages (ADG value = 59.7 g ; AWG value = 1601.9 g) or Salmonella phages (ADG value = 60.2; AWG value = 1788.8 g) were significantly higher (p ≤ 0.05) than those of untreated chickens (ADG value = 55.5 g ; AWG value = 1658.8 g ) or chickens receiving only sodium alginate (ADG value = 53.5 g ; AWG value = 1601.9 g). However, no differences in FI or FCR were detected across all treatment groups throughout the entire study period.

Humoral immune response to phage treatment

Phage specific antibody production in response to phage treatment was measured in the serum of phage treated chickens vs. untreated chickens. The primary antibody response (IgM) was recorded at d21 and d28 (14 and 21 days after initial phage treatment), while the secondary response (IgG) was measured at d28 and d35 (7 and 14 days after second phage treatment). Additionally, secretory antibody (IgA) production was measured in fecal samples at d21, d28 and d35. There were no significant differences (p > 0.05) observed in anti-phage antibody production levels (IgM, IgG, IgA) across all treatment groups and sample times (Figs. 1 and 2).

The cecal microbiome of chickens challenged with Salmonella Gallinarum was characterized at two different time points during the experiment: before any treatment or bacterial challenge (d7) and 14 days after the initial phage treatment (d21). A total of 59 cecal samples along with a microbial community standard (PC) were sequenced for microbiome analysis. Of the 59 samples, 4 samples were not used for further analysis due to their low read counts. The lowest retained feature count of any sample included in the analysis was 8,103. The analysis continued in different parts after subsampling (rarefied) the samples to a specific number of sequences (23,881 sequences per sample) to ensure all samples had the same sequencing depth while retaining most of the feature count. This approach helped keep more than 90% of samples and sufficient sequencing depth for analysis.

Alpha diversity metrics (Observed features, Faith PD, Chao1 and Shannon diversity) were used to measure the diversity, distribution and abundance of bacterial species within a given sample or community. Our analysis revealed a substantial increase in microbial communities’ richness and diversity from d7 to d21 within each treatment group, as shown in Fig. 3a and b. However, there were no significant differences in alpha diversity metrics (padj = q ≤ 0.05) between treatment groups at each time point (d7 and d21) using Kruskal Wallis pairwise comparison.

Beta diversity metrics, including Bray-Curtis (quantitative) and Jaccard (qualitative) indices, were used to assess dissimilarity in microbial communities based on species composition. While differences were detected overtime (d7 to d21) within treatments groups, no differences were detected in comparisons across treatments. In contrast, at d21, there were differences in weighted (quantitative) and unweighted (qualitative) UniFrac metrics, which describe dissimilarities in microbial communities according to phylogenetic relationships. At d21, based on the Weighted UniFrac measurements, cecal microbial communities of chickens treated with unencapsulated phages, chickens treated with microencapsulated phages, and chickens not receiving any treatment were phylogenetically different, with variations in the relative abundance of taxa. Kruskal Wallis pairwise PERMANOVA analysis suggested significant differences (q ≤ 0.05) in abundance of taxa across these treatment groups (Fig. 3c).

Similarly, there were significant differences (q ≤ 0.05) in cecal microbial communities between untreated chickens compared to chickens treated with unencapsulated phages and chickens treated with mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages based on Unweighted UniFrac metrics (Fig. 3d), with microbial communities in untreated chickens phylogenetically distinct from microbial communities in other groups. No clustering patterns were observed. Furthermore, these Unweighted UniFrac differences within treatment were seen over time, which was visually confirmed by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Additionally, there were no differences detected in Bray-Curtis and Jaccard metrics. Besides these, the PERMANOVA analysis based on Bray-Curtis distances showed low effect sizes (R² = 0.099 at d7; R² = 0.091 at d21; and R² = 0.127 overall) with non-significant p-values (p = 1.00). These results indicate that less than 13% of the total variation in microbial community composition could be explained by treatment or time, suggesting that overall community composition remained largely stable across time points.

Taxonomic analysis classified bacteria into seven phyla. Across all treatment groups, Firmicutes showed the highest relative abundance, reaching up to 95.75%, followed by Proteobacteria at 39.14% (Fig. 4a). Similarly, the relative abundance and variations of microbial communities at the phylum level across individual samples are shown in Fig. 6a (supplementary material). Differential analysis using DESeq2 and edgeR showed that at d21 (14 days after initial phage treatment), most of the bacterial taxa remained similar in chickens treated with the mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages compared to untreated chickens. However, the analysis showed differentially abundant taxa (q ≤ 0.05) in cecal microbial communities in chickens treated with mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages compared to untreated chickens as shown in Fig. 4b. Microbial families such as Lachnospiraceae, Actualibacteriaceae, Butyricocceae, and Ruminococceae and genera such as Lactobacillus, Blautia, Ornithococea, and Actualibacters were observed in greater abundance (q ≤ 0.05) in chickens treated with a mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages compared to untreated chickens. Similar results were found when comparing chickens treated with a mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages with chickens administered the Salmonella Galllinarum challenge but no phages. Certain bacterial families like Oscillospiraceae, Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococceae, and Actualibacteriaceae were more abundant (q ≤ 0.05) in chickens treated with a mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages compared to chickens administered the Salmonella Galllinarum challenge but no phages. Additionally, microbial genera such as Streptococcus, Enterococcus, Escherichia, and Eisenbergiella were reduced in chickens treated with a mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages and increased in chickens administered Salmonella Galllinarum challenge but no phages (Fig. 4c).

Chickens treated with unencapsulated phages contained 35 bacterial taxa in greater abundance (q ≤ 0.05) compared to chickens challenged with Salmonella Gallinarum but not treated with phages. Notably, some genera such as Clostridium, Blautia, and Actualibacter were more abundant (q ≤ 0.05) in chickens treated with unencapsulated phages compared to chickens challenged with Salmonella Gallinarum but not treated with phages. While others like Bacillus and Lachnoclostridium were reduced in the same group. In contrast, chickens administered microencapsulated phages did not show any differences in bacterial taxa compared to both the untreated chickens and chickens administered the Salmonella Gallinarum challenge but no phages.

The cecal microbiome of chickens treated with APEC phages was assessed prior to any phage treatment at day (d) 4 and five days after the initial phage treatment (d9). A total of 48 samples along with one community standard (PC) were sequenced. Diversity metrics were estimated after adjusting sampling depth to 13,435 sequences per sample. This helped to retain most of the feature counts while keeping more than 90% of samples with sufficient sampling depth. A total of four samples were lost for analysis due to their low count reads.

There were no differences in alpha and beta diversity metrics across different treatment groups at d4 or d9. Alpha diversity metrics at both time points (d4 and d9) revealed microbial communities that were closely related as shown in Fig. 5a. When each time point was analyzed separately, the microbial communities showed higher similarity within each time point but slightly diverged over time from d4 to d9. However, this change over time (temporal variation) was minimal, indicating the microbiome remained relatively stable across treatment groups throughout the experiment.

Taxonomic composition analysis showed that Firmicutes was the most abundant phylum in the cecal microbiome of chickens across all treatments, with a relative abundance of up to 93.37%, followed by Proteobacteria which reached up to 46.39%. Notably, the class Gammaproteobacteria was found to be relatively less abundant in chickens receiving a mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages compared to other treatment groups at d9 (Fig. 5b). The relative abundance of microbial communities at the phylum level across individual samples is shown in Fig. 6b (supplementary material).

The DESeq2 analysis comparing chickens receiving a mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages revealed significant differences in bacterial families; Lachnospiracea and Oscillospiracea were significantly higher (q ≤ 0.05) in chickens receiving a mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages. In contrast, Enterobacteriaceae and Borkfalkiacea were more abundant in chickens receiving APEC challenge but no phages compared to chickens receiving a mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages (Fig. 5c). At the genus level, chickens receiving a mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages had higher ratios (q ≤ 0.05) of genera such as Mediterranebacter, Blautia, and Plaudicola, compared to chickens receiving the APEC challenge but no phages. However, chickens receiving the APEC challenge but no phages had higher abundance (q ≤ 0.05) of Borkfolkia, Escherichia, Eryspelactoclostridium, and Clostridium ramosum compared to chickens receiving a mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages.

Further analysis revealed that bacterial families such as Erysipelotrichaceae and Oscillospiracea were in greater abundance (q ≤ 0.05) in chickens receiving a mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages compared to untreated chickens (Fig. 5d). Additionally, no differentially abundant taxa were detected across treatments at d9.

The taxonomic composition of the mock community was compared after sequencing to its known reference composition. This comparison helped to assess whether all expected microbial communities were detected in the samples and identify any potential contaminants in DNA extraction and sequencing stages. Our analysis confirmed the presence of all expected microbial species, including Bacillus subtilis, Escherichia coli, Salmonella enterica, Staphylococcus aureus, Listeria monocytogenes, Enterococcus faecalis, Limosilactobacillus fermentum, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, confirming the composition present in each sample was not affected by any contaminants.

Discussion

Poultry producers face significant challenges in protecting their flocks from infectious disease. Antibiotics are effective in preventing or controlling several bacterial infections, but poultry producers face calls to limit the use of the antibiotics to reduce the development and spread of antibiotic resistance bacteria. Numerous groups have shown that treating chickens with phage-based treatments can reduce colonization of targeted bacteria28,29. Indeed, oral administration of the phage cocktails described here was previously shown to significantly reduce Salmonella Gallinarum and avian pathogenic E. coli at the given concentrations30,31. Additionally, several other studies have described positive results of phage treatment using different concentrations (107 -1011) and through both oral gavage and feed supplementation10,15,32–34. While bacteriophages can effectively reduce bacterial colonization, there is still limited research on how phage therapy may impact the chicken itself. Here, we examined the impact of phage cocktails, previously shown to reduce targeted bacterial colonization, on the chicken gut microbiome, growth performance, and humoral immune response.

The current study showed that supplementation of broiler diets with phages under normal physiological conditions significantly enhanced AWG and ADG throughout the experimental period. These findings align with those of Sarrami et al.2022 who reported similar performance improvements in broilers supplemented with bacteriophages in their diets14. Similarly, Shaufi et al. (2023) observed that in a bacterial challenged group treated with a phage cocktail, phage treatment significantly improved growth performance in phage treated chickens compared to untreated chickens35. Additionally, Kim et al. (2015) reported improved laying performance of hens after phage supplementation36. However, other similarly designed studies by Kim et al.37, Pantoja-Don Juan et al.38, Wang et al.33, and Lee et al.39 found no or inconsistent impact of phage treatment on chicken growth37–40. Nevertheless, increases in body weight, when seen, could be attributed to the inhibitory effect of bacteriophages on harmful bacteria in GIT of chickens41,42 or increased growth of beneficial bacteria in the chicken gut as shown by our microbiome analysis.

The discrepancies among studies may result from several factors. Experimental conditions, such as higher stocking densities, can increase competition for feed and water, elevate stress levels, and lead to poor litter quality, all of which may negatively affect growth performance43. One study reported a slight increase in body weight gain of broilers during the starter phase (days 1–7) and finisher phase (days 22–35), with a significant (p < 0.05) increase observed over the overall experimental period (days 7–35)15. This improvement in body weight could be due to bacteriophages preventing harmful gut pathogens and stabilizing intestinal health15,42. Moreover, the gut microbiota tends to stabilize between 14 and 28 days of age in chickens44. Previous studies have also examined the effects of including bacteriophages at different concentrations (0.05% and 0.1%) on chicken growth performance41. Phage composition is critical, as higher dosages may be necessary to sustain growth performance. Supplementing bacteriophages at 1–1.5 g/kg of feed has been shown to enhance broiler performance14. Additionally, factors such as the number of phages, host range, and target species can influence results. Microencapsulation has been shown to improve phage stability against temperature, gastric conditions, and storage, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy1,3. Most studies have evaluated dietary supplementation of bacteriophages and its impact on growth performance due to consistent delivery and stability. Various administration routes have been tested, including oral gavage, water, spray, and feed. A 2021 meta-analysis suggest that administration via feed or oral gavage is more effective than delivery through drinking water or aerosol spray45. However, studies included in that meta-analysis were not conducted in commercial poultry environments. Thus, while administration of the treatment via feed may result in greater reductions of targeted bacteria, administration through water may be more feasible or economical for larger scale poultry farms. Likewise, questions remain as to how certain feed processing methods, e.g., pelleting via heat, may impact phage viability in manufactured feeds. While outside of the scope of the present study, future research should focus on identifying delivery methods that are both cost-effective and feasible in different production systems.

The current study found that long-term administration of phages did not induce any specific humoral immune response in the chicken. Assessing the immunogenicity of phages is vital, as a specific humoral immune response that neutralizes phages could limit their long-term efficacy46. Naghizedeh et al. (2019) found high levels of IgY and IgM against phages TM2 and TM4, which is in contrast with our findings. However, similar to our results, their study found that the level of antibodies in chickens treated with TM3 phage did not significantly differ from that of the control group47. Moreover, Huang et al. (2021) investigated the effect of bacteriophages on secretory immunoglobulins (sIgA) and serum immunoglobulins (IgM and IgG) in rabbits and did not find any significant differences in production of these antibodies in phage treated rabbits compared to the control group, which is also consistent with our findings48. The immune response to phages varies because phages have different structural proteins, resulting in different capsid elements exhibiting varying antigenicity and immunogenicity47,49–51. Antibody generation and the ability of those antibodies to neutralize phages depends mainly on specific phage type, particularly the composition of capsid and tail proteins. As, a result, phages differ in their immunogenicity and can trigger different antibody responses52,53. Thus, while phage treatment in the experiments described here did not result in an appreciable antibody response, it may be necessary to measure antibody responses to different phage cocktails used in different conditions.

Moreover, the host humoral response depends on many factors such as dose, route of administration, host immunity, and phage protein structure. Majewska et al. (2019) reported a weak immune response to oral administration of Staphylococcus phages in mice54, in contrast to their previous findings where they found that long-term oral administration of T4 phages in mice (over 240 days) induced serum and gut anti-phage antibodies. Notably, the induction of secretory IgA in response to oral phage delivery was slow, requiring over a month of continuous high-dose exposure. Even then, the mucosal immune response remained relatively weak. Notably, secretory IgA levels declined once phage administration stopped, allowing active phages to reappear in the gut55. These findings suggest that although some phages are immunogenic, they may not be effective in inducing a strong mucosal (local) immune response through oral administration. The distribution of phages in the body depends on the administration route, which could affect antibody induction56. Oral application may produce weak, or no phage specific antibody production23 as shown in our study. Whereas, intravenous and intraperitoneal injections may result in stronger antibody production than oral or topical administration23.

Most of the studies measuring phage-induced immune responses focus on neutralizing antibodies, which inhibit the infection of host bacterial cell by binding to phage tails. However, phage antibody interaction does not always lead to phage inactivation and the strength of humoral response appears to vary according to phage type57,58. It is important to consider that production of anti-phage antibodies can be stimulated not only by phages but also from bacterial components that could be present during phage preparation, especially lipopolysaccharides58.

Nevertheless, our results indicated that oral administration of phages did not produce a measurable increase in phage antibody production over the 35-day experiment. Coupled with results from other researchers, the rapid induction of phage-specific antibodies does not appear to be significant obstacle for therapeutic application of phages59. However, there is the possibility that repeated farm-scale use of phages can induce the production of neutralizing antibodies which could affect phage efficacy. Therefore, evaluating the survivability and effectiveness of multiple phages can help to optimize repeated phage therapy47. At the same time, additional long-term studies will be helpful to further investigate the effectiveness of repeated phage therapy especially in layer production.

In our study, treating chickens with Salmonella Gallinarum phages had only a limited effect on overall bacterial diversity (alpha and beta) while treating chickens with APEC phages did not produce a significant effect on bacterial diversity. As phages are highly specific for their host bacteria, this lack of effect on surrounding bacterial communities was expected and seen by others21,60. However, using the Weighted UniFrac metric, we observed differences in microbial communities between the chickens treated with unencapsulated Salmonella Gallinarum phages vs. chickens treated with microencapsulated Salmonella Gallinarum phages, indicating that chickens receiving Salmonella Gallinarum unencapsulated phages had higher microbial abundance according to phylogenetic diversity. These differences could be because unencapsulated phages are directly exposed to the gut environment when administered which could allow them to interact with targeted bacteria earlier and modulate surrounding microbial communities. Importantly, we included chicks fed alginate as a control as we used alginate in the microencapsulation of the phages. There were no appreciable differences in chick fed alginate vs. chicks fed only the basal diet.

We also observed differences in abundance of microbial communities between chickens treated with unencapsulated Salmonella Gallinarum phages and chickens treated with microencapsulated phages Salmonella Gallinarum vs. untreated chickens at d21. However, these groups were different from untreated chickens at d7, prior to any treatment or bacterial challenge. Thus, it is unclear whether the variations observed in these groups, compared to the untreated chickens, were solely due to phage treatment. Similar trends were noted in untreated chickens at d21 using the Unweighted UniFrac metric. Here, we observed higher phylogenetic diversity of microbial communities between untreated chickens vs. chickens treated with mixture of Salmonella Gallinarum unencapsulated and microencapsulated phages and chickens treated with Salmonella Gallinarum unencapsulated phages. Again, these differences were already present in untreated chickens at d7, prior to any treatment or challenge.

This variations in phylogenetic diversity could be due to different environmental conditions as seen in other studies61,62. In our study, chicks were housed in different rooms by treatment, but all rooms were in a single building under the same environmental conditions (e.g., temperature, lighting, flooring, stocking density, etc.) and experiments were conducted at the same time. However, housing in separate rooms may have introduced some room effects despite these controls. This suggests that the early differences likely reflect baseline variation or minor environmental effects61. Additionally, these differences were observed only with the UniFrac metrics and in one control group in comparison to other groups, and alpha diversity and other beta diversity metrics did not show such variations at d7. Therefore, these variations are unlikely to undermine the overall conclusion of phages impact on gut microbiome composition and normal development. Nevertheless, we observed that microbiome composition varied at different time points, generally stabilizing around d21 (Fig. 3). This aligns with the development of gut microbiome in broilers, as reported in previous studies63,64.

Our analysis confirmed that Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidetes were the predominant phyla in avian gut, which is consistent with previous findings65. The major families of Firmicutes include Lachnospiracea and Ruminococcaceae followed by Erysipelotrichaceae, Lactobacillaceae, and Veillonellaceae66. We found that chickens treated with a mixture of Salmonella Gallinarum microencapsulated and unprotected phages showed higher abundance of microbial families such as Oscillospiraceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Ruminococceae while chickens treated with mixture of APEC microencapsulated and unprotected phages also showed higher abundance of Butyricoccaceae and Lachnospiraceae. These families play significant roles in energy production pathways by metabolizing complex carbohydrates into short chain fatty acids67,68. Presence of these families indicates higher diversity of intestinal microbiota, as reported by Zeng et al. (2021)69.

At the genus level, chickens treated with a mixture of Salmonella Gallinarum microencapsulated and unprotected phages showed more abundance of Bifidobacterium, Blautia, and Lactobacillus while chickens treated with APEC phages showed a higher representation of Blautia and Mediterranebacter. These genera are known for enhancing the activity of digestive enzymes such as trypsin and lipases. Bifidobacteria are used as probiotics to improve gut health, and their higher abundance suggests that phage treatment may enhance growth of beneficial bacteria in chicken gut70. Additionally, Lactobacillus can stimulate butyrate producing microbiota, producing energy and helping to restore microbiome balance16,71. The genus Blautia may alleviate inflammatory and metabolic diseases while selectively inhibiting the proliferation of vancomycin resistant Enterococci and C. perfringens66,72. Liu et al. (2021) showed that Mediterranebacter as genus of Lachnospiracea family may also play a role in nutrient digestion73. Collectively, these bacterial groups maintain chicken intestinal health, improve nutrients absorption, and protect against pathogens. Thus, intestinal microbiota dysfunction can compromise growth performance. Research has also shown that phages could be utilized to promote the growth of beneficial bacteria and decrease harmful bacteria35,74.

In our study, higher abundance of Proteobacteria and reduction of Lactobacillus in chickens challenged with Salmonella Gallinarum but not receiving phage treatment suggests that Salmonella Gallinarum has a detrimental effect on the proliferation of beneficial microbiota in the chicken cecum. Additionally, significantly higher abundance of Streptococcus was observed in this same group compared to chickens receiving only Salmonella Gallinarum phages. Although, Streptococcus is a common microorganism in the GIT of broiler chickens, its elevated level may be indicative of disease in broiler chickens and is associated with reduced performance60,75,76. The abundance of Erysipelatoclostridium in chickens receiving APEC challenge and no phage treatment raises potential concern as Erysipelatoclostridium is considered as opportunistic pathogen in the human gut and predicts early life diarrhea in neonatal calves. Its function in poultry has not been thoroughly investigated66.

Taken together, findings from our microbiome analysis suggests that phage treatment (Salmonella Gallinarum or APEC phages) primarily had a direct effect by reducing targeted pathogens in the chicken gut37,77. The overall microbiome composition remained stable without any clear negative impact, thus suggesting limited microbiome mediated effect on growth15,78. However, a slight positive shift or higher abundance of beneficial taxa could contribute to maintaining homeostasis and intestinal health and inhibiting the growth of opportunistic pathogens79,80.

Conclusion

The data from this study indicate that treatment of chickens with bacteriophages increased both average daily gain and weight gain without triggering phage specific immune response or production of anti-phage antibodies. Additionally, the combination of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phage cocktail did not show any clear negative effect on chicken gut microbiome composition and development. In doing so, phage treatments may also promote the growth of beneficial bacteria and inhibit the growth of potential opportunistic pathogens in GIT. Thus, these findings suggest that treatment with these phage cocktails could be promising for controlling bacterial infections in chickens and could serve as cost-effective alternative to antibiotics. Going forward, we aim to investigate the efficacy of these phage cocktails through large scale animal trials in commercial settings in Pakistan. This will help us to assess their performance under local conditions and determine their potential as practical antibiotic alternatives. Additionally, future studies should also investigate whether the observed microbial shifts, such as the increased abundance of beneficial taxa, are related to any changes in the metabolic profile or whether phages themselves influence the host metabolism. In such cases, metabolomic studies will be helpful to investigate these interactions and impact of phage on host physiology. Finally, this study, like many others examining the efficacy of phage therapy, was conducted under research settings with small numbers of birds. Determining the feasibility of using phages to control these important bacterial pathogens will require the aforementioned large scale studies in production settings, but also thorough economic analysis to determine the best means of upscaling phage production and the most cost effective means of applying the treatment to thousands of birds simultaneously as would be needed if the phages were used for prevention or control of bacterial infections in large flocks.

Materials and methods

Ethical review

All animal experiments described here were developed according to ARRIVE guidelines and performed in accordance with protocols reviewed and approved by the Purdue University Animal Care and Use Committee (#2105002139E001).

Bacteriophages

The bacteriophages for the study were isolated from sewage water, wastewater, and poultry waste in the US and Pakistan. Characterization of those phages is the topic of another paper31. The Salmonella Gallinarum phage treatment used in the current experiment consisted of four unrelated Salmonella Gallinarum phages. Two of these phages were isolated in Pakistan and two were isolated in the US. Likewise, the APEC phage cocktail consisted of four unrelated APEC phages all isolated in the US30. Phages were microencapsulated using a sodium alginate-based method with some modifications as previously described81,82.

Growth Performance and Immune Response Model.

Growth Performance and Immune Response Model.

Growth Performance and Immune Response Model.

A total of 108 1-day-old broiler chickens (50:50 male: female) were obtained from a commercial hatchery. After a seven-day acclimation period, chickens were randomly allocated into four pens/treatment groups (27 chickens per pen): (1) chickens fed a control/basal diet without phage supplementation (CON group); (2) chickens fed a diet supplemented with 0.5 g sodium alginate beads + 0.5 mL LB broth per kg feed (ALG group); (3) chickens fed a diet supplemented with 0.5 mL unencapsulated + 0.5 g microencapsulated Salmonella Gallinarum phages (1.0–2.0 × 108 PFU/mL) per kg of feed; and (4) chickens fed a diet supplemented with 0.5 mL unencapsulated + 0.5 g microencapsulated phages of APEC phages (; 107 PFU/mL) per kg of feed. The experiment was performed in triplicate in a completely randomized block design. Chickens were raised under standard environmental conditions (temperature: 33 ± 1 °C for first 3 days and decreasing 3 °C per week until reaching 24 °C) with ad libitum access to water and feed. Lighting followed a standard lighting schedule used in commercial poultry production40. Body weights of chickens were recorded individually on d7, d14, d21, d28 and d35. The aggregated feed intake (FI) in each treatment group was recorded daily. Average daily gain (ADG), average weight gain (AWG), average daily feed intake, and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were then calculated for each bird40,83.

At d21, d28, and d35, six birds from each treatment group were euthanized by CO2 and cervical dislocation. Blood samples (2 mL/bird) were collected from birds’ hearts by using 1 in, 25-gauge needles. Blood samples (2 mL) were collected in blood vacutainers containing anti-coagulant (BD Vacutainer blood collection tubes; Fisher Scientific, Hanover Park, IL, USA). Serum was separated and stored at -20 °C to quantify levels of anti-phage IgM and IgG. For quantification of anti-phage IgA, fecal samples (~ 1.0 g) were collected into sterile Falcon tubes and diluted 1:10 in PBS. Subsequently, the samples were stored at -20 °C for quantification of IgA.

A flat-bottom 96-well plate (Fisher Scientific, Hanover Park, IL, USA) was coated with phages by adding 100 µL of a diluted phage suspension per well, prepared in 0.05 M carbonate-bicarbonate coating buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA). The plates were sealed and incubated overnight at 4°C84. Following incubation, the wells were washed five times with 1X PBS (0.05% Tween 20 solution; Fisher Scientific, Hanover Park, IL, USA). Subsequently, to block nonspecific binding, 100 µL of 1.0% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Fisher Scientific, Hanover Park, IL, USA) blocking buffer was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After removing the blocking solution, plates were washed with 1X PBS/0.05% Tween 20. Serum or fecal samples, diluted to 1:100, 1:200 and 1:400 in PBS for the detection of serum IgM, IgG, and fecal IgA, respectively85, were added (100 µL) per well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. The wells were then washed five times with washing buffer. Secondary antibodies (diluted 1:20,000 in 1% BSA), consisting of horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-chicken IgM (Fisher Scientific, Hanover Park, IL, USA), IgG (Novus Biologics, Centennial, CO, USA), and IgA (Fisher Scientific, Hanover Park, IL, USA), were added to the plates, respectively, and plates were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with no exposure to light. After another five washes with the washing buffer, 100 µL of tetramethylbenzidine (TMB; Fisher Scientific, Hanover Park, IL, USA) was added per well. After 15 min of incubation, 25 µL stop solution (1M H2SO4; Fisher Scientific, Hanover Park, IL, USA) was added to each well and absorbance measured at 450 nm. Background values were determined by using phage coated wells where PBS was added instead of the serum sample to serve as a blank control. The background/blank values were subtracted from the main readings and the average value of each triplicate was calculated.

Data were analyzed by ANOVA using the General Linear Models (GLM) procedure of SAS (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) with pen as the experimental unit for performance comparisons. Tukey’s tests were used to identify any differences between treatments and values were considered statistically different for multiple comparisons at p ≤ 0.05. The Kruskal Wallis test (p ≤ 0.05) was performed to analyze phage specific immune response.

2.Salmonella Gallinarum Challenge Model (Microbiome).

2.Salmonella Gallinarum Challenge Model (Microbiome).

Salmonella Gallinarum Challenge Model (Microbiome).

A total of 170 1-day-old broiler chickens (50:50 male: female) were obtained from a commercial hatchery. After a seven-day acclimation period, the chicks were randomly assigned to one of five pens (34 chicks per pen) corresponding to the five treatments: (1) chickens receiving no challenge or phage treatment (trt1); (2) chickens challenged with Salmonella Gallinarum but no phage treatment (trt2; 109 CFU/mL); (3) chickens challenged with Salmonella Gallinarum and treated with microencapsulated phages (trt3; 108 to 2 × 108 PFU/mL per phage; gavage); (4) chickens challenged with Salmonella Gallinarum and treated with unencapsulated phages (trt4; 1.0 to 2.0 × 108 PFU/mL per phage); (5) and chickens challenged with Salmonella Gallinarum and treated with 1:1 mixture of unencapsulated and microencapsulated phages (trt5; 1.0 to 2.0 × 108 PFU/mL per phage). The chickens were provided with standard environmental conditions (temperature: 33 ± 1 °C for first 3 d and decreasing 3 °C per week until reaching 24 °C) with ad libitum access to water and feed. Lighting followed a standard lighting schedule used in commercial poultry production40. Chickens in trt3, trt4, and trt5 received their respective phage treatments daily via oral gavage from d12-d17 while all challenged chickens receiving the Salmonella Gallinarum challenge did so on d14.

At d7, six chicks were randomly selected from each pen (n = 30) and euthanized by CO2 and cervical dislocation. The final necropsy was performed on d21, with cecal contents recovered from six birds per pen (n = 30). Cecal contents were recovered from each bird for microbiome analysis as described below.

3.APEC challenge model (microbiome).

3.APEC challenge model (microbiome).

APEC challenge model (microbiome).

A total of 104 1-day-old broiler chickens (50:50 male: female) were purchased from a commercial hatchery and randomly distributed into four treatment groups (n = 26 chicken per pen) as follows: (1) chickens receiving no APEC challenge or treatment (trt1); (2) chickens challenged with APEC (trt2; 1 mL at 107 CFU/mL; gavage); (3) chicken receiving 1:1 mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages, but no bacterial challenge (trt3; 0.5 g + 0.5 mL at 108 PFU/ mL); (4) chickens challenged with APEC and treated with 1:1 mixture of microencapsulated and unencapsulated phages (trt4; 0.5 g + 0.5 mL at 108 PFU/ mL). Chickens were provided with standard environmental conditions (temperature: 33 ± 1 °C) with ad libitum water and feed. Lighting followed a standard lighting schedule used in commercial poultry production40. Chicks were provided four days to acclimate prior to any administration of treatments. Chickens received their respective phage treatment from d4 to d7 and APEC challenge on d5. At d4, prior to administration of any treatments, six chickens from each pen (n = 24) were euthanized by CO2 and cervical dislocation, and their cecal contents were collected. The final necropsy was performed on d9 following phage treatments and bacterial challenge, and cecal contents from six chickens per pen (n = 24) were collected. Cecal samples (n = 48) were further processed for microbiome analysis as described above for Salmonella Gallinarum.

4.DNA Extraction and 16 S rRNA Gene Amplification.

4.DNA Extraction and 16 S rRNA Gene Amplification.

DNA Extraction and 16 S rRNA Gene Amplification.

For microbiome analysis, DNA was extracted from 0.5 g samples of cecal contents using a Fast DNA™ Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biologicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. DNA quality was assessed visually by gel electrophoresis and quantified by fluorometer (Qubit 4; Invitrogen, Hanover Park, IL, USA). The extracted DNA was stored at -20 °C for further processing. Afterwards, the hypervariable region V3-V4 of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR as described previously86. Briefly, the primers contained overhang adapters (before spacers) complementary to the flow cell bases utilized for Illumina sequencing87. Heterogeneity spacers were included (0–7 bp) in primers to minimize quality issues arising from lower sequence diversity88. The reaction mixture contained 25 mL KAPA Hifi Hotstart Ready Mix (Roche Sequencing and Life Sciences, Indianapolis, IN, USA), 10 µM primers (Integrated DNA Technologies, Redwood City, CA, USA), 2 µL extracted DNA and sterile ddH20 to reach a final volume of 50 µL. The PCR used the following cycling sequence: 1 cycle at 94 °C for 5 min, 25 cycles at 94 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s followed by a single extension cycle for 5 min at 72 °C. The amplified PCR products were initially visualized by gel electrophoresis.

The PCR amplicons were purified using Agencourt AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences, Indianapolis, IN, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A second PCR was done with the cleaned PCR amplicons for indexing samples prior to sequencing. The PCR mixture contained DNA (5 µL of PCR amplicon), 25 µL of KAPA Hifi Hotstart Ready Mix,10 µM index primers (TG Nextra XT Index Kit V2 Set A), and sterile ddH2O to reach a final volume 50 µL. Each sample was amplified with a different set of primers87 allowing for the pooling of all the samples for sequencing. The cycling conditions used were: 1 cycle at 94 °C for 3 min, 12 cycles at 94 °C for 30 s, 64 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s, and final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The integrity of amplified products was visually verified by gel electrophoresis and concentration was determined by fluorometer. The amplified PCR products were purified as discussed above and normalized for library synthesis89. Finally, purified PCR products from all samples were pooled together into a single library including the mock community90 in equimolar concentrations and quantified by fluorometer. Pooling the library was time and cost efficient while also ensuring consistent and reliable data quality. The pooled library was sent to Purdue Genomics Core Facility to conduct paired end sequencing (2 × 250) using the Illumina MiSeq (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) platform.

The resultant FASTQ files were analyzed through QIIME 2 software [v.2-2022.8]91, which is an open-source software package for the comparison and analysis of microbial communities. The quality of sequences was determined by the average PHRED Q (quality) score of bases. Afterward, the DADA2 denoise module was used for correcting and quality filtering of Illumina sequences. For chimera (sequences generated due to PCR amplification of multiple templates or parent sequences) identification and removal, Qiime2 UCHIME algorithm was used. Reads were trimmed and truncated based on quality score profiles using the following parameters: --p-trim-left-f 0, --p-trim-left-r 0, --p-trunc-len-f 250, and --p-trunc-len-r 250. Chimeric sequences were initially excluded (--p-chimera-method none), and subsequent chimera detection and removal was performed using the vsearch uchime-denovo and uchime-ref methods. The initial denoising step using DADA2 produced amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) at single-nucleotide resolution. These ASVs were subsequently clustered using the dbOTU (distribution-based OTU) algorithm, which groups sequences based on both sequence similarity and co-occurrence patterns across samples.

Diversity was assessed through alpha and beta diversity metrices and analyzed between treatments after samples were rarefied. All OTUs were aligned with MAFFT92(via q2-alignment) and used to construct a phylogeny with fasttree293 (via q2-phylogeny). For the taxonomic assignment, the Greengenes 2010.10 database and q2-feature-classifier were used. Differentially abundant taxa were identified using DESeq294 and edgeR95. The differential abundance analysis was performed using DESeq2 on unrarefied (raw) count data generated from the DADA2/DBOTU pipeline. DESeq2’s internal normalization procedure (estimateSizeFactors) was applied to account for library size differences. No batch or housing-related confounders were recorded in the experimental design; therefore, the model included only the DiseaseGroup variable. In DESeq2, count data were normalized, and a negative binomial model was applied using the Wald test to identify differentially expressed features. In edgeR, normalization was performed using the TMM (trimmed mean of M-values) method, followed by dispersion estimation. A generalized linear model (GLM) approach was applied using the quasi-likelihood F-test (glmQLFTest) to assess differential abundance. Statistical significance was determined with padj = q ≤ 0.05 using PERMANOVA between treatment groups and FDR p-value (q-value).
