Introduction

Brain involvement in hydatid disease accounts for 1–2% of all Echinococcus granulosus infections. Cerebral hydatid cysts are most commonly located in the supratentorial intracerebral region, with extradural lesions being exceptionally rare. These cysts often remain asymptomatic until they attain significant size. The clinical presentation of central nervous system involvement varies depending on lesion size and location, with headache and vomiting being the most common symptoms[1,2]. Diagnosis is based on epidemiological data, clinical findings, serological tests, and imaging modalities such as ultrasonography (US), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Surgical intervention remains the primary treatment, focusing on the removal of the cyst without rupture[3,4].

Case presentation

This report details the case of a 10-year-old male with a rare supratentorial extradural lesion, presenting with significant clinical symptoms and imaging findings.

The patient, from a rural area, presented with a 6-month history of progressive headache. Over time, weakness in the left limbs developed. On examination, left hemiparesis was noted, along with protrusion and fluctuation in the right parietal region. Routine laboratory tests were within normal limits. MRI revealed a large extradural cyst in the right temporoparietal region (Fig. 1). A parietal bone defect (3.0 × 3.0 cm) with cyst protrusion was identified. The cyst measured approximately 16 × 12 cm, causing a 12 mm leftward midline shift due to mass effect. The hemagglutination test for hydatid cysts was negative, and transthoracic echocardiography and abdominal tomography ruled out other hydatid cysts.Figure 1.Cranial MRI shows an extradural cystic lesion in the right parieto-temporal region.

The patient was diagnosed with a cerebral hydatid cyst and started on albendazole (10 mg/kg/day). After obtaining informed consent, a craniotomy was performed, and the cyst was removed without rupture (Fig. 2a, b). The eroded bone flap was refined and repositioned (Fig. 2c). The surgical site was irrigated with hypertonic sodium chloride. Histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of hydatid cyst. Postoperatively, albendazole treatment was continued. At follow-up, the patient exhibited minimal residual left hemiparesis, with imaging confirming resolution of the cyst and midline shift (Fig. 3). Discussion

Hydatid cysts result from infection by Echinococcus larvae. Humans become accidental intermediate hosts through contact with infected animals or ingestion of food contaminated with parasite eggs[1]. Orally ingested eggs hatch in the gastrointestinal tract, and the embryos disseminate hematogenously, developing into cystic larvae. In children, a high prevalence of cerebral hydatid cysts is attributed to the patent ductus arteriosus[2,3].

Cerebral hydatid cysts are categorized as primary or secondary. Primary cysts, typically singular, result from larvae bypassing hepatic and pulmonary filters and lodging in the brain. Secondary cysts, often multiple, occur following rupture of primary extracranial cysts[4].

Symptoms of cerebral hydatid cysts depend on location and size, with headache and vomiting being the most common. Neurological deficits such as hemiparesis, as observed in our patient, develop as intracranial pressure increases. Imaging, particularly MRI, is essential for diagnosis, providing superior detail of cyst walls and surrounding structures. Differential diagnoses include arachnoid cysts, porencephalic cysts, cystic tumors, and abscesses.

Surgical removal of the cyst without rupture is the cornerstone of treatment. The Dowling-Orlando technique is preferred, with irrigation using hypertonic sodium chloride to minimize recurrence risk. Albendazole is used adjunctively, given its ability to penetrate the blood-brain barrier. Complications such as subdural effusion, obstructive hydrocephalus, and postoperative epilepsy are rare but documented. Our patient experienced no seizures or significant complications.

Extradural hydatid cysts are particularly rare, with limited cases reported in the literature. These cysts exert mass effects, causing bone thinning and erosion, as seen in our patient. This case underscores the importance of considering hydatid disease in differential diagnoses of cystic brain lesions, particularly in endemic areas.

Conclusion

Hydatid disease, though uncommon, primarily affects the pediatric population and is typically supratentorial. Intracranial extradural hydatid cysts are exceptionally rare. Clinical features depend on cyst location, with symptoms arising from increased intracranial pressure and mass effects. Early diagnosis and surgical intervention are critical for favorable outcomes. This case highlights the need for awareness of this rare pathology, particularly in endemic regions.
