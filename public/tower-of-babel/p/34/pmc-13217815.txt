Improving cerebral palsy children’s oral hygiene: an 18-month school-based oral health promotion program in Bangkok, Thailand
Authors: Siriporn Songsiripradubboon, Sudaduang Krisdapong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionChildren with cerebral palsy (CP) have relatively poor oral health. Oral health behavioral programs previously reported were limited to oral health education. This study aimed to develop a community participatory oral health promotion program for CP children and to evaluate their oral hygiene over 18 months.Materials and methodsA longitudinal school-based intervention study was conducted at a Thai public school using a one-group quasi-experimental design and involving 23 CP children (mean age 12). The program included community study, policy advocacy, staff training, goal setting, curriculum development, dormitory arrangements, and sponsorship. Oral hygiene was assessed via dental plaque examinations at baseline and five follow-ups (1 week, 2 weeks, 3 months, 9 months, and 18 months). Data on age, sex, residency, and CP severity were collected. Oral hygiene was categorized as good, fair, poor. Statistical analyses included Wilcoxon signed-rank test, McNemar’s exact tests, and Mann-Whitney U tests (p < 0.05).ResultsSeven oral health activities were implemented. At the end of the program, mean plaque scores significantly decreased by 44% (Cohen’s d = 1.36). At baseline, no children had good oral hygiene. At 9- and 18-month follow-ups, 26% of children demonstrated good oral hygiene. Older children showed greater improvements (p = 0.038 for 1-week, p = 0.014 for 2-week follow-ups), while those with severe disabilities improved notably at the first (p = 0.022) and last follow-ups (p = 0.037).ConclusionOver an 18-month program, CP children effectively improved their oral hygiene. Our study explicitly highlights the success of the community participatory approach as a model for future interventions.

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a neurological condition affecting 1.5-3 out of every 1000 live newborns globally and is associated with substantial physical impairment [1]. Children with CP typically have worse oral health, namely, higher caries risk, malocclusion, and poor oral hygiene [2–6], and lower Oral Health-Related Quality of Life (OHRQoL) than children without CP [7, 8]. Regardless of age, their daily activities, including feeding and toothbrushing, typically depend on caregivers. The effectiveness of oral hygiene practices, either by themselves or by their caregivers, can be hampered by CP’s severity. In addition, coexisting medications may impact gingival health and saliva properties, thus increasing the risk of oral diseases [9, 10]. However, parents or caregivers may often rank oral health as a lower priority than children’s physical problems, contributing to relatively poor oral health [11]. Due to parental neglect and transportation challenges, CP children often visit dentists only for emergencies [12, 13]. Consequently, dental treatment is mostly limited to extractions, potentially attributed to the disease’s severity, a lack of cooperative behavior, and limitations of dentists, such as insufficient specialized training, equipment, and difficulties in building relationships [14, 15].

Poor oral health and difficulties in accessing appropriate dental services underscore the necessity of oral health promotion programs to enhance oral health behaviors of disabled children. However, behavioral programs for disabled children previously reported primarily focused on oral health education, namely, training patients and/or caregivers, and the use of specialized toothbrushing devices or chemical agents [16–18]. Adequate individuals’ skills obtained by health education do not guarantee sustainably better behaviors unless broader socio-environmental determinants of health have also been addressed [19]. World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended various oral health promotion interventions in schools including training teacher and school personnel, developing school’s commitment and policies, classroom health education, improving school health-related environments and collaboration with parents [20]. Such environmental and policy interventions require a community participatory approach. Dental professionals must collaborate with policymakers and school staff to establish oral health promotion activities and regulations suitable for each school [20].

Prior studies [17, 21, 22] on improving oral hygiene of disabled children focused on education only, not on a participatory approach addressing environmental and policy changes. Therefore, this study aims to (1) develop a community participatory oral health promotion program in a school for CP children; (2) evaluate oral hygiene outcomes over 18 months; and (3) compare outcomes by age, residency, and CP severity.

Methods

Study design and sample procedures

This project is an oral health promotion initiative, which by its nature does not necessarily require a control group [23]; accordingly, the study was designed as a one-group quasi-experimental study. A study was conducted in a Thai public school for CP children where mobile dental units provided free dental services twice a year. The school provides education up to lower secondary level. Study population was children in the first year of lower secondary education, typically aged 12 years. This group was selected because, first, the program involved classroom toothbrushing practices, and these children represented the youngest cohort in the school who possessed sufficient ability to learn and practice toothbrushing. We found that primary school CP children were too young, as they had not yet developed effective manual control. The second reason was that this group could be followed for the longest possible period. The original plan aimed to follow the children for as long as possible, more than 2 years. Eligible participants were students with CP who, together with their parents, agreed to participate in the oral health promotion program throughout the study period. Children with fewer than 12 teeth or those unable to cooperate with the oral examination were excluded. Throughout the program, no participants were withdrawn or excluded.

The minimum required sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1) for a paired t-test. Based on data from a previous study [21] (mean difference = 1.40, estimated SDdiff = 1.17), an effect size of Cohen’s d = 1.20 was used. With 80% power and α = 0.05, a minimum of eight participants was required. The assumed effect size was large and reflected the substantial short-term improvements reported in intensive interventions for vulnerable populations. However, after discussions with the school team regarding the practicality of implementing classroom toothbrushing practices and their potential benefits for students, all 23 eligible children in the first year of lower secondary level were included. This approach also helped ensure adequate statistical power and allowed for potential attrition. This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Chulalongkorn University (Approval No. HREC-DCU 2023-092).

Data collection and interventions

Children underwent oral examinations for dental plaque at baseline and five follow-up visits, occurring at 1 week, 2 weeks, 3 months, 9 months, and 18 months after launching the program. A timeline illustrating the sequence of steps for approaching the school, data collections, and interventions were present in Fig. 1. All oral examinations were conducted by a single, experienced pediatric dentist. To ensure data collection reliability throughout the long-term follow-up period, duplicate examinations were conducted at each visit on 10% of the sample (three children), with a minimum 30-minute interval between examinations. During this interval, the children were not allowed to consume food or perform any oral hygiene practices. Cohen’s kappa statistics were performed for the intra-examiner reliability, indicating a good reliability with a Kappa coefficient value (k) of 0.80. Dental plaque on six surfaces of six teeth was examined using the Debris Index-Simplified (DI-S) [25]. A plaque score ranging from 0 to 3 was recorded for each surface, indicating the extent of dental plaque coverage. Personal characteristics of the children, including age, sex, residency, and severity of CP disability, were collected from school records.

Following dental plaque examination, interventions were provided to children or parents on the same day. Over the 18-month program, 7 interventions were given to children and parents at 4 different times (Fig. 1). It is essential to note that oral health promotion involves changing behaviors and social conditions through context-appropriate interventions. Thus, determining appropriate interventions and launch times involves collaboration with the community in the longitudinal process, rather than solely relying on professionals. In this study, only the first intervention, conducted on the same day as baseline data collection, was pre-defined by the dental team. The subsequent 6 interventions were adaptively developed over time through dental and school team meetings. Community participation was evaluated through meeting logs and qualitative feedback of school teams, leading to an adapted study design or interventions according to school preferences (such as the inclusion of all 23 children instead of the calculated sample size of 8, as well as details of the 2nd to 7th interventions). Classroom teaching was usually divided into two classrooms. However, due to the small number of children, all participants were combined into a single group and received all interventions simultaneously, except for dormitory visit, which were conducted separately for male and female dormitories. The 7 interventions were as follows:

Dental plaque examination and individual toothbrushing training, by teachers and caregivers with the assistance of the dental team and physical therapists. Data analysis

Data analysis utilized SPSS version 19 for Windows (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). The plaque score for each child was calculated as the sum of 6 surface scores divided by 6, resulting in a range of 0–3. Children were categorized into good (0-0.6), fair (> 0.6–1.5), and poor (> 1.5-3) oral hygiene. This classification was based on the Oral Hygiene Index–Simplified (OHI-S), ranging from 0 to 6, which is the sum of the Debris Index-Simplified (DI-S), ranging from 0 to 3, and the Calculus Index-Simplified (CI-S), ranging from 0 to 3. The OHI-S has been categorized as good (0–1.2), fair (1.3–3.0), and poor (3.1–6.0) [25, 26]. Since this study assessed only DI-S, the cutoff values were accordingly halved [27]. Missing values were not imputed; analyses were performed using available-case analysis (pairwise deletion). For each Wilcoxon signed-rank test and McNemar’s exact test comparing baseline to a follow-up point (e.g., baseline vs. 1 week), only subjects with complete data for that specific pair of time points were included. This approach resulted in the varying sample sizes reported for each comparison.

Considering the typical age range of lower secondary school children (12–14 years), participants were classified into two groups: 12–14 years old and 15 years old or older. Children with diplegia and hemiplegia were classified as mild CP condition, while those with triplegia and quadriplegia were categorized as severe CP condition. To identify potential trends, subgroup comparisons based on age group, residency (home/school), and severity of CP disability were conducted as exploratory analyses. Mann-Whitney U tests were performed for comparing mean plaque scores at each visit based on these factors. Additionally, to assess variations in oral hygiene improvement at each visit among children with different personal characteristics, changes in plaque scores from baseline were calculated and compared between groups using Mann-Whitney U tests. Statistical significance was considered when the p-value was less than 0.05.

Results

Sample characteristics

Twenty-three children were initially enrolled (100% of first-year lower secondary school children), and 17 completed all visits. Some were absent at 1 week, 2 weeks, and 3-month follow-ups due to illness, sports competitions, and medical check-ups. However, all 23 children attended baseline, 9-month, and 18-month follow-up visits.

Among 23 children, 10 (43.48%) were girls and 13 (56.52%) were boys. Ages ranged from 12 to 17 years, with a mean of 14.75 (± 3.2) years. It should be noted that several children exceeded the typical age for this grade level due to delayed educational progression associated with cerebral palsy. Eleven children (47.83%) had diplegia, while 3 (13.04%), 1 (4.35%), and 8 (34.78%) children had hemiplegia, triplegia, and quadriplegia, respectively. The majority (16 out of 23, 69.57%) studied and lived at school, while the remaining seven children (30.43%) were day students, going back home after school hours.

Oral health promotion program in school

Using a community participatory approach, the school established seven oral health-promoting activities and/or regulations for children and parents as follows:

Classroom education by teachers and poster display

The 1-hour session on toothbrushing was integrated into the health and hygiene subject, scheduled once per semester. The lesson covered toothbrushing methods, dental plaque examination, classroom practice using the “spit, don’t rinse” technique, and dental plaque re-examination. The responsible teacher led the session, with class teachers assisting in plaque examination. A mirror was provided for children to see their own plaque. Initially, dark-colored tapered straws with rounded tips were used for plaque detection, later replaced with disclosing tablets for practicality based on teachers’ feedback. After brushing, children were re-checked, and if dental plaque remained, they repeated the toothbrushing process.

Fluoridated toothpaste in school dormitory

At the end of the program, all non-fluoridated toothpastes were replaced by fluoridated ones through parental cooperation and sponsorship. Caregivers were able to distinguish between fluoridated and non-fluoridated toothpaste.

Bedtime toothbrushing and poster display in dormitory

The school designed a method to ensure all children brushed their teeth outside the bathroom without using water taps, following the “spit, don’t rinse” technique advised by the dental team. Around 10 children formed a circle on the floor with a bucket in the middle. They brushed their teeth together, spat the toothpaste slurry into the bucket, and wiped their mouths with their own napkins. Caregivers later emptied and cleaned the buckets. This approach facilitated caregivers in monitoring all children’s toothbrushing and extended brushing time as children worked in groups. Children’s napkins were regularly washed by caregivers. For severely disabled children, caregivers provided individual assistance. The dental team visited school dormitory for assessing the new method as well as giving motivation to children.

Morning toothbrushing after breakfast

An additional set of toothbrush and toothpaste for all children were supported by a dental NGO. Morning toothbrushing was changed from before breakfast in the dormitory to after breakfast in the canteen. Parents were also informed in the meeting to provide 2 sets of toothbrush and toothpaste for their children in the future.

Parental involvements

After successfully establishing the toothbrushing activity in the dormitory with positive feedback from children and caregivers, the program extended to involve parents. A 1.5 h session of oral health education and toothbrushing practice was arranged for parents on an annual school-parents meeting day. The session covered self-care practices, toothbrushing using the “spit don’t rinse” technique. Parents were informed about the ongoing oral health promotion program and their required cooperation. The school committed to incorporate this session into the annual school-parents meeting, typically conducted at the beginning of the first school semester.

After lunchtime toothbrushing at school

Efforts were made to promote lunchtime toothbrushing for children. Following lunch at the canteen, children returned to their classrooms, where a toothbrushing song was used to signal the activity. Teachers assisted in encouraging and guiding the children. Children brushed their teeth in classrooms, corridors, and used nearby toilets for spitting toothpaste slurry and washing their toothbrushes, minimizing toilet usage time. Limited toilet space was not an issue. Additionally, a dental NGO supported toothbrushes and toothpaste for lunchtime brushing through our facilitation.

“No candy” regulations

Despite frequent donations of sugary snacks and drinks to children by the public, the school directors deemed it impossible to refuse all such donations currently. To initiate a reduction in sugary snack consumption, a rule prohibiting candy was established-specifically, a “no candy for donations” policy. Staff handling donations were instructed to communicate this rule to donors, and children were not allowed to consume candy at school. Pictorial signs indicating “no candy allowed” were displayed in school areas, and teachers assisted in inspecting and discarding candies if found.

Oral hygiene of the sample

Figures 2 and 3 illustrate overall comparisons throughout the 18-month period, of which statistical comparisons were not performed because the groups at the six visits were not identical, that is, the number of children varied between 21 and 23. By the first follow-up (1 week), a mean score reduction was 37% (from 1.63 to 1.02), thereafter, remained relatively steady, then further decreased to 44% (around 0.9) at the last 2 visits (9 and 18 months). Comparisons between the proportions of children with good, fair, and poor oral hygiene across the 6 visits, shown in Fig. 3, were in line with those in Fig. 2, that is, oral hygiene notably improved since the first follow-up visit (1 week). At baseline, no child had good oral hygiene, while proportions of children with good oral hygiene increased to 10% at the first follow-up, and further increased to nearly 30% at the last 2 visits (9 and 18 months). On the other hand, the proportion of children with poor oral hygiene decreased from 30% at baseline to less than 10% (only 2 children) at the last 2 visits (9 and 18 months).

Table 2 illustrates paired comparisons between baseline and each of the 5 follow-up visits. Each comparison included only children who attended both the baseline and the follow-up visit; thus, the groups were identical and qualified for statistical comparison. The number of pairs ranged from 21 to 23 (91–100% response rates). Comparisons of mean changed plaque scores from baseline to each follow-up visit between children with different personal characteristics

Table 4 presents mean changes in plaque scores from baseline to each follow-up visit by age, residency, and CP severity. The results were in line with Table 3. Statistically significant differences in changed plaque scores were found between age groups at the first and second follow-up visits, with older children showing greater improvement in oral hygiene. Significant differences were also observed between children with mild and severe disabilities at the first and last follow-up visits, as those with severe disabilities demonstrated greater plaque reduction. Although no significant difference was found by student residency, the improvement among children living at school was approximately twice that of those living at home at the second follow-up visit. In contrast, those living at home showed better outcomes at the end of the program.

Discussion

Our 18-months oral health promotion program noticeably improved oral hygiene of CP children, with significant improvements since the first week after the program. The improvement was greater and maintained for the 9 and 18-month follow-up visits with the plaque reductions of 43–44%. The proportions of children with good oral hygiene were also significantly higher than at baseline (6% vs. 0%) at the last two visits. The immediate improvement was consistent with findings from previous studies on oral practice training in various populations, including healthy and disabled children or adolescents [17, 28–30]. Limited research exists on oral practice programs for disabled individuals. For short-term effects (1–3 months), studies by Maiya et al. (2015) and Hartwig et al. (2017) reported a significant reduction in dental plaque scores after 6-week and 5-week toothbrushing programs for the caregivers of CP and disabled children, respectively [17, 31]. Additionally, Binkley et al. (2014) reported a significant decrease in dental plaque scores after a 1-month oral health program for disabled adults [29]. Therefore, the effectiveness of oral educational and toothbrushing program for disabled or healthy individuals during a few weeks to around 1 month could be expected.

Over a 3-month period, our study observed a sustained and significant reduction in dental plaque scores among CP children, consistent with findings from studies on healthy children of similar age [28, 30, 32]. However, they did not provide additional intervention before a 3-month evaluation as our study did, which were repeated education and a dormitory visit. In a study on a 3-month program for CP children, which included two repeated educational sessions, successful plaque reduction was reported at 3 months [22]. However, a 3-month program for disabled adults, featuring weekly reinforcements, failed to significantly reduce plaque scores in CP subjects, while plaque reductions were significant in overall and other subgroups subjects (mental disability, trisomy, and learning difficulties) [33]. One potential explanation is the age of the subjects; improving skilled behaviors in adults with CP may be more challenging than in children. Therefore, a successful 3-month oral program for CP children may require reinforcement or additional intervention, unlike in healthy children. Additionally, for older age groups of CP subjects, relying solely on repeated educational sessions may be insufficient.

For medium-term effects (6–9 months), previous studies on a 6-month program for healthy children reported varied results. Those with educational reinforcement at 3 months showed further plaque reduction at 6 months [30, 32], while a study with no reinforcement observed an increase in plaque scores at 6 months [28]. A 6-month program for CP adults that included one educational reinforcement could not maintain subjects’ oral hygiene as their plaque scores at 6-month were higher than that of 2-month evaluation [21]. Although repeated education was not provided, our study achieved best and sustained improvements at 9- and 18-month periods due to other interventions, i.e. dormitory visits, poster displays, and parental education. Therefore, repeated education may assist in sustaining proper oral hygiene for a 6-month period in healthy subjects, however, it might be insufficient for CP subjects.

For long-term effects (around a year or more), all previous studies were limited to healthy children, none involved disabled children. However, a continuation of oral hygiene improvement after receiving a program for one and half year or longer had never been reported. A 2-year program with monthly educational reinforcement during 8–13 months showed significantly better oral hygiene, but in-between oral hygiene was not reported [34]. Nine and 15-month toothbrushing programs for healthy children, with educational reinforcement every three months, demonstrated successful improvements [35]. An annual reinforcement was significantly less effective than a three-month reinforcement [35], supporting the idea that each educational session or reinforcement benefits healthy children for only three months.

While the study’s comparisons between children with different characteristics implied the influence of a school dormitory visit and parental involvement on children’s oral hygiene, it should be noted that these subgroup analyses were exploratory. Because the study was not initially powered for these comparisons, these specific findings should be interpreted with caution. After the dental team’s dormitory visit, children living at school had a significantly lower mean plaque score (0.89 vs. 1.17) and a notably higher changed plaque score, almost twice that of children living at home (0.73 vs. 0.48) (Tables 3 and 4). Similar trends were observed for children living at home at the last follow-up visit (18 months), after the dental team’s participation in a school meeting day that provided education and toothbrushing practice for parents as well asking for their cooperation in our program. This might also explain the greater improvement in severe CP children compared to those with mild disability at the last follow-up visit, as many severe CP children had their parents with them during school hours. In addition, there were limited number of physical therapists of schools, therefore, only severe CP children received individual assistance by therapists during the first toothbrushing practice session. Closer trained caregiver and parental involvements seem to be the key for greater improvement of severe CP children.

Despite the subjects and caregivers being highly motivated and committed to maintain proper practices for the long term, the effectiveness of educational and toothbrushing program in reducing plaque is only acknowledged in the short term [28, 36]. Periodic reinforcements or repeated education have been proposed as solutions [21, 37]. However, the sustainability of the program for over a year after the last repeated education until the program’s end did not seem solely explained by repeated education, but community participatory interventions. In partnership with school directors and caregivers, 7 oral health-promoting activities and regulations (classroom education, fluoridated toothpaste use in school dormitory, bedtime toothbrushing in dormitory, morning toothbrushing after breakfast, parental involvement, after lunchtime toothbrushing at school, and no-candy allowance) were developed making the focus extend from children to their environments. These interventions align with the Ottawa Charter and WHO’s recommendations in relation to creating school policies and supportive environments as well as empowering community actions [19, 20]. Previous studies showed that school oral health promotion programs involving teachers and parents, i.e. active parental participation, integrated oral health lessons in school curriculum, regular classroom teaching and daily toothbrushing practice, could achieve positive oral health outcomes [38–41]. Nevertheless, this study is novel in that it appears to be the first participatory oral health program for children with CP sustained beyond one year.

The study demonstrates significant and sustained improvement in CP children’s oral hygiene and offers a model for integrating health promotion into school environments for children with disabilities. The work is highly relevant to both dental public health policy and special needs dentistry, especially for scaling up similar initiatives in other schools or health systems. The approach should shift from being dentist-led to community-participatory, addressing school policies and environments. Nevertheless, it should be more cautious about generalizing the study’s results beyond the specific school context and acknowledge that findings may not be transferable to other settings. By nature, a community participatory program is tailored to the specific needs and context of each community. Professionals can use guidelines or previous studies to initiate ideas for health-promoting activities. However, these ideas need adjustments and finalization through collaboration with the school. The limited generalizability also includes the fact that the study sample consisted only of first-year lower secondary children; therefore, the findings may not be applicable to other age groups. In addition, other weaknesses of the study include the absence of a control group - which limits absolute causal inferences, although this single-group design remains well-suited for evaluating longitudinal changes in oral health promotion initiatives - as well as reliance on a single examiner, small sample size, and missing data at intermediate time points. Furthermore, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, although appropriate for paired comparisons, remains limited in its ability to model data and properly account for missing values compared with linear mixed models. Additionally, multiple pairwise comparisons across follow-up visits were conducted without formal p-value adjustments to prevent excessive Type II errors in this small-scale study. Future studies should employ mixed-effects models to account for repeated measures and missing data. Conducting dormitory visit separately for male and female dormitories was unavoidable. Although this separation could potentially raise concerns regarding differences in the intervention, the primary benefit that children received from the dormitory visit was emotional support and encouragement. In addition, the same team members conducted visits in both dormitories. Therefore, it is unlikely that the lack of simultaneous intervention resulted in meaningful differences in outcomes among the children. Future research in disabled children’s oral hygiene practice may also incorporate devices (such as electric toothbrush, custom-made toothbrush with a large handle, three-head toothbrush, special mouth prop, video-based education at home) as well as chemical agents for plaque control into the program since they have been proven to enhance the effectiveness of oral programs in disabled children [13, 22, 42]. Studies with a longer follow-up period (e.g. 2 years) are required, as the current study was originally intended to continue for more than 2 years until the children finished school. However, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the study was terminated after an 18-month follow-up period. Moreover, long-term studies integrating caries incidence and, importantly, Oral Health-Related Quality-of Life (OHRQoL) measures are recommended as a next step.

Conclusion

This 18-month community-based program significantly improved and sustained oral hygiene among children with cerebral palsy. A participatory school approach integrating education, environmental change, and caregiver as well as parental engagement may serve as a model for sustainable oral health promotion in special-needs populations. Programs must be tailored to each school context; findings may not generalize across settings.
