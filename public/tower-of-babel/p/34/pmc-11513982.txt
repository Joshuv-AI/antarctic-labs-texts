Soft elasticity enabled adhesion enhancement of liquid crystal elastomers on rough surfaces
Authors: Raja Annapooranan, Ram Hemanth Yeerella, Robert J. Chambers, Chenghai Li, Shengqiang Cai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe versatility of pressure-sensitive adhesives (PSA) has led to their widespread applications. However, maintaining adhesion on rough surfaces is challenging because surface asperities reduce contact between the substrate and cross-linked PSAs. Liquid-like adhesives can flow and conform to surfaces but lack cohesive strength and are prone to creep. In this study, we address these challenges using liquid crystal elastomer (LCE) adhesives, which can flow like a liquid and make good contact with the rough substrate due to soft elasticity and low cross-link density. Additionally, the cross-linked polymer network of the LCE adhesives provides the necessary cohesive strength. This unique combination enables LCE to conform to rough substrates over time, achieving stronger adhesion compared to a commercial adhesive.

Pressure-sensitive adhesives (PSA) are extensively used in engineering applications and consumer products to bond various surfaces quickly using gentle pressure, without requiring additional chemical reactions or stimuli such as heat or UV radiation (1–3). PSAs usually consist of loosely cross-linked viscoelastic polymers such as natural rubber, acrylates, silicones, and styrenic block copolymers. These adhesives are carefully designed to possess a low shear modulus (G <105 Pa) to facilitate good conformal contact on diverse substrates. PSAs are increasingly used in household products such as tapes and in automotive, aerospace, electronics, and medical industry due to their ease of application and compatibility with different materials (3). Consequently, there is a growing demand for high-quality PSAs to meet the needs of both current and emerging applications. The viscoelastic properties of PSAs are fine-tuned for specific applications by formulating the polymer network, cross-linking, and incorporating additives such as tackifiers, plasticizers, and rheological modifiers. Since most of the additives are uncross-linked, there are potential problems associated with their use. Due to incompatibility with the base adhesive, some additives may result in phase separation and property drift (4, 5). Migration and leaching of additives from the adhesive can affect the substrate and pose environmental and health risks (6, 7). Certain additives have been found to affect the aging properties and long-term stability of PSAs (1, 8). These complex interactions between the additives and the base polymer complicate the design of PSAs (9, 10). As a result, there has been significant interest in developing additive-free PSA in the recent times (11–13).

Real-world surfaces consist of various inherent irregularities or asperities ranging from hundreds of nm to microns, contributing to their roughness (14, 15). This has prompted numerous studies to explore the impact of roughness on adhesion (16–19). Roughness generally reduces the effective contact area between the adhesive and the substrate and decreases the adhesion energy of PSAs (15, 20). Due to the asperities on the substrate, the applied load during bonding can lead to strain energy gain within the adhesive. Upon removal of the load, the elastic detachment of the adhesive can result in a reduced contact area and weak adhesion upon debonding. Therefore, an adhesive with high compliance (1/modulus) can achieve good contact with rough surfaces. Compliance can be increased by reducing the cross-link density, but too low a cross-link density leads to liquid-like behavior with poor cohesive strength. Conversely, highly cross-linked adhesives can possess high cohesive strength but may struggle to form good contact with rough surfaces because of the large elastic energy penalties (21). This paradox between compliance and strength makes it challenging to design adhesives that can bond well on rough surfaces while maintaining high strength on debonding (21, 22). Commercial adhesive manufacturers typically issue warnings about potential adhesion loss due to substrate roughness and recommend preparing the substrate before applying the adhesive. While these treatments can address some issues, they are often impractical for most real-world substrates or components, which limits the application range of PSA.

Various strategies have been developed to overcome the trade-off between compliance and strength in adhesives. These methods include swelling the polymer network with solvents, curing liquid adhesive precursors, and using materials like bottlebrush elastomers, supramolecularly bonded gels, and shape memory polymers, as well as incorporating structural designs (23–25). Swelling a polymer with a solvent can reduce the modulus, but solvent leaching and interactions with the substrates are generally unfavorable. A liquid precursor can sufficiently wet rough surfaces but requires additional curing via heat or UV radiation (9). Bottlebrush elastomers achieve high compliance through a polymer architecture that favors disentanglement but often require complex synthesis (23, 24, 26). Supramolecular gels doped with ionic liquids, as proposed by Wu et al., can conform to various substrates due to their inherent softness and exhibit excellent adhesion through intermolecular interactions and rate-dependent phase separation (27). However, these ionogels are susceptible to humidity and long-time creep, limiting their applications. Leveraging their shape-retaining ability under heating and cooling, shape memory polymers have been shown to adhere to rough surfaces in the soft rubbery state and debond in the stiff glassy state (28, 29). However, the application of heat during the bonding process can be impractical for many substrate materials. Hence, an adhesive that is soft enough to conform over asperities without gaining excessive strain energy and possesses sufficient strength upon debonding is highly desired.

Liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs) are a class of polymeric materials that possess unique mechanical properties such as nonlinear viscoelasticity, soft elasticity, and stimuli-responsiveness, arising from the combination of liquid crystal mesogens with elastomeric networks (30–34). In addition to chain friction in traditional polymer networks, the rotation of liquid crystal mesogens contributes to the large energy dissipation of LCEs. These novel properties have been harnessed in various studies for applications such as soft actuators, impact damping, and, more recently, adhesion (35–41). On heating beyond the nematic transition temperature, LCEs become isotropic elastomers and show a pronounced decrease in viscoelastic dissipation (42–46). This temperature dependence has been exploited to develop reversible adhesives (38, 39, 47). Under uniaxial tension, loosely cross-linked LCEs exhibit pronounced ductility and large deformation at a constant plateau stress, a property known as soft elasticity (48–51). In a previous work on peeling of PSAs, the unique nonlinear stress–strain behavior of LCE was shown to result in an abnormally high-rate dependence compared to traditional polymeric adhesives (41). Guo et al demonstrated that the adhesion strength of LCE adhesives under peeling and probe tack tests increased significantly with long contact times (40). This was attributed to the relaxation of local stresses and the slow reorientation of the nematic domains in polydomain LCEs. A recent work by Pranda et al also reported the effect of liquid crystal orientation on the adhesive properties of LCEs using peeling (52).

Although various studies have investigated the adhesive properties of LCEs, the effect of substrate roughness on LCE adhesion has not been thoroughly explored. One study suggested that the surface roughness of a cross-linked LCE could be influenced by whether it was cross-linked in a nematic or isotropic state, but it is unclear how this would impact adhesion on a randomly rough substrate (39). A recent study revealed that under biaxial tension, polydomain LCEs behave like a liquid within the loading plane due to soft elasticity (51). This allows polydomain LCEs to change shape freely without mechanical energy costs, unlike traditional elastomers. Due to the incompressibility of elastomers (λxλyλz=1), biaxial tension is equivalent to uniaxial compression and the latter is the predominant mode of application of PSA on various substrates (1, 2). Consequently, this unique liquid-like property of LCE could be exploited to achieve good contact on rough surfaces with asperities. At the same time, cross-linking the polymer network results in good tensile strength and creep resistance (53). Thus, LCEs offer unique advantages compared to traditional elastomeric adhesives, as they can simultaneously flow like a liquid due to soft elasticity and possess cohesive strength due to cross-linking.

In this study, we explore the adhesion of polydomain LCEs on various rough surfaces using probe tack, lap shear, and nanoindentation experiments (54). We compare the performance of LCEs with that of a commercial acrylic foam tape (3M VHB 4910), which is widely used due to its excellent adhesion properties (55–59). We observe that the adhesion of both LCE and VHB is weakened by very rough surfaces. However, the adhesion strength of the LCE adhesive can be significantly enhanced on increasing the contact time from 1 to 1,000 min. We believe that the underlying mechanism responsible for the observation is the soft elasticity of LCE and its long relaxation time scale. This enables LCE to overcome the adhesion paradox faced by traditional polymers and broadens the scope of PSAs for more demanding real-world applications.

Results and Discussion

Fig. 1A presents a schematic comparison of the polymer network structure of a traditional pressure-sensitive adhesive and a polydomain LCE adhesive. A traditional PSA is a viscoelastic polymer that is loosely cross-linked to avoid creep and contains additives such as plasticizers and tackifiers to modify the modulus and rheological properties (1). LCEs comprise liquid crystal monomers known as mesogens, combined in an elastomeric network using flexible polymer spacers and cross-linkers (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). A simple one-step polymerization reaction results in a polydomain LCE network without any macroscopic alignment (60). VHB 4910, a commercial transparent acrylic adhesive from 3 M company was used for comparison (55–59). The glass transition temperature of VHB and LCE are −10 °C and 0 °C, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The nematic-isotropic transition temperature of LCE is 75 °C, which ensures a liquid crystalline state at room temperature. The storage shear modulus of LCE is 0.3 MPa and for VHB is 0.1 MPa at room temperature, both of which are under the Dahlquist criterion (0.3 MPa) (61, 62). This criterion reflects the balance between the surface energy gained upon adhesion and the strain energy stored per unit area during bonding, making soft elastomers good candidates for adhesives.

Under uniaxial tension (Fig. 1B), most polymers, such as VHB, exhibit a monotonic stress–strain behavior. Uniquely, LCEs can be largely deformed by applying a very small mechanical work, due to the rotation of mesogen directors. This distinctive property, known as soft elasticity, manifests as a stress plateau under uniaxial tension and an unusual in-plane liquid-like behavior under biaxial tension (51). Since this property is rate dependent, the long stress plateau due to soft elasticity has been shown to result in the unique rate-dependent adhesion energy of LCE in peeling and impact damping applications (37, 41, 43). LCEs exhibit a nonmonotonic stress–strain curve with the secant modulus reaching close to zero at 50% strain while simultaneously possessing a tensile strength of 0.6 MPa (Fig. 1 B, Right). Due to the low cross-linking density of LCE, there is no clear boundary between the soft elastic plateau and strain hardening in the uniaxial stress–strain curve, as typically observed in LCEs with higher cross-linking density (51). From 50% to 200% strain, the stress slowly increases and reaches a plateau at 0.05 MPa. This value remains constant between 200 and 500% strain, marking the end of the soft elastic plateau. From 500 to 1,300% strain, the stress continues to increase due to gradual strain hardening, as mesogens and polymer chains start to align. Between 1,300% and 2,000% strain, the stress increases rapidly due to pronounced strain hardening, as the mesogens and polymer chains become nearly aligned and extended, ultimately leading to rupture. A qualitatively similar stress–strain curve is observed when the strain rate is increased to 0.1 s−1, with two key differences. a) The values of plateau stress and breaking stress are higher due to viscous effects as observed in previous studies (41, 44). b) The secant modulus does not reach zero at 50% strain (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

When it comes to adhesion, compliance (1/modulus) can be used to evaluate the elastic energy changes caused by the indentation of the asperities, when an adhesive is pressed onto a rough surface. The balance between strength and the compliance of the adhesive is crucial for ensuring good conformability on rough surfaces during bonding and resistance to loads on debonding (1). Fig. 1C is an Ashby chart, comparing the compliance and the strength of various PSAs under uniaxial tension (63–71). Well-cross-linked adhesives, positioned at the top left, exhibit low compliance and high tensile strength. By reducing the cross-link density, the compliance of the adhesive can be increased, although this comes at the cost of lowered strength. On further reduction of cross-link density, the entanglements of the polymer chains will dominate and set an upper bound of compliance (~102 MPa −1) (53). Owing to soft elasticity and low cross-link density, LCE adhesives can achieve a higher compliance (>102 MPa −1), while maintaining good tensile strength (0.6 MPa). Due to the pointy shape of most asperities, the strain in the LCE adhesive resulting from the asperity indentation can be expected to fall outside linear elasticity regime. Therefore, the inverse of the resilience modulus at 100% strain (area under the stress–strain curve) was used to calculate the compliance of LCE. The computed compliance does not vary much if the selected strain is between 20% and 150%, so 100% strain was chosen as a representative value.

The consequence of high compliance enabled by soft elasticity and low cross-link density is reflected during bonding to rough surfaces, as illustrated in Fig. 1A. At typical contact pressures during bonding, a cross-linked solid adhesive with low compliance accumulates elastic strain energy, often resulting in partial detachment upon removal of the load. This reduced contact area leads to weakened adhesion strength during debonding. By using a minimally cross-linked polymer, one can increase the compliance of the adhesive and improve the conformance, but the adhesive typically exhibits poor cohesive strength and susceptibility to creep under constant loads. Common strategies to increase the compliance of a cross-linked adhesive is to dilute the network with large quantities of low molecular weight additives such as plasticizers and tackifiers. However, this approach introduces risks of property drift due to segregation or leaching of volatile compounds (10). In contrast, LCEs achieve high compliance through soft elasticity and low cross-link density, enabling good conformance to rough surfaces without the need for any additives. Upon debonding, strain stiffening combined with high tensile strength under large deformation enhances adhesion strength. Traditionally, the adhesive properties of PSAs have been studied primarily on smooth substrates such as glass or stainless steel (2). In this study, we explore the effect of surface roughness and contact time on adhesion using probe tack, indentation, and lap shear experiments as follows.

Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 present the schematic of the probe tack experiment. In this setup, the adhesive was bonded to a glass substrate fixed at the base of the tensile tester. Stainless steel spheres (25 mm diameter) served as probes and were connected to a force sensor using a gripper. Unlike cylindrical probes, spherical probes ensure uniform contact with the adhesive regardless of minor misalignments, eliminating the need for additional leveling adjustments. The stainless-steel spheres were subjected to gritblasting using aluminum oxide abrasives of 24 grit and 100 grit to produce medium and high roughness surfaces, respectively. The smooth sphere was used as received, without any gritblasting (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). The area roughness was measured using an optical profiler, which yielded RMS height values of Sq = 0.3 µm, Sq = 2.86 µm, and Sq = 20.98 µm for the smooth, medium roughness and high roughness surfaces, respectively (SI Appendix, Figs. S6–S8). During the experiment, the probe approached the adhesive at a slow rate of 0.01 mm/s to minimize viscous effects, until a compressive load of 0.5 N was reached. This load was held constant for a specified period, which we define as “contact time.” The probe was then retracted from the adhesive, and the peak force was recorded (Fig. 2C). This experiment was repeated for three contact times (1, 10, and 100 min), three probes with different roughness and two adhesives: LCE and VHB (Fig. 2 D–F). A rough surface can potentially possess slightly higher effective contact area compared to a smooth surface, which can be beneficial for liquid adhesives that can flow and cure. However, the elastic energy costs of a typical cross-linked adhesive overshadow the benefits of the marginally higher effective contact area, thereby resulting in poor overall adhesion (17).

As shown in Fig. 2 D–F, the adhesion force of LCE and VHB increased with longer contact times, regardless of the probe roughness. The constant compressive force of the probe leads to increased indentation depth due to viscoelasticity of the adhesive, which in turn increases the effective contact area (20, 72, 73). A probe-based stress relaxation test revealed further differences in the viscoelastic properties of LCE and VHB (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). After 100 min, the stress value of VHB dropped to 40%, while that of LCE dropped to 10% of their respective initial values. This indicates that despite having a similar initial elastic modulus (Fig. 1B), the compliance of LCE can be significantly higher than that of VHB after relaxation. Polydomain LCEs have previously shown fast relaxation at short time scales (<1,000 s) and a much slower relaxation at longer time scales comparable to isotropic elastomers (>1,000 s) (42). At a short contact time of 1 min, the adhesion force of VHB was at least 40% higher than that of LCE for all probe roughness. This can be attributed to the lack of tackifiers in the LCE adhesive. However, at contact times of 10 and 100 min, LCE showed a larger increase in adhesion force compared to VHB, reaching a maximum value of 2.1 N for the high roughness probe. In contrast, the adhesion force of VHB saturated at approximately 1.7 N, regardless of the probe roughness after 100 min. Increasing the contact time for the medium roughness probe to 1,000 min led to higher peak force for the LCE adhesives but had little effect on VHB (SI Appendix, Figs. S10 and S11).

Previous studies have shown that the tack of an adhesive on a rough surface with asperities is proportional to the true area of contact during bonding, which is influenced by both contact time and pressure (20, 72, 73). Due to the high compliance of LCE, applying a preload for an extended period results in improved contact with the substrate. This manifests in the profile of the force- vs. time curve as LCE adhesive takes a longer time to reach a plateau force after the peak value. SI Appendix, Fig. S12 shows the results of a representative probe tack test conducted with the medium roughness probe, with a contact time of 100 min, followed by an additional 100 min after reaching the peak force. The profile of the probe tack test corresponds to fibril formation or necking, extension of fibrils, strain hardening, gradual debonding of fibrils, and complete detachment of the adhesive and the probe (2). Previous studies have shown that adhesives with more cross-linking typically take a shorter time to reach a plateau after the peak force, due to the formation of a stable fibril cross-section, compared to loosely cross-linked adhesives with unstable necks and fibril fractures (66). As the probe was withdrawn from the adhesive surface, the polydomain LCE exhibited notable ductility as it progressively deformed and aligned into monodomain LCE under tensile force, prior to strain hardening and detachment from the probe (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). The large deformation of LCE before strain hardening compared to VHB may explain why LCE reaches a plateau force later than VHB after the peak. The gradual reduction of contact area of the adhesive during the retraction of the probe also contributes to the force drop after the peak. Although the mechanism can be more complex, involving cavitation and fibril fracture (2, 66), increasing the surface roughness might have simply improved the overall contact area of LCE during bonding. This could lead to a more gradual decrease in contact area during retraction and a longer time to reach plateau stress, compared to VHB.

The results from the probe tack experiment illustrate the collective interaction between numerous irregular asperities on the probe substrate and the adhesive layer. To replicate the interactions between a single asperity and the adhesive at a smaller length scale, nanoindentation test can be used (8). Fig. 3A shows the schematic of the nanoindentation experimental setup. A flat-ended conical indenter tip with a diameter of 109 µm is pressed onto the top surface of the adhesive adhered to a flat glass substrate at a rate of 1 µm/s until a force of 50 µN is reached. This force is held constant for various contact times (0.16, 1, and 10 min). The indenter is then unloaded at the same rate of 1 µm/s, and the peak force during debonding is defined as the adhesion force (Fig. 3B). The results of the nanoindentation experiments follow qualitatively similar trends to those observed in the probe tack experiment. At shorter contact times of 10 s and 1 min, VHB shows a higher adhesion force compared to LCE, owing to the higher tackiness of VHB (Fig. 3 C and D). However, when the contact time is increased to 10 min, LCE shows a higher adhesion strength (Fig. 3E). At contact times longer than 10 min, the adhesion force of LCE exceeds the maximum debonding force measurable by the instrument. Based on the probe tack experiment, we expect the adhesion force of LCE to continue increasing with longer contact times before eventually reaching a saturated value.

Following industry standards (ASTM D1002), we conducted lap shear tests on LCE and VHB adhesives, bonding them to substrates with varying surface roughness (Fig. 4 A, Inset shows schematics). The adhesive was bonded between two stainless steel plates of 25.4 mm width and 100 mm length. The adhesive overlap area between the plates was 25.4 × 25.4 mm2. A compressive pressure of 100 kPa was applied to the overlap area for various contact times, and then, the shear force was measured using a tensile tester. Fig. 4 A–F shows the shear stress vs. strain curves for LCE and VHB at different contact times and substrate roughness levels. At shorter contact times of 1 and 10 min, VHB exhibited higher shear strength than LCE, regardless of the substrate roughness. After the maximum contact time of 1,000 min, the shear strength of VHB was 100 kPa on smooth substrates, which dropped to 75 kPa on the high roughness substrate. In contrast, the shear strength of LCE reached similar values of 90 to 100 kPa after a contact time of 1,000 min, independent of substrate roughness. Since both contact time and pressure can influence adhesion strength, we tested the shear strength of LCE and VHB on the high roughness substrates using double the contact pressure (200 kPa) and a contact time of 1,000 min. Doubling the contact pressure had a marginal effect on the adhesion of VHB, while it improved the shear strength of LCE to 130 kPa. This result is consistent with previous studies, which suggest that increased contact pressure benefits more compliant adhesives while having little impact on less compliant adhesives (21, 28).

Macroscopically observing the LCE adhesives on the substrates after the lap shear test provides additional insights into their failure mechanisms (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). Polydomain LCE samples are initially opaque due to light scattering by randomly arranged nematic domains but become transparent under large deformation as the mesogens align along the direction of loading. Photographs of LCE adhesives after the lap shear tests show that LCE samples appeared opaque at short contact times (1 to 10 min) and more transparent after longer contact times (>100 min). This can be explained as follows: Due to the lack of tackifiers, the initial tack of the cross-linked LCE is lower than that of VHB, resulting in poor contact at short time scales. However, over longer contact times, LCE can flow and improve the overall contact area over the rough asperities. Additionally, LCE can reach a lower free energy state after long contact times due to the slow readjustments of local domains (40). Both effects increase the overall adhesion energy of LCE. Hence, at short contact times, we observe a brittle adhesive failure of LCE without significant bulk deformation. After sufficient contact time, the improved adhesion energy of LCE allows the specimen to resist more load and undergo substantial deformation before adhesive failure, resulting in the observed optical changes. The effect of higher contact pressures at short contact times was not studied in this work, based on previous analyses. Without sufficient time for stress relaxation, high contact pressures can result in large tensile stresses and local detachment at valleys between asperities, which can act as failure points (20). Air pockets trapped between the valleys could also contribute to premature failure (74). Under all the experimental conditions in this study, both LCE and VHB did not exhibit cohesive failure and failed adhesively without leaving significant residues.

In addition to the lap shear test, we compared the adhesive performance of LCE and VHB on a high roughness substrate under long-term static normal loads. One surface of the circular adhesive layers was adhered to a smooth stainless-steel cylinder (Fig. 5A), while the other surface of the adhesive was adhered to a sandblasted stainless-steel plate with high roughness (Sq > 20 µm). A compressive load of 500 kPa was applied for a contact time of 1,000 min. Different static normal loads were then applied using hanging weights, and the time of debonding was measured (Fig. 5B). For an applied load of 0.12 MPa, VHB debonded within 15 min, whereas LCE withstood the load for 1,500 min (Fig. 5C). When bonded to rough surfaces, adhesives are deformed by sharp asperities, leading to an increase in elastic strain energy (18, 20, 21). The presence of asperities reduces the effective contact area compared to a smooth surface and can contribute to crack formation near the edge of the adhesive. When a load is applied in the opposite direction, the release of strain energy drives crack growth, leading to adhesive failure. In the case of LCE, the high compliance due to soft elasticity and low cross-link density allows for good conformance after sufficient contact time and pressure. The stress relaxation enabled by mesogen domain realignment results in lower strain energy within the adhesive. This allows LCE to sustain loads for a longer period despite the high surface roughness. The maximum applied stress that did not cause debonding for over a week of loading was 0.0375 MPa for LCE and 0.025 MPa for VHB.

From the probe tack and lap shear experiments, we observed that rough substrates generally reduce the adhesive strength of VHB 4910 compared to smooth surfaces, while LCE recovers its adhesion strength after sufficient contact time. Although some commercial adhesives might outperform LCE, it is important to consider the differences in their structures. For an adhesive to perform well overall, it must possess good adhesive and cohesive strengths (2, 17, 75). High compliance is necessary to achieve better adhesion on rough surfaces, but some cross-linking is essential for substantial cohesive strength and creep resistance, which inevitably reduces compliance. To address this paradox, additives such as plasticizers and tackifiers are often incorporated into the base polymer to ensure good bonding, making adhesive formulation a delicate balance between multiple conflicting properties (17). For use as a mounting tape, such as VHB 4910, adhesive layers are coated on a cross-linked viscoelastic polymer foam that distributes stress and provides impact strength. This multilayered design helps mounting foam tape achieve high adhesive and cohesive strength. Despite the structural design, many commercial adhesive manufacturers advise the users to prepare the surfaces using polishing or primers and provide warnings about potential loss of adhesion. In contrast, the LCE adhesive used in this study contains no additives or separate foam core. We chose a simple chemical formulation to balance all the necessary properties. Due to soft elasticity and low cross-link density, the LCE adhesive can achieve high compliance. The reduction of strain energy due to mesogen relaxation, leads to a good bonding on rough surfaces after sufficient contact time (~1,000 min), which is comparable to the time required by many commercial adhesives to achieve complete bonding strength (48 to 72 h). The cross-linking of the LCE network provides cohesive strength upon debonding. The current LCE adhesive has some shortcomings such as a narrow application temperature range and a long bonding time. The nematic to isotropic transition temperature of the LCEs used in this study is ~70 °C. This restricts the application of the LCE adhesive to room temperature since heating above the nematic transition temperatures causes drastic reduction in viscoelastic dissipation (42, 45) and adhesion (38–41). There have been recent advances in polymer science that endow LCEs with higher nematic transition temperatures or enable LCEs to maintain a fraction of their properties at high temperatures using intermolecular interactions (76, 77). Future studies on the adhesion of LCE may benefit from incorporating these chemistries to broaden the application temperature range and achieve robust adhesion under short contact times.

Conclusion

Despite the numerous advantages of PSA, their adhesion strength is often compromised by surface roughness, which limits their real-world applications. In this study, we demonstrate that LCEs can be engineered to overcome the challenges faced by traditional adhesives on rough substrates. The asperities of a rough surface typically deform a cross-linked adhesive, increasing the strain energy, which reduces the contact area upon removal of load and weakens adhesive strength during debonding. Soft elasticity and low cross-link density endow LCEs with high compliance, allowing them to conform efficiently to rough substrates. The cross-linking in the LCE network also provides the necessary cohesive strength on debonding. This unique combination allows LCE adhesives to outperform a commercial acrylic foam tape on very rough surfaces with asperities ranging from a few microns to tens of microns. We hope that this study opens different possibilities for roughness-tolerant PSA and broadens their potential applications.

Materials and Methods

Materials.

1,4-Bis-[4-(3-acryloyloxypropyloxy)benzoyloxy]-2-methylbenzene (RM257; Chemfish Japan; 98%), 2,2′-(ethylenedioxy)diethanethiol (EDDET; Sigma-Aldrich; 95%), pentaerythritoltetrakis(3-mercaptopropionate) (PETMP; Sigma-Aldrich; 95%), dipropylamine (DPA; Sigma-Aldrich; 98%), butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT; 99% Sigma-Aldrich), and toluene (Fisher Scientific; 99%) were used as received without further purification. 304 Stainless steel strips were obtained from McMaster Carr, and 304 Stainless steel balls were obtained from Uxcell, Amazon.

Synthesis of LCE Adhesive.

In this current study, the LCE is in polydomain nematic state at room temperature (20 °C). LCEs are prepared by the polymerization of liquid crystal monomers, flexible spacers, and cross-linkers. Polydomain LCE adhesive was synthesized using Michael addition reaction between acrylate capped liquid crystal monomer (RM257), thiol spacer (EDDET), and thiol cross-linker (PETMP) in the presence of an amine catalyst (DPA) (60). Liquid crystalline monomer RM257 (15 g, 25.48 mmol) was dissolved in toluene (4.725 g) at 85 °C. To this mixture, EDDET (4.529 g, 24.84 mmol) and PETMP (0.155 g, 0.318 mmol) were added and stirred. This corresponds to a mol % ratio of RM257/EDDET/PETMP = 100%/ 97.75%/2.25%. The cross-linking density is chosen such that it is just above the gel point or point of percolation of the network. A small quantity (0.15 g) of the radical scavenger (BHT) was added to the mixture to prevent unwanted cross-linking between the acrylate groups. Finally, a solution of DPA (0.075 g, 0.74 mmol) diluted in toluene (1:50 by volume) was added to the reaction mixture and stirred. The solution was degassed, poured in a glass mold with a lid, and left at room temperature for 24 h. Then, the loosely cross-linked LCE was removed from the mold and placed in an oven at 85 °C to completely remove the solvent. The thickness of LCE adhesive was kept constant at ~0.9 mm, which is comparable to that of the commercial VHB adhesive (~0.9 mm) and much higher compared to the size of the asperities (tens of microns) to ensure good bonding on the rough substrates.

Substrate Preparation.

To introduce surface roughness, spheres and rectangular strips made using 304 stainless steel were subjected to identical gritblasting process using aluminum oxide particles (24 and 100 grit for the medium and high roughness cases, respectively) at a pressure of 80 psi (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). The roughness of the substrates was characterized using a Filmetrics Profilm 3D optical profiler by white light interferometry

Lap Shear Testing of Adhesives.

We performed the Lap shear test following the ASTM D1002 test procedure, using the universal mechanical testing system (5965 Dual column testing system, Instron) with a 1-kN load cell. The adhesive (~0.95 mm thickness) was placed in between two stainless steel strips (100 mm × 25.4 mm × 1.6 mm) with an overlap area of 25.4 mm × 25.4 mm. A 500 g load was placed on the strips for a certain time to ensure good bonding of the adhesive and the substrate. Then, the strips were placed between the grips of a tensile tester and separated at a rate of 1.3 mm/min. The shear stress was calculated by the relation F/(B*L), where F is the measured force, and B and L are the width and length of the overlap area of the adhesive, respectively. The experiment was repeated for different contact times and substrate roughness (n = 3 to 5).

Probe Tack Testing.

We performed the probe tack testing of adhesives using the Instron tensile tester. The adhesives were adhered to a flat glass substrate whose position was fixed. A spherical probe (304 stainless steel, 25 mm diameter) was brought into contact with the adhesive at a rate of 0.01 mm/s until a compressive force of 0.5 N was reached. This compressive force was maintained for various contact times (1 to 100 min). Then, the probe was retracted at a rate of 0.01 mm/s, and the peak force was recorded. This experiment was repeated for different contact times and probe roughness (n = 3 to 5).

Mechanical Characterization of Adhesives.

We performed the uniaxial tensile testing of the adhesives on the Instron tensile tester, using rectangular samples (length: 20 mm; width: 10 mm) bonded on acrylic plates. Strain (ε) is defined as ε = (LF – Lo)/Lo, where Lo is the length of the sample in the undeformed state, and LF is the height of the sample in the deformed state. The nominal stress was calculated by dividing the force by the original cross-sectional area of the sample. The experiment was performed at a strain rate of 0.05 %/s.

Nanoindentation.

We conducted a comparative analysis of the adhesion strength between LCE and VHB using the Bruker Hysitron BioSoft in situ indenter, which comes with a maximum displacement capacity of 175 µm and a maximum load limit of 10 mN during indentation, and 500 µN during debonding. To perform the indentation on the sample surfaces, we employed a flat-ended conical indenter tip (Ti-0149) with a diameter of 109 microns and a cone angle of 90°. The experiment involved indenting the adhesive samples up to a load of 50 µN and maintaining this load for varying durations (10 s, 1 min, and 10 min). After the desired holding period, indenter was unloaded until complete detachment from the sample occurred. During the loading and unloading of the indenter, displacement control with a rate of 1 µm/s was used. During the holding period, force control was used. The experiments were repeated for three to four times at each holding period.
