Modulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines by Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) gel in oral wound healing: An in vivo study
Authors: Alqarama Mahardhika Thalib, Khanisyah Erza Gumilar, Bahruddin Thalib, Shang-Ming Wang, Bai-Hung Huang, Jian-Hong Yu, Chung-Ming Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposeMechanical trauma and denture use mucosal ulcerations are common in the elderly population and are sustained by persistent pro-inflammatory cytokine activity. Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) contains bioactive compounds with anti-inflammatory properties, potentially acting through the modulation of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling and suppression of mediators such as Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), and Tumor Necrosis Factor alpha (TNF-α). This study aimed to evaluate the anti-inflammatory effects of 0.7 % Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) extract gel in a rat model.Materials and methodsTwenty-eight male Wistar rats were randomly assigned to control and treatment groups (n = 14/group). Standardized traumatic ulcers were induced on the lateral side of the tongue. The treatment group received topical application of 0.7 % Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) gel twice daily for seven days. Ulcer tissues were collected daily from two animals per group for cytokine analysis using Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA). Statistical analyses were performed using independent-samples t-tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis with calculation of the Area Under the Curve (AUC).ResultsMelaleuca leucadendron (L.) gel significantly reduced IL-1β and IL-6 expression compared to the controls, with strong discriminatory capacity reflected in ROC–AUC values of 0.95 and 0.889, respectively (P < 0.05). TNF-α levels also decreased, although its ROC –AUC indicated a more limited discriminatory ability. The gel also exhibited favorable physicochemical properties, including viscosity, spreadability, and homogeneity.ConclusionMelaleuca leucadendron (L.) gel effectively attenuates pro-inflammatory cytokine activity and may be a promising phytotherapeutic intervention for oral mucosal inflammation.Keywords: Cytokines, Inflammation, Mouth mucosa, Plant extracts, Wound healing

Introduction

Oral mucosal lesions, particularly those related to denture trauma, are common in elderly populations and can significantly impair mastication, speech, and nutrition, thereby reducing quality of life.1–4 Age-related factors such as mucosal thinning, immune decline, and reduced salivary flow further compromise tissue repair and increase susceptibility to chronic irritation and infection.2,3,5

One of the key pathological features of these lesions is an exaggerated and poorly regulated inflammatory response, in which interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) play pivotal roles.6 IL-1β acts as an early mediator that promotes leukocyte infiltration and extracellular matrix breakdown, IL-6 sustains the inflammatory process through acute-phase signaling and TNF-α amplifies the cascade by increasing vascular permeability and promoting apoptosis in damaged tissues.7,8 In geriatric patients, persistent activation of these mediators results in prolonged inflammation, delayed regeneration, and chronic non-healing ulcers.9 This highlights the need for therapeutic strategies capable of modulating multiple inflammatory pathways simultaneously.

Given the limitations of conventional pharmacologic agents, such as corticosteroids and antiseptics, which may worsen mucosal fragility or induce microbial resistance, there is growing interest in safer, plant-based alternatives with multi-target bioactivity. Melaleuca leucadendron (L.), commonly known as cajuput, is traditionally used in Southeast Asia for its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties.10–12 The therapeutic effects include modulating inflammation and promoting tissue repair, making it a promising alternative for oral mucosal disorders, particularly in elderly patients at higher risk from conventional treatments.

Beyond its traditional use and reported anti-inflammatory effects, recent computational studies have provided mechanistic insights into the bioactivity of Melaleuca leucadendron (L.).13,14 In silico docking identified key metabolites strongly binding to pro-inflammatory mediators, while drug-likeness evaluation using Lipinski’s rule of five and ADMET (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion and Toxicity) predictions support pharmacological feasibility and low toxicity, reinforcing its potential as a multi-target anti-inflammatory agent for in vivo validation.15–18

Despite these findings, in vivo validation of Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) in oral mucosal lesions remains scarce, particularly studies evaluating its concurrent impact on IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. These cytokines are interdependent regulators of inflammation and critical determinants of impaired wound healing in ageing tissues.19 Addressing this gap is essential to establishing the therapeutic relevance of this plant for oral mucosal care.

This study integrated molecular docking with in vivo evaluation in a rat model of oral ulceration. Specifically, it investigated the effects of a 0.7 % Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) extract gel on IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α expression. The 0.7 % concentration was selected based on prior literature demonstrating antibacterial and anti-inflammatory activity of the extract at this level, with documented biocompatibility for oral application.20 We hypothesized that topical application of 0.7 % Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) extract gel would significantly attenuate pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) in traumatic oral ulcers compared to untreated controls, potentially through suppression of the NF-κB signaling pathway, as supported by prior in vitro evidence and molecular docking predictions of its bioactive metabolites.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study employed a post-test-only control group design to assess the anti-inflammatory potential of Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) extract gel in an in vivo model of oral mucosal ulceration. All animal procedures were approved by the Health Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Dentistry, Hasanuddin University (Approval No. 0084/PL.09/KEPK FKG-RSGM UNHAS/2024) and complied with institutional and international guidelines for laboratory animal use.21

Molecular docking simulation

Molecular docking was performed using PyRx v0.8 (open-source software; The Scripps Research Institute, La Jolla, CA, USA). Protein structures of IL-1β (PDB ID: 1ITB), IL-6 (PDB ID: 1ALU), and TNF-α (PDB ID: 2AZ5) were retrieved from the research collaboratory for structural bioinformatics protein data bank. Ligand structures of eight metabolites (hinesol, viridiflorol, eucalyptol, linalool, caryophyllene, (+)-2-carene, terpinen-4-ol, and limonene) were obtained from public chemical information (Pub-Chem) and are summarized in Table 1. Protein preparation and water removal were performed in Discovery Studio v21.1.0.20298 (Dassault Systèmes Biovia Corp., San Diego, CA, USA). Molecular docking was performed to predict the binding sites and free binding energies (ΔG).

Drug-likeness, pharmacokinetics, and toxicity prediction

To complement docking, pharmacological feasibility of the metabolites was evaluated using the PASS (Prediction of Activity Spectra for Substances) tool (Way2Drug, Moscow, Russia), Protox II (Charité–Universitätsmedizin, Berlin, Germany) for toxicity estimation and Lipinski’s rule of five revolution for identifying lead- and drug-like compounds, and was used to confirm drug-likeness, predicted lethal dose 50, and safety class of the identified metabolites.

Plant material and gel formulation

Fresh leaves of Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) were collected from the highland region of Buru Island, Maluku, Indonesia, shade-dried, powdered, and extracted by cold maceration with methanol (1:3 w/v, 72 h). The filtrate was passed through Whatman No. 1 filter paper (Cytiva, Maidstone, United Kingdom) and concentrated using a rotary evaporator (Heidolph Instruments, Schwabach, Germany) at 40–45 °C to obtain a crude extract.22

A 0.7 % topical gel was formulated with Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) extract as the active ingredient, 20 % glycerin (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) as gelling/viscosity modifier, methyl 0.18 % paraben (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 0.02 % propyl paraben (Sigma–Aldrich), and 0.01 % butylated hydroxytoluene (Sigma–Aldrich) as preservatives and antioxidant, with distilled water to volume. The 0.7 % concentration was chosen based on Abd Wahab et al. (2022),20 who identified it as the minimum effective dose for antibacterial action with >85 % gingival fibroblast viability, combined with pre-formulation screening showing superior physicochemical stability and sensory profile compared to 0.5 % (sub-therapeutic) and 1.0 % (unstable, bitter) concentrations. The extract was incorporated into a base of 1 % Carbopol 940 (Lubrizol, Wickliffe, OH, USA), and triethanolamine (Sigma–Aldrich) was added to adjust pH and consistency.22

Gel stability was evaluated following established protocols, including organoleptic properties (color, odor, consistency on days 0 and 14), pH measurement (OHAUS Corp., Parsippany, NJ, USA), viscosity gel (Brookfield Viscometer; AMETEK Brookfield, Middleboro, MA, USA), spreadability gel (0.5 g gel between glass plates under 100 g weight), and homogeneity gel (0.1 g smear test).23,24 These assessments confirmed suitability of the formulation for topical application to oral mucosa.

Animal and sample preparation

Twenty-eight healthy male wistar rats (Rattus norvegicus), aged 3–4 months and weighing 350–400 g, were maintained under standardized conditions 22–24 °C in 12 h light–dark cycle with unrestricted access to pellet feed and water.25 After 7 days of acclimatization, the animals were randomly divided into two groups (n = 14/group).

Experimental oral ulcers were induced under general anesthesia using intraperitoneal Ketamine 5 mg/kg (Ketalar®; Pfizer Inc., New York City, NY, USA) and Xylazine 5 mg/kg (Rompun®; Bayer AG, Leverkusen, Germany), in accordance with previous studies.26 Following anesthesia, the oral cavity was disinfected with 0.12 % chlorhexidine gluconate, and a standardized incision (1 cm length, 1 mm depth) was created on the lateral tongue with a sterile acrylic plate (3 × 3 × 1.5 mm) as a guide. In selected animals, additional chemical trauma was induced by applying glacial acetic acid for 2 min to simulate denture-related mucosal injury, followed by saline irrigation.27

To assess cytokine dynamics (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), two rats from each group were sacrificed daily on days 1–7 for tissue collection. Each underwent a single sampling procedure and was euthanized under deep anesthesia. All procedures complied with institutional animal care and ethical standards, and biological waste was managed according to biosafety protocols.

Cytokine expression analysis by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Ulcerated tongue tissues were collected daily from two animals per group (days 1–7), excised under aseptic conditions, transferred into sterile cryotubes, and stored at −80 °C. All samples were processed in parallel at the end of the observation period to reduce inter-assay variability. Cytokine levels (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) were quantified using rat-specific ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay) kits (Quantikine®; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Tissue specimens were thawed, homogenized in cold phosphate-buffered saline, and centrifuged to obtain supernatants. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a Biobase EI10a microplate reader (Biobase, Jinan, China). Concentrations were interpolated from standard curves, with all assays performed in duplicate to ensure precision and reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using international business machine statistical package for the social science v25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Intergroup comparisons were performed using independent samples t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U test otherwise. Statistical significance was set at two-tailed P < 0.05. Sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power 3.1.9.7 for independent-samples t-test (α = 0.05, power = 0.80, allocation ratio 1:1).

Lipinski’s rule of five analysis confirmed that all compounds met criteria for drug-likeness. The effect of 0.7 % Melaleuca leucadendron gel on pro-inflammatory cytokine expression

Treatment with 0.7 % Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) gel significantly reduced cytokine levels compared with the control group. Mean IL-1β concentrations were 13.46 ± 2.27 pg/mL in the treatment group and 20.28 ± 4.51 pg/mL in the control group (P = 0.004). IL-6 levels were 5.41 ± 1.74 pg/mL in the treatment group and 8.04 ± 1.26 pg/mL in the control group (P = 0.002). Median TNF-α concentrations were 66.90pg/mL (range 53.75–79.56) in the treatment group and 111.79 pg/mL (range 72.85–204.36) in the control group (P = 0.001) (Table 6).

Temporal changes in pro-inflammatory cytokines over 7 days of treatment

The temporal profile of IL-1β showed a decline from 17.88 pg/mL on Day 1–10.99 pg/mL on Day 7 in the treatment group, while the control group remained consistently higher across all days (Fig. 3a). IL-6 decreased from 6.82 pg/mL to 2.02 pg/mL in the treatment group but increased in controls, peaking at 10.99 pg/mL (Fig. 3b). TNF-α declined from 77.20 pg/mL on Day 1–63.44 pg/mL on Day 7 in the treatment group, whereas controls increased progressively, reaching 121.91 pg/mL on Day 6 (Fig. 3c).

ROC-AUC analysis of diagnostic accuracy in cytokine modulation

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminatory ability of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels in distinguishing between the treatment and control groups. AUC for IL-1β was 0.95 (Fig. 4a). For IL-6, the AUC value was 0.889 (Fig. 4 b). In contrast, TNF-α demonstrated a lower AUC value of 0.41 (Fig. 4c).

Discussion

This study supports the anti-inflammatory activity of Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) gel through modulation of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in a rat oral ulcer model. Application of 0.7 % gel downregulated cytokine expression within 7 days and maintained physicochemical properties suitable for mucosal use.

The observed improvements in viscosity and spreadability suggest the formation of a cohesive gel matrix with greater resistance to shear stress, an important feature for mucosal applications.28,29 Higher viscosity and optimal spreadability facilitate application, minimize displacement, and support intimate mucosal contact, ensuring prolonged retention and localized delivery of active compounds.30,31 In addition, enhanced mucoadhesiveness and the formation of a more homogeneous microstructure (Fig. 2) improve interaction with epithelial tissue, promoting diffusion of bioactive molecules and more consistent cytokine modulation. Such structural and functional optimization highlights the importance of stability testing in developing effective bioadhesive therapeutic gels.32

The temporal profile of IL-1β showed a marked reduction in the treatment group between day 1 and 3 suggesting early suppression of acute inflammation. Since IL-1β drives neutrophil recruitment and extracellular matrix degradation, its downregulation indicates modulation of the initial inflammatory cascade, thereby limiting tissue damage and promoting a favorable healing environment.33 Similar findings were reported by Surh, who observed NF-κB inhibition by Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) extract in LPS-stimulated macrophages, supporting a mechanistic link between NF-κB suppression and reduced IL-1β expression.34 The high AUC value (0.95) further reinforces this mechanism, demonstrating robust modulation of IL-1β–driven pathways and highlighting the gel’s therapeutic potential in targeting early inflammatory mediators.35

IL-6 levels in the treatment group declined steadily over seven days, while controls showed a progressive increase. As a central mediator linking acute inflammation to tissue repair processes such as immune cell recruitment, angiogenesis, and epithelial regeneration.7,36,37 Its sustained reduction suggests that the gel’s bioactive compounds may act upstream to suppress cytokine production. The strong AUC value (0.889) further supports IL-6 as a reliable biomarker of therapeutic efficacy, underscoring the broader immunoregulatory potential of Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) gel in managing mucosal inflammation.

TNF-α levels in the treatment group declined steadily over seven days, indicating a consistent anti-inflammatory effect of the gel despite its modest ROC–AUC value (0.41), which limits its role as a predictive biomarker. Still, TNF-α suppression is biologically relevant, as it drives mucosal inflammation, vascular permeability, and epithelial apoptosis.38 Even without strong statistical distinction, its reduction may help resolve chronic inflammation and promote repair, especially alongside IL-1β and IL-6 downregulation. These findings are consistent with Low et al., who showed that Melaleuca alternifolia extract suppressed TNF-α via NF-κB inhibition, suggesting conserved anti-inflammatory activity across the Melaleuca genus.39 The gap between the modest AUC and evident biological effect may reflect inter-individual variability or the slower kinetics of TNF-α compared with IL-1β and IL-6. Thus, while less sensitive as an early marker, TNF-α remains fundamental in mucosal inflammation, and its combined suppression with IL-1β and IL-6 highlights the gel’s broad immunomodulatory capacity in supporting tissue regeneration, particularly in elderly patients with oral mucosal lesions.

This study highlights the clinical potential of Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) gel in modulating pro-inflammatory cytokines driving oral mucosal lesions. In elderly patients, these lesions often result from disrupted balance between inflammation and repair. The suppression of IL-1β and IL-6 suggests targeted anti-inflammatory activity that interrupts this cycle, promoting a shift from persistent inflammation to tissue regeneration and creating a microenvironment favorable for healing.

In older adults, who frequently experience reduced regenerative capacity, delayed wound healing, and chronic inflammation, persistent oral lesions not only impair the quality of life by causing pain and eating and speech difficulties but also heighten the risk of secondary infections and systemic complications. Thus, a safe, noninvasive, and well-tolerated therapy that can modulate the inflammatory cascade at the molecular level represents an important unmet need.

Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) gel, a plant-based and biocompatible formulation, fulfills these criteria. Beyond its anti-inflammatory effects, the gel showed favorable physicochemical traits, such as higher viscosity and better spreadability, supporting longer mucosal adhesion and sustained local release.40 These features help maintain efficacy, reduce dosing frequency, and may improve adherence in older adults. Its plant-based, biocompatible composition and ability to modulate inflammatory pathways make it a promising alternative or adjunct to conventional therapy, particularly for patients unsuitable for prolonged corticosteroid use. With its non-invasive application and low risk of systemic effects, Melaleuca leucadendron (L.) gel offers strong potential as a safe, patient-friendly option for managing mucosal inflammation.
