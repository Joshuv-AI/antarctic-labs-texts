NGS-based measurable residual disease in long-term progression-free CLL survivors following FCR therapy∗
Authors: Alexandre Bazinet, Alessandra Ferrajoli, Nitin Jain, Jan Burger, Musa Yilmaz, Mahesh Swaminathan, Hagop Kantarjian, Michael Keating, Philip A Thompson, William G Wierda
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

The chemoimmunotherapy (CIT) regimen of fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, and rituximab (FCR) is a highly effective first-line treatment for patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL).1 In a recently updated analysis from the original 300-patient FCR study, we observed a plateau in progression-free survival (PFS) in the subgroup of patients with mutated immunoglobulin heavy chain variable region (IGHV) gene with 19 years of median follow-up despite CLL being traditionally considered an “incurable” disease with CIT.2 Our experience suggested that a subset of patients, namely those with IGHV-mutated status, experience long-term disease control of CLL with first-line FCR. Indeed, patients with low-risk disease have survival similar to a matched normal population.3 It is currently unknown whether CLL in these patients is completely eradicated or persists at a level only detectable with highly sensitive methods. Next-generation sequencing (NGS)–based measurable residual disease (MRD) assessment allows the detection of residual leukemic cells down to the 10−6 level (MRD6), representing a substantially lower limit of detection compared to the flow cytometry–based assay (sensitive to the 10−4 level; MRD4).4 In this report, we evaluated MRD6 in patients with CLL who were long-term progression-free survivors following FCR therapy.

This was a prospective single-center study. All patients provided written informed consent. This study was approved by the institutional review board (PA19-0148) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. We identified patients treated between 1999 and 2015 on 1 of 5 first-line FCR-based clinical trials (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers: NCT00794820, NCT00254410, NCT00525603, NCT00381004, and NCT00759798) or the original FCR300 study5 at MD Anderson Cancer Center. We selected patients who achieved complete remission, had no known clinical relapse of CLL, no known detectable MRD4 by flow cytometry at last follow-up, and who had not received any further CLL therapy following first-line FCR. Patients underwent a peripheral blood draw to test for NGS-based MRD by the clonoSEQ assay (Adaptive Biotechnologies, Seattle, WA),6 either on-site or via a shipped kit. Additional serial samples for MRD6 testing are being obtained on a yearly basis where possible. The identification of the dominant immunoglobulin (Ig) sequence present at baseline was performed using banked genomic DNA or bone marrow biopsy slides from pretreatment specimens.

Eight hundred eight patients treated with FCR-based regimens were identified. Of these, 562 patients (70%) were known to have progressed and/or died. An additional 38 (5%) without known International Workshop on CLL progression but MRD4-positive at last follow-up were excluded. Of the remaining 208 potential candidates, 54 were successfully contacted, consented, and enrolled. Baseline NGS testing for Ig sequence identification and at least 1 follow-up peripheral blood MRD sample were obtained in 17 patients (supplemental Figure 1), which comprise the cohort reported here (subsequently referred to as the NGS MRD cohort). Follow-up for this cohort continues for annual blood MRD6 evaluation.

The baseline characteristics of the NGS MRD cohort are shown in Table 1. The median age at the time of initiation of FCR was 54 years (range, 39-70). At the time of the first NGS MRD testing, the median age was 68 years (range, 54-89). Following FCR, 13 of 17 (76%) patients were undetectable MRD4 (uMRD4) (9 peripheral blood samples and 4 bone marrow samples) using a standard MRD4 flow cytometry assay; the remaining 4 of 17 (24%) had no detectable CLL by 2-color flow cytometry, before the availability of standardized multicolor flow cytometry MRD evaluation. The median time from FCR start to first NGS-based MRD sampling was 15.7 years. The cohort consisted of 9 (53%) patients with mutated IGHV, 7 (41%) with unmutated IGHV, and 1 (6%) with unknown IGHV status. In terms of pretreatment cytogenetics, 6 (35%) patients had del(13q), 5 (29%) had trisomy 12, 1 (6%) had del(11q) (6%), and 5 (29%) had no fluorescence in situ hybridization abnormalities. Compared with the full 808-patient FCR cohort, patients included in the NGS MRD cohort had statistically similar baseline characteristics (age, IGHV status, and cytogenetics; supplemental Table 1). However, patients with del(17p) were notably absent from the NGS MRD cohort. Testing for TP53 mutations was not routine at the time that these patients were treated and is not available. IGHV gene usage and mutation status are shown in Figure 1. At the time of this report, 12 (71%) patients had a single MRD6 time point determination available, 3 (18%) had 2 MRD6 time point determinations available, and 2 (12%) had 3 MRD6 time point determinations available. In the patients with multiple MRD6 time points, the median observation time between the first and last MRD time point was 20.6 months (range, 12.0-36.5).

The blood NGS-based MRD levels over time for each patient are shown in Figure 1. All follow-up samples met the minimum 20-μg genomic DNA input and 1 000 000 cell equivalents for MRD6 sensitivity (median, 3 480 550 cells/sample; range 1 811 434-3 934 695). Most patients (13/17 [76%]) had detectable MRD6 at the first follow-up time point, whereas 4 (24%) had uMRD6. Of the patients with detectable MRD6, 3 (23%) had MRD levels below MRD6, 5 (38%) had MRD levels between MRD6 and MRD4, and 5 (38%) had MRD levels above MRD4 (Figure 1). One patient with no known clinical progression or subsequent CLL treatment (MDA44) had 55% CLL cells at the first MRD sampling time point (no recent flow cytometry data were available at the time of screening). This case likely represented recurrent indolent disease. In the patients with multiple MRD time point determinations available (n = 5), we generally noted stability in MRD levels over time. One patient (MDA01) had 3 stable positive MRD time points just over MRD4, 1 (MDA06) had 2 stable positive MRD time points between MRD4 and MRD6, 1 (MDA07) had 2 stable positive MRD time points near MRD6, and 1 (MDA14) had 2 consecutive uMRD6 time points. Interestingly, 1 patient (MDA11) had 2 positive MRD time points close to MRD6 with subsequent uMRD6 at the third time point (Figure 1). Overall, the MRD6 positivity rate was 6 of 9 (67%) in IGHV-mutated and 6 of 7 (86%) in IGHV-unmutated.

Although our patient numbers were extremely small to establish definitive associations between CLL genetic characteristics and uMRD6, we noted that, in patients with uMRD6 (n = 4), 3 (75%) were IGHV-mutated, 2 (50%) had del(13q), and 1 (25%) had trisomy 12.

Our group previously reported that MRD6 determination is a valuable tool for monitoring CLL following CIT and provides prognostic information in patients with uMRD4 by flow cytometry.7 This report, although in a small number of patients, provides further insights into CLL MRD6 in very-long-term, progression-free survivors following first-line FCR. For example, we noted that, in this select cohort of patients, approximately three-quarters had detectable CLL despite long-term clinical progression-free status. Given that we observed general stability in the level of CLL over time in a limited number tested, we hypothesize that the residual CLL population may have limited proliferative potential, existing in a somewhat “dormant” state, or may be under immunologic control. What is more striking is that just under half the patients in this cohort had unmutated IGHV, most of whom had a detectable clone, yet have remained in remission for over a decade.

Our finding that several patients had uMRD6 at 8, 11, 13, and 18 years following FCR, including 1 patient with 2 negative time points, indicates potential complete eradication of the CLL clone and challenges the paradigm of CLL as an “incurable” disease.

Although therapy for CLL has shifted away from CIT toward targeted agents, the value of MRD testing as a predictor of PFS was also demonstrated in patients treated with time-limited venetoclax-based regimens, but not with continuous Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitor–based regimens.8,9 Notably, MRD6 status has been shown to be predictive of PFS with time-limited venetoclax-based combinations in the CLL13 study.10 The novel triplet combination of the BTK inhibitor pirtobrutinib with venetoclax and obinutuzumab (PVO) was recently shown to yield extremely high rates (81%) of bone marrow uMRD6 following 13 months of time-limited therapy.11 It will be of interest to compare MRD dynamics and remission durability in this group vs FCR as longer-term follow-up data become available.

Our study has some notable limitations. For instance, only a small proportion of the cohort of FCR-treated patients was available for testing. This was due to a combination of factors related to the long time interval since receiving treatment, including progression/death in many patients and logistical issues in enrolling long-term survivors, many of whom could not be successfully contacted, were unable/unwilling to provide samples, or did not have adequate material for baseline testing. Therefore, our results apply to a small cohort of patients and results in a selection bias toward patients who had better outcomes and were likely younger. Finally, our results only apply to patients treated with CIT and further data are needed on MRD dynamics in patients treated with targeted therapies to determine if persistent clinical remissions can be observed despite the presence of detectable MRD.
