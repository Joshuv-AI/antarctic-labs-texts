Comparative study on the performance and early clinical outcomes of senior and junior orthopaedic surgeons in traditional versus robot-assisted total hip arthroplasty
Authors: Mingyou Wang, Xunzhou Song, Zhuodong Tang, Heng Xiao, Shaojiang Liu, Zaihai Yang, Hongping Wang, Yuping Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe differential impact of robotic assistance on surgeons with varying levels of experience in total hip arthroplasty (THA) remains underexplored. This retrospective study investigated whether robotic-assisted THA (RA-THA) differentially affects the surgical performance and early clinical outcomes of senior versus junior surgeons compared to manual THA (M-THA). A total of 211 patients who underwent primary THA were divided into four groups based on surgeon seniority (senior, with > 10 years of experience vs. junior, with < 2 years of experience) and surgical technique (RA-THA vs. M-THA). All six participating surgeons (three senior, three junior) completed standardized robotic training before performing RA-THA. Perioperative parameters, radiographic outcomes, and one-month clinical follow-up data were analyzed. The results demonstrated a significant effect with use of robotic assistance, which differed slightly based on seniority level. For junior surgeons, RA-THA dramatically elevated their performance to the level of senior experts. While junior surgeons in the M-THA group exhibited significant variability, with a lower rate of acetabular cups placed within the Lewinnek safe zone (79.2%) andless precise restoration of leg length and offset, robotic assistance enabled them to achieveresults similar to senior surgeonsfor these parameters, including a safe zone attainment rate of 94.2%. RA-THA further improvedthe precision of senior surgeons (safe zone rate from 88.7% to 94.3%) but at the cost of significantly prolonged operative time, with no observable advantage in early functional outcomes or complications. Notably, early clinical outcomes at one month were insignificant across all groups, suggesting that the precision benefits of RA-THA may require longer follow-up to fully elucidate .Ultimately RA-THA serves as a powerful equalizer between tiers of surgical experience, effectively enabling junior surgeons toclosely replicate the technical precision of their senior counterparts. For senior surgeons, the technology offers marginaltechnical benefit at the expense of efficiency, potentiallycreating barriers to adoption. These findings suggest that the value proposition of RA-THA should be tailored to surgeon seniority, prioritizing its use for training novice surgeons and managing complex cases.Keywords: Robotic-assisted surgery, Total hip arthroplasty, Surgeon seniority, Learning curve, Acetabular cup positioning, Clinical outcomes

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is the gold standard surgical procedure for treating end-stage hip arthrosis, providing pain alleviation and restoration of function. Its long-term success is closely related toproper positioning of both the femoral and acetabular prostheses, particularly the precise implantation of the acetabular cup [1]. The classic Lewinnek safe zone (40°±10° of abduction and 15°±10° of anteversion) is widely regarded as the critical target range for acetabular cup position in order to reduce postoperative complications such as dislocation, wear, and periprosthetic fractures [2, 3]. Conventional THA relies on the surgeon’s manualplacement and spatial judgment, resulting in considerable variability in the actual placement angles of the acetabular component, which may stray from safe zone values. The results are therefore often highly dependent on the surgeon’s experience and technical proficiency [4–6].

Literature has consistently demonstrated that surgeon experience directly correlates withtechnical precisionduring THA implantation and early clinical outcomes. Due to a steep learning curve and lacking experience, junior surgeons are more prone tosuboptimal technical outcomes during conventional THA such asplacement of theacetabular cup outside the safe zone and inadequate restoration of leg lengths, consequently leading to higher rates of early dislocation and revision risk [7, 8]. This experience-based disparity not only impacts patient safety but also poses a persistent challenge to surgical training and the homogenisation of medical care quality [9, 10]. In recent years, the development of robotic-assisted total hip arthroplasty (RA-THA) systems has provided a novel technological pathway to enhance the accuracy and predictability of THA [11, 12]. By utilising preoperative CT-based three-dimensional planning, intraoperative real-time navigation, and robotic-arm assisted reaming, this technology aims to achieve both “personalised” and “precise” implantation of the prosthesis. Multiple studies have confirmed that, compared to conventional surgery, RA-THA offers significant advantages in increasing the proportion of acetabular cups placed within the Lewinnek safe zone and reducing angular deviations [13].

Previous investigations into the value of RA-THAhave primarily focused on overall improvement in accuracy across all surgeon cohorts, orhave compared it as a single technology against conventional methods, often neglecting its potentially differential impact on surgeons with varying levels of experience. Some studies suggest that robotic assistance may shorten the learning curve for junior surgeons, enabling them to achieve a stable and precise surgical performance more rapidly [14, 15]. Conversely, for senior experts who have already developed stable surgical patterns and advanced manual skills, the introduction of a robotic system may entail changes to the surgical workflow and additional learning costs. Whether it can confer significant clinical benefits, particularly in terms of efficiency gains, remains questionable. This uncertainty questions the long-term value of utilizing the technology throughout the duration of a surgical career and my influence the willingness ofeither cohort to fully adopt this valuable new technology.

There are no current studies that the authors are aware of that compare the clinical efficacy of conventional versus robotic-assisted total hip arthroplasty when performed by surgeons of different seniority levels. Determining whether RA-THA universally elevates the performance of all surgeons, or whether it might diminish the inherent experience-based advantage of senior practitioners, holds significant practical implications for the rational allocation of healthcare resources, the optimisation of surgical training, and the formulation of strategies for technology adoption.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether differences exist in the surgical performance and early clinical outcomes between senior and junior orthopaedic surgeons performing conventional versus robotic-assisted THA. Theprimary goal is to analyse the impact of robotic assistance on the performance and outcome gaps between surgeons of different seniority levels, specifically examining its effect on enhancing the surgical quality for junior surgeons while concurrently assessing its concrete impact on the surgical efficiency of senior surgeons. The findings aim to provide empirical evidence for the clinical positioning and broader adoption of RA-THA.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was a single-centre, retrospective cohort study designed to compare and analyse thesurgical technical precision andpatient clinical outcomesfor senior and junior orthopaedic surgeons performing both conventionaland robotic-assisted total hip arthroplasty.

Study population

Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients undergoing primary unilateral THA for the first time, with a primary diagnosis of hip osteoarthritis (OA), osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH, Ficat stage III-IV), or developmental dysplasia of the hip (Crowe type I & II). (2) Age between 18 and 80 years. (3) Complete clinical and imaging data were available.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Previous history of surgery on the ipsilateral hip. (2) Severe osteoporosis or bone defects requiring complex reconstruction. (3) Active infection or neuromuscular disease. (4) Cases converted to another surgical procedure intraoperatively or those with incomplete data records.

Grouping criteria

Definition of surgeon qualification and seniority

In this study, based on clinical experience and training stage, all participating surgeons were right-handed. The six operating surgeons were clearly divided into two groups: a senior group and a junior group [16].

Senior Surgeon Group: Comprised of three orthopaedic (associate) chief physicians, each with over 10 years of independent experience in performing THA (coded as S1, S2, S3). This group represented senior experts possessing a stable surgical approach, extensive clinical decision-making skills, and the ability to manage complex situations.

Junior Surgeon Group: Comprised of three orthopaedic residents in their final year of specialised training (coded as L1, L2, L3). Each surgeon had less than 2 years of experience performing THA as the primary operator under supervision. This group represented novice surgeons at a steep phase of the learning curve, requiring standardised technical training.

Surgical technique groups and preoperative training

Surgical Technique Groups: Based on the surgical technique actually employed, all cases were divided into the conventional total hip arthroplasty (M-THA) group and the robotic-assisted total hip arthroplasty (RA-THA) group.

Standardised Training for Robotic Surgery: To control for confounding by the learning curve inherent to the robotic system itself, all six surgeons completed a simulator-based standardised training programme for the robotic system and passed the prescribed skills assessment before performing any of the clinical robotic-assisted THA procedures included in this study. This ensured a consistent level of proficiency with the robotic platform.

Final study groups and case composition

By combining surgeon seniority and surgical technique, patients were divided into the following four groups for comparative analysis: Group A (Junior - Conventional), Group B (Junior - Robotic), Group C (Senior - Conventional), and Group D (Senior - Robotic). The final total sample size for this study was 211 cases, with a balanced distribution across the groups. Surgical technique

All patients underwent general anaesthesia combined with regional block and were placed in the lateral decubitus position. A posterolateral approach was used for all total hip arthroplasties. In the conventional surgery group, the procedure was performed relying entirely on the surgeon’s experience and standard instrumentation, without the use of any computer navigation or robotic assistance. In the robotic group, the ARTHROBOT robotic-assisted surgical system was employed. Preoperatively, pelvic and femoral CT data were imported into the robotic system to determine the acetabular cup abduction angle, anteversion angle, centre of rotation, and femoral stem size. Intraoperatively, a femoral registration array was fixed at the level of the patella on the distal lateral femur, and a pelvic dynamic reference frame was secured at the anterior superior iliac spine. The skin and subcutaneous tissues were incised layer by layer to fully expose the hip joint. Femoral neck osteotomy was performed approximately 5 to 10 mm superior to the lesser trochanter, and the femoral head was removed. Subsequently, under the guidance of the robotic system, registration and mapping of the acetabulum were completed. Acetabular reaming was then performed under robotic-arm guidance to achieve the planned orientation and depth. The acetabular cup was implanted into the pre-planned position under the angle guidance of the robotic arm, its orientation was checked, and screw fixation was applied if needed before inserting the liner. Femoral preparation was carried out using conventional manual techniques; after adequate broaching, the preoperatively planned femoral stem prosthesis was implanted. Aftertrials to assess joint stability and limb length, the final femoral head prosthesis was implanted, and the joint was reduced. The wound was irrigated thoroughly, and the capsule and soft tissues were closed in layers. All K-wires and reference arrays were removed, and a sterile dressing was applied. As illustrated in Fig. 1, all patients received the same perioperative management protocol, including anti-inflammatory, analgesic, anticoagulation, anti-oedema therapy, and functional exercise, in accordance with the ‘Expert Consensus on Enhanced Recovery after Surgery—Perioperative Management Strategies for Hip and Knee Arthroplasty in China’.

Outcome measures and data collection

Data were extracted independently by two researchers who were not involved in the surgeries and were blinded to the group allocation. They used a standardised data collection form, retrieving information from the hospital’s electronic medical record system, the picture archiving and communication system (PACS), and the surgical anaesthesia records. In cases of disagreement, arbitration was performed by a third senior researcher.

Patient demographic and preoperative data were collected, including age, sex, body mass index, preoperative diagnosis, disease duration, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade, preoperative Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score, Harris Hip Score (HHS), hip range of motion (flexion-extension and internal-external rotation), and leg length discrepancy (LLD). Intraoperatively, the total incision length, system preparation and registration time, acetabular side operative time, femoral side operative time, total operative time, and intraoperative blood loss were recorded, along with postoperative wound healing status. Inflammatory markers, including white blood cell count (WBC), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and C-reactive protein (CRP), were measured three days postoperatively. Postoperative CT and pelvic radiographs were used to measure LLD, acetabular abduction angle (AAA), acetabular anteversion (AA), bilateral femoral offset difference (FOD), bilateral acetabular offset difference (AOD), and bilateral combined offset difference (COD) (Fig. 2). The proportion of acetabular cups placed within the Lewinnek safe zone was calculated. Postoperative length of hospital stay, hospitalisation costs, and time to full weight-bearing were also collected. Follow-up assessments at one month postoperatively recorded VAS pain score, Harris Hip Score, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) score for comfort, hip range of motion (flexion-extension and internal-external rotation), complication rates, and patient satisfaction.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 26.0). Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̅ ± s). For data following a normal distribution, comparisons between two groups were conducted using the independent samples t-test, comparisons among multiple time points within a group were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and post-hoc pairwise comparisons were carried out using the least significant difference (LSD) method. For data not conforming to a normal distribution, the rank-sum test was employed. Categorical data were analysed using the chi-square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

A total of 211 patients who met the inclusion criteria were ultimately enrolled in this study. The senior surgeon group completed 53 RA-THA and 53 conventional THA procedures, while the junior surgeon group completed 52 RA-THA and 53 M-THA procedures. No significant intergroup differences were observed across any baseline characteristics, including demographic data (age, sex, BMI), clinical presentation (preoperative diagnosis, affected side, disease duration, ASA grade), preoperative functional scores (VAS, HHS, WOMAC comfort), hip range of motion (flexion-extension and internal-external rotation), or leg length discrepancy (LLD) (all P > 0.05). The groups were therefore considered comparable (Table 2).

Surgical parameters

Surgery was completed successfully in all four groups. The incision length was longer for patients in both the RA-THA and M-THA subgroups performed by junior surgeons compared to those operated on by senior surgeons; however, the difference between RA-THA and M-THA within the junior group was not statistically significant. In contrast, for senior surgeons, the incision length for RA-THA was approximately 1 cm longer than for M-THA (P < 0.05). No system preparation or registration time was required for patients in the M-THA groups. Within the RA-THA groups, the system preparation and registration time for senior surgeons was approximately 1.4 min shorter than for junior surgeons (P < 0.05). During acetabular reaming, the reaming time was comparable between senior and junior surgeons when performing RA-THA (P > 0.05), averaging approximately 10 min. However, in the M-THA groups, the reaming time was significantly longer than in the RA-THA groups (P < 0.05). Furthermore, within the M-THA groups, the reaming time was significantly longer for junior surgeons compared to senior surgeons (P < 0.05). As femoral preparation was performed using conventional manual techniques, the operative time for this step was consistently shorter for senior surgeons compared to junior surgeons (P < 0.05). The total operative time for RA-THA was significantly longer than for M-THA for both senior and junior surgeons (P < 0.05). In the RA-THA cohorts, total operative time did not differ significantly between senior and junior surgeons (P > 0.05). Conversely, within the M-THA cohorts, procedures performed by senior surgeons required significantly less operative time than those performed by junior surgeons (P < 0.05). Across all four groups, no significant differences were observed in intraoperative blood loss, wound healing rate, postoperative hospital stay, or hospitalisation costs (P > 0.05) (Table 3).

Comparison of inflammatory markers at postoperative day 3 and radiographic outcomes

There were no statistically significant differences in the inflammatory markers (WBC, ESR, and CRP) measured on postoperative day 3 among the four groups (P > 0.05). Regarding LLD, no significant difference was found between patients in the senior and junior surgeon subgroups within the RA-THA groups (P > 0.05). However, LLD was significantly greater in the M-THA groups compared to the RA-THA groups for both senior and junior surgeons (P < 0.05). Furthermore, within the M-THA groups, LLD was significantly larger for patients operated on by junior surgeons than for those operated on by senior surgeons (P < 0.05). While the mean AAA showed no statistically significant differences among the four groups, the variance in AAA was larger in the M-THA groups compared to the RA-THA groups, with the greatest variance observed in theM-THA subgroup performed by junior surgeons. For AA, there was no significant difference between the senior and junior surgeon subgroups within the RA-THA groups (P > 0.05). In contrast, within theM-THA groups, both the mean AA and its variance were significantly larger than those in the RA-THA groups (P < 0.05). Additionally, within theM-THA groups, the mean AA was significantly larger, and its variance significantly greater, in the junior surgeon subgroup compared to the senior surgeon subgroup (P < 0.05). The differences in FOD, AOD, and COD among the four groups exhibited a similar pattern. Within the RA-THA groups, there were no statistically significant differences in FOD, AOD, or COD between patients operated on by senior and junior surgeons. However, in theM-THA groups, these offset differences were significantly larger compared to the RA-THA groups, with significantly larger values observed in the junior surgeon subgroup compared to the senior surgeon subgroup (P < 0.05). Analysis of the proportion of acetabular cups placed within the Lewinnek safe zone revealed that in the RA-THA groups, this proportion was maintained at 94% or higher for patients of both senior and junior surgeons. In contrast, the proportion within the Lewinnek safe zone was significantly lower in theM-THA groups compared to the RA-THA groups for surgeons at both seniority levels (P < 0.05). Furthermore, within theM-THA groups, the proportion of cups within the safe zone was significantly higher for senior surgeons than for junior surgeons (P < 0.05) (Table 4).

Comparison of complications

Postoperative complications among the four groups primarily included intramuscular venous thrombosis, hip dislocation, and wound exudate. Intramuscular venous thrombosis was the most frequently observed complication. One patient in theM-THA groups experienced hip dislocation due to a twisting injury while squatting; this was successfully managed by closed reduction under anaesthesia. In the senior surgeon RA-THA group, one patient developed postoperative wound exudate, which healed normally after wound dressing changes. No instances of periprosthetic joint infection, robotic-related complications, pulmonary embolism, or periprosthetic fracture occurred in any patient. There were no statistically significant differences in the overall complication rates among the four groups. Patient satisfaction during hospitalisation was similarly high across all groups, maintained at over 94% (P > 0.05).

Discussion

The precision of implant placement during THA is closely correlated with the surgeon’s experience, a traditional paradigm that the advent of robotic-assisted technology seeks to challenge. P reviousstudies have primarily evaluated robotic technology as a monolithic entity compared to manual techniques [17], with few studiesstratifying its impact on surgeonsbased on experience level. The present studyseeks to tease out these experience-based differential effects through comparison of technical and clinical outcomes for both junior and senior orthopaedic surgeons. Furthermore, it explores the clinical-behavioural logic underlying these differences and their practical implications for the dissemination of this technology.

Robotic assistance significantly enhances surgical precision in junior surgeons, bridging the experience gap between surgeons of different seniority levels

The most salient finding of this study is the benefit encountered for techincal precision of robotic assistance on the surgical quality of junior surgeons. In M-THA, the performance of junior surgeons exhibited characteristic features of a learning curve: substantial dispersion in both AAA and AA, with a Lewinnek safe zone attainment rate of only 79.2%, significantly lower than the 88.7% achieved by senior surgeons. More notably, in refined parameters such as LLD control and reconstruction of the centre of rotation (reflected by differences in FOD, AOD, and COD), the errors observed in the junior surgeon group were significantly larger than those in the senior surgeon group. These findingssupport the premise thatM-THA is highly dependent on the surgeon’s spatial perception and manual dexterity, two attributes that areinherent limitationsfor novice surgeons [18, 19].

However, when junior surgeons employed robotic assistance, their surgical performance demonstrated a significant improvement. Parameters including acetabular cup orientation, LLD, and centre of rotation position achievedprecision similar to that of senior surgeons. The Lewinnek safe zone attainment rate surged to 94.2%, showing no difference compared to the 94.3% in the senior RA-THA group, and was significantly superior to that of the senior M-THA group. This data robustly demonstrates that the RA-THA system, through preoperative CT-based three-dimensional planning, intraoperative real-time navigation, and precise robotic-arm guidance, transforms surgical decision-making from being “experience-dependent” to “data-driven”. Thisallows junior surgeonsto navigate the learning curve typically requiring hundreds of cases in a much more expeditious fas [20].

The significance of this technological empowerment for junior surgeons extends beyond the statistically demonstrated improvement in precision, carrying profound implications for clinical safety. The single case of postoperative dislocation observed in the junior M-THA group (while not reaching statistical significance, serves as a clinically noteworthy indicator) is a typical consequence of suboptimal prosthesis positioning stemming from subjective judgment errors inherent in the conventional approach. By eliminating this unpredictability associated with inexperience, robotic assistance fundamentally mitigates the risks of early revision and complications [21, 22] arising from malpositioned acetabular components or excessive LLD [23]. Therefore, we posit that junior surgeons’ receptiveness to RA-THA will be substantially higher than that of their senior counterparts, enabling them to confidently perform high-quality surgeries while safeguarding patient outcomes under the guidance of the robotic arm.

Robotic assistance negatively impacts surgical efficiency in senior surgeons, potentially affecting their technology adoption

In stark contrast to its overtly positive impact on junior surgeons, RA-THA presents a more complex picture within the senior surgeon cohort. Our data indicate that utlizing RA-THA allowed senior surgeons to further improve their Lewinnek safe zone attainment rate from 88.7% to 94.3%, confirming the precision-enhancing capability of robotic technology and its alignment with the high standards expected of senior practitioners. However, this gain in precision did not translate into perceptible benefits for patients in terms of clinical outcomes at one month postoperatively (VAS, Harris Hip Score, ROM, WOMAC comfort), nor did it reduce the rate of short-term complications.

One outcome of RA-THA utilization that warrants deeper investigation is surgical efficiency. For senior surgeons, the total operative time for RA-THA was significantly prolonged by 15 min compared to their M-THA procedures. While this additional time is ostensibly required for the technical steps of robotic system preparation and registration, when analysed through a clinical-behavioural lens, we propose it may also represent a period of adaptation and reconciliation between the surgeon’s accumulated experiential knowledge and the guidance provided by the robotic arm.

Senior surgeons, through years of clinical practice, have developed highly stable, almost intuitive surgical approaches and spatial judgment. During conventional surgery, they can execute smooth, real-time adjustments based on the bony landmarks of the acetabulum and intraoperative tactile feedback. In robotic-assisted surgery, however, they must partially cede decision-making authority to a digital plan based on preoperative CT. When a divergence arises between the reaming angle guided by the robot and their experiential judgment—for instance, in scenarios with poor exposure or osteophyte keep out—senior surgeons may instinctively question the guidance. This can lead to additional time spent on repeated verification, manual fine-tuning, or re-registration to confirm the accuracy of the robotic dictate. While this interplay between experience and data may ultimately ensure the precision of implant placement, it undoubtedly compromises the smoothness of the surgical workflow and prolongs the operative time. As astutely observed, this “autonomous judgment inertia” stemming from deep-seated experience may constitute a psychological barrier for senior surgeons in adopting robotic assistance. Furthermore, the slightly longer incision length observed in the senior RA-THA group compared to their M-THA group, while not affecting healing, reflects the more stringent exposure requirements of robotic surgery. Therefore, for senior surgeons, the value proposition of RA-THA should not be framed as an efficiency tool for routine work. Instead, its utility should be centred on its standardising and reproducible advantages—for example, in managing cases with complex anatomy (such as DDH [24, 25] or acetabular defects [26]), for teaching demonstrations, or as a digital means of validating one’s own technical consistency.

No significant differences in early clinical outcomes among patients in different technique and seniority groups; long-term follow-up remains necessary

Notably, despite significant differences in radiographic parameters among the four groups, functional scores, pain levels, and complication rates were homogeneous at the one-month postoperative assessment. This phenomenon suggests that in routine, non-complex primary THA cases, satisfactory clinical results in the short term can be achieved by orthopaedic surgeons of varying seniority using either conventional or robotic-assisted techniques, provided that standardised perioperative management and rehabilitation protocols are followed. The “precision advantage” demonstrated radiographically may require a longer time horizon to manifest clinically—for instance, theoretically, more optimal implant positioning should lead to lower long-term wear rates, reduced osteolysis, and a decreased risk of aseptic loosening, thereby extending implant survival. Therefore, the conclusions of this study must be cautiously confined to the early postoperative phase. Long-term follow-up of these patients for a minimum of 5 to 10 years will be essential to validate the true clinical value of RA-THA [27–29].

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-centre retrospective study, the sample size was limited, as a single-center study, our findings lack external validation in different clinical settings with varying patient populations, surgical teams, and practice patterns. Multi-center collaborative studies are needed to validate these findings and assess their generalizability across diverse healthcare environments. and the potential for selection bias cannot be entirely excluded, however, baseline characteristics were well-balanced across all four groups, suggesting that any selection bias was limited. Nevertheless, prospective randomized trials remain the gold standard to confirm these findings. Second, the follow-up period for clinical outcomes was short, precluding assessment of long-term implant survival. While no a priori power analysis was conducted, post-hoc analysis for the primary outcome (Lewinnek safe zone attainment) demonstrated > 80% power to detect the observed differences between groups, suggesting adequate sample size for our main conclusions. Third, the exploration of the “experience interference” phenomenon in senior surgeons was primarily based on clinical observation and logical deduction. Future research could employ qualitative methods, such as postoperative interviews or cognitive load measurements, to quantify these psychological and behavioural processes. Fourth, the health economic evaluation was confined to hospitalisation costs and did not account for the acquisition and maintenance costs of the robotic equipment, a factor that directly influences technology adoption decisions at the institutional level. Fifth, this study evaluated a single robotic system. While this controlled for technology-specific confounders, it limits generalizability to other platforms with different workflows. Future studies should compare multiple systems to determine whether our findings are platform-specific or broadly generalizable. Finally, our study focused on routine primary THA cases and excluded complex anatomies. This design choice minimized confounding but limits generalizability to complex cases. Future studies should specifically evaluate whether RA-THA offers enhanced benefits for surgeons of varying seniority when managing complex hip pathologies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that RA-THA exerts significantly differential effects on junior and senior orthopaedic surgeons. For junior surgeons, this technology enables them to achieve parity with senior surgeons in key parameters such as acetabular cup placement accuracy, leg length control, and reconstruction of the hip centre of rotation. It effectively bridges the gap in precise implant positioning between surgeon cohorts and may potentially reduce the risk of early dislocation, suggesting itmay provide great utility to novice surgeons. For senior surgeons, while this technology can furtheroptimize implant positioning precision, it does not yield superior early clinical outcomes compared to conventional surgery. Instead, it leads to a significant prolongation of operative time due to system preparation and potential “experience interference” behaviours, which may affect their willingness to adopt it. This indicates that the value of this technology for this group should be more focused on managing complex cases and for teaching demonstrations. In summary, the clinical dissemination of RA-THA should fully consider surgeon seniority. It is recommended that limited resources be prioritised for the standardised training of junior surgeons and the management of complex cases to maximise the technology’s benefits and advance the field of joint surgery towards greater precision and homogenisation of care.
