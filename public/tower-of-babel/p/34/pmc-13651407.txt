Mechanistic insights into TXNDC17-mediated ferroptosis suppression in glioblastoma progression
Authors: Zixuan Jing, Qingbo Wang, Chenglong Li, Xinyu Zhao, Dianhan Zhang, Zhibing Liu, Zefu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlioblastoma (GBM) is a lethal brain tumor with limited treatment options. Molecular targeted therapy is considered safe and promising for GBM. Ferroptosis is regulated by lipid, iron and cysteine metabolism. Induction of ferroptosis is a promising strategy.MethodsIntegrated bioinformatics analysis of GBM datasets (TCGA) and RNA-seq data from human gliomas was used to identify candidate genes. TXNDC17 expression was validated in glioma tissues and cell lines by qPCR, western blotting and immunofluorescence. In vitro functional assays (CCK-8, wound healing, Transwell) were performed to assess proliferation, migration and invasion following TXNDC17 knockdown (siRNA). Ferroptosis sensitivity was evaluated by measuring GSH and LPO levels after TXNDC17 manipulation. GPX4 rescue experiments were conducted. In vivo therapeutic efficacy was examined in orthotopic GBM mouse models using TXNDC17-silenced cells treated with RSL3 (an inhibitor of GPX4) with tumor growth monitored by bioluminescence.ResultsBioinformatics analysis identified TXNDC17 as a key prognostic risk factor. Molecular validation confirmed grade-dependent TXNDC17 overexpression in gliomas. TXNDC17 knockdown markedly suppressed GBM cell proliferation, migration and invasion. Mechanistically, TXNDC17 promoted glioma progression by regulating ferroptosis, specifically through cooperation with GPX4 to inhibit ferroptosis. Knockdown reduced GPX4 expression, thereby increasing ferroptosis sensitivity. GPX4 overexpression rescued proliferation and invasion defects caused by TXNDC17 knockdown, confirming its functional dependence. TXNDC17 silencing combined with RSL3 synergistically suppressed GBM growth in vitro.ConclusionTXNDC17 functions as a novel oncoprotein and ferroptosis suppressor in GBM by regulating GPX4 and promoting tumor progression. Targeting the TXNDC17–GPX4 axis through TXNDC17 silencing and RSL3 treatment represents a potent synergistic therapeutic strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00898-x.Keywords: GBM, Ferroptosis, TXNDC17, GPX4

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most common and aggressive primary malignant brain tumor in adults, accounting for more than half of all gliomas [1]. It is characterized by high heterogeneity, pronounced invasiveness, diffuse growth patterns and strong resistance to conventional therapies [2, 3] Despite the adoption of a comprehensive treatment strategy consisting of maximal safe surgical resection followed by concurrent radiochemotherapy (temozolomide combined with radiotherapy) and adjuvant chemotherapy, the prognosis of patients with GBM remains extremely poor. The median survival is only approximately 15 months, and the five-year survival rate consistently remains below 10% [4, 5]. Tumor recurrence is nearly inevitable, and treatment options after recurrence are severely limited with minimal efficacy. Therefore, elucidating the core molecular mechanisms underlying GBM malignancy and therapeutic resistance as well as identifying targetable vulnerabilities, constitutes a critical challenge in neuro-oncology that urgently requires resolution.

In recent years, the targeted modulation of regulated cell death (RCD) pathways, particularly ferroptosis, has attracted considerable attention for its role in tumorigenesis and treatment resistance, offering promising new avenues to overcome GBM recalcitrance. Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent, non-apoptotic form of cell death driven by the excessive accumulation of lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS). Its defining features include glutathione (GSH) depletion, loss of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) activity and lethal lipid peroxidation (LPO) [6, 7]. GPX4, a key intracellular antioxidant enzyme and the principal negative regulator of ferroptosis, utilizes reduced glutathione to convert toxic lipid hydroperoxides (L-OOH) into harmless lipid alcohols (L-OH) [8, 9]. This process is indispensable for maintaining the redox balance of membrane lipids and represents the final defense against ferroptosis. Therapeutic targeting of ferroptosis (e.g., GPX4 inhibition by RSL3 or disruption of cystine uptake to impair GSH synthesis) has been shown to induce cancer cell death effectively in various tumor models [10–12].

Importantly, the biological characteristics of GBM establish a distinctive association with ferroptosis. GBM cells display abnormal metabolic reprogramming, such as the Warburg effect, resulting in the generation of large quantities of ROS and thereby maintaining a state of persistent oxidative stress [13, 14]. Consequently, they are highly dependent on antioxidant systems to maintain redox homeostasis, including protection against ferroptosis. Standard treatments such as radiotherapy, chemotherapy (e.g., temozolomide) and microenvironmental factors (e.g., hypoxia) can exacerbate oxidative stress and promote susceptibility to ferroptosis [15]. Nevertheless, GBM cells effectively counteract this tendency, contributing to robust therapeutic resistance and treatment failure. Given their heavy reliance on redox balance, deliberate disruption of ferroptosis defense mechanisms (e.g., GPX4 inhibition) has been proposed as a highly promising therapeutic strategy. However, preclinical studies demonstrate substantial heterogeneity in GBM sensitivity to single ferroptosis inducers (such as RSL3), suggesting the presence of complex and incompletely understood intrinsic resistance mechanisms [16]. In particular, whether critical regulatory factors beyond the established System Xc–GSH–GPX4 axis play essential roles in sustaining ferroptosis resistance in GBM cells remains a central research question and a potential therapeutic breakthrough.

The thioredoxin (Trx) system constitutes another vital antioxidant and redox-regulating mechanism that maintains protein disulfide bond homeostasis, scavenges ROS and modulates multiple signaling pathways [17–19]. Together with the thioredoxin domain-containing (TXNDC) protein family, the Trx system forms a network that supports a reducing cellular environment [20]. Thioredoxin domain-containing protein 17 (TXNDC17), a member of this family, remains poorly characterized with respect to its biological functions and molecular mechanisms in tumors, particularly in GBM. Preliminary bioinformatic analyses suggest that TXNDC17 is significantly upregulated in GBM and is associated with poor prognosis. However, its involvement in GBM malignancy and the specific mechanisms by which it contributes to tumor progression, particularly in relation to ferroptosis, remain largely unexplored.

This study systematically elucidates the role of TXNDC17 as a key oncogene in GBM, integrating bioinformatics, molecular cell biology, and both in vitro and in vivo models. Our findings demonstrate that TXNDC17 expression increases with glioma malignancy and, by regulating GPX4 expression, suppresses ferroptosis. This suppression promotes GBM cell proliferation, invasion and progression. More importantly, we introduce and validate a novel combination therapeutic strategy: targeting TXNDC17 to relieve its inhibition of ferroptosis in conjunction with direct GPX4 inhibition (e.g., RSL3). This combined approach exhibited significantly greater antitumor efficacy in vivo compared with single-agent treatment. The subsequent sections of this paper provide a detailed description of the experimental design, data and mechanistic insights underlying these findings. The objective is to advance the understanding of ferroptosis resistance mechanisms in GBM and to provide a theoretical and experimental foundation for the development of innovative combination therapies targeting the TXNDC17–GPX4 axis, ultimately offering new hope for improving the prognosis of patients with this devastating disease.

Materials and methods

Data collection and filtering

Gene expression data for gliomas and the corresponding clinical information were obtained from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov ).

Human brain and glioma samples

Non-tumor control brain tissues were obtained from the surgical trajectory of five patients undergoing evacuation of intracerebral hemorrhage (sampled at least 1 cm away from the hematoma) and glioma tissue samples were obtained from five clinically diagnosed glioblastoma (GBM) patients. All samples were collected at Binzhou Medical University Hospital between January 2024 and September 2024 and were approved by the Clinical Ethics Committee.

Differential expression analysis and survival analysis

Download and organize the RNAseq data from the STAR process of the TCGA-GBM and TCGA-LGG projects from the TCGA database, and extract the data in TPM format. Use the Wilcoxon rank sum test statistical method for statistics, and visualize the data using the ggplot2 package. Use the survival package to conduct the proportional hazards assumption test and fit the survival regression. The results are visualized using the survminer package and the ggplot2 package, and then perform Bonferroni Correction on the original p-values.

Western blotting analysis

Glioma tissue samples of different grades and normal brain tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer for protein extraction, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Human BCAM/Cd239 ELISA Kit PicoKine®, Boster, Wuhan, China). The protein samples were separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Non-specific binding was blocked by incubating the membranes with rapid blocking solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at room temperature for 15 min. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: Anti-TXNDC17 (1:1000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), Anti-GPX4 (1:1000, Proteintech), Anti-ACSL4 (1:500, Servicebio), Anti-HO-1 (1:1000, Servicebio), Anti-SLC7A11 (1:1000, Proteintech), Anti-NRF2 (1:1000, Proteintech), Anti-FSP1 (1:1000, Proteintech) and Anti-GAPDH (1:1000, #5174, Cell Signaling Technology [CST], Boston, MA, USA). After incubation with secondary antibodies, protein bands were visualized using ECL Plus Western Blotting Substrate (Boster, Wuhan, China).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

For histopathological examination, tumor tissue Sect. (4 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, followed by eosin for 2 min. After dehydration and clearing, sections were mounted and observed under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Cell lines

The human glioma cell lines U251 and U87-MG and the mouse glioblastoma cell line GL261-luc were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China) following STR profiling verification. U251 and U87-MG cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂.

Knockdown of TXNDC17

TXNDC17 siRNA sequences were purchased from Qingdao Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and used in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The sequences were: si-TXNDC17-1, 5’-GGUAGAAUCUGAGUGUCUU-3’ and si-TXNDC17-2, 5’-GGAAAGAUCCAAAUAAUGA-3’. Transfection was performed using NB Transfection Reagent (Bioscien, Shanghai, China).

Viral vectors

Lentiviral shRNA vectors targeting human and mouse TXNDC17 genes (hU6-MCS-CBh-gcGFP-IRES-Blasticidin) were constructed and prepared by Qingdao Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). After infection of GL261-luc, U251 and U87 cells with lentivirus, stable cell lines were established by selection with blasticidin (2 µg/mL).

Real-time quantitative pcr

Twenty-four hours after transfection, RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. Total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the SweScript RT II First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Servicebio). Quantitative PCR was performed using AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme). The primer sequences were: TXNDC17 Forward: 5’-TCGCCTACTTTACGGGTTCTAAG-3’; GAPDH Forward: Human GAPDH qPCR Primer Pair (Beyotime). Relative expression levels, normalized to GAPDH, were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCT method by comparing expression levels with those of the control sample.

CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. U251 and U87-MG cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 5,000 cells per well. At 24, 48 and 72 h after transfection, CCK-8 reagent was added, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Ki-67 assay

Glioma cells were seeded on coverslips to reach approximately 70% confluence. After 24 h, cells were fixed for 15 min and incubated with 500 µL rabbit anti-Ki-67 monoclonal antibody at room temperature for 1 h. After washing, Cy3-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) was added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and coverslips were mounted on slides. Ki-67-positive cells were quantified by analyzing three regions of each coverslip using ImageJ software.

Wound-healing assay

Cells were seeded at 1 × 10⁶ cells per well in 6-well plates. After 24 h, a 200 µL pipette tip was used to create a scratch wound in the cell monolayer. Cells were incubated in serum-free medium for 24 h and imaged under an optical microscope (CKX53; Olympus). Wound areas were measured and quantified using ImageJ software.

Transwell assay

A Transwell invasion assay was performed to compare cell migration among different groups. The upper chambers of the Transwells were pre-coated with Matrigel, and 1 × 10⁶ cells in 100 µL of serum-free DMEM were seeded into the upper chambers. The lower chambers were filled with DMEM containing 20% FBS. After 24 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. Migrated cells were observed and photographed under an optical microscope (CKX53; Olympus), and cells were counted in three random fields using ImageJ software.

Lipid peroxidation assay

LPO levels were measured using an LPO detection kit. After cell collection, samples were lysed with Triton X-100. Anhydrous ethanol, sample solution, and color development solution were sequentially added, followed by vortexing and incubation at 45 °C for 60 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min, and 200 µL of supernatant was transferred into a 96-well plate. Absorbance was measured at 586 nm with a microplate reader and LPO levels were calculated.

Measurement of GSH levels

GSH levels were determined using a commercial assay kit. After collection, cells underwent two rapid freeze–thaw cycles followed by centrifugation to obtain the supernatant. Working solution was prepared and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Subsequently, 50 µL of 0.5 mg/mL NADPH solution was added and absorbance was measured at 412 nm using a microplate reader in kinetic mode.

Measurement of Fe²⁺ levels

Intracellular ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) concentrations were measured using a commercial FerroOrange assay kit. Absorbance values at 593 nm directly reflected intracellular Fe²⁺ levels.

Molecular docking

Protein-protein rigid docking was performed using the GRAMM platform. The three-dimensional structures of TXNDC17 and GPX4 were retrieved from the UniProtKB database. Both protein structures were submitted to the GRAMM system, and docking was carried out using the default protein-protein docking parameters. The system generated the top 10 docking poses ranked by binding compatibility, with the rank-1 conformation considered the optimal binding model. Binding free energy was calculated using the PDBePISA server. Visualization of the optimal docking complex, including the binding interface, hydrogen bond networks and key amino acid residues was performed using PyMOL version 3.1. Valid hydrogen bonds were defined as those with donor–acceptor distances within 1.5–3.5 Å.

Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay

U251 cells were transfected with sh-NC (negative control) or sh-TXNDC17 for 72 h. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with fresh complete medium containing either DMSO (vehicle control, 0 h time point) or cycloheximide (CHX, 50 µg/mL) for 6–12 h. CHX was freshly prepared as a 50 mg/mL stock solution in DMSO and then diluted 1:1000 in culture medium to reach the final concentration of 50 µg/mL. At each indicated time point (0, 6 and 12 h), cells were harvested and total protein was extracted for Western blot analysis of GPX4 and TXNDC17. Protein levels were quantified by densitometry and normalized to the loading control. The half-life of GPX4 was calculated by nonlinear regression analysis using GraphPad Prism.

Orthotopic brain tumor growth assay

GL261-luc cells stably transduced with shTXNDC17 lentivirus (1 × 10⁶ cells in 50 µL PBS per mouse) were stereotactically implanted into the brains of male C57BL/6 mice (8–9 weeks old, n = 5 per group; Jinmatai Co. Ltd.). The injection coordinates relative to bregma were: anteroposterior + 1.8 mm, mediolateral − 0.4 mm and dorsoventral − 2.9 mm 21. One group of mice received intraperitoneal injections of the GPX4-specific inhibitor RSL3 (10 mg/kg) twice weekly, while the other two groups were administered equivalent volumes of DMSO. Following implantation, all mice received intraperitoneal injections of 0.3 mL luciferase substrate for bioluminescence imaging. Tumor progression was monitored by quantifying photon flux using an IVIS Spectrum system on days 7, 14 and 21 post-implantation.

Statistical analysis

Raw high-throughput RNA sequencing data were analyzed using R software (version 4.2.1). Image processing was performed with ImageJ, while quantitative data analysis and statistical computations were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Intergroup comparisons were evaluated using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests for two-group analyses and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparisons among three or more groups. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics (version not specified).

Results

Comprehensive bioinformatics analysis confirmed that TXNDC17 is a critical risk factor and a potential therapeutic target in glioblastoma

Our study is the first to demonstrate, through bioinformatics data mining, that TXNDC17 expression was significantly elevated in glioma tissues compared with normal brain tissues, showing the most pronounced differential expression within the TXNDC family (p < 0.01, Fig. 1A).

The results showed that the HR value of COX analysis for TXNDC17 was the highest, and the corrected p value was less than 0.001. Survival analysis further revealed that patients with high TXNDC17 expression had a significantly shorter median overall survival (OS) of 22.4 months compared with 105.2 months in the low-expression group (log-rank p < 0.001, Fig. 1B-D). Multivariate Cox regression analysis of WHO grade, IDH status and TXNDC17’s expression

To assess the clinical predictive efficacy of TXNDC17, time-dependent ROC curves were generated. These demonstrated area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.809, 0.834 and 0.783 for predicting 1-year, 3-year and 5-year survival, respectively (Fig. 1E), significantly outperforming conventional clinical indicators. Standard ROC analysis further revealed an AUC of 0.915 for glioma diagnosis (Fig. 1F), indicating that TXNDC17 has both prognostic and auxiliary diagnostic value. Based on these findings, we constructed a clinically applicable prognostic nomogram integrating TXNDC17 expression, age, tumor grade and other relevant variables (Fig. 1G). Calibration curves confirmed the high accuracy of this model, providing a reliable tool for individualized prognostic assessment (Fig. 1H).

In summary, TXNDC17 demonstrates malignancy grade-dependent overexpression in gliomas and serves as an independent negative predictor of patient survival. Its strong prognostic and diagnostic performances collectively establish the translational value of TXNDC17 as a novel risk factor and potential therapeutic target in GBM.

Molecular experiments confirmed that TXNDC17 exhibits malignancy grade-dependent overexpression in glioma

To validate the differential expression of TXNDC17 between glioma and non-tumor control tissues, we collected five clinically diagnosed glioma samples and five non-tumor control brain tissues. The control tissues were obtained from the surgical trajectory of patients undergoing evacuation of intracerebral hemorrhage, with a minimum distance of 1 cm from the hematoma. Next-generation transcriptome sequencing revealed significant upregulation of TXNDC17 mRNA in grade IV gliomas (GBM) compared with control tissues (log₂FC = 1.24, adj. p < 0.001; Fig. 2A). RT-qPCR analysis confirmed a 4.54-fold increase in TXNDC17 mRNA expression in glioma versus control tissues (p = 0.0025; Fig. 2B). Western blotting further demonstrated that TXNDC17 protein expression was 2.94-fold higher in glioma tissues than in controls (p = 0.0013; Fig. 2C–D). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of tumor sections revealed that high-grade GBM tissues exhibited increased cellular atypia, dense cellularity, and prominent microvascular proliferation compared with control brain tissues (Fig. 2E). Immunofluorescence analysis of clinical GBM and control brain tissue samples corroborated these findings, revealing a grade-dependent increase in TXNDC17 expression and a significant difference between lesional and control tissues (Fig. 2E).

Knockdown of TXNDC17 significantly inhibits glioma cell proliferation, migration and invasion

To investigate the functional role of TXNDC17 in glioma cells, we performed TXNDC17 knockdown using siRNA in U251 and U87 cell lines. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed at both mRNA and protein levels by RT-qPCR and Western blotting, respectively (Fig. 3A–B). CCK-8 assays and Ki-67 immunofluorescence staining showed that TXNDC17 knockdown significantly suppressed cell viability and proliferation in both U251 and U87 cells (Fig. 3C–D, G). Because migration and invasion are prerequisites for cancer metastasis, we further assessed the metastatic potential of glioma cells. Wound healing and transwell invasion assays demonstrated that TXNDC17 knockdown markedly reduced the migratory and invasive capacities of glioma cells compared with control groups (Fig. 3E–F, H–L). Collectively, these findings highlight the pivotal role of TXNDC17 in regulating glioma cell growth and tumor progression.

TXNDC17 drives glioma progression by regulating the ferroptosis pathway

We next investigated the pathway through which TXNDC17 influences glioma cell growth and tumor progression. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed significant enrichment of cancer-related pathways in the TXNDC17 high-expression group (Fig. 4A–B). Furthermore, protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis using the STRING database (Fig. 4C) demonstrated that TXNDC17 interacts with multiple thioredoxin-related proteins, including Trx, thioredoxin reductase 1 (TXNRD1), and reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) [22–24]. Considering that ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation and given the established association between the thioredoxin system, redox balance and ferroptosis, these findings suggested that TXNDC17 promotes glioma progression by modulating the ferroptosis pathway.

Ferroptosis is biochemically characterized by decreased intracellular GSH levels, reduced GPX4 activity, accumulation of LPO and Fe²⁺ overload. The latter triggers Fenton reactions, generating excessive ROS that induce oxidative stress in organelles, including mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus [25, 26]. We therefore examined key ferroptosis biomarkers in glioma cells. Following TXNDC17 knockdown, GSH levels were significantly reduced (p < 0.0001; Fig. 4D), indicating depletion of antioxidant capacity. Concurrently, LPO levels were markedly elevated in sh-TXNDC17 cells (p < 0.05; Fig. 4E), indicating oxidative damage to cell membranes. In addition, assessment of cellular Fe²⁺ content revealed a significant increase upon TXNDC17 depletion (p < 0.05; Fig. 4F), providing further evidence of its regulatory role in ferroptosis.

To confirm the dependence of this mechanism, we investigated whether the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) could rescue the phenotypic inhibition induced by TXNDC17 knockdown. Stable TXNDC17 knockdown cell lines (sh-TXNDC17) were established and treated with 0.2 µM Fer-1. CCK-8 assays and immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that Fer-1 significantly restored the viability of sh-TXNDC17 cells (p < 0.0001; Fig. 5A) and increased the proportion of Ki-67⁺ proliferating cells (p < 0.001; Fig. 5B, E). Wound healing and Transwell invasion assays further showed that Fer-1 treatment partially rescued the suppressed migratory and invasive capacities of sh-TXNDC17 cells, significantly increasing both migration rate and the number of invading cells compared with untreated sh-TXNDC17 cells (p < 0.01; Fig. 5C–D, F–G).

TXNDC17 Cooperates with GPX4 to suppress ferroptosis

Cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis is determined by several factors, including GSH biosynthesis, iron metabolism and polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) metabolism. To elucidate the mechanism by which TXNDC17 confers ferroptosis resistance in gliomas, we analyzed the expression of key ferroptosis-related genes in sh-TXNDC17 glioma cell lines. Among these, GPX4, a central regulator of ferroptosis involved in the cysteine–glutamate pathway, exhibited significantly reduced expression in both sh-TXNDC17 cell lines. In contrast, no significant alterations were observed in other ferroptosis regulators, including ACSL4, SLC7A11, HO-1 and FSP1 (Fig. 6A–H).

To further investigate the potential interaction between TXNDC17 and GPX4, we performed colocalization analysis. Immunofluorescence revealed distinct colocalization of TXNDC17 (red) and GPX4 (green) within the cytoplasm of glioma cells (Fig. 6I). Molecular docking of the TXNDC17–GPX4 complex using the GRAMM platform predicted a stable interaction, with a binding free energy of − 6.8 kcal/mol. The docking model identified key hydrogen bonds between TXNDC17 (Cys71, Cys74) and GPX4 (Cys93, Arg152), supporting a direct interaction (Fig. 6J). In the docking model, TXNDC17 is shown in purple and GPX4 in yellow. CHX chase assay revealed that TXNDC17 knockdown significantly accelerated GPX4 protein degradation (Fig. 6K–L), indicating that TXNDC17 stabilizes GPX4 protein at the post-translational level.

Overexpression of GPX4 rescues the inhibitory effects of TXNDC17 knockdown on cell proliferation and invasion

To determine whether GPX4 mediates the oncogenic effects of TXNDC17 in gliomas, we generated U251 and U87 cells with combined TXNDC17 knockdown and GPX4 overexpression (sh-TXNDC17 + OE-GPX4). We provide Western Blot evidence of GPX4 protein levels in each experimental group to demonstrate that GPX4 protein can be successfully overexpressed and stably present under TXNDC17 deficiency (Fig. 7A). CCK-8 assays demonstrated that GPX4 overexpression effectively reversed the decrease in viability induced by TXNDC17 silencing, restoring viability to significantly higher levels (p < 0.0001; Fig. 7B). Similarly, Ki-67 immunofluorescence staining revealed a significant increase in the proportion of proliferating cells in the sh-TXNDC17 + OE-GPX4 group compared with the sh-TXNDC17 group (p < 0.05; Fig. 7C).

Overexpression of GPX4 rescues the effects of TXNDC17 on cell proliferation and invasion. (A) Expression of GPX4 in U251 and U87 cells. (B) Viability of U251 and U87 cells. (C) Representative immunofluorescence images of Ki-67 staining. (D) Effect of GPX4 overexpression on invasion capability in sh-TXNDC17 cells

We next examined the impact of GPX4 overexpression on the migratory and invasive capacities of TXNDC17-depleted glioma cells using wound healing and Transwell invasion assays. The results showed that GPX4 overexpression significantly restored both migration rate and invasive capacity compromised by TXNDC17 knockdown (p < 0.01; Fig. 7D). These findings indicate that GPX4 overexpression counteracts the inhibitory effects of TXNDC17 depletion on glioma proliferation and invasion, establishing that TXNDC17 promotes glioma progression primarily by modulating the GPX4-mediated ferroptosis pathway.

TXNDC17 Knockdown synergizes with the GPX4 inhibitor RSL3 to suppress glioma growth in vivo

To further investigate whether TXNDC17 mediates the regulation of ferroptosis by GPX4 in gliomas, we established a sh-TXNDC17 GL261-luc murine glioma cell line and treated the cells with 1 µM RSL3. CCK-8 assays and immunofluorescence staining showed that the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 significantly rescued the viability of sh-TXNDC17 cells (p < 0.001; Fig. 8A). Transwell invasion assays further demonstrated that TXNDC17 knockdown alone suppressed the migratory and invasive capacities of glioma cells. Notably, treatment with RSL3 (a GPX4 inhibitor) synergistically enhanced this suppressive effect, leading to a further reduction in migration and invasion compared with TXNDC17 knockdown alone (p < 0.001; Fig. 8B).

TXNDC17 knockdown combined with the GPX4 inhibitor RSL3 synergistically inhibits glioma growth in vivo. (A) Viability of GL261 cells. (B) Effect of sh-TXNDC17 and RSL3 on invasion capability in sh-TXNDC17 cells. (C) Schematic diagram of the animal experimental procedure. (D) In vivo bioluminescence imaging of orthotopic gliomas in mice injected with different cell/drug treatments. (E) Quantification of bioluminescence signal intensity. (F) Western blot analysis of relative TXNDC17 and GPX4 protein expression levels

Subsequently, 9-week-old male C57 mice (n = 5 per group) were stereotactically implanted with 1 × 10⁵ GL261-luc cells in the right striatum. The mice were divided into three groups: (1) control group (shCtrl, treated with DMSO solvent), (2) TXNDC17 knockdown group (sh-TXNDC17, treated with DMSO) and (3) combination group (sh-TXNDC17 plus RSL3). For the combination group, mice received intraperitoneal injections of the GPX4-specific inhibitor RSL3 (10 mg/kg) twice weekly, while the other two groups received equivalent volumes of DMSO solvent (Fig. 8C).

Longitudinal bioluminescence monitoring was conducted on days 7, 14 and 21 post-implantation following intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin (150 mg/kg), and quantitative photon flux analysis was performed using the IVIS Spectrum imaging system. The results revealed a significant reduction in tumor fluorescence intensity in the sh-TXNDC17 group compared with the control group. Moreover, the combination group (sh-TXNDC17 + RSL3) exhibited a synergistic suppressive effect (Fig. 8D-E). Glioma tissues were harvested from all three groups (n = 5 per group). Western blot analysis revealed downregulation of both TXNDC17 and GPX4 protein expression in the sh-TXNDC17 and combination groups compared to the control group (Fig. 8F). Notably, GPX4 downregulation was more pronounced in the combination group than in the sh-TXNDC17 alone group.

Discussion

GBM is the most lethal type of primary brain tumor [27–29]. The absence of effective treatments contributes to poor patient prognosis, a challenge further compounded by the unique metabolic characteristics of GBM, including its high antioxidant demand and abundance of polyunsaturated fatty acids. Moreover, the failure of Phase III immunotherapy trials for GBM (e.g., CheckMate-498) has highlighted the distinct features of the GBM tumor microenvironment (TME) and its constraints on therapeutic efficacy [5]. These findings underscore the urgent need to develop novel therapeutic strategies and agents.

TXNDC17 (Thioredoxin Domain Containing 17), a member of the Trx superfamily, plays essential roles in redox regulation, protein folding and cellular stress responses. As a key redox regulator, TXNDC17 utilizes its thioredoxin domain to participate in intracellular redox reactions, modulate multiple signaling pathways and maintain cellular homeostasis. Dysregulated expression or function of TXNDC17 has been implicated in the pathogenesis of several cancers. For example, TXNDC17 overexpression is associated with poor survival in patients with uterine papillary serous carcinoma (UPSC), and co-expression of TXNDC17 and BECN1 correlates with advanced stage and deep myometrial invasion in UPSC [30]. Furthermore, plasma TXNDC17 protein levels have shown diagnostic potential for early colorectal cancer (CRC) [31]. In neuroblastoma, TXNDC17 has been reported to modulate acquired resistance to first-line chemotherapeutic agents [32]. Consistent with its oncogenic roles in other malignancies, our study revealed that TXNDC17 expression is elevated in gliomas in a grade-dependent manner and serves as an independent negative prognostic factor for patient survival. In addition, TXNDC17 knockdown markedly suppressed the proliferation, migration and invasion of U251 and U87 glioma cells. Taken together, these findings suggest that TXNDC17 critically regulates glioma tumorigenesis and progression.

TXNDC17 primarily functions in intracellular redox reactions. Similar to other members of the thioredoxin superfamily, it regulates the cellular redox state, which is vital for maintaining normal cellular physiology. Specifically, through its thioredoxin domain, TXNDC17 reduces oxidized proteins, thereby supporting cellular defenses against oxidative stress. Supporting this function, TXNDC17 (also known as TRP14) has been identified as the rate-limiting enzyme for intracellular cystine reduction, which regulates the cysteine status of the proteome [33]. In addition, TXNDC17 modulates protein function by controlling persulfidation states through oxidation and reduction reactions [34], thereby participating in cellular responses to growth factors. Our study extends these findings to gliomas and ferroptosis. Through GSEA and PPI network analyses, we identified significant associations between TXNDC17 and neural cancer-related processes, as well as interactions with multiple redox-related proteins. Importantly, flow cytometry confirmed that TXNDC17 knockdown altered ROS levels in glioma cells. This link to redox regulation is particularly relevant because ferroptosis is fundamentally driven by redox imbalance, which promotes catastrophic lipid peroxidation. Conversely, the collapse of cellular redox systems, potentially involving key regulators such as TXNDC17, can accelerate ferroptosis. Our data suggest that TXNDC17 represents a crucial nexus connecting redox homeostasis, glioma biology and ferroptosis susceptibility.

Ferroptosis, a distinct form of regulated cell death dependent on iron accumulation and elevated ROS levels, was first described by Dixon in 2012 [35]. GBM cells are highly susceptible to ferroptosis because of their intrinsic hypermetabolic activity, which increases ROS generation, iron accumulation and lipid metabolism [36]. Recent studies suggest that targeting ferroptosis is a promising strategy to overcome resistance to conventional tumor therapies and immune evasion. Indeed, several ferroptosis-inducing therapeutic strategies have demonstrated efficacy against GBM. For instance, combining ferroptosis inducers with nanocarriers facilitates selective delivery to the GBM microenvironment [37]. Additionally, sonodynamic therapy (SDT) has been reported to induce quasi-immunogenic ferroptosis in tumor cells and promote immunostimulatory autophagy in macrophages within GBM [38]. Mounting evidence indicates that targeting ferroptosis-related pathways represents a promising approach for GBM treatment. Ferroptosis is triggered by the accumulation of ROS, which induces lipid peroxidation when ROS levels exceed the antioxidant capacity provided by GSH and GPX4 [39]. This form of cell death is characterized by iron accumulation, increased lipid peroxidation, elevated ROS, and dysregulation of genes governing iron homeostasis and lipid peroxidation metabolism. Glutathione synthesis and metabolism are central to ferroptosis regulation [40]. TXNDC17, which regulates protein S-cysteinylation, may indirectly influence ferroptosis. Because TXNDC17 primarily modulates redox balance and ferroptosis is fundamentally driven by lipid peroxidation and the collapse of antioxidant defenses, TXNDC17 likely serves as a regulator of ferroptosis. In glioma cells, we confirmed the involvement of TXNDC17 in ferroptosis by assessing key markers (e.g., GSH depletion, increased LPO and elevated Fe²⁺ levels) and demonstrated that the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) rescued the phenotype induced by sh-TXNDC17.

The core pathway of ferroptosis involves lipid peroxidation-driven cell death and encompasses three principal modules: iron metabolism, antioxidant defense and lipid oxidation [41]. Peroxidation of specific lipid species constitutes the central driver of ferroptosis and requires two essential enzymes: acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) and lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3). ACSL4 catalyzes the conjugation of long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids to coenzyme A (CoA), whereas LPCAT3 promotes their esterification and incorporation into membrane phospholipids [42]. Under physiological conditions, iron-mediated lipid oxidation is tightly regulated by antioxidant defense systems. GPX4 serves as a pivotal antioxidant enzyme that directly reduces lipid hydroperoxides within the bilayer, thereby preventing lethal accumulation of lipid ROS. Beyond GPX4, three additional mechanisms regulate ferroptosis: the FSP1/CoQ10/NADPH axis, the DHODH pathway and the GCH1/BH4 pathway [43, 44]. To determine the specific pathway through which TXNDC17 regulates ferroptosis, we examined key ferroptosis markers. Our results demonstrated that TXNDC17 specifically targets GPX4. Notably, GPX4 overexpression alleviated the inhibitory effects of TXNDC17 on cell proliferation and invasion.

The glutathione peroxidase (GPX) family comprises eight members, designated GPX1–8 [45]. Among them, GPX4 serves as a central regulator of ferroptosis. GPX4 utilizes GSH as a cofactor to reduce small-molecule peroxides and lipid hydroperoxides, thereby suppressing intracellular lipid peroxidation [46]. Increasing evidence indicates that GPX4 inhibition significantly promotes ferroptosis in glioma cells. Consequently, targeting GPX4 effectively modulates key phenotypic characteristics of these cells [47–50]. (1S,3R)-RSL3 (commonly referred to as RSL3) is a well-established ferroptosis inducer that directly inhibits GPX4 to initiate ferroptosis [51]. RSL3 induces ferroptosis in glioblastoma through activation of the NF-κB pathway and GPX4 depletion [52]. In addition, studies have shown that cetuximab enhances RSL3-induced ferroptosis in KRAS-mutant colorectal cancer by inhibiting the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway [53], while aspirin augments RSL3-induced ferroptosis in PI3K CA-mutant colorectal cancer by suppressing mTOR/SREBP-1/SCD1-mediated lipogenesis [54]. Consistently, our findings demonstrated that TXNDC17 silencing synergistically suppressed glioma growth in vivo when combined with RSL3.

One limitation of this study concerns the source of non-tumor control tissues. These tissues were obtained from the surgical trajectory of patients undergoing evacuation of intracerebral hemorrhage, sampled at least 1 cm away from the hematoma. While this sampling distance follows standard clinical practice and such tissues are generally considered to have preserved normal histology, we cannot completely exclude the possibility of subtle remote iron accumulation or oxidative stress induced by the hemorrhage. Future studies using truly normal brain tissues (e.g., from epilepsy surgery or autopsy) are needed to confirm the differential expression of TXNDC17 observed here. Readers should therefore interpret the expression data with this caveat in mind.

Conclusion

This study aimed to investigate the role of TXNDC17 in ferroptosis and its underlying molecular mechanisms in GBM. We found that TXNDC17 expression was significantly elevated in human GBM tissues compared with normal brain tissues and progressively increased with higher tumor grades. Knockdown of TXNDC17 reduced GPX4 expression and partially inactivated the classic cystine/GSH/GPX4 regulatory axis, thereby inducing iron overload and ferroptosis in GBM cells. Furthermore, GPX4 overexpression alleviated the suppressive effects of TXNDC17 knockdown on cell proliferation and invasion. Importantly, in vivo, TXNDC17 silencing synergized with the GPX4-specific inhibitor RSL3 to suppress glioma growth, demonstrating superior antitumor efficacy compared with monotherapies. Collectively, these findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting the TXNDC17–GPX4 axis, particularly through combinatorial strategies, for GBM treatment.
