Factors associated with intention to use generative artificial intelligence in nursing practice: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jeongin Choe, Kyungmi Woo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenerative artificial intelligence (GAI) can be used to support healthcare professionals. An in-depth understanding of how healthcare professionals adopt and use GAI in practice is lacking. We aimed to identify the intention to use GAI in nursing practice, its application areas, and the key factors associated with this intention.MethodsThis cross-sectional study comprised an online survey with 46 items to assess nurses’ intention to use GAI. Data were collected between March 17 and 28, 2025. The participants were 176 nurses working at a tertiary hospital in Seoul, Korea. Descriptive statistics, Mann–Whitney U tests, Kruskal–Wallis H tests, correlation analyses, multiple regression analyses, and an exploratory subgroup analysis were performed.ResultsOverall, 61.4% of nurses reported having no experience with GAI, whereas 38.6% had used it, primarily in educational material development (50.0%) and data analysis (26.5%). Voluntariness (coefficient = 0.358, p < .001), performance expectancy (coefficient = 0.344, p < .001), and GAI literacy (coefficient = 0.192, p = .010) were key factors associated with usage intention; experience with GAI, effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, and perceived risk were not significantly associated with the intention to use GAI. The subgroup analysis revealed that the influence of key factors was moderated by prior user experience.ConclusionsThis study revealed that voluntariness, performance expectancy, and GAI literacy are key drivers of nurses’ intention to use GAI. Notably, their importance differs according to user experience, highlighting the need for tailored strategies to get nurses to use GAI. In the future, the sustained value of GAI in nursing practice should be assessed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-03985-y.Keywords: Technology, Artificial intelligence, Generative artificial intelligence, Nursing, Nurses, Intention

Background

Recent innovations in the healthcare field have been driven by technological advancements. Among them, artificial intelligence (AI) has substantially contributed to improving the efficiency of healthcare professionals and enhancing patient safety and treatment outcomes in various ways, including supporting diagnosis, developing treatment plans, and predicting health outcomes [1].

Generative AI (GAI), such as ChatGPT, has the potential to provide more flexible and creative solutions than traditional AI technologies. Hence, it is rapidly gaining attention in various areas of healthcare services [2]. Unlike rule-based AI, GAI can generate new information beyond predictable patterns by leveraging existing data and learned knowledge [3]. This technology can be used to support healthcare professionals by summarizing patient data, providing the information necessary for diagnostic and treatment decisions, generating educational materials, and assisting with various other tasks.

However, when errors occur in the use of AI in healthcare services, they can severely harm patients, which could result in legal liability owing to medical malpractice. Additionally, the opaque functioning of AI algorithms may cause healthcare professionals to mistrust the results or misunderstand the decision-making process. Data privacy and security issues can arise when handling sensitive patient information; thus, ethical and legal considerations regarding the adoption and use of GAI technologies are essential [4, 5]. These constraints can negatively affect healthcare professionals’ acceptance of GAI technologies and hinder their effective use in clinical practice. Against this background, the intention of healthcare professionals to use GAI must be understood and the various factors influencing this intention determined to facilitate the adoption and active use of GAI in healthcare institutions.

Previous studies were primarily focused on evaluating the potential applications of GAI in areas such as technical performance, medical information provision, diagnostic support, education, and data analysis [2, 6–8]. However, those studies did not provide an in-depth understanding of how healthcare professionals adopt and utilize GAI in practice.

As frontline healthcare providers, nurses are deeply engaged both in direct patient care and in time-consuming documentation tasks. This role renders them especially positioned to benefit from the adoption of GAI to ease their administrative burden and enhance clinical decision-making. Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine nurses’ experiences with GAI usage and their intentions to use it, as well as to identify the factors associated with their intentions. The goal was to provide foundational data for strategy development for the optimal adoption and utilization of GAI in nursing environments.

To systematically investigate these factors, a robust theoretical framework is necessary. For this study, we drew on the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) and perceived risk theory as frameworks to analyse the factors influencing nurses’ intention to use GAI in their work. The UTAUT is a framework that explains technology acceptance through four key factors: performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions [9]. It can be effectively used to analyse the intention to use GAI. This theory has been applied in various healthcare technology fields, such as mobile health technology [10], digital health information systems [11], and virtual nursing [12], demonstrating that key factors such as performance expectancy and effort expectancy influence technology acceptance and intention to use.

According to the UTAUT, usage behaviour is influenced by both intention to use and facilitating conditions. Intention to use is determined by three key factors: performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and social influence. Performance expectancy refers to the belief that using technology will help improve job performance, whereas effort expectancy reflects the perception of ease and intuitiveness of using technology. Social influence refers to the degree to which peers, managers, and organizations encourage or expect technology use [9]. Facilitating conditions refer to the belief in adequate environmental, organizational, and technical support available to use technology. Venkatesh et al. [9] identified facilitating conditions as a direct influencing variable on usage behaviour, whereas subsequent studies have shown that facilitating conditions also positively impact intention to use [13, 14].

In the UTAUT model, moderating variables such as gender, age, experience, and voluntariness strengthen or weaken the relationship between independent and dependent variables [9]. However, variables such as experience and voluntariness can, in certain contexts, function as direct predictors of behavioural intention or actual use [15–18]. Thus, such variables may not only have moderating effects but also act as psychological drivers that influence users’ behavioural responses [17].

Furthermore, perceived risk theory is useful for analysing the risk factors perceived by users in the technology adoption process and explaining their negative impact on behavioural intentions [19]. Rather than being applied as a standalone theory, it is often combined with technology acceptance theories, such as the technology acceptance model or UTAUT, to comprehensively analyze both positive and negative factors, thereby providing a deeper understanding of technology acceptance and intention to use. Research on telemedicine acceptance [20], AI-supported technology adoption [21], and wearable medical device usage [22] has demonstrated the usefulness of this approach.

Recent studies have also suggested that digital literacy, which reflects users’ technical competence, plays an important role in technology adoption [16, 23, 24]. Digital literacy refers to an individual’s ability to understand and use technology; depending on the purpose and context of use, it may be termed AI literacy or GAI literacy [25].

Therefore, in this study, we set the key elements of the UTAUT—performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions—along with GAI usage experience, voluntariness, perceived risk, and GAI literacy as independent variables and nurses’ intention to use GAI in their work as the dependent variable. Additionally, demographic factors such as age, gender, education level, work experience, and department were set as control variables to control for their effects on the intention to use GAI (Fig. 1).

Methods

Aim(s) and objective

The purpose of this study was to identify nurses’ intention to use GAI and its areas of application in their work, as well as to apply the UTAUT and perceived risk theory to determine the key factors associated with their intention to use GAI.

Study design, setting, and sampling

This study was a cross-sectional survey conducted via an online questionnaire at a tertiary hospital in Seoul, South Korea. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling in March 2025.

Eligibility and recruitment

To capture a broad range of perspectives on the intention to use GAI, eligibility criteria were intentionally open. We included all registered nurses affiliated with the study hospital during the survey period, with no restrictions on employment status, department, or leave status. Recruitment was conducted by posting a notice on the hospital’s intranet bulletin board, wich included the research purpose, procedures, and a link to the online survey. Participation was entirely voluntary, and inclusion in the final sample required the provision of informed consent and completion of the survey.

Sample size and data integrity

Using the G*power 3.1.2 program, the required sample size for multiple regression analysis was calculated based on a significance level (α) of 0.05, a power (1 - β) of 80%, a medium effect size of 0.15, and 14 predictors, resulting in a required sample size of 135. Considering a dropout rate of 20%, the target sample size was set at 169. A total of 177 surveys were collected. To ensure data integrity, potential duplicate entries were screened by cross-checking timestamps and the phone numbers voluntarily provided for compensation. One duplicate entry was identified and removed; thus, data from 176 surveys were used for the analysis.

Measures

The online survey consisted of 46 items used to identify nurses’ intention to use GAI, its areas of application, and factors associated with their intention to use it. The tool used in this study was tailored to nurses with the original author’s approval and reviewed by a nursing professor.

Independent variables

The independent variables were performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, GAI usage experience, voluntariness, perceived risk, and GAI literacy.

Performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, voluntariness, and perceived risk were measured using a modified version of the tool developed by Kim and Lee [26] based on previous studies [9, 27–31].

Performance expectancy refers to the expectation that GAI will help improve job performance. It was measured using four items on a five-point Likert scale, with answers ranging from (1) “strongly disagree” to (5) “strongly agree.” The total score ranged from 4 to 20, with higher scores indicating a more positive performance expectancy toward GAI. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for performance expectancy was 0.938.

Effort expectancy refers to the perception of the ease and intuitiveness of using GAI. It was measured using four items on a five-point Likert scale, with answers ranging from (1) “strongly disagree” to (5) “strongly agree.” The total score ranged from 4 to 20, with higher scores indicating a more positive effort expectancy toward GAI. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for effort expectancy was 0.910.

Social influence refers to the degree to which the surrounding environment encourages or expects the use of GAI. It was measured using four items on a five-point Likert scale, with answers ranging from (1) “strongly disagree” to (5) “strongly agree.” The total score ranged from 4 to 20, with higher scores indicating a greater social influence on GAI use. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for social influence was 0.873.

Facilitating conditions refer to the belief that the necessary support for using GAI is available. It was measured using four items on a five-point Likert scale, with answers ranging from (1) “strongly disagree” to (5) “strongly agree.” The total score ranged from 4 to 20, with higher scores indicating a more positive perception of the facilitating conditions for GAI. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for facilitating conditions was 0.827.

Voluntariness in this study refers to the degree of freedom in using GAI. It was measured by modifying four items from Kim and Lee [26] on a five-point Likert scale, with answers ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” for a total score ranging from 4 to 20. Higher total scores indicate greater voluntariness in using GAI. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for voluntariness was 0.932.

Perceived risk refers to the perception of risks associated with GAI use. It was measured using four items on a five-point Likert scale, with answers ranging from (1) “strongly disagree” to (5) “strongly agree.” The total scores ranged from 4 to 20. Because all items in this scale were reverse-coded, the scores were transformed before interpretation. Therefore, a higher total score indicates a lower perception of risk related to GAI usage. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for perceived risk was 0.657.

Work-related GAI usage experience was categorized as “Yes” or “No.” Additionally, participants who responded affirmatively regarding work-related GAI usage were asked to select all relevant tasks from the following options: “patient record writing,” “diagnostic support,” “educational material development,” “patient counselling/response,” “research data analysis,” and “other” (to be specified).

GAI literacy was measured using a modified version of the Korean Generative AI Literacy Measurement Tool developed by Noh et al. [25], which was based on previous studies [32–34] and adjusted to fit the purpose of this study. The scale consists of 12 items across four domains: AI utilization skills, critical evaluation, ethical use, and creative application. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale, with answers ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” for a total score ranging from 12 to 60. Higher total scores indicate higher GAI literacy. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for GAI literacy was 0.902.

Dependent variables

In this study, the dependent variable, intention to use GAI, was measured by modifying three items from Kim and Lee [26]. It was assessed using a five-point Likert scale, with answers ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” The total score ranged from 3 to 15, with higher scores indicating a higher intention to use GAI. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for intention to use GAI was 0.946.

Control variables

Demographic data (age, gender, education level, work experience, and department) were used as control variables. Gender was categorized as male or female, and education level was divided into associate’s degree, bachelor’s degree, and master’s degree or higher. Age and work experience were entered as numerical values and measured as continuous variables. Work departments were classified as wards, outpatient departments, emergency rooms, intensive care units, and others.

Data collection and data analysis

The data were collected from March 17 to March 28, 2025. After obtaining approval from the hospital’s nursing department, a recruitment notice was posted on the hospital’s intranet bulletin board to recruit participants using convenience sampling. The notice provided information about the research purpose and procedures, along with an internet link and QR code to access the Google online survey. Interested nurses voluntarily accessed the link, completed the consent process, and participated in the self-reported survey.

Data analysis was performed using STATA 18, with a two-tailed significance level of 0.05. Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted on the participants’ demographic characteristics, GAI literacy, voluntariness, performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, perceived risk, and intention to use GAI. Mann–Whitney U tests and Kruskal–Wallis H tests were performed to examine differences in intention to use GAI based on research variables. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to explore the relationships between the research variables and intention to use GAI.

To identify the factors influencing intention to use GAI, multiple regression analysis was conducted. In this analysis, categorial variables such as gender, education level, and department were treated as dummy variables. Diagnostic checks of the regression model revealed that while the quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plot confirmed the approximate normality of the residuals, the residuals versus fitted plot indicated slight heteroscedasticity. As such a deviation from homoscedasticity can occur in analyses involving ordinal data like a Likert scale, heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors were applied to ensure valid statistical inference. Multicollinearity tests for the variables in the multiple regression analysis were performed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the study results. Additionally, to investigate the potential moderating role of experience, an exploratory, post-hoc subgroup analysis was conducted using a simplified multiple regression model.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (H-2502-112-1614). The study protocol conformed to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. An information sheet outlining the research purpose and procedures was provided on the first page of the online survey. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, and participants were informed that they could decline to participate or withdraw at any time without any negative consequences. All participants indicated their agreement to participate by checking a consent box before proceeding to the survey questions. For compensation, participants voluntarily provided their phone numbers, which were not used in the analysis and were discarded immediately after the compensation was delivered.

The correlation analysis revealed no significant correlation of age (p = .622) or work experience (p = .765) with the intention to use GAI. A moderate positive correlation (ρ = 0.558, p < .001) was found between GAI literacy and the intention to use GAI, indicating that higher GAI literacy tends to be associated with a higher level of intention to use GAI. Voluntariness exhibited a strong positive correlation (ρ = 0.748, p < .001) with the intention to use GAI, suggesting that a higher degree of willingness to use GAI voluntarily is associated with a stronger intention to actually use it. Performance expectancy also exhibited a strong positive correlation (ρ = 0.719, p < .001) with the intention to use GAI, indicating that the expectation of improved performance from using GAI is associated with the intention to use it. A moderate positive correlation (ρ = 0.509, p < .001) was observed between effort expectancy and the intention to use GAI, indicating that greater perceived ease of use and intuitiveness of GAI is associated with a greater intention to use it.

Social influence (ρ = 0.348, p < .001) and facilitating conditions (ρ = 0.255, p < .001) exhibited relatively weak positive correlations with the intention to use GAI, indicating that an increased number of people around an individual that encourages the use of GAI and more appropriate facilitating conditions are associated with the individual’s intention to use it. Perceived risk and the intention to use GAI also exhibited a moderate positive correlation (ρ = 0.411, p < .001), meaning that a lower perceived risk is associated with a stronger intention to use GAI.

Discussion

Work-related GAI usage experience and areas of application

Among the nurses participating in this study, 61.4% reported having no experience using GAI at work, while 38.6% reported having used GAI for such purposes. In a study by Aljamaan et al. [35], 18.3% of healthcare professionals used GAI in their work, and Gnambs et al. [36], who surveyed the general population, found that while AI was positively evaluated in the healthcare sector, actual experience with the technology was limited. These findings suggest that the adoption and acceptance of GAI in healthcare and nursing are still in their early stages. While GAI has the potential to become a useful tool in nursing tasks, its practical application is limited by factors such as a lack of related knowledge, issues with explainability, and perceived risks related to professional duties and employment [37].

In terms of GAI utilization, the most common GAI applications were educational material development (50.0%) and research data analysis (26.5%), whereas the utilization rate in patient record writing was relatively low (7.4%). These results are related to the fact that 48.3% of the participants worked in departments other than wards, outpatient departments, emergency rooms, and intensive care units. Specifically, GAI is primarily used in areas such as education and research, where the risk is lower and the tasks are more indirect and supportive than those in clinical nursing tasks. This suggests that the potential for GAI utilization may vary by job role or department, reflecting differences in autonomy related to actually utilizing GAI [36]. A previous study conducted by physicians yielded similar results [38]. In that study, GAI utilization was primarily focused on research and professional development activities. Although GAI is useful for automating research and administrative tasks, its use in areas directly related to patient care remains limited. To overcome these limitations and integrate GAI into clinical tasks, Schmidt et al. [38] suggested the development of systems that can improve patient outcomes and be seamlessly integrated into existing workflows.

Therefore, when introducing GAI in healthcare institutions, education and strategies must be tailored to the characteristics of each department and job role, along with the necessary technical support. This approach can increase GAI utilization and maximize its efficiency in nursing tasks.

Factors associated with the intention to use GAI

The analysis of factors associated with the intention to use GAI in nursing work revealed significant associations with gender, nurses’ work-related GAI usage experience, GAI literacy, voluntariness, performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, and perceived risk. However, when controlling for age, gender, education level, and department, the analysis showed that voluntariness, performance expectancy, and GAI literacy had significant effects on the intention to use GAI, in that order. In contrast, experience, effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, and perceived risk, which are typically important variables in technology acceptance, were not found to have a significant association with the intention to use GAI in this study. Therefore, interpretations that consider the unique characteristics of GAI are necessary.

In the UTAUT model, voluntariness refers to a user’s willingness to use technology voluntarily and has often been treated as a moderator variable between independent and dependent variables [9]. However, consistent with the findings of Park and Woo [15], voluntariness emerged as the factor most strongly associated with the intention to use GAI in this study. This suggests that users who voluntarily choose to use technology are more likely to have a higher intrinsic motivation and intention to use technology. This indicates that innovative technologies, such as GAI, are more effectively utilized when adoption is voluntary.

Performance expectancy was also significantly associated with the intention to use GAI in this study. This finding aligns with previous research highlighting the impact of technology utility on usage intention [39–41], indicating that the expectation of practical improvements in work performance is a major motivating factor for using GAI. Gnambs et al. [36] argue that many individuals in the healthcare sector may lack a clear understanding of the specific use cases of AI technology, which could act as a barrier to GAI utilization. Therefore, providing education that demonstrates effective GAI applications in real-world tasks plays a crucial role in increasing usage intention. As users experience the potential value of GAI, they are more likely to engage with it.

Interestingly, a relatively high correlation was observed between performance expectancy and voluntariness of use (ρ = 0.622). As the VIF values did not indicate a statistical multicollinearity problem, this correlation may suggest that, in the context of nursing practice, the two constructs are closely intertwined. That is, nurses’ voluntariness to use GAI may only be strongly manifested when they have a high performance expectancy that GAI will improve their job performance. This implies that in a busy clinical environment, where efficiency and accuracy are paramount, the perception of a technology’s usefulness is a key prerequisite to drive its voluntary adoption. This finding highlights the need for a deeper understanding of how the key variables of the UTAUT model interact within the context of specific professional domains.

Additionally, GAI literacy was significantly associated with the intention to use GAI in this study, leading to a conclusion consistent with those of previous studies [16, 23, 24]. According to Schiavo et al. [16], AI literacy plays a crucial role in helping users form positive perceptions of the utility and ease of use of AI technology, thereby facilitating its adoption. Therefore, GAI literacy is an important variable in promoting the adoption of GAI technology, and strategies to improve literacy are necessary for the effective implementation of GAI in healthcare institutions. These strategies should not only be focused on teaching nurses how to use technology but also clearly communicate the potential value of GAI and its ability to enhance work performance.

In this study, effort expectancy and social influence were not significantly associated with the intention to use GAI. Effort expectancy is generally considered a variable related to the ease of technology use; however, previous researchers have also reported that it does not have a direct association with the intention to use AI [39–41]. This finding supports the notion that effort expectancy is a relatively less important factor in the intention to use GAI. The fact that social influence was not significantly associated with the intention to use GAI suggests that the usefulness of the technology itself or the tangible benefits experienced by users might have played a more crucial role than social pressure. Romero-Rodriguez et al. [40] also reported that in the context of college students’ intention to use ChatGPT, performance expectancy played a more important role than social influence. This emphasizes that technologies such as GAI are more motivated by personal needs and expectations than by social recommendations.

Facilitating conditions are environmental factors that support technology usage; according to the UTAUT model, they can directly predict user behaviour but not behavioural intention. However, several studies have demonstrated that facilitating conditions can also impact behavioural intentions [41, 42]. In this study, facilitating conditions were not significant associated with the intention to use GAI, which is in line with the results of Romero-Rodriguez et al. [40]. This aligns with the fact that GAI is designed as a system that can be accessed anywhere with just an internet connection without the need for complex installation or infrastructure. Cloud-based AI systems minimize physical and technical constraints and maximize user accessibility.

Additionally, perceived risk was not significantly associated with the intention to use GAI in this study. Certain researchers have also shown that perceived risk is not a significant predictor of the intention to adopt technology [21, 22]. In a study by Zhai et al. [21], specialists expressed concerns about inaccurate information and privacy issues but still showed a willingness to use the technology because of its potential to reduce workload. Therefore, for innovative technologies, such as GAI, to be successfully adopted, their practical utility must be emphasized alongside risk management. This also supports the idea that factors such as performance expectancy act as stronger motivators than perceived risk does.

Moderating role of work-related GAI usage experience

In this study, the intention to use GAI significantly differed based on work-related GAI usage experience. This result is consistent with those of previous studies suggesting that actual technology usage experience influences intention to use the technology [16, 18]. However, the multiple regression analysis showed that work-related GAI usage experience did not have a significant effect on intention to use GAI. This suggests that, similar to the UTAUT model proposed by Venkatesh et al. [9], experience may function as a moderator variable, indirectly influencing the intention to use GAI.

Our exploratory subgroup analysis provided a notable finding: the determinants of GAI usage intention appear to evolve with user experience. For nurses who had not yet used GAI, the intention to adopt was influenced by a combination of personal willingness (voluntariness), perceived utility (performance expectancy), and knowledge (GAI literacy). However, for nurses who had already used GAI, their intention for continued use was predominantly driven by voluntariness. This may imply that once users have direct experience with GAI, its utility is either taken for granted or its value is already internalized, making the decision to continue using it a matter of ingrained personal motivation. This finding suggests that strategies to promote GAI use in nursing should be tailored: for novices, the focus should be on education and demonstration of the value of GAI, whereas for experienced users, the focus should shift to fostering an environment that supports and reinforces autonomous use.

Strengths and limitations of the study

In this study, we empirically investigated the intention to use GAI in nursing work and the key associated factors, drawing on the UTAUT and perceived risk theory. Additionally, by considering the characteristics of nursing work, we identified the key variables associated with the adoption and acceptance of GAI technology, which may provide essential foundational data to inform strategies for the adoption and dissemination of GAI.

However, this study has some limitations. First, it was a cross-sectional study conducted among nurses working in a specific hospital. As the focus was explicitly on GAI, the sample might have been subject to selection bias, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to other hospitals or settings. Second, we used a self-reported survey to collect data, which could have been influenced by respondents’ subjective evaluations. Third, this study treated ordinal data from a Likert sclae as continuous for multiple linear regression. while this is a common approach, it is a limitation as it may not fully satisfy the underlying statistical assumptions. Fourth, we did not sufficiently consider variables related to the actual use of GAI, such as differences in job roles, departmental support, and trust in technology. Additionally, the potential for GAI usage and the need for it may differ substantially depending on the characteristics of the nurse’s job role; therefore, these aspects need to be analysed more deeply. Fifth, a relatively high correlation was observed between voluntariness of use’and performance expectancy (ρ = 0.622). Although the VIF values did not indicate a statistical multicollinearity problem, this correlation may suggest a conceptual overlap between the two constructs. Therefore, the model’s high explanatory power (adjusted R2 = 0.697) should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect this redundancy. Although these two variables are theoretically distinct within the UTAUT framework and were significant predictors in our model, future research should be aimed at refining the measurement tools to better distinguish between these two concepts. Finally, the findings from the exploratory subgroup analysis should be interpreted with caution. It was a post-hoc analysis in which we used a simplified model that included only the three main predictors from the primary analysis, rather than the full set of control variables. Consequently, these preliminary results should be considered exploratory and require validation in future studies designed specifically to test for differences between user groups.

Conclusion

In this study, we examined nurses’ intention to use GAI and its current areas of application, revealing that intention is primarily driven by voluntariness, performance expectancy, and GAI literacy, whereas effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, and perceived risk were not significant factors. With use concentrated in education and research, exploratory findings suggest that prior work-related GAI experience moderates these determinants. Adoption should therefore pair department-tailored literacy programs with workflow-integrated, autonomy-preserving deployments that demonstrate clear performance gains. Future multi-site, longitudinal studies should be conducted to refine the measurement tools to distinguish voluntariness from performance expectancy and track real-world uptake and clinical/operational outcomes, thereby establishing the sustained value of GAI in nursing practice.
