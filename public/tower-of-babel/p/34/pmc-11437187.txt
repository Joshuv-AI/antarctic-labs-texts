Application value of magnetic resonance spectroscopy imaging in the diagnosis of prostate cancer
Authors: Tianhe Zhang, Zhiyong Zhou, Wuxue Li, Changbao Xu, Shuailin Zhao, Haiyang Wei, Zhiheng Huang, Xinghua Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRSI) can distinguish between benign and malignant prostate diseases. This study investigated the potential of MRSI for diagnosing prostate cancer and guiding prostate biopsy. We retrospectively reviewed 234 patients with suspected prostate cancer who underwent MRSI with targeted prostate biopsy. Patients were divided into two groups according to their puncture pathology: prostate cancer (n = 103, 44.02%) and benign prostatic disease (n = 131, 55.98%). The t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, or chi-square test was used to compare the groups. The diagnostic abilities of MRSI, prostate-specific antigen level, digital rectal examination, and magnetic resonance imaging without contrast for prostate cancer were compared using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC); the ARC-ROC values were 0.831, 0.768, 0.692, and 0.656, respectively. The AUC-ROC value for diagnosing prostate cancer using the CC/c ratio was 0.853. CC/c ratio > 0.97 was identified as the optimal threshold for diagnosing prostate cancer (sensitivity, 86.5%; specificity, 78.6%; Youden index, 0.651). Spearman correlation analysis revealed a correlation between the CC/c ratio and Gleason score (r = 0.737, p < 0.001). Using the CC/c ratio of MRSI as an adjunct to targeted prostate biopsy can improve the detection rate of positive biopsies and evaluate prostate cancer invasiveness.

Introduction

The occurrence of prostate cancer is multifactorial and involves genetics, age, diet, and obesity, among other complex factors1. This disease is particularly prevalent among middle-aged and elderly men, with global statistics indicating an annual occurrence of approximately 1.4 million and 400,000 deaths attributed to prostate cancer. In China, prostate cancer ranks sixth in incidence among male malignancies and seventh in terms of mortality2,3. Early diagnosis of prostate cancer can lead to improved survival for patients. Currently, prostate cancer screening relies primarily on digital rectal examination (DRE), serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). However, the specificity of serum PSA levels for diagnosing prostate cancer is limited owing to various interfering factors, such as benign prostatic hyperplasia, urinary retention, and urinary tract infections. In addition, DRE is considerably affected by examiner subjectivity and clinical experience. Notably, MRI without contrast agent may be influenced by several factors, such as chronic inflammation, prostate bleeding, and tumor size4.

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy imaging (MRSI) is a commonly used functional MRI technique for detecting prostate cancer5. MRSI improves the accuracy of prostate cancer diagnosis by detecting major metabolites in regions of interest (ROIs) located in the T2-weighted imaging (WI) sequence. It can identify high levels of citrate and low levels of choline compounds in benign prostate tissue, whereas in malignant prostate tissue, it can detect high levels of choline compounds and depleted citrate6,7. However, the close resonance peaks of choline and creatine make it difficult to measure choline values independently. Creatine levels in benign tissues are minimally different from those in malignant tissues7. Therefore, MRSI commonly uses the choline plus creatine-to-citrate (CC/c) ratio to differentiate between benign and malignant prostate tissues7,8.

According to the relevant literature, MRSI has a certain value for diagnosing prostate cancer; however, the literature reports varying values for sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing prostate cancer, ranging from 52.8 to 100% sensitivity and 68–92% specificity9–12. Other studies have shown that MRSI can be used to evaluate the invasiveness of prostate cancer13,14.

Therefore, this study retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of 234 inpatients suspected of having prostate cancer in our department from January 2020 to October 2023, exploring the application value of MRSI for diagnosing early-stage prostate cancer and assisting in targeted prostate biopsy.

Results

Comparison of the efficacy of MRSI with that of PSA, DRE, and MRI without contrast agent in diagnosing prostate cancer

Among the 234 patients included in this study, 103 cases of prostate cancer were diagnosed pathologically (44.02%). The general information of the included patients is shown in (Table 1). This study analyzed the AUC values for MRSI, PSA, DRE, and MRI without contrast for diagnosing prostate cancer. The AUC-ROC values for diagnosing prostate cancer using MRSI, PSA level, abnormal/normal DRE, and abnormal peripheral zone signals on MRI without contrast agent were 0.831, 0.768, 0.692, and 0.656, respectively (Table 2; Fig. 1). We compared the AUC values of the four methods using Delong’s test (Table 3). The AUC of the MRSI was significantly greater than that of MRI and DRE, and the difference was significant (p < 0.05). The AUC of PSA was slightly greater than that of MRI, and the difference was significant (p = 0.0110). There was no significant difference in the other comparisons.

Efficacy of the MRSI CC/c ratio in diagnosing prostate cancer

The 481 targeted punctures were divided into two groups according to the pathology results as follows: positive (n = 215; Gleason score [GS] ≥ 7) and negative (n = 266; GS < 7 or benign pathology). There were significant differences in the CC/c ratio between the two groups (Table 4). The AUC-ROC value for diagnosing prostate cancer using the CC/c ratio was 0.853; when the MRSI CC/c ratio was > 0.97, the sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing prostate cancer were optimal, with a sensitivity of 86.5% (95% CI 81.2–90.8%), a specificity of 78.6% (95% CI 73.1–83.3%), and a Youden index of 0.651 (Table 5; Fig. 2). In addition, Spearman correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between the CC/c ratio and GS (≥ 7) (r = 0.737**, P < 0.001).

Discussion

With the widespread use of serum PSA, DRE, multiparametric MRI, and targeted prostate puncture for screening and diagnosing prostate cancer, the overall detection rate of prostate cancer has increased. However, prostate cancers with low malignant potential have also been detected, resulting in a waste of medical resources15. Prostate cancer can be divided into clinically significant prostate cancer, which requires active treatment, and clinically nonsignificant prostate cancer, which only requires active monitoring16, based on the GS. Therefore, the accurate diagnosis of clinically significant prostate cancer is as important as that of overall prostate cancer. This study compared the diagnostic performance of four methods for detecting clinically significant prostate cancer. The AUC results revealed that MRSI (0.831) had the greatest diagnostic ability, followed by PSA (0.768), DRE (0.692), and MRI (0.656).

The low diagnostic ability of MRI could be attributed to several factors. First, the detection ability of biparametric magnetic resonance imaging (bp-MRI), which is composed of T2WI and DWI sequences, is positively correlated with the size of prostate cancer lesions. Using BP-MRI alone may lead to missed diagnoses of small lesions (< 1 cm)4. The patients in this study were mostly examined at the early stage of the disease. Second, lesions with PI-RADS = 3 and inconsistent evaluations can easily be misjudged due to the influence of image quality and the experience of radiologists17. Third, this study was a retrospective analysis to identify abnormal prostate pathology according to archived MRI results. As such, important details may not have been reported or available.

MRI provides excellent soft-tissue resolution, allowing clear visualization of prostate tissue contours, lesion relationships with surrounding tissues, and the presence of distant metastases18–20. This is particularly advantageous for early-stage prostate cancer diagnosis. However, atrophic changes in the prostate gland, benign hyperplasia, prostatitis, and scars caused by biopsy can also appear as low signals on T2W images21. Therefore, it is vital to identify the above situations with other sequences. The MRSI is an auxiliary sequence of MRI that indirectly measures the relative concentrations of specific biochemical compounds in tissues within single or multiple voxels by detecting differences in the chemical shifts of metabolites. In prostate MRSI, resonances of prostate metabolic fingerprints, such as those of choline, creatine, polyamines, and citrate, appear at different frequencies (approximately 3.2, 3.0, 3.1, and 2.6 ppm, respectively) or positions in the spectrum20,22. Therefore, MRSI can be used to evaluate the metabolism of normal and abnormal prostate tissues23,24. These metabolic changes in tissue may occur before morphological changes, theoretically assisting in the detection of malignant tumors that conventional histological sections may miss25. Zabihzadeh et al.26 reported that the CC/c ratio of prostate cancer patients was significantly greater than that of prostatitis patients (P < 0.01), and the sensitivity and specificity of the CC/c ratio in distinguishing between the two were 94.4 and 80%, respectively.

Analysis of the 481 targeted punctures in this study revealed that the optimal CC/ratio threshold was > 0.97, with a sensitivity, specificity, and Youden index of 86.5, 78.6%, and 0.651, respectively. This value can serve as an effective guide for prostate puncture. We also explored the relationship between the GS (GS ≥ 7) and the CC/c ratio in prostate cancer patients and found a significant positive correlation between them (r = 0.737, p < 0.001). This finding is similar to other reported results14,26,27. This correlation is possibly related to the increased aggressiveness of prostate cancer, with an increase in choline and a decrease in citrate. This still needs to be demonstrated by numerous future studies. Perhaps with the increased use of metabolic parameters in imaging, MRSI can prove more useful for targeted puncture and diagnosis in prostate cancer patients28.

This study has several limitations. First, 103 patients with prostate cancer were diagnosed through perineal prostate puncture but not by postradical prostatectomy pathology, which may have led to sampling errors. Second, this was a single-center retrospective cohort study; thus, larger-sample prospective studies with higher levels of evidence are needed to further assess the value of MRSI in the diagnosis and treatment of prostate cancer. Third, for the MRI evaluation of prostate cancer, only T2W1 and DWI were used without combined MRI enhancement. Future research could also combine more MRI sequences.

Conclusions

MRSI has high clinical value in patients with suspected prostate cancer who require puncture, especially for those with small lesions (< 1 cm)4. MRSI can be used as an adjunct for examination before prostate puncture. This approach can significantly improve the detection rate of prostate cancer and help assess its aggressiveness29.

Methods

Clinical data

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pelvic 3.0T MRI + MRSI examination before prostate biopsy; (2) transperineal MRSI combined with targeted prostate biopsy; and (3) availability of general medical history, DRE results, PSA results, and prostate biopsy pathology data. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) unavailability of MRI, PSA, or DRE findings; (2) history of prostate surgery or treatment before puncture; and (3) poor image quality. Data from 234 patients who met the inclusion criteria were collected from January 2020 to October 2023 at the Department of Urology, Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. A flowchart of the inclusion and exclusion of patients is shown in (Fig. 3). This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (No. 2023073) and was performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. All patients were informed of the study and provided written informed consent.

Physical examination and laboratory tests

In this study, when multiple measurements of PSA levels were taken for a patient, the median value was used. DRE was performed with an empty bladder as follows: patients were instructed to take the elbow knee position, whereas older adults and infirm patients took the lateral position. The examiner, wearing medical gloves, applied paraffin oil to the index finger and instructed the patient to relax. The process involved gently pressing the patient’s anus first, waiting for the anal reflex to dissipate and adapt, and then slowly and gently extending to the rectum. By moving the index finger along the anterior wall of the rectum, the upper margin of the prostate, the outer margin of the bilateral lobe, and the middle groove between the two lobes could be detected. If the prostate was enlarged asymmetrically, hard as a stone, or had localized nodules, a diagnosis of prostate cancer was considered. Upon DRE completion, the patient was provided with toilet paper, and any residual perianal lubricant was wiped away. Two senior attending physicians performed the DRE.

MRI imaging examinations

A 3.0T MRI scanner (Siemens VERIO, Germany) was used. Before scanning, the bladder was filled moderately, and the patient was placed in the supine position. The scanning center was 2.0 cm above the pubic symphysis. The prostate underwent axial, sagittal and coronal fast spin‒echo TSE T2WI, axial T1WI, axial SS-EPI DWI and axial RS-EPIDWI. The axial TSE T2WI parameters were as follows: TR: 6500 ms, TE: 104 ms, slice thickness: 3 mm, slice spacing: 0 mm, field of view (FOV): 180 mm × 180 mm, average: 2, and matrix: 384 × 307. The parameters of SS-EPIDWI were TR:4500 ms, TE: 85 ms, FOV: 260 x 185 mm, matrix: 90 × 90, layer thickness: 3 mm, layer spacing: 0 mm, and echo gap: 0.75 ms. The parameters of RS-EPI DWI were TR: 4800 ms, TE: 59 ms, layer thickness: 3 mm, layer spacing: 0 mm, FOV: 260 mm x 185 mm, matrix: 176 × 176, segmented readout times: 13, and echo gap: 0.30 ms. The generalized automatic calibration partial parallel acquisition (GRAPPA) was 2, and the B values (diffusion sensitivity coefficient) were 0 s/mm² and 800 s/mm². According to the Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) v2 published by the European society of urogenital radiology (ESUR) in 201530, the images were scored, and those with PI-RADS ≥ 3 were defined as the abnormal MRI group. The MRI reports were issued by two imaging specialists.

MRSI examination

All patients were fed a low-residue diet and were asked to refrain from strenuous exercise for 1 h before the examination to ensure moderate bladder filling. A 3.0T MRI scanner (Siemens VERIO, Germany) equipped with a phased-array flexible body coil was used. The positioning of the MRSI was based on conventional MRI, and the T2-WI sequence was used to localize the ROIs, avoiding rectal gas, surrounding fat, and the venous plexus. The scanning parameters were as follows: echo time: 120 ms, repetition time: 690 ms, FOV: 22 × 12 cm, and acquisition time: 10 min. After scanning, data processing was performed to obtain the peak spectra of choline, creatine, and citrate and to automatically calculate the CC/c ratio. The MRSI was deemed abnormal if the CC/c ratio was considerably elevated.

Transperineal prostate biopsy guided by MRSI

The patient was placed in a lithotomy position, and the surgical area was disinfected routinely and draped. A Foley catheter was then inserted and clamped. Anesthesia was performed in the perineal area via a combination of 5 ml ropivacaine and 10 ml lidocaine, and nerve block anesthesia was performed around the prostate under ultrasound guidance. A transrectal ultrasonographic probe was subsequently inserted. Under transrectal ultrasound guidance, after each patient underwent 2–3 targeted biopsies of the prostate under cognitive fusion imaging with MRSI, a standard 12-core prostate biopsy was performed, labeled, and fixed in formalin. After the procedure, the wound was disinfected, covered with gauze, compressed for 5 min, and bandaged. Biopsy tissues were labeled with the biopsy site and the CC/c ratio of the biopsy site. The fixed tissues were sent for pathological examination. The biopsy samples were diagnosed by one pathologist and graded according to the World Health Organization/International Society of Urological Pathology standards31. A pathologist validated the grading system.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed via SPSS 25.0 (Statistical Product and Service Solutions, IBM Information Management, Chicago, United States) and MedCalc 22.0 (MedCalc Software, Belgium). Normality tests were conducted for the measurement data, with normally distributed data expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and nonnormally distributed data expressed as the median (interquartile range). The patients were divided into two groups—prostate cancer and benign prostatic disease—based on the puncture pathology; the t test, Mann–Whitney U test, or chi-square test was used to compare the groups. Using pathological results as the gold standard, ROC curves were plotted to calculate the AUCs of PSA, DRE, MRI, and MRSI. Delong’s test was used to compare the AUC values of these four modalities. ROC analysis was used to determine the best cutoff value for the CC/c ratio of the puncture point and to calculate sensitivity and specificity. Spearman’s correlation was used to evaluate the relationship between CC/c ratio and GS. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.
