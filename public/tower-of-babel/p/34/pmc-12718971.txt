Winners of the 2023 honor awards for excellence at the annual meeting of the Chinese Medical Association-Taipei: Part VI
Authors: Peng-Hui Wang, Wen-Hsun Chang
Published: 2024

In this part VI, we are pleased to introduce Dr. Chou who is recognized the 2023 Excellent Researcher to win the Awards, supported by both Chinese Medical Association-Taipei and Melissa Lee Cancer Foundation.1 This article is one of the top nine excellent articles chosen from all original articles published in 2023 issues of the Journal of Chinese Medical Association (JCMA).1–9 Based on the fact observing that cancer has become the top one leading cause of mortality in Taiwan, the further improvement in cancer care is urgently needed.10,11 To achieve this goal, many strategies have been conducted, including cost-effective prevention programs (healthy lifestyle and risk identification and management as well as high-quality screening test to decrease the incidence of cancer), accurate diagnostic methods (early diagnosis) and adequate, optimal and effective treatment.10,11 However, it is not easy, partly because every strategy as shown above still includes many steps to be completed, and partly because some of them are under the development.12 The current excellent research work by Dr. Chou’s group may be a typical example, which focused on the predicting factors for adequate, optimal, and effective treatment.

Because of the challenge to determine the response in patients with locally advanced rectal cancers (LARC) after neoadjuvant concurrent chemoradiation therapy (NA-CCRT), Dr. Chou and his colleagues attempted to use the selected radiomic features extracted from pretreatment magnetic resonance images (MRI) to train and validate regression models to predict the therapeutic effect more efficiently.1 In the past years, although neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) has become popular in certain stages or certain types of cancers, there is no doubt that it is a watch-and-wait strategy, which raises the critique that this approach may delay the definite therapy for these patients and subsequently contribute to the worse oncologic outcome. Additionally, the image systems obtained from MRI features can be analyzed as T1-weighted, T2-wighted and T3-weighted images, and T3 images were the most common primary tumor classification, but the further advanced analysis from T3 images is less frequent. On contrary, T2 weighted MRI images may be more useful and effective in the predicting the therapeutic outcome, particular for those needing evaluation of response after NAT.1

Various kinds of T2 weighted MRI image-derived features, including five types of neighboring gray-tone difference matrix (NGTDM), three types of gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), one type of gray level dependence matrix (GLDM), and two types of gray level size zone matrix (GLSZM) can combine with six common clinical features (median age, sex, clinical T stage, clinical N stage, type of surgery, and tumor regression grading [TRG], ranging from TRG 0 [absence of residual tumor cells, {ypT0}], to TRG 3 [minimal evidence of tumor response]) to form the regression models in conducting the cross-validation training for hyperparameter search for the prediction of pathologic response after NA-CCRT. The results showed only application of regression models from 11 MRI features as shown above can successfully and effectively predict the complete clinical response of NA-CCRT in patients with LARC, but extra-adding six common clinical features did not remarkably improve model performance. Based on impressive findings of the authors, the followings are introduced to explore their value.

First, the background of NAT should be discussed. NAT, especially for neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) in cancer or NA-CCRT in the current LARC is an interesting approach for locally advanced cancer disease and has been applied in different types of cancer with aiming not only to decrease the tumor burden as well as shrink large local lesions, to make surgery or radiotherapy (RT) less extensively, which may be beneficial for cosmetic purposes and preserve an organ or maintain the function (fewer organ injuries caused by radical surgeries or RT induced fibrosis, better outcomes for quality of life [QoL] and better preservation of organ functions for diseased patients), and in situations such as unresectable disease without access to RT, to enable definite surgical treatment or in presence of resistant tumor cells to kill the majority of cancers and then continue to destroy resistant cells using either surgery or RT making the chance of subsequent dissemination less likely, but also to treat the metastatic disease from its original tumor area and offer an idea or server a predictor for the biology and outcome of the tumor, which makes physicians with much understanding the sensitivity of cancers to CT regimen based on the patient’s response and pathological specimens to better formulate the next treatment strategy, referring the reference to guide the better choice for further treatment.13–15 However, so far, many debated issues existed to argue the benefits of NAT before definite surgery (NAT-S), particularly for those patients with locally advanced cervical cancer (squamous cell type cancer), although the argument may not be reproducible in patients with LARC, because evidence supports the benefits of NA-CCRT and following curable surgery, including the successfully maintain the continence of anal function and less traumatic injuries to patients with LARC treated with NAT plus the following definite surgery.1,15

Second, the use of image to predict the therapeutic outcome of NAT may face the struggling because pseudo-progressive disease (pseudo-PR) may confuse physicians’ decision about NAT-related therapeutic response. In fact, accurate disease progression remains difficult, as the clinical evolution of the patients does not always correlate with the PD at a molecular, microscopic or even macroscopic levels. Therefore, the importance of posttreatment evaluation of cancers underscores a critical dimension of research, particularly in evaluating the efficacy of new treatments, such as CCRT in the current study. Pseudo-PR has become one of the most challenging issues when NAT is applied to cancer treatment. Pseudo-PR is often in the first time point after baseline than later in treatment of patients treated with CCRT or immunotherapy (IT)-based therapy. And the possible reason is secondary to accumulation of a large number of immune cells infiltrating the surrounding area of tumors and swelling of the tumor size due to cellular damage. However, this pseudo-PD is not true PD, but it frequently results in misjudgment. Because Dr. Chou’s image features were measured the tumor before NACT, this concern can be omitted.

Although Dr. Chou’s study is relatively promising, it is still uncertain why the use of their model can successfully predict the therapeutic response of NA-CCRT. In theory, their approach used the conventional image features obtained from MRI, although the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) was used. However, in the literature review, the predictive value of this approach is still yielding inconclusive and conflicting results.1 These image systems are popular and well accepted to detect the size and location of the tumors, but it is doubtful that they can reflect the reality of biological behaviors of the tumors, particularly for those cells classifying a relatively homogeneous morphology (similar cellular type) but presenting a relatively heterogeneous cellular behaviors. Therefore, it is hard to believe that the response to therapy can be predicted by these features obtained from the relatively “cold” image systems, even though many modifications, such as numerous quantitative features describing the intensity, texture, and geometrical characteristics within a given region of interest can be easily extracted from original digital medical images.1 Some data support that these features can be considered to represent the phenotypic characteristics of tumor for each individual, particularly the establishment of various kinds of multivariate predictive models by constructing these features and combining with other clinical features1; however, the genotypic characteristics of tumor may be heavily varied in the tumor population, such as CT- or RT-resistance. Based on absence of functional tests, it is hard to convince us about the value of using this conventional or modified MRI examination to predict the future response of NA-CCRT.

The aforementioned challenge can be easily explained by distinguishing benign uterine mass (uterine fibroids or myoma) from malignant uterine mass (leiomyosarcoma). Studies showed the potential diagnostic utility of diffusion-weighted image (DWI) and DWI-derived ADC sequences in differentiating between benign and malignant disease.16 Recently, a meta-analysis suggested ADC has a role to play in the diagnostic evaluation of leiomyosarcoma vs leiomyoma, with moderate to high sensitivity and specificity.16 Additionally, evidence supports the use of T2-weighted, diffusion-weighted (b value of 1000 s/mm2), and ADC mapping offers accuracy of 88% to 95% for detecting uterine leiomyosarcoma with sensitivity of 83% to 100% and specificity of 88% to 100%; the variability is attributable in part to nonuniform imaging technique and interpretation criteria.17 Although the meta-analysis and consensus statement highlighted the value of digit-modified MRI features in the differential diagnosis of benign or malignant uterine masses, reliance on ADC remains controversial.16 In fact, if the diagnosis should be made based on the cutoff value, the problem is followed. For example, ADC values of confirmed malignancy (leiomyosarcoma) and benignity (leiomyoma) overlap, and studies have used ADC in different ways, such as a sole determination of suspicion or as part of multiparametric assessment.16

Finally, we highlight the value of noninvasive tools in help us to manage patients with varied kinds of diseases, including cancer patients, because any treatment for cancer patients would face the potential troublesome problems, including in-efficient therapy delaying the immediate, prompt, adequate, and optimal treatment for cancer, which are the critical step to minimize the cancer- or therapy-related morbidity and mortality. That is why we intentionally focus on important findings addressing cancer research, as demonstrated by Dr. Chou’s contribution.
