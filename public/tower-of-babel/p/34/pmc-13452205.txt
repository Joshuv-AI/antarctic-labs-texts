Comparative analysis of gut virome signatures between ducks raised in aquatic and dryland environments
Authors: Fei Wang, Hongbo Zeng, Mingyue Fei, Xuezhen Guo, Jie Cai, Hua Yang, Yingping Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study comprehensively compared the viral community structure, functional potential, and virulence factors in ducks reared under WOW (without water) and WW (with water) conditions. Alpha diversity analysis indicated that the WW group exhibited significantly higher Shannon and Simpson indices compared with the WOW group (P < 0.01), though viral richness did not differ significantly between groups (P > 0.05). Beta diversity analyses (PCoA, and NMDS) revealed distinct separations between the viral communities of the two groups, highlighting the impact of rearing systems on viral composition. LEfSe analysis identified 35 viral families as biomarkers, with Crevaviridae, Drexlerviridae, and Demerecviridae enriched in the WOW group, while Straboviridae, Herelleviridae, and Mimiviridae were predominant in the WW group. Taxonomic composition analysis demonstrated that the WW group had significantly reduced abundances of Uroviricota and Spinunavirus but increased abundances of Nucleocytoviricota and Eurybiavirus (P < 0.05). Functional annotation revealed that viral genes in the WW group were enriched in core viral processes (e.g., virion assembly, DNA replication) and metabolic pathways (e.g., nucleotide metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism). Additionally, the WW group showed altered profiles of CAZymes and virulence factors, with increased antimicrobial resistance and biofilm formation capabilities. These findings demonstrate that water exposure reshapes the duck gut virome by enhancing viral diversity, altering taxonomic structure, and activating functional pathways associated with pathogenicity and nutrient metabolism. This study provides critical insights into the ecological impact of rearing systems on viral communities and offers a foundation for optimizing duck farming practices to mitigate health risks.Keywords: Duck, Gut virome, Rearing condition, Functional annotation, Virulence factors

Introduction

The gut microbiota, encompassing bacteria, archaea, fungi, and viruses, forms a complex ecosystem that is integral to host health, influencing nutrition, metabolism, and immune function (Richard and Sokol, 2019; Li et al., 2025). The gut virome refers to the collective viral community within the intestine, including eukaryotic viruses, bacteriophages, and virus-like particles, which play crucial roles in regulating host health, immune development, and disease resistance (Cao et al., 2022; Chica Cardenas et al., 2025). Viruses, particularly bacteriophages, modulate bacterial populations through lytic-lysogenic cycles, influence horizontal gene transfer, and encode auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) to enhance host-microbe interactions (Huang et al., 2024). In poultry, the gut virome is increasingly understood to modulate bacterial community structure, pathogen colonization resistance, and overall host performance (Aruwa et al., 2021). However, in contrast to the extensively studied gut bacteriome, critical aspects of the poultry gut virome, such as its composition, diversity, functional repertoire, and susceptibility to environmental and management factors, remain largely unexplored. A deeper understanding of these dynamics is essential for developing effective strategies to enhance animal health and promote sustainable production.

Ducks, as globally important economic poultry, play a significant role in meat and egg production worldwide (Chen et al., 2021). China, the largest duck farming country, produces over 4 billion ducks annually, accounting for more than 80% of global output (Cai et al., 2025). Rearing systems constitute a primary environmental determinant shaping the avian gut ecosystem (Zeng et al., 2025). Traditional waterfowl production often involves water-based systems, where ducks have access to water, which may facilitate natural behaviors but also introduce diverse environmental microbes and potential pathogens (Zhang et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2026). In contrast, modern intensive systems increasingly adopt without water practices, aiming to improve feed efficiency, disease control, and environmental management (Mi et al., 2020). While the impacts of such systems on bacterial communities, growth performance, and disease incidence have been documented, their specific and profound influence on the resident viral community has not been systematically investigated. Elucidating how WW and WOW systems sculpt the duck gut virome is a critical knowledge gap with direct implications for animal health and biosecurity.

Against this background, this study employs viral metagenomics to systematically compare the gut virome of ducks raised under WW and WOW farming systems. The findings are expected to enhance our understanding of how environmental factors shape the poultry virome and provide theoretical insights to support healthy duck farming and disease prevention strategies.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences in accordance with the relevant rules and regulations.

Animals and sample collection

Two experimental groups were established consisting of a WOW (Without Water) group reared without access to an open-air swimming pool and receiving water exclusively via drinkers and a WW (With Water) group provided with access to an open-air swimming pool under identical housing conditions. 70-day-old ducks were sourced from multiple independent floor pens, with a minimum of five replicate pens per treatment. All ducks were managed under standard husbandry practices and had ad libitum access to a standard commercial diet throughout the experimental period. Upon completion of the trial, 75 ducks from each treatment were randomly selected for sampling. Ducks were humanely euthanized and cecal contents were aseptically collected. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until subsequent viral metagenomic sequencing analysis.

DNA extraction

Total DNA was extracted from gastrointestinal samples using the E.Z.N.A.® Viral DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.) according to manufacturer’s protocols. High-quality DNA sample was used to construct sequencing library.

Library preparation and high-throughput sequencing

Metagenomic libraries were prepared following TruSeqTM Nano DNA Sample Preparation Kit from Illumina (San Diego, CA), using 1 μg of total DNA. DNA end repair, A-base addition and ligation of the NGS-indexed adaptors were performed according to NGS’s protocol. Libraries were then size selected for DNA target fragments of ∼400 bp on 2% Low Range Ultra Agarose followed by PCR amplified using Phusion DNA polymerase (NEB) for 15 PCR cycles. Metagenomic sequencing was performed by Shanghai Biozeron Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All samples were sequenced in the next generation sequencing platform with pair-end 150 bp (PE150) mode.

Reads quality control and mapping

The raw paired-end reads were trimmed and quality controlled by Trimmomatic (version 0.40). Then clean reads that aligned to the host genome were also removed. This set of high-quality reads was then used for further analysis.

Metagenomic de novo assembly, gene prediction and annotation

Clean sequence reads were generated a set of contigs of each sample using MegaHit. For the assembled genome, the above 2000 bp assembly sequences were selected, and those sequences were performed virus identification using VirFinder, VirSorter2 and IMG/VR (Integrated Microbial Genomes, https://img.jgi.doe.gov/cgi-bin/vr/main.cgi) database. vOTUs (viral operational taxonomic units) were clustered using Mummer software to compare candidate virus sequences, with greater than 95% similarity and 85% coverage of the total sequence length and the longest representative one within each cluster was considered as vOTUs. Using VPF-Class tool for virus species annotation and host prediction. All the genes were predicted by METAProdigal (http://compbio.ornl.gov/prodigal/) and searched against the NCBI protein nonredundant (NR), String, eggNOG, and KEGG databases using BLASTX to identify the proteins that had the highest sequence similarity with the given transcripts to retrieve their function annotations and a typical cut-off E-values less than 1.0 × 10-5 was set. BLAST2GO (http://www.blast2go.com/b2ghome) program was used to get GO annotations of unique assembled transcripts for describing biological processes, molecular functions and cellular components. Metabolic pathway analysis was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, http://www.genome.jp/kegg/).

Virome data analysis

Viral community alpha diversity indices, including richness and the Shannon diversity index, were calculated and visualized through rarefaction analysis using Mothur (version 1.21.1). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied for comparisons between the two rearing system groups, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Beta diversity analysis was performed based on Bray-Curtis distance using the vegan package in R. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) were also conducted to visualize community dissimilarities. Differential viral features were determined by the Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) algorithm, considering features with an LDA score > 2 as significantly discriminant.

Results

Diversity analysis

Alpha diversity of the viral community differed significantly between the WOW and WW groups (Fig. 1A-C). Compared to the WOW group, the Shannon and Simpson indices were significantly higher in the WW group (P < 0.01). However, there was no significant difference in the richness of virus types between the WW group and the WOW group (P > 0.05). Beta diversity analysis revealed a distinct separation of the viral communities between the WOW and WW groups. The separation was observed in principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) with the first two axes explaining 59.84% (PCoA1) and 13.44% (PCoA2) of the variance (Fig. 1D). A similar clustering pattern was shown in non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis (Fig. 1E).

Comparison of viral community diversity between the WOW and WW groups. (A) Richness. (B) Shannon index. (C) Simpson index. (D) PCoA. (E) NMDS. Each point represents an individual sample. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, the same below.

Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis

The LEfSe analysis was used to determine the viral taxonomic groups that exhibited differential abundance between the WOW group and the WW group. With a LDA value greater than 2.0 as the screening criterion, the results are shown in Fig. 2. A total of 35 viral families were identified as biomarkers that significantly distinguished the two groups. Specifically, viral families including Crevaviridae, Drexlerviridae, Demerecviridae, Mesyanzhinovviridae, Chaseviridae, Casjensviridae, and Peduoviridae were enriched in the WOW group. In contrast, viral families such as Straboviridae, Aliceevansviridae, Herelleviridae, Zierdtviridae, Lakviridae, Ackermannviridae, and Mimiviridae, etc., were enriched in the WW group. Notably, the WW group was characterized by a greater number of differentially abundant viral families compared to the WOW group.

(A) The LEfSe analysis between the WOW and WW groups at the family level. (B) The heatmap shows the average relative abundances on a log scale.

Virus composition

The viral communities in both groups were predominantly composed of the phylum Uroviricota and the Nucleocytoviricota. However, the relative abundance of specific taxa within these dominant groups differed between the two rearing systems. Specifically, compared with the WOW group, the WW group showed significantly decreased abundances of Uroviricota, Pisuviricota, and Cressdnaviricota, and significantly increased abundances of Nucleocytoviricota, Peploviricota, Phixviricota, Preplasmiviricota, and Produgelaviricota at the phylum level (P < 0.05, Fig. 3A-I). And the WW group showed significantly decreased abundances of Spinunavirus, Ittyvirus, Cimandefvirus, and Hubeivirus, and significantly increased abundances of Eurybiavirus, Hatfieldvirus, Coventryvirus, Cobrasixvirus, and Peeveelvirus at the genus level (P < 0.05, Fig. 3J-S).

Taxonomic composition and differential abundance of viral communities between the WOW and WW groups. (A-I) Stacked bar charts and boxplots at the phylum level. (J-S) Stacked bar charts and boxplots at the genus level.

Additionally, the WW group exhibited significantly decreased abundances of Caudoviricetes, Pisoniviricetes, Arfiviricetes, and Pokkesviricetes, and significantly increased abundances of Megaviricetes, Herviviricetes, Malgrandaviricetes, and Belvinaviricetes compared with the WOW group at the class level (P < 0.05, Fig. S1A and S1D). At the order level, compared with the WOW group, the WW group had significantly decreased abundances of Picornavirales, Pantevenvirales, Pimascovirales, Cirlivirales, and Chitovirales, and significantly increased abundances of Imitervirales, Grandevirales, Herpesvirales, Algavirales, Petitvirales, and Vinavirales (P < 0.05, Fig. S1B and S1E). At the family level, compared with the WOW group, the WW group showed significantly decreased abundances of Peduoviridae, Salasmaviridae, Chaseviridae, Mesyanzhinovviridae, Casjensviridae, Phycodnaviridae, Iridoviridae, Schizomimiviridae, Poxviridae, and Mesomimiviridae, and significantly increased abundances of Schitoviridae, Straboviridae, Herelleviridae, Lakviridae, Aliceevansviridae, Ackermannviridae, Zierdtviridae, Mimiviridae, Orthoherpesviridae, Allomimiviridae, Ludisviridae, Konodaiviridae, Microviridae, Vilmaviridae, Connertonviridae, Toyamaviridae, Forsetiviridae, Helgolandviridae, Chimalliviridae, Pachyviridae, Orlajensenviridae, and Nixviridae (P < 0.05, Fig. S1C and S1F).

Gene Ontology (GO) database annotation

Based on the Sankey diagram visualization, the interaction networks between the five dominant viral families and the five predominant host groups were characterized by distinct distribution patterns (Fig. 4). Peduoviridae, Salasmaviridae, and Straboviridae exhibited the strongest interaction intensities with Pyrococcus, as indicated by the widest connecting flows. Pyrobaculum and Mycoplasma served as extensively connected hosts, interacting with all five and four viral families, respectively. In contrast, Acidianus displayed specific interactions with Herelleviridae, Salasmaviridae, and Straboviridae, while Candidatus_Puniceispirillum showed a narrow association with Peduoviridae and Salasmaviridae.

Sankey plot showing the predicted hosts of the virus genera with the highest abundances (top 5).

Cooccurrence analysis of enteric viruses

The cooccurrence networks showed that the WOW group had the highest complexity, consisting of 100 nodes and 663 edges, while the WW group had the lowest complexity, consisting of 66 nodes and 132 edges (Fig. 5). In total, compared with the WOW group, the viral community exposed to water exhibited reduced network complexity, characterized by a decreased number of central nodes, fewer edges, and a lower average degree.

Cooccurrence network analysis of the interactions between viral taxa. Nodes represent different virus taxa, and edges represent correlations.

Database annotation

As shown in Fig. 6A-B, the distribution of predicted gene functions, classified into 22 major Clusters of orthologous groups of proteins (COG) categories. In both rearing systems, category L (Replication, recombination and repair) represented the most abundant functional class, comprising 26,165 genes in the WOW group and 20,168 genes in the WW group. Other prevalent categories shared between both groups included J (Translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis), K (Transcription), and O (Posttranslational modification, protein turnover, chaperones). Notably, the number of genes in most functional categories was higher in the WOW group compared to the WW group. The categories R (General function prediction only), Y (Nuclear structure), and Z (Cytoskeleton) contained no annotated genes in either group. The S (Function unknown) category accounted for a significant proportion in both viromes, highlighting a considerable fraction of uncharacterized viral proteins.

As shown in Fig. 6C, Gene Ontology (GO) functional classification of all annotated genes from the duck gut virome. Within the biological process (BP) category, the most abundant subcategories were “reproduction”, followed by “biological regulation” and “cellular process”. In the cellular component (CC) category, genes were most frequently associated with “intracellular”, “virion part”, and “other organism”. For the molecular function (MF) category, the predominant subcategory was “catalytic activity”, substantially higher than the second and third most abundant subcategories, “structural molecule activity” and “binding”. Subsequently, GO enrichment analysis of differentially expressed viral genes between the WW and WOW groups. GO terms with the highest statistical significance and gene count were associated with viral structural and replicative functions, including “virion”, “virion part”, and “viral life cycle”. Other notably enriched terms encompassed processes such as “viral process”, “DNA polymerase activity”, and “nuclease activity”. Lower-level but significant terms included “3′−5′ exonuclease activity” and “viral DNA genome packaging”. This enrichment pattern demonstrates that the differential viral gene expression between the two groups is primarily concentrated in core viral functions related to particle structure, genome replication, packaging, and the complete viral life cycle (Fig. 6D).

The Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) functional classifications of all the annotated genes from the WOW group and the WW group are shown in Fig. 6E. The predominant functional category was “Global and overview maps” (predominantly metabolic pathways), accounting for approximately 40% of the total annotated genes. The second and third most abundant categories were “Replication and repair” and “Carbohydrate metabolism”, which belonging to the “Genetic Information Processing” category. Fig. 6F displays the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the WW and WOW groups. Pathways associated with genetic information processing, including “DNA replication”, “Homologous recombination”, “Mismatch repair”, and “Base excision repair”, were significantly enriched. Similarly, pathways related to nucleotide biosynthesis, such as “Pyrimidine metabolism” and “Nucleotide metabolism”, also showed high enrichment. This indicates that alterations in the gut virome of ducks in different rearing systems are primarily concentrated in fundamental processes governing genome stability, cell cycle regulation, and nucleic acid synthesis.

Analysis of virulence factors of pathogenic bacteria (VFDB)

As shown in Fig. 7, the composition of virulence factor categories was similar between the two groups, with Immune Modulation (WOW: 26.01%, WW: 27.24%) and Nutritional Metabolic Factor (WOW: 19.77%, WW: 20.47%) representing the two most abundant categories. A direct comparison of the mean proportions is shown in Fig. 9C. Notably, compared with the WOW group, the Regulation category was significantly decreased in the WW group, while the Invasion category was significantly increased in the WW group (P < 0.05). Additionally, compared with the WOW group, the WW group significantly increased the Antimicrobial Activity Competitive Advantage, Biofilm, Immune Modulation, Nutritional Metabolic Factor, Motility, and Post Translational Modification categories, along with significantly decreased the Effector Delivery System, Exotoxin, Adherence, and Stress Survival categories (P < 0.05).

Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZy) database annotation

Fig. 8A-B illustrates the taxonomic and functional composition of CAZymes in the duck gut virome across different rearing systems, showing between‑group and within‑individual patterns, respectively. Compared to the WOW group, the WW group exhibited a significant increase in the relative abundance of AA, along with a decrease in the relative abundances of CE and Cellulosome (P < 0.05, Fig. 8C). Moreover, at the family level of AA, the WW group showed higher relative abundances of AA1, AA3, AA6, and AA16, but a lower relative abundance of AA4 compared with the WOW group. For CE families, the relative abundances of CE1, CE4, CE6, CE8, and CE14 were decreased in the WW group, whereas those of CE2, CE3, CE9, CE12, CE19, and CE20 were increased (Fig. 8D).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive characterization of the duck gut virome under two distinct rearing systems, revealing profound differences in viral diversity, taxonomic composition, and functional potential. The findings demonstrate that rearing conditions serve as a critical environmental filter shaping the gut viral ecosystem, with significant implications for host health and production management.

The observed higher alpha diversity in the WW system reflects the complex viral seeding from aquatic environments, where water acts as a continuous reservoir for viral particles (Pepin et al., 2023). This environmental exposure introduces a broader spectrum of viruses, including those infecting eukaryotic microorganisms, which may contribute to the increased diversity (Lebarbenchon et al., 2011). The beta diversity patterns further confirm that water presence selects for distinct viral communities, likely through multiple mechanisms including differential bacterial host availability, direct viral input from water, and environmental factors that modulate virus-host interactions (Ma et al., 2025). While high diversity often indicates ecosystem complexity, in this context it may also signal increased ecological instability and disease transmission risk, as water can facilitate the spread of pathogens across duck populations (Hu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024).

Taxonomic analysis revealed fundamental shifts in viral community structure between the two systems. The reduced relative abundance of Uroviricota (primarily Caudoviricetes) in WW ducks suggests altered phage-mediated regulation of bacterial populations, potentially affecting bacterial community dynamics and function (Liu et al., 2025). Concurrently, the enrichment of Nucleocytoviricota in WW ducks indicates increased viral pressure on eukaryotic microorganisms, likely reflecting the direct input of giant viruses from aquatic environments (Ai et al., 2026). The LEfSe analysis identified 35 differentially enriched viral families, with WW ducks showing specific enrichment of Mimiviridae and Phycodnaviridae. These giant viruses are known to infect amoebae and algae, respectively, and their presence directly links the gut virome to environmental water sources (Maruyama and Ueki, 2016; Ogilvie et al., 2018). In contrast, WOW ducks harbored a more streamlined viral community dominated by specific bacteriophage families, suggesting a more specialized and potentially stable virus-host relationship.

Functional annotation uncovered distinct metabolic and pathogenic profiles between the two systems. WW ducks exhibited enrichment in viral replication and DNA processing pathways, indicating a more dynamic and active viral community (Liu et al., 2021). This heightened activity may be driven by continuous viral input and the complex microbial environment in water-reared ducks (Schober et al., 2025). CAZyme analysis revealed that WW ducks possessed higher abundance of Auxiliary Activities (AA) enzymes, which are involved in lignin degradation and other complex carbohydrate metabolism processes (Onyango et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2024). This suggests that the WW virome may contribute to broader carbohydrate utilization capabilities, potentially enhancing energy harvest from diverse dietary sources(Ma et al., 2024; Yan and Yu, 2024). However, this metabolic versatility comes with a trade-off, as VFDB analysis showed significant enrichment of virulence factors in WW ducks, particularly genes related to immune modulation, biofilm formation, and invasion. These virulence genes can be horizontally transferred between bacteria, potentially increasing the pathogenic potential of the gut microbiota (Montanari et al., 2025). In contrast, WOW ducks demonstrated a functional profile characterized by enhanced core viral processes. Despite lower overall diversity, COG analysis revealed that WOW ducks maintained a larger gene pool for essential functions such as replication, recombination, and repair, indicating robust genetic maintenance capabilities. This suggests that the WOW virome may be more stable and better adapted to the relatively controlled environment (Huang et al., 2025). Importantly, WOW ducks showed significantly reduced abundance of virulence factors, particularly those associated with immune evasion and host invasion. This reduced pathogenic potential aligns with the goal of WOW systems to minimize disease risk through reduced environmental exposure (Chu et al., 2024).

From an ecological perspective, the two rearing systems represent different viral community strategies. The WW system fosters a high-diversity, high-activity virome with broad metabolic capabilities but increased pathogenic risk, reflecting an opportunistic strategy adapted to complex and dynamic environments (Ritz et al., 2024). In contrast, the WOW system cultivates a lower-diversity but functionally focused virome with enhanced genetic stability and reduced pathogenic load (Shkoporov et al., 2019). These differences likely have cascading effects on the broader gut ecosystem, influencing bacterial community structure, host immune responses, and overall gut health (Borodovich et al., 2022; Obeng et al., 2016). While WW systems may provide certain metabolic advantages through enhanced carbohydrate utilization, the increased virulence factor load raises concerns about disease transmission and antibiotic resistance (Brown et al., 2022; Luo et al., 2022). WOW systems, with their reduced pathogenic potential and stable viral communities, offer advantages for biosecurity and sustainable production (Federici et al., 2021).

While this study systematically compared the differences in the gut virome under the two rearing systems, it still has certain limitations. The research is primarily based on metagenomic sequencing data and lacks experimental validation of specific virus–host interactions. In the future, longitudinal studies could be conducted to track the dynamic changes of the virome over the rearing cycle. Integrating phenotypic data such as host growth performance and immune indicators will provide a more precise scientific basis for optimizing feeding management practices.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the rearing system is a critical environmental factor shaping the duck gut virome. The WW system fostered a highly diverse virome enriched with virulence factors, indicating high environmental adaptability coupled with potential risks. In contrast, the WOW system promoted a virome with a more focused functional core and a lower virulence load, demonstrating superior biosafety and stability. These findings provide crucial viromic insights for optimizing rearing practices to regulate gut ecosystem health.
