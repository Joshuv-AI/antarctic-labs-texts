Association Between Staphylococcus Aureus Colonization and Pediatric Atopic Dermatitis: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Phoomphut Sangaphunchai, Chawan Kritsanaviparkporn, Arucha Treesirichod
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Atopic dermatitis is a chronic inflammatory skin condition common in early childhood. Acute exacerbation is frequently associated with Staphylococcus aureus colonization.Aims and Objectives:This study aims to explore the relationship between S. aureus skin and nasal colonization with pediatric atopic dermatitis.Methods:A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted by comparing atopic dermatitis patients aged ≤18 years and nondiseased controls. A random-effects model was used to obtain the pooled prevalence and odds ratio of S. aureus colonization at eczematous skin, nonlesional skin, and nasal cavity. Subgroup analyses for colonization with methicillin-resistant S. aureus were also evaluated.Results:A total of 2,670 cases and 1,224 controls from 26 studies were included in the meta-analysis. S. aureus colonization at eczematous skin and nasal cavity is significantly higher in atopic dermatitis compared to control with odds ratios of 10.55 (95% confidence interval [CI]; 4.85-22.92, P < .001) and 2.38 (nasal cavity; 95% CI; 1.46-3.90, P < .001), respectively. The pooled prevalence of skin and nasal colonization were 55.0% (eczematous skin; 95% CI; 38.3-71.7), 23.3% (nonlesional skin; 95% CI; 12.6-33.9), and 56.3% (95% CI; 43.2-69.4), respectively. Methicillin-resistant S. aureus strain was obtained from the nares and eczematous skin with rates of 11.6% (95% CI; 6.5-16.7) and 8.5% (95% CI; 4.3-12.8), respectively.Conclusion:Children with atopic dermatitis are more prone to skin and nasal colonization by S. aureus compared to nondiseased individuals.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis is an inflammatory skin condition characterized by poorly defined erythema, edema, and vesicles in acute stages and lichenification in chronic stages. The prevalence of atopic dermatitis is high in early childhood, as children demonstrate frequent alternation between remission and exacerbation.[1] The pathogenesis of atopic dermatitis is multifactorial and involves an interplay between skin barrier defects and genetic, immunological, and environmental factors.[2] Treatment depends on the severity and stage of the disease. Currently, topical anti-inflammatory therapy is recommended as first-line, along with the elimination of the aggravating cause.[3] Because the underlying epidermal barrier defect predisposes patients to skin infections, a combination of topical or systemic antibiotics may be considered.[4]

Staphylococcus aureus, a gram-positive coccus bacterium, is a normal flora that colonizes the upper respiratory tract and skin. The bacterium is responsible for a wide range of infections, such as pneumonia, meningitis, osteomyelitis, sepsis, and soft-tissue infections. Common skin manifestations include folliculitis, impetigo, cellulitis, and abscess. Prior research demonstrates that atopic dermatitis patients demonstrate higher rates of S. aureus colonization on the skin and in the nares.[56] While skin colonization affects eczema severity, nasal carriage serves as the source for bacterial recolonization.[7] In moderate to high disease severity, acute exacerbation has also been associated with S. aureus skin infection.[89] Therefore, the bacterium appears to play an important role in the disease course of atopic dermatitis.

The prevalence of S. aureus colonization in atopic dermatitis varies depending on the type of patient, sample size, and bacterial detection methods.[10] Previous studies report a 46.4%-82.2% prevalence of nasal colonization in pediatric patients,[1112] as opposed to a 19.4%-34.1% in the control group.[131415] On the other hand, nasal colonization in diseased adults stands at 62.0%-69.8%.[11161718] The eradication of nasal colonization has been shown to decrease atopic dermatitis severity and rate of exacerbation.[19] Nevertheless, with the emergence of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), appropriate use of antibiotics should be considered and restricted only to relapses with signs of bacterial contagion. To provide an insight into the importance of S. aureus as a contributor to the disease, this study aims to investigate the association between S. aureus colonization and pediatric atopic dermatitis.

Methodology

This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42020203498) and conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses guidelines. The study has been approved by the Institutional Review Board and the Ethics Committee (SWUEC 142/63) Issue date 8 May 2020.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. The search included all English language articles from the database up to January 14, 2020. An example search term is listed in Supplement Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

We included observational and experimental studies of pediatric atopic dermatitis (≤18 years of age) with the following eligibility criteria: (1) study contains data on S. aureus colonization per patient. (2) Sampling is obtained from any source of lesional (eczematous) skin, nonlesional skin, and nasal cavity. Studies were excluded based on the following conditions: (1) review or case report, (2) patient’s age is not clearly identified, (3) study of skin infection, and (4) studies not written in English.

Study selection and data extraction

Study selection and quality assessment were independently reviewed by two authors (P.S. and C.K.). The titles and abstracts were assessed for relevance before full-text articles were evaluated. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or by adjudication of a senior assessor (A.T.). If identical populations were described in two separate studies over the same time period, a publication with a greater sample size was included. In controlled trial studies, the baseline number of patients colonized with S. aureus prior to intervention was included in the meta-analysis. Details of studies including number of participants, age and gender, inclusion and exclusion criteria, diagnostic criteria, severity score, study design, year of data collection, sample collection, and bacteriologic identification method were collected. The quality of the articles was assessed using the Observational Study Quality Evaluation (OSQE) for case-control studies and cross-sectional studies independently by two authors (P.S. and C.K.).[20] Controlled trials would be analyzed as a cross-sectional study because only the baseline characteristics were to be included for analysis.

Synthesis of results

The primary outcome is the odds of S. aureus colonization in nasal cavity in pediatric atopic dermatitis compared to controls. Secondary outcomes include odds of colonization in eczematous (lesional) skin and nonlesional skin sites, and prevalence of colonization in the nasal cavity, lesional skin, and nonlesional skin. A subgroup analysis was also performed on the prevalence of MRSA colonization at each site.

We analyzed a proportion meta-analysis using a random-effects model to obtain a pooled prevalence of S. aureus colonization. For dichotomous outcomes, the odds ratio with a 95% confidence interval between groups was calculated. As for continuous variables, studies that reported the data of participants using the median were converted to an estimated mean using Wan’s method.[21] Study heterogeneity was assessed using I2 statistic. We also performed a metaregression to delineate the effects of bacterial sampling year as a covariate. All statistical tests were two-sided and used a significance level of P value less than. 05. Analysis was performed on OpenMeta [Analyst].

The quality of evidence of the outcome was evaluated by the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations framework (GRADE). Risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, publication bias, magnitude of effect, and confounding bias were taken into consideration.[22]

Results

Study selection

The initial search retrieved 915 articles with 306 remaining after the removal of duplicates. After screening of titles and abstracts, 51 articles were identified. Through the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 26 studies were included in the meta-analysis [Figure 1].

Study characteristics

Studies were conducted between 1990 and 2018. Ten of the 26 studies compared atopic dermatitis patients with the control group head-on [Table 1]. Of these studies, 15 used a cross-sectional design, while one was a controlled trial [Table 2]. The number of childhood atopic dermatitis patients ranged from 17 to 298 in each study, with a total of 2,670 cases and 1,224 controls. The overall mean age was 5.57 years (1 month to 18 years). Eleven studies recruited patients who were less than 18 years old but gave no further details on the age of participants. Nineteen studies used the UK working party’s diagnostic criteria for atopic dermatitis or Hanifin and Rajka’s to diagnose atopic dermatitis, while the remaining seven did not mention their diagnostic criteria. Nine studies reported patient severity ranging from mild to severe (423 mild, 450 moderate, and 164 severe cases) using tools such as Eczema Area and Severity Index or Atopic Dermatitis Severity Index and the Scoring atopic dermatitis. The quality of case-control studies assessed via OSQE varied from 5 to 9 of 14 stars, and cross-sectional studies and one controlled trial varied from 2 to 5 of 7 stars.

Association between S. aureus nasal cavity colonization and pediatric atopic dermatitis

Children with atopic dermatitis have a significantly higher chance of nasal colonization than the control group with a pooled odds ratio of 2.38 (95% confidence interval [CI]; 1.46-3.90, I2 71.3%, P < .001, 451 cases, 981 controls) [Figure 2a].[23242526] The prevalence of S. aureus nasal colonization in the diseased group is 56.3% (95% CI; 43.2-69.4, I2 97.1%, 1,466 patients),[1112232425262728293031323334] compared to a 26.2% in the control (95% CI; 19.0-33.4, I2 82.3%, 981 controls).[23242526313536373839] Subgroup analysis shows that the MRSA colonization rate in atopic dermatitis is 11.6% (95% CI; 6.5-16.7, I2 94.6%, 1,236 patients).[11232425262728293233] Metaregression shows that sampling year is not a covariate for odds of colonization in the nasal cavity (P = .292). The GRADE certainty of these outcomes is ‘very low’ due to studies having an observational design, imprecision, and inconsistency results.

Association between S. aureus skin colonization and pediatric atopic dermatitis

The pooled odds ratio of S. aureus colonization on eczematous skin compared to control is 10.55 (95% CI; 4.85-22.92, I2 51.5%, P < .001, 468 cases, 379 controls) [Figure 2b].[24263536373839] The prevalence of colonization in diseased individuals is 55.0% (95% CI; 38.3-71.7, I2 99.3%, 2,053 patients),[122425262829323334353637383940414243444546] while nondiseased individuals show a 9.0% prevalence (95% CI; 3.7-14.4, I2 82.9%, 420 controls).[23242526313536373839] Subgroup analysis reveals that 8.5% are colonized with MRSA (95% CI; 4.3-12.8, I2 88.4%, 968 patients).[24252628123233374346] Meta-regression shows that sampling year is not a covariate for odds of colonization on the skin (P = .949). The GRADE certainty of these outcomes is also ‘very low’ due to studies having an observational design, imprecision, and inconsistency results.

The pooled odds ratio of S. aureus colonization on unaffected skin in atopic dermatitis is not significantly different to the control (odd ratio = 1.64; 95% CI; 0.89-3.00, I2 0%, P = .110) [Figure 2c].[2426353739] Patients demonstrate a colonization prevalence of 23.3% (95% CI; 12.6-33.9, I2 95.1%, 749 patients, nine studies).[242630353739414445] The GRADE certainty of these outcomes is regarded as ‘very low’ due to the included articles being observational in design, potential risk of bias, and imprecision.

Discussion

S. aureus, a common microbiota of the skin and nares, has been linked to the pathogenesis of atopic dermatitis.[2] There is a knowledge gap on its association specifically in the pediatric group, and thus, we aimed to compare S. aureus colonization in atopic dermatitis and nondiseased children aged less than 18 years.

Our result suggests atopic dermatitis patients have both higher odds and prevalence of colonization. This is consistent in eczematous skin and nasal cavity, but not in nonlesional skin sites. Strikingly, patients are 10.55 times more likely to have eczematous skin colonization than nondiseased children, as opposed to 1.64 and 2.38 times in nonlesional skin and nasal cavity, respectively. Thus, the clinical relevance of S. aureus colonization in an atopic dermatitis patient can manifest as stable atopic dermatitis without evidence of overt infection, eczema flare without clinical evidence of overt infection, and eczema flare with overt infection.[47] This is in concordance with the notion that the presence of S. aureus as well as the density of colonization may directly affect the severity of eczema.[24748] Mechanistically, the underlying skin defect and altered immunophysiology in atopic dermatitis, such as the deficiency in antimicrobial peptides, filaggrin, and filaggrin degradation products, and the overexpression of T-helper 2 cytokines render patients more susceptible to S. aureus colonization.[4749] Therefore, this supports the rationale that eradication of S. aureus colonization by bleach baths may be beneficial for treatment.[12] Although the colonization rates in our study reveal lower figures than prior findings from adult patients, this is likely due to age-specific differences.[16]

Our subgroup analyses show that prevalence of MRSA in the nose and skin of patients is higher than control (11.6% and 8.5%, respectively, versus 0.8%-6.1%).[1314155051] This is in line with Abad et al., which reported moderate or severe atopic dermatitis as risk factors for MRSA colonization (hazard ratio [HR] =3.26; 95% CI; 1.13-9.37).[27] Other factors, such as contact with persons colonized with MRSA (HR = 2.27; 95% CI; 1.16-7.54), cyclosporine treatment (HR = 5.84; 95% CI; 1.70-19.98), history of prior hospitalization, and preceding antibiotic therapy are also important risks.[4346] Given the scope of this analysis, it remains to be elucidated whether the increased prevalence in patients is due to greater S. aureus colonization or whether atopic dermatitis itself carries a specific inherent susceptibility to MRSA colonization. Nevertheless, the appropriate use of antibiotics for disease management should be cautioned, and surveillance for MRSA colonization may be beneficial for patients who respond poorly to the standard treatment regimen.

Limitations

The included studies demonstrate poor process of participant selections, lack of optimal external validity, and presence of confounders. Thus, the OSQE assessment for the methodological quality of each work mostly received medium to low stars. Many studies did not provide a detailed breakdown of their participant’s characteristics, such as sex, ethnicity, and severity of disease, which may act as a covariate or promote heterogeneity in our results. Additionally, in most cross-sectional studies, the prevalence of colonization was not compared head-on between diseased and control, so comparing prevalence figures within our study should be done with caution. Despite the subpar quality of studies, the analyses consistently demonstrate a positive relationship between S. aureus colonization and pediatric atopic dermatitis.

Conclusion

Pediatric patients with atopic dermatitis are more susceptible to S. aureus colonization on the skin and in the nasal cavity than nondiseased children. Nevertheless, higher-quality studies are warranted to demonstrate this association with greater confidence.

One Sentence Summary

Children with atopic dermatitis are more prone to skin and nasal colonization by S. aureus compared to nondiseased individuals.
