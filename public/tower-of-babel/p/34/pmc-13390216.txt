Six anthropometric indices and all-cause mortality in ageing populations: an observational cohort study in six longitudinal studies
Authors: Dongqin Xu, Jinnan Yin, Wenming Shen, Xuwen Zheng, Jin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBody-mass index (BMI) does not capture fat distribution and may contribute to the apparent obesity paradox in older adults. We compared six anthropometric indices in relation to all-cause mortality across multiple ageing cohorts.MethodsWe analyzed data from six longitudinal studies in 25 countries (CHARLS, HRS, ELSA, MHAS, SHARE, KLoSA). Adults aged 45 years or older with baseline anthropometry and mortality follow-up were evaluated (n = 89,745). Exposures were BMI, waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), body roundness index (BRI), A Body Shape Index (ABSI), weight-adjusted waist index (WWI), and conicity index (CI). Cox models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) per 1-SD increase with adjustment guided by a prespecified directed acyclic graph. Non-linear associations were examined with floating absolute risks and restricted cubic splines.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 10 years, 16,593 deaths occurred. BMI was weakly inversely associated with all-cause mortality (HR per 1-SD 0.96, [95% CI 0.93–0.98]), attenuating after exclusion of early deaths (0.97, [0.94–1.00]). ABSI (1.15, [1.11–1.19]) and CI (1.09, [1.06–1.13]) showed a consistent positive association. Dose–response patterns differed: BMI, BRI, and WHtR followed a shallow U-shape, whereas ABSI, WWI, and CI increased approximately linearly without a threshold.ConclusionsIn older adults, indices capturing central adiposity—particularly ABSI—are more consistently associated with higher all-cause mortality than BMI. Routine assessment of waist and height, and communication of simple shape metrics, could improve risk stratification and counselling in clinical and public health practice.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27852-w.

Background

Population ageing and rising adiposity have renewed interest in simple anthropometric measures for mortality risk stratification in later life. Body mass index (BMI), although widely used because it reliably normalizes weight for height across populations, does not capture fat distribution or body composition [1, 2]. Especially in older adults, an apparent “obesity paradox” has been reported, with higher BMI often associated with lower mortality possibly reflecting reverse causation from illness-related weight loss, confounding by smoking or pre-existing disease, and rather than true protection [3–6]. Clinicians and policy makers therefore need low-cost, scalable metrics that are comparable across age, sex, and diverse populations. Measures of central adiposity—including waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) and composite indices such as the body roundness index (BRI), A Body Shape Index (ABSI), the weight-adjusted waist index (WWI), and the conicity index (CI) have been proposed to better approximate visceral fat and cardiometabolic risk, but there are few studies comparing mortality risk in older adults across countries [7–11].

Central adiposity is mechanistically patho-physiologically linked to insulin resistance, low-grade inflammation, atherogenesis, and sarcopenic changes, all of which increase mortality risk in later life [2, 12–15]. In population-based cohorts, waist-related metrics—particularly WHtR—and composite indices such as ABSI, BRI, CI, and WWI often show independent associations with adverse outcomes and can outperform BMI for cardiometabolic risk prediction [7–9, 11, 16]. However, existing evidence is inconsistent and difficult to compare: most studies evaluate only one or two indices, are restricted to single-country samples with limited representation of older adults, and use heterogeneous measurements and covariate adjustments [17–19]. Non-linear dose–response associations and potential effect modification by age, sex, smoking, and socioeconomic status (SES) have seldom been assessed systematically [18–22]. Consequently, it remains uncertain which simple anthropometric marker offers the most robust risk stratification in older adults, and whether central adiposity metrics attenuate the apparent obesity paradox.

Against this background, we aimed to contrast six anthropometric indices—BMI, WHtR, BRI, ABSI, WWI, and CI—in relation to prediction of all-cause mortality among adults aged 45 years or older across multiple countries. These indices were selected a priori to cover complementary dimensions of adiposity that can be derived from measurements of weight, height and in a subset of subjects, waist circumference. We report findings for overall adiposity (BMI), central adiposity (WHtR/BRI), and 3 height and weight–adjusted waist circumference (WC) based indices: ABSI, WWI, and CI. We synchronized the indices and covariates across six longitudinal ageing cohorts and used a prespecified directed acyclic graph (DAG) to guide confounder adjustment. Cox models with flexible functions were applied to examine non-linear associations and potential effect modification by age, sex, and health behaviors. In sensitivity analyses, we assessed robustness to alternative specifications and cohort-specific features. We hypothesized that waist-based indices would outperform BMI for mortality risk stratification and help clarify the apparent obesity paradox, thereby providing simple, low-cost markers for clinical and public health use.

Methods

Study design and participants

In this population-based, cross-national cohort study, we used longitudinal data from six ageing cohorts covering 25 countries: the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), the US Health and Retirement Study (HRS), the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), the Mexican Health and Ageing Study (MHAS), the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), and the Korean Longitudinal Study of Ageing (KLoSA) [23–28]. These six cohorts were selected a priori because they are among the largest, well-established, nationally representative longitudinal studies of ageing with publicly accessible data, long-term follow-up, and verified or linkable all-cause mortality outcomes. Collectively, they span major world regions and diverse populations (East Asia, North America, and Europe), which improves the generalizability of findings and enables cross-country comparisons. In addition, these cohorts collect harmonized baseline anthropometric measures (height and weight in all cohorts, and waist circumference in several cohorts) and a broadly comparable set of sociodemographic and behavioral covariates, allowing a head-to-head comparison of multiple anthropometric indices within a unified analytic framework. For comparability, we analyzed data from overlapping timeframes: 2011–20 for CHARLS (waves 1–5), 2010–20 for HRS (waves 10–15), 2008–18 for ELSA (waves 4–9), 2012–21 for MHAS (waves 3–6), 2011–21 for SHARE (waves 4–9), and 2010–20 for KLoSA (waves 3–8).

Participants were eligible if they were aged 45 years or older at baseline, completed an interview and examination with measured height and weight sufficient to derive anthropometric indices, and had verified vital status and follow-up for all-cause mortality. WC was measured by trained staff using cohort protocols. CHARLS and HRS measured at the level of the navel with the tape horizontal and snug, recorded at end-expiration. ELSA measured at the midpoint between the lower rib and iliac crest, repeated (third measure if the first two differed by ≥ 3 cm). MHAS measured at the midpoint between the last rib and iliac crest (confirmed bilaterally), with repeat measurements for reliability. Any residual measurement error is expected to be largely non-differential. In KLoSA and SHARE, which did not collect waist measurements, it was predicted from BMI using prespecified sex- and age-specific equations [29]. Participants with implausible anthropometric values were excluded. Outliers were defined using Tukey’s rule (k = 1.5): values below the first quartile minus 1.5 × interquartile range (IQR) or above the third quartile plus 1.5 × IQR [30]. We used publicly available, de-identified data from the included cohorts. The study followed the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for the original surveys was obtained by their respective study teams (Additionalfile 1 pp 4–5), and no additional approval was required for the present analysis.

Procedures

We prespecified a DAG to represent hypothesized causal relations between anthropometric indices and all-cause mortality (Additionalfile 1 p 1) [31]. The minimal sufficient adjustment set included age, sex, SES, physical activity, ethnicity, smoking, alcohol use, genetic predisposition, diet quality, preclinical illness, and sleep (Additionalfile 1 pp 2–3). These constructs were mapped to available measures across the six cohorts, prioritizing harmonized baseline definitions; prespecified proxy variables were used when direct measures were unavailable. Detailed definitions of variables are provided in Additionalfile 1 pp 6–8.

Statistical analysis

Data for age, sex, and all anthropometric indices were complete. The proportions of missing data for SES, smoking, drinking, physical activity, self-reported health, diet quality, and sleep problems were all less than 10%, whereas information on parental early death was missing in nearly half of the participants. We compared continuous variables using t tests and categorical variables using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\chi }^{2}$$\end{document} tests. Correlations between anthropometric indices were estimated with Pearson coefficients. Cox regression was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for all-cause mortality per 1-SD increase in each index, after standardisation to Z-scores (calculated by subtracting the sample mean from each observed value and dividing the result by the sample standard deviation). Proportional hazards (PH) assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Three models were specified: model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 adjusted for age, sex, and socioeconomic status; and model 3 adjusted for all covariates in the prespecified minimal sufficient set except ethnicity. We undertook several sensitivity analyses. First, age was modelled as a categorical variable to reduce residual confounding by age. Second, analyses were restricted to participants aged 55 years or older to mitigate survivor bias and between-cohort heterogeneity. Third, multiple imputation was used to address missing covariates, with predictive mean matching for continuous variables and classification and regression trees for categorical variables. Fourth, an HRS-only analysis was performed with additional adjustment for ethnicity. Fifth, participants who died within 2 years of baseline were excluded to reduce potential reverse causation (e.g., illness-related weight loss preceding baseline). Sixth, analyses were restricted to CHARLS, ELSA, HRS, and MHAS to minimize bias from imputed WC in SHARE and KLoSA.

To examine non-linear associations, each index was divided into five quintiles based on its distribution among participants who subsequently died, ensuring a similar number of deaths per group. For each category, including the reference (HR = 1.00), we reported group-specific (“floating”) confidence intervals to reflect the available information. Analyses were based on model 3 and repeated in all sensitivity settings. HRs were plotted at the mean value of each quintile to show associations between indices and mortality [32]. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were additionally applied to validate the observed trends.

We conducted prespecified subgroup analyses by age (< 65 vs ≥ 65 years), sex, smoking status (never, former, current), current drinking (yes vs no), and physical activity (high vs low frequency). Within each stratum, model 3 was refitted to estimate adjusted HRs. Effect modification was tested on the multiplicative scale by adding interaction terms between the standardized index and each subgroup variable; for multi-level factors such as smoking, cross-product terms were tested jointly with Wald tests. Non-linear associations were further examined within subgroups when significant heterogeneity was observed by age or sex.

All analyses were done in R (4.5.1). To control for multiple testing, a Bonferroni correction was applied, yielding a significance threshold of p < 0.008 (0.05/6) [33].

Results

A total of 89,745 participants were included: 12,165 from CHARLS, 7,742 from HRS, 7,724 from ELSA, 1,709 from MHAS, 52,969 from SHARE, and 7,436 from KLoSA (Additionalfile 1 pp 15–26). During a median follow-up of 10 years, 16,593 deaths occurred. Compared with survivors, decedents were older (mean age 73.5 vs 62.4 years), more often male (53.7% vs 42.6%), and more likely to be aged 65 years or older (78.9% vs 37.9%). They also had lower SES, higher rates of current or former smoking, slightly lower prevalence of drinking, less frequent high-intensity physical activity (19.3% vs 35.1%), and poorer self-reported health, diet quality, and sleep. For anthropometric measures, decedents had lower BMI (mean 25.8 vs 26.2 kg/m2) but higher central adiposity indices, including BRI (4.95 vs 4.73), WHtR (0.574 vs 0.565), ABSI (0.085 vs 0.083), WWI (11.3 vs 11.1), and CI (1.34 vs 1.30); all comparisons were statistically significant (p < 0.001; Table 1). Discussion

In this large international analysis of almost 90,000 adults aged 45 years or older across six ageing cohorts from 25 countries, we found that indices capturing central adiposity—most notably ABSI—were consistently and linearly associated with higher all-cause mortality, whereas BMI showed a weak inverse or shallow U-shaped association that attenuated after exclusion of early deaths. Specifically, each 1-SD increase in ABSI was associated with a 15% higher mortality risk, with no evidence of a protective threshold, while the corresponding estimate for BMI suggested a modest reduction, largely driven by reverse causation and disappearing in sensitivity analyses. Other waist-derived indices such as BRI and WHtR followed shallow U-shaped patterns, but effect sizes were smaller and less consistent. These results underscore that, in older adults, body shape and central fat distribution are more informative for mortality risk than overall body mass, and that reliance on BMI alone may obscure or misclassify risk.

Our findings build on and extend previous work by providing a direct, harmonized comparison of six anthropometric indices in older adults across multiple continents. Prior studies, often confined to single countries or restricted age ranges, have suggested that waist-related metrics such as WHtR or composite indices outperform BMI in predicting cardiometabolic events and mortality [7, 34–36]. However, most earlier reports examined only one or two indices in isolation, relied on heterogeneous covariate adjustments, or lacked sufficient representation of the elderly population, limiting cross-study comparability [7, 35, 37–39]. Correlation analysis showed a perfect correlation between BRI and WHtR. This is consistent with findings from a recent NHANES-based study, which noted that BRI is mathematically reducible to a function of WHtR, indicating substantial overlap in the information captured by these two indices [39]. We also noted that the correlation between BMI and ABSI in our pooled sample was weakly negative, whereas it was close to zero in the NHANES study. This difference may be attributable to differences in age structure, ethnic composition, and cross-cohort heterogeneity across the multinational ageing cohorts included in our analysis. The consistent and approximately linear association observed for ABSI in our analysis aligns with reports from the NHANES and other regional cohorts, which have highlighted ABSI as a robust predictor of mortality across diverse ethnicities and behaviors [11, 40–44]. In addition, longitudinal trajectory analyses suggest that persistently increasing BRI is associated with higher long-term risk of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality, supporting the relevance of dynamic central-adiposity measures beyond single baseline values [45]. By contrast, the attenuated or inverse association of BMI that we observed is consistent with the so-called obesity paradox described in older adults, but our sensitivity analyses reinforce that this paradox is more likely explained by illness-related weight loss, smoking, and survival bias than by a protective effect of higher adiposity [46–48]. Notably, our results add nuance to the literature on WHtR and BRI: although both indices showed U-shaped associations with nadirs close to conventional cut-offs, effect sizes were modest and heterogeneous across subgroups, suggesting that their utility may vary by age and health status. The present study is, to our knowledge, the first large-scale international comparison to demonstrate that ABSI provides a more consistent gradient of risk than BMI or other waist-based indices in ageing populations, supporting the prioritization of body shape measures in clinical and public health practice.

The pattern that ABSI tracked all‑cause mortality more consistently than BMI is biologically coherent and methodologically plausible. By scaling waist to height and mass in a way that reduces collinearity with overall size, ABSI isolates central adiposity—an anthropometric proxy for visceral and ectopic fat—which is tightly linked to insulin resistance, low‑grade systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and atherosclerosis, culminating in higher fatal event risk [49–52]. In contrast, the weak inverse BMI association that disappeared once early deaths were excluded is compatible with reverse causation and confounding by smoking and pre‑existing disease: illness‑related weight loss and selective survival can shift low‑BMI groups towards higher baseline risk, while greater lean mass in some older adults inflates BMI without commensurate metabolic hazard [53–55]. Heterogeneity among waist‑based composite measures (BRI, WHtR, WWI, CI) likely reflects differences in how each index weights stature versus girth, single‑time anthropometry that cannot capture trajectories or fat redistribution with ageing, and modest measurement error (including modelled rather than measured waists in some cohorts) that would attenuate effects towards the null [19, 52, 56, 57]. Signals of PHs non‑constancy further suggest time‑varying mechanisms: early follow‑up dominated by disease‑related wasting, later follow‑up by adiposity‑mediated cardiometabolic pathways [58–60]. Taken together, these considerations provide a coherent mechanistic framework in which body shape and fat distribution—rather than body mass per se—drive mortality risk in later life.

Our dose–response analyses highlight distinct patterns across indices, underscoring the importance of interpreting obesity measures in context. BMI, BRI, and WHtR all exhibited shallow U-shaped relationships with mortality, with nadirs at approximately 25.4, 5.61, and 0.608, respectively, suggesting that both low and high values carry risk—low values likely reflecting frailty, undernutrition, or illness-related weight loss, and high values reflecting excess central adiposity. By contrast, ABSI demonstrated an approximately monotonic positive association, with no evidence of a threshold or safe zone, reinforcing the view that higher central adiposity is uniformly detrimental across the full distribution. These patterns indicate that while conventional indices such as BMI and WHtR may suggest “optimal” ranges [61], their interpretation in older adults must account for reverse causation and competing risks, whereas ABSI provides a continuous and more reliable gradient of risk. Such distinctions are clinically important: they suggest that public health targets in ageing populations should move beyond static BMI cut-offs and incorporate measures of body shape and fat distribution for more nuanced risk stratification.

Subgroup analyses provided further insight into the heterogeneity of associations, although these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. The gradient of risk for ABSI was steeper among women than men, consistent with sex-specific patterns of body composition, including postmenopausal shifts towards visceral adiposity and greater vulnerability to sarcopenia–adiposity coexistence. For BMI, the apparent inverse association was concentrated among drinkers, suggesting that reverse causation and selective survival may operate more strongly in this behavioral subgroup. Age-related modification was evident for BRI and WHtR: values below 65 years were associated with higher risk, whereas in those aged 65 years or older the associations flattened or even suggested a shallow U-shape, likely reflecting age-related changes in body composition and the influence of competing risks. Taken together, these patterns underscore biologically plausible effect modification, but they do not overturn the overall conclusion that central adiposity—rather than total body mass—is the more consistent marker of mortality risk in ageing populations.

Our study has several methodological strengths. First, we assembled six ageing cohorts spanning 25 countries and applied harmonized definitions of exposures, covariates, and outcomes, enabling a head‑to‑head comparison of six simple anthropometric indices within a single analytic framework. Second, confounding control was guided by a DAG–informed minimal sufficient adjustment set that was implemented uniformly across cohorts, with robustness probed through pre‑specified sensitivity analyses (age banding and ≥ 55‑year subset, exclusion of early deaths, race adjustment in HRS, and multiple imputation for missing data). Third, functional form and model assumptions were handled a priori: we interrogated non-linearity with grouped displays using floating absolute risks and validating these with RCS. Finally, by reporting effect sizes on a common scale and presenting continuous gradients across indices, our approach enhances interpretability and transportability.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, as an observational analysis, residual confounding and reverse causation cannot be excluded despite a DAG–informed adjustment set, exclusion of early deaths, and multiple sensitivity analyses; moreover, baseline self‑report of physical activity, diet, sleep, and pre-clinical illness is prone to misclassification. Second, BMI and waist circumference have inherent measurement and interpretative limitations. BMI cannot distinguish adiposity from lean mass or bone mass and may be particularly difficult to interpret in older adults because of sarcopenia, height loss, and illness-related weight loss. Waist circumference was modelled rather than directly measured in SHARE and KLoSA, which likely introduced non-differential measurement error and may have attenuated associations toward the null. In addition, waist circumference was not measured at identical anatomical sites across all cohorts: some cohorts measured waist circumference at the navel, whereas others used the midpoint between the lower rib and iliac crest. Although all protocols were intended to capture central adiposity, these differences may have introduced between-cohort heterogeneity and reduced the comparability of waist-based indices. Third, evidence of PH non‑constancy suggests time‑varying effects that we could not fully characterize without repeated measures or time‑updated covariates; age‑related stature loss may also bias shape‑based indices that scale by height. Fourth, outcome harmonization focused on all‑cause mortality; cause‑specific deaths, hospitalizations, and functional outcomes were not uniformly available, limiting mechanistic interpretation. Finally, our percentile‑based communication anchors (e.g., the first‑quintile mean of ABSI) were derived from these datasets and should be re‑calibrated to local age‑ and sex‑specific distributions before external implementation.

Conclusions

In older adults, indices that capture central adiposity—particularly ABSI—are more consistently linked to higher all‑cause mortality than BMI alone. Clinically and in public health practice, risk assessment should therefore shift from a weight‑centric to a shape‑centric paradigm: routine recording of waist and height, calculation of simple body‑shape metrics with serial tracking can support counselling and follow‑up, whereas BMI should not be used in isolation. Future work should evaluate time‑updated trajectories of weight and waist, examine cause‑specific mortality and functional outcomes, test interventions that specifically reduce central adiposity, and validate pragmatic anchors across diverse settings and ethnicities using both anthropometry and imaging. Taken together, these steps can enable a practical, low‑cost, and equitable refinement of cardiometabolic risk management in ageing societies, aligning routine care with the biology of body shape rather than body mass per se.
