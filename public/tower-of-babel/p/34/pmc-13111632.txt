Aerodynamic performance optimization of the archimedes spiral wind turbine: combined experimental and CFD analysis of step ratio and blade number effects
Authors: Ahmed Essa Faisal, Chin Wai Lim, Balasem Abdulameer Jabbar Al-Quraishi, Jassinnee Milano, Chung Hong Tan, Chai Phing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents an integrated numerical and experimental investigation of the aerodynamic performance of the Archimedes Spiral Wind Turbine (ASWT) under wind speeds ranging from 5 to 10 m/s. The analyses were conducted at a Reynolds number (Re) of approximately 9.6 × 104, with the operational range of the tip speed ratio (TSR) varying between 0.5 and 3. The study focuses on examining the combined effects of step ratio () and blade number on the energy conversion efficiency of ASWT. A series of ASWT rotor models was designed using SolidWorks 2020 and analyzed through three-dimensional simulations in ANSYS CFX 2020 R2, with results validated experimentally under controlled wind tunnel conditions. To isolate the effect of , configurations ranging from 0.05 to 0.45 were examined while maintaining a constant aspect ratio (L/D). The analysis identified the intermediate configuration (PR-5) as the most aerodynamically efficient model. This configuration was subsequently adopted to evaluate the influence of blade number, which varied from two to six blades, to assess its impact on rotor solidity, torque generation, and flow stability. The three-blade model based on the PR-5 geometry (PR-5–3) demonstrated the best overall performance, achieving a maximum power coefficient () of 0.264 at a TSR of 1.88, while the two-blade configuration (PR-5–2) showed relatively better adaptability at higher TSR. These findings provide a structured framework for optimizing ASWT geometry and blade configuration to enhance aerodynamic performance and operational reliability in low-speed and multidirectional wind environments.Keywords: Archimedes spiral wind turbine (ASWT), Computational fluid dynamics (CFD), Step ratio (S₁/S₂), Urban wind turbine, Wind energySubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

The rapid advancement of modern technologies has substantially improved living standards and accelerated global development. However, it has also led to an unprecedented surge in energy demand, resulting in excessive dependence on fossil fuels. This reliance has exacerbated several critical environmental challenges, most notably the increasing frequency and severity of droughts, hurricanes, and floods, phenomena closely linked to global warming caused by greenhouse gas emissions. These environmental consequences extend beyond natural ecosystems and profoundly affect urban areas, particularly densely populated cities. Accelerated urbanization has contributed to severe ecological degradation, rendering many regions nearly uninhabitable and posing significant threats to public health1–4. Considering these pressing challenges, there is a critical need to adopt sustainable and efficient energy solutions that not only mitigate environmental damage but also align with the demands of modern development. Among the most effective strategies is the acceleration of the transition to a sustainable energy future through the integration of renewable sources such as solar, hydroelectric, and wind energy. These alternatives provide practical and environmentally friendly options for meeting energy demands. Notably, wind energy emerges as a particularly promising solution due to its scalability, high energy potential, and minimal environmental footprint5,6.

Despite the growing deployment of wind energy, integrating wind turbines within urban environments remains a major engineering challenge due to low wind speeds, frequent directional changes, and high turbulence induced by surrounding buildings and surface roughness. These conditions significantly limit the performance of conventional turbines optimized for uniform and unidirectional inflow. In response, Archimedes Spiral Wind Turbine (ASWT) have recently emerged as a novel wind energy concept specifically developed for small-scale and urban applications. Inspired by the Archimedean spiral, ASWT employ a continuous helical blade geometry and operate based on a hybrid lift–drag aerodynamic mechanism, enabling self-orientation and reliable operation under multidirectional and turbulent wind conditions without the need for yaw-control systems7–9.

The high-solidity, multi-blade configuration enhances self-starting capability and torque generation at low wind speeds, allowing ASWT to operate effectively in environments where conventional turbines struggle, including low and intermittent wind regimes. Such self-starting enhancement is commonly associated with increased rotor solidity and hybrid lift-drag behavior, which improves torque production at low rotational speeds and favors operation at low tip speed ratio (TSR). In parallel, low-TSR aerodynamic optimization, through blade geometry refinement and improved pressure distribution, has been shown to promote stable operation and efficient energy extraction under low-speed conditions10–12. The spiral blade geometry also contributes to smoother rotation and reduced acoustic emissions, making ASWT suitable for noise-sensitive urban areas13–17. However, these advantages are accompanied by important limitations that must be acknowledged. Compared with conventional horizontal-axis wind turbine (HAWT), ASWT generally exhibit lower peak aerodynamic efficiency due to increased drag losses and inter-blade aerodynamic interference18–22. Moreover, the large blade surface area subjects the rotor to higher aerodynamic and structural loads, while the complex three-dimensional spiral geometry increases manufacturing complexity and limits scalability relative to conventional straight-bladed designs. These aerodynamic challenges and design trade-offs have been widely explored in vertical-axis wind turbine (VAWT), indicating that the ASWT concept represents a horizontal-axis adaptation of similar hybrid lift–drag principles rather than an isolated design approach23,24.

Accordingly, ASWT should be regarded as a complementary and emerging technology rather than a replacement for high-efficiency HAWT, with their application best suited to low-speed, urban, and distributed wind energy systems where operational robustness, self-starting capability, and adaptability outweigh the need for maximum power coefficient ().

Recent investigations have extensively focused on evaluating and optimizing the aerodynamic performance of ASWT rotors through both experimental measurements and Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations. Attention has been given to refining geometric parameters, including aspect ratio (L/D), blade angle (α), and profile shape, to enhance energy capture efficiency. Ansari et al. examined an ASWT rotor with a 60 cm diameter (D) and four blades, testing α between 60° and 70°. Their experimental and numerical analyses revealed that a α of 70° produced the highest aerodynamic efficiency, achieving a maximum of 0.303 at a TSR of 2.5, with an average deviation of about 3% between experimental and simulated results25. Nepal et al. conducted CFD-based estimations to determine the geometric parameters that influence a 150 mm ASWT operating at a wind velocity of 3.5 m/s. By varying the step (S) from 0.25 to 2 times the radius and the opening angle between 30° and 90°, the optimal configuration was found at a S of 1.5 r and an opening angle of 60°. The selected model achieved a of 0.25 at a TSR of 1.5, demonstrating that proper S and angle selection significantly enhance aerodynamic performance across different wind speeds26.

Labib et al. conducted experimental and numerical investigations using ANSYS CFX 19 to examine the influence of α on the performance of an ASWT. α of 50°, 55°, 60°, and 65° were evaluated at wind speeds ranging from 5 to 12 m/s in increments of 1 m/s. The results showed that the configuration with a 50° α provided the best overall performance, achieving a maximum torque coefficient () of 0.24 at a TSR of 0.62, and a maximum of 0.22 at a TSR of 1.7227. Hamid and Abd El-Maksoud performed a CFD analysis in ANSYS Fluent, comparing Archimedean and logarithmic spiral profiles with α varying from 40° to 90° in 10° increments. The Archimedean profile exhibited superior aerodynamic characteristics, reaching a maximum = 0.2683 at TSR = 2.5 when the α was set to 60°28. Nawar et al. further investigated the role of α distribution by comparing two ASWT designs, one with a fixed angle and another with individually variables α. Their results demonstrated that the variable-angle configuration achieved a maximum = 0.227, marking a 14.7% improvement over the fixed-angle rotor29. Building upon this, Kamal et al. experimentally and numerically analyzed the effects of α, thickness, and step to diameter ratio (S/D) on the performance of the ASWT. The modified ASWT, designed with α of 25°, 50°, and 60°, achieved a of 0.286 compared to 0.233 for the standard model, a 22.75% improvement at TSR = 2 and 10 m/s. Further optimization using a NACA 4401 aerofoil with S/D of 0.30 raised the maximum to 0.3045, confirming that precise geometric refinement enhances aerodynamic efficiency and operational range30. Ke Song et al. conducted a CFD-based investigation of five ASWT rotor configurations, considering both fixed- and variable-angle α designs. Their results showed that variable-angle configurations generally outperformed fixed-angle ones, with the , , and combination achieving the highest performance, characterized by a maximum at TSR = 1.5. Nevertheless, among the fixed-angle cases, the configuration with exhibited the highest aerodynamic efficiency31.

Most previous studies on the ASWT have focused on individual geometric parameters such as α, D, or L/D. In contrast, the step ratio (), a defining geometric feature that dictates the spiral’s progression and the effective helix angle of the rotor, has received limited analytical and experimental attention. Earlier research often adopted fixed or arbitrary values without establishing a systematic relationship between this parameter and the turbine’s aerodynamic performance or flow stability26,28,30. Consequently, despite significant advances in ASWT modeling and fabrication, a comprehensive understanding of how the interacts with other geometric variables to influence aerodynamic efficiency remains insufficient. Addressing this knowledge gap is therefore essential for optimizing ASWT configurations and improving their adaptability to varying wind conditions.

In this study, the limitation is addressed by systematically varying while maintaining a constant L/D, allowing for a clear assessment of its influence on pressure distribution, flow uniformity, and overall aerodynamic performance. Fixing the rotor D and length (L) ensures that any performance variations arise solely from changes in rather than from other geometric differences. Without this constraint, the analysis would become nondeterministic, as numerous D and L combinations could produce overlapping effects on flow behavior and performance metrics. Maintaining a constant L/D, therefore, establishes a controlled framework that isolates the true aerodynamic contribution of and enhances the reliability of the comparative evaluation.

While analyzing the effect of provides important insights into the geometric optimization of the ASWT, this parameter alone does not fully describe the turbine’s aerodynamic complexity. Another critical geometric variable that influences flow distribution, torque generation, and overall energy conversion efficiency is the number of blades, which directly affects the rotor’s aerodynamic loading and flow interaction characteristics. In conventional HAWT, the three-blade configuration is widely recognized as the most effective design, offering an optimal balance between aerodynamic performance, structural stability, and manufacturing simplicity. This arrangement minimizes vibration and torque fluctuations while ensuring consistent power output and reliable operation across a wide range of wind conditions. However, the aerodynamic behavior of ASWT differs fundamentally from that of conventional HAWT. Due to their hybrid lift–drag mechanism and continuously curved spiral geometry, ASWT exhibit unique flow patterns and pressure distributions that alter their energy extraction characteristics. Consequently, the conventional three-blade configuration, proven effective for traditional HAWT, may not necessarily represent the most efficient design for this turbine type. To address this uncertainty, the present study systematically examines the influence of blade number on ASWT performance, focusing on its effects on torque generation, flow stability, and overall aerodynamic efficiency under multidirectional and low-speed wind conditions.

Adeyeye et al. reported that increasing the number of blades from two to three improved turbine efficiency by about 6%. However, adding more blades produced little additional gain because the higher blade counts increased drag and reduced the optimal rotational speed, which lowered the Cₚ. These results clearly show that the number of blades must be optimized to achieve the best balance between torque generation and aerodynamic efficiency32. Vedovelli et al. conducted an experimental investigation to assess the influence of blade numbers on the aerodynamic and dynamic response of a small HAWT. Their study focused on changing the number of blades from three to five to examine its impact on performance characteristics. The results revealed that increasing the blade count from three to five did not significantly change the . Still, they improved operational stability at low wind speeds and reduced vibration levels by operating at a lower rotational speed. Although the three-blade rotor achieved higher revolutions per minute (RPM) and a steeper performance slope, the five-blade configuration demonstrated superior reliability and smoother operation, particularly under turbulent and low-speed wind conditions33.

Wen et al. investigated the effect of blade number on the aerodynamic performance and wake dynamics of small-scale HAWT using large-eddy simulation (LES) and optimal mode decomposition. The study examined rotors with two to five blades and found that increasing the blade count broadened the near wake, intensified turbulence, and accelerated wake recovery, though at the cost of higher drag and slightly lower aerodynamic efficiency. Overall, the three-blade configuration achieved the best balance between , flow stability, and wake coherence34. Zhang et al. employed a combined experimental–numerical approach by integrating wind tunnel measurements with LES. Their results confirmed the superiority of the three-blade configuration and further quantified its performance by reporting a maximum exceeding 35%, in addition to demonstrating enhanced wake uniformity and improved flow stability under experimental conditions35.

Eltayesh et al. experimentally analyzed the aerodynamic behavior of HAWT equipped with three and five blades under varying TSR and wind velocities. Their findings indicated that increasing the number of blades improved flow uniformity and reduced sensitivity to Re, leading to steadier performance at low wind speeds. However, the three-blade rotor exhibited slightly higher aerodynamic efficiency, whereas the five-blade configuration showed marginally lower efficiency due to increased solidity and drag. These results confirmed that additional blades improve stability but may compromise energy conversion effectiveness36.

Although numerous studies have examined the influence of blade numbers on the aerodynamic performance of conventional HAWT, no prior research has systematically investigated this parameter in the context of ASWT. The absence of such studies leaves a significant knowledge gap regarding how variations in blade number affect the aerodynamic behavior, torque generation, and efficiency of this distinct spiral-type rotor. To fill this gap, the present study integrates numerical simulations and experimental measurements to evaluate the combined effects of and blade number on ASWT performance, aiming to establish an optimized design framework that enhances its aerodynamic efficiency and operational stability under low-speed, multidirectional wind conditions.

The axial progression of the helical surface defines the airflow path and directly influences the efficiency of wind-to-rotation energy conversion. Among the geometric variables defining this progression, represents a key intrinsic parameter that governs the axial development and curvature distribution of the helical surface.

Previous research on ASWT has mainly focused on external geometric parameters such as α and L/D, which describe the global shape but do not capture the internal helical structure that controls flow attachment, pressure gradients, and torque generation. Effect of blade number on the aerodynamic performance of the ASWT

This section investigates the influence of blade numbers on the aerodynamic performance of the ASWT. Building upon the previous analysis of effects, this phase focuses on blade count as a key design variable that directly modifies the aerodynamic loading, flow interaction, and overall power extraction capability of the rotor. Variations in blade number alter the balance between torque generation and rotational speed, thereby defining the turbine’s operating efficiency and optimal TSR.

Increasing the number of blades generally enhances torque generation and starting capability due to greater surface area exposed to the flow, but it also increases aerodynamic drag and lowers the optimal TSR. Conversely, reducing the blade number improves rotational speed and efficiency at higher TSR, though at the expense of low-speed performance and structural stability. Balancing aerodynamic efficiency and torque generation is critical for defining the optimal ASWT configuration under varying wind conditions.

All geometric parameters were fixed according to the optimal reference configuration (PR-5) identified in the previous phase, ensuring that aerodynamic variations resulted solely from changes in blade number. Five rotor configurations were developed, PR-5-2B, PR-5-3B, PR-5-4B, PR-5-5B, and PR-5-6B, corresponding to two through six blades33,36,37. The three-blade model (PR-5-3B), consistent with the conventional ASWT design, was adopted as the baseline for comparison.

Each rotor configuration, as illustrated in Fig. 3, was modeled in SolidWorks 2020 with uniform azimuthal spacing between blades to preserve geometric symmetry and ensure consistent flow distribution across the swept area. This modeling setup enabled a controlled aerodynamic assessment based on , torque characteristics, and flow field behavior under steady wind conditions.

Numerical simulation setup

The geometric configuration of the ASWT was first modeled in SolidWorks 2020 based on the Archimedean spiral formulation. The finalized geometry was then imported into ANSYS CFX 2020 R2 for CFD analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

Workflow of the CFD-based numerical analysis.

The aerodynamic performance of the turbine was evaluated by solving the Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) equations using the Finite Volume Method (FVM) within the CFX solver. The computational domain was divided into two regions: a rotating domain containing the turbine rotor and a stationary domain representing the surrounding airflow. The Multiple Reference Frame (MRF) approach was employed to simulate the steady-state interaction between the rotating blades and the stationary fluid field. This setup enabled detailed analysis of the flow behavior, including velocity and pressure distributions, wake formation, and vortex evolution, which are critical to assessing the and torque performance of the ASWT under steady wind conditions.

Computational domain of ASWT

The computational domain for the ASWT was established in ANSYS CFX 2020 R2, a CFD solver capable of accurately reproducing wind tunnel environments with high geometric resolution. To replicate the conditions of the AUT wind tunnel, the numerical domain was configured to match the actual test section dimensions. In addition, the same ASWT geometric model used in the experimental setup was employed in the CFD simulations, with no geometric scaling or dimensional modifications applied. This ensured full geometric consistency between the numerical and experimental configurations and enabled a direct comparison of the aerodynamic results. The ASWT geometry was initially created in SolidWorks 2020 and exported in STEP format to preserve geometric accuracy and ensure full compatibility with ANSYS modules. After import, the model was refined in ANSYS Design Modeler, where the computational boundaries were defined, and the domain was prepared for meshing28,39,40.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, the CFD setup consisted of two regions: a rotating domain enclosing the rotor and a stationary domain representing the test section of the wind tunnel. The rotating domain extended to 1.04L and 1.04D, slightly larger than the rotor dimensions to promote fully developed flow and prevent boundary-induced distortions30,31,41. The stationary region measured 600 mm × 305 mm × 305 mm, corresponding precisely to the physical dimensions of the wind tunnel test section. The turbine axis was positioned 74.5 mm from the side walls to minimize blockage effects and prevent vortex interference near the domain boundaries.

Sectional view of a computational domain.

A frozen rotor interface was applied to couple the rotating and stationary zones, enabling steady-state data transfer across the interface and ensuring numerical stability. The central alignment of the rotor within the stationary domain replicated the experimental setup, maintaining symmetrical inflow and uniform boundary influence42–44. This configuration ensured improved convergence, minimized numerical dissipation, and enhanced the agreement between simulation and experimental results.

Computational modeling

An accurate definition of boundary conditions is essential for predicting the aerodynamic performance of the ASWT. The computational domain was modeled as isothermal, with air treated as a continuous, incompressible fluid under standard atmospheric conditions. Six inlet velocities, ranging from 5 to 10 m/s, were applied sequentially to evaluate turbine behavior under different operating conditions. A uniform velocity inlet was specified, while the outlet was set to a zero-gauge pressure condition to allow free flow discharge and prevent backpressure effects46,50–56.

The rotor blades were assigned no-slip wall boundary conditions to capture near-wall viscous effects accurately. The TSR varied between 0.5 and 3, incremented in steps of 0.5, allowing evaluation of torque and efficiency across a broad operational range. The frozen rotor interface was employed to couple the stationary and rotating regions, enabling consistent data exchange between both domains.

To simulate rotor motion, the Moving Reference Frame (MRF) technique in ANSYS CFX 2020 R2 was employed. This method accounts for the relative motion between the rotating rotor and the stationary flow domain by introducing additional momentum source terms into the governing equations, enabling a steady-state representation of rotational effects with high computational efficiency. An absolute velocity formulation was adopted, whereby the flow field is solved in a stationary reference frame while incorporating the effects of rotation through the MRF source terms. The rotating domain encloses the turbine rotor, whereas the stationary domain represents the wind tunnel test section.

The steady-state MRF approach was adopted to evaluate the time-averaged aerodynamic performance of ASWT, focusing on global performance indicators such as and across a wide range of , blade numbers, and TSR. This formulation is particularly appropriate at low and moderate TSR, where the flow remains predominantly quasi-steady and governed by pressure loading and mean momentum exchange rather than strong time-dependent effects. Although transient simulations are capable of resolving unsteady phenomena such as vortex shedding and wake fluctuations, these effects tend to primarily influence instantaneous flow structures and are expected to have a limited impact on the averaged performance metrics considered in this study. Moreover, performing fully transient simulations for all investigated configurations would entail a prohibitive computational cost. The suitability of the adopted steady-state approach is further supported by the strong agreement between numerical predictions and experimental measurements, confirming that the dominant aerodynamic interactions are accurately captured36,37,46,52,57,58.

The governing equations were solved using the steady-state, incompressible RANS equations with SST k–ω turbulence closure. Spatial discretization was performed using FVM, allowing accurate computation of torque, pressure, and force distributions on the rotor blades. The SST k–ω model was adopted to resolve turbulence, particularly in regions of flow separation9,38,41,50,52. The SST approach merges the advantages of the k–ε model in the free-stream region and the k–ω formulation near walls, enhancing predictive stability29,36,59–61.

A high-resolution upwind scheme was employed for spatial discretization, while the COUPLED algorithm ensured robust pressure–velocity coupling in solving the momentum and turbulence equations. The General Grid Interface (GGI) handled data transfer between the rotating and stationary meshes45,50,62,63. Solution convergence was monitored through residual tracking, with a root mean square (RMS) criterion of 1 × 10⁻4 for all governing variables. The maximum number of iterations was capped at 20,000 to ensure numerical stability41,62,64. Experimental setup

The aerodynamic performance of the ASWT configurations was experimentally verified through a series of controlled wind tunnel tests. To ensure consistency between the experimental observations and numerical simulations, the investigation focused on key measurable parameters, including airflow velocity, rotor rotational speed, and the corresponding electrical power output. These measurements provided a reliable basis for validating the computational model and identifying opportunities for further design refinement.

The experimental setup, including the F100 subsonic wind tunnel at ATU and the associated measurement equipment, is shown in Fig. 8. Experiments were conducted in this wind tunnel, which is located at the College of Engineering, ATU. The tunnel’s test section is a rectangular chamber enclosed by 10 mm-thick transparent polycarbonate panels mounted on a curved metallic frame. It measures 600 mm in length and 305 mm in both width and height, ensuring uniform airflow distribution throughout the test zone. The transparent configuration allows direct visual monitoring of the model during operation and simplifies adjustment of setup parameters when required.

The F100 subsonic wind tunnel et al.-Furat Al-Awsat Technical University (ATU) with experimental equipment.

All experimental measurements were conducted in an open-circuit wind tunnel, in which blockage effects are generally less pronounced than in closed-circuit facilities. The ASWT model was centrally positioned within the test section to ensure uniform inflow conditions and to minimize wall-induced interference.

The blockage ratio (Br), defined as the ratio of the rotor swept area to the test-section cross-sectional area, was approximately 19%. For VAWT tested in subsonic wind tunnels similar to the present study, suitable Br thresholds under 30% have been adopted13,14. For comparison, ASWT has reported an adequate Br threshold under 30%30, diffuser-equipped HAWT has been tested with acceptable Br limits below 30%16, and HAWT rotors suggest a recommended Br threshold under 25%17,66. The value of Br used in the present experiments is below the Br limits reported in the literature, as outlined above. This indicates that blockage effects are expected to have a limited and systematic influence on the aerodynamic results. Therefore, no explicit blockage corrections or adjustments were applied to the aerodynamic measurements.

To ensure consistency between experimental and numerical analyses, the computational domain in the CFD simulations was designed to match the dimensions of the experimental test section. This approach inherently accounts for comparable blockage conditions in both methodologies, enabling a meaningful comparison between numerical and experimental results. Consequently, while absolute performance values may be influenced to some extent by wind tunnel confinement, relative performance trends and configuration comparisons remain reliable.

The current understanding of blockage effects for ASWT is largely inferred from studies on other wind turbine configurations, as no dedicated investigations on acceptable blockage limits for ASWT have been reported to date. In the absence of such dedicated studies, the commonly adopted blockage limits for geometrically and aerodynamically comparable wind turbine configurations are therefore used here as conservative reference values.

A variety of techniques are available for determining turbine output power, including torque-based systems, mechanical braking mechanisms, and electrical output measurements13,15,49,67–69. In the present work, the electrical measurement approach was selected due to its high stability, repeatability, and suitability for the confined laboratory environment70–78.

To ensure precision and consistency, a custom-designed load control board was incorporated into the test circuit. This unit enables fine adjustment of the generator’s electrical load and continuous recording of voltage and current under systematically varied operating conditions. By incrementally applying controlled resistive loads to the DC generator, the board recorded the corresponding electrical response at different rotor speeds, providing a reliable means of evaluating the turbine’s mechanical and aerodynamic performance.

The complete experimental test rig was designed to deliver accurate, repeatable, and structurally stable measurements of the ASWT output characteristics. As shown in Fig. 9, the rig consists of a rectangular mounting platform fixed to the tunnel’s lower transparent panels and equipped with two vertical columns (152 mm high) for structural support. A ball bearing is installed on the front column, while a three-phase generator is mounted on the rear one. The central shaft, supported by the front bearing and directly connected to the generator at the back, carries the rotor assembly between the two supports. This configuration ensured precise alignment, smooth power transmission, and minimal vibration during testing.

Experimental test rig setup and load control and measurement board for ASWT.

The brushless three-phase generator used in the setup consists of a rotor embedded with permanent magnets and a stator containing nine copper coils. The alternating current (AC) produced by the generator is converted into direct current (DC) via a three-phase full-wave bridge rectifier composed of six diodes. Although this configuration offers high efficiency and lower conversion losses than single-phase systems, it introduces small voltage ripples in the DC output. To smooth these fluctuations, a filtering capacitor is installed immediately after the rectifier to stabilize the voltage and current by storing charge during peaks and discharging during troughs.

All structural and rotor components were designed using SolidWorks 2020 and exported in STL format for additive manufacturing on a Creatbot D600 Pro 3D printer. Polylactic acid (PLA) filament was selected due to its high strength-to-weight ratio, dimensional stability, and capability to withstand aerodynamic stress under the experimental flow conditions. The printed components faithfully replicated the digital geometry, ensuring accurate aerodynamic behavior in the wind tunnel.

To account for measurement uncertainty, each data point was averaged over multiple readings, and the corresponding standard deviations were computed to ensure statistical reliability of the recorded performance data.

This step establishes the CFD model as a reliable tool for evaluating ASWT performance and supports its application in future design and optimization studies.

As described earlier, the power output of a wind turbine is governed by two primary parameters: torque and rotor angular velocity. In this study, the angular velocity was measured directly in RPM using a tachometer (DT-2234B), while the was determined indirectly from the electrical output of the generator coupled to the rotor. This method provides a practical and reliable means of evaluating turbine performance, offering high measurement stability, straightforward implementation, and good repeatability under controlled laboratory conditions.

At each load level, the voltage and current were measured using a digital display, voltmeter, and ammeter, while the rotor speed was recorded with a tachometer. Performance results of blade number variation

Building upon the previous analysis of blade geometry, which established the influence of on aerodynamic efficiency, this section examines the effect of blade number on the aerodynamic performance of the ASWT. Unlike geometric parameters that primarily determine local flow features around individual blades, the blade count controls the overall flow distribution, aerodynamic loading, and torque generation on the rotor. This parameter is closely related to rotor solidity, the ratio of total blade area to the swept area, which directly affects both energy capture and operational stability. In general, increasing the number of blades enhances starting torque and improves energy extraction at lower wind speeds due to the greater surface area exposed to the flow. Conversely, reducing the blade count decreases solidity, reducing aerodynamic interference and favoring higher rotational speeds and improved efficiency at elevated TSR. Analyzing ASWT configurations with varying blade numbers thus provides essential insight into optimizing the balance between aerodynamic efficiency and operational stability across different wind conditions. In this investigation, the optimized PR-5 configuration was retained as the baseline geometry, while the blade number was systematically varied from two to six to evaluate its influence over a range of TSR. The objective was to identify the configuration that achieves the most favorable balance between aerodynamic efficiency, torque generation, and adaptability under varying operating regimes. Analysis of pressure and velocity fields

The examination of pressure and velocity contours offers critical insight into the aerodynamic flow behavior surrounding the ASWT under different blade number configurations. This analysis aims to interpret how variations in blade count influence flow acceleration, pressure loading, and wake development, key factors governing the overall aerodynamic performance of the turbine.

Numerical simulations were conducted for all rotor configurations (ASWT-2B, ASWT-3B, ASWT-4B, ASWT-5B, and ASWT-6B) under identical boundary conditions, with a uniform inlet velocity of 10 m/s to ensure consistent comparison. The resulting contour distributions reveal the evolution of pressure and velocity fields around the turbine blades, emphasizing the effects of increasing blade number on flow uniformity, wake structure, and the pressure gradient across the rotor surface.

These visualizations establish a qualitative framework for interpreting the influence of blade-number variation on the overall aerodynamic behavior of the ASWT. The contour plots presented in this section form the basis for the subsequent discussion of flow structures, pressure distributions, and their correlation with aerodynamic performance metrics.

Figure 14 illustrates the pressure contours for the ASWT rotors with two to six blades at an inlet velocity of 10 m/s. Across all configurations, the characteristic pressure field of the ASWT rotor is evident, featuring a high-pressure region on the windward (pressure) side and a corresponding low-pressure zone on the leeward (suction) side of each blade. The magnitude and distribution of these regions govern the torque generation capability and overall aerodynamic efficiency of the turbine.

Pressure contours for ASWT rotors with different blade numbers at a velocity of 10 m/s.

For the two-blade configuration (PR-5-2B), the pressure difference between the suction and pressure surfaces is relatively weak and spatially non-uniform, leading to reduced aerodynamic loading and early wake expansion. As the number of blades increases to PR-5-3B, the contours reveal a well-defined and uniformly distributed pressure gradient across each blade, with strong low-pressure zones on the suction side and compact high-pressure regions on the windward side. This balanced distribution enhances lift generation while minimizing adverse pressure gradients and flow separation, resulting in a more stable and efficient aerodynamic response.

The four-blade model (PR-5-4B) maintains a similar pressure pattern but exhibits signs of mild flow interference between adjacent blades, reducing the effective pressure differential. For higher blade counts (PR-5-5B and PR-5-6B), the interaction among pressure fields becomes more pronounced, forming overlapping regions of compressed flow and enlarged low-pressure wakes. This behavior indicates excessive solidity, which diminishes the pressure contrast and weakens the net aerodynamic torque.

Overall, the PR-5-3B configuration demonstrates the most favorable pressure distribution, characterized by a pronounced yet stable pressure differential between the suction and pressure sides, limited wake expansion, and balanced aerodynamic loading. These attributes confirm its superior performance observed in the power and torque analyses, validating the three-blade rotor as the optimal design for efficient energy extraction under the tested conditions.

These characteristics directly enhance torque generation and explain the superior performance observed at the optimal operating TSR, where the balance between pressure gradients and flow stability governs the aerodynamic efficiency of the rotor.

The combined analysis of static pressure, velocity, and total-pressure contours offers an integrated perspective on the aerodynamic behavior of the ASWT across different blade-number configurations. As the number of blades increases, the rotor experiences higher solidity, leading to intensified inter-blade interference, wake broadening, and a gradual loss of pressure uniformity. These effects weaken the effective pressure gradients acting on the blades, reduce torque generation, and ultimately limit the efficiency of momentum transfer and energy recovery. Solidity-driven mechanisms, such as increased wake interaction and reduced aerodynamic effectiveness at higher blade counts, have been widely reported in hybrid and assisted Darrieus-based VAWT operating at low to moderate TSR. These mechanisms provide a consistent physical framework for interpreting the present ASWT results.

In contrast, PR-5-3B demonstrates a distinctly balanced aerodynamic field, characterized by smooth pressure gradients, coherent wake development, and a well-distributed total-pressure pattern along the blade surfaces. This flow structure promotes stable lift generation and sustains torque production while minimizing flow separation and turbulence. Comparable flow-field characteristics have been associated with optimized blade-number and solidity configurations in Darrieus rotors, where a balance between torque enhancement and wake stability is essential for achieving improved performance at low TSR. As a result, the PR-5-3B configuration achieves superior power and torque performance at the optimal operating TSR, as observed in the corresponding and trends.

These flow characteristics provide a physical explanation for the trends observed in the and curves across the full range of TSR. In particular, the balanced pressure distribution and coherent wake structure of the three-blade configuration explain its consistently superior performance over a broad operating range, while the increased flow interference in higher-solidity designs and the reduced loading in lower-solidity configurations account for their respective performance limitations at different TSR regimes. Such trade-offs between blade number, rotor solidity, and low-TSR aerodynamic behavior are well documented in the broader literature on VAWT, reinforcing the generality of the mechanisms identified in the present ASWT study.

The results presented in this study confirm that ASWT exhibits favorable aerodynamic characteristics for operation under low wind speed and multidirectional flow conditions. The spiral blade geometry, combined with a multi-blade configuration, promotes stable torque generation at low TSR, eliminates the need for active yaw mechanisms, and contributes to reduced acoustic emissions. These aerodynamic advantages make ASWT particularly attractive for small-scale, urban, and distributed wind energy applications; however, they are inherently linked to specific geometric and operational features that also influence practical implementation.

In addition to aerodynamic performance, the practical viability of ASWT is strongly influenced by their geometric and operational characteristics. The high-solidity, multi-blade configuration that enables effective torque generation at low wind speeds also leads to increased aerodynamic and structural loads on the blades, particularly as wind speed increases. Moreover, the continuous three-dimensional spiral geometry introduces additional manufacturing complexity compared with conventional straight-bladed turbines, requiring careful consideration of fabrication methods and material selection. These practical constraints mirror challenges previously identified in hybrid rotor concepts, further supporting the interpretation of ASWT as a horizontal-axis adaptation of established low-TSR, hybrid lift–drag turbine principles. Accordingly, understanding where ASWT stands relative to other wind turbine technologies in terms of manufacturability and practical implementation is essential for assessing their realistic integration into modern energy systems.
