Usefulness of Mavacamten in the Challenging Association of Aortic Stenosis and Obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Pierre Vanhaecke, Yohann Bohbot, Chloé Di Lena, Emilion Hucleux, Mokrane Yemmi, Chistophe Tribouilloy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe association of aortic stenosis (AS) and obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy raises questions about AS quantification, the management of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, and AS treatment. Recently, mavacamten demonstrated its efficacy in reducing left ventricular outflow tract obstruction gradient. This case reports on mavacamten use in this challenging association.Key Words: aortic valve, cardiomyopathy, echocardiography

History of Presentation

An 85-year-old woman was referred to our heart valve team for severe aortic stenosis (AS) and pre-transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) assessment. She was symptomatic, with NYHA functional class III exertional dyspnea and dizziness. Physical examination revealed an intense systolic murmur.

Past Medical History

The patient had severe renal failure and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease but no history of arterial hypertension.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial differential diagnosis was severe AS, because the left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) could lead to an overestimation of the aortic mean gradient, and it was difficult to differentiate moderate AS from true severe AS. Another differential diagnosis was the overestimation of LVOTO by considering the mitral regurgitation jet in continuous Doppler.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed asymmetrical septal hypertrophy of 25 mm, a moderately calcified aortic valve, and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 70%. The mitral valve was calcified, with moderate mitral regurgitation but no evidence of systolic anterior motion of the mitral leaflets. LVOTO related to septal hypertrophy and left ventricular hypercontractility was severe, with a peak gradient of 101 mm Hg (Figure 1A). Continuous wave Doppler through the aortic valve showed a peak aortic velocity of 4.4 m/s and a mean gradient of 56 mm Hg, consistent with severe AS (Figure 1B). The left ventricular outflow tract velocity time integral measurement was unreliable for the estimation of aortic valve area (AVA) using the continuity equation due to LVOTO (Video 1). Cardiac magnetic resonance confirmed the hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) diagnosis, with a maximum septal thickness of 27 mm and the absence of focal fibrosis (Figure 2A). There was no evidence of cardiac amyloidosis by cardiac magnetic resonance or bone scintigraphy.

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) showed significant aliasing just upstream of the septum, without systolic anterior motion (Figure 3A), and a moderately calcified tricuspid aortic valve. The AVA was measured at 1.14 cm2 by TEE and 1.39 cm2 by cardiac computed tomography (Figures 3B and 3C). The calcium score was 1,134, in a gray zone between likely and unlikely severe AS according to guidelines.1 Despite a mean aortic valve gradient >50 mm Hg, we concluded that the AS was moderate and assumed that the symptoms were related to obstructive HCM.

Management

The patient was initially treated with beta-blockers (bisoprolol 10 mg), but this did not reduce the LVOTO, and she remained symptomatic. Given the high surgical risk of this 85-year-old patient, alcohol septal ablation was performed (Figure 2B). It was limited by the coronary anatomy, because the first septal artery was occluded, and alcohol was injected into the second septal artery. This procedure was complicated by a high-degree atrioventricular block treated by permanent pacing. Three months later, there was no significant change in the LVOTO gradient (85 mm Hg) or aortic valve mean gradient (50 mm Hg) (Figure 4). In December 2023, mavacamten became available in France. Initiation of mavacamten 5 mg resulted in dramatic symptomatic improvement to NYHA functional class I and the resolution of dizziness. One-month follow-up transthoracic echocardiography showed a normal LVOTO peak gradient of 7 mm Hg and a preserved ejection fraction (65%). Because there was no longer any significant LVOTO, moderate aortic valve stenosis was confirmed, with a mean gradient of 32 mm Hg, a peak velocity of 3.4 m/s, and an AVA of 1.20 cm2 by the continuity equation (Figure 5, Video 2).

Discussion

The association of obstructive HCM and AS raises diagnostic and therapeutic issues. Echocardiographic quantification of AS severity in the presence of obstructive HCM is challenging. LVOTO compromises the Doppler hemodynamic assessment of AS, leading to an overestimation of the aortic valve mean gradient and peak velocity. Bernoulli’s simplified equation, used to approximate the aortic valve gradient, assumes a maximum left ventricular outflow tract velocity of <1.5 m/s, which is not the case in the presence of significant LVOTO.2 The continuity equation cannot be used to estimate AVA either, due to the acceleration of subaortic flow and the loss of its laminar nature. Therefore, in this context, multimodality imaging is valuable for assessing AS severity independently of hemodynamic status. The anatomic AVA is approached by direct planimetry by computed tomography and/or TEE. However, this measurement is difficult, requires experience, and the limit values are not well established because the anatomic AVA is greater than the functional AVA. The noncontrast aortic valve calcium score is useful in this situation due to its independence from hemodynamic status.1

Correct assessment of the severity of AS and LVOTO is crucial because this has implications for therapeutic decisions. The best option for symptomatic patients with low surgical risk with significant LVOTO and moderate or severe AS appears to be aortic valve replacement and concomitant surgical myectomy.3 However, the therapeutic strategy is not well established for patients with high surgical risk, such as the current patient, and is often difficult to determine. During the TAVR procedure, LVOTO, when present, can lead to migration or suboptimal placement of the prosthesis. After the procedure, immediate afterload reduction by TAVR may significantly increase LVOTO, which can lead to serious clinical events (eg, suicidal left ventricle, cardiogenic shock, death).4 Therefore, proper LVOTO gradient management before TAVR is crucial for these patients. Treatment with beta-blockers is often disappointing, with only a limited effect on the LVOTO, and percutaneous invasive treatment by alcohol ablation is usually proposed. Khan et al5 reported that alcohol septal ablation before TAVR is an interesting option for limiting TAVR complications. However, in an elderly population with comorbidities, this invasive procedure can lead to serious complications, including conduction disturbances, pericardial effusion, stroke, and induced infarct complications. Moreover, this procedure is not always effective, as in the case of this patient, and depends on the coronary anatomy and the mechanism of obstruction. Recently, the EXPLORER-HCM trial demonstrated the effect of mavacamten, a cardiac myosin inhibitor, on symptom improvement for patients with obstructive HCM.6 This study enrolled symptomatic patients with a peak LVOTO gradient ≥50 mm Hg, and mavacamten was effective in reducing the gradient. We report the first case, to our knowledge, of LVOTO gradient reduction after mavacamten treatment in this particular combination of HCM and AS. This case report highlights 2 strengths of this therapeutic strategy: 1) the avoidance of misclassification of AS severity by reducing the LVOTO gradient, enabling the use of the continuity equation and Bernoulli’s assumptions; and 2) the medical treatment of LVOTO in preparation for TAVR in an aging population with an increased risk of complications associated with invasive treatment or surgery.

Conclusions

The association of obstructive HCM and AS is a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge, particularly for patients with a high surgical risk. Multimodality imaging is essential for better assessment of AS severity. Surgical or percutaneous management requires discussion by the heart team. When a TAVR is being considered, LVOTO reduction is necessary before TAVR, and mavacamten appears to be safer than alcohol septal ablation and should probably be discussed as first-line treatment in this situation.
