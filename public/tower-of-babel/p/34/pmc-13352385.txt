Assessing the impact of dietary interventions on the resistomes of broiler chickens☆
Authors: Ana Fonseca, Sophia Kenney, Stephanie Bierly, John Boney, Erika Ganda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major One Health concern, and while natural feed additives such as probiotics and phytotherapeutics are increasingly used as alternatives to antimicrobial growth promoters (AGPs) in poultry production, their potential effects on the selection of antibiotic resistance genes remain poorly understood. Therefore, our objective was to characterize the effects of a probiotic and an essential oils blend on the broiler resistome. Cobb 500 1-day-old chicks (N=320) were randomly allocated in 32 cages, with eight replicates of ten broilers per cage per treatment and were raised until day 21. Treatments consisted of four diets: a basal diet (negative control), a basal diet with Bacitracin Methylene Disalicylate (BMD) at 50 g/ton, a basal diet with an essential oil blend at 100 g/ton, and a basal diet with a probiotic (Bacillus subtilis) at 226.8 g/ton. Excreta samples were collected at three-time points (1, 10, and 21 days) to characterize broilers’ resistome. The DNA extracted from these samples was sequenced using shotgun metagenomics on the NovaSeq platform and statistical analyses were done using Kruskal-Wallis and PERMANOVA to assess gene diversity. Across all samples, 823 unique ARGs were identified. These genes spanned a broad spectrum of classes, including multi-compound, metals, drugs, and biocides resistance. No significant differences in alpha diversity of these genes (P = 0.51) were observed between treatment groups; however, AMR gene diversity varied by age (P < 0.001). A statistically significant difference was observed in beta diversity across ages (P = 0.001), but not between treatments (P = 0.95). While age impacted AMR gene diversity, under our experimental conditions, antibiotics or other in-feed additives did not significantly alter broiler resistomes. This study advances poultry AMR surveillance by demonstrating that resistome diversity and composition in broiler chickens are predominantly shaped by age-dependent microbial succession, while neither in-feed antibiotics nor non-antibiotic feed additives induced persistent or treatment-specific alterations in ARG profiles under the conditions tested.Keywords: Feed additives, Resistome, Antimicrobial resistance, One health

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major concern in poultry production due to its implications for animal health, therapeutic efficacy, and the dissemination of resistance determinants through the food chain (Koorakula et al., 2022; Abreu et al., 2023). AMR is a natural process that emerges from bacterial evolution and competition, through mechanisms such as antimicrobial inactivation, target modification, reduced membrane permeability, and efflux-mediated exclusion (Sultan et al., 2018; Schrader et al., 2023). These traits are frequently encoded by antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) which can be located on mobile genetic elements (MGEs), including plasmids, integrons, and transposons, enabling horizontal gene transfer among enteric bacteria such as Salmonella and Escherichia coli (Carattoli, 2013; Partridge et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2019; Schrader et al., 2023).

While antimicrobial resistance has traditionally been evaluated on single isolates through in vitro antibiotic susceptibility testing, the prevalence of metagenomics has shifted attention toward the resistome - the totality of a microbial community’s ARGs, their precursors, and associated MGEs (Wright, 2007; Kim and Cha, 2021). Unlike culture-based resistance monitoring, resistome profiling captures intrinsic, acquired, dormant, and proto-resistance genes, offering a more comprehensive assessment of the evolutionary and transmission potential of AMR within the production chain. The resistome framework is particularly relevant in poultry production, where high-density animal rearing, rapid microbial turnover, and continuous environmental exposure facilitate horizontal gene transfer and enable ARGs to persist across the broader microbial community, not only in pathogenic bacteria (Wright, 2007; Koorakula et al., 2022). This systems-level perspective aligns with the One Health paradigm in which poultry-associated resistomes are not limited as an on-farm risk, but are capable of disseminating through animal waste, litter, processing environments, and, ultimately, to human food (Thänert et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2023; Abou-Jaoudeh et al., 2024). Thus, understanding how nutritional strategies, antimicrobial use, and production practices modulate the broiler resistome is central to mitigating ARG flow, and therefore potential AMR, at the animal-environment-human interface.

Historically, subtherapeutic antimicrobial growth promoters (AGPs) were widely implemented to enhance growth rate, feed conversion, and intestinal integrity in broilers. More recent regulatory action such as the U.S. FDA restrictions of medically important antimicrobials for growth promotion (AGP) and the institution of the Veterinary Feed Directive have increased the regulations on AGP’s use (Stokka, 2016; Food and Drug Administration, 2021). Consequently, AGP withdrawal has been associated with increased enteric disease, compromised performance, and elevated production costs, prompting the investigation of alternative feed additives with functional equivalency (Yang et al., 2021; Buiatte et al., 2022; da Silva et al., 2024).

Probiotics and plant-derived essential oils have emerged as leading AGP replacements due to their capacity to modulate intestinal microbiota, enhance mucosal immunity, and improve nutrient utilization (Hill et al., 2014; Leyva-López et al., 2017; Chang et al., 2020; Fonseca et al., 2024). For example, Bacillus spores support the proliferation of commensal taxa involved in short-chain fatty acid synthesis. Additionally, essential oils rich in phenolic compounds like carvacrol and thymol exert antimicrobial and antioxidant activity, with documented beneficial effects on performance, microbiota structure, and gut morphology (Corduk et al., 2013; Rivera-Pérez et al., 2021; Ayalew et al., 2022; Ge et al., 2023; Fonseca et al., 2024). Despite extensive evaluation of these products at the production, physiological, and microbiome levels, their effects on the resistome are not well characterized.

The present study assessed the impact of a Bacillus-based probiotic and an essential oil blend on broiler excreta resistome composition in comparison with a basal diet and a conventional AGP treatment. We hypothesized that dietary intervention would differentially change ARGs diversity and structure of broilers’ excreta microbiome. This work provides insight into the consequences of antibiotic-free dietary strategies on AMR dynamics in modern poultry.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

All animal procedures were approved by the Pennsylvania State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (PROTO202101779) and conducted at the Poultry Education and Research Center (PERC). The performance outcomes, bacterial and fungal excreta microbiota were previously published (Fonseca et al., 2024). The present study specifically evaluates the excreta resistome at three biologically relevant time points: day 1 (placement), day 10 (starter-to-grower), and day 21 (end of trial). A total of 320 one-day-old male Cobb 500 broilers were individually weighed upon arrival and randomly assigned to 32 cages, with 10 birds per cage, in a completely randomized block design with eight replicates per treatment. Birds were raised under standardized management and environmental conditions from d1 to d21. All experimental diets were formulated as corn-soybean based diet. Four dietary treatments were tested: a non-supplemented basal diet, the basal diet supplemented with a Bacillus subtilis probiotic at 5 × 10⁵ CFU/g, 226g/ton [Calpis America, Inc., Peachtree City, GA] the basal diet supplemented with an essential oil blend containing oregano, rosemary, and red pepper at 100 g/ton [Ew Nutrition, Adel, IA] and the basal diet supplemented with bacitracin methylene disalicylate at 50 g/ton [Zoetis, Parsippany, NJ] as a conventional antimicrobial growth promoter control. Feed and water were provided ad libitum for the duration of the study.

Sample collection

Chicken excreta (fecal or cecal droppings) samples were collected on a daily-basis by placing sterile paper liners beneath each cage for a 90-minute period beginning at 07:00 h. The collected material consisted of excreta from all 10 broilers within each cage, generating one composite sample per replicate that was transferred into sterile Whirl-Pak® bags and stored at −80°C until further processing.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Prior to DNA extraction, samples were randomized, thawed, and preprocessed by adding 50 mL of molecular-grade water (Intermountain Life Science, UT) to each Whirl-Pak® bag containing the excreta papers, followed by 2 min homogenization in a Stomacher® 400 Circulator. From each homogenate, 4 mL was transferred to microcentrifuge tubes for storage. Genomic DNA was extracted from 200 µL of homogenate using the MagMAX™ Microbiome Ultra Nucleic Acid Isolation Kit on the KingFisher platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Each extraction batch included a ZymoBIOMICS microbial community standard as a positive control and a reagent-only negative control. Additional controls included an environmental paper control (paper placed in an empty cage) and an autoclaved paper control. From the 320 birds sampled, a subset of days 1, 10, and 21 samples and all controls were selected for shotgun metagenomic sequencing. DNA quality and concentration were assessed via Nanodrop, and samples were shipped on dry ice to Novogene (Sacramento, CA) for library preparation and sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Shotgun metagenomic reads had quality control and length trimming performed using FastQC v0.11.9, and Trimmomatic v0.39 tools (Bolger et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2018). Paired-end reads were trimmed to remove low-quality bases and adapter sequences, a sliding window approach was applied, whereby a 4-base window was trimmed if the average Phred quality score dropped below 15. Leading and trailing bases were removed if their Phred scores were below 3. Reads shorter than 36 bases after trimming were discarded. Adapter contamination was removed using the TruSeq3-PE-2.fa adapter file provided by Trimmomatic package (Chen et al., 2018).

Host-associated reads were removed with BWA-MEM aligning to the Gallus gallus reference genome assembly GCA_016699485. The remaining high-quality, host-filtered reads were then used for antimicrobial resistance profiling with AMRPlusPlus v1.0, coupled with the MEGARes 2.0 resistance gene database (Lakin et al., 2017; Bonin et al., 2023) and downstream antimicrobial resistance gene (ARG) classification and annotation were conducted using ResistomeAnalyzer (Bonin et al., 2023). For each sequencing batch, AMRPlusPlus produced count matrices of detected ARGs along with categorical assignments across the MEGARes hierarchical ontology (gene, mechanism, class, and broadclass). These matrices were subsequently aggregated into a unified dataset in R v4.4.2 for downstream statistical, ecological, and compositional analyses.

Resistance gene alpha diversity (Shannon and Observed ASVs) was compared across treatments and ages using Kruskal-Wallis. Beta diversity (Aitchison distances) was evaluated via PERMANOVA (Adonis test, 10,000 permutations) with Center Log Ratio (CLR) transformed data that was visualized by NMDS using the microViz package (Barnett et al., 2021). Pairwise comparisons were calculated using the vegan package (Oksanen et al., 2022). Differential abundance analysis across age and treatments was performed using ALDEx2 (Dirichlet-multinomial model to infer abundance from counts) (Nixon et al., 2022). Differences were deemed significant at P ≤ 0.05 and to control for multiple comparisons, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate procedure (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995).

Results

Sequencing results

Before read preprocessing, the 214 shotgun metagenomic samples yielded an average of 26.46 million raw reads per sample. Following quality filtering and removal of host-derived sequences, approximately 22.8 million high-quality non-host reads per sample remained for downstream analysis. All raw sequencing data are available under BioProject accession number PRJNA928060. Across all samples, a total of 823 unique ARGs were identified. Owing to the comprehensive coverage of the MEGARes database, the detected genes encompassed a broad spectrum of resistance classes, including multi-compound, metal, drug, and biocide resistance across treatments and ages.

ARG relative abundance

The excreta resistome was consistently dominated by genes conferring resistance to biocides, metals, and clinically relevant drugs, with a similar proportional structure observed in birds fed the Basal Diet, Antibiotic, Probiotic, and Essential oils treatments and some changes across age as shown in Fig. 1 (B and C). Within the biocide resistance category, multi-biocide resistance genes represented approximately half of all biocide-associated ARGs (48 – 52%), followed by acid resistance (21 – 24%), acetate resistance (11%), peroxide resistance (8%), and phenolic compound resistance (5 – 6%). A comparable distribution was observed for metal resistance genes, where multi-metal resistance accounted for approximately 48% of all metal-associated ARGs, followed by copper (21 – 24%), nickel (7%), and arsenic (6 – 7%) resistance. For drug resistance genes, β-lactam resistance was the most abundant class (29 – 32%), followed by aminoglycosides (20 – 21%), tetracyclines (18 – 21%), and macrolide-lincosamide-streptogramin (MLS) resistance (15 – 17%). Despite the differences in feed exposure, the relative abundance of resistance classes within each broad category remained conserved across treatments.

To evaluate whether dietary interventions influenced the drug-associated resistome, we examined Z-score–normalized abundance profiles of drug-resistance classes across treatments (Antibiotic, Basal Diet, Essential oils, and Probiotic) and ages (d1, d10, d21) (Fig. 1(A)). Overall, the heatmap revealed highly consistent drug-resistance patterns across treatments, with no major class-level shifts attributable to any dietary intervention. Interestingly, samples in the Antibiotic group did not exhibit broad enrichment of drug-resistance classes, instead, most categories displayed neutral or reduced relative abundance. A closer examination of Antibiotic samples at d10 and d21 showed moderate increases in trimethoprim, tetracycline, sulfonamide, and fosfomycin resistance classes. Profiles from the Essential oils and Probiotic groups were indistinguishable from one another, although the Essential oils group showed slightly higher relative abundances across bacitracin, cationic antimicrobial peptide, fluoroquinolone, glycopeptide, and metronidazole resistance classes compared to the other groups. Finally, the Basal Diet group exhibited relatively higher abundances in several drug-resistance categories, including phenicol, sulfonamide, and bacitracin resistance.

Differential relative abundance

To better understand the resistome and the implications of drug-associated resistance in poultry production, we focused specifically on genes classified under the drug resistance category. Fig. 2 (A) presents the 20 most differentially relatively abundant drug-resistance genes across d1, d10, and d21, highlighting clear age-associated shifts in AMR gene abundance. Several drug-resistance genes including TET34, SULR, ABAF, OXA, and APH3-PRIME were differentially relatively less abundant at d1 compared with d10, indicating an early-life deficit in these resistance genes that increases as the microbiome matures. In contrast, genes such as VANRL, CBLA, and DFRD were more differentially relatively abundant at d10 relative to d1, suggesting that specific drug resistance mechanisms become enriched during early chicks’ development. Comparisons between d1 to d21 confirmed the maturation-driven patterns where several genes such as BLAEC, CME, OXA, LSA, MUS, CFRB, FUSC and ANT9 were differentially relatively less abundant at d1 whereas MEFE and CFR were relatively more abundant at d21. Moreover, comparing d10 to d21, we observed other genes that were differentially relatively more abundant on d21 including AQU, MPHC, TETD, ERM30, BLAPLA, and ABAF. No statistical differences were seen between treatments. Fig. 2 (B) shows the mean CLR-transformed relative abundances of different genes across treatments. For example, OXA, and ANT9 were slightly higher in Antibiotic-treated broiler excreta compared to the other treatments. TETM was higher in the probiotic than in the other groups.

No ARGs alpha diversity differences were observed between dietary treatments (Shannon: P = 0.51). The Basal Diet, Probiotic, and Essential oils groups exhibited similar patterns on the age-driven changes along the experiment period. ARG beta diversity

Beta diversity analyses revealed that age was the major determinant of resistome composition across all samples. NMDS ordination (Fig. 4 (A)) revealed clear age-driven (R² = 0.30, P < 0.001) clustering of resistome profiles, with samples from d1, d10, and d21 forming distinct and minimally overlapping groups. Pairwise PERMANOVA identified strong temporal restructuring of the gut resistome, with the largest community differences occurring between Day 1 and Day 10 (R² = 0.34, P < 0.01) and between d1 and d21 (R² = 0.29, P < 0.01), while differences between Day 10 and Day 21 were comparatively smaller (R² = 0.11, P < 0.01). We did not observe a treatment effect on excreta resistome (R² = 0.02, P = 0.99). However, an interaction between age and treatment was observed, where excreta from birds fed Probiotic had a different ARG profile than those fed Antibiotic at d10 (R² = 0.09, P = 0.04). A substantial overlap was observed among treatment groups (Fig. 4 (B)), indicating that dietary additives did not alter the overall structure of the AMR gene community. To further understand within-treatment temporal trajectories, centroids were plotted separately for each treatment (Fig. 4 (C)). We observed that centroid trajectories within each treatment revealed a consistent age-driven progression in resistome composition. In all treatments, the d1, d10, and d21 centroids shifted in a similar direction across the NMDS space, indicating a shared developmental trajectory irrespective of dietary intervention. The magnitude and direction of these shifts were nearly identical among the Essential oils, and Antibiotic groups, and a small difference between Basal Diet and Probiotic with the others demonstrating that treatment effects were minimal compared with the dominant influence of age.

Discussion

Antimicrobial resistance remains one of the most pressing challenges in modern poultry production, where high-density rearing, rapid microbial turnover, and continuous environmental exposure facilitate the amplification and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) across the animal-environment-human interface (Abou-Jaoudeh et al., 2024). As the poultry industry transitions away from medically important antimicrobial growth promoters (AGPs), there is an urgent need to understand how alternative feed additives influence the resistome, an essential consideration of One Health AMR stewardship (Wright, 2007; Kim and Cha, 2021; Schrader et al., 2023). In this study, we evaluated the impact of a Bacillus-based Probiotic and an Essential oil blend on broiler excreta resistome dynamics in comparison with a Basal Diet and a conventional AGP (BMD). Across 214 metagenomic samples, our results demonstrate that dietary additives exerted minimal influence on ARG composition, whereas age was the dominant driver of resistome structure, producing clear temporal shifts in gene diversity, relative abundance, and structure from d1 to d21. Despite their well-documented effects on microbiota composition and host physiology, neither the probiotic nor the essential oils altered overall ARG or ARG class-level distribution under the conditions of this study. Instead, maturation was associated with progressive enrichment of specific drug-resistance genes, indicating that resistome remodeling during early life is potentially shaped by ecological succession regardless of feed-based interventions. These findings underscore the importance of age-related resistome dynamics in broilers and highlight the need for AMR mitigation strategies that consider the natural developmental trajectory of the microbiome.

The strong age effect observed in this study aligns with the well-established concept that early-life microbial succession is a major determinant of functional gene repertoires in broilers (Awad et al., 2016; Proszkowiec-Weglarz et al., 2022; Fonseca et al., 2024). The largest shift in ARG composition occurred between d1 and d10, a period characterized by rapid changes in gut structure, nutrient acquisition, and microbial colonization (Hu et al., 2017; Kim and Cha, 2021). Although overall ARG alpha diversity was primarily age-driven, the Antibiotic treated birds exhibited a distinct temporal pattern characterized by a transient reduction in diversity from d1 to d10 followed by a sharp increase thereafter. According to Thänert et al. (2022), resistome composition is closely linked to the taxonomic structure of the microbiome. Therefore, the reduction in ARG diversity observed at d10 may reflect a temporary restructuring of the gut microbial community during a critical stage of microbiome development. Notably, this period coincides with the transition from initial colonization toward a more stable microbial community, suggesting a narrow window during which dietary interventions may exert the greatest influence on microbiome and resistome composition (Huang et al., 2023). This interpretation is further supported by our previous study, in which excreta bacterial alpha diversity also declined between days 9 and 11 in broilers receiving dietary additives (Fonseca et al., 2024). The subsequent recovery in ARG diversity observed after d10 suggests that these effects were transient and that resistome dynamics were primarily driven by age-related microbial maturation rather than persistent treatment-induced alterations (Dogra et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021; Thänert et al., 2022).

Across all samples, a total of 823 unique ARGs were identified. Together, these genes are associated with resistance to 211 drug classes. Relevant to poultry systems, 22 ARG are related to bacitracin or tetracycline resistance, both being antibiotic classes commonly administered as AGPs (Wicker et al., 1977; Crisol-Martínez et al., 2017; Roth et al., 2019). As the birds developed, we observed an enrichment of drug-resistance genes such as TET34, SULR, and ABAF, (Fig. 2(A)) likely reflecting expansion of early-colonizer facultative anaerobes capable of harboring these resistant genes. In a comprehensive study of ARG distribution in the infant gut, Li et al. (2021) showed that Escherichia coli is an important reservoir of ARGs and that low microbiome maturity is associated with high ARG load. Moreover, they also showed that the high ARG load originated not only from the environment and antibiotic exposure, but from infants’ mother. Interestingly, in our previous study we described the excreta microbial community using 16S rRNA and observed the presence of genera including Escherichia–Shigella and Enterococcus in early days (Fonseca et al., 2024). However, contrary to their study, here we observed a lower diversity of ARGs in the excreta resistome which correlates with fewer microbes colonizing the gut when the birds were younger indicating that vertical ARGs transference is likely low in younger chickens. Jansen et al. (2020) showed that while antibiotic residues can be vertically transferred from parent hens to eggs, shells, and subsequently to chicks, these residues are excreted rapidly, remain at very low levels in feathers, and are undetectable in tissues at market age, supporting limited vertical transmission of antimicrobial compounds and potential ARGs in chickens. By d21, the resistome exhibited signs of stabilization, with consistent enrichment of genes including MEFE, CFR, MPHC, and TETD (Fig. 2 (A)), suggesting that mature intestinal communities acquired ARG profiles distinct from those present at early stage, which may be explained by gut environment changes and immune system maturation. These data highlight that the broiler resistome is not static but undergoes systematic developmental changes, wherein chicken excreta seem to carry fewer ARGs acquired from the environment or the hens.

The feed additives in our study did not significantly alter the resistome of broiler excreta, supporting the meta-transcriptomic findings of Koorakula et al. (2022) wherein there was a limited overall impact of non-antibiotic feed additives on the gut resistome. While phytogenic feed additives did not increase resistome diversity and in some cases reduced the expression of several ARG classes in their study, our Essential oils and Probiotic treatments did not enrich drug-resistance categories and maintained resistome profiles comparable to the Basal Diet fed birds. Both studies therefore support the growing evidence that natural feed additives do not exacerbate the resistome and may help stabilize ARGs expression relative to antibiotic-based interventions. Additionally, Koorakula et al. (2022) found that tetracycline, aminoglycoside, and MLS resistance genes were consistently more abundant across treatments, including controls, which mirrors the stable chicken resistome observed in our study as shown in Fig. 1(B).

Although no significant changes in this study were observed, these findings should be interpreted within the context of the study duration. Resistome development is a dynamic process influenced by microbial succession and long-term selective pressures (Zhang et al., 2026), and the 21-day trial may not have been sufficient to capture cumulative effects of dietary additives. This consideration is particularly relevant for probiotics and essential oils, whose mechanisms of action often depend on sustained gastrointestinal colonization and gradual modulation of the gut microbial community (Naeem and Bourassa, 2025). Therefore, it is important to point out that these results do not preclude the possibility that longer-term supplementation could influence resistome composition over a full commercial production cycle.

Furthermore, the limited or no effects observed in the resistome suggest that dietary interventions alone may not be sufficient to substantially reduce the environmental burden of ARGs on poultry systems. Effective mitigation of antimicrobial resistance likely requires a multifactorial approach combining nutritional strategies with appropriate hygiene, litter, and management practices (FAO, 2025). Long-term studies are needed to determine whether sustained dietary supplementation, together with integrated farm management, can alter resistome dynamics over a full commercial production cycle.

Our findings diverge slightly from Koorakula et al. (2022), particularly regarding the role of antibiotics. Their study showed distinct compositional and transcriptional shifts in response to AGP and therapeutic-level bacitracin, including increased abundance of Escherichia, changes in Lactobacillus, and treatment-specific enrichment of bacitracin-associated ARGs. In contrast, the antibiotic treatment in our study (BMD) did not induce broad or sustained restructuring of the resistome, and overall class-level patterns remained conserved across treatments. Nonetheless, we did detect a significant interaction between day and treatment, with the Antibiotic group differing from the Probiotic group specifically at Day 10, indicating that antibiotic exposure can transiently modulate the resistome trajectory even when the global resistome structure remains largely stable. It is noteworthy that these transient effects were outweighed by the dominant influence of age, with the major resistome shifts occurring between d1 and d10 across all treatments. Thus, while both studies highlight the resilience of the poultry resistome, our data suggest that under a cage designed study, feed additives including AGP exert weaker and more transient resistome-level effects compared with the stronger transcriptional responses reported for higher-dose bacitracin regimens.

The dominance of biocide and metal-associated resistance genes observed in our study is consistent with evidence that these compounds play a major role in shaping microbial resistomes in poultry systems. Pal et al. (2015) demonstrated that biocide and metal resistance genes are nearly ubiquitous across bacterial genomes and frequently co-occur with antibiotic resistance genes primarily through chromosomal rather than plasmid-mediated linkages. This genomic framework helps explain the highly conserved proportional structure of multi-biocide and multi-metal resistance classes across all treatments in our dataset and the limited influence of feed additives, including the Antibiotic group, on drug-resistance profiles (Fig. 1 (A and B)). More recently, Shen et al. (2023) provided field-level confirmation that heavy metals exert strong ecological pressure on poultry-associated resistomes, showing that metals such as zinc were present at high concentrations in chicken feces and accounted for a substantial proportion of ARGs variation (22.25%) across breeding cycles, exceeding the selective contribution of antibiotics. In our study, we observed a higher abundance of zinc resistance genes across treatments and age. These studies support the interpretation that environmental and physiological factors, particularly metal exposure, biocide residues, and age-related microbial succession, structure the poultry resistome. In this context, the minimal treatment effects detected in our study align with other studies that co-selection pressures arising from metals and biocides overshadow those imposed by short-term dietary interventions.

While the study offers interesting insights into how feed additives affect the excreta resistome, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, excreta samples reflect contributions from multiple regions of the gastrointestinal tract, which may obscure site-specific ARG dynamics occurring within the ceca or ileum. Second, although shotgun metagenomics provides broad coverage of ARGs, our analysis was limited to gene presence and relative abundance rather than quantitative expression or functional activity. Furthermore, no culture-based isolation or phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed; therefore, the detected ARGs cannot be assumed to be actively expressed or directly associated with phenotypic resistance, and some may represent silent genetic elements. Integrating metatranscriptomic, metaproteomic, and culture-based approaches would provide deeper insight into the functional relevance of detected resistance genes. Third, only a single inclusion level and formulation of each dietary additive were evaluated in the present study. Different supplementation rates, formulations, or feeding strategies may produce distinct effects on microbiome and resistome dynamics. Future research should therefore investigate a broader range of dietary interventions while incorporating longitudinal sampling, genomic reconstruction of ARG-carrying taxa, and characterization of mobile genetic element transfer to better resolve the mechanisms underlying ARG persistence and movement within the broiler gut.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive characterization of the broiler excreta resistome in a 21-day trial period and in response to Probiotic, Essential Oils, and Antibiotic dietary interventions. We observed that age was the dominant factor shaping excreta ARG composition, diversity, and structure, with the most pronounced shifts occurring between d1 and d10. Dietary treatments did not produce substantial or sustained alterations in overall resistome structure, suggesting that natural feed additives exerted minimal selective pressure on the excreta resistome under the conditions tested. While a transient treatment effect was detected at d10, this influence was modest relative to broader age-associated trends and reflect the transition from initial colonization to a more stable community.

The predominance of biocide and metal resistance genes across all treatment groups aligns with previous evidence that non-antibiotic co-selection pressures are pervasive in poultry environments and may reinforce conserved ARG distributions over time. These findings highlight the importance of early-life microbiome maturation as a key determinant of resistance gene dynamics, while also indicating that the natural feed additives evaluated here did not alter the drug-associated resistome. Because these findings were derived from excreta samples, future studies should evaluate cecal contents to determine whether similar resistome patterns occur at the primary site of microbial fermentation and nutrient digestion in broilers. Such investigations, combined with quantitative, functional, spatial, and longitudinal approaches, will further refine our understanding of ARG transmission, persistence, and resilience within poultry production systems.
