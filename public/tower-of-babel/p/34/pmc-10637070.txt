Exosomal miR‐222‐3p contributes to castration‐resistant prostate cancer by activating mTOR signaling
Authors: Weixi Wang, Piaoping Kong, Kangle Feng, Chunhua Liu, Xubo Gong, Tao Sun, Xiuzhi Duan, Yiwen Sang, Yu Jiang, Xiang Li, Lingyu Zhang, Zhihua Tao, Weiwei Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the clinical benefits of androgen deprivation therapy, most patients with advanced androgen‐dependent prostate cancer (ADPC) eventually relapse and progress to lethal androgen‐independent prostate cancer (AIPC), also termed castration‐resistant prostate cancer (CRPC). MiRNAs can be packaged into exosomes (Exos) and shuttled between cells. However, the roles and mechanisms of exosomal miRNAs involved in CRPC progression have not yet been fully elucidated. Here, we find that miR‐222‐3p is elevated in AIPC cells, which results in remarkable enhancement of cell proliferation, migration, and invasion ability. Furthermore, Exos released by AIPC cells can be uptaken by ADPC cells, thus acclimating ADPC cells to progressing to more aggressive cell types in vitro and in vivo through exosomal transfer of miR‐222‐3p. Mechanistically, Exos‐miR‐222‐3p promoted ADPC cells transformed to AIPC‐like cells, at least in part, by activating mTOR signaling through targeting MIDN. Our results show that AIPC cells secrete Exos containing miRNA cargo. These cargos can be transferred to ADPC cells through paracrine mechanisms that have a strong impact on cellular functional remodeling. The current work underscores the great therapeutic potential of targeting Exo miRNAs, either as a single agent or combined with androgen receptor pathway inhibitors for CRPC treatment.

INTRODUCION

Prostate cancer (PCa) is still the most common malignant tumor of the urinary system in developed countries and is also the second leading cause of cancer‐related deaths in men. 1 Androgen receptor (AR) plays a crucial role in the progression of both androgen‐dependent PCa (ADPC) and androgen‐independent PCa (AIPC). Currently, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) is the main therapeutic strategy to suppress the production of testicular androgen through surgical castration for PCa patients. Use of anti‐androgens or AR antagonists (e.g., abiraterone or enzalutamide) is also an important therapeutic strategy. 2 Although these standard treatment regimens initially achieve favorable outcomes, the disease eventually progresses to a more aggressive form of PCa, commonly termed AIPC or castration‐resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), becoming the major obstacle of clinical therapy. 3

The mechanism of AIPC transformation has been a scientific concern, and the main research focus is the AR pathway. The AR‐dependent AIPC transformation is closely related to abnormal activation of the AR pathway, including the increased sensitivity of PCa cells to androgen, AR gene mutation, and aberrant AR coregulator expression or activity. 4 However, the above mechanism cannot explain the AIPC transformation phenomenon induced by complete blocking of the AR pathway. Therefore, in addition to the AR‐dependent pathway, there should also be an AR‐independent bypass pathway for CRPC progression. 5

Exosomes (Exos) are microvesicles originating from multivesicular bodies (MVBs) that can participate in intercellular communication by delivering intracellular cargos. 6 , 7 A growing number of investigations suggest that microRNAs (miRNAs), extracellular vesicles (EVs), and Exos play an important regulatory or communication role in tumor development. 8 Exos contain abundant miRNAs, which can work locally or distally. There is also evidence that these Exos can be absorbed into adjacent or distant cell types to regulate the function of receptor cells. 9 These studies prompt us to propose a scientific hypothesis that CRPC cells can secrete Exos containing miRNAs, which act as evildoers to promote ADPC cells progress to more aggressive cell types.

In this study, we found and demonstrated that miR‐222‐3p contributed to AIPC transformation. In addition, we report that AIPC cells that secrete Exos containing miR‐222‐3p can be absorbed by ADPC cells in vitro and in vivo, leading to cell morphology and function remodeling. Furthermore, exosomal miR‐222‐3p confers resistance to AR inhibition, and compared to age‐matched ADPC patients, exosomal miR‐222‐3p is highly enriched in the plasma of CRPC patients. Finally, we demonstrate that exosomal miR‐222‐3p contributes to CRPC partially by activating the mTOR signaling through a mechanism likely related to the inhibition of its target gene midnolin (MIDN).

METHODS

Ethics

The Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University School of Medicine Second Affiliated Hospital ratified our study. Prior to conducting the study, informed consent forms were obtained from all PCa patients participating in the study. All treatment plans for experimental animals have been approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhejiang University.

Patients and specimens

Clinical samples were obtained from the Department of Laboratory Medicine the Department of Pathology, Zhejiang University School of Medicine Second Affiliated Hospital. More specifically, 15 ADPC paraffin‐embedded tissue samples and 10 CRPC paraffin‐embedded tissue samples were obtained from the patients who were diagnosed and underwent surgery between July 2016 and September 2021. Patients did not receive any treatment before clinical samples were collected. Between September 2016 and June 2020, we collected the plasma of 15 ADPC patients and 15 CRPC patients before surgery and stored it at −80°C. In this study, none of these PCa patients received preoperative cancer treatment.

Cell lines and cell culture

PC3 and LNCaP cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection, and the methods of cell culture have been described in detail in our previous article. 10 LNCaP‐AI cells were generated as previously described. 11 To establish the androgen‐independent cell line, LNCaP was first maintained in phenol red‐free DMEM/F‐12 medium (Gibco) with 10% charcoal/dextran‐treated FBS (Biowest). After culturing for 5 weeks, cells were transferred to the above medium with 1.0 × 10−7 mol/L flutamide (Schering Plough) and cultured for another 105 passages. Ultimately, LNCaP‐AI cells were grown in RPMI‐1640 medium with 10% dextran–charcoal stripped FBS (CS‐FBS; Gibco Life Technologies).

Reagents and antibodies

Methyl‐β‐cyclodextrin (MβCD), GW4869, and enzalutamide (MDV3100) were purchased from Selleck. PKH26 was purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich. Antibodies against GAPDH (#5174), mTOR (#2972), and pmTOR (#5536) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology. Anti‐human CD9 (sc‐51575) and TSG101 (sc‐7964) antibodies were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Antibodies against CD63 (Cat No. 25682‐1‐AP), GRP94 (Cat No. 14700‐1‐AP), Ki67 (Cat No. 27309‐1‐AP), and MIDN (Cat No. 18939‐1‐AP) were obtained from Proteintech.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation assay was performed as described in our previous study. 12

Migration and invasion assay

Migration and invasion assays were conducted in the current study as described in our previous paper. 10

RNA sequencing

The small RNA of PCa cells was sequenced by Novogene. Total RNA was isolated from PCa cells using TRIzol Reagent (Takara) following the manufacturer's procedure. Briefly, approximately 500 ng of RNA was extracted and collected for library construction and sequencing. The detailed sequencing and analysis methods are as described in a previous publication. 13 In addition, differentially expressed mRNAs between LNCaP and LNCaP‐AI were determined using high‐throughput Illumina sequencing technologies as previously described. 14 p‐value of 0.05 and fold change >1.5 were set as the threshold for significantly differential expression by default.

RNA isolation and quantitative real‐time PCR

RNA isolation and qPCR were performed as described in our previous study. 10 Total RNA was isolated from PCa cells, tissues, and Exos using TRIzol Reagent (Takara) and reversely transcribed using a PrimeScript RT‐PCR Kit (TaKaRa Biotechnology) according to the protocol. The qRT‐PCR was performed on the ABI 7900 Prism HT (Applied Biosystems), followed by melting curve analysis. The 2‐ΔΔCt method was used to assess the gene expression levels. For the qRT‐PCR detection of mature miRNA expression, we purchased the Bulge‐Loop miRNA qRT‐PCR Primer Set (RiboBio) and miRNA qRT‐PCR Control Primer Set (RiboBio). GAPDH and U6 were used as endogenous controls, respectively. The following primers were used: MIDN, forward: AGAAACGGCTCCGTAGAAAGG; reverse: GACTTGATGTCAGGGTTGACTTC. GAPDH, forward: AACAGCCTCAAGATCATCAGCA; reverse: CATGAGTCCTTCCACGATACCA.

qNano assay

We used the qNano system and tunable resistive pulse sensing technology (TRPS, IZON Technologies) to analyze the size and particle density of Exos. We used 1 × 1013 particles/mL calibration beads to normalize and determine the density of Exos. Izon Control Suite software v.3.3.2.2000 was utilized to analyze the data.

Cell transfection

Cy3‐modified miR‐222‐3p mimic, Cy3‐modified mimic control (Mimic Ctrl), miR‐222‐3p inhibitor (anti‐miR‐222‐3p), and inhibitor negative control (anti‐miR‐NC) were purchased from RiboBio. According to the manufacturer's instructions, cells were transfected with 100 nM miRNA mimics or inhibitors using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen).

Conditioned complete medium preparation and exosome isolation

Approximately 2 × 106 PCa cells were cultured in dish with a diameter of 10 cm. After 24 or 48 h, conditioned complete medium (CM) was replaced with 10% exosome‐deleted FBS and incubated including the specific period of time to conduct subsequent experiments. Conditioned complete medium (CM) preparation was obtained as previously described. 15 For conditioned CM Exo purification, conditioned CM was collected and centrifuged as previously described. 15 To obtain exosome‐free conditioned CM, the conditioned CM was spun down successively at 300 g for 15 min, 2000 g for 15 min, and 12,000 g for 70 min to deplete Exos from the media. For conditioned CM Exo purification, conditioned CM was collected and centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min. The supernatant was then collected and re‐centrifuged at 10,000 g for 20 min to remove whole cells, cell debris and aggregates. The debris‐free supernatant was filtered and spun down in an ultracentrifuge tube at 120,000 g for 70 min. The pellet was recovered and washed in PBS followed by a second spin at 120,000×g for 70 min. The pellet was recovered and washed in PBS. Finally, Exos was resuspended in 100 μL PBS for immediate use or stored at −80°C.

Isolation and purification of Exos from plasma

Isolation and purification of Exos from plasma were performed as previously described. 15 Approximately 500 μL of plasma was collected from PCa patients. Human sera were centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min and then at 10,000 g for 10 min to remove whole cells, cell debris and aggregates. The serum was subjected to 0.22 μm filtration and ultracentrifuged at 120,000 g for 70 min. Pelleted vesicles were suspended in 1×PBS, ultracentrifuged again at 120,000 g for washing, resuspended in 1×PBS, and prepared for subsequent experiments and analyses. Finally, Exos from plasma were resuspended in 100 μL PBS and prepared for subsequent experiments and analyses.

Transmission electron microscopy

The morphology of Exos was investigated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) as described in our previous study. 16

Flow cytometry analysis

The protein expression level of CD9 and CD63 on Exos were determined using flow cytometry as described in our previous study. 16

Fluorescence assay

PKH26 was used to label Exos according to the manufacturer's instructions. DAPI was used for cell nuclear staining.

Co‐culture assay

Transwell membranes (pores 0.4 μm, Merck Millipore) were used to perform the co‐culture assay. PCa cells were pretreated with GW4869 (20 μM) or DMSO for 24 h in advance. PCa cells on the permeable membranes were treated with GW4869 or DMSO, and the co‐culture was performed for 24–72 h. Then another PCa cell line below the membranes was ready for further analysis.

Immunohistochemistry and evaluation of immunostaining

Immunohistochemistry staining with mTOR, pmTOR, Ki67, and MIDN levels was performed as previously described. 10

Luciferase reporter assay

Target‐scan 7.0 predicted that there was a binding site between miR‐222‐3p and MIDN 3′‐UTR. PGL3 luciferase reporter gene vector was utilized to build wild‐type (Wt) and mutant‐type (Mt) MIDN 3′‐UTR luciferase report vectors. PCa cells were co‐transfected with the MIDN‐Wt or MIDN‐Mt and miR‐222‐3p mimic, Mimic Ctrl, anti‐miR‐222‐3p, or anti‐miR‐NC. After incubation for 48 h, luciferase activity was detected according to the instructions of the dual‐luciferase reporting system (Promega).

Lentiviral particle transfection

Prostate cancer cells were infected with shAR lentiviral particles to establish AR‐silencing cells as described in our previous study. 10 LV2‐Control and LV2‐shAR were purchased from Shanghai Gene Pharma Company. The sequence of shAR was as follows: 5′‐GGAACTCGATCGTATCATTGC‐3′. The sequence of shMIDN was as follows: #1‐5′‐GTTAATGGTTTCAGATGTGAA‐3′ and #2‐5′‐GTCATTATGCACTTTACAGAT‐3′. A scrambled sequence was used as a negative control with the following sequence: 5′‐TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT‐3′.

Western blot analysis

Total protein of cells or Exos was extracted with RIPA buffer (Beyotime) and quantified with the BCA assay (Beyotime), and western blot was performed as described in our previous study. 12

In vivo experimental Exos‐miR‐222‐3p

For xenograft studies, ten 4‐week‐old female BALB/c‐nu mice were randomly divided into two equal groups. On Day 0, 3 × 106 LNCaP cells suspended in 0.2 mL of PBS were inoculated subcutaneously in the buttocks. To evaluate the function of Exos‐miR‐222‐3p in vivo, LNCaP cells and LNCaP‐AI cells derived Exos (30 μg in 100 μL PBS every 7 days) were adoptively transferred into recipient mice via tail vein injection. Tumor sizes and tumor volume were measured daily. At the 30th day, all mice were killed, and tumors were dissected and stored in liquid nitrogen or fixed in formalin for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data was shown as the mean ± SD. One‐way ANOVA with Dunnett's test or the Newman–Keuls test and Student's two‐tailed t‐test were used to calculate significant differences. p < 0.05 was considered significant.

RESULTS

MiR‐222‐3p contributes to androgen‐independent prostate cancer transformation

Recently, several reports suggest that miRNAs play a crucial role in AIPC transformation. 17 , 18 , 19 In our previous investigation, 11 using high‐throughput Illumina sequencing technologies, we found that 83 miRNAs were markedly differentially expressed between LNCaP and LNCaP‐AI cells, indicating that these miRNAs may be involved in AIPC progression. To further clarify the expression and role of miRNAs in CRPC progression, differentially expressed miRNAs were delineated in LNCaP, LNCaP‐AI, and PC3 cells. The top 20 miRNAs differentially expressed between LNCaP and LNCaP‐AI cells are shown in Figure 1A and Table S1; The top 40 miRNAs differentially expressed between LNCaP and PC3 cells are shown in Figure 1B and Table S1. Compared to LNCaP cells, miR‐221‐3p, miR‐222‐3p, miR‐221‐5p, and miR‐10a‐5p are highly expressed in both LNCaP‐AI and PC3 cells. qPCR results further validated the expression levels of these four miRNAs (Figure 1C–F). Among them, miR‐221‐3p, miR‐222‐3p, and miR‐221‐5p are the three most upregulated miRNAs in AIPC progression. In addition, the expression abundance of miR‐221‐3p and miR‐222‐3p is also dramatically higher than that of the other three miRNAs in both LNCaP and LNCaP‐AI cells (Table S2), indicating that these two miRNAs may play a critical role in AIPC transformation. In line with our results, Sun et al. 20 found that miR‐221 and miR‐222 were obviously upregulated in CRPC cells as well. Furthermore, miR‐221 has been proved to be involved in the reprogramming of AR signaling through the downregulation of HECTD2 and RAB1A, which in turn may promote CRPC progression. 21 However, the function and mechanism of miR‐222‐3p involved in CRPC progression remain unclear.

Compared to LNCaP cells, we further verified that the expression of miR‐222‐3p was distinctly enhanced in LNCaP‐AI cells by qRT‐PCR (Figure 2A). Furthermore, to investigate the role of miR‐222‐3p in AIPC transformation, Cy3‐modified miR‐222‐3p and mimic control were successfully transfected into LNCaP and LNCaP‐AI cells (Figure 2B). The expression level of miR‐222‐3p was dramatically increased in miR‐222‐3p mimic‐treated cells (Figure 2C,D). Cell proliferation was markedly enhanced after 48 h transfection with miR‐222‐3p (Figure 2E). Additionally, overexpression of miR‐222‐3p leads to significant enhancement of cell migration and invasion in PCa cells (Figure 2F–H). Most notably, miR‐222‐3p could partially restore the impaired cell migration and invasion capability caused by androgen deprivation in LNCaP cells (Figure 2G). These results suggest that miR‐222‐3p contributes to AIPC transformation.

Androgen‐independent prostate cancer‐derived exosomes enhance androgen‐independent prostate cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

Recently, miRNAs have been identified in Exos, and increasing evidence suggests that Exos are key participants in intercellular communication. 22 Exosomal cargos also seem to convey instructions to other cells involved in modulating cancer growth, invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance. 23 According to the previous series of research results and related publications, 24 , 25 we propose a potential novel mechanism of AIPC transformation: ADT and/or AR inhibitor treatment directly slow the growth of ADPC cells, while a small number of AIPC cells in a latent state are awakened to obtain selective growth advantage under this condition. These awakened AIPC cells act on adjacent ADPC cells by secreting their own Exos carrying miRNAs (AIPC‐Exos‐miRNAs) to acclimate ADPC cells to transforming into AIPC‐like cells. We temporarily call this AIPC transformation mechanism “AIPC‐Exos paracrine regulation.”

To confirm this hypothesis, Exos were isolated from LNCaP cells culture medium (CM) and LNCaP‐AI cells charcoal‐stripped androgen‐free culture medium (AFCM) by ultracentrifugation as previously described. 26 Exosomes were eluted in PBS and evaluated by TEM (Figure 3A), qNano for particle size and counts (Figure 3D,E), and molecular profiles by western blot (Figure 3B). Exos‐specific protein markers CD63, TSG101, and Exos‐associated protein marker CD9 were detected (Figure 3B). GRP94, an endoplasmic reticulum protein marker, was non‐detectable in PCa cell‐derived Exos (Figure 3B). Furthermore, over 50% of Exos released by PCa cells carried CD63 and CD9, as identified by flow cytometry assay (Figure 3C). The diameter of obtained Exos is mostly between 50 and 100 nm (Figure 3D–F). These morphological experiments and characterizations indicated that we had successfully extracted PCa Exos. Exos secreted by PC3 cells were also isolated and purified by differential ultracentrifugation.

Considering the more malignant phenotype of AIPC cells, next, we evaluated whether the Exos secreted by AIPC cells affect the proliferation and survival of ADPC cells. First, we investigated the effects of PC3 conditioned medium (PC3‐CM) and PC3‐derived Exos (PC3 Exos) on the proliferation and survival of LNCaP cells. Compared with the control, our results revealed that PC3‐CM and PC3‐Exos significantly promoted LNCaP cell proliferation (Figure 4A). Intriguingly, we found that when Exos were physically removed from PC3‐CM through ultracentrifugation, this dramatically reduced the ability of PC3‐CM to increase LNCaP cell proliferation (Figure 4A). Second, Exos secreted by LNCaP (LNCaP‐Exos) and LNCaP‐AI (LNCaP‐AI‐Exos) were successfully ingested by LNCaP cells (Figure 4B). Compared to LNCaP‐Exos, LNCaP‐AI‐Exos dramatically promoted the growth activity (Figure 4C) and enhanced the migration and invasion ability (Figure 4D,E) of LNCaP cells both in CM and AFCM. Collectively, the above results indicated that Exos released by AIPC cells accelerate ADPC cells proliferation, migration, and invasion.

Exosomal transfer of miR‐222‐3p from androgen‐independent prostate cancer cells to androgen‐dependent prostate cancer cells

A growing number of studies show that exosomal miRNAs have a crucial function in modulating tumor progression. 27 , 28 Additionally, the role of miR‐222‐3p in promoting AIPC transformation has been confirmed at the cellular level (Figure 2). Therefore, we hypothesized that AIPC transformation could be mediated by exosomal miR‐222‐3p.

To clarify the potential function of Exos‐miR‐222‐3p in AIPC transformation, the expression level of miR‐222‐3p in ADPC‐ and AIPC cell‐derived Exos were determined using qPCR. We found that AIPC cells, both LNCaP‐AI‐ and PC3 cell‐derived Exos, showed a significant increase in miR‐222‐3p expression (Figure 5A). In addition, after incubation with Exos secreted by LNCaP AI and PC3 cells, a remarkable increase in miR‐222‐3p expression was observed in LNCaP cells (Figure 5B). Furthermore, the expression of miR‐222‐3p was elevated in CRPC tissues compared with ADPC tissues (Figure 5C). In addition, LNCaP‐AI and PC3 cells expressed notably higher levels of endogenous miR‐222‐3p compared with LNCaP (Figure 1C). These data suggest that AIPC cells may directly deliver miR‐222‐3p to ADPC cells in the tumor microenvironment, leading to the upregulated expression of miR‐222‐3p in ADPC cells. To determine whether AIPC cells can directly transport miR‐222‐3p to ADPC cells through Exos, LNCaP cells were co‐cultured with PC3 cells (Figure 5D). We observed that LNCaP cells incubated with PC3 cells expressed a higher level of miR‐222‐3p (Figure 5E) and exhibited stronger cell growth activity (Figure 5F). In contrast, we found that blocking the Exos secretion of PC3 by GW4869 markedly reduced the capability of PC3 cells to enhance the level of miR‐222‐3p and cell proliferation in LNCaP (Figure 5E,F). Moreover, LNCaP‐AI cells transiently transfected with Cy3‐labeled miR‐222‐3p were co‐cultured with LNCaP cells for 48 h (Figure 5G). The Cy3‐tagged miR‐222‐3p was observed in the LNCaP cells (Figure 5G), indicating that miR‐222‐3p was transferred from LNCaP‐AI to LNCaP cells via Exos. Furthermore, miR‐222‐3p levels in LNCaP cells were markedly enhanced upon incubation with miR‐222‐3p overexpressed LNCaP‐AI but not with mimic control (Figure 5H), which resulted in enhanced cell growth activity (Figure 5I). Moreover, when PC3‐CM was treated with RNase A alone, there was no significant change in the expression level of miR‐222‐3p. However, when treated with RNase A and Triton X‐100 simultaneously, its expression was significantly reduced (Figure S1), indicating that extracellular miR‐222‐3p was mainly encapsulated within the membrane rather than directly released. Thus, these findings revealed that functional miR‐222‐3p was transferred from AIPC cells to ADPC cells via Exos.

Exos‐miR‐222‐3p contributes to castration‐resistant prostate cancer partially by activating mTOR signaling through targeting midnolin

Furthermore, the underlying mechanisms by which Exos‐miR‐222‐3p contributes to CRPC were investigated. First, the target mRNAs of miR‐222‐3p were investigated in PCa. Eleven potential target genes, including ZNF704, MIDN, GMFB, ARHGAP19, PLEKHA6, SNTB1, GPRIN2, BMF, SERPINB5, TNFSF13, and REEP3, were predicted in the three databases targetscan, miRDB, and miRTarBase (Figure 6A). Considering the functions (oncogenes or tumor suppressors) of these 11 potential targets, MIDN, a potential tumor suppressor, 29 was highly suspected to be a novel target of miR‐222‐3p. Interestingly, this speculation was confirmed by RT‐qPCR; miR‐222‐3p overexpression significantly reduced the mRNA expression of MIDN (Figure 6B). The above data indicates that MIDN is a putative direct target for miR‐222‐3p. We further found that miR‐222‐3p overexpression observably reduced luciferase activity in LNCaP cells expressing wild‐type MIDN 3′ UTR reporters, whereas this inhibition can be specifically eliminated by anti miR‐222‐3p. In addition, this inhibitory effect of miR‐222‐3p can also be eliminated by mutations in the miR‐222‐3p binding region of MIDN 3′ UTR (Figure 6C,D).

Second, based on the results of full transcriptome sequencing, KEGG analysis showed that the mTOR signaling pathway was markedly activated in LNCaP‐AI compared to LNCaP (Figure 6E; Tables S3 and S4). We further investigated the mechanism of miR‐222‐3p in AIPC transformation at the protein levels. LNCaP cells were stably infected with lentivirus control or with miR‐222‐3p or anti‐miR‐222‐3p. Our data showed that miR‐222‐3p overexpression markedly stimulated the phosphorylation of mTOR and decreased the MIDN protein expression, although there was no significant change in the protein level of mTOR (Figure 6F). Our results further indicated that LNCaP cells were stably infected with anti‐miR‐222‐3p lentivirus and the phosphorylation of mTOR (pmTOR) was significantly reduced. In contrast, MIDN protein expression was dramatically increased, and it seems to have almost no effect on the level of mTOR protein (Figure 6F). Furthermore, our western blot results showed that silencing MIDN could significantly downregulate pmTOR, but the impact on mTOR is relatively minor (Figure 6F). These results indicated that miR‐222‐3p contributed to CRPC partially by activating mTOR signaling by targeting MIDN. To further confirm that AIPC cell‐derived Exos can carry miR‐222‐3p and promote AIPC transformation through this pathway, LNCaP cells were grown in control CM and PC3‐CM or incubated with 20 μg/mL Exos from PC3 cells or LNCaP‐AI cells for 48 h. We found that both PC3 and LNCaP‐AI cell‐derived Exos could significantly enhance the pmTOR and markedly decreased MIDN protein expression, although the protein level of mTOR seems to be unchanged (Figure 6G). Compared to control CM, similar results were observed in PC3‐CM cultured LNCaP cells (Figure 6G).

Our results demonstrated that Exos secreted by AIPC cells can carry miR‐222‐3p, which promotes CRPC progression partially by activating mTOR signaling by targeting MIDN.

Exos‐miR‐222‐3p confers resistance to androgen receptor inhibition and is highly enriched in castration‐resistant prostate cancer patients compared to androgen‐dependent prostate cancer patients

In addition to ADT, AR blocking is an effective treatment strategy for PCa. We found that either treatment with MDV3100 or shAR dramatically reduced the proliferative activity of LNCaP cells (Figure 7A,B). However, this anti‐tumor effect was dramatically mitigated after treatment of 20 μg/mL PC3‐Exos (Figure 7A,B). Moreover, this anti‐tumor effect was substantially alleviated after miR‐222‐3p overexpression (Figure 7C,D). These findings revealed that Exos‐miR‐222‐3p confers resistance to an AR‐targeted therapeutic strategy.

To further study the role of exosomal miR‐222‐3p in CRPC progression, we isolated and purified Exos from blood plasma of 15 ADPC patients and 15 CRPC patients by differential ultracentrifugation. To confirm the purity of Exos, GRP94 was used as a cellular marker and CD9, CD63, and TSG101a as exosomal markers (Figure 7E). Compared to age‐matched ADPC patients, as depicted in Figure 7F, exosomal miR‐222‐3p was highly enriched in the plasma collected from CRPC patients. Furthermore, it can be seen from the statistical data in the Table S5 that the PSA level and Gleason score of patients in the CRPC group were significantly higher than those in the ADPC group, which indicates that the level of miR‐222‐3p in tissues (Figure 5C) and plasma Exos may be positively correlated with the PSA level and Gleason score (Figure 7F). In view of this result, Exos isolated from CRPC patients (CRPC‐Exos) were co‐cultured with LNCaP cells. Furthermore, CCK8 assay showed that the growth activity of LNCaP cells was significantly enhanced (Figure 7G). However, before incubation of cells with 20 μg/mL CRPC‐Exos, LNCaP cells were pretreated with 2.5 mM methyl‐β‐cyclodextrin (MβCD), a potential inhibitor of vesicle uptake. We found that MβCD pretreatment almost completely blocked the cell growth promoting effect of CRPC‐Exos, and MβCD pretreatment alone did not seem to have any effect on cell growth activity (Figure 7G), indicating that CRPC‐Exos exhibits powerful capability to promote the growth of ADPC cell line, LNCaP cells. Western blot results further suggested that CRPC‐Exos uptake markedly decreased MIDN and activated mTOR signaling (Figure 7H). Thus, these data demonstrated that Exos‐miR‐222‐3p is highly enriched in the plasma procured from CRPC patients, indicating its potential to become a differential diagnostic marker for ADPC and CRPC.

LNCaP‐AI‐derived exosomal miR‐222‐3p promotes LNCaP cells proliferation in vivo

To determine whether AIPC cell‐secreted Exos‐miR‐222‐3p affected the tumorigenesis of LNCaP cells, a xenograft model was established by subcutaneously injecting LNCaP cells. Seven days after the injections, mice were intravenously injected with Exos (30 μg in 100 μL PBS every 7 days) derived from LNCaP cells and LNCaP‐AI cells. We found that there was little difference in weight between the two groups in mice 30 days after inoculation (Figure 8A). However, the tumors formed that were treated with LNCaP‐AI‐Exos grew faster than those in the LNCaP‐Exos group (Figure 8B). Furthermore, as shown in Figure 8C, mice injected with LNCaP‐AI‐Exos exhibited an obvious increase in tumor weight in comparison with that of LNCaP‐Exos mice. These results revealed that LNCaP‐AI‐Exos were involved in LNCaP cell growth in vivo. Moreover, the expression of miR‐222‐3p was dramatically enhanced in xenograft tissues treated with LNCaP‐AI‐Exos. Interestingly, MIDN expression was negatively correlated with miR‐222‐3p expression via qPCR analysis. Furthermore, as detected by immunohistochemical staining and western blotting, an opposite trend was observed between MIDN protein level and miR‐222‐3p (Figure 8F,G). Functionally, LNCaP‐AI‐Exos treatment increased the proportion of Ki‐67‐positive cells and markedly promoted the pmTOR, but the total mTOR protein expression was not obviously altered (Figure 8F,G). These data suggested that LNCaP‐AI‐derived exosomal miR‐222‐3p promotes LNCaP cells proliferation in vivo, partially by activating mTOR signaling pathway through targeting MIDN.

DISCUSSION

In this present study, we demonstrated that miR‐222‐3p contributed to AIPC transformation. miR‐222‐3p overexpression enhanced cell proliferation, migration and invasion capability both in LNCaP and LNCaP‐AI cells. Furthermore, Exos released by AIPC cells can be uptaken by ADPC cells either treated by Exos alone or by cell co‐culture both in vitro and in vivo. We showed that miR‐222‐3p‐containing AIPC‐Exos induced ADPC cells to acquire castration‐resistant ability by directly affecting cellular growth and migration activity. Our data suggests that miRNAs within secreted AIPC‐Exos can be uptaken by ADPC cells and significantly affect cellular morphology and function remodeling.

Secreted EVs were isolated from PCa cells CM by differential ultracentrifugation. These EVs were identified as Exos with a diameter of 50–150 nm by electromicroscopy, qNano analysis, and characterization analysis of exosomal membrane markers CD9, CD63, and TSG101. 30 Direct transport of Exos‐miRNAs into recipient target cells were demonstrated using several methods. First, LNCaP‐AI‐derived Exos were directly labeled with PKH26. LNCaP cells were incubated with these labeled Exos, and uptake of the fluorescent label into LNCaP cells was directly observed. In a second approach, we transfected LNCaP‐AI cells with Cy3‐tagged miR‐222‐3p and applied these cells to the upper chamber with LNCaP cells in the lower chamber of a transwell dish. After incubation, fluorescently labeled miR‐222‐3p expression was observed in LNCaP cells with a multifold increase. This was also demonstrated by elevated miR‐222‐3p in LNCaP cells co‐cultured with PC3 cells, and the effect was counteracted by treatment of the PC3 cells with GW4869. Taken together, these experiments indicate that AIPC cells produce miR‐222‐3p containing Exos and the miR‐222‐3p can be directly delivered into ADPC cells in vitro. Our in vivo investigations further show that AIPC Exos leads to enhanced expression of miR‐222‐3p and promotes LNCaP cells proliferation.

Our PCa cells EVs preparations were obtained using established techniques, 9 , 16 and EVs preparations were characterized by morphological structure (Figure 3A), size (Figure 3D–F), and Exos protein markers (Figure 3B,C). Based on the characterization of these EVs related to previously published papers, 6 , 31 it is clear that our EV preparations are PCa cell‐secreted Exos, and it is reasonable to refer to them as ADPC‐derived Exos (ADPC‐Exos) or AIPC‐derived Exos (AIPC‐Exos). Despite many innovations in the field of Exos separation and identification, such as the separation of Exos using density gradient centrifugation, 32 as well as reassessment and classification of Exos, 33 this does not affect the positive results and conclusions of our Exos‐related investigations because the EVs we extracted completely conform to the definition of exosome. Therefore, we recognize that this specific term is an operational definition 7 and that this field is constantly evolving in PCa studies of Exos.

The phenomenon that cargos within Exos can be transported to other cells is supported by a growing number of investigations. 34 Among them, miRNAs are the most common customers in these cargos. 9 , 35 , 36 , 37 Treatment with macrophage‐derived Exos leads to insulin resistance by delivering miR‐155 to insulin target cells. 9 Furthermore, breast cancer‐cell‐secreted miR‐122‐containing Exos can be taken up by β cells, which significantly destroy systemic glucose homeostasis, thus contributing to tumor growth. 36 In addition, ovarian cancer cells can reprogram stromal fibroblasts into proinflammatory cancer‐associated fibroblasts through conveying exosomal miR‐141, facilitating metastatic colonization. 37 With respect to studies of Exos and CRPC, Del Re, Biasco 38 reported that plasma‐derived exosomal AR‐V7 RNA is a predictor of resistance to ADT. PCa cells secreted exosmal brain 4 (BRN4) was identified as a novel transcription factor that drives oncogenic reprogramming of PCa cells to CRPC, 39 indicating that PCa‐derived Exos can indeed be ingested by the adjacent cells, so that the contents in Exos can acclimate the receptor cells and reshape their functions. In addition, Huang et al. 40 performed RNA sequencing to identify candidate exosomal miRNAs associated with overall survival of CRPC patients in peripheral blood, and exosomal miR‐1290 and miR‐375 were demonstrated to be prognostic markers in CRPC. 40 To some extent, these reports further confirm the reliability of our results.

However, in our investigations, we initially did not screen the differential expression of miRNAs in Exos from ADPC and CRPC peripheral blood. First, differentially expressed miRNAs were screened between ADPC cells (LNCaP) and AIPC cells (LNCaP‐AI and PC3). We further identified and demonstrated that miR‐222‐3p overexpression contributes to CRPC (Figures 1 and 2). Based on these cellular experiments, we want to know whether the miR‐222‐3p contained in Exos plays an important role in the progress of CRPC. As expected, we observed that AIPC cell‐derived Exos, both LNCaP‐AI‐ and PC3‐derived Exos, showed a remarkable increase in miR‐222‐3p expression compared to ADPC cells (Figure 5A). Similar results were further obtained in Exos derived from plasma of ADPC and CRPC patients (Figure 7F), indicating it hopefully becomes a non‐invasive marker for identifying ADPC and CRPC. Furthermore, with regard to the role of Exo‐ miR‐222‐3p, we showed that AIPC‐Exo‐ miR‐222‐3p could be uptaken by ADPC cells and promoted ADPC cells transformed to AIPC‐like cells (Figure 5). More importantly, CRPC‐Exos co‐cultured with LNCaP cells significantly enhanced its growth activity (Figure 7G). The ADT (either MDV3100 or AR shRNA mediated AR inhibition) induced anti‐tumor effect was dramatically mitigated after treatment of PC3‐Exos in LNCaP cells (Figure 7A,B), suggesting that Exos‐miR‐222‐3p confers resistance to ADT. Despite all this, we cannot completely ignore the role of other Exo‐miRNAs in the progression of CRPC, including previously mentioned exosomal miR‐1290 and miR‐375, 40 and miR‐26a, a critical regulator involved in Exo secretion and PCa progression. 41 In addition to miR‐222‐3p, according to our results of most differentially expressed miRNAs between ADPC and AIPC cells (Figure 1), miR‐221‐3p, miR‐221‐5p, and miR‐10a‐5p may also be contained in Exos and contribute to AIPC transformation. Additionally, it is possible that several miRNAs within CRPC‐Exos could work together to induce AIPC aggressive phenotypes that we have observed. Further experimental work will be necessary to reveal the full set of CRPC‐Exo miRNAs contributing to AIPC transformation. Nevertheless, our current results strongly prove the fact that Exos‐miR‐222‐3p facilitates the progress of CRPC.

To further elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which Exos‐miR‐222‐3p contributes to CRPC, 11 potential target genes were predicted using TargetScan, miRDB, and miRTarBase and the three overlapping target genes (Figure 6A). Through functional prediction, qPCR, and luciferase reporter assay, we successfully demonstrated that MIDN is a potential target mRNA of miR‐222‐3p, which was further confirmed by western blot (Figure 6D). Theoretically, each miRNA can target hundreds of transcripts and more than one miRNA can direct against a single mRNA target. 42 Zhai et al. 43 suggested that miR‐222‐3p could directly target HIPK2 and activate the HIPK2/ERK/c‐myc axis, thus promoting pancreatic cancer progression. Data demonstrated that Exos‐miR‐222‐3p acts as a significant modulator of gemcitabine resistance by targeting suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3) in lung cancer. 44 Therefore, it is unlikely that MIDN is the sole target mRNA of miR‐222‐3p, as there are most likely other potential target mRNAs that play important functional roles. However, these uncertain target mRNAs do not affect whether Exos‐miR‐222‐3p can lead to the malignant progression of PCa by activating the mTOR signal pathway, which was demonstrated in vitro (Figure 7) and in vivo (Figure 8). This result is not inconsistent with our previous reports 10 , 12 since our concept is that stimulating the phosphorylation of mTOR facilitates PCa growth and metastasis. This concept has also been confirmed in a variety of tumors, especially in the field of tumor therapy drug resistance research. 45 Interestingly, the current study, ADT induced AIPC transformation, is also a kind of tumor remodeling mediated by therapeutic resistance.

Although studies have shown that the expression of miR‐222 in PCa is significantly downregulated compared with prostate adjacent tissue, and it is considered that miR‐222 may be a suppressor in PCa. 46 In addition, some studies have also shown that miR‐222 can act as an oncogene to promote tumor progression in other cancers, including non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), 44 colorectal cancer, 47 and breast cancer, 48 which indicates that the functions of miR‐222 in different cancers and stages may be inconsistent. Given the complexity of CRPC transformation and our results, miR‐222‐3p is indeed very likely to be a key molecule that promotes this process.

Based on the above experimental results and discussion, we have preliminarily confirmed the novel mechanism “AIPC‐Exos paracrine regulation” (Figure 9): ADT and/or AR inhibitor treatment significantly reduce the growth of ADPC cells, while a small number of latent AIPC cells can survive successfully and obtain selective growth advantage under ADT conditions. These proliferating AIPC cells secrete abundant Exos carrying miRNAs, such as miR‐222‐3p. These AIPC‐Exos‐miRNAs can be taken up by adjacent ADPC cells. Afterwards, Exos‐miR‐222‐3p acclimates ADPC cells to transform into AIPC‐like cells partially by activating mTOR signaling through targeting MIDN.

In addition to AIPC‐Exos paracrine regulation, increasing investigations demonstrate that immune cells 49 and cancer‐associated fibroblasts 50 can secrete Exos, reshaping the tumor microenvironment and accelerating tumor progression, 51 , 52 which means that the progression of CRPC may also be mediated by Exos derived from the tumor microenvironment. Moreover, gut microbiota contributes to CRPC by providing an alternative source of androgens. 53 Therefore, it cannot be ruled out that intestinal microorganisms may also communicate with PCa cells through Exos, thus participating in the progress of CRPC. These concepts need to be further clarified.

In this study, we found that miRNA containing Exos secreted by AIPC cells could induce endocrine resistance. Furthermore, Exos‐miR‐222‐3p is highly enriched in the AIPC cells and plasma procured from CRPC patients. Moreover, treatment with AIPC‐Exos confer resistance to AR inhibition in vitro and promote ADPC cells’ progress to more aggressive cell types in vitro and in vivo. It is recognized that interactive signal networks, including paracrine, 54 , 55 autocrine, 56 and endocrine signaling events, 56 play an important role in the occurrence and progression of PCa. In view of these investigations, we suggest that AIPC‐Exo miRNAs represent a particular paracrine signaling system, whereby AIPC cells can reshape the function of adjacent ADPC cells. The current work sheds insight into the molecular mechanisms of castration resistance development and underscores the great therapeutic potential of targeting Exo miRNAs, either as a single agent or in combination with AR pathway inhibitors in the treatment of CRPC.
