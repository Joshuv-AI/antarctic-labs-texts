Identification of disease-specific regulatory networks in prurigo nodularis versus atopic dermatitis through ATAC/RNA single-nucleus profiling
Authors: Yuntian Wu, Rachael Bogle, Matthew T Patrick, Gharaee-Kermani Mehrnaz, Xianying Xing, Allison C Billi, J Michelle Kahlenberg, Valérie Julia, Johann E Gudjonsson, Lam C Tsoi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Prurigo nodularis (PN) is a chronic neuroimmune skin disorder characterized by chronic itch and multiple pruriginous nodules, and is related to atopic dermatitis (AD). While different transcriptomic studies have been conducted, the regulatory mechanisms and differences between PN and AD remain poorly understood.Objective:We aimed to uncover shared and distinct regulatory mechanisms between PN and AD. Specifically, we evaluated how molecular factors driving the distinction between lesional and nonlesional skin differ in these two diseases.Method:We profiled gene expression and chromatin accessibility at the single-nucleus level from paired lesional and nonlesional skin samples of 4 PN patients and 5 AD patients. We utilized joint modeling and pseudobulk techniques to identify disease specific regulatory network.Results:The epigenomic alterations in PN were more numerous and of greater magnitude compared to AD skin. PN-biased cis-regulatory elements showed correlation with PN-biased differentially expressed genes in the same cell type, and we identified 199 regulatory modules specific to PN, in contrast to 3 modules specific to AD. We also identified PN-specific AP-1–induced regulatory module that induces RUNX2 in fibroblasts, confirmed by immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining, suggestive of its contribution to fibrosis in PN.Conclusion:This study provides a chromatin RNA expression atlas and underscores its efficacy in revealing distinct regulatory mechanisms inherent to PN and AD.Keywords: Prurigo nodularis, atopic dermatitis, single-cell RNA sequencing, single-cell ATAC sequencing, mechanism, nemolizumab, regulatory mechanism, lesional skin

Prurigo nodularis (PN) is a chronic neuroimmune skin disorder marked by multiple pruriginous lesions and persistent intense itch.1,2 Atopic dermatitis (AD), although clinically distinct, also presents with intense pruritus and chronic inflammation,3,4 and it often shares overlapping immunologic features with PN. Previous studies have indicated that both PN and AD are characterized by activation of type 2 cytokines, including IL-225–8 and IL-139,10 inflammatory responses, contributing to a complex interplay of neuronal dysregulation, aberrant keratinocyte signaling, and immune activation.11–14 While IL-3113–16 appears to be more strongly associated with PN pathogenesis, IL-2617–19 has been more prominently linked to AD.

Given these overlapping yet distinct molecular features, comparative transcriptomic studies have sought to further elucidate the similarities and differences between PN and AD. Several genomic studies have profiled the expressions of both diseases,5,7,20–25 revealing both shared and divergent transcriptional programs. However, some of these insights were derived from bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) data, which lacks cellular resolution. More recently, single-cell or single-nucleus RNA-Seq studies have begun to address this limitation. For instance, one comparison of PN against AD skin showed differences in epidermal function and immune-related processes, including antimicrobial responses and regulation of T-cell trafficking, which were observed to a greater extent from lesional AD than in lesional PN keratinocytes.7 Another single-cell RNA-Seq study of PN skin showed prominent features of fibrosis, angiogenesis, tissue remodeling, and neurogenesis.20 Furthermore, dysregulation in fibroblasts and myofibroblasts has been reported in PN skin.22

Although single-cell RNA-Seq has advanced our understanding of genes and pathways involved in PN and AD, it does not capture the underlying elements that governs gene expression. These regulatory mechanisms are critical to understanding how disease-specific gene programs are initiated and maintained. Identifying regulatory elements and transcription factor (TF) activity26 can offer unique insights that single-cell RNA-Seq alone cannot provide, permitting integration and analysis of multimodal genomic and epigenomic data at the single cell level for advancing our understanding of these diseases.

To address this gap, we conducted joint transcriptomic and epigenomic profiling using a single-nucleus multiome technique, assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC/RNA-Seq), on both lesional and nonlesional skin samples from patients with AD and PN. Our objective was to uncover shared and distinct regulatory mechanisms between the two diseases and specifically elucidate whether the factors driving the distinction between lesional and nonlesional skin differed in PN and AD and how these may contribute to the persistent expression of disease-related genes. This approach allowed us to move beyond descriptive transcriptomics and toward a mechanistic understanding of cell type–specific gene regulation in chronic inflammatory skin diseases.

METHODS

Patient cohort and sample acquisition

For single-nucleus RNA-Seq and single-nucleus ATAC-Seq, paired lesional and nonlesional skin biopsy samples (n = 18 total samples) were collected from 9 patients, 5 with AD and 4 with PN. Diagnoses were confirmed by board-certified dermatologists. All patients had active moderate-to-severe disease at the time of enrollment, defined by either multiple disseminated nodular lesions for PN or >10% body surface area involvement for AD. No participants were receiving systemic immunomodulatory treatment at the time of biopsy. All subjects had discontinued topical treatments for at least 2 weeks before biopsy. Biopsy samples were taken from inflamed lesional and matched nonlesional skin sites. The patient cohort included 7 female and 2 male subjects ranging in age from 23 to 78 years. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with institutional review board–approved protocols.

Generation of multiome RNA and ATAC-Seq data

For multiome processing, the biopsy samples were placed into a labeled 2mL 0.4% dispase (Life Technologies, catalog 17104–041) in Hanks balanced salt solution at 4°C overnight. The next day, the epidermis was removed with forceps and minced into 1–2 mm squares and incubated with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) and DNAse for 1 hour. The dermis was transferred to a separate tube with 0.2% collagenase II (Life Technologies, 17101–015; stored at 4°C) + 0.2% collagenase V (Sigma, C9263–1G) in plain medium and DNase for 1 hour. Samples were then strained through a 30 μm preseparation filter (Miltenyi Biotec) into a 50 mL conical tube and spun down, and number of cells per microliter was determined. Epidermal and dermal cells were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and submitted for multiome sequencing targeting 10,000 nuclei and 20,000 reads per nucleus for gene expression and 50,000 reads per nucleus for ATAC-Seq.

Raw data processing and quality control

Data processing alignment to the hg38 genome and initial processing were performed by Cell Ranger software (10x Genomics). The samples were merged into a single expression matrix and peak matrix by CellRanger-aggr without depth normalization.

For the bioinformatics workflow, aggregated datasets were processed by Seurat v4.3.0 and its companion package, Signac v1.9.0. The Seurat object was created by the CreateSeuratObject function. Given that 10x Genomics single-nucleus assays often capture freely floating transcripts during the droplet-based process, we used SoupX v1.6.2 to correct the expression file on the basis of estimated background contamination. Additionally, cell barcodes from the 10x Genomics chromium assay may include barcode multiplets; thus, barcode multiplets were detected using scDblFinder v1.12.0 on the basis of expression profiles for each sample and were excluded from downstream analysis. Low-quality cells were removed from the aggregated single-nucleus multiome dataset using sample-specific filtering thresholds with hard thresholding (nucleosome signal < 4, transcription start site enrichment > 2, percentage of mitochondrial genes < 0.25%). After filtering, the final single-nucleus multiome library contained a total of 36,601 unique genes and 328,188 unique peak regions among 83,453 cells. Cluster-specific ATAC peaks were called using MACS2 with the Signac wrapper and default parameters. A new Seurat object was then created using MACS2 peaks and the FeatureMatrix function. Normalization was performed using term-frequency inverse-document frequency and singular value decomposition with default parameters for the peak assay, and SCTransform for the single-nucleus RNA assay, ensuring normalization and variance stabilization. Batch effects for filtered libraries were corrected by Harmony v1.0 using the RunHarmony function in Seurat with the SCT (PCA reduction) and peaks (LSI reduction) assay. Dimensional reduction was performed with the FindMultiModalNeighbors function using dimensions 1:50 for the SCT and 2:50 for peak assay. Clusters were then identified using the FindClusters function on the basis of a weighted combination of RNA and ATAC data. Differential expression and differential chromatin accessibility between cell types was assessed by the FindMarkers function.

Differential analysis

For each cell type, we performed pseudobulk differential peak and gene expression analysis, calling differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially accessible regions (DARs) between different conditions using DESeq2 v1.40.1 in R software. Raw count matrices after quality control filtering were used as input, with major demographic factors (age, sex, and race) included as covariates. A false discovery rate (FDR) of < 0.05 (P values adjusted by the Benjamini-Hochberg method) was used to identify DEGs, while P value < .01 was used to identify DARs, both with an absolute fold change (FC) of 1.2. A joint modeling technique was used to assess the differences between PN and AD in the context of lesional versus nonlesional samples. Pseudobulk analyses were utilized to investigate the effect of disease on expression or chromatin accessibility changes for lesional differences. FDR ≤ 10% and original P < .01 were used as criteria in the interaction analysis for single-nucleus RNA and single-nucleus ATAC, respectively.

We defined disease-biased DEGs and cis-regulatory elements (CREs) as those genes or peaks that were significant in both the interaction analysis and in only one of the lesional comparisons were defined as disease-biased DEGs and CREs. At the same time, genes or peaks that were significant in the interaction analysis and in the lesional comparisons for both PN and AD had their bias direction determined by the magnitude of the FC. For example, if the absolute FC in the lesional comparisons for PN was higher than that for AD, the bias was classified as PN biased. In total, we observed 70 AD-biased DEGs and 123 AD-biased CREs, as well as 527 PN-biased DEGs and 1,927 PN-biased CREs, which agrees with the pattern we observed in the magnitude differences in FCs.

Regulatory network analysis via DIRECT-NET

Given the advantage of simultaneous profiling of gene expressions and chromatin accessibilities at the single-nucleus level, we used a machine-learning method that was based on gradient boosting, known as DIRECT-NET, to elucidate CRE-to-gene relationships. First, we targeted all DEGs identified in the interaction analysis. For each gene, we considered open chromatin regions within a 1 million base pair window on both sides, excluding the promoter region (defined as any peak within 500 bp upstream of the transcription start site), as distal candidate functional regions. Second, we filtered CRE-gene modules based on CREs with differential accessibility identified in the interaction analysis, ensuring that the disease bias direction of both DEGs and CREs are matched at the same time. Consequently, we identified 199 PN-biased CRE-gene modules and 3 AD-biased CRE-gene modules, predominantly in keratinocytes and fibroblasts.

Motif enrichment and TF activity analysis

To identify transcriptional regulators associated with disease-specific cis-regulatory modules, we performed a 2-step analysis integrating motif enrichment and motif activity scoring. Motif enrichment analysis was conducted using the FindMotifs function in the Signac R v1.9.0 package on differentially accessible peaks identified between PN- and AD-biased regulatory modules, stratified by cell type. Motifs significantly overrepresented in PN-biased or AD-biased peaks (absolute FC > 1.2 and Benjamini-Hochberg FDR < 0.05) were retained.

Motif activity was assessed by the ‘chromVAR’ algorithm (implemented in Signac) to calculate per-cell deviation scores for each motif. Deviation scores were averaged across nuclei from PN and AD samples separately within each cell type. A Wilcoxon rank-sum test was performed to identify motifs with significantly different activity between lesional and nonlesional skin. Only TF motifs showing both significant enrichment and significant differential activity (Bonferroni-corrected P < .05 and absolute FC > 1.2) were retained for downstream interpretation and visualization. All motif names were converted to human-readable labels using JASPAR motif metadata.

Regulatory module analysis of IL-31 receptor blockade

To evaluate the transcriptional programs affected by IL-31 receptor blockade, we utilized an independent bulk RNA-Seq dataset profiling lesional PN skin at baseline and after 16 weeks of treatment with nemolizumab or placebo. Differential gene expression analysis was performed by DESeq2. Two gene sets were derived: genes significantly downregulated at week 16 compared to baseline in the nemolizumab group (FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.5); and genes with significantly lower expression at week 16 in the nemolizumab group compared to placebo (FDR < 0.05). The intersection of these gene sets was used to define the “nemolizumab-restored” immune module.

To assess activity of this module across cell types in PN skin, we calculated cell type–specific module scores using the Add-ModuleScore function in Seurat, based on single-nucleus RNA-Seq profiles. Statistical comparisons of module scores between lesional and nonlesional PN samples were performed by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections (AD lesional, PN lesional, and control) were heated at 60°C for 30 minutes, deparaffinized, and rehydrated. Slides were placed in PH9 antigen retrieval buffer and heated at 125°C for 30 seconds in a pressure cooker. For immunohistochemistry, after cooling, slides were treated with 3% H2O2 (5 minutes) and blocked using 10% goat serum (30 minutes). Overnight incubation (4°C) was then performed using anti-human runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2; Abcam, catalog ab192256) at a concentration of 2.5 μg/mL. Slides were then washed, then treated with secondary antibody peroxidase (30 minutes) and diaminobenzidine substrate. For immunofluorescence staining, slides after pressure cooker were blocked using 10% donkey serum (30 minutes). Overnight incubation (4°C) was then performed using anti-human RUNX2 and vimentin (Millipore, MAB3400). Slides were then washed, treated with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibody for 30 minutes, washed again, and placed on mounting buffer.

Cell clustering and cell type annotation

After quality control, including thresholds for the unique molecular identifier counts per cell, nucleosome signal, transcription start site enrichment, and mitochondrial transcripts percentages, the final single nuclei–multiome dataset included 36,601 unique genes and 328,188 unique peak regions across 83,453 cells. Clusters identified after batch correction were annotated by comparing the cluster markers with marker genes. We identified 14 major cell types across all samples: keratinocytes (KRT14+ basal, KRT10+ differentiated, and FLG+ keratinized compartments), fibroblasts, pericytes, melanocytes, nerve cells, eccrine cells, myeloid cells, mast cells, T cells, endothelial cells, lymphatic endothelial cells, and hair follicle cells (Fig 1, B, top left). All cell types were well represented across samples and modalities, and no sample- or condition-specific cluster was identified (Fig 1, B, top right). Marker genes and peaks for each cell population showed a clear separation between these major cell types (Fig 1, C and D). The majority of cells captured (~70%) were keratinocytes and fibroblasts. Moderate shifts in cell type proportions were observed: PN lesional samples exhibited a higher proportion of hair follicle cells (5.91%) compared to other skin types (AD lesional samples, 1.64%; AD nonlesional samples, 1.23%; PN nonlesional samples, 2.02%); the proportion of keratinocytes was the highest in AD nonlesional samples (Fig 1, E). For both fibroblasts and endothelial cells, PN nonlesional samples showed the highest proportion, and T cells were most enriched in PN lesional skin.

To understand the epigenomic landscape across cell types, we performed clustering utilizing only ATAC data. We juxtaposed the resultant clustering with our integrative approach that compounded both gene expression profiles and chromatin accessibility data (ie, based on the weighted nearest neighbor approach) (Fig 1, B). Comparison between ATAC and weighted nearest neighbor clustering results using normalized mutual information when comparing with permuted clusters shows significant concordance (ie, normalized mutual information = 0.41 vs ~0.0017; P < .001; see Fig E1 in this article’s Online Repository at www.jacionline.org), indicating the degree of similarity for the ATAC-derived partitions. This outcome underscores a significant, albeit not full, consonance between stand-alone epigenomic clustering and integrative multiome clustering, also suggesting significant epigenomic difference between cell types.

Distinct PN/AD lesional and nonlesional genome in keratinocytes and fibroblasts

Pseudobulk approach on single-cell genomics data has been illustrated to be powerful and robust in highlighting variability between individual samples (see Fig E2 in the Online Repository at www.jacionline.org).27 Therefore, we examined molecular changes between the different diseases and disease states for each identified cell type using pseudobulk analysis. We were able to highlight that both the epigenome and transcriptome of keratinocytes and fibroblasts exhibited heterogeneity between lesional and nonlesional skin in both PN and AD, as evidenced by distinct clustering (Fig 1, F). Significantly, we observed prominent genomic (and modest epigenomic) differences between AD and PN in differentiated keratinocytes for both lesional and nonlesional skin, suggesting different transcription regulation for these two diseases. We did not observe significant changes in genomic or epigenomic profiles for other cell types, potentially due to insufficient power from the lower number of cells.

Differentially accessible chromatin regions in PN versus AD lesional comparisons

To characterize epigenomic perturbations characteristic of AD and PN, we identified DARs by comparing nonlesional and lesional skin samples for each cell type, conducted for AD and PN separately. Our analysis revealed 4,583 DARs for AD across all cell types, alongside a more substantial figure of 9,739 DARs identified for PN. Across all cell types, there are 737 (16.08%) DARs in AD and 2,492 (25.59%) DARs in PN located within a promoter region (<1 kbp from the transcription start site) (Fig 2, A and B, and see Figs E3 and E4 in the Online Repository at www.jacionline.org). Specifically, in differentiated keratinocytes, we observed 4,313 DARs (see Table E1 in the Online Repository) in PN (32.18% located in promoters and 40.76% with higher accessibility in lesional skin) and 2,456 DARs (Table E1) in AD (12.21% located in promoters and 75.90% with higher accessibility in lesional skin). In fibroblasts, we found 3,254 DARs in PN (13.09% located in promoters and 64.38% up in lesional skin) and 1,171 DARs in AD (13.32% located in promoters and 66.35% up in lesional skin). DARs in promoter regions tend to exhibit larger effect sizes (Fig 2, C and D), but besides promoter regions, DARs were mostly detected in distal intergenic and intronic regions. For instance, in differentiated keratinocytes from PN, 22.44% of DARs were in distal intergenic regions and 31.23% were in intron regions (Fig 2, A). In fibroblasts, 31.1% of DARs were in distal intergenic regions and 41.06% were in intronic regions (Fig 2, B). This emphasizes the crucial role of distal genomic elements in modulating the differential gene expression patterns observed between lesional and nonlesional skin.

We defined CREs as ATAC peaks outside the promoter region (>1 kb), and our dataset identified a total of 3,650 unique differential CREs in AD and a more extensive aggregation of 6,913 unique differential CREs within PN. The majority of these CREs exhibited increased accessibility in lesional skin, a pattern that was evident across differentiated keratinocytes (55.52% up in lesional skin in PN; 78.99% up in lesional skin in AD), fibroblasts (66.80% up in lesional skin in PN; 68.28% up in lesional skin in AD) and myeloid cells (98.39% up in lesional skin in PN; 73.53% up in lesional skin in AD) in both PN and AD (Table E1). It is noteworthy that the CREs identified in PN comparisons exhibited more substantial FCs across most of the cell types (including keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and T cells) compared to AD (Fig 2, E and F), indicating the epigenomic alternations in PN are not only more numerous but also of a greater magnitude on average. For instance, we found ATAC peaks in 5q23.3 (chr5-128920334-128920706, near SLC27A6), 6q21.1 (chr6-45697687-45697886, near RUNX2), 4q31.22–23 (chr4-147517982-147518344, near EDNRA) in fibroblasts that are significantly more accessible only in lesional skin of PN (FC > 8) but not in AD. Interestingly, a similar pattern was observed in the expression levels (see Fig E5 in the Online Repository at www.jacionline.org). This suggests that the molecular changes in PN lesional skin are quantitatively and qualitatively more pronounced than in AD. Focusing on the distribution of the CREs that are DARs, our findings also indicate that a substantial majority—about 95%, or 6,605 of 6,913 in PN and 3,459 of 3,650 in AD—were cell type specific. We conducted similar analysis for DEGs comparing nonlesional versus lesional and observed 81% (PN) and 84% (AD) of the DEGs to be cell type specific. This observation emphasizes the cell type–specific nature of the epigenetic modifications linked with each disease.

PN-specific regulatory modules

To better understand the unique regulatory mechanisms underlying AD and PN, we characterized disease-biased DEGs and CREs. We devised an integrative analysis that jointly models the interaction effect to identify AD- or PN-specific changes in lesional PN and AD skin. Concordant with the above findings, differentiated keratinocytes and fibroblasts exhibited higher numbers of PN-biased features (Fig 3, A and B). Thus, in differentiated keratinocytes, there were 307 PN-biased DEGs and 846 differential CREs, while there were only 63 and 49 AD-biased DEGs and differential CREs, respectively.

A, Bar plots depicting number of disease-biased DEGs identified within each cell type. B, Bar plots showing number of disease-biased differential CREs within each cell type. C and D, Venn diagrams illustrating overlap between CRE-associated genes and DEGs in context of PN/AD bias. Numbers annotated in red indicate cell type–matched overlaps.

We used gradient boosting (DIRECT-NET) to discern the connections between disease-biased DEGs and CREs. To identify gene regulatory mechanisms that distinguish the two diseases, we evaluated whether the differential peaks and genes are in the same cell type and whether they have a consistent directional disease bias. Significantly, our analysis revealed that PN-biased CREs tend to significantly associate with PN-biased DEGs within the same cell type (P < .001). In addition, we observed 260 (52.74%) of 493 DEGs linked to differential CREs in PN, while only 4 (5.71%) of 70 DEGs linked to differential CREs in AD (Fig 3, C and D), highlighting the presence of more integrated regulatory circuits in PN. All together, we identified 199 regulatory modules (ie, differential CRE-gene pairs) specific to PN (see Table E2 in the Online Repository at www.jacionline.org) and 3 modules specific to AD (see Table E3 in the Online Repository). These findings highlight the precision of cell type–specific regulatory phenomena that underpin the differential gene expression observed in PN. Functional enrichment analysis further revealed biological functions among PN-specific modules, including androgen receptor signaling regulation (FDR = 4.75 × 10−2 among CREs out of the PN-modules in differentiated keratinocytes) and response to growth factor stimulus (FDR = 2.16 × 10−5 among PN-biased CREs in fibroblasts).

We constructed gene regulatory networks by linking TFs to disease-specific regulatory modules through differentially accessible CREs. For each cell type, we identified TF motifs that were significantly enriched in PN-biased regulatory modules using motif enrichment analysis, and we further prioritized TFs with differential motif activity between PN and AD as determined by chromVAR. This analysis identified 9 significant PN-specific TF motifs that were enriched in disease-biased CREs and showed increased activity in PN skin (Fig 4, A), including modules regulated by TFs JUNB and BACH2 (BTB domain and CNC homolog 2) (Fig 4, B and C). These were subsequently linked to disease-specific regulatory modules. In fibroblasts, motifs for activator protein 1 (AP-1), including FOS and JUN, and BACH2 were found within a differential CRE (chr6-44701405-44702034) that exhibited higher accessibility in lesional PN skin. This CRE was associated with elevated expression of the target gene RUNX2, which is also upregulated in PN lesional skin (Fig 4, D). We further illustrate in Fig 4, E, that this CRE linked to RUNX2 is fibroblast specific, and we confirmed elevated protein expression of RUNX2 in PN lesional fibroblasts by immunohistochemistry (Fig 5, and see Fig E6 in the Online Repository at www.jacionline.org). In differentiated keratinocytes, the PN-specific CRE (chr18−59864192−59864731) and its linked target gene PMAIP1 exhibited higher accessibility and expression, respectively, in nonlesional samples than in lesional samples in PN with the CRE encompassing PN-biased motifs for AP-1 and JDP2 (see Fig E7 in the Online Repository).

Regulatory signaling with IL-31 receptor blockade

The IL-31 receptor inhibitor nemolizumab is effective in treating both PN and AD.1,28–30 To infer the potential regulatory modules affected by nemolizumab treatment, we compiled a gene list representing immune-activated pathways restored by the drug using an independent cohort (see Table E4 in the Online Repository at www. jacionline.org). Genes that were downregulated at 16 weeks of treatment compared to baseline and that had significantly lower expression in the treatment group compared to placebo were used for analysis. The intersection of these two lists was identified and a module score was calculated across various cell types in PN skin. The most substantial changes were detected in differentiated keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and hair follicle cells (P < 2 × 10−16) (Fig 6, A and B). These findings suggest that nemolizumab treatment markedly restored the transcriptional profile of PN skin toward nonlesional state across multiple different cell types, with particularly notable effects in fibroblasts and keratinocytes.

Furthermore, we identified 24 unique genes with expressions normalized by nemolizumab and specifically upregulated in PN lesional skin (Fig 6, C). Six of these genes were linked to CREs specific to PN, including TF PITX1 and its associated peak (chr5:135156551-135157286) in differentiated keratinocytes (Fig 6, D). MX2, which was downregulated by nemolizumab, showed higher expression in lesional skin compared to nonlesional skin within differentiated keratinocytes, and its expression negatively correlated with accessibility at ATAC peak chr21-42056425-42057789 (see Fig E8 in the Online Repository at www.jacionline.org).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we took advantage of the paired design with both nonlesional and lesional skin of the same individual, as well as joint modeling analysis using a pseudobulk approach, to highlight genomic and epigenomic differences between nonlesional and lesional skin in PN compared AD. This extends our understanding of PN beyond transcriptomic studies. Our data provide deeper insights into the regulatory and transcriptional mechanisms involved in PN skin, particularly in differentiated keratinocytes and fibroblasts, and less so for immunocytes.

The finding of lower numbers of DEGs and differential CREs observed in AD compared to PN was unexpected but may reflect greater heterogeneity in AD9,24 and more homogeneous disease manifestations in PN. This is further supported by the functional enrichment analysis of PN-specific CREs and their demographic and clinical characteristics (see Fig E9 and Table E5 in the Online Repository at www.jacionline.org). As a multifaceted disease influenced by genetic, environmental, and immunologic factors, AD presents with various clinical phenotypes. This phenotypic complexity leads to significant variability in gene expression and chromatin accessibility across different patient samples, complicating the identification of consistent DEGs and CREs. It is well documented that such high heterogeneity results in low reproducibility of DEGs, thus undermining the generalizability of differential expression findings.31 Despite this challenge, our dataset includes more AD cells and samples (10 AD samples comprising 48,177 cells vs 8 PN samples comprising 35,276 cells), theoretically providing us with greater statistical power to detect even modest effects in lesional comparisons within the context of AD.

Our results also showed that nemolizumab significantly normalized the transcriptional profiles of PN lesional skin, especially in differentiated keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and follicle cells, with highly significant changes (P < 2 × 10−16). Notably, we identified 24 unique genes normalized by nemolizumab, which were upregulated in PN lesional skin, 6 of which were linked to upregulated CREs specific to PN. For instance, PITX1 is associated with a specific ATAC peak in differentiated keratinocytes, and MX2 is downregulated by nemolizumab and negatively correlated with CRE in lesional skin. These findings suggest that the IL-31 signaling pathway, targeted by nemolizumab, plays a role in modulating the transcriptional and epigenetic landscape in keratinocytes and fibroblasts in PN.

Last, our data provide novel potential insights into some of the regulatory networks involved in PN pathogenesis. This includes BACH2, a TF essential for immune cell differentiation regulation and immune homeostasis maintenance. BACH2 has been linked to several allergic and autoimmune diseases,32 including AD, where it is considered a significant genetic risk factor.33 BACH2 activity influences whether maturing T cells become inflammatory or regulatory. Excessive skewing of adaptive immunity toward TH2 due to persistent antigen exposure can trigger TH2-mediated inflammatory conditions like AD.34 BACH2 plays a crucial role in regulating these TH2 responses.35 Previous studies have shown that regulatory T-cell differentiation is impaired in the absence of BACH2, with BACH2 deficiency in CD4+ T cells leading to defective regulatory T-cell induction.35 Interestingly, BACH2 can form a complex with BATF and bind to AP-1 consensus motif to suppress AP-1 regulation,35 and our bulk RNA-Seq studies5,9,24 have illustrated that BACH2 is downregulated in lesional skin of different inflammatory conditions. Therefore, the downregulation of BACH2 in lesional skin would enable the binding of AP1 to the shared binding sites. Our analysis specified shared binding sites of AP-1/BACH2, including the differential CRE located at chr6-44701405-44702034. This CRE exhibited higher accessibility in lesional PN samples and was correlated with increased expression of the target gene RUNX2, which is also elevated in PN lesions. RUNX2 (together with SLC27A6) has been shown to play a role in fibrosis.36,37 Our findings highlight the competitive transcription regulation of AP-1 versus BACH2 on RUNX2.

In summary, our findings offer new insights into the pathogenesis of PN and reveal molecular features that distinguish it from AD. Moreover, this study presents a single-nucleus chromatin RNA expression atlas integrating transcriptomic and epigenomic data, providing a valuable resource for future research on PN and AD.
