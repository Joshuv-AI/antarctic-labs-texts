Filament sensing tension: A bionic artificial tendon for self–force-regulated artificial muscle–driven wearable robotics
Authors: Disheng Xie, Haoyu Wang, Yujie Su, Shuk Fan Tong, Xiaolu Li, Minghao Liu, Xiangqian Shi, Shaoshuai He, Jingjing Fu, Xian Song, Xin Xia, Joanne Yiu Wan Yip, Zheng Li, Yunlong Zi, Raymond Kaiyu Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial muscles have been in development for decades and play a crucial role in the field of wearable robotics. However, a universal device for both sensing and regulating forces in artificial muscles is still absent, rendering scenario-required force control unfeasible. Inspired by Golgi tendon organs, this work proposes bionic ExoTendon for force sensing and regulating in artificial muscles as a general solution. Leveraging the principles of triboelectrification and electrostatic induction, the ExoTendon offers high linearity, minimal hysteresis, and adjustable sensitivity and range, making it suitable as the tendon of artificial muscles. Specifically, the ExoTendon effectively enable adjustment to the pretension of the artificial muscles, yielding various assistive forces under the same input. With the optimized pretension level and closed-loop force control, the artificial muscle–based exosuit achieves self–force regulation, substantially improving walking balance and speed in stroke subjects under low input, demonstrating its promise in wearable robotics and rehabilitation medicine.

INTRODUCTION

Over the past few decades, artificial muscles emulating the contraction movements of skeletal muscles have attracted considerable attention (1) and advanced because of innovations in materials science (2). With advantages such as high output force–to–weight ratio, intrinsic compliance, and compatibility with human physiology (3, 4), artificial muscles have found extensive applications in wearable robotics, such as revolutionary rehabilitation and prosthetic technologies (5–7). Since the introduction of the McKibben muscle (8), diverse designs have emerged, including muscle-like type such as pleated pneumatic artificial muscle (9, 10) and geometry-based actuators that contract and elongate (11), filamentous type like twisted polymer actuators (12) and cavatappi (13), and planar type including the pouch motor (14, 15), fluid-driven origami-inspired artificial muscles (16), and ExoMuscle (17). Current research has concentrated on integrating contraction sensing (18–22) into artificial muscles, analogous to the role of muscle spindles in skeletal muscles (23). However, most artificial muscles lack force sensing abilities, akin to the role of Golgi tendon organs (GTOs) in skeletal muscle tendons (24), achieving self–force regulation. This deficiency restricts their applicability in force-controlled environments, such as the wearable robots designed for lower limb assistance (25). While exoskeletons equipped with load cells can effectively measure and regulate interactive tension forces (26, 27), artificial muscles with non–muscle-like structures lack this regulation capability, thereby confining their control mechanisms to empirical open-loop operations and impeding overall performance in comparison to exoskeleton systems.

The challenge of incorporating the self–force-regulating ability into artificial muscles primarily arises from two factors. First, artificial muscles with filamentous types necessitate specialized filamentous sensors; when they are deployed in bundles (13) to get higher output force, this configuration leads to impractical numerous-channel sensing requirements for real-world applications. Planar-type artificial muscles induce uneven tension distributions, particularly in wearable devices, thereby necessitating the strategic placement of filamentous sensors at intervals, which also requires numerous sensing channels. Second, unlike tremendous soft strain sensors using large stretches of materials producing signal in recent years (28), the filamentous force sensors should resist large stretches, i.e., be stiff in the axial direction and even maintain sensitivity in the axial direction while exhibiting robustness against forces from other directions, complicating the design process. These highly stretchable tensile strain sensors, without rigid attachments, are unable to sense force while effectively transmitting it, causing the force to be converted into material stretch and preventing transmission along its length to the target position, damaging system efficiency (29), as detailed in note S1, table S1, and fig. S1. In addition, although planar vector sensors developed in these years are popular (30) and may have advantages when applied on planar artificial muscle, the filamentous force sensor demonstrates its own advantages of the need for fewer measuring channels as stated in note S1 and fig. S2. Current explicitly stated tension-sensing methodologies are attached to rigid structures that detect microdeformations to estimate tension, such as strain gauges and fiber Bragg gratings (31, 32). However, these methods are ill-suited for flexible devices, as deformations can compromise measurement accuracy, resulting in a trade-off between sensitivity and robustness. In addition, the necessity for indirect measurements also introduces rigid components that diminish compactness, particularly as the number of measurement channels increases.

To address these challenges, inspired by the GTOs in skeletal muscle tendons, providing load perception (33), effective force transmission, and self–force regulation abilities, the ExoTendon is presented with the abilities the same as skeletal muscle tendons when integrated with artificial muscle. Specifically, the ExoTendon is constructed on the basis of tension-sensing filaments using the mechanism based on triboelectrification, which has been applied in mechanical energy harvesting and strain sensing (34–36). The single ExoTendon filament features a linear input-output relationship, adjustable sensing range and sensitivity, flexibility, effective force transmission ability, and robust performance. Compared with filamentous strain sensors reported these years and conventional tension-sensing methods such as strain gauges (37) and fiber Bragg gratings (32), the ExoTendon filament is more suitable for the soft system ascribing to its superior sensing characteristics and device properties, with details stated in notes S1 and S2, table S2, and figs. S3 and S4. Given these advantages, multiple ExoTendon filaments can be seamlessly connected end to end to create an ExoTendon with a single channel to multiply the testing range to hundreds of newtons, with the output representing driving force or the total force exerted by the artificial muscle, regardless of tension distribution when connected serially with artificial muscles. Supported by theoretical and simulations from finite element models, which enhance its interpretability, the ExoTendon addresses long-standing deficiencies in effective tension measurement techniques. Its ability to empower wearable robotics driven by artificial muscle with self–force regulation efficacy was validated in a hip exosuit, representing a substantial advancement for next-generation intelligent wearable devices with a self–force regulation ability.

RESULTS

Concept of the ExoTendon filament

In the human body, the force regulation ability of skeletal muscles is based on GTOs for measuring the output force of skeletal muscles, thus allowing the perception of load on the hand, as illustrated in Fig. 1A. Located between the skeletal muscle and its tendon, GTOs connect the two serially (inset in Fig. 1A). These organs consist of 1b sensory axons intertwined with collagen fibrils. When the collagen fibrils are tensioned, the axons are compressed, generating action potentials (Fig. 1B) to notify the central nervous system of changes in the load on the external environment. As shown in Fig. 1C, when the output force of the biceps increases over time, the 1b sensory axon of GTOs detects this increase, showing a correlation between the firing rate and the tension. Last, the output force of skeletal muscles in the elbow is regulated to control the interaction force between the hand and the rope. Inspired by this principle, ExoTendon filaments are proposed, as shown in Fig. 1B and the micrograph in fig. S5. ExoTendon filaments can be directly connected to filamentous-type artificial muscles to achieve tension-sensing and tension-regulating abilities. ExoTendon filaments consist of a positive electrode filament and a negative electrode filament with different dielectric coatings, forming a two-filament configuration, as shown in Figs. 1B and 2A, fig. S6 (A and B), and note S3. Through the yarn twisting process or using an industrial braiding machine, the ExoTendon filament can be manufactured fast, steadily, and with uniform quality, as shown in Fig. 2B, fig. S7, and note S4, where a uniform and smooth production of 50-m ExoTendon filament is demonstrated in Fig. 2C.

The ExoTendon filament operates on the basis of triboelectrification and electrostatic induction. When the filaments come into contact, charge transfer occurs—the positive filament loses electrons on its surface, while the negative filament gains them. In the resting state, the contact area between the filaments is minimal, resulting in the largest potential difference (Fig. 1BI and fig. S6BI). When a tensile force is applied, the helical negative filament’s center diameter decreases, increasing the contact area between the filaments. This reduces the distance between the positive and negative charges, and the overall tribocharge density on the overlapped surface approaches zero. Only the tribocharges on the noncontact area now induce charges on the inner electrode, decreasing the potential difference (Fig. 1BII and fig. S6BII). Further tension increases the contact area, reducing the potential difference further (Fig. 1BIII and fig. S6BIII). Upon unloading, the ExoTendon filament returns to its original state, and the potential difference is restored (Fig. 1BIV and fig. S6BIV). By measuring the potential difference in the structure, the ExoTendon filament can effectively sense the magnitude of applied force.

The small diameter of the fibers and the weak output of each single winding are effectively overcome by the proportional amplification resulting from the accumulated charges across hundreds of windings, leading to a noticeable voltage output even under minimal force. Specifically, a comprehensive model in note S5 explains this cumulative effect, which enables the ExoTendon filament to measure forces in a distinct way compared to previous approaches. Rather than enhancing sensitivity on a small volume or area, the ExoTendon filament leverages this cumulative, distributed effect to determine the tension exerted on the filament.

Besides the two-filament configuration, three-filament configuration is introduced for different sensing ranges and sensitivities in various scenarios in Fig. 1B, note S3, and fig. S6 (C and D). The positive filament is moved to the outer layer, with the core filament being a pure dielectric for easy lectotype. Triboelectrification now occurs between the core and positive filaments, as well as between the core and negative filaments. By substituting the core material, the desired range and sensitivity can be achieved for specific applications. It is worth noting that the three-filament configuration can only be made using the industrial braiding machine, as shown in fig. S7 and note S4. This paper mainly discusses four configurations: two two-filament configurations [polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE)-silver and PTFE-polyvinyl chloride (PVC)] and two three-filament configurations (PTFE-0.4Nylon-silver and PTFE-1.0Nylon-silver), where the names represent the electrode materials and diameter used. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, the data presented are from the PTFE-0.4Nylon-silver configuration.

Performance evaluation of the ExoTendon filament

To quantitively study the sensing ability of the ExoTendon filament, the experiment platform was set by controlling various influential factors, as shown in fig. S8, where the input force can be accurately controlled. On the basis of this experiment platform and the linear input tension, the output of the ExoTendon filament sample with different original lengths is experimentally investigated, with the method stated in note S6 and results shown in fig. S9. Summarizing this result in Fig. 1C, the ExoTendon filament shows a linear relationship between input tension and output voltage, showing the same functionality with 1b sensory axons in GTOs. In addition, a theoretical model of the ExoTendon filament is studied, as shown in note S5. The corresponding theoretical and finite element method (FEM) result achieved by COMSOL also demonstrates the linear nature of this structure, which suits the experimental result. As shown in Fig. 2D and fig. S9, the output voltage at the same tension exhibits a linear increase as the ExoTendon filament original length is increased from 200 to 500 mm under constant tension. This indicates that the output signal has a proportional relationship with the ExoTendon filament length, demonstrating the aforementioned cumulative effect. As discussed in the theoretical model, the ExoTendon filament uses the integration of surface charge density to obtain the potential difference per unit length of the cross section, thus showing this linear characteristic. This result suggests that output can be enhanced by increasing the effective length of the ExoTendon filament, while expanding its testing range requires placing ExoTendon filaments in parallel, as illustrated in Fig. 2E and fig. S10. Although the ExoTendon filament with a PTFE-0.4Nylon-silver configuration has a linear testing range of only 10 N, connecting multiple ExoTendon filaments in parallel can increase the range to hundreds of newtons; this forms the basis for the large output force measurement of artificial muscle. It is worth noting that the sensitivity remains unchanged, whereas the testing range increases multiple times with a parallel arrangement. By increasing the length of the sensor, the system can resolve forces as low as 0.02 N, as shown in fig. S11.

The response of the ExoTendon filament in high frequency is also tested, as shown in Fig. 2F, using the configuration detailed in note S6.1 and figs. S12 and S13. The output range remains constant as the frequency of the vibration exciter increased up to 8 Hz, demonstrating the ExoTendon filament’s suitability for deployment in artificial muscle’s force measurement scenarios. In addition, shorter ExoTendon filaments are used to reduce the amplitude requirements on the vibration generator to further study the response in a higher frequency range. The results shown in fig. S14 indicate that when the input force range is consistent, the output range of the ExoTendon filament first remains constant up to 10 Hz, then increases, and subsequently decreases, which meets demands for high-frequency applications. As shown in fig. S15, the lifetime test for the ExoTendon filament, conducted under the same configuration, demonstrates no output decay after more than 6000 cycles, highlighting the stability of the configuration. After 60,000 cycles, the output range decays by only less than 5%, as shown in fig. S16.

To further assess performance, five key metrics have been defined (the details are shown in note S7): (i) range: the maximum input force at which the ExoTendon filament maintains linear output response; (ii) linearity: the linear correlation coefficient between output voltage and input force at the upper bound of the range; (iii) sensitivity: the maximum output voltage at the upper bound of the range, divided by the range and length; (iv) hysteresis: the maximum difference in output voltage at the same input force during tensioning and unloading, divided by the maximum output range; (v) robustness: the ratio of sensitivity to radial pressing sensitivity. These five metrics are applied to evaluate the performance of the different ExoTendon filament configurations in a radar plot analysis.

Subsequently, the major influential factors to the performance of the ExoTendon filament are investigated. Because the ExoTendon filament consists of a helical filament wound around a core, the helix pitch strongly affects the sensitivity. The ExoTendon filament can be produced with variable pitches through the manufacturing process illustrated in note S4 and fig. S7. As shown in Fig. 2G, increasing pitch from 1 to 2 mm improves the sensitivity, yielding a larger output for the same input and original length. However, the sensitivity then decreases as the pitch is increased further. Samples with three pitches are built with the PTFE-0.4Nylon-silver configuration, and the testing results match this modeled trend. Increasing the pitch further causes lower sensitivity. This is because as the pitch increases, the length of the ExoTendon filament decreases faster than the contraction of the core diameter of the helix. Ultimately, all four configurations are chosen with a 2.5-mm pitch, as this offered a good balance of higher sensitivity and manufacturing efficiency.

To further investigate the sensing performance with different materials and structures, samples with the abovementioned four configurations are tested, and the results are shown in Fig. 2H. All configurations exhibit high linearity, with the PTFE-silver configuration achieving up to 0.9982, and low hysteresis, with the PTFE-1.0Nylon-silver configuration reaching as low as 4.6%. The configurations also exhibit varying range and sensitivity, with the PTFE-PVC configuration achieving up to the 34-N range and the PTFE-silver configuration achieving up to 1.59 V/N·m sensitivity, as detailed in the radar graph in Fig. 2I. Increasing the core filament’s elastic modulus increases the range but decreases sensitivity, as can be seen from the gradient and range of the horizontal axis in Fig. 2H, fig. S17, and note S6.2. Notably, the hysteresis between the force and sensor output is lower than the force and strain of whole filaments, regardless of whether the configuration has two or three filaments, as shown in fig. S17. This phenomenon reveals that the hysteresis metric is independent of the core property, enabling the flexible combination of materials for diverse scenarios. This result also matched with the theoretical result, where the model in note S5 derived that the output is from the contact between the helix and core filaments, independent of the core filament’s strain.

Because the ExoTendon filament leverages this cumulative, distributed effect to determine the tension exerted on the thread, the radial pressing will affect the output. To obtain the robustness metric defined in note S7, the four configurations are pressed radially by a linear motor, as shown in fig. S18 and note S6.3, all showing a linear relationship between the radial pressing force and output voltage. The result shows that for the PTFE-0.4Nylon-silver configuration, although the pressing force reaches up to 30 N, the output is only 0.028 V per 60 mm, which is relatively low compared to the axial output per force, as shown in Fig. 2D. This configuration exhibits the highest robustness, reaching up to 44.2045 as shown in Fig. 2I. The result also shows a trend that the stiffer the core filament, i.e., the higher the elastic modulus, the lower the robustness metric achieved. This is because the axial sensitivity is highly dependent on the elastic modulus of the core filament, whereas the radial sensitivity is not. In practical applications, ExoTendon filaments may encounter multiaxial stresses. It also shows a low shear force response as compared to its response under the axial tension (note S6.3 and fig. S19), with the torsion along the filament avoided as required (note S6.3 and fig. S20). Furthermore, the filament itself shows good durability, withstanding more than 10,000 cycles of direct abrasion (note S6.3 and fig. S21). The encapsulation is also robust, tolerating environments with up to 90% relative humidity (note S6.3 and fig. S22) and more than 20°C temperature rises (note S6.3 and fig. S23). In addition, cables are often guided through pulleys in practical scenarios. To show the effect of the metrics of robustness, the impact of pulleys on the sensor output is investigated. The same tension range is applied to the same ExoTendon filament with a PTFE-0.4Nylon-silver configuration through different pulleys, as illustrated in fig. S24 and note S6.4. The results show a less than 3% difference, suggesting that pulleys have a minimal effect on the sensor output. This indicates the ExoTendon filament’s robustness to lateral compression, as the special structure of the ExoTendon filament and guided length only account for a small part of the total length.

Integration of the ExoTendon with filamentous and planar artificial muscle

Skeletal muscle architecture, composed of filamentous muscle fibers (Fig. 3A) (38), as stated in the previous part, has inspired the development of filamentous artificial muscles (39–41). In recent years, planar-shape artificial muscles have also emerged (42, 43), demonstrating high compatibility when applied to the human body. For instance, pouch motors (14, 15) and ExoMuscles (17) have achieved various applications in assisting joints such as the ankle (44, 45), hip (46), and elbow (47). The ExoTendon filament can be directly integrated serially with these filamentous artificial muscles. With a typical output range of 100 to 101 N, the four ExoTendon filament configurations are inherently suitable for such applications. However, when deploying bundled filamentous or planar artificial muscle architectures to replicate the behavior of skeletal muscles (13), challenges arise not only from the complexities of multichannel measurements but also from distributed tension sensing. The ExoTendon filament features linear and low hysteresis output, and because of a stable manufacturing method, its sensitivity along the length can be regarded as uniform. Consequently, when a single ExoTendon is folded multiple times, the total output force is the sum of the forces distributed across the folds, regardless of how the tension is distributed.

As illustrated in Fig. 3 (A and B), the ExoMuscle’s bionic structure aims to mimic the behavior of skeletal muscle when worn externally. The ExoTendon filament is sewn onto a fabric in parallel with the ExoMuscle, serving as the tendon equivalent to the Golgi tendon in skeletal muscle. Thus, the parallel ExoTendon filament sewn onto the fabric is referred to as ExoTendon. Regardless of how the output force is distributed, the total output from the ExoTendon filaments remains consistent, as shown in Fig. 3 (C and D). Validation of this conclusion is conducted in two steps. As depicted in fig. S25, the ExoTendon filament is fixed in parallel using a frame. The upper end connects to a load cell as a reference, while weights are added to the lower side incrementally. When a weight is placed in the middle of the frame, the force on each ExoTendon filament remains equal. However, when the weight is positioned near the side, the tension on the ExoTendon filaments varies, causing the filament on the opposite side to loosen. Despite these changes, the calibrated results remain consistent with those obtained when the weight is centrally hung.

The structure shown in fig. S25 is impractical for use, as the filaments are prone to scatter when tension is lost. Therefore, a fabric base is necessary to stabilize the ExoTendon and maintain its parallel shape. An embroidery machine is used, with the lower threads replaced by the ExoTendon filament, as shown in fig. S26 (A and B), ensuring that each segment of the ExoTendon filament remains straight and of uniform length. With high compatibility with the embroidery machine, the ExoTendon can be produced in various shapes and patterns. For instance, when testing the same distribution as shown in Fig. 3D and fig. S27, the ExoTendon is subjected to the same weight, and the output matches the load cell reference, confirming that the ExoTendon effectively senses different distributed tension changes. Its detailed manufacturing method and encapsulation are shown in note S8 and fig. S28, providing stable shape and resistance to interference.

To further validate the ExoTendon to sense the sum of tension regardless of tension distribution along its width, a two-module ExoMuscle is made and connected serially with the ExoTendon. A weightlifting test is conducted, as shown in Fig. 3 (E and F), where the weight is hung on the planar artificial muscle using a strap sewn onto it. This setup resulted in concentrated force distribution in the middle, with lower forces at the sides. The results, illustrated in Fig. 3F, indicate only a 1.9% root mean square error compared to the load cell measurement at the top.

An intriguing feature of skeletal muscle architecture is its adaptability (48), as shown in Fig. 3G. While parallel fiber alignment (where muscle fibers run parallel to the tendon direction) (49) maximizes the contraction ratio, human lower limbs use pennate architecture (48)—where fibers angle relative to the tendon—to prioritize force output. This pennate configuration allows greater fiber packing density within the same cross-sectional area, trading the contraction range for increased force generation. The ExoMuscle replicates this biological advantage through its planar design as shown in Fig. 3H, demonstrating pennation capability when fiber-tube angles form during nonaxial movement. Notably, dual ExoTendons with differing pattern angles enable simultaneous measurement of both force magnitude and direction as shown in Fig. 3I as a hypertendon because normal tendon can only sense force magnitude. The detailed analysis and calculations are shown in note S9 and fig. S29.

ExoTendon empowers hip exosuits for stroke rehabilitation with self–force regulation

Stroke survivors often experience sensorimotor impairments and disabilities in their lower extremities (50); one characterization is weakened hip flexor (51), profoundly affecting their ability to engage in community activities. Various hip robotic devices have been developed, ranging from rigid hip exoskeletons (52, 53) to soft exosuits driven by cable (54, 55) and artificial muscles (25). Compared to a motor-driven device using strain gauges, an artificial muscle–driven device lacks effective force regulation (45), as shown in Fig. 4A, where users can only wear the artificial muscle in an empirical way, and the control is open-looped without maintaining the required force. To address these challenges, we first sewed the ExoTendon with the ExoMuscle as shown in Fig. 3B to endow this artificial muscle with force-sensing, effective transmitting, and self–force-regulating abilities. Then, we designed a compact exosuit driven by these self–force-regulated artificial muscles, aimed at providing walking assistance for patients with stroke and verifying the significance of the ExoTendon in empowering artificial muscle applications.

(C) On the basis of the ExoTendon, first, when wearing the artificial muscle, an optimized pretension can be determined during wearing on the body. Then, using the ExoTendon as the medium to regulate force during closed-loop force control, the required assistive force can be achieved stably and reliably. (D) When artificial muscle is worn on the body, the lumped model is that artificial muscle connected to the joint through the elastic part with an unknown elastic modulus. (E) The maximum assistive force increases with the pretension level at the same input pressure. (F) The maximum extension angle decreases with the increasing pretension level. As illustrated in Fig. 4 (B and C), the hip exosuit consists of a waist strap, a thigh strap, and two ExoMuscles connected serially with the ExoTendon to acquire the assisting force exerted by the ExoMuscle on the body. The ExoMuscles wrap around the thigh in the same configuration as described in (47). When the exosuit is pressurized, it assists hip flexion. ExoMuscle inflating and deflating occur during 40 to 80% of the gait cycle, as shown in fig. S30. This scenario represents a pure force control situation, where only contraction or strain sensing of the artificial muscle cannot be used for control; the reason is stated as follows. As shown in Fig. 4D, the artificial muscle connects to the body through elastic straps, and the skin and muscle also have their own elastic moduli. Consequently, the lumped model of the artificial muscle at the hip resembles a serial elastic actuator (56). This implies that the artificial muscle is linked to an elastic module without the knowledge of its elastic modulus, especially when the elastic module exhibits nonlinear elastic characteristics. Therefore, the contraction length or the strain of the ExoMuscle does not directly reflect the assistance force on the thigh necessary to establish the required force profile. As a breakthrough, with the ExoTendon connected serially, self–force regulation or effective closed-loop control of the exosuit driven by the artificial muscle becomes feasible, as shown in Fig. 4C. The control system uses on-off valves; because of the presence of the elastic module in the lumped model, the resulting assisting force can be a smooth curve, as shown in fig. S30.

Another crucial consideration is that unlike cable-driven exosuits where the cables can be loosened or tightened through continuous rotation of the cable spool, the artificial muscle operates with a fixed contraction ratio. If the artificial muscle is configured with low pretension, the force response is delayed, and the required assisting force may not be achieved, as all the contraction length may be consumed in pulling the elastic module. If the artificial muscle is set with excessive pretension, hip extension becomes restricted, leading to a decreased hip extension angle. Thus, maintaining the appropriate pretension force is critical for any joint exosuit driven by artificial muscles. The ExoTendon provides force feedback to adjust the optimal pretension when wearing the exosuit, enhancing system efficiency. As shown in Fig. 4E, the result shows that with the same input pressure level, higher pretension corresponds to higher assisting force on the body, thus higher flexion angle assistance for stroke survivors. Although excessive pretension brings a higher flexion angle, the increased resistance force during extension hinders the extension of the stroke survivors, as shown in Fig. 4F. Hence, the optimized pretension force is chosen as the middle level for subjects.

To further validate the impact of the ExoTendon-empowered self–force-regulated hip exosuit, closed-loop assistive force control using the ExoTendon is conducted on three stroke subjects for evaluation. The control is based on a fine-tuned proportional integral controller that uses the force error at each step. As depicted in Fig. 4G, as time goes by, the actual assistive force gradually converges to target. The control results indicate that although the assistive force approximates the target, the required input pressure fluctuates over a 50-kPa range. This demonstrates that open-loop pneumatic control alone cannot reliably determine the effect on the body. Therefore, closed-loop assistive control using the ExoTendon increases system stability, making soft wearable robots a reliable tool for studying biomechanical changes resulting from external forces. This also establishes a foundation for intelligent control algorithms on wearable robots driven by artificial muscles. As shown in Fig. 4H and fig. S31, wearing the pretension optimized hip exosuit resulted in an average hip range of motion increase of 5° on the impaired side and walking velocity increases over 0.1 m/s for all three subjects at low input, demonstrating the efficacy of the ExoTendon-powered exosuit system.

DISCUSSION

We have reported a bionic artificial tendon for self–force-regulated artificial muscle–driven wearable robotics. This proposed ExoTendon mimics the function of GTO in muscle tendon for skeletal muscle through triboelectric filaments as a tension sensor. This sensor exhibits high linearity, low hysteresis, and high robustness to radial pressing and bending. Furthermore, it demonstrates the ability to adjust the range and sensitivity by changing the core materials and pitch. Both analytical and FEM models have been constructed to verify and predict the performance of the single filament. With its adjustable performance metrics, this filament can be deployed in filamentous artificial muscle that requires tension sensing. Compared to indirect conventional tension-sensing methods, it offers advantages of being soft, compliant, compact, and seamlessly integrable in both concentrated and distributed tension-sensing scenarios. Using an embroidery machine to integrate it into parallel arrangement on fabric, the structure of the ExoTendon is formed, which has the ability to sense the sum of the force exerted regardless of its distribution. Two ExoTendons can also act as a hypertendon for artificial muscle that can sense not only the magnitude but also the direction of the force. This ExoTendon is applied in a hip exosuit that helps stroke survivors to flex their hips, which reveals that a higher pretension level yields higher assistive force at the same input level, whereas excessive pretension force hinders hip extension, which demands the ExoTendon as a force regulator for optimized pretension. Equipped with a self–force-regulating ability empowered by the ExoTendon, the hip exosuit achieves stable and controllable assistive force exerted on the body and helps increase the impaired side hip range of motion and walking velocity of stroke survivors effectively by 5° and 0.1 m/s, respectively, at low input. The proposed ExoTendon addresses the shortcomings of artificial muscles in force regulation, enhancing the integration of perception and actuation in artificial muscles. In addition, this method provides an approach for the next-generation wearable robots with a self–force regulation ability and plays a substantial role in fields such as assistive and rehabilitation robots.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The nonstretchable core of the ExoTendon filament necessitates high sensitivity at small strains; therefore, enhancing sensitivity primarily depends on the choice of material. Two materials with different series can transfer charge, and the two should have a sufficiently large separation in their triboelectric series (57). Considering the large amount of elemental filament required for mass production, we prefer to use existing commercial materials.

In the two-filament configuration of the ExoTendon filament, the negative filament is a commercial PTFE-coated silver-plated copper wire. PTFE is a dielectric material with a highly negative triboelectric series (57). The positive filament is either a commercial silver/polyester fiber–blended yarn for the PTFE-silver configuration or a commercial PVC-coated steel wire for the PTFE-PVC configuration.

In the three-filament configuration of the ExoTendon filament, the negative filament is also a commercial polytetrafluoroethylene-coated silver-plated copper wire. The positive filament is a silver/polyester fiber–blended yarn, and the core filament is a commercial nylon filament. This configuration is denoted as PTFE-0.4Nylon-silver or PTFE-1.0Nylon-silver, where the core nylon has a diameter of 0.4 or 1.0 mm, respectively.

It is worth noting that beyond the pitch discussed in Fig. 2G, the twist direction and twisting tension affect the final performance of the sensor, as detailed in note S4. For all ExoTendon filament configurations, the twisted ExoTendon filament is coated with a polyurethane layer for isolation and packaging, providing a stable shape and consistent force-output characteristics. The encapsulation method of the ExoTendon is introduced in note S8.
