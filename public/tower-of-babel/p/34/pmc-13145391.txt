Epidemiological characteristics and non-linear risk factors of surgical site infections in patients with cancer: a propensity score–matched case-control study
Authors: Yongzheng Ma, Yue Rong, Junyong Guo, Shijing Jia, Jixiang Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundSurgical site infection (SSI) imposes a significant burden on patients with cancer, yet comprehensive studies on the cancer-specific SSI remain limited.MethodsA case–control study analysed SSI epidemiology in 20,370 cancer patients who underwent surgery at a specialized hospital in Shandong Province, China, between 2021 and 2023, using propensity score matching (PSM) to adjust for confounders. Multi-variable conditional logistic regression and restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to identify risk factors and non-linear associations.ResultsSSI incidence was 1.23%, predominating in males, class II wounds, organ/space SSI, and colorectal surgery. Microbiological testing recovered 277 pathogens, mainly Gram-negative bacteria (67.1%). Gram-negative isolates showed high resistance to ampicillin and cephalosporins, while carbapenems and amikacin remained effective. Independent risk factors were body mass index (BMI) (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 1.090), postoperative antibiotic therapy (aOR: 3.662), and prolonged postoperative antibiotic therapy (aOR: 1.214). Protective factors included extended pre-operative antibiotic therapy on the day of surgery (aOR: 0.958) and elective surgery (aOR: 0.325). A non-linear relationship appeared between SSI risk and pre-operative white blood cell (WBC) counts. SSI risk increased linearly with BMI above 25 kg/m2 and operative time exceeding 161 minConclusionIn cancer patients, SSI is mainly caused by Gram-negative bacteria resistant to ampicillin and cephalosporins. Risk factors included higher BMI, prolonged surgery, and postoperative antibiotic use, whereas pre-operative antibiotic therapy on the day of surgery and elective surgery was protective. A non-linear relationship was observed between SSI risk and pre-operative WBC counts. These findings inform prevention strategies but require validation in multi-centre prospective studies.Keywords: Surgical site infection (SSI), Cancer, Epidemiological characteristics, Non-linear risk factor, Case–control study

Introduction

Surgical site infection (SSI) is defined as an infection caused by pathogens from the skin and the environment that proliferate at or near the surgical site [1]. If the pathogens penetrate into a surgical cut and proliferate, patients may develop fever (>38°C), severe pain or tenderness, localized swelling, erythema, and purulent drainage from the incision, which may contribute to delayed wound healing, disability, and even death [2]. It not only increases postoperative morbidity and mortality but also significantly prolongs hospitalization, increases medical costs, and may even necessitate reoperation in some patients [[3], [4], [5]].

Clinical studies have reported that SSI is one of the most frequently reported types of hospital-acquired infection (HAI), accounting for approximately 20% of all cases of HAIs [6]. Data from the United States Centers for Disease Control National Nosocomial Infections Surveillance (CDC NNIS) system showed that the incidence of SSI varies from 2% to 5% in the month following surgery [7,8]. Based on the estimates by the World Health Organization (WHO), the SSI rate in low- and middle-income countries was 2–20 times higher than that in high-income countries [9]. The latest study demonstrated that the overall incidence of SSI in China is 3.18%, ranging from 1.0% to 14.89% [10]. These studies imply that SSI remains a relatively common, hidden, and serious burden for patients and healthcare systems.

Cancer is one of the most important factors increasing the risk of SSI [11]. Previous studies have reported that the incidence of SSI following malignant bone tumour surgery was 3–10 times higher than that after general orthopaedic surgeries [12,13]. A recent retrospective case–control study proved that the risk of SSI in patients with malignant uterine tumours was markedly higher than that in patients with benign uterine tumours [14]. Kamboj et al. investigated the risk of SSI in patients with and without cancer and identified potential predictors of SSI after colorectal surgery [15]. They reported that the risk of SSI following colorectal resection is higher in patients with disseminated cancer. In another study with 740 patients who underwent colorectal surgery for neoplastic diseases, the risk of SSI was higher among patients with advanced tumours (stage ≥ IIB) than among those with local diseases [16]. Patients with cancer often have several risk factors for SSI, such as advanced age, poor nutritional state owing to loss of appetite, low immunity caused by radiotherapy or chemotherapy, and surgical site dermatologic problems caused by postoperative irradiation [[17], [18], [19]]. However, SSI has received less attention among patients with cancer, which requires more comprehensive, systematic, and in-depth studies.

To address this research gap, this study employed propensity score matching (PSM) and restricted cubic splines (RCS) models and investigated the epidemiological characteristics and risk factors of SSI among patients with cancer at a specialized cancer hospital in Shandong Province from 2021 to 2023. This study sought to establish a critical evidence base for perioperative prevention and early treatment of SSI in patients with cancer.

Patients and methods

Study population and design

A retrospective case–control study was conducted at a specialized cancer hospital in Shandong Province. Patients who underwent surgery at our institute from January 2021 to December 2023 were included in this study.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients diagnosed with cancer confirmed based on clinical or pathological diagnosis; (2) patients who underwent surgery at our institute between January 2021 and December 2023; and (3) patients aged 18 years or older during the operation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who were not diagnosed with cancer; (2) patients who underwent surgery for other diseases; (3) patients younger than 18 years at the time of surgery; (4) patients with other acute infectious diseases before surgery; and (5) patients with missing data ratios of more than 50%. Collection of demographics and clinical data

The required data were extracted from nosocomial infection surveillance records and patients’ charts. Before data collection, all staff members involved in data collection were trained. According to the objective and content of this study, demographic data, including sex, age, height, weight, fasting blood glucose (FBG), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and pre-operative white blood cell (WBC) counts of the participants, were collected. The body mass index (BMI) of participants was calculated using their height and weight. Hyperglycaemia was defined as FBG of ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or a self-report history of hyperglycaemia. Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or a DBP of ≥ 90 mmHg or a self-report history of hypertension.

The clinical data included surgical department classification, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, National Nosocomial Infections Surveillance System (NNIS) risk index, type of surgery (elective surgery or emergency surgery), length of surgery, surgical wound classification, and perioperative antibiotic therapy.

Diagnostic criteria of SSI

The diagnosis of SSI was made following the diagnostic criteria for nosocomial infection (Trial), issued by the Ministry of Health of the People’s Republic of China in 2001. Based on the guideline, the SSI was defined as an infection of the surgical site occurring within 30 days after the procedure or one year if a prosthesis was implanted. Moreover, SSI has been classified into the following three categories: (1) superficial incisional SSI involving only the skin and subcutaneous tissues at the site of incision, (2) deep incisional SSI involving fascial and muscle layers, and (3) organ/space SSI involving a body cavity or visceral organs.

To guarantee the accuracy of diagnosis, SSI was identified by physicians and infection control practitioners using the same criteria. Only patients with cancer who were diagnosed with SSI by a physician and an infection control practitioner were included in this study.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.2.2 (R Core Team [2024]; R: A language and environment for statistical computing; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; URL https://www.R-project.org/). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (first quartile [Q1], third quartile [Q3]). The normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables with normal distribution were compared using the Student's t-test, but continuous variables with non-normal distribution were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Qualitative variables are expressed as frequencies (proportions) and were compared using the χ test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

PSM was employed to minimize potential confounding effects and balance the differences between the two groups in terms of baseline characteristics. Age, sex, and department of surgery were the variables used for matching. The MatchIt package (1:1 matching with the nearest neighbour with caliper matching, caliper = 0.1) was employed for calculation. Covariate balance before and after matching was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMDs). An absolute SMD < 0.1 was considered adequate balance. The balance improvement was visualized using a Love plot.

After matching, univariate analyses were performed using the conventional tests described above. Variables with a P value ≤ 0.2 in the univariate analysis were included in a multi-variable conditional logistic regression model to adjust for confounding and identify independent risk factors for SSI. Results were presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). The potential non-linear associations between SSI and perioperative variables, including both patient-specific and surgical procedure–related parameters, were identified using the RCS model. A P value < 0.05 (two-tailed) was considered statistically significant.

Results

Epidemiological and department distribution of SSI

We initially screened 24,324 hospitalized patients with cancer. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 20,370 patients were finally included. Among them, 299 patients developed SSI, with an incidence rate of 1.23%. Demographic analysis revealed a male predominance among patients with SSI (174 males vs 125 females), and infected male patients exhibited a higher mean age (61.87 ± 10.14 years) than their female counterparts (56.18 ± 12.17 years) (Figure 2A).

Epidemiological and departmental distribution of surgical site infection (SSI) (total = 299). (A) Sex, (B) wound classification, (C) distribution of SSI subtypes, and (D) department distribution of SSI.

Categorical analysis of surgical wound classifications demonstrated that class II wounds (clean-contaminated) were observed in the majority of patients with SSI (n = 182, 60.9%), whereas class I wounds (clean) exhibited the lowest frequency (n = 52, 17.4%) (Figure 2B).

Distribution analysis of SSI subtypes indicated that organ/space SSI was the most common (n = 172, 57.7%), followed by superficial incisional (n = 71, 23.8%) and deep incisional SSI (n = 56, 18.7%). The distribution of SSI subtypes is presented in Figure 2C.

Department-level stratification identified colorectal surgery as the highest-risk specialty, accounting for 32.8% of all SSI cases. This was followed by gastrointestinal surgery (21.4%), breast surgery (12.0%), hepatobiliary/ orthopaedic surgery (7.7%), and oesophageal surgery (7.7%) (Figure 2D). In addition, department-specific SSI rates varied considerably, with the highest rates observed in gastrointestinal (3.42%), colorectal (3.35%), hepatobiliary/orthopaedic (2.83%), oesophageal (1.88%), and urological surgery (1.50%) (Supplementary Figure S1).

Bacteriological spectrum in SSI

We conducted a comprehensive microbiological analysis of pathogens isolated from 299 patients with SSI, identifying 277 distinct microbial isolates. The taxonomic distribution identified Gram-negative bacteria as the predominant category, comprising 186 isolates (67.1% of total pathogens). Among them, Escherichia coli demonstrated the highest prevalence with 81 isolates (29.2%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (32 isolates, 11.6%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (19 isolates, 6.9%), and Enterobacter cloacae (10 isolates, 3.6%). Other Gram-negative isolates included Serratia marcescens (2.9%), Proteus mirabilis (2.2%), Enterobacter hormaechei (1.8%), Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (1.8%), Citrobacter freundii (1.4%), Acinetobacter baumannii (0.7%), and Morganella morganii (0.7%).

Gram-positive organisms represented the second-highest microbial population, with a total of 88 isolates (31.8%). Staphylococcus aureus emerged as the dominant Gram-positive species with 34 isolates (12.3%). Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) comprised 28 isolates (10.1%), including Staphylococcus epidermidis (18 isolates, 6.5%), Staphylococcus hominis (3 isolates, 1.1%), Staphylococcus haemolyticus (3 isolates, 1.1%), Staphylococcus capitis (2 isolates, 0.7%), Staphylococcus xylosus (1 isolate, 0.4%), and Staphylococcus lugdunensis (1 isolate, 0.4%). A detailed distribution of CoNS isolates is presented in Table S1. Other Gram-positive isolates included Enterococcus faecium (10 isolates, 3.6%), Streptococcus anginosus (6 isolates, 2.2%), and Enterococcus faecalis (4 isolates, 1.4%).

Fungal pathogens represented a few cases, and only three Candida species were isolated (1.1% of total microbial burden). Complete results are presented in Table I.

Antimicrobial susceptibility and resistance profiles in SSI

A detailed analysis of antimicrobial susceptibility and resistance profile for the top three Gram-negative and Gram-positive isolates was performed. The highest resistance rates to ampicillin were observed for Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae, with resistance rates of 94.4% and 100.0%, respectively (Table II). Additionally, resistance to cephalosporins, such as cefazolin, cefuroxime, and ceftriaxone, was also substantial for Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae. In contrast, carbapenems (meropenem and imipenem) and amikacin retained excellent activity against Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae, with susceptibility rates exceeding 95.0%. Pseudomonas aeruginosa remained highly susceptible to aminoglycosides (amikacin, gentamicin, and tobramycin), and susceptibility to fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin) also remained high (>94.4%). It was noteworthy that Pseudomonas aeruginosa showed variable susceptibility to carbapenems, maintaining high susceptibility to meropenem (94.4%) while exhibiting measurable resistance to imipenem (11.1%).

Comparative analysis of demographic characteristics before and after PSM

A 1:1 PSM protocol was implemented to mitigate demographic disparities in terms of age, sex, and departmental distribution between cases and controls while controlling for confounding biases. Successful matching was achieved for 298 paired subjects (99.67% matching efficiency), with only one case excluded due to a matching failure.

Prematching analysis revealed significant differences. The non-SSI group had a mean age of 56.94 ± 11.92 years compared with 59.44 ± 11.05 years in the SSI group (t = −3.604, P < 0.05). Substantial disparities were also observed in terms of departmental distribution (χ2 = 197.686, P < 0.05), confirming baseline imbalance across surgical specialties.

After PSM adjustment, patients’ characteristics were effectively harmonized. Sex distribution demonstrated parity between groups (non-SSI: 176 males, 122 females; SSI: 173 males, 125 females; χ2 = 0.062, P = 0.803). Age equivalence was achieved with a mean age of 59.44 years in the SSI group compared with 59.49 years in the non-SSI group (t = −0.051, P = 0.959). Departmental allocation equilibrium was achieved across colorectal surgery, gastrointestinal surgery, breast surgery, hepatobiliary/orthopaedic surgery, oesophageal surgery, etc. (χ2 = 0.043, P = 0.999) (Table IV). Covariate balance was further confirmed by SMDs, all of which were < 0.1 after matching (Table S2). The improvement in balance was illustrated by the Love plot (Figure S2).

Risk factor analysis for SSI

Univariate analysis after PSM

We conducted a univariate analysis to identify potential risk factors associated with SSI after PSM. The results showed that higher BMI, prolonged surgery, emergency surgery, high NNIS risk index, postoperative antibiotic therapy, and prolonged antibiotic therapy were risk factors for SSI (P < 0.05 for all).

However, no significant differences were observed in terms of diabetes, hypertension, pre-operative antibiotic therapy patterns (including the use and duration of antimicrobial therapy on the day of surgery and pre-operatively, and compliance with 0.5–1 h or 0.5–2 h prophylaxis guidelines), intra-operative antibiotic therapy, pre-operative WBC counts, ASA classification, or wound classification categories (P > 0.05 for all) (Table V).

Multi-variable conditional logistic regression analysis after PSM

Variables with a P value less than or equal to 0.2 in univariate analysis were included in a multi-variable conditional logistic regression analysis to control for confounding factors and identify independent risk factors for SSI. The final adjusted model (Figure 3) revealed that BMI (aOR = 1.090, 95% CI: 1.025–1.160; P < 0.05), postoperative antibiotic therapy (aOR = 3.662, 95% CI: 1.228–10.919; P < 0.05), and prolonged postoperative antibiotic therapy (aOR = 1.214 95% CI: 1.162–1.267; P < 0.05) were independent risk factors for SSI. Quantitatively, the risk of SSI increased by 9.0% for every 1-unit increase in BMI. Patients who used antibiotics postoperatively had up to a 3.66 times higher risk of SSI than those who did not use antibiotics postoperatively. In addition, the risk of SSI increased by 21.4% for each additional day of postoperative antibiotic therapy. Notably, prolonged pre-operative antibiotic therapy on the day of surgery (aOR = 0.958, 95% CI: 0.935–0.982; P < 0.05) and elective surgery (aOR = 0.325, 95% CI: 0.121–0.875; P < 0.05) was identified as a protective factor. Specifically, for each additional hour of pre-operative antibiotic therapy on the day of surgery, there was a 4.2% decrease in the risk of SSI. Compared with emergency surgery, the risk of elective surgery was decreased by 67.5%.

The intricate relationship between the risk of SSI and patient-related or surgery-related factors

An RCS model was employed to further explore the intricate relationship between the risk of SSI and patient-related or surgery-related factors. The RCS analysis revealed a non-linear relationship between the risk of SSI and pre-operative WBC counts (P overall < 0.05, P non-linear < 0.05). Specifically, the risk of SSI was significantly decreased when the pre-operative WBC count was less than 6.91 × 109/L or more than 8.27 × 109/L. On the contrary, the risk of SSI significantly increased when the pre-operative WBC count was between 6.91 × 109/L and 8.27 × 109/L. There was a linear relationship between the risk of SSI and BMI (P overall < 0.05, P non-linear > 0.05). No significant linear or non-linear relationships were found between the risk of SSI and other patient-related factors, such as FBG, SBP, and DBP (P overall > 0.05, P non-linear > 0.05) (Figure 4A-4E).

In terms of surgical factors, there was a linear relationship between the risk of SSI and the duration of postoperative antibiotic therapy (P overall < 0.05, P non-linear > 0.05). The risk of SSI significantly increased when the duration of postoperative antibiotic therapy exceeded 9 days. Similarly, the risk of SSI was linearly related to the length of surgery (P overall < 0.05, P non-linearly > 0.05). In particular, the risk of SSI steadily increased when the duration of surgery exceeded 161.26 min. No significant association was observed between the risk of SSI and other surgery-related factors, such as the duration of antibiotic therapy on the day of surgery and the duration of pre-operative antibiotic therapy (P overall > 0.05, P non-linear > 0.05) (Figure 4F-4I).

Discussion

SSI, an important type of HAI, significantly affects the prognosis and economic burden of cancer. In this study, we investigated the incidence of SSI, distribution of pathogenic bacteria, and risk factors of SSI in patients with cancer in a specialized cancer hospital in Shandong Province from 2021 to 2023. Furthermore, using the RCS model, we studied the intricate relationship between patient-related and surgery-related factors and the risk of SSI in patients with cancer.

The incidence of SSI and distribution of pathogenic bacteria in patients with cancer

The incidence of SSI was 1.23% in the specialized cancer hospital in Shandong Province from 2021 to 2023. This incidence was similar to that reported by Li et al. on the SSI of patients with cancer in a specialized cancer hospital in Gansu Province (1.07%). However, the incidence identified in our study was higher than that observed in a cancer hospital in Shanghai (0.43%) and in a cancer hospital in Heilongjiang Province (0.21%) [[20], [21], [22]].

We found that Gram-negative bacteria accounted for 67.1% of pathogens. Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae were the most common Gram-negative bacteria, which is consistent with the results of other reports from China [23]. Gram-positive bacteria accounted for 31.8% of all pathogens, with Staphylococcus aureus being the predominant pathogen. The proportion of fungal infections was only 1.1%, which may be related to better perioperative measures for fungal infections.

Antimicrobial susceptibility and resistance patterns of SSI in patients with cancer

Consistent with previous studies, our findings revealed the exceptionally high resistance rates of Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae to ampicillin and cephalosporins [[24], [25], [26]]. In contrast, carbapenems (imipenem, meropenem) and amikacin showed high efficacy against Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae in the present study, which makes them reliable agents for initial, broad-spectrum coverage [[27], [28], [29]]. It is reassuring that Pseudomonas aeruginosa generally remains susceptible to standard antipseudomonal agents such as aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and meropenem. However, the observed resistance to imipenem underscores the need for continuous susceptibility testing and consideration of alternative treatments when carbapenem non-susceptibility occurs. Collectively, these resistance patterns have invalidated the use of ampicillin, cephalosporins (e.g. cefazolin, ceftriaxone), and fluoroquinolones as reliable empirical options for Gram-negative coverage in our patients with cancer.

The results also indicated that Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis exhibit resistance to methicillin, erythromycin, and clindamycin, while no resistance was observed to vancomycin, teicoplanin, or linezolid. This confirms that vancomycin, teicoplanin, and linezolid remain reliable therapeutic options for Gram-positive pathogens amidst the escalating threat of antimicrobial resistance [[30], [31], [32]]. The susceptibility of Enterococcus faecium to vancomycin, despite its intrinsic resistance to many other drug classes, simplifies treatment choices for enterococcal infections. These findings provided an actionable perspective for empirical antibiotic therapy in patients with cancer-related SSI.

BMI was an independent risk factor for SSI in patients with cancer

Multi-variable conditional logistic regression demonstrated that each unit increase in BMI was associated with a 9.0% increased risk of SSI among patients with cancer. A meta-analysis of 12 independent studies revealed that patients with obesity suffered from a 1.27-fold higher risk of SSI following knee arthroscopy than non-obese counterparts [33]. Similarly, Wilson et al. reported a positive association between the risk of SSI and BMI after knee replacement surgery [34]. Obese patients often suffer from chronic inflammation, and their adipose tissue secretes inflammatory factors (e.g. tumour necrosis factor alpha [TNF-α]) that inhibit T-cell response to epithelial injury, which interferes with the release of cytokines and growth factors and impairs wound healing [35]. Furthermore, obesity alters lymphoid tissue architecture and leucocyte function, thereby compromising the co-ordination between innate and adaptive immune responses and increasing susceptibility to infections [36]. Collectively, these findings indicated that higher BMI may increase the risk of SSI in patients with cancer, which may be attributed to obesity-related chronic inflammation and impaired immune function.

Prolonged surgery increases the risk of SSI in patients with cancer

The length of surgery is considered to be an important risk factor for SSI. A study on herniorrhaphy showed a significant correlation between prolonged surgery and elevated risk of SSI [37]. Multi-centre observational studies also revealed associations between prolonged surgery and an increased risk of SSI across diverse surgical procedures [38].

In this study, the RCS model revealed that the incidence of SSI in patients with cancer was significantly increased when the duration of surgery exceeded 161 min, suggesting that this threshold can serve as an early warning indicator for patients with cancer at a high risk of SSI. Previous studies have shown that prolonged tissue exposure to the operating room environment increases the risk of wound contamination by environmental micro-organisms, thereby facilitating pathogen colonization and invasion [39,40]. Second, prolonged surgery induces tissue ischaemia-reperfusion injury, local hypoxia, and structural tissue damage. These pathological alterations collectively compromise the integrity of the skin barrier and disrupt local micro-environmental homeostasis [41,42]. Furthermore, prolonged anaesthesia and surgical trauma can synergistically suppress the immune response, resulting in impaired defence mechanisms [43]. In patients with cancer, pre-existing factors, including impaired pre-operative nutritional status and diminished immune reserves due to prior radiotherapy/chemotherapy, may further exacerbate these adverse effects [44,45]. Therefore, patients with cancer may be more likely to develop SSI as a result of prolonged surgery.

A non-linear relationship between pre-operative WBC counts and the risk of SSI in patients with cancer

Pre-operative WBC count, as an important biological indicator of immune status and systemic health, has been extensively recognized for its clinical value [46]. A previous clinical study confirmed that a pre-operative WBC count greater than 11 × 109/L is an independent risk factor for SSI in patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery [47]. Notably, the present study identified a non-linear relationship in which patients with pre-operative WBC counts at the upper limit of normal (6.91–8.27 × 109/L) exhibited a significantly increased risk of SSI, whereas those with counts at the lower or upper limit of the normal range showed a lower risk. However, the observed non-linear associations should be cautiously interpreted. This phenomenon may reflect a statistical artefact due to sparse data in the distribution tails, which warrants validation through larger prospective studies. Nevertheless, our findings suggested that clinicians should consider serial monitoring of C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT). Enhanced infection surveillance is warranted when CRP levels are ≥ 10 mg/L and PCT concentrations are ≥ 0.25 μg/L, irrespective of peripheral WBC counts.

The protective effects of pre-operative antibiotic therapy on the day of surgery and elective surgery in patients with cancer

There is still controversy regarding the optimal timing for administering antibiotics pre-operatively. A large-scale prospective cohort study found that the incidence of SSI was lowest in the group receiving medications 30–74 min before surgery. The study also reported that administering antibiotics less than 30 min or more than 74 min before surgery significantly increased the risk of SSI [48]. In contrast, another study showed that there was no significant difference in the incidence of SSI between the 120–60 min and 60–0 min groups [49]. Our results revealed that the risk of SSI decreased by 4.2% for each additional hour increase of pre-operative antibiotic therapy on the day of surgery. This phenomenon may be attributed to the unique immunosuppressive state of patients with cancer. Patients with cancer, especially those who have undergone radiotherapy, commonly exhibit impaired cellular immune function, decreased neutrophil chemotaxis, and a low nutritional risk index, which makes such patients more susceptible to SSI [[50], [51], [52]]. Therefore, a longer duration of pre-operative antibiotic therapy on the day of surgery may reduce the risk of SSI. However, it is important to note that our findings should be interpreted with caution. In clinical practice, prolonged use of prophylactic antibiotics should be avoided to prevent the development and proliferation of drug-resistant strains.

Our results also showed that the risk of SSI in patients with cancer undergoing elective surgery is only 32.5% of that in patients undergoing emergency surgery. Compared with emergency surgery, elective surgery allows physicians to comprehensively assess and adjust the patient’s holistic health before surgery and screen for and treat potential sources of infection. These measures effectively enhance patients’ immune responses to infections [53]. Meanwhile, elective surgery offers superior disinfection of the operating room, meticulous pre-operative skin preparation and disinfection, and standardized antimicrobial prophylaxis [54,55]. These factors significantly lower the risk of SSI in elective surgery than emergency surgery.

The double-edged sword effect of antibiotic therapy in patients with cancer

As described earlier, pre-operative antibiotic therapy on the day of surgery effectively reduces the risk of SSI. However, prolonged postoperative antibiotic therapy may not further reduce the risk of SSI [[56], [57], [58]]. Multi-variable conditional logistic regression showed that for each additional day of postoperative antibiotic therapy, the risk of SSI increased by 21.4%. The RCS model also identified a significantly elevated risk of SSI when antibiotics were postoperatively used for more than 9 days. It has been reported that the duration of antibiotic therapy after the surgery is a risk factor for SSI in posterior lateral approach lumbar fusion surgery (aOR = 1.122, 95% CI: 1.023–1.232), which is consistent with the results of this study [59]. This counterintuitive finding strongly suggests inappropriate antibiotic use. Our antimicrobial susceptibility data discovered widespread resistance among predominant Gram-negative pathogens. This phenomenon suggests that prolonged postoperative regimens often involve agents with poor efficacy. This ineffective ‘pseudo-coverage’ not only fails to prevent SSI but also consistently promotes the selection of multi-drug-resistant organisms and disrupts the host microbiome, ultimately increasing the risk of infection [38]. Therefore, in clinical practice, precise strategies for antibiotic therapy should be formulated based on the individualized risk of patients, avoiding uniform prophylactic regimens irrespective of surgical complexity or patients’ comorbidities.

Heterogeneous results regarding the association of diabetes with SSI among patients with cancer

Previous studies have reported that chronic comorbidities, such as diabetes, are independent risk factors for SSI, by causing hyperglycaemia-induced immune dysfunction [60,61]. Studies have indicated that hyperglycaemia can increase the risk of bacterial colonization and infection at the surgical site by inhibiting the chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and bactericidal activity of WBCs while also weakening the ability of neutrophils to remove pathogens [62,63]. However, our study found no significant association between diabetes and the risk of SSI, which may be closely associated with perioperative blood glucose management. An expert consensus on SSI following instrumented spinal surgery indicated that perioperative glycaemic control is a key strategy for preventing SSI [64]. Furthermore, another study suggested that strict perioperative glycaemic control (glycated haemoglobin [HbA1c] ≤ 7.7% or blood glucose < 7.8 mmol/L at any time) significantly reduces the incidence of SSI in patients undergoing bone and joint replacement [65]. Therefore, for patients suffering from cancer and diabetes, improving blood glucose management programmes based on evidence-based guidelines may effectively reduce the risk of SSI.

Recommendations for clinical practice

Based on the aforementioned findings, this study recommends optimizing SSI prevention strategies via comprehensive perioperative management. Enhanced pre-operative nutritional support, stringent intra-operative aseptic techniques, and shorter length of surgery are recommended for patients with cancer, BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, and estimated duration of surgery > 161 min. Antibiotics should be used thoroughly as pre-operative prophylaxis, with limited postoperative coverage, to avoid prolonged use without indication. Emergency surgical cases need multi-disciplinary rapid-response protocols to stabilize vital signs pre-operatively while achieving infection prevention. Clinicians should pay attention to pre-operative upper normal WBC counts in patients with cancer. Combined dynamic monitoring of CRP ≥ 10 mg/L and PCT ≥ 0.25 μg/L is also recommended. A progressive increase in the levels of these inflammatory biomarkers should trigger infection alerts regardless of normal WBC counts.

For antimicrobial stewardship, our resistance data compellingly recommend against the use of standard first-line antibiotics, like ceftriaxone or levofloxacin, for empirical therapy in postoperative cancer patients. Instead, they support an upfront, more targeted approach using carbapenems for high-risk Gram-negative coverage and glycopeptides for Gram-positive coverage, guided by local antibiograms and risk factors of individual patients. Specifically, we recommend (1) using carbapenems or piperacillin-tazobactam as first-line empirical therapy for high-risk Gram-negative infections, (2) restricting the empirical use of vancomycin to cases with confirmed methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus risk factors or signs of Gram-positive infection, and (3) mandating automatic stop orders for surgical prophylaxis at 24 h after surgery, except for specific indications. This strategy is crucial for improving patient outcomes and upholding the principles of antimicrobial stewardship.

Limitations

There are some limitations to this study. First, the retrospective study design relies on the inherent data structure of electronic medical records. There may be unrecorded confounding factors affecting the objectivity of our conclusions. Second, the generalizability of the results of single-centre studies is limited by regional differences and population heterogeneity, necessitating validation through multi-centre prospective cohorts. Third, the absence of resistance genotype correlation analysis restricts the development of precision antimicrobial optimization protocols. Future studies should integrate metagenomic sequencing to characterize resistance gene profiles and establish regional multi-disciplinary consortia to enhance the clinical representativeness of study populations. Fourth, several cancer-specific confounders, including cancer stage, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and prior radiotherapy, were not included due to incomplete data in the electronic medical records. These factors may influence both the risk of SSI and the likelihood of receiving perioperative interventions. Future studies should incorporate these variables to better clarify their independent contributions to the risk of SSI in patients with cancer. Fifth, no power analysis was performed because this was a retrospective exploratory study. However, the possibility that associations with smaller effect sizes were not detected due to limited statistical power cannot be excluded. Future confirmatory studies should include a priori sample size calculations based on the observed effect sizes.

In summary, this study found that the incidence of SSI was 1.23% among patients with cancer, with Gram-negative bacteria being the predominant pathogens. In addition, our resistance data indicated that common antibiotics, like ampicillin and cephalosporins, show high resistance against Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae. In contrast, carbapenems and amikacin remained highly effective, as did vancomycin and linezolid against Gram-positive pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis.

We also identified BMI, surgical duration, and postoperative antibiotic use as risk factors for SSI. Pre-operative antibiotic therapy on the day of surgery and elective surgery was a protective factor for SSI. Additionally, there was a non-linear relationship between the risk of SSI and pre-operative WBC counts. This study provides an evidence-based support for optimizing SSI prevention and control among patients with cancer. However, due to the inherent bias, future validation of relevant conclusions through multi-centre prospective studies is needed.
