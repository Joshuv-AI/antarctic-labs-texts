A case of transverse colon volvulus in a young woman with intellectual disability: a case report
Authors: Fakhroddin Keiany, Abbas Alibakhshi, Nafiseh Shabani Mofrad, Minoo Moshajjari, Abdul Latif Abid, Omid Rezaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:A better understanding of the clinical presentation and management of transverse colon volvulus is needed to allow earlier diagnosis and improved outcomes. Here, we report on a woman with transverse colon volvulus successfully treated at our center.Case presentation:A 37-year-old woman with intellectual disability presented with 1 day of obstipation, progressive abdominal distension, nausea, vomiting, and oral intake intolerance. Physical examination revealed a soft but distended abdomen without peritoneal signs. An abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) scan showed marked dilation of the ascending and transverse colon with collapsed distal loops and a whirlpool sign at the splenic flexure, suggestive of distal transverse colon volvulus. Based on clinical and imaging findings, the patient underwent surgery, which confirmed the diagnosis.Clinical discussion:This case report describes a young woman with intellectual disability who presented with large bowel obstruction symptoms, a profile consistent with a group considered at higher risk for transverse colon volvulus. Whirlpool sign, a transition zone, and dilated bowel loops in the abdominopelvic CT scan, which was emergently performed for the patient, supported the clinical diagnosis, which was crucial in the appropriate management of the patient.Conclusions:While transverse colon volvulus is a rare condition, certain groups, such as individuals with intellectual disabilities, are at higher risk. Timely diagnosis and surgical treatment are key to a favorable outcome, and an abdominopelvic CT scan could be helpful in confirming the diagnosis.

Introduction

Volvulus is defined as the twisting of the intestine around itself and its mesentery or another fixed point and accounts for up to 5% of cases of either partial or complete gastrointestinal obstruction[1–3]. Volvulus can occur in different regions of the gastrointestinal tract, but it most commonly affects the colon, especially the sigmoid colon, which accounts for more than 70% of cases with colonic volvulus[4,5]. The transverse colon is a rare site for volvulus, accounting for less than 0.5% of colonic obstruction cases, probably due to its anatomical features[2]. However, a 2021 systematic review identified only 317 reported cases of transverse colon volvulus in major scientific databases, and, given its approximately 20% mortality rate, which is relatively higher than that of other types of volvulus, there remains a need for better characterization of its presentation and management to facilitate earlier diagnosis and intervention[6]. This is particularly important given that transverse colon volvulus may have unusual presentations, making diagnosis challenging and potentially negatively affecting patient outcomes[7,8]. Additionally, given the limited number of reported cases of transverse colon volvulus and the fact that its risk and predisposing factors have not been well defined, further studies are warranted to identify individuals at higher risk[6,9]. Therefore, given these gaps in the literature, in this study, we described a woman with a transverse colon volvulus that was successfully managed in our center, a tertiary hospital in Tehran, the capital of Iran.

Case presentation

The patient was a 37-year-old woman with a known history of intellectual disability who presented to the emergency department with a 1-day history of obstipation, abdominal pain, and progressive abdominal distension. The patient was also experiencing nausea, vomiting, and oral feeding intolerance. She had no prior history of gastrointestinal diseases or previous surgery. Her past medical history was otherwise unremarkable apart from intellectual disability. She was receiving nortriptyline, risperidone, tranqopine, and a ferrous sulfate–folic acid supplement. She had no history of smoking, alcohol consumption, or substance use, and her family history was unremarkable.

On admission, the patient was afebrile, and her vital signs were stable. Physical examination revealed a soft but markedly distended abdomen without signs of peritonitis. Initial laboratory evaluation (Supplemental Digital Content Table S1, available at: http://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A82) was notable for leukocyturia on urinalysis, which was suggestive of a urinary tract infection (UTI). Initially, the patient underwent intravenous fluid resuscitation with normal saline and was started on pantoprazole, ciprofloxacin for the management of the UTI, and metronidazole.

Due to suspicions of gastrointestinal obstruction and to rule out the possible causes, such as volvulus, cancers, pseudo-obstruction, strictures and adhesions, and ileus, after a supine chest and abdominal X-ray, the patient underwent abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) scan (Figs 1 and 2), which demonstrated marked dilatation of the ascending and transverse colon, with a maximum luminal diameter of 75 mm. A transition point (T2) was identified at the level of the splenic flexure. Given the collapsed appearance of the distal colonic loops, the findings were suggestive of a high-grade partial large bowel obstruction. At the transition point, a whirlpool sign was noted, which was suggestive of volvulus of the distal transverse colon near the ligament of Treitz as the underlying cause of obstruction. Dilatation of ileal loops was also observed, suggesting incompetence of the ileocecal valve. There was no evidence of pneumatosis intestinalis or ischemic changes in the colonic wall, and mural enhancement appeared preserved. Overall, the imaging findings confirmed the diagnosis of transverse colon volvulus. Given the imaging findings and clinical presentation, the patient underwent surgical intervention with an initial diagnosis of transverse colon volvulus, which was later confirmed during the surgery (Fig. 3). Following laparotomy, a markedly dilated colon was identified. The volvulus was localized to the mid-transverse colon, and detorsion was performed. Despite the absence of frank necrosis or gangrene, the involved bowel segments appeared moderately congested, and the mesentery was noted to have an elongated vascular pedicle, raising concern for compromised perfusion and risk of recurrence. Therefore, a partial colectomy was performed, including resection of the ascending colon, transverse colon, and proximal descending colon with end ileostomy. Additionally, around 5 cm of the distal ileum was resected. For the procedure, the middle colic vessels were ligated at their origin, and the ileocolic vessels were divided as required. The left branch of the middle colic artery was also ligated at its base. Following resection, the abdominal cavity was thoroughly irrigated, and an end ileostomy was matured. Although the bowel appeared macroscopically viable intraoperatively, definitive resection was performed due to the high risk of recurrence associated with simple detorsion alone in transverse colon volvulus. In addition, marked colonic dilatation and redundancy raised concern for recurrent volvulus and potential future ischemic events. Therefore, resection was considered the safest and most definitive therapeutic option. Histopathological examination of the resected specimen revealed mild colonic wall atrophy and mild ischemic changes. No other significant histopathological abnormalities were identified. A total of 25 reactive lymph nodes were examined, with no evidence of dysplasia or malignancy in the resected parts of the colon. The patient was discharged in good condition, without any surgical complications (Supplemental Digital Content Table S2, available at: http://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A82).

The patient was scheduled for a follow-up visit at 1 month. However, she did not attend that visit. Upon contacting her family members, it was reported that she had developed respiratory symptoms, including cough and dyspnea, during the second week after discharge. She subsequently sought medical care at a local hospital, where she lost her life due to pneumonia. As the patient was managed at another hospital, further details regarding her clinical course and management were not available.

Discussion

In this case report, we presented a patient with transverse colon volvulus, detailing the clinical presentation, radiologic findings, and management. A notable feature in this patient that raised suspicion for transverse colon volvulus is the history of intellectual disability. Even though certain congenital anomalies of the transverse mesocolon and right colon may predispose individuals to transverse colon volvulus, it is predominantly an acquired condition, often associated with factors such as chronic constipation. Furthermore, conditions such as scleroderma, Hirschsprung’s disease, and Chilaiditi’s syndrome, as well as a history of abdominal surgeries causing adhesive bands, can further increase the risk of transverse colon volvulus[2,6,11]. Distal colonic obstruction due to neoplasms or strictures may similarly increase the risk of transverse colon volvulus[2]. Additionally, genetic factors may play a role in the development of this condition, as cases of transverse colon volvulus have been reported in monozygotic twins[6,12]. More than half of individuals with intellectual disability are reported to experience chronic constipation, which is a major risk factor for transverse colon volvulus, as it can increase the distance between the splenic and hepatic flexures, as well as transverse colon mobility, thereby predisposing the colon to axial twisting and subsequent transverse colon volvulus[2,13]. According to previous studies, up to 40% of patients with transverse colon volvulus have a history of mental illness[2], which can serve as an important clinical clue, as it should raise suspicion for transverse colon volvulus in individuals with intellectual disability who present with acute symptoms of gastrointestinal obstruction.

While various modalities can be used to diagnose volvulus and other causes of gastrointestinal obstruction, the CT scan is the gold standard for the diagnosis of transverse colon volvulus[14]. In particular, the whirlpool sign, a transition zone, and dilated bowel loops can support the clinical diagnosis[14], as was the case in our patient. Finally, surgical resection is the treatment of choice in patients with transverse colon volvulus[6], which was emergently performed for the patient described in this case report and might have been crucial in the successful management and favorable outcome. There are several surgical and non-surgical options, such as detorsion and colopexy, available for the management of patients with transverse colon volvulus. However, surgical treatment, particularly segmental resection, which is performed in approximately one-fourth of cases, is the most commonly used intervention[6]. We also performed a partial colectomy given the patient’s urgent condition, the limited efficacy of colonoscopic decompression in transverse colon volvulus, and the limited role of conservative management in these patients[6].

This case report has several limitations that are worth mentioning. First, given that the patient did not admit to our center for the pneumonia following discharge, we have limited information about the course of the disease and the management of that condition, which led to her death, which is a major limitation of this case report. Second, because the patient presented to the emergency department in an acute and emergent condition, a comprehensive history of possible chronic symptoms was not obtained, which represents another limitation of this study. Additionally, her underlying intellectual disability hindered the ability to obtain a comprehensive history of chronic symptoms. Due to the same reason, we were unable to obtain a detailed history of the characteristics of pain in the patient, which could provide further insight into the clinical presentations of the condition.

Conclusions

While transverse colon volvulus is a rare condition, certain groups, such as individuals with intellectual disabilities, are at higher risk, and it should be considered in the differential diagnosis for patients with suggestive symptoms and compatible demographic characteristics, particularly in countries located within the “volvulus belt.” Timely diagnosis and surgical treatment are key to a favorable outcome, and an abdominopelvic CT scan could be helpful in confirming the diagnosis.
