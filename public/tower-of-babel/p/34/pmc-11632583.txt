Polybenzimidazole‐Reinforced Terphenylene Anion Exchange Water Electrolysis Membranes
Authors: Rossana Gentile, Sabrina C Zignani, Marta Zatoń, Marc Dupont, Frédéric Lecœur, Nicolas Donzel, Alina Amel, Ervin Tal‐Gutelmacher, Angela Salanitro, Antonino S Aricó, Sara Cavaliere, Deborah J Jones, Jacques Rozière
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnion exchange membrane water electrolysis (AEMWE) for hydrogen production combines the advantages of proton exchange membrane water electrolysis and alkaline water electrolysis. Several strategies have been adopted to improve the performance of AEMWE and to obtain membranes with high hydroxide ion conductivity, low gas permeation, and high durability. In this work AEMs reinforced with poly[2,2’‐(p‐oxydiphenylene)‐5,5’‐benzimidazole] (PBIO) polymer fibres have been developed. A fibre web of PBIO prepared by electrospinning was impregnated into the poly(terphenylene) mTPN ionomer. The membranes are strengthened by the formation of a strong surface interaction between the reinforcement and the ionomer and by the expansion of the reinforcement over the membrane thickness. The hydroxide ion conductivity, thermal stability, dimensional swelling, mechanical properties, and hydrogen crossover of the reinforced membranes were compared with the characteristics of the non‐reinforced counterpart. The incorporation of PBIO nanofibre reinforcement into the membrane reduced hydrogen crossover and improved tensile properties, without affecting hydroxide conductivity. PBIO‐reinforced mTPN membrane was assessed in a PGM‐free 5 cm2 AEMWE single cell using NiFe oxide anode and NiMo cathode catalysts, at a cell temperature of 50 °C and with 1 M KOH fed to the anode. The performance of the cell increased continuously over the 260 hours test period, reaching 2.06 V at 1.0 A cm−2.Keywords: Anion exchange membrane water electrolysis, terphenylene, polybenzimidazole, nanofibre reinforcement, hydrogen production

Introduction

The electrochemical splitting of water is one of the easiest technologies for a large‐scale clean hydrogen production.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] The cost of hydrogen production can be significantly reduced by developing anion exchange membrane water electrolysis (AEMWE), which combines the advantages of proton exchange membrane water electrolysis and alkaline water electrolysis systems. By using non‐platinum group metal (PGM) catalysts, capital costs may be reduced compared to proton exchange membrane water electrolysis, [4] which requires noble metal catalysts (Ir, Ru, Pt).[ 5 , 6 , 7 ] Furthermore, using either pure water or a dilute alkaline solution, e. g. 1 M KOH or lower decreases the likelihood of leaks associated with the corrosive concentrated alkaline solution (5–7 M) used in the alkaline electrolyser. [8] Moreover, the use of a polymer membrane as electrolyte facilitates ease of handling and reduces the size and weight of the electrolyser compared to alkaline water electrolysis, as well enhances the capability to increase the differential pressure. [7] One of the main challenges of AEMWE is the development of highly stable and conductive anion exchange membranes (AEMs). The AEMs′ alkaline stability is determined by the polymer backbone structure and the position of the positively charged groups within it, as they are susceptible to nucleophilic attacks by the OH− group via SN2 substitution of the cationic side chains, Hoffman elimination, and ylide intermediate formation.[ 9 , 10 , 11 ] In this regard, several strategies have been adopted to prepare stable AEMs with high hydroxide conductivity, low gas permeability, and high durability.[ 12 , 13 ] AEMs with a range of polymer backbone structures (Fumatech FAA‐3, Tokuyama A‐201, Ionomr Innovations AEMION, Dioxide Materials Sustainion, Orion mTPN) have been the object of comparative studies for their AEMWE durability and performance.[ 14 , 15 , 16 ] Reduction of membrane thickness has gained attention since the pioneering work of WLGore for proton exchange membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) membranes.[ 17 , 18 ] Use of thinner membranes decreases mass transport resistance and ohmic resistance, so reducing the cell overpotential. [19] Further, using a thinner membrane for both proton exchange and anion exchange membrane fuel cells and electrolysers enhances water back‐diffusion, resulting in a more uniform through‐plane water distribution under operating conditions.[ 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 ] However, the use of thin membranes detrimentally impacts both the mechanical properties and permeation,[ 19 , 21 , 22 ] which is particularly crucial in the context of membrane electrolysis, due to the possible high differential pressure and associated safety considerations. These can be remediated by the use of porous reinforcements,[ 23 , 24 ] initially of expanded poly(tetrafluoroethylene) (ePTFE) and later of alternative woven or non‐woven fibre supports.[ 21 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 ]

Pintauro et al. pioneered the use of electrospun polymer nanofibres for reinforcement of ion exchange membranes for fuel cells,[ 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 ] which has been further developed by ourselves[ 13 , 29 , 30 , 37 , 38 , 39 ] and others.[ 24 , 40 , 41 , 42 ] Our work has introduced the electrospinning of polymers that can actively interact with the ionomer by ionic or covalent crosslinking to produce gas‐tight, structurally and morphologically stable proton exchange membranes for fuel cells,[ 13 , 39 ] and the approach was recently extended to anion exchange membranes for water electrolysis by Najibah et al., [43] who reported the performance of a stable AEM material based on the poly(terphenylene) ionomer mTPN, reinforced with poly[2,2’‐(m‐phenylene)‐5,5’‐bisbenzimidazole] (mPBI), previously functionalised for covalent crosslinking. [43] In the present study, the poly(terphenylene) anion exchange ionomer (mTPN) (Figure 1a) developed by Bae et al. [44] was used as the ionomer component for membranes reinforced with nanofibre webs of another member of the PBI family, poly[2,2’‐(p‐oxydiphenylene)‐5,5’‐benzimidazole] (PBIO) (Figure 1b). The presence of partial polymer blending at the interface between the fibres and ionomer assures continuity between the reinforcement and the matrix polymers and contributes to the gas‐tightness of the membrane. The physico‐chemical characterisation of the PBIO‐mTPN reinforced membranes including the hydroxide ion conductivity, thermal stability, dimensional swelling in water and KOH solutions, mechanical properties, and hydrogen crossover properties, was carried out and the results compared with the characteristics of the non‐reinforced counterparts. An assembly comprising non‐PGM catalysts and a PBIO‐mTPN membrane was characterised in an AEMWE single cell, showing performance of 1 A cm−2 at a cell voltage of 2 V, and good stability over 260 hours of operation.

Experimental Section

Materials and Membrane Preparation

Materials

Anion exchange polyarylene ionomer (mTPN) in bromide form and poly[2,2’‐(p‐oxydiphenylene)‐5,5’‐benzimidazole] (PBIO) were prepared according to published work,[ 44 , 45 ] poly(ethylene)oxide (Sigma‐Aldrich MW ~106 (nominal)), dimethylacetamide (DMAc, CH3CON(CH3)2, 99.8 %), 2‐propanol ((CH3)2CHOH≥99.5 %, Sigma‐Aldrich), 1‐methyl‐2‐pyrrolidinone (NMP, anhydrous, 99.5 %), aluminium oxide (Al2O3 20 % in water, colloidal dispersion, VWR), sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3, ACS reagent,≥99.7 %, Sigma‐Aldrich), potassium hydroxide (KOH, ACS reagent,≥85 %, pellets, Sigma‐Aldrich), silver nitrate (AgNO3 ReagentPlus®, ≥99.0 % (titration) Sigma‐Aldrich), potassium chromate (K₂CrO₄, ACS reagent,≥99.0 % Sigma‐Aldrich) were used as received.

PBIO Nanofibre Preparation

A solution of PBIO (6 wt %) and poly(ethylene) oxide 106 MW (0.18 wt %) was prepared in DMAc under stirring at 80 °C for 14 hours. PBIO nanofibre webs were prepared by electrospinning (Linari Engineering) this solution by applying 20 kV, using a needle‐collector distance of 10 cm, a flow‐rate of 0.4 mL h−1, a rotation speed of 300 rpm, and controlling the temperature and relative humidity at 30 °C and <30 %, respectively. The fibres were collected on a rotating drum collector, until a PBIO web thickness of 17±3 μm was achieved. The web was thermally cured in air at 360 °C for 2 h to enhance its chemical stability and insolubility in an alkaline environment as indicated by 1H NMR (not shown) and to decrease its solubility in organic solvents.

Membrane Preparation

Membranes were prepared starting from the mTPN ionomer in bromide form (mTPN‐Br). The ionomer solution was prepared by dissolving mTPN‐Br (18 wt %) in 2‐propanol: NMP (70 : 30 wt.%). A single layer of the mTPN‐Br solution was cast, using a doctor blade with a gate thickness of 500 μm at a slide‐speed of 12.5 mm/s, on a thick glass plate that had been pre‐treated using Al2O3. The reinforced membrane was prepared using a three‐step casting procedure[ 13 , 46 , 47 ] (Figure S1). A first layer of ionomer was cast using a blade with thickness of 200 μm, followed by the integration of the PBIO fibre web into the ionomer. Then a third layer of ionomer was cast using a doctor blade with a thickness of 500 μm. Before removal of the non‐reinforced and reinforced membranes from the glass plate, thermal treatment was carried out at 150 °C under vacuum for 15 minutes to eliminate any solvent present.

Membrane Characterisation

Characterisation by Electron Microscopy

The morphology of the electrospun PBIO nanofibre web and the nanofibre reinforced composite membranes were analysed by field emission‐scanning electron microscopy (FE‐SEM) using a Hitachi S‐4800 N microscope, after coating with an Au layer of few nanometres (≈2–3 nm). To examine membrane cross‐sections, the samples were prepared by freeze‐fracture with liquid nitrogen and specimens were supported onto metallic pin tips with carbon tape. The diameter of PBIO electrospun nanofibres was evaluated using the ImageJ software on more than 100 objects in different micrographs.

TEM analysis of reinforced membrane cross‐sections was carried out using a JEOL 1200 EXII microscope operating at 120 kV and equipped with a CCD camera SIS Olympus Quemesa (11 million pixels). The composition through the fibre diameter was evaluated using a JEOL JEM 2100F – EDS microscope operating at 200 kV equipped with a CMOS Gatan RIO16IS 4K*4K camera.

Membrane Conductivity

The in‐plane hydroxide ion conductivity was calculated from resistance measurements on non‐reinforced and reinforced membranes using a method based on CO2 purging. [48] The membranes in Br− form were first exchanged to bicarbonate form (mTPN‐HCO3) by immersion in a solution of NaHCO3 1 M for 48 h at 40 °C and subsequent washing with MilliQ water 3 times for 45 min at 25 °C. The electrical resistance of the membranes was determined using 4‐probe in‐plane BekkTech BT‐512 conductivity test system, which includes a Keithley 2400 electric generator for electrical measurements. The experimental conditions were: 100 % RH, 50 °C, and gas flow of nitrogen (N2 99.9 %) at 500 sccm/min. For carbon dioxide purging a direct current of 100 μA was applied and then interrupted to allow measurement of the resistance every 50 min. The evolution of the membrane conductivity was plotted as a function of time until a plateau was reached (ca. 48 h).

Membrane Ion Exchange Capacity

The ion exchange capacity (IEC) of the non‐reinforced and reinforced membranes in Br− form was evaluated by Mohr titration. [49] 5×5 cm of membranes were dried at 80 °C and subsequently weighed and then soaked at 20 °C in 100 mL of 0.5 M sodium nitrate (NaNO3) for 48 hours, in order to exchange the Br− ions of the membrane with NO3 − ions. The sodium nitrate/bromide solution was then titrated with 0.02 M silver nitrate standard solution, with potassium chromate (K2CrO4) as indicator. After all the bromide ions exchanged out from the membrane were converted to silver bromide in the solution, the excess silver ions reacted with the potassium chromate indicator to produce a red‐brown precipitate of silver chromate (Ag2CrO4) which indicates the equivalent point. Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

Thermal analysis of the membranes in Br− form was carried out on a NETZSCH STA 449F1 Instrument in the temperature range between 25 and 700 °C under nitrogen atmosphere at a heating rate of 5 °C min−1. TGA was also performed on PBIO fibre webs before and after thermal curing at 360 °C. [45]

X‐Ray Diffraction (XRD)

The X‐ray diffraction pattern of PBIO fibre webs was recorded before and after the thermal curing using an X′PERT F diffractometer with a Cu Kα source, λ=1.5818 Å in an angular scan in the range 5–60° with a step size of 0.33°.

Stress‐Strain Tests

The tensile mechanical properties were assessed by a tensile tester (Zwick Roell), using a maximum charge of 150 N and the security maximum charge of 120 N. Stress‐strain measurements were performed on 5 cm×1 cm samples of each membrane at 50 °C and 80 % RH. The tensile speed was 1 mm/min and 5 mm/min in the elastic and in the plastic regions, respectively. At least five replicate film strips were analysed.

Upon full exchange in KOH 1 M at 50 °C for 24 hours, stress‐strain tests were conducted on the wet reinforced and non‐reinforced membranes at 50 °C and 80 % RH. It is well‐known that anion exchange membranes undergo carbonation in a matter of minutes in ambient air (>50 % carbonation in 5 minutes), and therefore all precautions were taken to minimise carbonation of hydroxide form mTPN membranes by rapid transfer and by performing mechanical measurements under controlled atmosphere. [51]

Hydrogen Permeation

A gas chromatograph (GC, Agilent Technologies 490 Micro GC) was used to measure the hydrogen permeated through mTPN reinforced and non‐reinforced membranes in hydroxide form. The cell utilized comprises two circular graphite monopolar plates and two circular stainless steel current collectors, each with an active area of 8 cm2, fitted with an automatic clamping system. The hydrogen and nitrogen gases were humidified by passing through a water reservoir, and the humidity was regulated by controlling the dew point. The nitrogen side was connected to a gas chromatograph to measure the concentration of hydrogen that crosses the membrane. The membranes in Br‐form were exchanged with KOH 0.5 M at 50 °C for 24 hours. The permeation experiment was conducted at atmospheric pressure at 50 °C, 100 % RH and a cell compression of 1 bar abs. A flow of hydrogen gas of 100 sccm was fed in one side of the cell, while a flow of nitrogen of 100 sccm was fed in the other side. The hydrogen that crossed the membrane was then measured using GC as concentration of hydrogen in nitrogen.

Anion Exchange Membrane Water Electrolysis

Membrane‐electrode assemblies (MEAs) were prepared using PBIO‐reinforced mTPN membranes of 50 μm thickness in hydroxide form with NiMo supported on Ketjen Black 300 J (KB) electro‐catalyst at the cathode and electro‐catalyst NiFeOx at the anode. [52] Catalyst inks for the cathode and anode were prepared using NiMo/KB, and unsupported NiFeOx catalyst that were mixed with mTPN anion exchange ionomer and dispersed in ethanol, in proportions 33 wt % ionomer/67 wt % ethanol. The dispersions were sonicated for 30 minutes before use. The cathode and anode catalysts were spray‐cast on to a Sigracet 39BC substrate and on to Ni felt respectively (Bekaert SA).

The total catalyst loading in the final electrodes was 2.5 mg cm−2 for the NiFeOx anode, and 5 mg cm−2 for the NiMo/KB cathode, and the geometrical active area of the electrodes was 5 cm2. The electrodes were assembled with the membrane without hot‐pressing to avoid any membrane degradation, the membrane‐electrode assembly was inserted into the nickel cell housings, and the cell was compressed at 5 N m for each tie rod. A dry cathode configuration was used, while 1 M KOH solution was circulated at the anode compartment via a peristaltic pump, maintaining a flow rate of 5 mL/min. The cell temperature was held at 50 °C. Polarisation experiments were carried out with an ITECH 6522C (80 V/120A) power supply system in combination with a FLUKE 8846 A (240 V–6.5 Digit Precision) multimeter. Galvanostatic pseudo‐steady‐state polarisation curves were recorded at a scan rate of 5 mV/s after a dwell time of 2 min at each experimental point in the polarisation curve. The stability of the MEA was evaluated through galvanostatic measurements at different current densities between 0.2 and 1.0 A/cm2. AC electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis was employed as diagnostic tool, using a Biologic potentiostat/galvanostat equipped with a 100 A booster and a frequency–response analyser module for impedance spectroscopy. The impedance measurements were carried out in a frequency range between 100 MHz–10 mHz at 1.5, 1.8 and 2.0 V, with an oscillation voltage of 10 mV rms and the spectra were fitted using an equivalent circuit comprising a resistance, in series with two simple Randles circuits with a constant phase element replacing the double layer capacity, and including the anode and cathode polarisation resistances.

Results and Discussion

Electrospun Polymer Nanofibres

Composite membranes were prepared using as a reinforcement a network of polymer fibres obtained by electrospinning. Regular, non‐welded and homogeneous PBIO nanofibres with diameter of 200 nm and narrow distribution were obtained with a web thickness (gauge) of 17±3 μm (Figure 2a, e). This thickness corresponds to 3 grams of PBIO per square meter (gsm), from which a porosity of the nanofibre web of 87 % is calculated using the density (1.32 g cm‐3) of PBIO. [53]

SEM micrographs of PBIO (a) before and (b) after thermal treatment at 360 °C, air, 2 h, (c) after immersion in 0.5 M KOH, 50 °C, (d) after immersion in 1.0 M KOH, 50 °C, and their respective fibre diameter distribution (e, f, g, h).

In concentrated aqueous potassium hydroxide at high temperature, polybenzimidazole has been shown to undergo hydrolytic degradation of the polymer backbone. PBIO is reported as being more stable than mPBI, however in 1 M KOH deterioration of the mechanical properties of a methylated PBIO membrane have been reported after 1 day at 60 °C. [54] Nevertheless, a membrane of PBIO functionalised with a tri‐cationic side chain has been described as relatively stable with a reduction of conductivity of 22 % after >672 hours in 2 M KOH at 60 °C. [55]

Due to the high solubility of PBIO in organic solvents, the fibre web was stabilised by thermal curing. Thermal curing has been reported to lead to non‐specific crosslinking reactions [56] that increase insolubility, and to improve the resistance to swelling and the mechanical characteristics. [57] The properties of the fibre webs before and after the thermal curing, in terms of thermogravimetric analysis, XRD, and stress‐strain curves, are provided in the supplementary information (Figures S1, S2, and S3). Such thermal treatment resulted in 16 % weight loss, but no change in fibre or overall web dimensions was observed (Figure 2b, f). The weight loss can be attributed to the complete removal of water and alterations caused by recrystallisation or phase transformation, influencing the fibre web density. [57] The stability of the fibres in alkaline environment was studied to confirm that the reinforcement using the treated fibres is suitable for working in AEMWE, where a KOH solution is often required. The fibres were immersed in a 0.5 and 1 M KOH solution for 1 day at 50 °C. A weight gain of 10 and 16.2 % at 0.5, and 1 M respectively was determined after the alkaline treatment due to the absorption of water molecules, hydroxide and potassium ions. Aili et al. showed that in 5 wt % KOH solution, mPBI can be partially deprotonated and therefore absorb additional KOH and water (0.2 mole KOH per PBI repeat unit, 4 water molecules). [58]

Following immersion of thermally cured PBIO in 0.5 and 1 M KOH at 50 °C for 1 day, the webs were removed and examined using SEM. After the KOH treatment, the fibres appear neither damaged nor dissolved (Figure 2c, d), and analysis of the fibre diameters indicates a progression to higher diameters from ca. 200 to 250 and 300 nm, in 0.5 and 1 M KOH, respectively (Figure 2g, h). The porosity of the PBIO fibre web was reduced from ca. 80 % to ca. 70 % due to the swelling of the fibres in KOH.

Preparation of Reinforced and Non‐Reinforced mTPN‐Br Membranes

The aim of the present work was the preparation of reinforced membranes with a 3D spread of the PBIO fibre web within the ionomer to improve the mechanical properties and durability of the membrane. mTPN‐Br membranes were prepared using one‐step laminar casting. The mix of solvents, their ratio and the concentration of the ionomer in the casting solution were chosen with the objective of obtaining a membrane with a thickness close to 50 μm. The final thickness of the non‐reinforced and reinforced membranes was 45±5 μm and 47±5 μm respectively. Specific interaction between the nanofibre web and the ionomer, coupled with their respective positions within the membrane, play a crucial role in determining membrane properties.[ 37 , 46 ]

Najibah et al. [43] reported AEM reinforced with a layer of functionalized and covalently bonded PBI fibres at the centre of the membrane.

The percentage of the fibre web in the membrane was set at ca. 6 wt %. Cross‐section images of reinforced membrane mTPN‐Br/PBIO are depicted in Figure 3. It may be observed that the PBIO polymer fibre web is well‐distributed all over the membrane thickness with an expansion factor for the thickness of about 2.5, providing a 3D reinforcement which, as in our previous work on PBIO reinforced PFSA membranes, is expected to enhance durability, strength, thermal and chemical stability of the composite material.[ 37 , 46 ] In Figure 3b,c the cross‐section of the reinforced membrane after iodide exchange is depicted at two magnifications. A distinct interface region is observed around the nanofibres, which suggests polymer blending resulting from the interaction between the anionic ionomer and the reinforcement. The peripheral contrasted dark regions infer penetration of iodide exchanged mTPN into the fibre, thus assuring continuity between the PBIO polymer fibre web and the ionomer of the membrane, and enhancing the dimensional stability and gas tightness of the composite membrane, strongly impacting its durability. [13]

(a) SEM image of the cross‐section of the PBIO nanofibre reinforced mTPN membrane at 1.5 k magnification; (b,c) TEM images representing PBIO fibres and the interface region between the fibre and the iodide exchanged ionomer.

Elemental mapping was conducted across the diameter of the (Figure S5). The element nitrogen (present in both PBIO and mTPN but in greater amount in PBIO than in mTPN) increases from a relative mass of 65 % in the ionomer to a maximum of ca. 81 % at either side of the interface region between mTPN and PBIO and is ca. 77 % within the PBIO fibre. The observation of maxima in the nitrogen at either side of the fibre is consistent with the existence of interpenetrating polymers within a peripheral interface region of ca. 40 nm. The line scans for the elements iodine and fluorine are consistent with these observations.

Ion Exchange Capacity

The ion exchange capacity (IEC) of mTPN membranes reinforced with PBIO was evaluated by Mohr titration and the results were compared with those obtained with the non‐reinforced mTPN membranes to assess the impact of the reinforcement on this property. The IEC of the non‐reinforced mTPN‐Br and mTPN‐OH membranes was 1.92 mmol g−1 and 2.22 mmol g−1 respectively, close to the potentiometrically determined value (different batch of mTPN ionomer) of 2.01 mmol g−1. These values are consistent with the IEC of mTPN−Cl membranes heated at 120 °C, 1.94 mmol g−1. [43] The reduction in the ion exchange capacity of 9 %, to 1.75 mmol g−1 for the mTPN‐Br/PBIO membrane, corresponding to 2.02 mmol g−1 for the reinforced membrane in the hydroxide form PBIO reinforced membrane, is slightly higher than would be expected taking into account the weight fraction of PBIO. Further, this reduction in IEC seems to have little effect on the electrical resistance of hydroxide ion form membranes, as described below.

Dimensional Swelling and Mass Gain in Water and KOH

Membrane dimensional stability is crucial for the application of AEM in water electrolysis. The swelling behaviour of reinforced and non‐reinforced mTPN‐Br membranes in water and KOH solution was investigated at 20 °C and 50 °C (temperature of operation of the AEMWE cell). At a temperature of 20 °C, the water uptake values were 10±2 and 12±3 wt % for the reinforced and non‐reinforced membranes, respectively. Upon raising the temperature to 50 °C, still no significant variations were observed, with both the reinforced and non‐reinforced membranes exhibiting an uptake of approximately 11±2 and 12±3 wt %.

The mass gain in KOH and the dimensional change of the membrane from its dry state in bromide‐form after 24 hours of immersion in KOH are plotted Figure 4. The area swelling and mass gain of the reinforced membranes are significantly lower than those of the non‐reinforced mTPN membranes after immersion at 20 °C in 0.1 M, 0.5 M and 1 M KOH. As the temperature and the concentration of KOH were increased, the mass gain increased for both non‐reinforced and reinforced membranes. This can be interpreted by the substitution of the bromide counterion of the membrane with OH− ions and the increased hydration sphere around the polymer chains, which is enriched with OH−, K+ ions and water. After immersion in KOH 0.1 M and 0.5 M at 50 °C, the reinforced membranes present lower dimensional swelling and lower mass gainKOH than the non‐reinforced membranes. Specifically, for the non‐reinforced membranes, the mass gain remained relatively unchanged, even with higher concentrations of KOH, suggesting their complete exchange to the OH− form. At all KOH concentrations, including at 50 °C, the introduction of the nanofibre reinforcement led to a strong reduction in area swelling in the membranes. This is advantageous for both the fabrication of catalyst‐coated membranes (CCMs), and their use, as it reduces the probability of crack development in the electrode layer resulting from delamination at the interface between the catalyst layer and the membrane.[ 59 , 60 ]

Thickness (Stk), area (SA) and volume (Sv) swelling and mass gain of mTPN and nanofibre PBIO reinforced mTPN after immersion for 24 hours in KOH 0.1, 0.5 and 1 M at (a) 20 °C and (b) 50 °C.

The weight increases for the membranes in Br− form in water and 0.1, 0.5, 0.8 and 1.0 M KOH by mTPN and PBIO‐reinforced mTPN membranes at 50 °C are represented in Figure 5. In the present work, the mass gain by non‐reinforced mTPN membranes is more or less unvaried in 0.1, 0.5 and 1.0 M KOH. In the same range of KOH concentration, the weight gain by the PBIO reinforced membranes is continuous. Further, using more concentrated KOH (0.8 and 1 M), the reinforced membranes showed a higher mass gain than non‐reinforced membranes. This change in behaviour at high KOH concentration in the present work may be partially ascribed to KOH uptake by the PBIO fibres, [61] although the KOH doping is not high enough to impact the conductivity. Previous work on mPBI[ 61 , 62 ] and PBIO [61] doped with 1 M KOH determined conductivity of the order of 10−4 S cm−1 for mPBI and 10−5 S cm−1 for mPBI and PBIO respectively. This influence of KOH concentration in the range of 0.1–1.0 M is in contrast with the observation that for covalently bonded mPBI reinforced mTPN membranes, [43] little variation of the uptake is observed for both non‐reinforced and reinforced membranes between 0.1 and 1.0 M KOH. This difference in behaviour might arise from the difference in the macroscopic structure of the reinforced membranes and the fact that the mPBI is covalently bonded to the ionomer and present in a central layer, while in the present work the PBIO reinforcement is not covalently bonded and is fully expanded throughout the membrane thickness.

Membrane Conductivity

The hydroxide ion conductivity is a crucial property for the application of anion exchange membranes in AEM water electrolysis. Anion exchange membranes are extremely sensitive to air due to the fast reaction between OH− ions and ambient CO2 that converts the charge carriers in the membrane to bicarbonate/carbonate ions, following which the conductivity determined is not related to hydroxide ions, but to less mobile bicarbonate/carbonate ions. For that reason, the CO2 purging method was implemented to evaluate in‐plane hydroxide ion conduction properties of the membrane initially exchanged to bicarbonate form (mTPN‐HCO3) and then converted by an electrolysis process into the OH− form. [63]

The conductivity was calculated from resistance measurements and was plotted as a function of the time the membrane was held under CO2 purge conditions, to follow the displacement of bicarbonate by hydroxide ions and the subsequent increase in ion conductivity up to a plateau. The measurements were performed on non‐reinforced and reinforced membranes to assess any influence on conductivity of the presence of the PBIO fibre web reinforcement (Figure 6).

(a) Bicarbonate/hydroxide ion conductivity of PBIO reinforced (•) and non‐reinforced (○) mTPN membranes over 48 h. Conditions: 100 % RH, 50 °C, and gas flow of nitrogen (N2 99.9 %) at 500 sccm/min. A direct current of 100 μA was applied and then interrupted to allow measurement of the resistance every 50 min and (b) evolution of the conductivity with time in 1 M KOH at 50 °C of PBIO reinforced (•) and non‐reinforced mTPN membrane.

The resistance of reinforced and non‐reinforced membranes in bicarbonate form was measured until it reached a stable value after two hours, corresponding to a conductivity in each case of around 20 mS/cm. A current of 100 μA was then applied to the membrane to convert the bicarbonate to hydroxide ions, and in consequence the conductivity increased progressively until it reached a stable value of 80 mS cm−1 for the non‐reinforced mTPN, consistent with other reported values, [64] and 77 mS cm−1 for the PBIO nanofibre reinforced mTPN membrane. The conductivity was calculated using a thickness measured for the membrane in the carbonate form at time 0, and otherwise (for all other durations) using a thickness determined for the membrane immediately after disassembling the cell. This implies that for the points on the rise to the plateau, which corresponds to different ratios of (bi)carbonate and hydroxide ions, the conductivity is not estimated accurately. The results are consistent with those published for mPBI‐reinforced mTPN determined at 60 °C, if the 10 °C difference in temperature is considered (10 mS/cm higher conductivity for mPBI‐reinforced mTPN membrane). [64] It should be noted that, in the present work, the incorporation of uncharged reinforcement in the membrane does not result in a reduction in conductivity. Instead, the concentration of fixed charge per cm3 of absorbed water balances the dilution impact of charged groups caused by the uncharged reinforcement.[ 13 , 39 , 65 , 66 ] These results align with our work using electrospun polymer reinforcements for proton exchange membranes.

It can be concluded from the results displayed in Figure 6 that the conductivity of the mTPN membrane is undiminished by the presence of the PBIO fibre web. In both cases, the membranes demonstrated stability over the 48‐hour testing period, reaching a plateau at a high and consistent value of hydroxide conductivity, similar to that of FAA‐3 at the same temperature (75 mS cm−1). [64]

Further experiments were conducted to assess membrane stability by determining the conductivity of the PBIO‐reinforced mTPN membrane following immersion in 1 M KOH solution at 50 °C (Figure 6b) for different durations, measuring the membrane thickness at each point. The initial conductivity of the reinforced membrane was 80 mS/cm and it remained relatively unchanged over a period of 21 days in 1 M KOH. In contrast, for non‐reinforced mTPN, the initial conductivity of 82 mS/cm decreased progressively over the 21‐day period, reaching 60 mS/cm. Similar trends over time were observed for mTPN and covalently bonded mPBI‐mTPN after immersion in 1 M KOH at 60 °C. [64]

Thermal Properties

The thermal stability of the non‐reinforced mTPN and PBIO‐reinforced mTPN membranes in bromide form was investigated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (Figure 7).

Thermogravimetric analysis in nitrogen atmosphere at a heating rate of 5 °C min−1 of: thermally cured PBIO fibre web (×), mTPN‐Br membrane (○) and PBIO‐reinforced mTPN‐Br membrane (•).

The thermally cured PBIO undergoes (Figure 7 and Figure S2) minor mass reduction up to 445 °C, while at higher temperature the degradation of the polymer backbones starts. [67] For both mTPN and PBIO‐reinforced mTPN membranes, the weight loss below 100 °C is due to water evaporation from the ionomer, that between 200 and 300 °C to the loss of functional quaternary ammonium groups from mTPN, and that between 420 and 550 °C to the degradation of the ionomer main chain.[ 45 , 68 ] The thermal degradation trend of the reinforced and non‐reinforced membranes is practically identical, not showing any specific influence of the embedded PBI web on thermal weight loss properties. Under the conditions and the time frame of the TGA analysis, mTPN is stable to 240 °C, however this not preclude decomposition under the specific conditions of a fuel cell or of water electrolysis.

Mechanical Properties of mTPN and PBIO‐Reinforced mTPN Membranes

The comparison between the mechanical behaviour at 25 °C, 50 %RH and at 50 °C, 80 %RH of the reinforced and non‐reinforced mTPN‐Br membranes is provided in Figure 8.

Stress‐strain curves of PBIO‐reinforced and non‐reinforced mTPN‐Br membranes determined at different temperatures and relative humidity and after immersion in 1 M KOH, 24 hours.

The stress‐strain curve of the non‐reinforced mTPN membrane at 25 °C and 50 % RH correspond to a Young′s modulus of 900 MPa, with stress and elongation at break values of 32 MPa and 11 % respectively. After introduction of the reinforcement, the mTPN‐Br/PBIO membrane is characterised by a higher breaking stress, similar Young′s modulus, similar yield stress while the elongation at break showed small variation in comparison to the non‐reinforced counterpart.

An increase in the temperature and relative humidity to 50 °C and 80 % RH respectively is expected to reduce the Young′s modulus and breaking stress and increase the elongation at break of the polymer. Indeed, under these conditions the mTPN membrane presents lower breaking stress, lower Young′s modulus, and higher elongation at break. The Young′s Modulus was reduced from 900 MPa to 420 MPa for the non‐reinforced mTPN‐Br and from 880 MPa to 543 MPa for the PBIO‐reinforced membrane. The yield modulus decreased from 30 MPa to 19 MPa for the mTPN‐Br membrane, and from 33 to 26 MPa for the mTPN/PBIO membrane. This condition also lowered stress at break to 19 and 26 MPa for the non‐reinforced and reinforced membrane while the elongation at break increased for both membranes. These effects are probably attributable to swelling of the membrane and the plasticizing effect of water molecules.

To measure the membrane mechanical properties under conditions more closely resembling those of the AEMWE, stress‐strain measurements were carried out on membranes that had been in contact with 1 M KOH solution at 50 °C for 24 hours. The stress/strain tests were performed on wet membranes at 50 °C and 80 % RH with precautions taken to avoid carbonation (Table 1).

Hydrogen Permeation

For the implementation of efficient and safe water electrolysers, it is essential to develop solid polymer electrolytes with low gas permeation. The incorporation of reinforcement is crucial for minimising gas crossover. As the gas crossover is inversely proportional to the thickness of the membrane, membranes with thickness of about 40 μm were compared. Gas chromatography was used to measure hydrogen that crosses the membrane at atmospheric pressure at 50 °C and 100 % RH. The concentration of hydrogen in nitrogen in ppm was measured and from this value, the gas crossover as mL of H2 per minute and mL of H2 per minute by area was calculated.

The non‐reinforced membrane is characterised by a low H2 crossover rate of 2.60 10−3 mL of H2 min−1 cm−2, which decreased to 1.96 10−3 mL of H2 min−1cm−2 for the PBIO‐reinforced membrane, corresponding to a reduction in hydrogen permeation of 24 %. It may be concluded that the reinforcement with PBI web is effective in leading to enhanced mechanical and dimensional stability as well as improved tightness against hydrogen crossover, and no reduction in hydroxide ion conductivity over time.

The single cell fixture comprising the PBIO reinforced mTPN membrane was inserted into the AEMWE test station, and characterisation was performed at 50 °C with a 1 M KOH solution fed at the anode side. Following a 24‐hour activation period at 0.25 A cm−2, the cell was operated in galvanostatic mode by increasing the current density step‐wise from 0.6 A cm−2 to 1 A cm−2 over a period of 230 hours (Figure 9a). Improvement in cell performance was observed after 70 hours, since the cell voltage at a current density of 0.8 A cm−2 was observed to decrease. Although it is possible that the catalyst may partially detach and the membrane degrade during long‐term activation, in the present work, neither catalyst detachment nor degradation was observed. The enhanced cell performance may be attributed to a better liquid electrolyte recirculation or to the activation of the MEA during operation, involving both membrane and catalyst activation. Previous work using the same catalysts has shown improvement due to the reduction of surface oxides on the NiMo/KB cathode catalyst and an increased uptake of hydroxides by the Ni−Fe anode. Additionally, a better interface between the membrane and electrode can contribute to this enhancement. These suppositions were confirmed by the decrease in Rs, and Rp. [52] The current density was further increased to 1 A cm−2 for 130 hours, during which time a further decrease in cell voltage was noted, indicating progressive improvement in cell performance (increase of voltage efficiency).

Galvanostatic mode AEMWE (a) stepwise increase of the current density to 1 A cm−1, (b) polarisation curves at 40 h after the beginning of the test (red), after 140 h (green) and at the end of the test after 260 h (blue), (c–e) Nyquist plots at 1.5 V (red), 1.8 V (blue) and 2 V (orange) at 40 h (c), 140 h (d) 260 h (e), polarisation resistance ( ▴ ) and series resistance (•), during the galvanostatic test.

Polarisation curves (Figure 9b) were recorded throughout the test in galvanostatic mode. The initial polarisation curve displayed a current density of approximately 0.48 A cm−2 at 2.06 V. The improvement in the performance of the MEA was confirmed by the polarisation curves obtained after 140 hours of test, which showed a current density of 0.70 A cm−2 at 2.06 V. At the end of the 260‐hour test, the continued improvement of the MEA was confirmed by a decrease in activation energy and ohmic drop, reaching 1 A cm−2 at 2.06 V.

Electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) were recorded as a diagnostic tool for assessing the MEA performance. The Nyquist plots are presented in Figure 9c, d, e. They can be described as two overlapping, depressed semi‐circles. Their intercept with the real axis at high frequency is associated with the Ohmic resistance (Rs), while the difference between the second intercept at low frequency and the first intercept gives the activation resistance, Rp. Analysis using equivalent circuits was performed and the results are provided in Figure 9f. The EIS indicates continuous improvement in cell performance with time. The high frequency resistance (Rs) is the sum of contributions from the resistance of the membrane, the interface between the catalyst layer and the membrane, and the electrolyte solution and the current collector. At low cell voltage the polarisation resistance dominates the overall performance since charge transfer resistance is inversely related to the electrode over‐potential; whereas the series resistance becomes comparable to the charge resistance at a cell voltage of 2 V. From fitting of the EIS of Figure 9, all the resistances were observed to decrease with time: at 1.8 V, i. e. in the region of a usable practical current density, Rs decreased from 0.18 to 0.14 Ohm cm2 and Rp from 0.40 to 0.24 Ohm cm2. The decrease of polarisation resistance with time was in part due to the progressive increase of current density; however, since from 130 to 260 hrs the current was kept constant at 1 A cm−2 the corresponding decrease of the total resistance contribution is possibly associated with an improvement of the interfacial properties with time. The decrease of the series resistance (Rs) was moderate and possibly associated with an internal cleaning procedure (e. g. removal of impurities or carbonates) induced by the electrolysis process; this may also occur for the interface. The resistance of the electrolyte solution dominates Rs at low (0.1 M) concentration, while at higher (1.0 M) concentration, the membrane resistance becomes more significant and, at 1 A cm‐2, it represents around 40 % of the total Rs (calculated using the ex situ measurement of the membrane resistance).The MEA at the end of the test at 260 hours demonstrated a high‐frequency resistance that was lower than that of the reinforced mPBI/mTPN membrane reported in the literature, [43] although it is noted that that study was not focussed on high performance achievement. The results are consistent with membrane and catalyst layer activation and improved interfaces within the MEA over time, all without any indications of degradation.

Conclusions

This work was focussed on the development of advanced reinforced anion exchange membranes and characterisation of their properties of relevance for anion exchange membrane water electrolysis. Interaction between the PBIO fibres and the anion exchange ionomer allowed the development of a polymer blend region, with a continuity between the fibre and the ionomer which confers high stability on the composite membranes. The reinforced and non‐reinforced membranes present high hydroxide ion conductivity of about 80 mS cm−1, which is fully retained in the PBIO‐reinforced mTPN membranes after 1 month of immersion in 1 M KOH. In contrast, the conductivity of the non‐reinforced mTPN declines by 25 % over the same period. The PBIO‐reinforced mTPN was characterised in a water electrolysis cell fed with 1 M KOH at the anode side, and using non‐noble metal catalysts at the anode and the cathode. The performance of the cell was observed to increase continuously over the 260 hours test period, the cell reaching 1.0 A cm−2 at 2.06 V at 50 °C, using PGM‐free catalysts, although no optimisation of the catalyst layer composition was made. There was no evidence for membrane or catalyst layer degradation.
