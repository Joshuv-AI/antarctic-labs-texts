Feasibility of an online nutrition intervention to improve adherence to healthy and sustainable diets in young Australian adults: protocol for a pilot pre-post intervention study
Authors: Bill Tiger Lam, Ewa A Szymlek-Gay, Christel Larsson, Claire Margerison
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCurrent diets which are commonly high in meat and ultra-processed foods are unhealthy and unsustainable and contribute significantly to climate change, environmental degradation and poor health outcomes. Transitioning to healthy and sustainable diets that are rich in plant-based foods and low in animal products could reduce environmental impacts and improve population health. Young Australian adults are a critical target group for dietary intervention as they are motivated towards climate action and have the lowest diet quality out of all adult age groups. As such, this study proposes a digital nutrition intervention to promote healthy and sustainable diets in this population group.Methods and analysisA 4-week pilot pre-post intervention will be conducted on the Deakin Wellbeing mobile application between July and August 2025. 32 young adults (18–25 years old; current student and/or staff at Deakin University; consume less than 260 g/week of legumes or 175 g/week nuts, living in Australia) will receive 4 weeks of the intervention to improve their adherence to a healthy and sustainable diet. Primary outcomes include feasibility (retention rate) and acceptability (engagement and user experience). Secondary outcomes include sustainable food literacy, legume and nut intakes, and adherence to a healthy and sustainable diet. Primary outcomes will be reported with descriptive statistics, while changes in secondary outcomes at each study time point will be measured using repeated measures Analysis of Variance, Friedman tests and McNemar’s tests.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval to conduct the study was granted by the Deakin University Human Research Ethics Committee (2024/HE000163). A summary of findings will be disseminated to key stakeholders, for example, Deakin University Student Engagement groups, and will also be presented to the wider research community at conferences and via peer-reviewed publications. Introduction

Current food systems are exacerbating the climate crisis, environmental degradation1 and the double burden of malnutrition.2 Diets high in meat and ultra-processed foods are largely accessible in high-income countries; however, they have large negative impacts on the environment due to their more intensive energy inputs and subsequently, higher greenhouse gas emissions.3 These diets may also contribute to a host of health problems such as cardiovascular disease, premature mortality4 and type 2 diabetes.5 To combat these pressing issues, healthy and sustainable diets need to be promoted and adopted for the preservation of planetary and human health. These diets are characterised by a high consumption of minimally processed plant-based foods such as fruits, vegetables, wholegrains, nuts and legumes, and lower intakes of animal source and ultra-processed foods.6 Evidence shows that transitioning to these diets can reduce the environmental impact of Australian diets by 46%, while also improving core food group intake and nutrient adequacy.7

Young Australian adults (aged 18–25 years) may be a pivotal age group in promoting demand for these diets through digital platforms, as they often hold strong pro-environmental values8 and are highly engaged with digital media.9 Despite this, research indicates that young adults have the poorest diet quality of all adult age groups, characterised by the highest intakes of ultra-processed foods10 and lowest intakes of fruits and vegetables.11 As poor health behaviours adopted in childhood and young adulthood often persist into later life without intervention,12 young Australian adults are a priority group for dietary intervention. Poor diet quality is associated with adverse health outcomes,13 reduced quality of life14 and increased risk of diet-related chronic diseases15 such as obesity, the prevalence of which is rapidly increasing in this age group.16 Although young Australian adults are motivated to take action on climate change, they tend to underestimate both the environmental impacts of food production and the benefits of adopting sustainable dietary patterns.8 Additionally, barriers such as limited cooking skills, low food literacy and lack of availability and accessibility of healthy and sustainable food options, both when grocery shopping and dining out, reduce young adults’ capacity to follow healthy and sustainable diets.17 Therefore, developing and implementing online nutrition interventions tailored to this age group are imperative to help establish and sustain healthy eating habits across their lifespan. These interventions should promote increased consumption of fruits, vegetables, nuts and legumes, while encouraging reduced intake of meat and ultra-processed foods.1 To effectively engage young adults, a variety of digital media formats, such as videos, images, audio and text, should be employed.

Before implementing nutrition interventions, it is essential to ground the intervention functions in behaviour change theory to ensure the content is relevant and effectively addresses the needs of the target population. Using a combination of the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behaviour (COM-B) model and the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) when consulting individuals can help identify relevant and specific opportunities for behaviour change.18 19 The Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW)18 can then be used to determine the most appropriate intervention and policy functions to address an individual’s behavioural needs, as identified through the COM-B model and TDF domains. Previous online nutrition interventions underpinned by behaviour change theory have shown promise in promoting healthier dietary habits in young adults, such as increasing fruit and vegetable intake.20,22 However, few studies have focused on promoting overall healthy and sustainable dietary patterns in this age group.23 24 Instead, most have targeted the improvement of a specific food group (eg, fruits or vegetables), or the reduction of an unhealthful food group (eg, processed meat).20 25 Accordingly, the current study aims to develop, implement and evaluate a pilot healthy and sustainable nutrition intervention, underpinned by behaviour change theory, to improve overall adherence to these diets in young Australian adults.

Methods

The Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2013 Statement and checklist (online supplemental material S1) were used to guide the reporting and structure of the protocol. The Template for Intervention Description and Replication checklist (online supplemental material S2) was also used as an extension to the SPIRIT checklist to provide detailed information about the characteristics, development and implementation plan of the intervention.

Study design and setting

The study will be a pilot single-arm pre-post intervention study. This study design was chosen to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability and preliminary efficacy of the intervention. The intervention will take place between July and August 2025. All participants will receive the intervention, which will involve using and engaging with the Deakin Wellbeing mobile application for 4 weeks. Online Qualtrics surveys will be administered at baseline, during the final week of the intervention and 1 month after the completion of the intervention to measure changes in sustainable food literacy, nut and legume intakes and adherence to a healthy and sustainable diet. The study, as well as data collection, will be coordinated online.

Participants

Young adults (aged 18–25 years) who are currently living in Australia will be recruited to take part in the research. As the Deakin Wellbeing mobile application is currently only accessible to Deakin university staff and students, the setting for this study will be the Deakin University community. To be eligible, participants must consume below half of the Planetary Health Diet recommendations for legumes (260 g/week) or nuts (175 g/week), have a smartphone that is able to download and access the Deakin Wellbeing mobile application, and can understand written and verbal English to engage with the app content. Young adults who are pregnant or breastfeeding, have a legume or nut allergy, are currently participating in other nutrition interventions, or are currently receiving care from a nutritionist or dietitian will be excluded from the study. Participants will confirm their eligibility via a 16-item online screening survey.

Patient and public involvement

Young Australian adults from the public were not directly consulted about the design of the intervention. However, their priorities, experiences and preferences were investigated in previous studies. Results from these informed the research question and outcome measures.

Intervention

A pilot nutrition intervention programme was designed by dietitians and nutritionists and will be implemented using the Deakin Wellbeing mobile application. The intervention will be delivered at the group level and no individual consultations with a nutritionist or dietitian will be involved. The intervention mapping framework26 was used to guide the development, and implementation and evaluation plan of the intervention.

Development

The development of the pilot nutrition intervention programme was underpinned by the intervention mapping framework principles: (1) identifying the logic model of the problem, (2) defining programme outcomes and objectives, (3) programme design and (4) programme production.

First, the health problem (poor diet quality) in the young Australian adult population group was investigated via a cross-sectional study and literature review. The study was conducted between February and October 2024 and involved a 15 min online survey and completion of up to four 24-hour diet recalls. The aim was to understand young Australian adults’ knowledge, attitudes and behaviours towards general and healthy and sustainable nutrition. Questions were underpinned by the COM-B model and TDF to identify relevant and specific opportunities to improve adherence to healthy and sustainable diets. Findings from the literature review and study suggested that young Australian adults had poor diet quality and low adherence to a healthy and sustainable diet. Furthermore, the study identified that unfavourable attitudes towards healthy and sustainable diets, lower self-efficacy and specific barriers, enablers and food motives were associated with lower adherence to these diets.

Following the identification of the health problem and the potential contributing factors, a working group was created, consisting of nutritionists, dietitians, health promoters and student engagement officers, to discuss the context, goal, outcomes and objectives of the pilot nutrition intervention. The goal of the intervention is to improve adherence to a healthy and sustainable diet, while impact evaluation outcomes involve improving legume and nut intakes towards the Planetary Health Diet recommendations.1 These food groups were selected as the previous study showed that young adults did not adhere to the nuts and legumes Planetary Health Diet recommendations. Also, as legumes and nuts are considered traditional meat alternatives, it is expected that this will lead to a consequential reduction, and improved adherence to the red meat, poultry, fish and eggs food group recommendations. Impact evaluation objectives include improving sustainable food literacy components, including knowledge of, and attitudes, behaviours and intentions towards adopting a healthy and sustainable diet.

Second, a qualitative study27 was conducted to consult young Australian adults on the design of the programme. This study was conducted between December 2023 and February 2024 and involved a 30 min individual interview with each participant. The aim was to explore young Australian adults’ preferences, perceptions and use of online nutrition content and programmes. Preferences for programme features included nutrition information and practical advice, tracking and goal setting, activities, for example, challenges, and community and dietitian support. These features were considered when selecting the platform of the pilot intervention programme, and designing the nutrition content.

The Deakin Wellbeing mobile application, developed by StudentXP.ai, was chosen as a mode of delivery to facilitate behaviour change as it incorporates the above features. A plan for the scheduling and content of educational information, habits and challenges was produced. All aspects of the plan were justified with reference to the BCW (online supplemental material S3). The plan was discussed with the working group for feedback before programme implementation.

Intervention components

Intervention components include educational content, a habit tracker and a healthy eating challenge all located within the Deakin Wellbeing mobile application.

Educational content

Educational content will be scheduled as posts/notifications and more detailed information will be accessed in a ‘Resources’ tab. Types of educational content include short videos, image posts, infographics and articles about the importance of eating healthier and more sustainably, practical strategies for example, snack swaps and recipes. Included content was either sourced from a third-party content creator who has formal training in nutrition, for example, university-qualified nutritionists on Instagram or verified website/organisation, for example, Category: Fact Sheets | Nutrition Australia and The Planetary Health Diet - EAT, or self-created by a team of university-qualified nutrition professionals. If sourced from a third party, either permission was obtained from the creators before downloading and posting the content on the Deakin Wellbeing app or a link to the content will be shared on the platform. All content posts/notifications were underpinned by the BCW. Behaviour change techniques and intervention functions were selected to target knowledge and attitudes deficits as identified in our previous unpublished study.

Habit tracker

Participants can schedule and record healthy eating habits in a ‘Habits’ tab. The habits they can record are (1) substitute meat with legumes, (2) have a nut-based snack, (3) have a legume-based snack, (4) add nuts to food, (5) have five different vegetables in a meal and (6) swap refined grains for wholemeal. A weekly and monthly progress chart will also be available in this section to allow participants to track their habits. A Question & Answer section will be available within the mobile application for each habit, where the participants can ask questions to other participants. This section will be moderated by a qualified dietitian. The dietitian will facilitate discussion and ensure accurate information is shared and misconceptions are corrected.

Healthy eating challenge

Participants will be given the opportunity to participate in a healthy eating challenge. Information about this will be provided in a ‘Challenge’ tab. The challenge will involve participants making any recipe from the Planetary Health Recipes - EAT during the last week of the 4-week intervention. They will record this as a new habit in the Habit Tracker and also upload a photo of their creation in the end of intervention survey. If they choose to complete the challenge, they will be given the option to go into the draw to receive an additional $30 voucher.

Participant commitment

The pilot intervention will require participants to use the Deakin Wellbeing mobile application for four consecutive weeks. Content will be posted three to four times a week via posts/notifications. Each content post will take less than 5 min to engage with. However, participants will be encouraged to access and engage with the other intervention components, that is, resources, habit tracking and challenge. It is expected that total usage time will not surpass more than 1 hour per week. As the study is a feasibility trial, usage and interaction of the app will be left to the participants’ discretion.

Discontinuation of intervention

Participants can withdraw at any time up to 1 week after the follow-up survey is administered. If they do so, they will be asked to delete the Deakin Wellbeing mobile application and sign a withdrawal form if they want all their Qualtrics data deleted from the study. Participants will not be influenced by the researchers to withdraw at any stage of the study, except if they report that the intervention content caused any harm, for example, physical and/or psychological. In this case, the researchers will offer the option to withdraw and provide general health resources, for example, local doctor contacts, via email to the participant. They will also be given the option to sign a withdrawal form to delete their data from the study. There will be no alternative interventions for these participants.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes for this study include programme feasibility and acceptability. For feasibility, retention rates will be measured. Retention rate will be calculated by dividing the total number of participants who completed the end of intervention survey by the total number who were recruited and eligible to participate, that is, completed baseline survey. Feasibility will be considered successful if the retention rate ≥80%. These cut-offs are based on previous health interventions in young adults.28 29

Acceptability will be measured by evaluating participants’ engagement and user experience. The Deakin Wellbeing app will collect app engagement data daily, that is, unique active users, number of habits completed, number of participants in challenge and clicks to content posts, to monitor adherence. Engagement will also be self-reported in the end of intervention survey, that is, frequency of use of intervention components. User experience of the app will be measured using the Post-Study System Usability Questionnaire,30 while close-ended and open-ended questions for user experience, that is, satisfaction and perception of dietary improvement, of the intervention components and the research study will be guided by Proctor’s outcomes for implementation research.31 Feasibility and acceptability outcomes will be measured at the end of the intervention only. These outcomes measure participants’ immediate experiences with the intervention and, as such, immediate post-intervention feedback reduces the risk of recall bias.

Secondary outcomes include impact evaluation outcomes such as mean changes in sustainable food literacy (ie, knowledge, attitudes, skills, action intent and action strategies), nut and legume intakes, and adherence to a healthy and sustainable diet. These will be measured in the baseline, end of intervention and follow-up surveys (20–30 min each) to assess changes as a result of the intervention, and sustained changes over time (follow-up). An original 37-item food checklist guided by the Planetary Health Diet guidelines1 will be used to assess dietary intakes and sustainable food literacy will be measured using a validated questionnaire,32 while adherence to a healthy and sustainable diet will be assessed using the World Index for Sustainability and Health score.33 The timeline for enrolment, intervention and outcome assessment is presented in figure 1.

Communication to participants will occur via an email address provided in the eligibility screening survey. Reminder emails will be sent to participants at enrolment, that is, after completion of the eligibility screening survey, baseline, at the start of the intervention, end of intervention and follow-up to complete data collection and maintain adherence to the intervention.

Sample size

For pilot studies that aim to estimate a SD for sample size calculations, a sample size between 24 and 50 is recommended.34,37 This study will aim to recruit 32 participants, to detect a significant increase in legume intake based on a large effect size of d=0.8, power of 80%, assumed attrition rate of 20%, and 95% confidence level. Mean legume intake for the young Australian adult population was calculated to be 28±51 g/day, based on our previous unpublished study.

Recruitment

Recruitment will be conducted between June and July 2025. Participants from previous research studies will be contacted if they expressed interest in participating in future research studies. An email will be sent to these participants to advertise the study to them. In addition, advertising materials such as flyers will be posted on designated poster spaces within Deakin University campuses. Electronic flyers will be posted on Deakin University course sites, disciplinary social media channels and Student Communication recruitment channels. These flyers will provide information about the study, and a link or Quick Response code to the landing page of the eligibility screening questionnaire. Prospective participants will be prompted to read the Plain Language Statement. They will be prompted to provide their informed consent (online supplemental material S4) by selecting ‘Yes, I consent’, before answering the screening questions.

Data management

Identifiable data from the screening questionnaire, that is, email addresses, will be collected to contact participants. The email addresses will be kept in a separate file to contact participants during the trial and for reimbursement purposes, that is, sending of shopping vouchers shortly after completion of follow-up survey. When the email addresses have been used for this reason, the files containing the email addresses will be destroyed. Stored data will be non-identifiable and will be stored in a secured file to maintain participant security and confidentiality.

A data monitoring committee is not needed for this study as it involves minimal risk and is a small-scale pilot study and therefore, data are insufficient to perform interim analysis.

Data analysis

Primary outcomes (feasibility and acceptability) will be reported using descriptive statistics, for example, frequencies, percentages, means and SD. Changes in secondary outcomes (diet intakes and sustainable food literacy) between baseline, end of intervention and follow-up will be measured using repeated measures Analysis of Variance, Friedman tests and McNemar’s tests. Participant data will be included in the analysis at all available time points. No additional analysis is planned.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval to conduct the study was granted by the Deakin University Human Research Ethics Committee (DUHREC) (Approval ID: 2024/HE000163) and has been registered as a clinical trial in the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ANZCTR). There are no plans to make changes during the study; however, if needed, protocol amendments will firstly be approved by DUHREC, recorded on the ANZCTR website and then communicated to trial participants, if necessary. Only the research team will have access to the data; however, access to the data may be made available by the principal investigator on reasonable request and on approval by DUHREC.

A summary of the findings will be disseminated to key stakeholders, for example, Deakin University Student Engagement groups, and will be sent to all participants via email. The findings will also be presented to the wider research community at conferences and via peer-reviewed publications.
