Peritoneal dialysis-related peritonitis caused by Mycobacterium brisbanense: A case report and literature review
Authors: Teppei Hasegawa, Daisuke Ono, Takayuki Kawamura, Yamamoto Ryo, Kohji Komori, Tetsuo Yamaguchi, Akito Maeshima, Kazuyuki Mimura, Kunihisa Tsukada, Hideaki Oka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mycobacterium brisbanense, a rare, rapidly growing, nontuberculous mycobacterium, is typically associated with respiratory infections. Herein, we report the first known case of peritoneal dialysis (PD)-related peritonitis caused by M. brisbanense in a male patient in his 70 s with end-stage renal disease due to chronic glomerulonephritis and renal sclerosis. Despite initial empirical treatment with cefazolin and ceftazidime for co-isolated bacteria (Staphylococcus warneri and Kocuria marina), the symptoms worsened. A subsequent acid-fast bacillus culture of the PD effluent was positive, and the microorganism was identified as M. brisbanense using time-of-flight mass spectrometry and whole-genome sequencing. Antibiotic susceptibility testing revealed susceptibility to all the following administered agents. The patient was successfully treated by PD-catheter removal and intravenous administration of amikacin, imipenem/cilastatin, and oral azithromycin for 40 days, followed by oral azithromycin and sitafloxacin for 153 days. M. brisbanense is a potential opportunistic pathogen in PD-related peritonitis that requires tailored antimicrobial therapy.

Introduction

Mycobacterium brisbanense, a rapidly growing non-tuberculous mycobacterium (NTM) in the Mycobacterium fortuitum third biovariant complex group, is ubiquitously present in the environment, including soil, dust, and water. It is known for its ability to degrade herbicides and was isolated from herbicide-contaminated soil in Brisbane, Australia [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]. M. brisbanens shares virulence genes with pathogenic mycobacteria such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Mycobacterium abscessus, which might contribute to its potential as an opportunistic pathogen [7]. Moreover, the unknown resistance profile to first-line anti-tuberculosis drugs necessitates careful consideration during treatment, especially given the limited data available on M. brisbanense. To date, reports of M. brisbanense infection in the literature have been scarce, with most documented cases involving respiratory infections. [7], [8], [9]. To the best of our knowledge, peritoneal dialysis (PD)-related peritonitis caused by M. brisbanense has not been reported in the literature. Herein, we report a case of PD-related peritonitis caused by M. brisbanense.

Case report

A man in his 70 s had been undergoing PD for 7 years due to end-stage renal disease secondary to chronic glomerulonephritis and renal sclerosis. The patient experienced two episodes of PD-related peritonitis, caused by bacterial pathogens including skin commensals and Enterobacterales, which improved with antibiotic therapy. The patient visited the outpatient department of our hospital, complaining of fever and cloudy PD effluent (day 0) (Fig. 1). His body temperature was 37.5 °C, with no other abnormal vital signs, and physical examination revealed no abnormalities, including no abdominal tenderness. Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with PD-associated peritonitis. Outpatient treatment was initiated with intravenous cefazolin (1 g every 24 h, dose adjusted based on renal function) and ceftazidime (1 g every 24 h). On day 2, two PD effluent culture samples submitted on day 0 yielded Staphylococcus warneri and Kocuria marina, with each organism isolated from one culture set, while concurrently submitted blood cultures were negative. These findings raised the possibility of polymicrobial PD-associated peritonitis during the early clinical course. Although susceptibility testing revealed that both strains were susceptible to cefazolin and ceftazidime, fever persisted and abdominal tenderness developed, suggesting that these bacteria alone did not fully explain the subsequent clinical deterioration. Laboratory findings.

On day 10, the patient was admitted with worsening fever and abdominal tenderness (Fig. 1). On admission, his vital signs were as follows: Glasgow Coma Scale, E4V5M6; temperature, 37.6 °C; blood pressure, 122/58 mmHg; and pulse rate, 92beats/min. Physical examination revealed abdominal tenderness and board-like rigidity suggestive of peritonitis. Blood test results revealed elevated WBC count (18,700/μL) and C-reactive protein level (CRP; 24.1 mg/dL) (Table 1). Subsequently, the blood cultures and PD effluent cultures submitted on admission were negative. Computed tomography revealed increased fat density in the peritoneum near the greater omentum, consistent with peritonitis-induced inflammation. Owing to the lack of clinical response to intravenous cefazolin and ceftazidime, and with concern for gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria resistant to these agents, therapy was empirically escalated to intravenous meropenem (0.5 g every 24 h) and vancomycin (1 g every 24 h). PD was discontinued, and the patient was switched to hemodialysis. Despite the initial improvement in WBC count and CRP level following treatment, difficulties in drainage of the PD tube persisted. On day 16, a residual sample of PD effluent collected on admission was subjected to acid-fast bacilli (AFB) culture since no bacteria were detected in the blood or PD effluent cultures. The AFB culture was positive six days after incubation (day 22). Given the lack of a sustained response to treatment targeting the initially isolated bacteria, the negativity of subsequent routine bacterial cultures, and the detection of acid-fast bacilli from the PD effluent collected before antimicrobial escalation, rapidly growing mycobacteria (RGM) were considered to be clinically significant pathogens rather than contaminants. The PD catheter was removed the following day (day 23).

Cultures were obtained from the catheter tip, inner cuff, outer cuff, and ascitic fluid. However, all the subsequent AFB cultures yielded negative results. On day 24, the antibiotic treatment was switched to intravenous amikacin (300 mg on dialysis days), intravenous imipenem/cilastatin (0.5 g of imipenem every 24 h), and oral azithromycin (250 mg every 24 h) (Fig. 1), an antibiotic regimen targeting RGM. On day 27, time-of-flight mass spectrometry confirmed that the RGM strain was M. brisbanense. Draft whole-genome analysis of the strain was performed using the MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The species was identified as M. brisbanense based on Fast Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) against type strain genomes using the "Taxonomy check" function of the DNA Data Bank of Japan Fast Annotation and Submission Tool [10]. ANI % values (97.4%) were interpreted based on the criterion that strain pairs with an ANI of > 95% are considered indistinguishable at the species level [11]. Based on previous literature on M. brisbanense cases, which suggested susceptibility to amikacin, imipenem/cilastatin, and clarithromycin [8], [9], the antibiotic regimen was continued. On day 64, the M. brisbanense strain was confirmed to be susceptible to all administered antibiotics based on the susceptibility testing performed using the broth microdilution method (BrothMIC RGM®; Kyokuto, Tokyo, Japan) according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute M24S guidelines (2nd edition) (Table 2) [12]. Discussion

Herein, we report a case of PD-related peritonitis caused by M. brisbanense. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of PD-related peritonitis caused by M. brisbanense.

In this case, M. brisbanense was considered to be the causative pathogen of PD-associated peritonitis. During the early clinical course, the detection of S. warneri and K. marina in the initial PD effluent culture raised the possibility of polymicrobial PD-associated peritonitis involving these organisms. However, treatment with cefazolin and ceftazidime, which were expected to be effective based on the susceptibility testing, did not result in any clinical or laboratory improvements. In contrast, the administration of amikacin, imipenem/cilastatin, and azithromycin, to which M. brisbanense is susceptible (Table 2), resulted in both clinical and laboratory improvements (Fig. 1).

In addition, temporal improvements in the blood WBC count and CRP level, as well as negative cultures of the catheter tip, inner cuff, outer cuff, and ascitic fluid, were observed after meropenem administration. Based on the susceptibility testing results for M. brisbanense, treatment with meropenem may result in temporal improvements and culture negativity. Taken together, these results suggest that M. brisbanense was one of the causative pathogen of PD-associated peritonitis in this case.

M. brisbanense is a species within the M. fortuitum third biovariant complex, which was newly designated based on its isolation from Brisbane, Australia [1]. To date, very few cases of M. brisbanense infections have been reported, primarily involving the respiratory tract [7], [8], [9] (Table 3). Therefore, this case of PD-associated peritonitis represents a rare presentation of M. brisbanense infection. Peritonitis is a major complication in patients on PD [13]. Typically, the causative pathogens are skin commensals or enteric bacteria; however, in rare cases, unusual pathogens, such as NTM or fungi, are involved. Among the NTM, M. abscessus, M. fortuitum, and Mycobacterium chelonae are the most commonly reported causes of PD-associated peritonitis [13]. NTM peritonitis in patients undergoing PD is difficult to manage because evidence-based recommendations for antimicrobial selection, treatment duration, and catheter management remain limited [14]. In a recent systematic review, PD catheter removal was performed in 139 of 162 patients (85.8%) with NTM peritonitis, highlighting the frequent need for source control in this setting [14]. Since NTM are not typically detected by routine bacterial cultures and require specific mycobacterial testing, diagnostic delays may lead to treatment failure. In PD-related peritonitis caused by mycobacteria, antimicrobial therapy should be guided by in vitro susceptibility results, and initial treatment generally may involve combination therapy that includes one or more intravenous agents for 2–6 weeks, followed by prolonged oral antimicrobial therapy for at least 6 months [13], [14], [15], [17]. Moreover, conservative treatment with antimicrobial therapy alone has poor outcomes in PD-related peritonitis caused by NTM or fungi; therefore, catheter removal is recommended [13]. Because treatment often requires prolonged multidrug therapy and catheter removal, early submission of mycobacterial cultures should be considered in patients with refractory PD-related peritonitis. In this case, the catheter was removed on day 23.

Combination antibiotic therapy is generally recommended. However, when a rare NTM such as M. brisbanense is the causative pathogen, evidence regarding the optimal treatment regimen is limited. Previous studies have examined the antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of M. brisbanense [1]. M. brisbanense is resistant to ampicillin, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, erythromycin, minocycline, doxycycline, and vancomycin and susceptible to amoxicillin/clavulanate, imipenem/cilastatin, ciprofloxacin, amikacin, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. Antimicrobial susceptibility and treatment regimens have been described in reported M. brisbanense infections(Table 3). In the case reported by Poh et al., the isolate was susceptible to ciprofloxacin, clarithromycin, amikacin, imipenem/cilastatin, and linezolid, and treatment with rifampicin, ethambutol, and levofloxacin, followed by clarithromycin and levofloxacin, was conducted [8]. In another case reported by Gynthersen et al., the patient was successfully treated with oral clarithromycin, moxifloxacin, and inhaled amikacin, although susceptibility testing was not reported [9]. In the case reported by Lee et al., the isolate was susceptible to amikacin, imipenem, and moxifloxacin and intermediate to linezolid; the patient was treated with oral moxifloxacin, linezolid, and clarithromycin [16]. In the present case, antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using the broth microdilution method (BrothMIC RGM®; Kyokuto, Tokyo, Japan) (Table 2). Notably, we demonstrated that the isolated M. brisbanense strain was susceptible to meropenem, azithromycin, and linezolid, for which no prior susceptibility data have been reported. In the present case, the patient initially received a combination of intravenous amikacin, imipenem/cilastatin, and oral azithromycin, all of which resulted in favorable susceptibility (Table 2). Subsequently, the patient was switched to oral azithromycin and sitafloxacin for a total treatment duration of 193 days. Based on this case as well as previous literature, M. brisbanense was susceptible to amikacin, imipenem/cilastatin, azithromycin, and fluoroquinolones [1], [8], [9]. In addition, comparison with a report summarizing the antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of seven human-derived M. brisbanense isolates identified in the Americas showed that the susceptibility profile of the present isolate was generally consistent with those findings [18]. Although the appropriate treatment might differ based on the epidemiology, infection focus, and severity, empirical therapy with a combination of amikacin and imipenem/cilastatin with either azithromycin or fluoroquinolone may be considered appropriate for the treatment of M. brisbanense infections. In addition, adjusted therapy based on antimicrobial susceptibility results is recommended.

In summary, we report the successful treatment of PD-related peritonitis caused by M. brisbanense after PD catheter removal and the administration of intravenous amikacin, imipenem/cilastatin, and oral azithromycin, followed by oral azithromycin and sitafloxacin. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of PD-related peritonitis caused by M. brisbanense. Although mycobacteria are rare causes of PD-related peritonitis and may be overlooked, mycobacterial cultures should be considered in refractory cases. Species-level identification and susceptibility testing are important because antimicrobial susceptibility varies among NTM species. The present case also supports the importance of catheter removal as source control combined with multidrug antimicrobial therapy. Given the limited data onoptimal treatment strategies, this report provides additional insights into the clinical management of M. brisbanense infections. In addition, extrapulmonary NTM infections may be under-recognized, even in immunocompetent patients; therefore, testing for mycobacteria should be considered in chronic or refractory infections.

Further accumulation of clinical data and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies.
