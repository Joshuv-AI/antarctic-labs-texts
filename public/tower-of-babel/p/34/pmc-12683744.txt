Triglyceride-Glucose Body Mass Index Predicts Asthma Exacerbations: A Prospective Study
Authors: Fan Gao, Jiahui Lei, Yingjie Guo, Limin Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeInsulin resistance, a central feature of metabolic syndrome, has been associated with asthma exacerbations. However, the role of metabolic markers, specifically the triglyceride-glucose body mass index (TyG-BMI), in predicting asthma exacerbations is not fully explored. This study explored the predictive value of the TyG-BMI, a biomarker of metabolic syndrome and insulin resistance, in the risk of asthma exacerbations.MethodsThis single-center prospective cohort study enrolled 484 patients with stable asthma between March and October of 2023. The study employed K-means clustering analysis to categorize patients according to TyG-BMI. The primary endpoint was the first occurrence of asthma exacerbation during one year of follow-up. Additionally, weighted quantile sum regression was performed to quantify the contribution of fasting triglycerides (TGs), fasting blood glucose, and BMI to the TyG-BMI, aiming to provide a more comprehensive explanation.ResultsOf the 484 patients who met the study criteria, 359 experienced exacerbations during the study period. The fully adjusted model showed an odds ratio of 10.65 (95% confidence interval, 2.51–45.25) for patients with higher TyG-BMI levels. Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a threshold effect at the TyG-BMI of 215, beyond which the asthma exacerbation rate increased significantly. Weight assignment analysis indicated that TG was the primary contributor to the TyG-BMI.ConclusionsThe TyG-BMI is an independent risk factor for asthma exacerbation, highlighting its potential utility in identifying high-risk patients and guiding personalized interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Asthma is one of the most common chronic non-communicable diseases worldwide, posing significant challenges to public health and imposing a substantial economic burden. According to the Global Asthma Report 2018, approximately 300 million people suffer from asthma globally, with exacerbations directly leading to about 450,000 deaths annually.1 Given the high prevalence and low control rates of asthma,2 developing a cost-effective, reproducible, and easily scalable clinical indicator for early screening and disease management is crucial to reducing its impact.

Insulin resistance, a core mechanism of metabolic syndrome, has garnered increasing attention in asthma research in recent years. Patients with metabolic syndrome often present with chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, obesity, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia.3 These metabolic abnormalities may significantly aggravate airway inflammation and airway remodeling, thus making asthma control more difficult.4 In particular, insulin resistance is considered a critical pathological factor and a potential therapeutic target in obese patients with asthma or those with insulin resistance-associated asthma.5 Recent studies highlight the potential of insulin-sensitizing agents like metformin in asthma management, showing benefits beyond improved insulin sensitivity, including symptom relief and reduced exacerbation risks through anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and metabolic regulatory effects in asthma.67 Therefore, insulin resistance may serve as a crucial link between inflammation and metabolic disorders, and could act as an indicator for predicting asthma exacerbations, offering new insights for the precision treatment.

The triglyceride glucose-body mass index (TyG-BMI) is a novel and effective surrogate marker for insulin resistance that integrates metabolic and obesity indicators to comprehensively assess systemic metabolic status. It is particularly effective in reflecting the combined effects of obesity and metabolic disorders.8 Compared with other TyG-based composite indices such as triglyceride glucose-waist circumference index (TyG-WC) or triglyceride glucose-waist to height ratio (TyG-WHtR), TyG-BMI provides a more direct measure of overall body mass, which may influence lung function through mechanical load and obesity-related inflammation. This dual representation of metabolic and mass-related factors makes TyG-BMI particularly relevant in asthma, where both systemic metabolic disturbances and excess body mass are implicated in disease progression. Additionally, TyG-BMI is cost-effective, simple to calculate, and widely applicable, making it practical and convenient in clinical practice. Although previous studies have established homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance and TyG as independent risk factors for predicting asthma exacerbations, there is a lack of systematic research on the association between TyG-BMI and asthma exacerbations.91011 This study aimed to explore the predictive value of TyG-BMI for asthma exacerbations and further examine its relevance across different subgroups. Moreover, we compared the relative contributions of fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglycerides (TGs), and body mass index (BMI) to clarify the distinct roles of these metabolic components.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and population

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital of Zhengzhou University (approval No. 2022-158). All participants were fully informed of the study objectives and procedures and provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, including consent for anonymized data to be published.

Between March and October of 2023, a total of 506 patients with stable asthma were enrolled from the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine at Henan Provincial People’s Hospital. After one year of follow-up, 484 patients completed all scheduled assessments.

Some participants were excluded on the basis of the following criteria: 1) age of under 18 years; 2) pregnancy or a planned pregnancy within the 1 year; 3) asthma exacerbations or respiratory infections within the past 4 weeks; 4) comorbid chronic respiratory diseases (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pulmonary fibrosis) or severe respiratory failure; 5) severe coronary heart disease, stroke, major psychiatric disorders, or life expectancy less than 1 year; 6) those deemed ineligible by investigators or who voluntarily withdrew from the study.

Data collection and definitions

Baseline data were collected at recruitment and included personal information, medical history within the past 6 months, medication history, laboratory test results, and pulmonary function parameters. Personal information comprised case number, age, sex, BMI, blood pressure, smoking and alcohol consumption history, and educational level. Medical history covered history of allergen exposure, presence of allergic rhinitis, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Fasting venous blood samples were obtained within 24 hours of hospital admission by professional medical personnel. Laboratory indicators included immunoglobulin E (IgE), neutrophil count, eosinophil count, FBG, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), uric acid (UA), creatinine, total cholesterol (TC), TGs, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). Pulmonary function parameters included forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) as a percentage of the predicted value (%pred), forced vital capacity (FVC) %pred, FEV1/FVC %pred and maximum mid-expiratory flow (MMEF) %pred.

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the following formula: 141 × min (Scr/κ, 1)α × max (Scr/κ, 1)−1.209 × 0.993Age × (sex adjustment), where κ was 0.9 for males and 0.7 for females; α was −0.411 for males and −0.329 for females; and the sex adjustment factor was 1 for males and 1.018 for females.

The exposure variable of this study was TyG-BMI, calculated as: Ln {TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2} × {Weight (kg)/Height (m)2}, where TG and FBG values were converted from mmol/L to mg/dL using conversion factors of 88.57 and 18.02, respectively.

According to the current Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) guidelines,12 asthma diagnosis is based on recurrent respiratory symptoms (such as wheezing, shortness of breath, cough, and chest tightness) that typically vary over time and in intensity, often worsening at night or early morning. Diagnosis requires confirmation of reversible airflow limitation through lung function tests, including: 1) FEV1 increase ≥ 12% and ≥ 200 mL after bronchodilator use (e.g., salbutamol); 2) peak expiratory flow variability ≥ 20% within 2 weeks; or 3) a decline in FEV1 ≥ 20% from baseline after a standard acetylcholine challenge, while excluding other diseases with similar symptoms.

Asthma exacerbation12 is defined as a significant worsening of respiratory symptoms (such as wheezing, dyspnea, cough, or chest tightness) from baseline within a short period. It is accompanied by a decline in lung function (e.g., reduced peak expiratory flow or FEV1), requires adjustment of the current treatment regimen (such as increased use of reliever medications or systemic corticosteroids), and may necessitate emergency care or hospitalization.

Asthma treatment steps were classified according to the GINA guidelines.12 Steps 1–2 includes as-needed low-dose inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) plus short-acting β2-agonists (SABA). Step 3 consists of maintenance low-dose ICS plus long-acting β2-agonists (LABA) with as-needed SABA. Steps 4–5 involves medium- to high-dose ICS plus LABA maintenance with as-needed SABA, with consideration of add-on low-dose oral corticosteroids (OCS) or biologic therapy.

Covariates

Potential risk factors influencing asthma exacerbations were included as covariates in multivariable adjustments. These covariates comprised demographic variables (age, sex, educational level), smoking status, blood pressure, pulmonary function parameters, laboratory indicators, medication use (lipid-lowering, glucose-lowering and asthma medications), history of allergen exposure, presence of allergic rhinitis, anxiety and depression status, as well as diabetes and dyslipidemia. Laboratory indicators included neutrophil count, eosinophil count, IgE, HbA1c, UA, eGFR, TC, HDL-C, and LDL-C.

Follow-up and outcomes

Participants were followed for one year. Professional medical staff conducted telephone follow-ups every three months to record asthma control status. The primary study endpoint was the occurrence of asthma exacerbations. Patients who remained free of exacerbations were followed until the end of the 1-year study period. This design aimed to comprehensively assess the risk of asthma exacerbations and ensure the accuracy and completeness of follow-up data.

Statistical analysis

Participants were classified according to TyG-BMI clusters and the occurrence of endpoint events occurred during follow-up. An unsupervised K-means clustering algorithm was used to divide TyG-BMI values into 3 clusters. Three random values were selected as initial cluster centers and iteratively optimized to maximize within-cluster similarity while minimizing inter-cluster similarity. This data-driven method was chosen to avoid reliance on arbitrary or pre-defined clinical cutoffs, allowing natural grouping based on the distribution of TyG-BMI in our population. Such an approach can better reflect heterogeneity in metabolic status and may offer improved clinical stratification compared to traditional thresholds.

All variables were categorized as continuous or categorical. Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and those with non-normal distributions as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentage). For group comparisons, normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed with independent t-tests (2 groups) or one-way analysis of variance (3 or more groups). Non-normally distributed variables were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests (2 groups) or Kruskal-Wallis tests (3 or more groups). Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests when expected frequencies were < 10.

We employed a restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression model, with knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the TyG-BMI, to explore the linear association between the TyG-BMI and the endpoint events. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to evaluate the effect of different TyG-BMI levels on the incidence of asthma exacerbations, and intergroup differences were compared using the Log-rank test. Predictive performance of relevant metabolic indicators for asthma exacerbations, including the TyG-BMI, BMI, and the TyG index, was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The area under the curve and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to evaluate and compare their discriminative ability. The optimal cutoff value was determined using the Youden index (Sensitivity + Specificity − 1), and the corresponding sensitivity and specificity were calculated. Correlations between the TyG-BMI, other risk factors, and asthma exacerbations were evaluated using Pearson (for normally distributed variables), Spearman (for non-normally distributed variables), or point-biserial (for binary variables) correlation coefficients.

We used binary logistic regression to assess the independent effect of TyG-BMI on asthma exacerbations, constructing four adjusted models by sequentially adding covariates, including age, sex, educational level, smoking status, blood pressure, neutrophil count, eosinophil count, HbA1c, UA, eGFR, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, FEV1%pred, FVC %pred, FEV1/FVC %pred, MMEF %pred, IgE, history of allergen exposure, as well as diabetes, dyslipidemia, and asthma medication history. Additionally, we conducted a Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to explore the impact of the TyG-BMI on asthma exacerbations during the 1-year follow-up period. The adjustment models were consistent with those used in the binary logistic regression analysis. We further stratified the population based on age, sex, smoking status, history of allergen exposure, presence of allergic rhinitis, anxiety and depression status, diabetes, dyslipidemia, IgE levels, and LDL-C levels to evaluate interactions among different subgroups, calculating P values for interaction.

Moreover, we performed a Weighted Quantile Sum (WQS) regression analysis to assess the relative contribution of individual variables (TG, FBG, BMI) within the TyG-BMI to the outcome of asthma exacerbations by calculating a weighted linear index and assigning corresponding weights. Higher weights indicated a more significant effect of the variable on asthma exacerbations.

Finally, we conducted 3 sensitivity analyses: 1) including only never-smokers in the Cox regression analysis; 2) conducting a binary logistic regression analysis exclusively for patients with low IgE levels (< 100 IU/mL); and 3) including only risk factors positively associated with asthma exacerbations in the WQS model.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), Prism 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), and R 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Correlation between TyG-BMI and risk factors for asthma exacerbations

Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationship between TyG-BMI and risk factors for asthma exacerbations. TyG-BMI was positively correlated with BMI, DBP, SBP, neutrophil count, FBG, HbA1c, TC, TG, LDL-C, and UA (all P < 0.05) and negatively correlated with HDL-C (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S1).

Impact of TyG-BMI on asthma exacerbations

In the RCS model, the association between TyG-BMI and the risk of asthma exacerbations was statistically significant overall (P for overall < 0.001) and demonstrated a nonlinear pattern (P for nonlinear = 0.002) (Fig. 2B). The curve indicated a relatively flat risk when TyG-BMI was below approximately 215, followed by a sharp increase in hazard ratio beyond this threshold. Logistic regression analysis showed that each standard deviation increase in TyG-BMI was associated with an odds ratio (OR) of 4.95 (95% CI, 3.22–7.62) for values < 215, and 4.17 (95% CI, 3.36–5.16) for values ≥ 215. All associations were statistically significant (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2C).

To assess the incidence of asthma exacerbations across different TyG-BMI levels during the follow-up period, unadjusted Kaplan-Meier curves were generated (Supplementary Fig. S2). The cumulative incidence of exacerbations increased with higher TyG-BMI levels as categorized by K-means clustering (all Log-rank P < 0.001). Furthermore, ROC analysis revealed that the optimal cutoff value for TyG-BMI was 195.7, yielding a sensitivity of 0.79 and a specificity of 0.60 (Youden index = 0.43). For BMI, the optimal cutoff was 23.7 (sensitivity 0.67, specificity 0.64; Youden index = 0.31), and for the TyG index, the optimal cutoff was 8.83 (sensitivity 0.38, specificity 0.70; Youden index = 0.08). These results indicate that TyG-BMI demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy for asthma exacerbations (Supplementary Fig. S3).

During the 1-year follow-up, 359 patients experienced exacerbations, resulting in an exacerbation rate of 74.1% (Table 2). Supplementary Table S2 shows the association between TyG-BMI and asthma exacerbations. After adjusting for confounding factors (Model 4), the OR for exacerbations in Class 2 and Class 3 compared to Class 1 were 2.67 (95% CI, 1.41–5.06), and 10.65 (95% CI, 2.51–45.25), respectively.

Cox regression analysis further confirmed TyG-BMI as an independent risk factor for asthma exacerbations. The multivariable Cox proportional hazards model included not only variables statistically significant in univariate Cox regression (Supplementary Table S3) but also potential confounders that could influence exacerbations. In all adjusted models, TyG-BMI remained significantly associated with exacerbations (all P < 0.001) (Table 3). In the fully adjusted model (Model 4), the hazard ratio (HR) for exacerbations was 3.30 (95% CI, 2.51–4.33) in Class 2 and 22.65 (95% CI, 14.84–34.58) in Class 3 compared to Class 1. The risk of asthma exacerbations increased significantly with rising TyG-BMI levels (P for trend < 0.001). Similarly, when TyG-BMI was modeled as quartiles, HRs for exacerbations in T2 and T3 compared to T1 were 2.88 (95% CI, 2.12–3.92) and 10.61 (95% CI, 7.50–15.00), respectively. The incidence of asthma exacerbations showed a statistically significant trend across T2, T3, and T1 (P for trend < 0.001).

Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the robustness of the association between TyG-BMI and asthma exacerbations, we conducted 2 sensitivity analyses. First, we excluded former and current smokers and performed Cox proportional hazards regression. Second, we excluded patients with high IgE levels and performed binary logistic regression. The results consistently showed that TyG-BMI was an independent risk factor for asthma exacerbations after excluding these potential confounders (Supplementary Table S4). Additionally, considering the dimensionality reduction feature of WQS regression and its requirement for directional consistency (i.e., all included variables must have effects in the same direction on the outcome), we only incorporated risk factors positively associated with asthma exacerbations into the WQS model to quantify the overall combined effect of metabolic indicators and demonstrated good model robustness (Supplementary Fig. S4).

DISCUSSION

Our study showed that TyG-BMI is independently associated with the risk of asthma exacerbations. Notably, we identified a significant nonlinear relationship: when TyG-BMI exceeded the threshold of 215, the risk of asthma exacerbations showed a sharp upward trend. Unadjusted Kaplan-Meier survival curves further demonstrated a clear dose-dependent increase in the risk of asthma exacerbations across the three groups classified by K-means clustering as TyG-BMI levels increased. These findings suggest that TyG-BMI is not only a sensitive indicator for assessing the risk of asthma exacerbations but also provides a reliable quantitative basis for early identification of high-risk patients, with important clinical implications for optimizing asthma management.

Insulin resistance, a hallmark of metabolic syndrome, is associated with various metabolic disorders. Recent research has uncovered a close pathophysiological relationship between this metabolic abnormality and chronic inflammatory airway diseases such as asthma. Extensive clinical evidence suggests that asthma pathogenesis is significantly associated with multiple components of metabolic syndrome, including obesity, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance, which may play crucial roles in the development and progression of asthma.913 The TyG-BMI, which integrates FBG, TGs, and BMI, has become a valuable surrogate marker for insulin resistance, showing remarkable predictive value in metabolic-related diseases. Studies have demonstrated a close relationship between TyG-BMI and metabolic risk factors for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, with higher TyG-BMI levels being associated with more pronounced clinical and metabolic abnormalities.14 Furthermore, in early-onset type 2 diabetes, elevated TyG-BMI levels were found to be independently associated with severe insulin resistance, metabolic dysregulation, and overweight/obesity.15 These findings provide new insights into the interaction between asthma and metabolic disorders, suggesting that metabolic abnormalities in asthma patients may increase the risk of exacerbations through systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and airway inflammation. This also highlights the potential of TyG-BMI as a biomarker for predicting asthma exacerbation risk.

Allergic rhinitis, a common comorbidity of asthma, plays a critical role in the pathological process of asthma exacerbations. Interestingly, our results showed a stronger association between TyG-BMI and the risk of asthma exacerbations in patients without allergic rhinitis. This finding may be related to the unique immune characteristics of allergic rhinitis. Elevated IgE levels, a hallmark of allergic rhinitis, may mitigate the low-grade inflammation associated with metabolic diseases by inhibiting the release of Th1-type inflammatory cytokines. Additionally, patients with allergic rhinitis typically exhibit higher levels of adiponectin, which enhances anti-inflammatory effects and insulin sensitivity.16 These findings align with Elkoshi’s research,17 which noted that the inflammatory pattern of allergic rhinitis (dominated by Th2 cytokines, localized in the upper airway) is significantly distinct from and inversely related to the systemic inflammatory pathways associated with metabolic diseases. Based on these immune and metabolic differences, we hypothesize that TyG-BMI may have better predictive value for asthma exacerbations in patients without allergic rhinitis. However, this hypothesis requires further validation in larger asthma cohorts.

WQS regression analysis revealed that TGs are the primary contributing factor within the TyG-BMI to asthma exacerbations, highlighting dyslipidemia as an important therapeutic target in asthma management. As a key indicator of lipid metabolism, elevated TG levels may influence asthma progression through multiple mechanisms. Studies have shown that high TG levels can cause adipose tissue dysfunction, activating macrophages to release inflammatory cytokines and triggering inflammatory signaling pathways such as the JNK pathway and the NOD-like receptor protein 3 inflammasome via endoplasmic reticulum stress.1819 Additionally, elevated TGs can induce oxidative stress through lipid peroxidation, thereby inhibiting insulin signaling.20 Collectively, these mechanisms exacerbate systemic inflammation, potentially leading to asthma exacerbations.

Notably, in asthma patients with low LDL-C levels, the correlation between TyG-BMI and asthma exacerbation risk is more pronounced. This may be because inflammatory responses and lipid metabolism in low LDL-C patients rely more heavily on TG levels, making its driving effect more direct. Conversely, high LDL-C patients often use statins, whose anti-inflammatory effects may weaken the association between TyG-BMI and asthma exacerbations. In contrast, low LDL-C patients are less influenced by lipid-lowering medications, which may more accurately reflect the role of TyG-BMI. These findings are consistent with Zhang et al.’s study,21 where PCSK9-mediated LDL-C levels were associated with lung function decline. Current evidence also suggests that lipid-lowering medications may improve insulin resistance and delay the progression of diabetes and metabolic syndrome.22 These findings highlight the potential value of lipid-lowering therapy in asthma management. However, further studies are needed to refine the criteria selecting target populations and to optimize treatment protocols, especially in the context of metabolically driven asthma phenotypes.

This study systematically classified TyG-BMI using clustering analysis and conducted an in-depth analysis of subgroup populations, providing robust evidence for its use as a clinical marker for predicting asthma exacerbation risk. The biochemical parameters that comprise TyG-BMI are easy to obtain, the calculation method is simple, and insulin measurements are not required, which significantly reduces costs. Furthermore, the index effectively reflects metabolic abnormalities closely associated with chronic low-grade inflammation−a core pathological mechanism of various chronic diseases, including asthma exacerbations. This enables more accurate identification of high-risk asthma populations. The inclusion of BMI in the TyG index has a solid pathophysiological rationale, as obesity is a major risk factor for insulin resistance and plays a crucial role in asthma exacerbations. This mechanism involves adipose tissue-mediated inflammation, elevated free fatty acids, oxidative stress, and disrupted insulin signaling pathways. The superior predictive accuracy of TyG-BMI over the TyG index alone was validated in our ROC analysis.

However, some limitations of this study must be noted. First, as a single-center prospective study, participant recruitment was restricted to one hospital, potentially introducing selection bias. Although the sample size may have limited statistical power, our sensitivity analysis demonstrated the robustness of the results. Further validation in external cohorts is warranted to confirm the applicability of TyG-BMI in predicting asthma exacerbations. Secondly, while TyG-BMI has been shown to be a convenient and reliable marker of insulin resistance, the lack of comparison with the gold standard (hyperinsulinemic euglycemic clamp) limits the ability to directly explain its association with asthma exacerbations. Thirdly, information on prior asthma exacerbation history was not available in our dataset. Given that prior exacerbations are among the strongest predictors of future events, the lack of adjustment for this variable may have led to residual confounding.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the TyG-BMI, a novel marker closely related to metabolic syndrome, is independently associated with the risk of asthma exacerbations. By underscoring the significant contribution of TGs to asthma exacerbations, we provide insights into the potential mechanisms underlying TyG-BMI. Patients with elevated TyG-BMI may represent priority targets for future metabolic interventions, offering new directions for personalized treatment and prevention strategies in asthma management.
