High coverage with delayed doses: Cross-sectional study of parental acceptance and uptake of varicella vaccination in children aged 4–12 years in Chaoyang District, Beijing
Authors: Yang Cao, Xinyu Li, Ziwei Zhao, Zhen Li, Fang Liu, Pu Liu, Jianxin Ma, Qian Shi, Nan Zhang, Bin Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRegional disparities exist in China’s varicella vaccination policies, with Beijing’s voluntary two-dose program requiring self-payment having been implemented for more than a decade. In this study, we investigated parental acceptance and vaccination patterns among 2,189 children aged 4–12 years in Beijing’s Chaoyang District, where policy integration is under consideration. The results revealed high baseline vaccination willingness (81.8%), potentially increasing to 86.3% with inclusion of an immunization program. Key determinants extended beyond demographic factors and disease knowledge to include parental satisfaction with immunization services and utilization of parenting apps. Current coverage rates were 74.5% for the first dose (VarV1) versus 53.2% for the second dose (VarV2), indicating significant second-dose attrition. Primary barriers to vaccination included limited awareness of the option to vaccinate, safety concerns, and perceptions that a single dose offers sufficient protection. Notably, 58.8% of children received VarV1 later than the recommended schedule, partly due to vaccination scheduling. School-based verification requirements emerged as a critical driver of compliance. Our study provides actionable insights for immunization policy optimization. We advocate targeted communication strategies via new media platforms, such as parenting apps, to address knowledge gaps, emphasize two-dose protocols, and boost vaccine confidence. This approach fosters a cycle of tool usage, knowledge acquisition, and timely vaccination, leveraging the demonstrated synergy between app usage and vaccine knowledge in enhancing adherence.

Introduction

Varicella is a highly infectious disease caused by the varicella-zoster virus (VZV). Varicella vaccination (VarV) has been proven to be the most effective public health measure for preventing and controlling the disease. China does not yet have a uniform nationwide VarV strategy. VarV has been available in Beijing since 1997, with gradual popularization of the single-dose regimen. To further reduce incidence and control outbreaks, a two-dose schedule was recommended in 2012, specifying one dose administered at 18 months of age and a booster dose given at 4 years of age. Following implementation of this program in Beijing, varicella incidence decreased significantly from 103.2 per 100,000 in 2012 to 64.2 per 100,000 in 2017.1 An analysis of varicella in Chaoyang District from 2007 to 2019 showed that the average annual incidence rate fell by 39.02%, from 60.24 per 100,000 (pre-two-dose schedule) to 36.80 per 100,000 (post-implementation). After the implementation of the two-dose schedule, the incidence rate dropped significantly among 0–9-year-olds, particularly in the 5–9 age group (the target group for the second dose), demonstrating the strategy’s effectiveness.2 Because VarV remains a voluntary and self-paid vaccination that is not included in the routine immunization schedule, both its vaccination coverage and policy effectiveness require ongoing evaluation. Although Beijing’s two-dose VarV program has been in place for over a decade, existing research predominantly focuses on vaccine efficacy assessment.3–6 Assessments of real-world vaccine coverage in the local district in Beijing are limited, and even fewer have focused on vaccination timeliness.7,8 Moreover, existing literature reveals ongoing debates regarding the optimal timing for initial VarV administration within Beijing’s immunization program.7,9 Understanding the current status of VarV in the region, identifying potential problems, and analyzing possible influencing factors will facilitate policy adjustment and formulation.

The insufficient population-level protection provided by single-dose VarV highlights the necessity for enforcing a two-dose regimen as a mandatory public health measure to control outbreaks in high-transmission-risk school settings.10 However, limited available data have revealed that whereas the coverage rate of the first dose (VarV1) reached 80.0% in Beijing, coverage of the second dose (VarV2) fluctuated between 40.0 and 73.0%, indicating suboptimal compliance with the two-dose vaccination regimen.7 Chaoyang District reports nearly the highest number of varicella cases each year in Beijing, with most cases occurring among students and nursery school-aged children. Chaoyang District is a densely populated area with a high proportion of migrant children and high incidence of aggregated outbreaks in childcare institutions and schools; thus, there is an urgent need to clarify the specific reasons for low VarV2 coverage under the self-funded strategy in this specific group within the population.

Notably, the vaccination rate of VarV1 in Beijing has reached 80.0% despite being a self-funded vaccine – a figure that is considerably higher than that of other self-funded vaccines (e.g., rotavirus vaccine).11 Analyzing the mechanisms underlying VarV decision-making could provide critical insights for improving the uptake of vaccines that are not included in the National Immunisation Programme (NIP), such as vaccines against human papillomavirus and influenza, thereby informing strategies to bridge the gap between self-funded and government-subsidized immunization programs.

International evidence demonstrates that incorporating VarV into the NIP can substantially enhance vaccination coverage and reduce the disease burden. As the pioneer in introducing the varicella vaccine (VarV) into the routine childhood vaccination program, the United States has witnessed an overall reduction in varicella disease incidence by over 97.0% in the 25 years since its introduction.12 Significant improvements have also been observed in those provinces implementing free VarV schedules in China. For example, shortly after the free one-dose vaccination was implemented in 2013 and the free two-dose vaccination was introduced in 2016, varicella incidence decreased by 0.135 cases per 100 000 population (P < .001) and increased by 1.189 cases per 100 000 population (P=0.039) in Qingdao city, respectively.13 In the pilot cities of Jiangsu province with two doses of free VarV, the number of varicella outbreaks declined from 306 in 2017 to 123 in 2021, and the incidence rate also dropped from 2.01/100 to 1.33/100.14 Although Beijing has yet to adopt VarV as a free public health service, formal policy deliberations have commenced.15 However, implementing a free vaccination policy requires careful consideration of parental vaccine acceptance dynamics and the related determinants. According to Beijing’s immunization schedule, children aged ≥4 years should receive two doses of varicella vaccine. We therefore planned to survey parents of pupils aged 4–12 years in Chaoyang District to (a) assess their willingness to complete the two-dose series, (b) identify factors influencing this willingness, and (c) estimate the actual varicella vaccination coverage in this age group. The findings will provide baseline data for refining local varicella vaccination strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample size examination

We enrolled the parents of children aged 4–12 years between March and April 2024. The sample size was calculated using the formula: N = μα2 × p (1 - p)/δ2 × deff, with μα = 1.96, δ = 0.1p and deff = 2.5. Previous reports state that the willingness of parents to vaccinate their children against VarV exceeds 80.0%.15–18 Taking p = 80.0%, N was calculated to be approximately 243 participants. Considering a 20.0% non-response rate, the minimum sample size for the overall study was adjusted to 304 participants. Based on the division of streets/areas in Chaoyang, Beijing, we selected 12 out of the total 43 streets/areas using the Probability Proportional to Size (PPS) sampling method, with explicit consideration of population and economic levels. Subsequently, we randomly selected one kindergarten and one elementary school in each of these 12 streets/areas. Considering that most children in the lower grades of kindergartens are under 4 years old, we conducted surveys among parents of children in the middle and upper grades of kindergartens, as well as grades 1 to 6 of elementary schools. To ensure the statistical stability of subgroup analyses for each grade, we specified that at least 15 valid questionnaires should be completed for each grade in each surveyed institution. Given the grade coverage of the sampling design, the final actual sample size was determined to be 1,440 participants.

Data collection

Data were collected via an online questionnaire administered through “Questionnaire Star” (www.wjx.cn), a dedicated online survey platform. The survey was designed as an anonymous self-completed questionnaire to protect participant privacy.

Before initiating the survey, a comprehensive explanation of the study objectives and procedures was provided to the selected schools to secure approval and cooperation from school administrators. With the assistance of school staff, the survey was promoted through WeChat groups. Prior to completing the survey, parents were informed of its purpose and their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. After obtaining their informed consent, participants accessed the questionnaire by scanning a QR code linked to “Questionnaire Star” and completed it independently.

Study content and methodology

On the basis of a literature review and expert opinion, we designed a questionnaire for use in this study. We conducted a pre-survey with 24 participants to assess the clarity and feasibility of the questionnaire items, and revised any ambiguous questions based on the feedback. Three CDC experts, one public health professor, and one community health worker were invited to conduct a content validity evaluation. The content validity index (CVI) was 1, indicating that the questionnaire has good relevance and appropriateness.

The survey included parents’ and children’s demographic characteristics, parents’ knowledge of varicella and VarV, parents’ access to information about varicella and VarV, parents’ willingness to vaccinate their children against VZV and their children’s VarV status, and parents’ satisfaction with vaccination services. Correct answers to items in the section assessing parents’ knowledge about chickenpox and VarV were scored 1 point, and incorrect answers or responses of “do not know” were scored 0 points, out of a total of 6 points. Evaluation of satisfaction with vaccination services was based on a 10-point scale.

Statistical analysis

We used IBM SPSS 29.0 software for the statistical analysis. For categorical variables, the frequency and percentage (%) are reported. For relevant knowledge scores that conformed to a normal distribution, the mean was used; for skewed distributions, the median was used. Scores greater than the mean or median indicated a high level of knowledge, and scores equal to or below the mean or median indicated low knowledge levels. Satisfaction with vaccination services was considered low with a score of 0–5, medium with a score of 6–7, and high with a score of 8–10. The χ2 test was used for univariate analysis of parents’ willingness to vaccinate their children against VZV. Statistically significant variables were included in the multivariate analysis using a binary logistic regression model at α = 0.05. Additionally, to explore potential effect modifications between key variables, Logistic regression analysis incorporating interaction terms was conducted.

Ethical approval

This study is a component of the parent research project titled Beijing Natural Science Foundation – Changing Innovation Joint Fund Project (L234005). The overall protocol of this project was reviewed and formally approved by the Ethical Review Committee of the Beijing Chaoyang District Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Approval Number: CYCDPCIRB-20240806-1). This anonymous survey strictly adheres to the anonymization requirements specified in the approved parent protocol: (a) All collected data were fully anonymized at the point of collection (e.g., no names, contact details, or identifiers were recorded); (b) Data analysis exclusively used aggregated datasets with no traceability to individual participants; (c) The research scope, methodology, and risk-management measures align with the parent project’s approved framework, with no substantive modifications requiring additional ethical review.

Results

Basic characteristics of survey respondents

A total of 2,375 questionnaires were distributed in this study, and 2,189 valid questionnaires were finally collected (response rate of 92.2%). Among the 2,189 parents of children surveyed, 436 (19.9%) were male and 1,753 (80.1%) were female; 1,383 (63.2%) had local household registration, and 806 (36.8%) had foreign household registration; 1,003 (45.8%) were living in urban areas and 1,186 (54.2%) in rural areas. In this study, 1405 (64.2%), 538 (24.6%), 88 (4.0%), and 24 (1.1%) of respondents reported their occupation as working in government institutions, enterprises, or public institutions; engaging in commercial services or being self-employed; being a domestic worker; or being retired, respectively. Additionally, most parents surveyed had a bachelor’s degree or higher (76.2%).

Parents’ awareness about varicella

A normality test indicated a non-normal distribution of the survey scores (P < .001). The median knowledge level score was 2 points. Knowledge scores ≤ 2 points were categorized as a poor knowledge level. Only 7.2% (158 out of 2,189) of respondents scored 5 points or above. Respondents’ knowledge levels were as follows: 92.2% knew that varicella is an infectious disease; 80.2% knew that varicella is spread by direct contact with vesicular fluid from skin lesions; 42.7% knew that respiratory transmission of VZV is possible; 40.0% knew about the two-dose VarV program; and 31.3% knew that exposure to someone with skin lesions caused by herpes zoster can lead to varicella in someone who has never contracted the disease.

Willingness to vaccinate and influencing factors

A total of 1,791 respondents said they were willing to vaccinate their children via VarV, representing 81.8% of all respondents. This proportion rose to 86.3% under the hypothetical condition that vaccination was offered at no cost.

Univariate analysis revealed significant differences (P < .05) in vaccination willingness among parents according to age, sex, residential area, education level, per capita monthly household income, and history of varicella/herpes zoster illness and vaccination, as well as level of concern, knowledge level, broadcast news as the main information source, and satisfaction with vaccine services (Table 1). Further multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that parents aged >40 years were less willing to vaccinate their children than those aged ≤40 years. Males were also more willing to vaccinate than females; parents in rural areas were more willing than those in towns and cities; and parents with per capita monthly household income >10,000 RMB had greater willingness to vaccinate their child against VZV. Moreover, concerns about varicella transmission, knowledge about varicella and VarV, mainly receiving information via parenting apps, and satisfaction with vaccination services all influenced parents’ vaccination willingness, as detailed in Figure 1. Analysis of VarV willingness and influencing factors.

Logistic regression analysis incorporating interaction terms revealed that the combined effect of parenting apps usage and vaccine knowledge (app*knowledge) significantly increased VarV1 adherence (OR = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.05–2.76, P = .032). Specifically, parents utilizing parenting apps with higher vaccine knowledge levels had 1.70 times higher odds of vaccinating their children on schedule compared to those without app usage or lower knowledge. In contrast, interactions between parenting apps usage and parental education (app*education: OR = 0.95, P = .77) or household income (app*income: OR = 1.04, P = .82) showed no statistically significant effects.

Current vaccination status and reasons for not vaccinating children aged 4–12 years

In total, 79.0% (1,415/1,791) of parents surveyed translated their willingness to vaccinate into actual vaccination behavior. Among the 376 parents who were willing but had not yet vaccinated their children against VZV, the main reasons were not knowing that their children could be vaccinated via VarV (131/376), concerns about adverse effects of the vaccine (60/376), not considering vaccination to be urgent (52/376), and concerns about vaccine quality (51/376).

A total of 217 parents vaccinated their children despite having a reluctant attitude toward VarV. The primary reasons were requirements from childcare institutions and schools (56/217), and fear of VZV infection (41/217).

In this study, the rate of completing the full two-dose VarV schedule among children who had reached 4 years of age ranged from 48.15 to 60.0%. Specifically, the rate of VarV1 was 74.5% (1,631/2,189). Among children of different ages, the highest rate of VarV1 (84.4%) was observed in those aged 4 years (Figure 2). Regarding timing of VarV1 vaccination (Table 2), only 11.3% of children were vaccinated on time – at age 18–19 months – according to the recommended schedule in Beijing; 29.9% were vaccinated before age 18 months; 58.8% were received VarV1 later than the recommended schedule. Among these, 23.6% had a delay of 2–6 months compared to the recommended schedule, and 35.2% had a delay exceeding six months. Among children who received VarV1 on time, the delay rate for VarV2 was 18.6%. In contrast, this proportion was 33.0% among children with a delayed VarV1 dose, a difference that was statistically significant (χ2 = 30.27, P < .001). The relative risk (RR) was 1.78 (95% CI: 1.44–2.20), indicating that children experiencing a delayed first dose had 1.78 times the risk of also having a delayed second dose compared to those vaccinated on time. The main reasons for vaccinating earlier than the recommended schedule were fear of VZV infection after exposure to an individual with suspected chickenpox (194/487), and a doctor’s recommendation (93/487). The main reasons for delay were requirements from childcare institutions and schools (223/959), and arrangement of the vaccination clinics (150/959).The main reasons for not vaccinating with VarV1 were a lack of awareness among parents that their child could be vaccinated, fear of adverse reactions after vaccination, and concerns about vaccine quality (Table 3). The rate of VarV2 was 53.2% (1,165/2,189), with the highest rate (60.0%) in 6-year-old children (Figure 2). Among those who received VarV2, 53.5% were vaccinated on time, at 4 years of age (Table 2). In total, 77.3% (901/1,165) of parents reported completing the VarV2 as prescribed in the vaccination program owing to fear of VZV infection in their child. Additionally, 29.9% and 21.7% of parents vaccinated their children with VarV2 because it was required by a childcare institution or school. The main reasons for not administering VarV2 were that parents were unaware that their child could receive the VarV, parents believed that one dose was sufficient protection against VZV infection, and the child had not reached the appropriate age for vaccination (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, parents of children aged 4–12 years in Chaoyang District, Beijing, demonstrated a high level of acceptance to vaccinate their children against VarV. This finding is similar to the results of studies conducted in Changzhou City and Weifang City.16,17 However, a gap remains the results of our survey and findings in Nanjing City (willingness to vaccinate > 94%).18 In our study, multifactorial analysis revealed that the willingness to vaccinate against VarV was higher among mothers and participants with a high family income, a high level of concern about chickenpox, and a high level of awareness about chickenpox. These findings are consistent with those of other studies.19,20 In our study, parents aged ≤40 years were more willing to have their children vaccinated with VarV than older parents, which may be related to health perceptions.21 Older parents tended to view chickenpox – a self-limiting disease – as less worrisome than younger parents, which influenced the willingness to vaccinate their children. Parents of children living in urban areas were less willing to vaccinate against VZV than those living in rural areas. This may be attributed to the fact that urban parents have abundant sources of information about VarV and are exposed to more negative news about the vaccine, which exacerbates vaccine hesitancy. Furthermore, the more satisfied parents were with vaccination services, the greater their willingness to vaccinate. This indicates that improving the healthcare environment and satisfaction with healthcare services remains an important focus of vaccination services. Among more than half of parents, knowledge about varicella and VarV originated from community or hospital medical workers, suggesting that these professionals may play a positive role in disseminating vaccination knowledge to their clients and families.22 Parents who obtained varicella- and VarV-related information via parenting apps were more willing to receive VarV, suggesting that these parents are more proactive in seeking information and may have a more comprehensive understanding of varicella and VarV.

China lacks a unified VarV strategy, leading to VarV1 and VarV2 coverage rates that differ substantially across the nation, ranging from 20.8 to 97.8%.5,23 In our survey, Chaoyang District had higher VarV1 coverage than the national average.11,23 However, it was lower than that from previous surveys in Beijing.7,8 The VarV2 coverage in Chaoyang District is close to that in Jinan, which matches the national average.24 Yet, the overall two-dose VarV coverage is below the 80.0% recommended by the World Health Organization.25 Because VarV is self-funded and voluntary in Beijing, coverage would increase substantially if it were included in the NIP. We also found that parents were positive about including VarV in the NIP.

The high VarV coverage in Chaoyang District, Beijing is mainly the result of two factors: awareness among the public regarding the contagious nature of varicella and inclusion of VarV in Beijing’s mandatory vaccination certificate checks for nursery and school enrollment. This study showed that the proof-of-VarV requirements of childcare institutions and schools motivate some initially reluctant parents to comply with vaccination. Such institutional requirements have been shown to significantly boost VarV rates.12,26

High VarV coverage still has risks. According to instructions for the VZV vaccine, VarV can be administered at 12 months of age; in Beijing, however, VarV1 is recommended at 18 months. Our survey revealed that children were not being vaccinated against VZV in a timely manner, with serious delays. Most children received the VarV1 at age 20 months or older. Part of the delay is not due to subjective factors but to the scheduling of vaccination clinics. This may be because at age 18 months, children receive numerous vaccines (inactivated hepatitis A vaccine; measles, mumps and rubella vaccine; and diphtheria, tetanus and pertussis vaccine) that are part of the NIP. Thus, VarV has lower priority as it is not part of the NIP.

Despite current vaccination strategies to reduce varicella incidence in Beijing among children aged 0–9 years, the incidence among 1-year-old children is relatively high.2,4 A study comparing VarV and the varicella incidence in Ningbo and Beijing found that 1-year-old children (aged 12–24 months) in Beijing had a much higher incidence of chickenpox than those in Ningbo.8 This might be owing to differences in the timing of VarV1, which is at 12–18 months in Ningbo and 18 months in Beijing; this difference may cause susceptible children aged 12–18 months to miss earlier immunization. The results of our previous studies revealed the risk of chickenpox in children vaccinated at 18–23 months was 1.38 (95%CI, 1.08–1.76) times that in children vaccinated at 12–17 months.3 Delays in vaccination would further increase this risk. Our survey also revealed that some children were vaccinated with VarV1 at 12–17 months, mainly owing to local chickenpox cases, which motivated parents to vaccinate their children out of concern about VZV infection. In 2023, the Expert Group recommended that children without a VarV history receive VarV1 at age 12–18 months and VarV2 at age 3–4 years.27 For the above reasons, it is feasible to advance the VarV program in Beijing, especially with regard to adjusting the timing of VarV1. However, the appropriateness of advancing this vaccination program to 12 months of age requires more support of relevant health economic evidence.

The present survey showed that although most parents know about varicella, they lack sufficient knowledge about the VarV program, with many not vaccinating their children owing to a lack of awareness about this option. Many parents incorrectly believe that their children are too young for vaccination. Unlike VarV1, where concerns over vaccine quality and post-vaccination reactions were predominant issues, the key reason for not vaccinating via VarV2 was the belief that one dose is sufficient to protect against VZV. This finding highlights parents’ lack of knowledge about VarV and the insufficient promotion of information about VarV. Studies indicate that the vaccine effectiveness (VE) of VarV1 alone is 85.0% whereas that of VarV2 can exceed 95.0%, with two doses significantly reducing varicella outbreaks.14,28 Strengthening health education regarding the effects and importance of VarV can boost rates of VarV2.

In this study, online, anonymous self-reporting without identifiers precluded validation against the National Immunization Information System. Although parents were instructed to consult their child’s booklet, recall or reporting bias may have under- or over-estimated VarV coverage in Chaoyang District. Despite the adoption of a multi-stage sampling strategy and promotion through school channels to enhance representativeness, the online survey methodology may still introduce selection bias. Additionally, the lack of real-time clarification for online respondents could further affect data accuracy. Future studies could address these limitations by combining online surveys with offline data collection methods to capture a more diverse sample, while integrating serological data collection to allow for a more comprehensive assessment of immune protection alongside vaccination behavior.

Conclusion

We found that overall, parents of children aged 4–12 years in Chaoyang District, Beijing have high VarV willingness; however, rates of VarV, especially VarV2, need further improvement. The fact that most children receive VarV1 later than the recommended schedule underscores the need to optimize the timing of VarV1. We therefore recommend moving VarV1 from 18 months to 12 months of age. This change would both reduce the injection burden at the 18-month visit and provide timely protection for 12-month-old children. In the future, fully utilizing new media platforms and other communication channels to disseminate information about the two-dose VarV strategy and the importance of timely vaccination, as well as to strengthen vaccine confidence among the general public, can boost VarV willingness and rates among parents.
