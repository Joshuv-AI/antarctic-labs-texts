Digital technology use among age-friendly community initiatives in the United States
Authors: Emily A Greenfield, Natalie E Pope, Rebecca Martin, Uri Amir-Koren, Tawfiq Ammari, Michelle Putnam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesAlthough studies have established the importance of various types of resources for age-friendly community (AFC) initiatives, there has been little research on digital technologies. This study explored the extent of, and contexts for, digital technology use among AFC initiatives in the United States.Research Design and MethodsThis concurrent mixed-methods study used data from AARP’s 2024 Survey of the National Network of Age-Friendly States and Communities (NAFSC) (N = 200) alongside qualitative interviews with 13 communities that reported in the survey relatively high levels or novel types of digital technology use.ResultsOver 90% of survey respondents indicated using at least one technology, the most common being online survey platforms (65%), social media (64%), cloud-based video conferencing (57%), organizational websites (56%), and collaborative file sharing (54%). Initiatives later into the NAFSC program cycle, led by nonprofits or higher education, and with more sources of support, reported using a greater variety of tools. The qualitative analysis identified human capital, alongside the digital infrastructure and financial capital of collaborating organizations, as conditions for technology use and access. Shifting initiative circumstances, as well as perceptions of older adults’ digital capacities and preferences, also influenced technology use.Discussion and ImplicationsOur findings support digital capital as a central component of the development and implementation of AFC initiatives in the context of organizational, network, and community conditions. We discuss how findings can guide future research to enhance the strategic use of technology toward greater AFC impact.Keywords: Program theory, Gerontechnology, Information and communication technology, Artificial intelligence, Organizational development

This study is among the first to examine digital technology use within age-friendly community (AFC) initiatives. We drew on mixed-methods data with members of the AARP Network of Age-Friendly States and Communities (U.S.). Most AFC initiatives reported using at least one digital tool. Leaders used their own capacities and resources, as well as those of their teams and partners, to adopt these tools. Results suggest that digital tools are part of a mix of capitals that AFC initiatives leverage toward social impact. Findings indicate the importance of planning for digital capital as part of the infrastructure for AFC initiatives.

Age-friendly communities (AFC) have emerged as a global movement to make localities better for long and healthy lives. Currently, over 1,700 subnational governments, including over 1,000 municipalities or counties in the U.S., have formally committed to age-friendly progress (AARP, 2025; World Health Organization [WHO], n.d.). This local-level work is typically organized through AFC initiatives, which are deliberate, cross-sectoral efforts to improve aspects of the built, social, and service environments in place-based communities for people as they age (Greenfield et al., 2015).

As the AFC Movement approaches its third decade, a burgeoning body of research is advancing knowledge on AFC initiative development and implementation (Torku et al., 2021)—the strategies and operations that translate AFC aspirations into practices and policies. While studies in this area have identified multiple types of resources necessary for AFC initiatives to launch and sustain (refer to Forsyth & Lyu, 2024; Neville et al., 2025; Torku et al., 2021), very few studies have oriented to digital technologies in AFC practice. This gap is important to address given a growing emphasis on digital technologies within health and aging services (e.g., Fanning et al., 2024) and in the public and nonprofit sectors more generally (e.g., Haug et al., 2024).

This mixed-method study drew on data collected with AFC initiative leaders across the U.S. in 2024–2025 about their digital technology use. Using quantitative data from a national survey of initiatives, we aimed to identify patterns in the reported use of various digital tools and platforms. We also conducted and analyzed qualitative interviews with a select set of survey respondents to explore barriers and facilitators to digital technology use and adoption.

Background

The global age-friendly communities movement, as spurred by the World Health Organization (WHO) and promulgated in the U.S. by AARP, calls for community leaders to engage in a participatory planning approach to improve environments for aging (WHO, 2018). The model involves convening a diverse group of people within a community, including older residents, to conduct community assessments and launch and develop a data-informed action plan for age-friendly progress. Communities are encouraged to implement and evaluate their age-friendly efforts in multi-year cycles (WHO, 2007, 2018). Accumulating research suggests that AFC initiatives take on a broader range of activities beyond planning operations to build the capacity of communities to be more responsive to aging (Greenfield et al., 2022a). Examples include enhancing capacity for multi-sector collaboration, altering mental models and collective motivations for action on aging, embedding age-friendly principles in local-level plans and policies, and influencing more age-inclusive practices and programs among community organizations and public agencies (Black & Oh, 2022; Coyle et al., 2022; Forsyth & Lyu, 2024; Greenfield & Pope, 2025).

Researchers have explored the resources necessary to launch and sustain AFC initiatives (Forsyth & Lyu, 2024), demonstrating that financial resources alone are not wholly sufficient for motivating groups and organizations to pivot aspects of their work on aging (Russell et al., 2022). Other types of capital, such as knowledge, values, and skills of specific individuals (human capital), norms of trust and respect across community groups (social capital), and facility space and equipment (tangible capital) are also critical for robust AFC work (Greenfield et al., 2022b). Moreover, research has suggested that AFC activities often occur through a loose and dynamic network of individuals and groups that work together in both formal and informal ways (Pestine-Stevens & Greenfield, 2022) and that AFC work at the local level is increasingly being spurred and supported by higher systems levels, including regional, county, and state networks (Pope et al., 2025).

Despite these emerging insights, there remains very little research on the use of digital technologies within AFC development and implementation. Scholars have critiqued the WHO’s core AFC model as not meaningfully acknowledging or integrating digital technologies despite digitalization shaping lived experiences worldwide (Marston et al., 2020; Marston & Van Hoof, 2019; Podgórniak-Krzykacz et al., 2020; van Hoof et al., 2021; van Hoof & Marston, 2021). In response, multiple scholars have explored how technology is part of implementation actions to advance specific domains of community age-friendliness. Examples include studies on digital applications to promote safe mobility and transportation (Loos et al., 2020), increasing the digitization of the built environment (Marston et al., 2024; Marston & Van Hoof, 2019), policies to promote technology for supporting community support and health services (Xu et al., 2025), and case illustrations of technology interventions enhancing communication, civic participation, and social participation (Pedell et al., 2021; Reuter et al., 2020). Other scholars have developed conceptual frameworks for digital technology as part of AFCs. For example, Marston and colleagues (2020) blend the WHO model with the “Smart Cities” approach to formulate the “Concept of Age-friendly Smart Ecologies (CASE)” framework, which positions AFCs as multi-level environments wherein the eight WHO domains (i.e., housing, transportation, community support and health services, outdoor spaces and buildings, social participation, respect and social inclusion, civic participation and employment, communication and information) are facilitated across age-friendly virtual spaces, physical environments, and digital tools.

Despite this discourse on how digital technologies can facilitate age-friendly environments, no studies have systematically examined digital technology use in the implementation of AFC initiatives overall. Evidence from prior studies suggests that digital practices are likely relevant. For example, studies have passingly mentioned information and communication technologies (ICTs) as part of AFC initiatives, including newsletters, websites, and social media (Greenfield & Pope, 2025), especially to connect with older adults during the COVID-19 pandemic (Dabelko-Schoeny et al., 2022; Greenfield et al., 2022b). Most notably, Reuter et al. (2020) used a participatory action research approach in England to study how digital technologies advanced the two AFC domains of civic participation and information and communication, specifically exploring how an older adult stakeholder group transitioned their communications strategy from a Word-based PDF newsletter to a digital e-marketing software. Findings indicated a range of capacity needs for switching, launching, and maintaining ICTs in AFC work, including financial resources, digital communication skills, time commitments, and inter-organizational collaboration.

Focus of the current study

Our study aimed to develop nascent knowledge on the use of digital technologies as part of AFC development and implementation. We were broadly guided by a Community Capitals Framework, which highlights the importance of various types of capital, or assets, in interaction with each other to enact progressive community change (Emery & Flora, 2009). Accordingly, we approached digital technologies as a resource that AFC leaders could leverage as part of their operations toward AFC impact. Specifically, for this exploratory investigation, our research questions were: (a) What are the most common types of digital technologies reportedly used as part of AFC initiatives in the U.S.? and (b) What conditions influence patterns of technology adoption across initiatives?

In exploring these questions, we assumed that communities’ use of digital technologies would vary. This assumption stemmed from the understanding that AFC initiatives and their community and organizational contexts differ from each other in light of the high level of autonomy and flexibility that communities have in designing AFC initiatives (Black & Oh, 2022). For example, AFC initiatives in their early stages might use fewer tools than more mature AFC initiatives that implement activities across various age-friendly domains. Moreover, AFC initiatives with greater overall resources might have more access to digital technologies relative to those with fewer resources. We were also sensitized to ways in which technology use might vary by geographic settings given prior findings that AFC initiative operations differ between rural and more urban settings (Russell et al., 2021).

Methods

Research design

We used a convergent mixed-methods study design, which consists of concurrent quantitative and qualitative components that are separate, albeit complementary, in their data collection and analysis (Creswell & Creswell, 2018; Fetters et al., 2013). For the quantitative component, we drew on data from a 2024 U.S. national survey of AFC initiatives to explore the range of digital technologies utilized. We then collected qualitative data in spring 2025, exploring factors that influence technology use from a subsample of participants in the quantitative dataset. The analysis of the quantitative data was roughly concurrent with the collection of the qualitative data. The defining feature of our convergent mixed-methods study was the independent analyses and subsequent synthesis of the quantitative and qualitative components. Study procedures received approval from the Institutional Review Board at Rutgers, The State University of New Jersey.

Quantitative methods

Quantitative sampling and data collection

The quantitative sample was developed through the annual survey of the AARP National Network of Age-Friendly States and Communities (NAFSC). In September through November 2024, AARP invited organizational entities representing 904 communities (D. Hwang, personal correspondence, July 28, 2025) to participate in the survey, including municipalities or counties that represented themselves as well as entities leading regional approaches encompassing multiple municipalities or counties. Communities that had joined the NAFSC within the past six months or more were eligible to participate (for more information about the criteria and processes for enrolling in the NAFSC, refer to AARP, 2025b). The research team received a dataset with 229 unique survey responses: 215 from respondents representing a single initiative at the municipal or county level, and 14 from respondents representing a region of communities (ranging in size from two to 26 municipalities or counties). Refer to Supplementary Section 1 for an overview of additional analyses regarding sociodemographic differences between communities that were represented in the survey versus those that were not. Refer also to Supplementary Section 2 for additional information on the partnership between AARP and the research team that led to the development of the survey items regarding digital technology.

Measures

As displayed in Table 1, we first created binary variables indicating whether the community reported the use of each of 16 digital platforms, spanning 11 different tools such as online survey platforms, websites, and design software, in addition to five social media platforms (e.g., Facebook, Instagram) and paying to boost posts on social media (1 = reported use; 0 = no reported use). We then created a composite variable Total Technology Use, which was a count of how many unique digital tools and social media platforms their initiative reported using. We also created variables to assess potential correlates of AFC technology adoption. These variables included initiative administrative level, rural geographic setting, NAFSC process phase, lead agency type, sources of support, and belonging to a regional entity. Quantitative data analysis

The research team received data from AARP with 229 respondents. To arrive at the analytic sample, we first excluded 25 initiatives that had no responses to any of the items in the digital technology module. Analyses, not displayed, indicated that there were no systematic differences in the above-described correlate variables with these 25 initiatives, with the exception that the initiatives that did not complete the module on digital technology were more likely to report being in their first cycle of implementation or not knowing their implementation stage. Missing responses on items regarding the use of a given digital technology were subsequently coded as “0,” given that the respondent indicated at least one valid, non-missing response to at least one other question on digital technology use. We further excluded four initiatives with no responses regarding sources of support for a final analytic sample of 200 initiatives. We conducted univariate and multivariate analyses to explore the distribution of technology use within the sample and whether organizational and community contexts were associated with patterns of digital technology use.

Qualitative methods

Qualitative sampling and data collection

The unit of analysis for the qualitative sample was the AFC initiative that participated in the 2024 NAFSC survey. We conducted multiple rounds of theoretical sampling to engage a diversity of communities, a strategy to dynamically deepen insights into emerging themes (Patton, 2015). (Refer to Supplementary Section 3 for additional information.) We worked with partners at AARP, who invited lead contacts from 37 communities to participate in interviews, of which 13 completed (35%). Participating initiatives received a $50 gift card in appreciation for their time. While we broadly covered similar themes across interviews, we customized protocols for each initiative based on data from their survey responses and publicly available information (e.g., initiative websites). We uploaded the audio files for transcription (GoTranscript.com) and de-identified the transcripts for confidentiality.

Qualitative data analysis

We used a constructivist epistemic approach, which emphasizes the interactive roles of participants and researchers in co-constructing representations of reality through qualitative research (Braun & Clarke, 2022). Supplementary Table 2 summarizes our strategies for enhancing rigor (Nowell & Albrecht, 2019). Memo-writing and coding were our primary analytic techniques. Regarding memos, we developed post-interview memos to summarize the context of each AFC initiative, the specific digital tools discussed, and reflections on facilitators, challenges, and purposes of technology use and access. We also developed analytic memos throughout the data coding process, which encompassed two rounds of iterative coding consistent with thematic analysis (TA)—the systematic construction of themes reflecting patterns of shared meaning in qualitative data (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Supplementary Table 3 displays the codes developed across two rounds of coding. Round 1 codes emerged from open coding the post-interview memos from the first five interviews. We then transferred this initial coding framework into qualitative analysis software (ATLAS.ti) to continue the open coding with the remaining transcripts. For the second round of coding, we implemented sub-coding to create more conceptually refined codes. We used analytic memoing throughout to organize these more refined subcodes into broader thematic categories, as presented in the results below.

Quantitative findings

As displayed in Table 1, the vast majority (n = 183, 92%) of the 200 initiatives in the analytic sample reported using at least one digital tool. Initiatives in the sample reported using an average of just under five different tools or platforms (range: 0–15) of the total 16 possible digital tools. Illustratively, 65% of initiatives reported using between 2–7 tools, with 16% (n = 32) reporting 0–1 tools, 32% (n = 64) reporting 2–4 tools, 33% (n = 66) reporting 5–7 tools, 16% (n = 32) reporting 8–10 tools, and 5% (n = 9) reporting 11 or more tools.

Figure 1 displays the prevalence of reported use for each digital tool, in addition to any social media use and paid social media (boosting). The five most common tools, used by 50% to 65% of initiatives, were online surveys, any social media, video conferencing, websites, collaborative file sharing, and design software. Facebook was by far the most used social media platform, with 96% of initiatives using any social media using this platform. In contrast, the five least-used digital technologies (all with 25% or less of the sample reporting usage) were e-marketing, chatbots, project management software, paid social media, customer relations software, group text threads, and social media platforms other than Facebook (Instagram, YouTube, X/Twitter, LinkedIn).

Qualitative findings

Analysis of the qualitative interview data indicated four categories of influence on AFC initiative leaders’ access and use of digital technologies. Expanded illustrations, quotes, and details for select sub-themes under each theme are provided in Supplementary Table 5, as denoted by superscripts in the text below.

Human capital of the core team, key partners, and other influencers

One of the most prominent themes regarding facilitators of technology was human capital—having people with both the requisite skills and availability to leverage digital technologies to accomplish tasks and advance operations. Many of the AFC leaders described leveraging their own digital capacities—in tandem with their allocated time on the initiative (in many cases as staff)—to use technology as part of their practice. For example, when asked what allowed her to advance the initiative’s digital communications (e.g., through website development and creating an e-newsletter), one participant (Community [C]7) reflected, “It’s a matter of just using the technical skills that you have, or finding somebody else who can do it for you or with you. I happen to have those skills already.” At the same time, reflecting capacity in terms of available time, this participant recognized her inability to incorporate social media platforms as part of the initiative’s digital marketing, stating, “There’s only so much communications work a single person can take on.”

Several participants reflected on how they had a career history and underlying enthusiasm to leverage digital tools for key operations, exuding an overall confidence in their ability to figure out how to leverage digital technologies. For example, a participant (P1) from one of the initiatives described with enthusiasm his use of a small recording device that can generate AI-based notes from meetings. When discussing his adoption of this device, the other participant in the interview (P2)—a community volunteer—commented, “We…lovingly…call P1 a geek. P1’s our geek, thank goodness.” (C4) At the same time, P2 described her own technology capabilities as sufficient to use requisite tools for key operations, such as accessing Zoom-based meetings.

Throughout the group interviews, it was common for participants to note differing degrees of technology capacity among core team members, partners, consultants, interns, and volunteers—areas where their colleagues’ skills or ease of using certain tools compensated for limitations of their own. They also commonly described working as a team and through networks to optimize their use of digital technologies. For example, a participant in one community (C3) reflected on the value of having a student intern help with digital-related tasks, such as regularly updating the website, sharing that these activities would require her to pause from her other priorities and would take her more time compared to the student.1 (Refer to Supplementary Table 5 for additional details on the points as denoted through superscripts.) Other participants, especially in the public sector, explained how the support they received from the administration’s communications team helped them manage their communications, such as e-marketing and social media.

Many participants described the importance of training to learn how to leverage specific tools. Such training was provided by a variety of sources, including other AFC core team members (or past leaders who had left training documentation), colleagues within the auspice organization, university staff and interns, paid consultants, as well as online resources (e.g., YouTube videos). One participant, for example, described the value of having her departmental supervisor provide training on using Excel more deeply for program monitoring, and another participant recalled completing an online micro-credentialing program on accessible web design. In other instances, the core team leaders served as regular providers of technology training to others, including colleagues on their core teams, key partners, volunteers, and age-friendly leaders in other communities.2 In other cases, participants stated that they did not need training on a particular tool, but rather, found it intuitive to use or that they were able to learn how to use it through experience alone.3

Other organizations’ digital infrastructure and financial capital

A recurring theme was the AFC initiatives’ use of digital technologies maintained by their broader auspice organizations, encompassing private nonprofit organizations, municipal departments and administrations, as well as institutions of higher education. Examples of technologies accessed through auspice organizations included collaborative file-sharing systems, website hosting and content, email inboxes, online survey platforms, and other software applications (e.g., Microsoft 365, Canva, Zoom). Some initiatives reported they were able to access more innovative or novel technologies because of their auspice organization, such as one initiative that was able to access a Customer Relationship Management (CRM) license to help keep track of registrations and attendance at relevant events and programs after learning about another division in their department that was already using the technology.4

Initiatives within the public sector reported varying degrees of departmental control over software used for key operations. For example, one community explained that their own department “makes our own decisions on the programs and the things that we use, even our website” (C13), whereas other participants stated that they had more limited control.5 Conversely, communities embedded within the public sector also shared how their technology use was constrained by the policies of the broader administration. For example, in some cases, age-friendly commissions within municipal government could not use digital tools such as group email and collaborative file-sharing (e.g., Google Docs) for internal communications because of public meeting laws concerning quorum and transparency in conducting public business.

Another way in which other organizations supported digital technology use was by providing grant funding in support of the AFC initiatives’ digital operations. These funds were allocated for staff time to initiate and maintain the use of digital tools, to hire consultants to create digital infrastructure, and to purchase and maintain digital subscriptions and equipment. For example, one leader of an AFC initiative in the civic sector described the necessity of continued grant funding to sustain basic digital services, including the initiative’s website and e-marketing account.6

Other organizations also supported digital technology access and use by providing digital resources in-kind to local AFC initiatives, such as national organizations (e.g., AARP’s online community assessment platform), state resources (e.g., a CRM system provided through a statewide partnership between a university and local AFC initiatives), and regional networks (e.g., a county AFC listserv). One initiative, operating in the nonprofit sector, described working with a software company to develop a better CRM solution for their work, initially using grant funding to access the software and then requesting additional features from the developers to improve its functionality for them. This platform was then made available in-kind to other AFC initiatives throughout the state as part of a pilot program led by higher education and a statewide nonprofit.7 Another initiative in a rural county further described their strategy to embed information about their age-friendly assessment in the e-newsletters of local partners, while recognizing that the reach might be constrained because of the limited number of such digital newsletters in their region.8

Evolving needs and shifting conditions

Temporal conditions constituted another thematic category of influences on AFC leaders’ access and use of digital technologies. Evolving needs and shifting conditions—both internal and external to the AFC initiatives—influenced the use of digital technologies, as described below.

As the above sections indicate, in many cases, access to and use of digital resources was contingent on the availability of human capital brought into the AFC initiative (e.g., core team leader, volunteer, intern, partner), or on time-limited grants or in-kind donations. Participants noted that the availability of such resources was not indefinite. For example, one participant laughingly shared her hope that two key individuals on the AFC core team, who oversee most of the digital operations, “stay with us forever” (C10). Another community expressed grave concern over whether their initiative would be able to sustain, considering the forthcoming retirement of the auspice organization’s long-time director, on whom they relied for many digitally-based operations,9 and another participant expressed similar concerns for when she herself retires.10 Other initiatives with access to innovative technologies through pilot programs or grants expressed uncertainty regarding what would happen after the pilots ended, questioning whether there would be renewed or alternative funding. On the flip side, some initiatives anticipated increased access to new technologies because of growth within their auspice organizations or through their broader partnership networks and collaborators.11

Participants also described changing their use of digital technologies in response to their initiatives’ evolving needs and priorities over time. This included adding digital technologies as new components to programmatic work, switching technologies considering their own negative experiences with a tool and the availability of a better option, and deepening their use of a given platform (e.g., using it for more purposes, optimizing its configurations). For example, one of the communities shared multiple ways they switched to or added digital technologies as their initiative’s emphasis on information and communications expanded. When their initiative was launched, their web presence was limited to a single page with a contact email address on a partner organization’s website. As they expanded, they realized they needed a standalone website, which they first created using a basic platform that over time grew to include additional features (e.g., an e-newsletter). They ultimately transitioned to different software that offered even more functionality.

Some participants also described how their use of digital technologies changed in response to larger societal shifts. One prominent example was the COVID-19 pandemic. Prior to the pandemic, many AFC initiative leaders were holding committee meetings exclusively in person. The social-distancing practices associated with the pandemic required initiatives to shift meetings to Zoom. Another community shared how COVID-19 brought to their awareness the need to better support many older community members with digital access out of concern for equity and public health. As a result, they obtained a license to operate as a site for a national digital platform that provides individuals with access to online classes and enrichment programs. Participants also mentioned other social trends following the pandemic, such as changes in social media platforms in response to evolving platform features, policies, and focal audiences.12

Perceptions of older adults’ digital capacities and preferences

A final theme indicated how participants’ perceptions of older adults’ use of technologies—specifically their access to ICTs such as email, websites, and social media—influenced their own use of these tools as part of AFC operations. In short, some participants described not using these technologies, in part or at all, because they perceived them as not being useful for communicating with older adults in their communities. For example, one participant said, “Ever since I started working here, I’ve had so many older adults say, ‘Everything is going online, and I am not getting the information I need. I’m not going to go online, and I’m not going to try to find it.’” (C9) Another participant noted that many older adults in their communities were not regularly connecting online because of financial considerations.13

In response to digital capacity and access issues among older adults, many leaders discussed employing older technologies (e.g., telephones, radio) and printed materials alongside ICTs. One participant stated: “We’re trying to use all options that are available to us. There’s print, and there are flyers, there’s certainly the digital communications, but I do think that to really be inclusive and to engage the diversity and the different skill levels, it takes all of those.” Further illustrating this point, two rural-based AFC initiatives described using a plurality of older technologies and analog modalities as a strategic choice to reach both public officials and older residents in rural communities, including in-person meetings, printed material (flyers/surveys) in the post office, newspaper and radio content, and (aspirationally) television-based promotion.14 Another strategy was to include digital literacy and accessibility programs with community residents as part of their AFC action plans, such as skill-building workshops, one-on-one digital tutoring, and digital device lending programs.15

Discussion

This U.S.-based study is the first comprehensive exploration of the use of digital technology among AFC initiatives. While prior studies have identified the importance of various types of resources for AFC development and implementation (Forsyth & Lyu, 2024; Russell et al., 2022), our study is novel in positioning and calling attention to digital technologies specifically, alongside the necessary conditions to access and use them. Synthesizing across the quantitative and qualitative results, our findings indicate that AFC initiatives leverage a range of digital technologies in conjunction with social, financial, and human capital in the context of their organizations, partnership networks, and broader communities. We interpret results in two parts: first, by applying the concept of digital capital, and second, by attending to considerations of diversity and equity concerning social contexts for procuring and leveraging digital technologies as part of AFC operations and impact.

A digital capital perspective

While our study was broadly oriented to digital technology as a type of resource for AFC initiative development and implementation, findings on how digital technology was accessed and leveraged in the context of other types of capital (human, social, and financial) suggest the relevance of the concept of digital capital more specifically. Building from classic social scientific theorizing, Ragnedda (2018) posits digital capital as a distinct type of capital, defining it as “the accumulation of digital competencies (information, communication, safety, content-creation and problem-solving), and digital technology” (p. 2367). In this sense, digital technologies are one of two components of digital capital, with the other being digital competencies (knowledge, understanding, and skills in the context of human, social, and financial capital). Synergies between digital technologies and digital capacities result in digital capital–resources employed for social gain, which, in the case of AFC initiatives, can be theorized as age-friendly systems change (see Greenfield & Pope, 2025, for further discussion).

Although the current study was not designed to examine the strategic purposes of using digital technologies, the findings suggest how they might serve those purposes. Of note, the most used digital technologies (reported by at least half of the quantitative sample) were those that helped to communicate information, share resources, and facilitate interpersonal interaction: online survey platforms, social media, websites, and design software, as well as cloud-based meeting software and collaborative file-sharing. Moreover, the qualitative findings prominently highlighted the use of digital tools across AFC core team members, auspice organizations, and broader partnership networks, a relational and shared application of technology that requires human, social, and financial capitals. As theorized by Menec (2017), social connectivity is a central process involved in AFC initiatives, achieving goals that include building connections and empowerment, diffusing social influence, and spreading access to resources across the individual, organizational, and community levels. Consistent with this framework, our findings suggest that digital technology—in conjunction with digital competence—can serve as an important form of capital for such social connectivity processes to occur.

A digital capital perspective further emphasizes that digital technology use among AFC initiatives is not simply a matter of accessing the tools but also having the human capital to use them optimally (Ragnedda, 2018). This insight suggests the value of incorporating digital technology skills—especially with respect to commonly used tools such as social media and design software—as part of requirements for AFC staff and ensuring that AFC implementation teams include people with relevant digital competencies. It also suggests the importance of technology training for AFC leaders, especially in light of ways in which AI capabilities are transforming tool availability and use (Broderick & Karla, 2025). Results further encourage AFC initiatives to budget for and plan technology consultants and partnerships in ways that enhance their internal capacities to use digital technologies, ranging from formal training to “learning by doing” on projects that expand existing technological capacities.

Digital technology use in organizational, network, and community contexts

Findings are further congruent with social scientific theorizing on how digital capitals are embedded within broader social systems and inequities (Ragnedda, 2018). Specifically, our findings suggest that AFC initiatives vary in their use of digital technology across organizational, network, and community contexts. The quantitative findings identified several organizational-level factors that condition digital technology use. First, regarding sectoral affiliation, we found that AFC initiatives in private non-profits and higher education reported using a greater variety of tools than initiatives led by public administrations. This finding echoed insights from the qualitative interviews with initiatives based in the public sector that described navigating varying degrees of both departmental and administrative parameters in their technology access and use. In addition to highlighting the overall influence of an auspice or backbone organization conditioning AFC implementation (Brossoie et al., 2021; Wolff, 2016), this finding may reflect the greater administrative flexibility in the private sector relative to bureaucratic constraints of the public sector (Seo et al., 2025).

The number of sources of support (both financial and in-kind support) was also a robust correlate of digital technology use. Complementing this quantitative finding, the qualitative interviews highlighted the importance of in-kind support from organizational partners and auspice organizations, as well as grant funding, as key sources that facilitate and enable technology use. These findings on types of support are consistent with the idea that digital capital is both contingent upon and interactive with other forms of financial, social, and human capital, especially within organizational contexts (Korovkin et al., 2023; Ragnedda, 2018).

The results further identified the implementation phase as an additional organizational predictor of total technology use, consistent with the fact that AFC initiative implementation and impact typically take place over many years (Greenfield et al., 2015; Russell et al., 2022). Illustratively, the quantitative findings demonstrated that initiatives in later stages of implementation reported using a greater variety of tools. The cross-sectional design of this study’s quantitative component precludes the ability to examine causal dynamics underlying this and other trends. This pattern could reflect a maturational effect, in that more mature initiatives might adopt additional tools as their efforts expand. On the contrary, this finding could demonstrate selection effects, as initiatives with greater initial capacity to adopt technologies might be more likely to sustain their long-term operations and to continue using these technologies as their initiatives mature. Regardless, the qualitative findings indicate that digital technology use fluctuates over time, supporting the view that digital adoption is not a “one and done” status but rather a multi-faceted and evolving feature of ongoing operations (Saghafian et al., 2021). It also indicates the need for prospective studies to observe digital technology use over time, with insights into how the depth and breadth of technology could serve as indicators for the depth and breadth of initiatives’ broader implementation and potential impact in some contexts.

Our findings also corroborate the understanding that AFC initiatives are variable in their development and implementation across community contexts. In particular, quantitative findings indicated that AFC initiatives in rural community contexts might use slightly fewer digital technologies (although such differences were not as robust in supplementary analyses that included indicators of overall population size, as well as community age, socioeconomic, and racial/ethnic composition). This finding could reflect the above-discussed idea that technology use might both reflect and be conditioned by the level of resources available to an AFC initiative–both in an organizational context, but also in terms of its broader community context, to the degree that rural communities might have relatively fewer resources in terms of financial and human capital, as well as social service and digital infrastructures (Lee et al., 2021; Russell et al., 2021).

At the same time, this pattern might not be a simple illustration of place-based digital inequities. Instead, our qualitative findings suggest that lower levels of technology use in rural communities might be a more nuanced process of local-level responsiveness and organizational adaptation to community dynamics. Specifically, our qualitative findings suggest that AFC leaders might modulate their digital technology use based on localized knowledge of how community members, especially older adults, typically receive information and connect with one another and their community. This interpretation is illustrated in the case of the rural-based AFC initiative that strategically used older technology and analog forms of engagement to connect with constituents. In other words, the “lower” use of technology might, in part, reflect a strategic responsiveness to local dynamics rather than digital resource constraints. This finding aligns with literature to date that has similarly characterized variability in AFC implementation in terms of responsiveness to local needs, assets, and priorities (Black & Oh, 2022).

Building on these insights, our findings encourage greater investigation into the complex relationship between digital capital and digital inequities in AFC implementation. Briefly, the digital divide is understood as occurring across three levels, with the first relating to access (and acceptance of) digital technologies, the second reflecting differences in the skills, abilities, and competencies necessary to use digital tools, and the third encompassing differential effects, outcomes, or benefits derived from technology use occurring across social groups (e.g., by cohort, primary language, socioeconomic status, etc.) and place-based/geographic contexts (Bentley et al., 2024; Hoyos Muñoz & Cardona Valencia, 2025; Korovkin et al., 2023). Future research could explore how differences in technology use might reflect complex interactions between access and acceptance (level one) versus skills and competencies (level two), including the possibility that lower technology use might, in fact, reflect strategic and nimble choices and uses of digital technology in certain organizational and community implementation contexts.

A second and related line of inquiry could explore the ways that digital capital (and lack thereof) may compound, amplify, and interact with other forms of capital toward AFC impact and outcomes. This area of research addresses the second and third levels of digital inequities, delving into the ways in which digital capital might interact with other organizational and community capitals to affect human capital for using digital technologies (level two) and the initiative’s overall impact (level three). Such analysis could draw on a core insight of the Community Capitals Framework: strengthening one type of capital can lead to compounding and self-reinforcing cycles of cumulative increases across multiple types of capital (Emery & Flora, 2009). This analysis also follows from Ragnedda’s (2018) theorizing on how digital capital might interact with other capitals, such that high levels of digital competencies might lead to increases in other forms of capitals. Research into this topic could offer important insights into the ways digital capital might serve to redress inequalities in AFC implementation (i.e., how technology use might be leveraged to overcome or compensate for other capital deficits) versus technology as a reflection (and potentially exacerbator) of overall differentials in organizational and place-based inequalities, both between and within communities (Ragnedda, 2018).

Limitations and Conclusion

There are several key limitations of this mixed-methods study, as described in Supplementary Table 5. In summary, these issues concern potential selection bias, limitations concerning the theoretical sampling and saturation of the qualitative analysis, limited depth of the quantitative survey items, and the cross-sectional design. Nevertheless, this study, as one of the first focused on digital technologies in AFC initiatives overall, can serve as a foundation for continued research on digital capital to optimize AFC operations and impact. It also supports the importance of continued gerontological research into technology adoption as part of broader systems-change work on aging (Fulmer et al., 2025). In conclusion, translational research on how to better deploy digital capital alongside other forms of capital has the potential to bolster the viability, impact, and reach of AFC initiatives and related social innovations, especially in the context of continuously accelerating socio-digital transformation and diverse implementation contexts.
