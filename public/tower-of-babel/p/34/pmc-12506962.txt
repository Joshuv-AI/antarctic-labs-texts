Interplay between noble gases and MOFs: Insights from 129Xe and 83Kr NMR spectroscopy
Authors: Wanli Zhang, Vinicius Martins, Jeffrey Collins, Reza Moshrefi, Samantha Michelle Gateman, Victor V. Terskikh, Yining Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Efficient separation and storage of radioactive noble gas isotopes [krypton-85 (85Kr) and xenon-131m/133/135 (131m/133/135Xe)] from off-gas during spent nuclear fuel reprocessing are vital for environmental protection and noble gas recovery. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are promising adsorbents, yet atomic-level insights into Xe and Kr adsorption remain limited. Here, we report the first application of high-field solid-state 83Kr nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), alongside extensive 129Xe NMR, to investigate gas adsorption in eight MOFs representing three design strategies: ultramicroporous one-dimensional–channel MOFs with pore sizes matching noble gas diameters, functionalized MOFs, and MOFs with open metal sites. Single-gas and coadsorption studies reveal distinct adsorption sites, guest-host interactions, and gas dynamics in each MOF. Most MOFs retain crystallinity after exposure to 60-kilogray γ radiation. Several radiation-sensitive MOFs exhibit enhanced stability in the presence of Xe, suggesting that Xe adsorption enhances framework stability and may broaden the range of MOFs usable under γ radiation in nuclear off-gas separation. These findings offer valuable molecular-level design insights.

INTRODUCTION

The increasing global energy demand necessitates the expansion of alternative, low-emission energy sources, as fossil fuels are both economically and environmentally unsustainable. With its exceptionally high energy density, nuclear fission offers a viable alternative that produces substantially lower carbon emissions than fossil fuels (1). Currently, nuclear power contributes ~11% of global electricity (2–4). Future advancements in civilian nuclear energy depend on the efficient and safe management of used nuclear fuel (UNF). Reprocessing UNF improves efficiency and supports waste management but generates off-gas containing volatile radionuclides, including radioactive noble gases [e.g., xenon-131m/133/135 (137,131m,135Xe) and krypton-85 (85Kr)]. Without proper management, these gases pose serious environmental risks. While radioactive Xe isotopes decay quickly (e.g., 133Xe with half-life of 5.3 days), 85Kr has a long half-life (10.8 years), necessitating Xe/Kr separation (5, 6). In addition, Xe has industrial applications in lighting, lasers, anesthetics, and medical imaging (5). Given that Xe concentrations in nuclear plant off-gas [400 parts per million (ppm)] are notably higher than in air (0.087 ppm), nuclear reprocessing presents a valuable opportunity to recover Xe for industrial use (5, 6).

Cryogenic distillation is the most common technology to separate Xe from Kr, taking advantage of their different boiling points (165.03 K for Xe and 119.93 K for Kr) (7). However, the extremely low temperatures, which are required, make this method costly and energy intensive. Compared to cryogenic distillation, adsorption-based separation using porous solid materials offers a low-cost and energy-efficient alternative (5, 6, 8, 9). Materials with a high affinity for Xe or Kr can be used to selectively adsorb specific gas from mixtures. Therefore, developing materials with high Xe and Kr adsorption capacity and good Xe-over-Kr separation selectivity is crucial.

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), which are a unique type of crystalline porous material composed of metal ions or metal-inorganic clusters linked by organic bridging ligands (10, 11), are promising materials for the removal and storage of these radioactive species (5, 6, 8, 9, 12). They offer better selectivity and lower energy use compared to cryogenic distillation. However, separating Xe and Kr is challenging because they are both inert atomic gases, have very similar kinetic diameters (Xe: 4.047 Å; Kr: 3.655 Å), and exhibit similar polarizabilities (Xe: 4.04 × 10−25 cm3; Kr: 2.48 × 10−25 cm3) without dipole or electric quadrupole moments (5, 6). MOFs often favor Xe due to stronger van der Waals interactions, but adequate Kr adsorption is also critical for technologies such as the “two-bed breakthrough setup” (13, 14). In this approach, the first bed selectively adsorbs Xe, allowing other gases to pass through, while the second bed specifically captures Kr, releasing carrier gas free of both Xe and Kr. Therefore, understanding the behaviors of both Xe and Kr within MOFs is equally important to developing MOF-based materials for Xe/Kr separation and storage. While research has extensively focused on adsorption isotherms and breakthrough experiments (5, 8, 9, 12), atomic-level insights remain limited.

Solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is a powerful tool for characterizing MOF-based materials (15–20), particularly for probing the behavior of small gas molecules adsorbed within MOF frameworks (19, 21–23). It provides insights into gas location, binding strength, and dynamics. In this work, we use NMR of nonradioactive 129Xe and 83Kr as surrogates for their radioactive counterparts in spent fuel processing to uncover atomic-level details of Xe and Kr behavior in MOFs. 129Xe (I = 1/2) has a relatively high gyromagnetic ratio (γ = −7.45210 × 107 rad T−1 s−1) and a natural abundance of 26.4% (24), making it well suited for NMR spectroscopy. 129Xe NMR has been used as a probe molecule to investigate porosity of materials such as zeolites and related materials since the 1980s (25, 26). Most existing 129Xe NMR studies of MOFs (e.g., MIL-53) have also focused on porosity determination (27–34). Our focus, however, is on Xe as the actual adsorbate. In contrast, 83Kr NMR is highly challenging due to its quadrupolar nature and poor receptivity. 83Kr, as the only NMR-active isotope of Kr, has a nuclear spin number of I = 9/2 and a moderate nuclear quadrupole moment (Q = 0.26 barn) (35). With a very small gyromagnetic ratio (γ = −1.03310 × 107 rad T−1 s−1) and a low natural abundance of 11.5%, 83Kr has an NMR sensitivity that is only 3.9% of that of 129Xe (24). Because of the intrinsically low sensitivity, 83Kr NMR on porous materials has been rarely explored and is mainly limited to zeolites (e.g., NaY and CaA), where relatively narrow 83Kr signals were used to assess the porosity (36, 37). Because of the experimental challenges, the application of 83Kr NMR in MOFs remains an untapped field. One approach that can be used to overcome the challenges with 83Kr NMR is to perform experiments at very high magnetic fields. As magnetic field strength increases, the population difference between the spin states also increases, while the second-order quadrupolar interaction (QI) is inversely proportional to the magnetic field strength. For instance, 83Kr NMR at 21.1 T was used to study the Kr captured in para-tertiary-butyl-calix[4]arene and β-quinol clathrate hydrate materials, which is the only reported example of QI-dominated 83Kr NMR spectra (38).

In this research, the behavior of Xe and Kr adsorbed inside MOFs is systematically studied using 129Xe and 83Kr NMR spectroscopy. Six representative MOFs [α-Mg3(HCOO)6, SIFSIX-3-M (M = Zn and Cu), FMOF-Zn, UTSA-74, and ZnAtzOx] were selected on the basis of their Xe and Kr adsorption capacities and structural diversities (14, 39–42). All these MOFs are promising candidates for Xe and Kr separation and adsorption based on previous isotherm studies (see table S1 for Xe and Kr adsorption capacities). These MOFs can be categorized into three classes (Fig. 1) (5, 12).

The structures of MOFs investigated in this study, where M represents metal ions in MOFs α-M3(HCOO)6 and SIFSIX-3-M.

The MOFs are classified into three types as illustrated at bottom (green sphere: metal centers; red column: organic linkers). See text for details.

1) Ultramicroporous MOFs (type I): MOF pores are slightly larger than or close to the size of Xe, thereby enhancing van der Waals interactions with the framework and improving Xe binding affinity. For instance, both MOFs α-Mg3(HCOO)6 and SIFSIX-3-Zn feature a pore size of 4 to 5 Å with a high Xe capacity of ~2 mmol/g (14, 39).

2) MOFs with functionalized linkers (type II): Functional groups (e.g., -F, -OH, -NH2, etc.) of the linkers are potential binding sites of Xe and Kr. For example, there are -F groups in the SiF62− pillar in SIFSIX-3-Zn/Cu (14) and -NH2 groups in the organic linker 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole in ZnAtzOx (42), providing potential gas adsorption sites.

3) MOFs with open metal sites (OMSs) (type III): Coordinatively unsaturated metal sites in MOFs can enhance the intermolecular affinity, providing additional electrostatic interaction between metal ions and Xe and Kr atoms. For example, Xe-over-Kr separation selectivity up to 8.4 (273 K and 1 bar) was observed in UTSA-74 MOF, which features dual Zn2+ OMSs (41).

We used 129Xe and 83Kr NMR to directly probe the adsorption sites, host-guest interactions, and binding strength during Xe and Kr adsorption in MOFs. Single-component Xe and Kr adsorption was investigated first for each MOF, followed by investigations into their coadsorption. Our results show that the preferred Xe adsorption in MOFs is due to the strong interaction between the Xe and both the organic linkers and their functional groups (e.g., -H of the HCOO− linker, -F of the SiF62− pillar, -NH2 in the 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole linker, and aromatic H in the 2,5-dihydroxyterephthalic acid linker), as well as OMSs (Zn2+ in UTSA-74). The interaction between Kr and MOF framework is directly characterized with 83Kr NMR at 21.1 T. The behavior of Kr in MOFs in the presence of Xe is also elucidated. Because CO2 is also present in the off-gas mixture and needs to be removed by the first bed along with Xe in a two-bed adsorption apparatus, coadsorption of CO2 and Xe was studied via 13C wideline NMR of 13CO2 and 129Xe NMR, as CO2 is known to adversely affect Xe/Kr separation. Furthermore, the effect of γ radiation on MOF stability and Xe and Kr adsorption is examined using an in-house 60Co source to simulate operational conditions. The preliminary results of this study were reported in Zhang’s thesis (43).

RESULTS

We first discuss two microporous MOFs: α-Mg3(HCOO)6, with zigzag channels, and SIFSIX-3-Zn, with straight channels, both featuring pore sizes matching Xe diameters. Next, we examine FMOF-Zn, which contains two types of pores, focusing on dynamic exchange behavior during coadsorption. Last, we investigate Xe and Kr adsorption in MOF ZnAtzOx, which features amino functional groups, and MOF UTSA-74, which has OMSs. In all cases, 129Xe and 83Kr solid-state NMR are used to probe the behaviors of Xe and Kr adsorbed in MOFs with single gases and gas mixtures. When applicable, grand canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) simulations and plane-wave density functional theory (DFT) calculations assist in interpreting experimental results (44, 45). The maximum Xe or Kr capacity per unit cell of each MOF used for discussion is summarized in table S1.

Xe and Kr adsorption within α-Mg3(HCOO)6

One of the first microporous MOFs tested for Xe separation from other noble gas mixture was the α-M3(HCOO)6 family (M = Mg, Zn, Co, and Ni) (39), which features one-dimensional (1D) zigzag-shaped channels (~4.8 Å in diameter) independent of the metal centers (Fig. 2A). Breakthrough experiments at 298 K show that α-Co3(HCOO)6 exhibits a Xe/Kr selectivity of ~6.3 (39).

129Xe NMR of Xe@α-Mg3(HCOO)6

Static 129Xe NMR experiments were performed on α-Mg3(HCOO)6 loaded with 2 Xe per unit cell [~half of the maximum Xe capacity (39)]. The 129Xe NMR spectrum shows a typical chemical shift anisotropy (CSA) powder pattern often found for 129Xe NMR in other porous materials (46–48), best simulated with a single Xe site yielding δiso = 134.1 ppm, Ω = 34.0 ppm, and κ = −0.90 (Fig. 2B). The anisotropy of the chemical shift tensor arises mainly from the interaction between the Xe and the channel wall (49). The relatively small span (34.0 ppm) suggests a small but noticeable distortion of the Xe electron density from spherical symmetry (49), while the skew (κ = −0.90) indicates a nearly axially symmetric CSA tensor, consistent with Xe adsorption in 1D channels. The small but finite span confirms the absence of distinct isotropic motion of Xe in this microporous MOF.

To investigate the effect of temperature, we performed variable-temperature 129Xe NMR experiments (fig. S1). As the temperature increased from −80° to 80°C, only a minor increase in the isotropic chemical shift (~7 ppm) to higher chemical shift direction is observed. For Xe in zeolites and MOFs, the chemical shift of 129Xe NMR is empirically expressed as (26, 50)δ=δ0+δS+ρXeδXe‐Xe+δE+δM(1)

where δ0 is the chemical shift of gaseous Xe at zero pressure, δs is attributed to collisions between Xe atoms and the surfaces of the channels and cavities, and δXe-Xe·ρXe arises from Xe-Xe collisions, which are expected to vary linearly with Xe density. δE accounts for the electric field effect caused by exchanged cations, and δM represents a term accounting for the presence of paramagnetic species. Considering that the α-Mg3(HCOO)6 framework is rather rigid (51, 52), the slight increase in chemical shift with increasing temperature is likely due to increased thermal motion leading to a slightly larger contribution of the δS term (53).

We further examined the 129Xe NMR spectra of Xe@α-Mg3(HCOO)6 at two different loadings. At a higher loading of 4 Xe per unit cell, an extremely weak NMR signal from free Xe gas (“+” in Fig. 2B) is observed, indicating that most Xe is adsorbed within the framework, with only a negligible amount remaining unadsorbed. However, the position of 129Xe NMR spectrum of adsorbed Xe at a loading of 4 Xe per unit cell remains nearly unchanged. This contrasts with the pressure-dependent chemical shift observed in zeolites and some MOFs with larger cages, where the δXe−Xe·ρXe term in Eq. 1, arising from Xe-Xe collisions, typically causes a linear dependence of the Xe chemical shift with pressure (54). The 129Xe NMR results indicate that each Xe atom is strongly adsorbed at a specific site, and increasing Xe loading does not lead to a random distribution of Xe within the channel of α-Mg3(HCOO)6 (fig. S2B). Furthermore, the 129Xe magic angle spinning (MAS) NMR spectrum (Fig. 2B) shows a single narrow peak at the isotropic chemical shift of 134.0 ppm, in agreement with the simulation from the static NMR spectrum, confirming that Xe is adsorbed at a single well-defined adsorption site, independent of loading.

The organic linker (HCOO−) of α-Mg3(HCOO)6 is likely the adsorption site. Therefore, to investigate the interaction between Xe and HCOO− linkers, we conducted 1H → 129Xe cross-polarization (CP) experiments at different contact times (Fig. 2C). In a 1H → 129Xe CP experiment, polarization is transferred from 1H atoms to neighboring 129Xe nuclei via heteronuclear dipolar interaction, which is sensitive to 1H-129Xe internuclear distances and Xe mobility. Figure 2D shows the peak area of the CP NMR spectra as a function of contact time. By fitting the CP curve (55), the dominant dipolar coupling constant was estimated to be 480 Hz (DH-Xe = 480 Hz) (see the Supplementary Materials for fitting details and additional discussion). An alternative approach is to perform Fourier transformation (FT) of the CP intensity as a function of contact time, yielding the characteristic Pake doublet pattern (56). The line splitting between the singularities of the Pake pattern, corresponding to D, provides a direct estimate of the heteronuclear dipolar interaction. The FT result is shown in fig. S3A, with the dipolar coupling constant measured at ~500 Hz, which is close to the analytical simulation. The dipolar coupling between two unlike spins, I and S, depends on their gyromagnetic ratios ( γI,γS ) and the internuclear distance (r), following the relationship D=μ0γIγSh16π3r3 . With D = 480 Hz, the distance between Xe and the nearest H is estimated to be 4.13 Å. To confirm NMR results, we initially estimated the Xe position within the pore by GCMC simulations, which provided a rough Xe-H distance. This distance was then used to place Xe in the unit cell, and subsequent DFT optimization refined the Xe-H distance to 3.73 Å. The distance determined from CP experiments is slightly larger than that from DFT optimization (fig. S2A). Because the DFT optimization is on static Xe at 0 K, this discrepancy may be due to the local thermal motion of Xe partially averaging the 129Xe-1H dipolar interactions, leading to an overestimated distance. This discrepancy could also partially result from the limitations of the analytical model used in fitting the CP curve. Combining the CP experiments and GCMC simulations and DFT optimization, we can conclude that Xe is adsorbed near the hydrogen of the HCOO−, ~4 Å from hydrogen, with localized motion along the 1D channel direction (Fig. 2G).

Previous studies suggested different metal ions in α-M3(HCOO)6 MOF had little impact on their Xe adsorption capacity, although slight differences in their unit cell parameters have been observed (39). Here, we examine the impact of different metal centers on the interactions between α-M3(HCOO)6 (M = Mg, Zn, Ni, and Co) and Xe. The 129Xe NMR spectrum of α-Zn3(HCOO)6 (fig. S3B) shows a δiso of 142.9 ppm and Ω of 38.5 ppm, both of which are higher than the δiso = 134.1 ppm and Ω = 34.0 ppm observed in α-Mg3(HCOO)6. This increase in δiso and Ω is attributed to the presence of filled 3d orbitals in Zn2+, leading to higher polarizability, thereby a slightly stronger interaction with Xe. In α-Ni3(HCOO)6, the metal ion is paramagnetic Ni2+ with two unpaired electrons (S = 1; [Ar] 3d8). The 129Xe NMR spectrum exhibits a Gaussian-type line shape, broadened to ~30 kHz and centered at 312 ppm. The large shift, compared to diamagnetic Mg2+ or Zn2+, results from the hyperfine interaction between the unpaired electron of metal ions and 129Xe. When the metal ion is paramagnetic octahedral Co2+ with three unpaired electrons (high spin, S = 3/2; [Ar] 3d7), the chemical shift is centered at 301 ppm, slightly smaller than α-Ni3(HCOO)6, while the line shape is broader than α-Ni3(HCOO)6, with a width of ~60 kHz. This pronounced spectral broadening confirms that Xe is in proximity to metal ions, corresponding to the distance between Xe and the nearest metal ion of ~5.1 Å from GCMC simulations (Fig. 2G). Therefore, the hyperfine interaction between M2+ and Xe is attributed to the spin-dipolar mechanism (“through-space”) instead of the Fermi contact mechanism (“through-bond”) (57).

83Kr NMR of Kr@α-Mg3(HCOO)6

The MOF α-M3(HCOO)6 exhibits a lower Kr capacity compared to that of Xe (table S1) (39). We acquired the 83Kr NMR spectrum of α-Mg3(HCOO)6 loaded with 2 Kr per unit cell, as shown in Fig. 2F. Even at the high field of 21.1 T, the acquisition of 83Kr NMR spectra remains highly challenging due to intrinsic insensitivity and low concentration. For the sample of α-Mg3(HCOO)6 loaded with 2 Kr per unit cell, the 83Kr NMR experiment took 20 hours, resulting in a signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio of ~13.1. The 83Kr NMR exhibits a rather wide spectral breadth of ~300 ppm. The broad 83Kr spectrum unambiguously indicates that Kr experiences interactions with the MOF framework. To determine whether the 83Kr NMR spectrum is influenced by the second-order QI or anisotropic magnetic shielding interaction, we performed the plane-wave DFT calculations on the optimized structure with static, immobile Kr in the MOF. The calculations revealed a quadrupolar coupling constant CQ of 11 MHz and a span Ω of 41.6 ppm, suggesting that the 83Kr NMR spectrum is primarily affected by the second-order QI rather than the CSA. Considering the mobility of Kr within the channel at room temperature, the actual CQ and Ω values are expected to be smaller than the calculated ones. Therefore, QI is likely the dominant factor contributing to the broadening of 83Kr NMR lineshape. Thus, the 83Kr NMR spectrum was simulated with δiso = 44.3 (4) ppm, CQ = 4.0 (5) MHz, and ηQ = 0.72 (4) (Fig. 2F). These uncertainties are due to the 83Kr NMR spectrum being acquired at a single magnetic field with a limited S/N ratio.

GCMC simulations and DFT optimization show that Kr occupies a similar adsorption site to Xe (Fig. 2H). The region of localized motion for Kr is larger than that for Xe. This is consistent with the higher binding energy of Xe (−34.3 kJ/mol) compared to Kr (−24.8 kJ/mol) obtained from DFT calculations (see Materials and Methods for details), indicating that this MOF type has a greater affinity for Xe than for Kr.

Coadsorption of Xe and Kr in α-Mg3(HCOO)6

To further understand the competitive adsorption of Xe and Kr in a MOF, it is essential to study the behavior of a mixture of Xe and Kr within the framework. 129Xe and 83Kr NMR provide direct insights into the adsorptive behaviors of Xe and Kr in the MOF when both gases coexist. The integration of two NMR techniques allows for the estimation of Xe/Kr selectivity using NMR.

Figure 2E shows the 129Xe NMR spectrum of Xe and Kr coloaded α-Mg3(HCOO)6. In the MOF loaded with a Xe:Kr = 1:1 mixture (2 Xe + 2 Kr per unit cell), the 129Xe NMR spectrum exhibits a lineshape identical to that of α-Mg3(HCOO)6 loaded with pure Xe (2 Xe per unit cell). This suggests that the presence of Kr does not notably influence Xe adsorption and that Xe atoms occupy the same adsorption sites as in the MOF loaded with pure Xe. When the MOF α-Mg3(HCOO)6 is loaded with a Xe:Kr = 1:2 mixture (2 Xe + 4 Kr per unit cell, exceeding the nominal maximum loading of four molecules per unit cell), the 129Xe NMR spectrum is dominated by a CSA powder pattern identical to that observed in the MOF loaded with pure Xe at 4 Xe per unit cell. A very weak sharp peak at 0 ppm is also present, corresponding to a minor amount of free Xe gas, likely due to competitive adsorption with Kr. These results indicate that the interaction between Xe and the framework is sufficiently strong, preventing notable Xe displacement by the excess Kr atoms, and that nearly all Xe atoms remain adsorbed within the channels.

The 83Kr NMR spectrum of α-Mg3(HCOO)6 coloaded with Xe and Kr (2 Xe + 2 Kr per unit cell) is shown in Fig. 2F. It displays a very strong signal from free Kr and a secondary signal corresponding to weakly adsorbed Kr species. Through deconvolution, it is estimated that the ratio of free Kr atoms to adsorbed Kr atoms to be ~63%:37% [a detailed discussion of the central transition (CT) and satellite transition (ST) intensity analysis is provided in the Supplementary Materials]. This suggests that some adsorption sites usually available for Kr are occupied by Xe due to the higher binding energy of Xe compared to Kr.

On the basis of the 129Xe and 83Kr NMR spectra of {Xe,Kr}@α-Mg3(HCOO)6, the MOF adsorbs nearly 100% of the Xe and only 37% of the Kr when exposed to a Xe:Kr = 1:1 gas mixture in our case. The selectivity (SAB) for a binary gas mixture is defined asSAB=xA/yAxB/yB(2)

where xA and xB are the mole fractions of gases A and B in the adsorbed phase and yA and yB are their mole fractions in the bulk phase. From the 129Xe and 83Kr NMR data, the Xe/Kr selectivity of α-Mg3(HCOO)6 at room temperature is estimated to be ~3, which is smaller than the value of ~6 from the breakthrough experiment (39). The observed discrepancy may partially arise from relaxation effects associated with different Kr species, thereby making the NMR-based method a semiquantitative tool for evaluating Xe/Kr separation selectivity.

Xe and Kr adsorption within SIFSIX-3-Zn

SIFSIX-3-Zn is an ultramicroporous MOF well suited for the selective adsorption of small gases; for example, SIFSIX-3-Zn exhibits selective CO2 capture in the presence of water (58, 59). SIFSIX-3-M (M = Zn, Cu, and Co) has demonstrated high selectivity for not only Xe over Kr but also for Kr over N2, making them promising candidates for Kr separation and storage (14). In SIFSIX-3-Zn, the Zn2+ center is coordinated to four N atoms from four pyrazine ligands, as well as two F atoms from two SiF62− ions. The pyrazine linkers are oriented parallel to the crystallographic c axis, forming square-shaped channels measuring ~3.8 Å (Fig. 3A).

129Xe NMR of Xe@SIFSIX-3-Zn

We examined SIFSIX-3-Zn at two loading levels of 0.5 and 1.0 Xe per unit cell. At lower Xe loading, the 129Xe NMR spectrum exhibits a well-defined CSA powder pattern with δiso = 165.6 ppm, Ω = 187.7 ppm, and κ = −0.89 (Fig. 3C), corresponding to a single type of Xe species. The 129Xe MAS NMR spectrum shows a single sharp line, confirming that there is only one type of Xe in the unit cell. Compared with Xe@α-Mg3(HCOO)6, a much larger chemical shift was observed in SIFSIX-3-Zn, which can be attributed to a more confined environment in SIFSIX-3-Zn due to a smaller pore size. The observed span is the largest observed among the diamagnetic MOFs studied in this work, indicating a strong anisotropic interaction between Xe and the framework. The skew κ = −0.89 indicates the near-axially symmetric CSA tensor, which suggests that Xe resides near the center of the channel. At higher Xe loading, a small NMR signal from free Xe gas is observed along with the adsorbed Xe (δiso = 188.5 ppm, Ω = 179 ppm, and κ = −0.83). As GCMC simulations indicate that the distance between two strong Xe binding sites is ~7.6 Å along the c axis (fig. S4A), higher pressure appears to slightly shorten the Xe-Xe distance (fig. S4B), resulting in an increase of chemical shift by 23 ppm due to the effect of ρXeδXe−Xe term in Eq. 1.

In SIFSIX-3-Zn, the Xe can potentially interact with either the protons of pyrazine linker or F atoms in SiF62− pillars. To investigate these interactions, we conducted 129Xe{1H} and 129Xe{19F} spin-echo double-resonance (SEDOR) (60) experiments to examine the proximities between Xe and H or F (see fig. S5 for pulse sequence used) (61). A SEDOR experiment includes two parts (fig. S5) with the first one being a standard 129Xe spin-echo experiment (control experiment). The second is SEDOR experiment; during the spin echo on 129Xe, a π-pulse is applied to the dephasing nucleus (either 1H or 19F channel). This π-pulse perturbs the refocusing of heteronuclear dipolar coupling, leading to a diminished echo intensity. The SEDOR fraction or decay (ΔS) is obtained by subtracting SEDOR spectrum (S) from the control spectrum (S0), and it indicates dipolar coupling between 129Xe and 1H or 19F (fig. S6A). A faster decay (i.e., a larger ΔS) indicates a stronger dipolar coupling, providing qualitative distance information in this study. The plots of SEDOR fraction, ΔS/S0 as a function of the placement of the π-pulse on the dephasing nucleus for 129Xe{1H} and 129Xe{19F} SEDOR experiments, are shown in Fig. 3D, which indicates that Xe is likely in closer proximity to the framework fluorine (SiF62−) than the proton in pyrazine, as 129Xe{19F} SEDOR effect is larger. We subsequently performed DFT optimization of the Xe position in the unit cell. The optimized Xe position, shown in Fig. 3B, reveals a Xe-F distance of ~3.6 Å and a Xe-H distance of ~3.8 Å. These findings highlight that the -F atom serves as a strong Xe binding site in this ultramicroporous MOF.

MOF SIFSIX-3-Cu is an analog of SIFSIX-3-Zn with the same topology but higher stability to radiation (14). The 129Xe NMR of SIFSIX-3-Cu loaded with 0.5 Xe per unit cell exhibits a well-defined CSA powder pattern with δiso = 105 ppm, Ω = 272 ppm, and κ = −0.84 (Fig. 3G). The span value increases from 187.7 ppm in SIFSIX-3-Zn to 272 ppm in SIFSIX-3-Cu. This is likely due to the anisotropic hyperfine interaction between Cu2+ and Xe, corresponding to the Xe…Cu2+ distance of ~5.6 Å from DFT-optimized structure. Compared to the pronounced lineshape changes induced by Co2+…Xe interactions observed in α-Mg3(HCOO)6, the variations in δiso and Ω due to Cu2+, which has only one unpaired electron, in SIFSIX-3-Cu are notably smaller (62).

83Kr NMR of Kr@SIFSIX-3-Zn

Despite the experimental challenges, we successfully acquired the 83Kr NMR spectrum of Kr-loaded SIFSIX-3-Zn at 21.1 T over ~22 hours (Fig. 3F). The observed lineshape is primarily dominated by the QIs, with a minor CSA contribution (simulations with and without CSA are shown in Fig. 3F). The best-fit NMR parameters are δiso = 67.1 (3) ppm, Ω = 61.2 (3) ppm, κ = −1.0 (2), CQ = 6.4 (3) MHz, ηQ = 0.19 (2), and β = 50° (4). The near-zero ηQ and κ = −1.0 indicate that the Kr adsorption site is nearly axially symmetric, suggesting that Kr preferentially adsorbs near the center of the channel. Compared to α-Mg3(HCOO)6, the larger CQ(83Kr) for Kr@SIFSIX-3-Zn reflects stronger Kr-framework interactions, suggesting enhanced Kr affinity for the MOF. Similarly, the 83Kr NMR spectrum of Kr-loaded SIFSIX-3-Cu (Fig. 3G) is well simulated using a single site with δiso = 39.0 (3) ppm, CQ = 5.9 (2) MHz, and ηQ = 0.10 (3). The comparable CQ values for SIFSIX-3-Zn and SIFSIX-3-Cu suggest that the 83Kr electric field gradient (EFG) tensor is minimally influenced by the paramagnetic Cu2+ centers.

Coadsorption of Xe and Kr in SIFSIX-3-Zn

We investigate the Xe and Kr coadsorption through 129Xe and 83Kr NMR as shown in Fig. 3 (E and F). The 129Xe NMR spectrum shows nearly identical lineshape when introducing the gas mixture of Xe:Kr = 1:1 (0.5 Xe + 0.5 Kr per unit cell) and even Xe:Kr = 1:2 (0.5 Xe + 1.0 Kr per unit cell, exceeding the highest capacity of one molecule per unit cell). This means that SIFSIX-3-Zn MOF is preferable to adsorb Xe with nearly unchanged adsorption site.

On the contrary, the 83Kr NMR spectrum of {Xe, Kr}@SIFSIX-3-Zn at a loading of 0.5 Xe + 0.5 Kr per unit cell exhibits two signals from the free Kr and the adsorbed Kr with an areal intensity ratio of ~1:6, yielding an atomic ratio of ~1:16 (see detailed discussions on CT and STs in the Supplementary Materials). This indicates that although SIFSIX-3-Zn is preferable to adsorb Xe, there is still a notable amount of Kr (~0.47 Kr per unit cell) trapped in the MOF due to the smaller pore size when comparing to α-Mg3(HCOO)6.

The MOF FMOF-Zn is constructed from Zn2+ and a flexible V-shaped organic building block, 2,2′-bis(4-carboxyphenyl)hexafluoropropane (40). The Xe and Kr uptake of FMOF-Zn is ~0.8 and ~0.2 mmol/g in single-component adsorption experiments, respectively (40). There are two types of pores in this MOF as shown in Fig. 4A and fig. S7.

This relative order of δiso and Ω is in agreement with DFT-calculated 129Xe NMR parameters for immobile Xe in LPs and SPs (δiso,cal,LP = 103.5 ppm < δiso,cal,SP = 171.7 ppm; Ωcal,LP = 76.7 ppm < Ωcal,SP = 314.8 ppm; and κcal,LP = −0.1, κcal,SP = 0.1). To further probe the origin of these signals, we performed 1H and 19F decoupling experiments. Compared to the 129Xe NMR spectrum acquired without any decoupling, the S1 signal due to Xe in LP exhibits increased intensity and reduced linewidth under 1H decoupling (fig. S8A), indicating the dipolar interactions between Xe and protons in the linkers. Conversely, the S2 due to Xe in SP is more pronounced in the 129Xe NMR spectrum with 19F decoupling, suggesting a stronger dipolar interaction between Xe and fluorine. This confirms that (i) S1 originates from Xe in the LP, which is primarily surrounded by aromatic components of the organic linkers, and (ii) S2 corresponds to Xe in the SP, where it interacts with –CF3 groups (fig. S8B).

129Xe NMR spectra at different Xe loadings were also acquired (Fig. 4B). As the loading increases, the intensity ratio of the SP signal relative to the LP signal increases (table S2), indicating that more Xe atoms are entering the smaller pores. The accommodation of additional Xe in SP could be attributed to the flexibility of the FMOF-Zn framework (63). As the Xe loading increases, the S2 signal corresponding to Xe in the SP shifts to increasingly higher chemical shifts (Fig. 4B and table S2). The shift is due to increased Xe-Xe interactions as additional Xe atoms occupy the SP.

By τmix = 1000 ms, clear exchange is also detected between Xe in the LP and SP. 83Kr NMR of Kr@FMOF-Zn

At a loading of 4 Kr per unit cell, the 83Kr NMR spectrum shows two signals (Fig. 5C). A strong, relatively broad signal (S2) appears at ~40 ppm, which is attributed to Kr confined within the SP (~3.7 Å in diameter), a dimension comparable to the van der Waals diameter of Kr. The second peak (S1), observed at ~5 ppm, corresponds to Kr in the LP. To validate these assignments, we performed DFT calculations on the optimized structure with immobilized Kr, yielding the following NMR parameters: δiso,cal,LP = 23.8 ppm, CQ,cal,LP = 1.8 MHz, and ηQ,cal,LP = 0.6; and δiso,cal,SP = 64.1 ppm, CQ,cal,SP = 4.4 MHz, and ηQ,cal,SP = 0.9. The relative ordering of the calculated isotropic chemical shifts aligns with the proposed spectral assignments (see fig. S10 for simulated spectra from DFT calculations). Notably, Kr mobility within the cages notably averages the QIs, leading to a substantial reduction in the linewidth. At the loading of 6 Kr per unit cell (the maximum theoretical capacity), a prominent free Kr signal (labeled +) overlaps with the S1 signal corresponding to Kr in the LP (fig. S10). If the MOF is overloaded with 8 Kr per unit cell, then the free Kr gas peak becomes much stronger, confirming the signals of free gas and Kr in the LP overlap. In contrast, at the lowest loading of 2 Kr per unit cell, only the S2 signal from Kr in the SP is observed, reflecting the stronger adsorption affinity of the SP. The adsorption behavior of Kr differs notably from that of Xe in the same MOF. At a low loading of 2 Xe per unit cell, the 129Xe NMR spectrum displays signals for Xe in both the LPs and SPs (Fig. 5B), indicating no strong preference for either site. In contrast, Kr preferentially adsorbs in the SP, likely due to the close match between the Kr atomic diameter and the pore size, which enhances confinement effects.

Coadsorption of Xe and Kr in FMOF-Zn

The 129Xe NMR spectrum of Xe and Kr coloaded FMOF-Zn (2 Xe + 2 Kr per unit cell) is shown in Fig. 5B. Unlike the behavior observed in the 129Xe NMR spectrum of Xe and Kr coloaded α-Mg3(HCOO)6 and SIFSIX-3-Zn, Xe adsorption in FMOF-Zn is distinctively altered by the introduction of Kr. Specifically, for the binary adsorption with a loading of 2 Xe + 4 Kr per unit cell, the δiso of Xe signal in SP (S2) increased from 169.7 to 201.4 ppm, Ω increased from 40.3 to 52.2 ppm, and κ changed from 0.05 to −0.48, compared to single Xe adsorption at a loading of 2 Xe per unit cell. The increased chemical shift is due to increased pressure, therefore enhanced Xe-Xe and Xe-Kr interactions. The change in skew is particularly interesting, as it suggests that skew is sensitive to Xe local symmetry variations induced by the presence of Kr (68). Compared to the MOF loaded with 2 Xe per unit cell, the areal ratio of the S2 signal is reduced in the MOF loaded with 2 Xe + 4 Kr per unit cell (table S2). This suggests that Xe and Kr effectively compete for adsorption in the SP.

The 83Kr NMR spectrum of Xe and Kr coloaded FMOF-Zn (Fig. 5C) shows two signals, indicating that Kr occupies both SPs and LPs. Compared to FMOF-Zn loaded with 2 Kr per unit cell, the introduction of Xe causes a notable redistribution of Kr from the SP to the larger one, suggesting that Xe effectively displaces Kr from the SP. Combined 129Xe and 83Kr NMR results confirm that both gases compete for adsorption in the SP. In addition, the introduction of Kr affects the Xe exchange dynamics. A comparison of 2D 129Xe exchange spectra of FMOF-Zn loaded with 2 Xe per unit cell and the same MOF loaded with 2 Xe + 2 Kr per unit cell (fig. S11) shows that at a mixing time of 500 ms, the cross peak signals indicating exchange between Xe in the LPs and SPs disappear in the presence of Kr. This indicates that Kr slows down the Xe exchange process. By fitting the intensity ratio using Eq. 3, the exchange rate of Xe between the SPs and LPs is estimated to decrease from 0.17 to 0.11 s−1 upon Kr introduction.

In summary, when only a single component is present, FMOF-Zn preferentially adsorbs Xe over Kr, as demonstrated by both adsorption isotherm measurements (40) and NMR analysis. However, under coloading conditions, Xe and Kr compete for adsorption sites (SP), and the MOF exhibits comparable affinities for both gases, as evidenced by the 129Xe and 83Kr NMR results.

Xe and Kr adsorption within ZnAtzOx

The pores measure 5.3 × 5.3 Å along the a axis and 6.4 × 6.4 Å down the b axis (69). This MOF exhibits a Xe adsorption capacity of 2.45 mmol/g and Kr capacity of 0.9 mmol/g, where the aminofunctional group of the Atz linker is assumed to provide the binding site for Xe atoms (42).

129Xe NMR of Xe@ZnAtzOx

The 129Xe NMR spectrum of Xe-loaded ZnAtzOx MOF exhibits a well-defined CSA powder pattern, simulated with δiso = 160 ppm, Ω = 98.5 ppm, and κ = −0.25 (Fig. 6D). The isotropic chemical shift is similar to that of Xe@SIFSIX-3-Zn (δiso = 165.6 ppm), indicating strong interaction between the Xe and framework. The skew value of κ = −0.25 suggests that Xe is in a nonaxially symmetric pore environment. This is not unexpected because the pore structure is not a straight 1D channel as in the SIFSIX-3-Zn MOF.

To further investigate the interaction between Xe and the Atz linkers, we conducted 1H-129Xe 2D heteronuclear correlation (HETCOR) experiment at room temperature. The 1H MAS NMR spectrum of activated ZnAtzOx shows two signals at 5.2 and 8.1 ppm (Fig. 6D), which are assigned to the NH2 (H1) and CH (H2) protons from the linkers, respectively (70). The 1H-129Xe HETCOR spectrum (Fig. 6E) reveals correlations between the adsorbed 129Xe signal and 1H signals from both the amino (H1) and CH (H2) in the triazole ring of the linker, which is consistent with the DFT optimized positions of Xe at 0 K (Fig. 6B).

89Kr NMR of Kr@ZnAtzOx

The 83Kr NMR spectrum of Kr@ZnAtzOx shown in Fig. 6C was simulated with EFG-only model [δiso = 58 (2) ppm, CQ = 3.0 (2) MHz, and ηQ = 0.96 (3)]. The plane-wave DFT calculation on the MOF loaded with static Kr shows a CSA span of 43 ppm and a CQ,cal of 11.2 MHz, suggesting that the Kr is largely affected by the QI interactions, with only small CSA contribution. The magnitude of quadrupolar coupling is comparable to that of α-Mg3(HCOO)6 [CQ (83Kr) = 4.0 MHz], indicating a similar interaction strength but is notably smaller than that of SIFSIX-3-Zn [CQ (83Kr) = 6.4 MHz].

Coadsorption of Xe and Kr in ZnAtzOx

For the Xe and Kr coloaded MOF (4 Xe + 4 Kr per unit cell), the 1D 129Xe NMR spectrum is identical to that of the pure Xe adsorption in ZnAtzOx with 4 Xe per unit cell. However, the 1H-129Xe 2D HETCOR spectrum (Fig. 6E) shows a weaker correlation between the Xe and NH2 (H1) groups, with Xe more proximate to −CH (H2) protons. This is likely due to the introduction of Kr atoms, which competitively occupy the binding sites near the NH2 (H1) groups. Acquiring the 83Kr NMR spectrum of Xe/Kr-coloaded MOF was attempted but unsuccessful, possibly due to the low 83Kr concentration and/or short T2 relaxation times.

Xe and Kr adsorption within UTSA-74

MOF UTSA-74 is a representative Zn-MOF for Xe and Kr separation with dual OMSs (41). At 298 K and 1 bar, UTSA-74 demonstrated a Xe uptake of 2.71 mmol/g, whereas the Kr capacity under the same conditions was only 0.58 mmol/g. This makes it suitable material for Xe and Kr separation. UTSA-74 has a discrete binuclear Zn cluster as a secondary building unit. In each Zn dimer, the first Zn site (Zn1) is saturated and tetrahedrally coordinated to two carboxylate oxygen atoms and two hydroxyl oxygen atoms from 2,5-dioxido-1,4-benzenedicarboxylate (DOBDC) linkers. The second metal site (Zn2) is octahedrally linked to four DOBDC4− oxygen atoms in equatorial positions along with two terminally coordinated water molecules in axial positions (Fig. 7A). The two water molecules coordinated to Zn2 can be removed upon activation, creating two OMSs in axial position on the same metal. The large 1D channel has a diameter of ~8 Å and can adsorb guest molecules such as CO2 and NO via OMSs (71).

129Xe NMR of Xe@UTSA-74

We began with structural-related MOF-74(Zn). As a framework isomer of UTSA-74, the well-known MOF-74(Zn) features a honeycomb structure with a 1D channel system formed by infinite helical chains of edge-sharing ZnO6 octahedra along the c axis, connected by DOBDC4− linkers (Fig. 7B) with each Zn2+ bound to five framework oxygen atoms from the linkers and a solvent molecule (usually water) (71). Upon activation, the removal of the solvent molecule from each metal ion creates a single five-coordinated OMS, enabling the adsorption of guest molecules. The 129Xe NMR spectrum of Xe-loaded MOF-74 (18 Xe per unit cell, i.e., 1 Xe per Zn) shown in Fig. 7D displays two resonances. The intense sharp free Xe gas signal at ~0 ppm indicates that this loading reaches the maximum capacity at 298 K. The adsorbed Xe appears as a near-symmetric NMR signal at ~184.5 ppm with a linewidth of ~16 kHz, corresponding to the Xe adsorbed at Zn2+ (72, 73).

In contrast, the 129Xe NMR spectrum of Xe-loaded UTSA-74 at the same loading (Fig. 7C) exhibits a broad asymmetric peak. Although it initially resembles a CSA pattern, subsequent CP experiments indicate that it consists of two overlapping signals (Fig. 8A), which can be deconvoluted into two components at 147.3 and 157.8 ppm in a 64%:36% ratio. The chemical shift is comparable to the Xe adsorbed in Zn-exchanged zeolites (74). This indicates the presence of two inequivalent Xe species, corresponding to the Xe atoms adsorbed at two OMSs on the same Zn site. At this Xe loading (1 Xe per Zn), there is no free gas signal observed in UTSA-74, suggesting higher Xe adsorption capability of UTSA-74 than MOF-74. Despite the relatively LP size of ~8 Å, the isotropic chemical shift values of adsorbed Xe atoms are comparable to those of the ultramicroporous MOF SIFSIX-3-Zn, due to the additional strong interaction between Zn2+ and Xe.

In principle, each Zn2 can bind two Xe atoms at maximum capacity. Therefore, we investigate a higher loading of 36 Xe per unit cell (i.e., 2 Xe/Zn). At this loading (Fig. 8A), the intensity ratio of the two signals at 146.2 and 163.5 ppm is ~46%:54%, which closely matches the expected theoretical ratio of 1:1. As shown in Fig. 8A, the intensity ratio of signals at 146.0 and 162.5 ppm is ~66%:34%. The intensity ratio of the two adsorbed Xe in CP experiments is higher than in the direct polarization (DP) NMR results. This likely reflects the fact that while Xe is localized near the OMSs, the LP size allows for sufficient motional averaging, such as reorientation or tumbling of Xe at the adsorption site, which effectively averages out the CSA.

83Kr NMR of Xe@UTSA-74

The 83Kr NMR spectrum of Kr@UTSA-74 (36 Kr per unit cell) exhibits only a single sharp signal at about 0 ppm, while adsorbed Kr is not observed (Fig. 8D). This observation suggests that unlike Xe, Kr does not bind to Zn OMSs strongly. The observed sharp signal is likely not from free Kr gas alone but rather arises from fast exchange between weakly adsorbed Kr inside the LPs and Kr in the external gas phase.

Coadsorption of Xe and Kr in UTSA-74

For the Xe/Kr-coloaded UTSA-74 (18 Xe + 18 Kr per unit cell), the 129Xe NMR spectrum exhibits two signals at 142.8 and 157.9 ppm (Fig. 8C). No free Xe gas is observed. The chemical shifts of these two peaks only slightly moved to a lower chemical shift direction compared to the 129Xe NMR spectrum of UTSA-74 loaded with 18 Xe per unit cell. These observations suggest that Xe adsorption is largely unaffected by the addition of Kr. The fact that all the Xe atoms are retained inside the channels with a large size indicates that Xe is very strongly adsorbed in UTSA-74. The high Xe affinity is attributed to the presence of dual OMSs on the same Zn2+ ion, which provide specific Zn2+-Xe interactions. The NMR results indicate that the dual OMSs on the same Zn ion have a very strong affinity to Xe. This unique Zn-Xe interaction is the reason why among all the MOFs examined in this work, UTSA-74 has the highest Xe intake. Meanwhile, the very weak Zn2+…Kr interaction coupled with the LP size of UTSA-74 leads to its weak Kr adsorption capability. It appears that UTSA-74 is an excellent MOF for Xe-selective separation but less suitable for Kr capture.

CO2 and Xe coadsorption study in MOFs

Although present in low concentration, CO2 does exist in the off-gas mixture generated during reprocessing of UNF, and it is known to influence Xe/Kr separation. Moreover, if off-gas mixture is used as a source of Xe for industrial applications, then CO2 removal is essential for purification. Therefore, understanding the competitive adsorption behavior between CO2 and Xe is crucial for designing MOFs with high selectivity for Xe capture. Previously, we studied pure CO2 adsorption in MOFs α-Mg3(HCOO)6 and SIFSIX-3-Zn using 13C wideline NMR of 13CO2 (75, 76). In this work, we extended our investigation to CO2 and Xe coadsorption in α-Mg3(HCOO)6, SIFSIX-3-Zn, and FMOF-Zn, using 13C and 129Xe NMR spectroscopy as probes. For α-Mg3(HCOO)6 and SIFSIX-3-Zn, which feature 1D channels, the 13C and 129Xe NMR spectra of the CO2/Xe mixture closely resemble those obtained when the MOFs are loaded with each gas individually (figs. S12 and S13) (75, 76). This indicates that the Xe and CO2 occupy their respective adsorption sites within the channels without notably influencing each other.

In contrast, the 13C and 129Xe NMR results for FMOF-Zn reveal a notable difference. Because 13C static spectra of 13CO2 adsorbed in this MOF have never been previously reported, we first measured 13C wideline spectrum of 13CO2 adsorbed in FMOF-Zn at a loading of 4 CO2 per unit cell (Fig. 9A). The 13C NMR spectrum exhibits two distinct CSA powder patterns (Fig. 9A): S1 (red), simulated with δiso = 122.0 ppm, Ω = 24 ppm, and κ = 0.49; and S2 (green), simulated with δiso = 125.7 ppm, Ω = 14 ppm, and κ = −0.86 (table S3). These signals correspond to two CO2 molecules adsorbed in two different cages. The broader pattern (S1) originates from CO2 located in the SPs, and the narrower pattern (S2) from CO2 residing in LPs. When both Xe and CO2 are loaded into FMOF-Zn, the 13C NMR spectrum of the coadsorbed sample changes notably. The resonance (S2) corresponding to CO2 in the LPs disappears, while the signal (S1) from CO2 in the SP remains, with CSA parameters of δiso = 122.0 ppm, Ω = 18 ppm, and κ = 0.73 similar to those of S1 in the CO2-only sample. In addition, a sharp, intense peak appears at 125 ppm, indicative of free CO2 gas. These results suggest that the incoming Xe displaces CO2 from the LPs: some CO2 molecules migrate into the SPs, while others desorb into the gas phase. This interpretation is consistent with GCMC simulations (Fig. 9, C and D), which show that CO2@FMOF-Zn has two distinct adsorption sites corresponding to the S1 and S2 NMR signals. However, in the Xe/CO2 mixture, simulations reveal that CO2 becomes confined primarily to the SPs, with negligible occupancy in the LPs. The affinity of CO2 for the SP likely arises from interactions with the –CF3 groups lining the pore walls.

(A) Static 13C and (B) static 129Xe NMR spectra of FMOF-Zn loaded with pure 13CO2, pure Xe, and a Xe/CO2 mixture. + represents 129Xe NMR signal from free Xe gas. Spatial distribution of CO2 in FMOF-Zn loaded with (C) pure CO2 and (D) a Xe/CO2 mixture derived from GCMC simulations.

The 129Xe NMR spectrum of the Xe/CO2 mixture in FMOF-Zn (4 CO2 + 4 Xe) reveals several signals (Fig. 9B). The resonance at ~92 ppm (red) is due to Xe in LPs, and the peak at ~210 ppm (green) is assigned to the Xe in SPs. Compared to the Xe-only adsorption (4 Xe per unit cell), the 129Xe NMR intensity of Xe in SP decreases markedly (table S2). This marked decrease confirms that the SP is predominantly occupied by CO2. Both 13C and 129Xe NMR results indicate that CO2 dominantly occupies the SPs, leaving very limited space for Xe to occupy. In the 129Xe spectrum of the Xe/CO2-coadsorbed MOF, a resonance appears at ~70 ppm. We tentatively assign this signal to Xe residing in a distinct adsorption site associated with the LPs. Given the known flexibility of FMOF-Zn, it is plausible that the strong interaction of CO2 with the framework induces structural distortion, creating a new Xe-accessible site near the LP region, offering an alternative location for Xe that cannot enter the CO2-filled SPs. These results demonstrate that FMOF-Zn exhibits high selectivity for both Xe and CO2, with distinct pore preferences: the LPs preferentially accommodate Xe, while the SPs favor CO2 adsorption. This dual-site behavior makes FMOF-Zn particularly suitable for the simultaneous removal of CO2 and Xe by selectively adsorbing them in different pore environments. In the context of Xe/Kr separation, the high selectivity of FMOF-Zn for both Xe and CO2 would be well suited as the first bed in a two-bed separation system. A second MOF with higher affinity for Kr would then be required in the downstream bed to effectively capture the remaining Kr.

Radiation stability of MOFs

If used for UNF reprocessing, then MOFs will be exposed to β and γ radiations. While γ radiation doses in this environment are significantly lower than those in a reactor core, they can still reach hundred kilograys (kGy) (77–79). However, studies on the radiation stability of MOFs remain scarce (14, 79–81). To address this, we investigated the stability of the MOFs used in this study. We exposed them to a controlled γ radiation dose (from a 60Co source) ranging from 10 to 60 kGy and monitored their structural integrity using powder x-ray diffraction (PXRD). In addition, we examined the impact of γ irradiation on Xe-loaded MOFs by performing 129Xe NMR, allowing us to probe changes in the adsorbed Xe environment postirradiation.

The PXRD patterns in fig. S14 show that the activated (empty) FMOF-Zn, UTSA-74, ZnAtzOx, and SIFSIX-3-Cu retain their long-range order after γ irradiation up to 60 kGy, indicating good radiation stability and potential for UNF processing. In contrast, α-Mg3(HCOO)6 remains stable only up to 30 kGy and transforms into an unidentified phase at 60 kGy. SIFSIX-3-Zn exhibits even lower stability, undergoing a phase change at just 10 kGy. Elsaidi et al. (14) previously reported that while SIFSIX-3-Cu remains structurally intact after 50 kGy, the crystal structure of SIFSIX-3-Zn begins to degrade at doses as low as 1 kGy.

We then loaded FMOF-Zn, UTSA-74, ZnAtzOx, α-Mg3-(HCOO)6, SIFSIX-3-Cu, and SIFSIX-3-Zn with Xe and sequentially exposed the Xe-loaded MOFs to γ radiation doses of 10, 30, and 60 kGy, acquiring 129Xe NMR spectra after each dose to monitor changes in Xe adsorption behavior. As shown in fig. S15, for each MOF, the 129Xe NMR spectra acquired after each γ radiation dose appear nearly identical to those of the corresponding nonirradiated Xe-loaded MOFs. This result is expected for FMOF-Zn, UTSA-74, ZnAtzOx, and SIFSIX-3-Cu, as their frameworks remain crystalline and intact up to 60 kGy. However, this finding is surprising for α-Mg3(HCOO)6 and SIFSIX-3-Zn, whose empty frameworks are altered after 30 and 10 kGy, respectively. Why do their 129Xe spectra remain unchanged even after 60-kGy irradiation?

One possible explanation is that the presence of Xe atoms within the channels helps stabilize the framework against radiation-induced damage. Guest molecules such as Xe can interact with the pore walls and reduce framework flexibility or prevent collapse under γ irradiation. As a result, although the empty forms of α-Mg3(HCOO)6 and SIFSIX-3-Zn undergo structural transformations at relatively low doses (30 and 10 kGy, respectively), the Xe-loaded forms show little to no change in their 129Xe spectra, even after 60 kGy. To test this hypothesis, we recorded PXRD patterns of Xe-loaded α-Mg3(HCOO)6 and SIFSIX-3-Zn after sequential exposure to 10, 30, and 60 kGy of γ radiation. As shown in fig. S16, the crystal structure of Xe-loaded α-Mg3(HCOO)6 remains fully intact even after cumulative exposure to 60 kGy. Similarly, Xe-loaded SIFSIX-3-Zn shows only minor changes in its diffraction pattern after 60-kGy irradiation (Fig. 10), with most of the original reflections still present (marked with +), and only few very weak signals corresponding to decomposition products. These notable results indicate that even at relatively low loadings, Xe adsorption greatly enhances the radiation resistance of these otherwise sensitive frameworks. This also explains why the 129Xe spectra of the irradiated samples are nearly identical to those of the unirradiated ones. This finding suggests that the presence of Xe can notably enhance the radiation tolerance of MOFs and should be considered when evaluating materials for Xe capture under nuclear fuel reprocessing conditions.

DISCUSSION

This study provides essential atomic-level insight into Xe/Kr adsorption and separation in MOFs using solid-state NMR spectroscopy, including the first application of challenging 83Kr NMR in this context. We systematically examined representative MOFs from three key classes recognized for their excellent potential for Xe/Kr separation performance.

Using a combination of 129Xe wideline NMR, 1H-129Xe, 19F-129Xe CP, 2D HETCOR, and 129Xe{1H} and 129Xe{19F} SEDOR experiments, we identified Xe adsorption sites and the nature of the interactions between Xe and the MOF frameworks at the atomic level. The information about Xe exchange among different adsorption sites was also obtained whenever applicable. Despite its low sensitivity and quadrupolar character, 83Kr NMR at 21.1 T was successfully implemented in Xe/Kr separation studies and provided unique insights into the behavior and mobility of Kr, particularly how Kr is perturbed in the presence of coadsorbed Xe.

We further demonstrated that coadsorption of Xe and Kr can be effectively probed by combining 129Xe and 83Kr NMR. The 129Xe and 83Kr NMR results together show that Xe dominates the adsorption process in all studied MOFs. Our data reveal that Xe adsorption in α-Mg3(HCOO)6, SIFSIX-3-Zn, and UTSA-74 is essentially unaffected by Kr under competitive conditions, while Kr uptake is notably suppressed in the presence of Xe. Xe environments in FMOF-Zn and ZnAtzOx are slightly affected. FMOF-Zn can efficiently and simultaneously adsorb Xe and CO2 in different pores, making it a promising candidate for the first bed in a two-bed Xe/Kr separation system. For UTSA-74, NMR data indicate that Xe strongly interacts with the dual Zn OMS, whereas the interaction with Kr is much weaker. The power of solid-state NMR to reveal atomic-level adsorption phenomena is exemplified by FMOF-Zn, which contains two distinct pore types. Whereas gas adsorption isotherms report only the total uptake, solid-state NMR can resolve the specific gas locations, spatial distribution, and dynamic exchange of adsorbed molecules. Together, these techniques provide a more complete picture of adsorption behavior.

Our radiation resistance tests show that several MOFs retain their crystallinity after exposure to γ radiation up to 60 kGy. A particularly notable finding is that Xe adsorption improves radiation resistance: SIFSIX-3-Zn, normally highly sensitive to radiation (decomposing after 1 kGy), retains much of its structural integrity even after 60 kGy when loaded with Xe. This protective effect of Xe is highly relevant to MOF application in nuclear environments and should be considered in future material design.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

All starting chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification.

1) α-Mg3(COOH)6 (39): Three millimoles (0.77 g) of Mg(NO3)2·6H2O, 6 mmol (0.28 g) of formic acid (HCOOH), and 10 ml of N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF) were mixed in a 23-ml Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave and then heated at 110°C for 2 days. The white crystalline product was washed with DMF and ethanol.

2) SIFSIX-3-Zn (14): A solution was made by adding 1.3 mmol (0.10 g) of pyrazine to 2.0 ml of methanol. In a glass vial, the pyrazine solution was then gently layered on a solution of 0.6 mmol (0.13 g) of ZnSiF6·xH2O in 2.0 ml of methanol. The product was obtained as yellow crystals after 3 days and washed three times with methanol.

3) SIFSIX-3-Cu (14): A methanol solution (5.0 ml) of pyrazine (0.30 g, 3.0 mmol) was carefully layered over a methanol solution (5.0 ml) of CuSiF6·xH2O (0.325 g, 0.6 mmol) in a glass tube. Upon layering, light violet powder formed rapidly and was left in the mother solution for 24 hours. The resulting powder was then collected, thoroughly washed with methanol, and dried under vacuum.

4) FMOF-Zn (40): One millimole of zinc nitrate hexahydrate (0.7 g) and 1 mmol of 2,2-bis(4-carboxyphenyl)hexafluoropropane (0.95 g) in 25 ml of DMF and 21 ml of ethanol were mixed in a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 110°C for 12 hours. The resulting white powder was then collected and thoroughly washed with ethanol.

5) UTSA-74 (41): Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O (1 g, 4.50 mmol), 2,5-dihydroxy-1,4-benzenedicarboxylic acid (0.5 g, 2.50 mmol), and dimethyl sulfoxide (50 ml) were mixed in a 100-ml Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave and heated at 110°C for 72 hours. The resulting powder was filtered and collected under vacuum filtration.

6) ZnAtzOx (42): Zinc carbonate basic (0.11 g, 0.20 mmol), oxalic acid (0.090 g, 1.0 mmol), and 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (0.42 g, 5.0 mmol) were added to a 6.0-ml solution of distilled water and butanol [50:50 (v/v)] in a 23-ml Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 30 min, after which the autoclave was heated at 180°C for 2 days. The colorless microcrystalline powder was collected and washed three times with 15 ml of a water-methanol solution [50:50 (v/v)].

Activation and guest loading

To prepare the sample for the NMR experiments, MOFs were loaded into L-shaped 5-mm glass tubes connected to a Schlenk line, where they were heated at 100° to 200°C for 12 hours under vacuum. After cooling to room temperature, the sample was exposed to 40 to 90 mbar of Xe or/and Kr in the calibrated volume (83 ml) and then condensed at liquid nitrogen temperature (77 K). The tube was then flame sealed and left to equilibrate at 60°C for 12 hours.

Solid-state NMR characterization

NMR experiments at 9.4 T: 129Xe NMR experiments were performed on a Varian Infinity Plus spectrometer operating at a magnetic field of 9.4 T (Larmor frequency: 110.15 MHz) in Western University, Canada. 129Xe 1D static experiments were conducted with a 5-mm HX solenoid Varian/Chemagnetics probe, and 129Xe static NMR spectra were collected using one pulse or spin-echo (90° to 180°) pulse sequence with an optimized 90° pulse length of 2.85 μs and a recycle delay of 4 s. The interpulse delay was set to 40 μs. The 129Xe chemical shifts were externally referenced to gaseous Xe extrapolated to zero pressure at 0 ppm. 1H decoupling of 50 kHz was applied during the acquisition. The 129Xe 1D MAS experiments were conducted with a 5-mm HFXY Varian/Chemagnetics probe at a spinning rate of 10 kHz. The 129Xe NMR spectra were collected using a rotor-synchronized spin echo (90° to 180°) with an optimized 90° pulse length of 3.25 μs and a recycle delay of 4 s. 1H-129Xe static CP spectra were acquired using contact times varying from 0.5 to 10 ms. The 1H spectra were referenced to tetramethylsilane (TMS) at 0 ppm. The 129Xe{19F} SEDOR experiment was performed with the spin-echo pulse sequence (90° to 180°) on 129Xe and the dephasing 180° pulse of 10.4 μs on 19F at different t1 (see fig. S5 for pulse sequence). The interpulse delay for the spin echo was set to 1500 μs. 1H-129Xe HETCOR experiments were conducted using the frequency-switched Lee-Goldburg (FSLG)–HETCOR pulse sequence (82) with a pulse delay of 5 s and a contact time of 8 ms. 2D 129Xe static EXSY experiments were performed at room temperature with 512 transients for each of 64 t1 increments with Δt1 = 12.5 μs. The 2D EXSY spectra mixing time (τmix) ranged from 5 ms to 1 s.

NMR experiments at 21.1 T: The 83Kr NMR experiments were performed on a Bruker Avance II 900-MHz instrument (Larmor frequency at 34.6 MHz) at the National Ultrahigh-Field NMR Facility for Solids in Ottawa, Canada. Static powder spectra were obtained on a homebuilt 7-mm solenoid probe. A quadrupolar echo (90° to 180°) with selective 90° pulse width of 2 μs was used in static experiments. The interpulse delay was 70 μs. Between 4096 and 131,072 scans were acquired with recycle delays ranging from 0.5 ms to 5 s. 83Kr NMR experiments under MAS conditions were attempted; however, no signals were observed, likely because the weak adsorption of Kr in MOFs led to Kr loss during spinning. Analytical simulation of NMR spectra was performed using ssNake package to obtain the spectral parameters (83).

NMR interactions

The static 129Xe NMR spectrum is dominated by magnetic shielding interactions. The CSA is modeled as a tensor with three principal components: δ11, δ22, and δ33 (δ11 > δ22 > δ33). The Herzfeld-Berger convention (84) is used in this work, where the isotropic chemical shift (δiso), span (Ω), and skew (κ) are defined asδiso=13(δ11+δ22+δ33)(4)Ω=13(δ11−δ33)(5)κ=3(δ22−δiso)Ω(6)

The span measures the anisotropy of chemical shift and determines the spectral breadth of the CSA powder pattern. The skew quantifies the axial symmetry of the CSA tensor and influences the shape of the powder pattern. The skew ranges from −1 to +1, with κ = ±1 indicating an axially symmetric tensor.

The quadrupole moment of 83Kr interacts with the EFG from the surrounding environment through QI, where the principal components of the EFG tensor are V11, V22, and V33 (|V33| ≥ |V22| ≥ |V11|). QI is described with two parameters: quadrupolar coupling constant (CQ) defined as CQ = eQV33/hour and the asymmetry parameter ηQ = (V11 − V22)/V33. A larger CQ indicates reduced spherical symmetry, resulting in broader spectra. The axial symmetry of the EFG tensor is quantified by the asymmetry parameter, which ranges from 0 (perfect axial symmetry) to 1 (axial asymmetry). In addition to QI, 83Kr NMR spectra may also be affected by the CSA. The relative orientation of the EFG and CSA tensors is described with three Euler angles (α, β, and γ).

γ Irradiation experiments

The γ irradiation experiments were performed using a MDS Nordion GammaCell 220 Excel Cobalt-60 irradiator in the Department of Chemistry, Western University, Canada. The MOF samples with and without gas loading were placed in an autoclave and exposed to a uniform field of γ radiation for varied times at a dose rate of 725 Gy/hour.

GCMC simulations

The GCMC was performed using RASPA 2.0 code (85). Nonbonded interactions among adsorbates and framework atoms were described using 12-6 Lennard-Jones and Coulomb potentials by applying (86)Uij=4εij[(σijrij)12−(σijrij)6]+(qiqj4πε0rij)(7)

The universal force field (87) is used for the framework, and gas molecules were modeled using TraPPE force field (88). Partial atomic charges were calculated using extended charge equilibration method (89). The simulations included 1 × 106 MC cycles with the first half for equilibration and the remaining for the production run. The VisIt software is used for visualization (90).

DFT calculations

DFT calculations were performed using the CASTEP 22 code (91). Geometry optimizations used the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional (92) and a plane-wave basis set with a cutoff energy of 800 eV. The initial position of Xe and Kr was taken from the GCMC simulations. Grimme’s DFT-D3 dispersion correction (93) was applied during the optimization. Following the geometry optimization, the NMR parameters were calculated using the gauge-including projector augmented wave method (94). The DFT calculations were performed for static Xe or Kr at 0 K without any thermal excitation. where EGas+MOF , EGas , and EMOF are the total energies of adsorbed structure, Xe/Kr gas, and a unit cell of MOF, while N denotes the number of molecules adsorbed in one unit cell.
