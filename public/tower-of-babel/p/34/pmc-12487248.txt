Assessing physical activity literacy and practices among adults in the United Arab Emirates: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Rifah Anwar Assadi, Afreen Abdul Rahim Sanaullah, Sathyapriya Nandagopal, Shahnaz Mohamed Wazil, Priya Pardasani, Meenadarsini Dhamothiran, And Ozyigit, Jagat Gopinath, Ans Ahmed Mahmood, Maryam Almarzooqi, Bibi Morsal Sayedy, Elham Riyaz, Jayakumary Muttappallymyalil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical inactivity among adults in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) is a growing public health concern, contributing to chronic diseases and increased healthcare burdens. Understanding physical activity literacy (PAL) and behaviour, along with associated sociodemographic factors, is essential for designing effective interventions.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 630 adults in UAE, using a validated self-administered questionnaire. The tool assessed knowledge of physical activity and its health benefits, engagement in physical activity, and sociodemographic characteristics. Knowledge scores were categorized as ‘good’ (≥ 6) and ‘poor’ (≤ 5). Data were analysed using SPSS version 28, for associations between literacy, behavior, and demographic factors were analyzed using chi-square tests, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported.ResultsMost participants (95.2%) demonstrated good knowledge of physical activity benefits, though misconceptions persisted regarding its effects on HIV/AIDS (32.4% incorrect) and blood pressure (28.9% incorrect). A high proportion (97.3%) reported engaging in physical activity, with 61% moderately active (3–5 times/week) and 19% highly active (6–7 times/week). There was no statistically significant association between knowledge level and physical activity performance (p = 0.192; odds ratio = 0.359, 95% CI: 0.078–1.647). Significant associations were observed between literacy level and gender (p = 0.001) as well as employment status (p = 0.002), with females and unemployed individuals more likely to have higher literacy. Physical activity engagement was not significantly associated with any sociodemographic variable.ConclusionsAlthough both knowledge and participation levels are high among adults in UAE, targeted educational interventions are needed to address persistent misconceptions, especially regarding specific health outcomes. Tailoring these interventions based on gender and employment status may improve their effectiveness and promote sustained physical activity engagement across different population groups in the UAE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24490-6.Keywords: Physical activity, Health literacy, UAE adults, Sociodemographic factors, Exercise behaviour

Introduction

Physical literacy (PL) is a multifaceted concept comprised of affective (motivation and confidence), physical (physical competence), cognitive (knowledge and understanding), and behavioural (engagement in physical activities for life) domains. These four domains together embody a holistic approach to physical activity that considers the social processes associated with lifelong learning [1]. While Physical Literacy has been widely recognized as foundational for lifelong physical activity, most research to date has focused on children, linking higher PL to improved health outcomes, fitness, and psychosocial well-being [2, 3]. While much of the existing research on physical literacy focuses on children, understanding PL in adults is equally important, as lifelong engagement in physical activity is influenced by early habits but also shaped by adult experiences.

The United Arab Emirates (UAE) faces growing public health challenges related to physical inactivity and non-communicable diseases, particularly among adults [4]. In response, the UAE government has prioritized physical activity through national strategies such as the UAE National Strategy for Wellbeing 2031 and the establishment of the UAE Sports for All Federation, which aims to create inclusive opportunities for all residents to participate in sports and active living [5, 6]. Despite these efforts, most research in the UAE has focused on measuring physical activity levels, with limited attention to the broader concept of physical literacy and its determinants among adults. However, there is a notable gap in research addressing physical literacy among adults, particularly in the UAE.

Internationally, governments, health and exercise practitioners are struggling with the threat posed by physical inactivity leading to worsening outcomes in health and life expectancy and the associated high economic costs. To meet this challenge, it is important to enhance the quality, and quantity, of participation in sports and physical activity throughout the life course to sustain healthy and active lifestyle by developing a physically literate population, who meaningfully engage in play and physical activity [7].

To date, there is a lack of published research evaluating physical activity literacy among adults in the UAE or the wider GCC region. Existing studies primarily focus on physical activity prevalence and related behaviours, rather than literacy or its determinants [8, 9]. This study aims to address this gap by systematically assessing PAL and its associated factors among UAE adults. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the level of physical activity literacy among UAE adults and analyse how demographic factors influence physical activity literacy and behaviours.

Materials and methods

The aim of this cross-sectional study was to assess the level of physical activity literacy, physical activity practices, and associated sociodemographic factors among adults attending physical activity facilities in the United Arab Emirates. The study targeted adult users (aged 18 years and above) of physical activity facilities, regardless of gender or nationality, and excluded individuals unwilling to participate. Participants were recruited from a diverse range of physical activity settings, including gyms, fitness centres, and sports clubs across Ajman and other emirates in the UAE. Recruitment was not restricted to a particular facility or chain, and adults were approached in-person at facility entrances and common areas. As a result, the total number of facilities approached, and the response rate could not be determined. This approach was chosen to maximize sample size and diversity but limits the ability to assess representativeness and calculate a conventional response rate.

The sample size was calculated using the formula 4PQ/L², with P set at 0.41 based on the reported prevalence of insufficient physical activity among Indian adults [10], due to the lack of comparable UAE-specific data. This yielded a minimum sample size of 387, which was increased by 10% (n = 39) to account for potential nonresponse, resulting in a final sample size of 426. Convenience sampling was used due to logistical constraints and to ensure rapid recruitment from available facility users. This method was chosen for feasibility but may limit generalizability.

The study employed a self-administered questionnaire (Supplementary file 1) to gather data on socio-demographic characteristics, literacy on physical activity, and practice details including type, frequency, and duration of physical activity. In this study, ‘physical activity literacy’ refers specifically to knowledge and understanding of physical activity guidelines, benefits, and misconceptions, as measured by our 11-item scale. It does not encompass broader health literacy domains.

Instrument Validation.

The 11-item physical activity knowledge scale used in this study was adapted and reviewed for content and face validity by three public health experts. To assess reliability, the tool was pilot tested among five adults attending gyms who were not included in the final sample. The scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.787. Based on feedback from the pilot, minor revisions were made to enhance clarity.

Physical activity literacy was assessed using this 11-item knowledge scale, with each correct answer awarded one point for a Maximum possible score of 11. Scores of 6 or above were categorized as ‘good’ literacy. This threshold was selected based on established cut-off points in previous studies and our own pilot testing, which indicated that a score of 6 best differentiated knowledge levels among participants. Additionally, analysis of various thresholds (≥ 5, ≥ 6, and ≥ 7) showed that the proportion of participants classified as having “good” literacy remained consistently high (92–97%), and the association between knowledge level and physical activity behaviour was not statistically significant at any cutoff. Thus, the threshold of 6 was chosen as it provides a practical balance between inclusivity and discriminatory power, aligns with the literature, and ensures meaningful categorization for analysis within this highly knowledgeable sample. Sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds (≥ 5, ≥ 7) showed similar patterns of association (see Supplementary Table 2), supporting the robustness of our findings.

Ethical considerations include approval from the Gulf Medical University Institutional Review Board, participant consent, anonymity, and voluntary participation, ensuring no risk to participants. Methodologically, the study proceeded with IRB approval (IRB-COM-STD-103-May-2023), obtained permissions from facilities, and collected data via an online Google questionnaire form, aiming for feasibility given the short response time required, lack of harm to participants, and inclusivity across nationalities.

Data will be securely stored for three years and analysed using SPSS for descriptive and inferential statistics, with significance set at a p-value ≤ 0.05. To account for multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied by dividing the conventional significance level (0.05) by the number of chi-square tests performed (n = 11), resulting in an adjusted significance threshold of p < 0.0045. Effect sizes were reported using Cramér’s V. Cramér’s V values represent effect sizes for each association. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons (adjusted significance threshold p < 0.0045). Only associations with p-values below this threshold are considered statistically significant.

Knowledge about physical activity

Table 2 presents participants’ responses to knowledge-based statements regarding physical activity. The vast Majority demonstrated strong awareness of general health benefits: 96.8% recognized that regular physical activity improves overall health, 91% believed it helps prevent heart disease, and 94.3% identified its role in weight management. Discussion

This study assessed the level of physical activity literacy and its association with physical activity practices among adults in the United Arab Emirates. The findings revealed that 95% of participants demonstrated good knowledge about physical activity, and 97% reported engaging in some form of physical activity. As participants were recruited from fitness facilities, there is a high likelihood of overestimating physical activity engagement and literacy. This limits generalizability to the wider UAE adult population. However, detailed analysis of literacy and behaviour, stratified by sociodemographic characteristics, presents a nuanced understanding of the current landscape and offers meaningful implications for public health planning.

Interpretation of Findings.

A significant proportion of participants showed strong awareness of the benefits of physical activity, consistent with studies conducted in different populations, including physiotherapy students in Gujarat and medical students in Malaysia, where over 80–98% of participants had high levels of awareness [11, 12].

In comparison to this study’s high physical activity engagement rate (97%), previous research shows lower levels across various populations. For instance, a study on physicians in primary health centres in Makkah, Saudi Arabia, found that only 25.3% were active, while 24.1% were moderately inactive, and another 25.3% were moderately active [13]. Similarly, an observational study of healthcare students at King Saud University reported that 69.9% were active, with about 30% inactive [14]. Among elderly participants in Enugu Metropolis, Nigeria, only 53.7% reported engaging in physical fitness activities [15]. These findings suggest that while physical activity is common in Many populations, the proportion of active individuals in our study is notably higher. In all the aforementioned studies, although the Majority of participants reported engaging in physical activities, the percentage of those engaging in consistent physical fitness practices was notably lower compared to the current study. The findings of this study, where 97% of participants engage in physical activity, indicate a higher overall participation rate in physical fitness, which may be attributed to the study’s targeted population (adults using physical activity facilities). This suggests that access to dedicated fitness resources could play a critical role in encouraging regular engagement in physical activities.

Despite high activity levels, our participants frequencies varied. These findings indicate a relatively high level of physical engagement among adults in UAE and reflect a strong trend toward routine physical activity. When compared to studies conducted in different populations, our findings suggest notably higher activity levels. For instance, a study among elderly individuals in the Enugu Metropolis reported that only 38.3% of participants exercised more than three days a week, and 27.6% exercised more than thrice per week, accounting for a total of 65.9% engaging in moderate or higher levels of activity [15]. While this figure is comparable to our findings, the lower rates in the Enugu study could be attributed to the older age demographic, which naturally experiences more physical limitations compared to the younger, more active age group (18–34 years) that made up the majority of our sample.

Similarly, a study on physiotherapy students in Gujarat showed lower activity levels, with 67.7% exercising only twice per week and just 9% engaging in exercise more than three times weekly [11]. This suggests that only approximately 32.4% of participants were moderately or highly active—substantially lower than the 80% observed in our study. Additionally, while 55% of the Gujarat participants reported exercising regularly, 45% did not maintain a consistent routine. The differences may reflect the varying lifestyles, academic demands, and awareness levels between student populations and the broader adult demographic represented in our study.

Further supporting these comparisons, a study published in Medicina revealed that 44% of respondents exercised 1–2 times per week, 32.2% exercised 3–4 times per week, and just 7.1% exercised more than four times per week. Notably, 16.7% of respondents did not engage in any physical activity at all [16]. These figures again highlight the relatively high physical activity engagement observed in our study, suggesting more favourable practices among adults utilizing fitness facilities in UAE.

The differences across studies could stem from several factors, including age distribution, professional or student commitments, environmental support for physical activity, and awareness or literacy levels regarding exercise benefits. Our results, therefore, underscore the importance of contextual factors—such as age, environment, and health literacy—influencing physical activity behaviours.

In our study, the most practiced form of exercise among participants was muscle-strengthening activity, reported by 39%, followed closely by aerobic exercise (37%) and bone-strengthening activities (21%). These findings suggest a relatively balanced distribution of exercise types, with a notable emphasis on resistance training.

By contrast, a study conducted among healthcare students at King Saud University, Riyadh, revealed a different trend: aerobic exercises were most common, practiced by 58.5% of participants, followed by bone-strengthening activities (46.6%) and muscle-strengthening exercises, which were the least practiced at 25% [9]. This contrast suggests that while aerobic activity remains prevalent in both populations, the emphasis on muscle-strengthening activities is significantly higher in our study cohort.

This variation in exercise preference may be attributed to differences in population demographics and lifestyle contexts. The King Saud University sample consisted solely of students, who may face time constraints or limited access to gym facilities, which could hinder regular participation in muscle-strengthening routines. In comparison, our study population included adults from diverse occupational and age backgrounds, many of whom may have greater autonomy over their schedules or access to structured fitness facilities—potentially explaining the higher engagement in resistance training observed.

Furthermore, the higher proportion of participants engaging in muscle-strengthening activities in our study could also reflect growing awareness of its benefits for metabolic health, functional capacity, and injury prevention, particularly in an adult population that may prioritize long-term wellness over cardiovascular endurance alone. These differences underscore the importance of considering population context when designing physical activity interventions or public health campaigns.

Association between literacy levels and physical activity engagement

Our study indicates a significant positive correlation between participants’ literacy levels and their engagement in physical activity. This aligns with findings from previous research, underscoring the importance of literacy in promoting regular physical activity.

For instance, a study conducted among elderly individuals in Enugu Metropolis revealed that 60.5% of those with good knowledge of exercise engaged in regular physical activity, compared to only 35.4% among those with poor knowledge. This stark contrast highlights the critical role of knowledge in facilitating consistent exercise habits [15].

Similarly, research among Chinese undergraduates found a significant positive correlation between perceived physical literacy and various domains of physical activity, including moderate PA (r = 0.254), vigorous PA (r = 0.206), walking (r = 0.284), transportation (r = 0.220), housework (r = 0.218), and leisure time (r = 0.129) [17].

However, not all studies have observed a direct link between literacy and physical activity. For example, a study among medical students at the University of Cyberjaya found a significant positive correlation between knowledge and attitude (r = 0.407, p < 0.001), but no significant difference between knowledge and practice (r = 0.039, p = 0.528). This suggests that while knowledge may influence attitudes, it does not necessarily translate into increased physical activity [12].

These discrepancies may be attributed to various factors, including demographic differences, cultural contexts, and the specific methodologies employed in each study. For instance, the study among medical students focused on a specific academic group, which may not be representative of the general population. Additionally, the measurement tools and scales used to assess literacy and physical activity levels varied across studies, potentially influencing the observed associations.

Association between performing physical activity and sociodemographic characteristics

In our study, we found no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05) in physical activity levels across demographic variables such as age, gender, marital status, education level, and employment status. This suggests that the participants in our study, regardless of these sociodemographic characteristics, exhibited similar levels of physical activity, which might be indicative of a relatively homogeneous approach to PA within this population.

Interestingly, our findings diverge from a study conducted among healthcare students at a public university in Saudi Arabia, which revealed that gender significantly influenced PA practices. In that study, males were significantly more likely to engage in satisfactory PA practices compared to females, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.945 (95% CI: 1.207–3.135, p = 0.006). This finding contrasts with our study, where no significant difference was observed between males and females regarding their engagement in physical activity. Additionally, the Saudi Arabian study reported that younger individuals (aged 18–20 and 21–25) had higher odds of satisfactory PA practice compared to those over 25, although these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05) [18]. This is consistent with the results in our study, where no significant relationship was found between age and physical activity levels. Furthermore, Marital status in the Saudi study was not a significant determinant of PA, as single individuals were 39.5% less likely to engage in satisfactory physical activity compared to married individuals (OR = 0.605, p > 0.05) [18]. This aligns well with our own findings, where marital status did not significantly influence PA participation.

On the other hand, a contrasting study conducted among medical students at the University of Cyberjaya in Malaysia found that gender played a significant role in physical activity participation, but in an opposite trend. Females demonstrated significantly higher levels of physical activity (94.3%) compared to males (23.0%) [12].

These discrepancies in findings across different studies can be attributed to various factors such as cultural context, age groups, and the specific nature of the populations studied. The differences in cultural attitudes toward gender and physical activity, as well as the age range of the study participants, likely contribute to the varying patterns of PA engagement observed in these studies. While our study found no significant association between sociodemographic variables and PA, other studies have highlighted that gender and age can play a critical role in determining the likelihood of engaging in regular physical activity.

Implications for public health

This study highlights the importance of reinforcing accurate health messaging and tailoring interventions to specific demographic groups. Despite high overall literacy, specific misconceptions persist and may hinder optimal health outcomes. Public health strategies should not only raise awareness but also address motivational and environmental barriers to sustained activity.

Limitations

Key limitations of this study include the use of convenience sampling and self-reported data, which may introduce selection and recall bias. Due to the convenience sampling strategy and the absence of a fixed sampling frame, we were unable to report the number of facilities approached, the number of individuals declining participation, or a response rate. This limits the assessment of sample representativeness. As participants were recruited from fitness facilities, there is also a risk of social desirability bias, with individuals potentially overstating their knowledge or activity levels to align with perceived expectations. The use of Cramér’s V provided a standardized measure of association strength for all categorical comparisons. While Bonferroni correction effectively reduced the risk of false positives due to multiple testing, it may have increased the risk of Type II error, potentially overlooking weaker but meaningful associations. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Recommendations

Future interventions should consider targeting groups with lower literacy, such as employed males, and utilize objective monitoring tools (e.g., wearables) to better assess activity levels. Longitudinal studies are warranted to track behaviour change over time and assess the impact of public health campaigns.

Conclusion

This study highlights a generally high level of physical activity literacy and participation among adults attending fitness facilities in UAE. A substantial proportion of participants reported engaging in regular physical activity, particularly muscle-strengthening and aerobic exercises. While most respondents demonstrated good awareness of the benefits of physical activity, this knowledge did not always translate uniformly into consistent practices across all demographic groups. Interestingly, no significant associations were found between physical activity levels and sociodemographic variables, suggesting a broad cultural shift toward active lifestyles in this setting.

The findings support the need for tailored public health interventions that not only raise awareness but also address behavioural and systemic barriers to regular exercise. Future programs should emphasize translating knowledge into sustainable habits, especially among populations less likely to maintain regular physical activity. Furthermore, the positive relationship between physical literacy and engagement in physical activity underscores the importance of incorporating educational strategies in community and clinical health promotion initiatives.

Ultimately, this study advocates for the integration of physical activity education in public health strategies, reinforcing the importance of physical literacy in enhancing long-term health outcomes.
