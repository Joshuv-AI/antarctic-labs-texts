Introduction

The appendix, a vestigial organ is a small, tubelike structure that belongs to the midgut of the digestive tract system. The most common location of the appendix is retrocecal (74 %), followed by the pelvic region (21 %). Other locations include subcecal (1.5 %), preileal (1 %), and postileal (0.5 %) positions [1]. The appendix is very vulnerable to acute inflammatory processes, and acute appendicitis continues to be one of the most frequently encountered surgical emergencies in pediatrics and adults [2].The site of normally placed appendix and its classical presentation of appendicitis are well documented in the literature. However, the variations in the anatomical position of the appendix contribute to the difficulty in diagnosing appendicitis [2].

Appendicitis occurring in the subhepatic Appendix that is, subhepatic appendicitis (SHA) is largely uncommon, and it occurs as a result of intestinal malrotation and/or non-decent of the cecum during embryonic development [3]. Owing to its unusual site, subhepatic appendicitis presents a substantial challenge in its diagnosis and management as it can mimic upper abdominal conditions [3]. Diagnostic dilemma may arise in case there is abnormal location and atypical presentation which leads to significant challenge to diagnose and management, also cause higher complication and morbidity [4]. We wrote the case report following the SCARE guideline [5].

Case presentation

A 7-year- old, otherwise healthy child, presented to emergency department with an acute onset vague abdominal pain experienced for three days. The pain was associated with three episodes of vomiting of ingested matter, high grade intermittent fever and three episodes of loose stool. The patient was treated symptomatically in our hospital as acute gastroenteritis and dyspepsia discharged with Oral cotrimoxazole syrup 10 ml Po BID and Omeprazole 20 mg Bid for 7 days. Two days later, he presented again with moderate epigastric and right sided abdominal pain. The pain was crampy continuous, non-radiating and associated with loss of appetite and fever. On clinical examination, the patient was mildly dehydrated. The patient vital sign were BP 120/82 mmHg and PR of 106 beats/min, temperature 38.4 °C, RR 18 breath per minute and SaO2 98 % on room air. Examination of abdomen show tenderness without rebound and guarding present only in the right upper quadrant and epigastrium. The abdomen was not distended, there was no palpable mass and normal bowel sound present.

Blood investigation show total white count was elevated 14.5 × 109/l with predominantly neutrophilia of 87 %, hemoglobin was 14.7 g/dl and platelet were 265 × 109/l. Further ultrasound was performed that there is markedly inflamed and edematous mesentery at right lower quadrant of abdomen with loculated hypoechoic collection with internal debris seen measuring 2.3* 1.5 cm with surrounding hyperemia. It showed dilated non compressible appendix perforated at its base measuring 1.2 cm, with fecaliths seen in the middle of collection with multiple mesenteric Lymphadenopathies consistent with features suggestive of acute appendicitis. Exact location of appendix was not described.

After obtaining informed written consent, he was explored under general anesthesia with a diagnosis of acute abdomen secondary to acute appendicitis.

This patient underwent Open appendicectomy through Transverse Right lower quadrant incision which later was extended medially. During exploration, we noted that an abnormally high lying cecum below liver in the right hypochondrium; the ascending colon was short and dilated transverse colon was seen. The appendix was located immediately medial to the ileocecal junction craniocaudally with the tip trailing superiorly in Morison's pouch (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). It was acutely inflamed gangrenous at the tip with two fecaliths and healthy base (Fig. 2). Appendectomy and local mopping was done. The patient was started on intravenous ceftriaxone 50 mg/kg/day in divided dose twice daily, intravenous metronidazole 15 mg/kg/dose three times daily, and intravenous paracetamol 15 mg/kg/day three timed per day in the wards until discharge.

The patient had an uneventful recovery and was discharged home on the third postoperative day with Oral Augmentin Syrup 40 mg/kg/day given in divided dose three times daily for five days. The patient was seen in the outpatient clinic, on his 7th an 30th post operative days at which time he had good state of health with healed wound and he was in his usual state of health.

Discussion

Acute appendicitis remains one of the most common diseases treated by the general surgeon [6]. Because of abnormal location of subhepatic appendix and high up cecum its disease creates a lot of confusion in both diagnosis and surgical exploration which may lead in complication and delay in treatment. This case report demonstrates the same diagnosis dilemma and management. In 1955, King described the first case of subhepatic appendicitis and concluded that in such conditions the cecum remains in the subhepatic area [7]. Sub hepatically located appendix is very rare and to date only reported in 0.8 % of all appendicitis, which equals an annual incidence rate of approximately 0.09 per 100,000 population [2]. .Although the majority of reports mentioned in the literature discuss the incidence of subhepatic appendicitis in adults, only a few cases have been reported in the pediatric age group [3,8,9]. different review of literature which are reported before mentioned below (Table 1).

Subhepatic location of appendix may occur as appendix resides at right upper quadrant before caecum descends to right iliac fossa during eleventh week of fetal development [4]. In the classic presentation, the patient describes the onset of abdominal pain as the first symptom. The pain is typically periumbilical in nature with subsequent migration to the right lower quadrant as the inflammation progresses. Although considered a classic symptom, migratory pain occurs only in 50 to 60 % of patients with appendicitis. In SHA the symptoms are same except pain in upper abdomen; similarly, the tenderness and other palpating sign demonstrate the pathology in right upper quadrant which may mimic hepatobiliary or gastric pathology clinically. Such atypical presentation may lead to diagnostic dilemma that leads to diagnosis delayed and complication such as sepsis suppuration and perforation [7]. Therefore, radiologic investigations are important to support diagnosis. Ultrasound is the first-line investigation that preferred due to its easily available and eases to perform. It is the best modality to diagnose and to rule other differential diagnosis related. For example, in our case ultrasound finding show features suggestive of acute appendicitis. Since our case was readily diagnosed by abdominal sonography, we did not contemplate using CT scan study, which entails additional cost and radiation. In advanced set up, unlike ours where CT scan of abdomen and pelvis is readily available it can also provide high sensitivity (100 %), specificity (95 %), and accuracy (98 %) in identifying acute appendicitis [16].

Broadly speaking, there are two main surgical approaches, namely open and laparoscopic appendectomy. Accumulating evidence from earlier studies demonstrated superiority for the laparoscopic appendectomy over the open appendectomy [2,17]. The laparoscopic appendectomy approach yields numerous advantages, such as reduced incidence of postoperative ileus, lower frequency of surgical wound infection, shorter duration of hospitalization, lesser utility of postoperative pain killers, decreased occurrence of adhesions, minimal body mutilation and faster recovery to previous status of well-being [17]. We, the authors, are in favor of the laparoscopic appendectomy approach too. However, the reason for not performing a laparoscopic appendectomy in our case was attributable to the lack of laparoscopic facility and available expertise in our healthcare center.

Conclusion

In conclusion, subhepatic appendicitis is a unique and rare presentation, making its diagnosis and management challenging. Surgeons must be aware of this atypical presentation and how patients can present late due to considering other possible nonsurgical causes such as gastritis. Due to unusual location of appendix in sub hepatic region, its diagnosis may delay or can create confusion in case of appendicitis. High degree of suspicion, good knowledge and higher imaging modalities can aid in diagnosis and results in earlier management.
