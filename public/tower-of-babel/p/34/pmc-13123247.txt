Pilea: profiling bacterial growth dynamics from metagenomes with sketching
Authors: Xi Chen, Xiaoqing Xu, Yunqi Lin, Xianghui Shi, Dou Wang, Tong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundQuantifying bacteria’s growth rates is essential for understanding their ecological roles and for building predictive models in environmental and clinical settings. Peak-to-trough ratios (PTRs) derived from shotgun metagenomes offer a culture-independent proxy for in situ growth rates of bacterial species, yet their reliable computation remains challenging.ResultsWe introduce Pilea (https://github.com/xinehc/pilea), an alignment-free, sketching-based method that incorporates statistical models for robust PTR estimation. Pilea achieves speed improvements over existing methods while also enhancing accuracy, as demonstrated on both simulated and real datasets.ConclusionsBy scaling efficiently to comprehensive reference collections such as the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB), Pilea enables large-scale analyses of bacterial growth dynamics across biomes, unlocking new insights for ecological research. Introduction

Bacteria grow at the microscale, and the rates at which they do so shape the structure and function of microbial communities [1]. Accurately characterizing these dynamics is essential for interpreting their physiological status (metabolic states) and understanding how they interact with each other and with their environments [2, 3]. Yet, profiling the growth dynamics of individual species within complex communities remains technically difficult [4]. While time-series measurements combined with absolute quantification can reveal shifts in community composition [5], their temporal resolution is often limited by how frequently samples can be collected [6, 7]. In many settings, repeated sampling may not be feasible at all [8].

A promising alternative that sidesteps the sampling frequency constraint is to infer growth dynamics from sequencing coverage patterns [9]. Since most bacterial species replicate their chromosome bidirectionally from a fixed origin to a fixed terminus [10], the resulting difference in sequencing coverage between these two loci, known as the peak-to-trough ratio (PTR), reflects population-level replication activity and therefore growth dynamics [9]. This approach enables researchers to extract single-time-point snapshots of bacterial growth directly from metagenomic datasets, even allowing retrospective analysis of growth dynamics from archived, historical samples [7, 11–13]. PTRs have been associated with disease status (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease and type II diabetes) [9, 14] and applied in ecological contexts to assess community responses to soil disturbance and ocean depth [12, 15].

Several tools (e.g., CoPTR [14], DEMIC [15], GRiD [16], and iRep [17]) exist for PTR estimation. Their implementations differ, but all rely on aligning sequencing reads to reference genomes to compute genome-wide coverage, a step that can easily become a computational bottleneck for large-scale studies involving either deep sequencing or thousands of reference genomes [18]. To address this issue, we developed Pilea, an alignment‑free, sketching‑based method that profiles the growth dynamics of bacterial populations from (meta)genomic samples. Pilea incorporates robust methods to mitigate noise caused by, e.g., inter- and intraspecific repeats—a known confounder in PTR estimation. In our tests on a 100 Gbp marine dataset, Pilea profiled against 136,646 GTDB [19] species representatives in under ten minutes using 32 CPU threads, while also demonstrating improved accuracy over existing tools. This scalability and robustness enable extensive surveys of bacterial growth dynamics across biomes, facilitating downstream applications such as ecological modeling [20] and environmental monitoring [21].

Each non-overlapping window corresponds to a segment of the genome. In principle, with a suitably small window size, a single ZTP distribution should suffice for representing the window-level \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document}k-mer counts, even for rapidly replicating bacteria [23] (Supplementary Fig. S1). In practice, however, sequencing biases, read errors, genomic repeats, and cross-species homologies all inflate variance and introduce multimodality, making a single ZTP inadequate [24]. To mitigate this, Pilea adopts a mixture of ZTP distributions for count modeling, retaining only the dominant component (i.e., the one with the highest mixing weight) for each window and assigning its rate parameter as the window’s expected coverage. Once computed for all windows, the selected rates are sorted, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{log}}_{2}$$\end{document}log2-transformed, and fitted against their indices using random sample consensus (RANSAC) [25] linear regressors for robust PTR estimation. Pilea is motivated by recent developments of sketching-based taxonomic profiling tools, including sourmash [22] and sylph [26].

To test Pilea’s performance in real-world settings, we ran it on the marine dataset from Long et al. [4]. This dataset included metagenomic sequencing of surface water at 4–5 timepoints over two days, along with growth rates for 98 bacterial metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) calculated via cell-counting-based absolute quantification. Although Pilea provided PTR estimates for only a small number of MAGs (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=18$$\end{document}n=18), its \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{log}}_{2}({\mathrm{PTR}})$$\end{document}log2(PTR) estimates showed the highest median correlation with the observed growth rates (Fig. 3g). Of these 18 MAGs, only two exhibited negative correlations (Supplementary Figs. S7 and S8). While other tools might return more estimates, their median correlations were close to zero or even negative. To investigate whether Pilea provides more accurate estimates or simply filters out hard-to-estimate MAGs more effectively, we performed pairwise comparisons via two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on the shared set of MAGs. When using GTDB bacterial representatives (R226, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=136,646$$\end{document}n=136,646) as references, we obtained 166 PTR estimates (previously 294 estimates), with only a moderate increase in computational time and peak memory usage (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Discussion

Pilea offers improvements in both speed and accuracy compared to existing PTR inference tools. Owing to its alignment-free design, PTR estimation can be fast, even for large reference collections containing over one hundred thousand genomes, such as GTDB species representatives. Its implementation of GC-bias correction and adoption of statistical models for coverage computation also yield more reliable PTR estimates. Conceptually, Pilea can be viewed as a taxonomic profiler that not only identifies the species present in a sample and quantifies their abundances (genome copies), but also reports on their growth status.

Although PTRs reflect growth status, it is important to note that they serve only as proxies for true growth rates and are theoretically linked to them via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{log}}_{2}({\mathrm{PTR}})=\frac{C}{\tau }$$\end{document}log2(PTR)=Cτ (where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C$$\end{document}C represents the C-period of the cell cycle and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}τ the generation time) [9, 14, 27]. Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C$$\end{document}C depends on genome size and DNA replication speed (both of which vary by species), PTRs are not directly comparable across distantly related taxa. In the E. coli experiment, we demonstrated that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{log}}_{2}$$\end{document}log2-transformed PTRs and OD-based growth rate estimates are linearly correlated, and consequently \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C$$\end{document}C can be approximated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C\approx 0.6032+0.2948\tau$$\end{document}C≈0.6032+0.2948τ (in hours). Our four-species growth experiment also corroborated this linearity, but translating PTRs into growth rates across taxa would still require data from large-scale cultivation to support the prediction of species-specific C-period parameters from genomic features via e.g., machine learning. We leave this for future work.

While Pilea works well with both complete-genome and contig-level assemblies, it performs best when reference genomes are of high quality and contain relatively few contigs. However, MAGs derived from short-read metagenomics are often highly fragmented, which can impact Pilea’s accuracy. To mitigate this, Pilea allows users to supply either (1) their own assembled MAGs or (2) a pre-built GTDB database of species representatives as references. We show that relying solely on assembled MAGs or the pre-built GTDB database does not markedly increase computational time—a feature that distinguishes Pilea from other alignment-based PTR inference tools. In addition, assembled MAGs can be easily integrated into the pre-built GTDB database or merged with other context-specific genome catalogs, such as SMAG [34] (soil), GOMC [35] (marine), and UHGG [36] (human gut), to enable more comprehensive PTR profiling.

One potential limitation of Pilea lies in its inherent reliance on a 95% ANI cutoff for species identification. For wild samples where uncharacterized “dark matter” lacking close references dominates, advanced strategies such as amino-space translation (e.g., Metabuli [37]) or adaptive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document}k-mer sketching (e.g., sourmash [22]) may improve the detection of distantly related organisms. However, incorporating these features often involves a trade-off between sensitivity and computational complexity. Instead, for samples that are expected to contain novel taxa, we recommend assembling them even if the resulting MAGs may be fragmented, and utilizing Pilea’s flexible database integration to augment these sample-specific MAGs with standard reference collections. As assembly-based workflows become increasingly routine in metagenomic studies, this strategy allows Pilea to maintain sensitivity without sacrificing speed.

Due to its alignment-free implementation, Pilea is also constrained by a requirement for higher coverage than alignment-based methods to achieve reliable PTR estimation. This is particularly true for third-generation long reads (Oxford Nanopore or PacBio), where coverage distortions arising from size selection during library preparation and post-sequencing length filtering complicate PTR estimation by violating the V-shaped origin-to-terminus coverage assumption (Supplementary Fig. S11). Consequently, Pilea implements a default 5 × coverage cutoff for short reads (estimated via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document}k-mer counts) and currently does not support long reads. Like other methods, Pilea may also perform suboptimally for complex communities where multiple closely related strains grow at different rates, especially if the provided references contain collapsed repeats that hinder the effective masking of non-unique \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document}k-mers. Nevertheless, given the ever-expanding reference collections and the vast amount of data archived in resources like NCBI SRA, we believe Pilea’s efficient scalability holds the potential to enable the determination of growth dynamics across diverse organisms and environments, facilitating comprehensive ecological analyses of both existing and future (meta)genomic datasets.

Benchmark

Escherichia coli dataset

We retrieved 45 Escherichia coli raw-read sequencing samples from NCBI SRA under BioProject ID PRJNA615952. Three samples lacking growth measurements (run-out experiments) were excluded. The remaining samples spanned 16 growth conditions, and their nutrient-imposed growth rates were taken from Zheng et al. [27]. We applied Pilea v1.3.6, CoPTR [14] v1.1.6, GRiD [16] v1.3, and iRep [17] v1.1.14 to this dataset using the complete-genome assembly GCF_000005845.2 as the reference. DEMIC [15] v1.0.2 failed to return estimates if the reference was complete, so we instead used a contig-level assembly (GCF_028618655.1). Simulation experiment

We recorded the names of all bacterial species listed in NCBI Pathogen (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pathogens/organisms/) and retrieved their corresponding reference assemblies from RefSeq. Species lacking complete-genome assemblies were excluded, resulting in a total of 120 assemblies (119 species, with Escherichia coli having two reference assemblies). Marine dataset

We downloaded 20 marine raw-read sequencing samples from NCBI SRA under BioProject ID PRJNA551656, and 101 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from figshare (10.6084/m9.figshare.9730628). The observed growth rates were obtained directly from Long et al. [4]. We discarded negative growth rates and included only MAGs that had more than three pairs of observed growth rates and PTRs. All tools were run with default parameters. Computational time and peak memory usage were recorded using GNU time (‘gtime -v’) on an M3 Ultra (32 cores) Mac Studio. To ensure a cold cache state, we executed ‘purge’ before each computational benchmark.
