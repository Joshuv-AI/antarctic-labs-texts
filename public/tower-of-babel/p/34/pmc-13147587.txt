The role of innovative financing to bridge the gap between implementation and commercialisation
Authors: Tristana Perez, Robert Kelly, Dominic Hein, Karuna Luthra, Ariane McCabe, Dennis Di Mascio, An Vermeesch, Jonathan Hutchins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The deployment of novel medical products requires more than one party willing and able to shoulder the risk of failure. While GSK’s RTS,S/AS01E malaria vaccine received a positive scientific opinion from the European Medicines Agency, further evidence was needed for the World Health Organization (WHO) to be willing to recommend the vaccine for broad use. This increased uncertainty around whether WHO would ultimately issue a recommendation and when and who might buy vaccines to enable the wider scale-up to occur. With no large-scale demand outside of Gavi-financed markets, GSK was faced with a decision whether to continue production of the RTS,S antigen. Creative risk-sharing solutions can ensure and accelerate access to medical products, particularly where risks are intolerably high for key actors. Innovative finance providers deploy financial products to share risk and address these breaks in the product development and deployment pathway. To support continued manufacturing of the RTS,S antigen while waiting for a WHO policy recommendation and Gavi funding decision, MedAccess, Gavi, and GSK structured a risk-sharing arrangement. Sustained vaccine manufacturing due to the MedAccess-Gavi-GSK innovative financing agreement, was projected to result in 8.7M malaria cases averted and 36.3k deaths averted in children. Available capital can be deployed in creative ways, tailored to the individual needs of the market and perceived risks to achieve health benefits in the populations with the greatest need. Gaps in risk coverage created by changing timelines or market failures can be identified in advance so that innovative financing can be secured sooner rather than later. The partnership between WHO, MedAccess, Gavi, and GSK to support RTS,S/AS01E demonstrates the ability of the global health community to develop creative risk-sharing solutions that can help to support timely access to new innovations for populations in need.

Problem statement & standard approach

The development and deployment of novel medical products requires more than one party willing and able to shoulder the risk of failure [1]. To name a few, researchers and product developers take the risk that effective products may not emerge, policymakers take the risk that products may not be as safe or cost-effective as expected, and suppliers take the risk that investments in development of products and scale up of manufacturing will not be aligned with demand.

Even when a product completes clinical trials, data may be insufficient to inform programme implementation in specific countries. This can contribute to a highly uncertain view of the future and increased risk for product suppliers, particularly in lower income markets and where there are no clear alternatives through which to offset risks. Policy makers, funders, and implementing countries with varying levels of risk appetite may be unwilling to take the next step, such as publishing policy recommendations for use or allocating budget to purchase the product, instead opting for further evidence generation or programmatic activities aimed at mitigating risk. This creates a ripple effect which increases the level of uncertainty and risk for others along the value chain.

RTS,S/AS01E, the world’s first malaria vaccine, developed by GSK in partnership with PATH and other research institutions, received a positive scientific opinion from the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in July 2015. The Phase 3 programme to evaluate the RTS,S/AS01E malaria vaccine included a large-scale efficacy and safety trial which enrolled more than 15,000 infants and young children across seven sub-Saharan African countries. Based on the evidence available at the time, the agency found that the quality of the vaccine and its risk-benefit profile for users were favourable from a regulatory perspective. However, it is not the EMA’s remit to assess wider contextual elements or implementation feasibility relevant to sub-Saharan African settings where malaria burden is highest and the vaccine would be delivered [2].

For the World Health Organization (WHO) to be willing to recommend this vaccine for broad use in African countries and be comfortable with the risk-benefit profile in these contexts, further evidence generated by an implementation pilot was first needed, the first and only time to date a programme of this scale was required prior to recommendation [3]. By 2016, open questions remained regarding the feasibility of implementing a vaccine with a four-dose schedule in children, the value of the vaccine within the context of other deployed malaria control measures such as seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) and long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), and safety and cost-effectiveness of routine use within these settings [3]. To address concerns around safety and cost-effectiveness, a pilot implementation programme was designed to assess the feasibility of administering the recommended four doses of the vaccine in children; the vaccine’s potential role in reducing childhood deaths; and its safety in the context of routine use [4].

Four years following the positive EMA opinion, the Malaria Vaccine Implementation Programme (MVIP) began in 2019. The MVIP was coordinated by the WHO, funded by Gavi, the Global Fund, and Unitaid, and implemented by Ministries of Health in three sub-Saharan African countries–Ghana, Kenya and Malawi. The vaccine was introduced through routine immunisation programmes in selected areas of moderate-to-high malaria transmission in these countries. GSK committed to donate 10 million doses for use in the MVIP. The MVIP was scheduled to finish in 2023, some eight years after the EMA’s positive opinion [4].

The MVIP functioned to generate additional data to enable a potential WHO policy recommendation for broad use of the vaccine. These were necessary to ensure countries would be able to introduce the malaria vaccine and achieve high coverage. Gavi, which is the main funder of malaria vaccine programmes at scale and was expected to support RTS,S/AS01E procurement on behalf of African countries, required a WHO policy recommendation and WHO prequalification of the vaccine in order to consider and approve financial support for a malaria vaccine programme.1 Without Gavi funding, RTS,S/AS01E could not be deployed at scale [5]. As a result, the recommendation for a large-scale pilot implementation programme had wider effects along the pathway to commercialisation and unintentionally created uncertainties and risks for other stakeholders.

The decision to gather additional data increased uncertainty around whether WHO would ultimately issue a recommendation for broad use of the vaccine and when and who might buy vaccines to enable the wider scale-up to occur. GSK manufactures the RTS,S antigen in a facility that is fully dedicated to this vaccine and cannot be repurposed for other vaccine production. Once GSK had manufactured 10 million donation doses for the MVIP, with no Gavi decision to procure the vaccine and no large-scale demand foreseen outside of Gavi-financed markets, GSK was faced with a decision whether to continue production of the RTS,S antigen without visibility on a potential buyer for the vaccine. Shutting down and potentially later restarting a manufacturing facility after a WHO policy decision and a Gavi funding decision would have resulted in additional costs and time needed to re-hire and train dedicated workers for the manufacturing facility, re-start and ramp up production of the antigen. These additional steps following a Gavi funding decision would have added significant time to the timeline of when implementing countries would have received malaria vaccines.

New perspective

Innovative finance providers deploy financial products to share risk and address breaks in the product development and deployment pathway, including those caused by market uncertainties that prevent medical innovations from reaching people quickly. While a standardised and recognisable innovative finance toolkit is growing, tailored innovative finance solutions can also be developed on a case-by-case basis to meet particular and specific challenges that arise. These are often bespoke and neither replicable nor scalable. In designing these tailored solutions with companies, global health organisations, governments and civil society, all of whom contribute towards their success, innovative finance providers are often also playing a convening role.

Creative risk-sharing solutions can be used to ensure and accelerate widespread access to medical products, particularly in markets where risks are too high compared to the rewards for the key actors in development, production, supply, or procurement. As different stakeholders take on various facets of risk, new actors willing to share and tolerate different risk types are critical to align incentives and help bridge the gap between timing of clinical trial conclusion, commercialisation and market uptake. What is innovative financing for health?Innovative finance in health markets includes a range of financial solutions and mechanisms that can be used to share the financial risk of uptake and demand for a medical product, bridging the gap between supply and demand. Innovative financing tools have been used to support and shape global health markets over the past few decades, and include mechanisms such as advanced market commitments, prizes, and guarantees.Innovative Financing Tools for Health Markets ExamplesAdvance Market Commitment (AMC): Commitment to purchase agreed volumes of product that meets the target product profile at an agreed price, potentially from a range of suppliers.Advance Purchase Commitment (APC): Advance payment and commitment to purchase a minimum volume of a product from a supplier at an agreed price, if conditions are met, including licensing and regulatory approval. Prize: Financial reward to innovator for achieving a pre-determined research and development outcome. Volume Guarantee: Guarantee of minimum sales to supplier in return for lower prices and other commitments to meet projected demand. Procurement Guarantee: Guarantee to intermediate buyer, aggregator, or agent of products to cover the risk of failure within the procurement process, often over longer-term contracts, without which the supply or procurement of such products would not be possible.

Within the vaccine space, Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, has been deploying innovative financing tools since its inception in 2000. Launched in 2009, Gavi was responsible for a $1.5bn AMC for pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) that resulted in widescale introductions and massive scale-up for Gavi-supported countries, dramatic tail-price reductions and eventually the entrance of a third WHO-Prequalified lower-priced supplier [6]. The AMC also generated important learnings such as the challenge of agreeing an appropriate price when the purchaser has little or no information about costs. Gavi also engaged in an APC transaction with Merck/MSD that locked in availability of investigational doses of Ebola vaccine to be used in outbreak settings prior to final licensure, leading to the early establishment of a market and incentivising the developer to license the product [7]. This also ultimately led to the current global stockpile of licensed & WHO-Prequalified Ebola vaccines, against Ebola Zaire.

MedAccess uses its available $170 million paid-in capital from British International Investment to provide innovative financing when suppliers are prevented from upscaling on the basis of perceived risk within the target market. MedAccess is the only organisation established solely to use innovative finance products, such as guarantees, to mitigate the market risks that prevent people from accessing health products. Guarantees help spread the potential risk of a loss due to lower-than-expected demand. By being willing to take on the possibility of loss, MedAccess can facilitate suppliers to move forward, entering new markets, expanding capacity, lowering prices and improving availability. To date, MedAccess has provided volume guarantees across multiple disease areas and product types, including next-generation malaria long-lasting insecticide-treated nets, HIV diagnostics and tuberculosis preventative treatment. MedAccess has also provided procurement guarantees to support the purchase of COVID-19 supplies and COVID-19 vaccines.

Much of the value of innovative financing comes from the ability to tailor agreements to address the needs of all stakeholders taking risk and the uncertainties of various market dynamics.

Vaccine development and deployment involve particular risks which innovative finance can play a role in bridging. Vaccine development is a difficult and labour-intensive process that requires years of investment. In the case of malaria, a parasitic disease, prevention through vaccination is a particularly complex. RTS,S/AS01E emerged as the first promising candidate but as with any breakthrough innovation, carried inherent uncertainty.

In this instance, the risk that required bridging was one of a single market with limited options to offset risks and timing, where decisions on manufacturing investment and scale up were required before sufficient evidence was available to provide confidence for recommendation and wider deployment. In such situations, novel financing structures can be tailored to cover unusual risks and enable all stakeholders to continue moving forward rather than pausing until uncertainty is removed.

The innovative finance support for the RTS,S/AS01E malaria vaccine can be characterised as an example of a bespoke solution, illustrating how aligned partners with complementary expertise and resources have significant potential to find new solutions to unprecedented problems and supply unmet demand.

Key evidence

Note that here we focus on the derisking of antigen manufacturing because the adjuvant was not a limiting factor for GSK supply. In 2022, GSK publicly committed to double AS01 supply from 15 to 30 million doses for the malaria vaccine: GSK announces £1 billion research and development investment over 10 years to get ahead of infectious diseases in lower-income countries | GSK. To support continued manufacturing of the RTS,S antigen and ensure no delay in future access to the vaccine, MedAccess, Gavi, and GSK structured a tailored risk-sharing arrangement. Gavi agreed under a bilateral contract with GSK to extend funding to cover working capital necessary to continue production of RTS,S/AS01E on the condition that MedAccess in turn would provide financial support to Gavi under a separate, back-to-back agreement. Under this separate arrangement MedAccess would minimise losses to Gavi in the event the vaccine could not be rolled-out and utilised by Gavi-supported countries. Gavi and MedAccess entered into an antigen production support agreement, a unique innovative financing product at a critical point [8].

While funding for the MVIP had been secured, the innovative financing agreement helped unlock additional funding from Gavi for ongoing antigen production by protecting actors from a total loss in the worst-case scenario of a negative decision for the broad use of RTS,S/AS01E [9]. This risk sharing mechanism was an important enabler of ongoing manufacturing of RTS,S/AS01E while waiting for a WHO policy recommendation and a Gavi malaria vaccine programme funding decision. It ensured vaccine doses were available when Gavi-supported countries were ready to start the wider introduction and scale-up of RTS,S/AS01E after successful applications to the newly opened funding window. Ongoing manufacturing was also critical for the product transfer to Bharat Biotech of India, and in parallel allowed for the continuation of manufacturing process improvements in a production facility that until that point had only manufactured a few million antigen doses.

MedAccess assesses both the public health and cost benefits of widespread product uptake, alongside the potential financial risk to manufacturers of limited demand when considering whether its innovative financing can address a key barrier to access [10]. This analysis draws from both the clinical evidence and a commercial market assessment, enabling MedAccess to make independent decisions regarding risk types.

When MedAccess undertakes this assessment, the potential impact of innovative financing tools is also contextualised by comparing against a counterfactual scenario where no intervention is provided, in order to estimate the impact of MedAccess’ contribution.

Through the partnership between MedAccess, Gavi and GSK, the vaccine doses supported by these agreements will contribute to an estimated additional 7.5M children fully vaccinated with RTS,S/AS01E by ensuring vaccine doses were available at the time wider scale-up and introduction beyond the MVIP countries began. For these projections, MedAccess modelled out vaccine availability scenarios based on discussions with Gavi, GSK, WHO and other stakeholders. This model factored in how a pause in production and the timing of key decisions, such as a WHO recommendation and positive Gavi funding decision, might affect supply and therefore access once wider roll-out might begin. In the scenario where production was not paused thanks to the partnership, an initial stockpile of doses would be produced by the time of a Gavi funding decision, which would facilitate a faster roll-out. In the scenarios where the partnership did not ensure continued production and therefore production ramp-up was restarted following the Gavi funding decision, insufficient/delayed supply was projected, thus delaying vaccine roll-out compared to the innovative financing scenario. Those children older than 12 months who would have benefitted from earlier roll-out were considered to miss out on the benefits of vaccination with RTS,S/AS01E. Ensuring availability as soon as possible increases the number of total children vaccinated.

On the basis of interim data from the MVIP, WHO issued its recommendation for use of RTS,S/AS01E in October 2021 [11]. Gavi opened a funding window weeks later, enabling the market for RTS,S/AS01E vaccine to become manifest [12]. Countries were able to start planning almost immediately for introductions because there were no interruptions in manufacturing and therefore doses were available for allocation under WHO’s RTS,S framework once country applications had been approved, instead of having to wait for production to restart. By taking on some of the risk in decision-making, MedAccess helped ensure continued production of the RTS,S antigen by GSK and reduced the gap in access when wider roll-out began.

Drawing from evidence generated by clinical trials and the MVIP, it is estimated that the sustained vaccine manufacturing due to the MedAccess-Gavi-GSK innovative financing agreement, is projected to result in an 8.7M malaria cases averted and 36.3k deaths averted in children [13].

Conclusions

Available capital can be deployed in creative ways, tailored to the individual needs of the market and the risk others are unable to undertake to achieve the positive health benefits in the populations with the greatest need. Innovative financing to support risk-sharing across the product development and deployment pathway can flex to fill the shape of the gap and potentially function more efficiently than finite sources of traditional grant capital. When deployed in creative ways, innovative financing can help public health funding go further and plug financial gaps to maintain essential programmes where no alternative exists.

Early involvement of innovative financing partners can help achieve public health objectives that may otherwise be under threat because they are either too risky, too costly, or time-consuming. Gaps in risk coverage created by changing timelines or other market failures can be identified in advance so that innovative financing can be secured sooner rather than later.

Early alignment on the policy, regulatory and funding decisions along the pathway to market would enable suppliers and stakeholders to make appropriate investment decisions in research and development and manufacturing. The WHO recommendation and Gavi funding window decisions, eventually provided certainty that malaria vaccines could be procured, thus mitigating manufacturers’ risk and facilitating decision-making for others in the value chain, including potential new market entrants. The innovative financing agreements between MedAccess, Gavi, and GSK allowed partners and countries to move forward with planning for the roll-out of the malaria vaccine as soon as the WHO recommendation and Gavi Board approval were in place, paving the way for an accelerated roll-out.

With greater certainty around supply and additional data generated by the MVIP, countries are able to plan and make more informed decisions regarding how and when to roll-out the vaccine to maximise effectiveness. With greater certainty regarding demand, suppliers can more easily make decisions regarding entry into the malaria vaccine market, applying for regulatory approval, and increasing production capacity.

The partnership between WHO, MedAccess, Gavi, and GSK to support RTS,S/AS01E demonstrates the ability of the global health community to develop creative risk sharing solutions that support accelerated access to new innovations for populations in need. It is important to have these de-risking tools available to support the scale up of manufacturing and ensure the availability of timely supply.
