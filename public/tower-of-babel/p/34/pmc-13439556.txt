A pathogenic alpha synuclein variant exacerbates disease progression in a neuron-specific Gba-KO mouse☆
Authors: Hannah BD Duffy, Colleen Byrnes, Hongling Zhu, Y Terry Lee, Samantha Olmsted, Galina Tuymetova, Sydney N Pettit, Frances M Platt, Richard L Proia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGBA variants are among the most significant genetic risk factors for synucleinopathies including Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies. The GBA gene encodes the lysosomal enzyme glucocerebrosidase (GBA), which is essential for glycosphingolipid catabolism. There is a reciprocal relationship between GBA and α-synuclein (α-syn), in which reduced GBA levels lead to elevated α-syn. To further explore this connection specifically within neurons in vivo, we have introduced a human pathogenic variant of α-syn, A53T, into a neuron-specific Gba-KO mouse. This double variant mouse exhibited a reduced lifespan and more pronounced weight loss relative to the neuron-specific Gba-KO mouse, demonstrating a faster disease course and more severe phenotype. Additionally, their brains showed elevated levels of glucosylceramide and phosphorylated α-syn, while glucosylsphingosine levels were unchanged. Pathogenic α-syn had a greater effect on lifespan when combined with the neuron-specific Gba-KO than with KOs in Hexa or Hexb, genes linked to Tay-Sachs and Sandhoff disease, respectively. This model reveals that pathogenic α-syn amplifies GBA-related disease mechanisms within neurons, leading to an earlier and more severe disease course than with deficiencies in other glycosphingolipid-degrading enzymes.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, Gaucher disease, Glucocerebrosidase, Glucosylceramide, Glucosylsphingosine, Alpha synuclein, Synucleinopathy

Introduction

Glucocerebrosidase (GBA) is a lysosomal enzyme essential for sphingolipid catabolism, cleaving glucose from glucosylceramide (GlcCer). Homozygous GBA variants that reduce GBA activity cause Gaucher disease (GD), the most prevalent lysosomal storage disease. With insufficient GBA activity, GD patients accumulate GlcCer and its degradation product glucosylsphingosine (GlcSph) (Aerts et al., 2019; Kaye et al., 1986; Nilsson et al., 1985; Nilsson and Svennerholm, 1982). Gba variants and conditional Gba knockouts have been used to model GD in mice with varying degrees of utility (Duffy et al., 2024; Enquist et al., 2007; Enquist et al., 2006; Liu et al., 1998; Mistry et al., 2010; Mizukami et al., 2002; Xu et al., 2003). GD type I is non-neuronopathic and can be treated with intravenous enzyme replacement therapy. Those with GD type I present with hepatosplenomegaly, anemia, cytopenia, and bone disease (Aerts et al., 2019; Grabowski, 2008; Stirnemann et al., 2017). GD types II and III are neuronopathic. GD type II typically results in hepatosplenomegaly, opisthotonos, bulbar signs, oculomotor paralysis, apnea, and myoclonic epilepsy (Grabowski, 2008; Stirnemann et al., 2017). Life expectancy is about one year (Stirnemann et al., 2017). GD type III is associated with hepatosplenomegaly, anemia, cytopenia, bone disease, kyphosis, ophthalmoplegia, hydrocephaly, cerebellar ataxia, myoclonic epilepsy, and dementia (Stirnemann et al., 2017). Patients with GD type III can live into early adulthood (Grabowski, 2008). The neurological complications associated with GD are resistant to current treatments.

In the 1990s, GD type 1 patients were discovered to occasionally develop Parkinson’s-like symptoms (Neudorfer et al., 1996; Tayebi et al., 2001). Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurological disease characterized by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and the accumulation of α-synuclein-(α-syn)-rich inclusions known as Lewy bodies. PD is associated with bradykinesia, a resting tremor, postural instability, and rigidity. Some of these symptoms have also been described in GD patients (Neudorfer et al., 1996; Tayebi et al., 2001). Further examination revealed that, compared to the general population, a higher proportion of those with GD had relatives with PD (Goker-Alpan et al., 2004; Tayebi et al., 2003), implicating heterozygous GBA variants in PD. GBA variants are now recognized as one of the most significant genetic risk factors for PD, conferring a greater risk than genes such as LRRK2, PINK1, and DJ-I (Neumann et al., 2009; Sidransky et al., 2009). GBA variants are also linked to dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) (Goker-Alpan et al., 2006; Nalls et al., 2013), another synucleinopathy, underscoring the broader impact of GBA on neurodegenerative diseases.

GBA and α-syn appear to have a reciprocal relationship. When GBA was reduced both in vitro and in vivo, α-syn increased (Cleeter et al., 2013; Manning-Bog et al., 2009; Mazzulli et al., 2011; Rocha et al., 2015b; Rockenstein et al., 2016). Conversely, increasing GBA activity in mice overexpressing α-syn or in Gba-variant mice, reduced α-syn (Rockenstein et al., 2016; Sardi et al., 2011). Moreover, knocking out the α-syn gene, Snca, resulted in increased GBA activity (Fishbein et al., 2014), and overexpressing α-syn reduced GBA activity in vitro (Mazzulli et al., 2011; Yang et al., 2016). GBA activity is reduced in both idiopathic PD and PD-GBA (Alcalay et al., 2015; Chiasserini et al., 2015; Gegg et al., 2012; Parnetti et al., 2017; Rocha et al., 2015), although SNCA variants appear to have minimal or no impact on GBA activity in mouse models (Fishbein et al., 2014; Rockenstein et al., 2016; Tayebi et al., 2017).

While the mechanisms underlying the relationship between α-syn and GBA are not well understood, a prevailing hypothesis suggests the two may impact each other by means of a positive feedback loop in which alterations in one component exacerbate changes in the other (Mazzulli et al., 2011). Proposed explanations for the reciprocal relationship between GBA and α-syn include both gain-of-function and loss-of-function effects resulting from GBA variants (Siebert et al., 2014).

In this study, we investigated the combined effects of neuronal GBA deficiency and pathogenic α-syn by introducing the SNCA (A53T) variant into a neuron-specific Gba-KO mouse (Duffy et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2002). A tamoxifen-inducible neuron-specific Cre system was used to avoid the perinatal lethality exhibited by global Gba knockout mice and to directly investigate the in vivo interaction between neuronal GBA loss and α-synuclein (Sinclair et al., 2007). The addition of this pathogenic SNCA variant accelerated disease progression, shortening the lifespan and enhancing disease pathology of the neuron-specific Gba KO. These double-variant mice exhibited elevated levels of GlcCer and phosphorylated α-syn in the brain, and showed transcriptional changes that were largely similar to Gba KO mice. Pathogenic α-syn had a greater effect on lifespan when combined with GBA deficiency than with deficiencies in Hexa or Hexb, genes linked to Tay-Sachs and Sandhoff disease, respectively. This novel neuron-targeted double-variant model indicates that pathogenic α-syn amplifies preexisting GBA-related disease mechanisms in neurons, driving an earlier onset and more severe disease course.

Results

Impact of pathogenic α-syn on disease progression in lysosomal gene deficiency models

In our previous study we showed that neuron-specific Gba-KO (Gba-nKO) mice exhibit weight loss, reduced lifespan, tremors, and ataxia (Duffy et al., 2024). To investigate the impact of a pathogenic α-syn variant on disease progression in Gba-deficient mice, mice expressing the A53T variant of human SNCA (hSNCAA53T) were crossed with Gba-nKO mice to produce double variant Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice. Both Gba-nKO and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice displayed similar manifestations of tremor and ataxia near the end stage of disease (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Video 1). Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice were monitored and weighed weekly along with Gba-nKO, hSNCAA53T, and control (Gbafl/fl) littermates. Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice began losing weight at an earlier time point and lost significantly more weight per week than Gba-nKO mice (Fig. 1), indicating an earlier onset of the disease course. Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice also had a significantly shorter median lifespan (12.8 weeks) compared to Gba-nKO mice (15.6 [females], 16.1 [males] weeks) (Fig. 2A).

We also investigated whether pathogenic α-syn could cause increased disease progression in microglia-specific Gba-KO (Gba-mKO) mice, which have no noticeable phenotype (Duffy et al., 2024). Mice expressing hSNCAA53T were crossed with Gba mKO mice to produce double variant Gba-mKO/hSNCAA53T mice. However, these double variant mice did not have a significantly different lifespan compared to hSNCAA53T mice (Fig. 2B). This indicates that, even under the additional neuronal stress imposed by pathogenic α-syn expression, Gba deletion restricted to microglia does not exacerbate disease progression, underscoring the critical interplay between the GBA deficiency and α-syn specifically within neurons.

To determine whether pathogenic α-syn interacts with other lysosomal genes involved in sphingolipid degradation to affect lifespan, we also crossed Hexa- and Hexb-KO mice with hSNCAA53T mice. Variants in these genes are linked to Tay-Sachs and Sandhoff disease, respectively. The Hexa-KO/hSNCAA53T mice did not have a shortened lifespan compared to hSNCAA53T mice, which are shorter lived than the single variant Hexa-KO mice. Hexb-KO/hSNCAA53T mice did have a slightly shortened lifespan compared to Hexb-KO mice (Fig. 2C-D). However, a far greater portion of the lifespan was reduced in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice (81 % of control lifespan) as compared to Hexa-KO/hSNCAA53T (99 %) and Hexb-KO/hSNCAA53T (96 %) mice (Table 1).

Neuropathology in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T and Gba-nKO mice

To identify the extent of neurodegeneration, control, hSNCAA53T, Gba-nKO, and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T brain sections from 13 to 14-week-old mice were silver stained (Braak et al., 1988; Gallyas, 1980; Gallyas et al., 1992a; Gallyas et al., 1992b; Ishida et al., 2004). Marked silver grain deposition was apparent in both Gba-nKO and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T brains compared to control and hSNCAA53T brains (Fig. 3). Quantification revealed marginally fewer silver deposits in the whole brain of Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice compared to Gba-nKO mice, and no change in the thalamus, brainstem, or substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Silver staining was significantly higher in Gba-nKO and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T brain sections relative to control and hSNCAA53T mice in the thalamus, brainstem, and whole brain, but not the SNc (Supplementary Fig. 2). While Gba-nKO and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice exhibit some subtle regional differences in silver deposits, overall, both of these genotypes show evidence of neurodegeneration. Fluoro-Jade C staining corroborated these findings, revealing evidence of neurodegeneration in both 13–14-week-old Gba-nKO and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Quantitative analysis of Nissl-stained sections showed no significant differences in the number of Nissl-positive neurons among control, hSNCAA53T, Gba-nKO, and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice in the brainstem (Supplementary Fig. 4). Consistent with this result, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) revealed very few or no apoptotic cells across all groups (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results indicate that, at the disease stage examined, neuronal loss and apoptosis are minimal in the Gba-nKO, and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice despite the histological evidence of neurodegeneration detected by silver and Fluoro-Jade C staining. Taken together, the data suggest that the neuropathology at this time point represents an early phase of neurodegeneration, characterized by neuronal injury without substantial cell loss or apoptosis.

To assess protein aggregation and amyloid-like pathology, thioflavin S staining was performed. At 13–14 weeks of age, thioflavin S-positive fluorescent puncta were nearly undetectable in hSNCAA53T, Gba-nKO, or Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice compared to age-matched controls, indicating an absence of fibrillar protein aggregates (Supplementary Fig. 6). This absence of aggregates was in contrast to the numerous thioflavin S-positive fluorescent puncta observed in the brainstem of 15-month-old hSNCAA53T single-variant mice, consistent with the delayed onset of α-syn aggregation in this model (Lee et al., 2002). These findings suggest that GBA deficiency does not promote overt α-synuclein aggregation by the end stage of disease in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice.

Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice exhibit increased phosphorylated α-syn but maintain equivalent GBA activity

As GBA and α-syn appear to have a reciprocal relationship (Siebert et al., 2014), we explored whether either GBA or α-syn levels were altered in the Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice. Both GBA levels and GBA activity were reduced in Gba-nKO and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice, as expected (Fig. 4). However, neither GBA levels nor activity were significantly different between Gba KO and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice.

Total α-syn levels were slightly reduced in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice compared to hSNCAA53T mice (Fig. 5A-B). We also investigated levels of S129-phosphorylated α-syn (p-α-syn), which is associated with pathology (Fujiwara et al., 2002). When quantified relative to total α-syn levels, p-α-syn was significantly higher in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice (Fig. 5C-D). This suggests that neuronal GBA deficiency elevates pathologic α-syn levels.

Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice exhibit greater GlcCer accumulation

To investigate whether sphingolipid accumulation was altered in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice, we measured GlcCer, GlcSph, sphingosine (Sph), and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) concentrations via SFC MS/MS (GlcCer and GlcSph) and HPLC MS/MS (Sph and S1P). Total GlcCer levels were significantly greater in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice compared to Gba-nKO, hSNCAA53T, and control mice (Fig. 6A). Although Gba-nKO mice did not exhibit a significant increase in total GlcCer levels at this timepoint, upon examination of individual subspecies, they did show elevated C18- and C24-GlcCer relative to control and hSNCAA53T mice and elevated C24:1-GlcCer relative to control mice (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Fig. 7A). In contrast, Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T exhibited greater C18- and C24:1-GlcCer levels relative to all other groups and greater C20-, C22-, and C24-GlcCer levels relative to control and hSNCAA53T mice (Supplementary Fig. 7 A). hSNCAA53T mice exhibited an increase in C24:1-GlcCer relative to control mice but no other GlcCer subspecies were significantly different from control mice, nor were total GlcCer levels (Fig. 8A, Supplementary Fig. 8A). GlcSph levels were elevated in Gba-nKO and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice compared to control groups, but not significantly different from each other (Fig. 8B).

Ceramide levels were largely similar across all groups, although C18:1-Cer was elevated in hSNCAA53T mice relative to all other groups (Supplementary Fig. 7B). C18:1-Cer was also elevated in Gba-nKO and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice relative to control mice (Supplementary Fig. 7B). C24:1-Cer levels were reduced in Gba-nKO mice relative to control mice and in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice relative to control and hSNCAA53T mice (Supplementary Fig. 7B).

GalCer levels were generally similar across groups, although Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice showed a reduction in C24:1-GalCer relative to control and hSNCAA53T mice (Supplementary Fig. 7C).

Concentrations of C16-dihydroceramide (dhC16-Cer), dihydrosphingosine (dhSph), and dihydrosphingosine-1-phosphate (dhS1P) were also quantified. hSNCAA53T and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice exhibit reduced dhC16-Cer levels relative to control mice, while no significant changes in dhSph or dhS1P levels were observed (Supplementary Fig. 8). Sph levels were also not different among any of the groups, but S1P was elevated in both Gba-nKO and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice compared to control and hSNCAA53T mice (Fig. 6C-D).

We also performed high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to assess potential changes in other glycosphingolipids. While no changes were detected in LacCer, GM3, GD3, GA2, GM2, GD1b, GT1b, GD1a, or GQ1b, Gba-nKO mice exhibited elevated GM1a levels compared to hSNCAA53T mice and elevated GA1 levels relative to both hSNCAA53T and control mice (Supplementary Fig. 9). Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice also exhibited significant elevations in GA1 relative to hSNCAA53T mice (Supplementary Fig. 9). Overall, the ganglioside biosynthetic pathway downstream of GlcCer appears minimally altered.

Together, these findings demonstrate a significant and specific increase in GlcCer accumulation in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T, while levels of its direct downstream metabolite, GlcSph, remain unchanged compared to Gba-nKO mice.

RNAseq reveals limited unique changes to Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice

Bulk RNA sequencing was performed on brain tissue from Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T, Gba-nKO, hSNCAA53T, and control mice. Principal component analysis revealed that control and hSNCAA53 mice clustered together while Gba-nKO and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice clustered apart (Fig. 7A). Along principal component 1 (PC1), the Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T samples were farthest from the control/hSNCAA53T cluster, while Gba-nKO samples were in an intermediate position.

Analysis revealed 22 genes that were significantly increased in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice relative to all other groups (Fig. 7B, Table 2), while 17 genes were significantly decreased in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice relative to all other groups (Fig. 7C, Table 2). In total, 56 genes were differentially expressed in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice relative to Gba-nKO mice (Fig. 7D-E, Supplementary Table 1, Fig. 8, Supplementary Fig. 10). However, gene ontology enrichment analysis and KEGG analysis revealed no significant terms from these 56 genes. The most significant of these genes was Prnp. This change in gene expression is an artifact of the hSNCAA53T mouse’s design, which used the Prnp promoter and does not impact the coding region of the gene. Overall, when compared to control mice, the transcriptional changes seen in Gba-nKO mice and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice appeared very similar, with both groups demonstrating a profound inflammatory response (Fig. 7F-H, Table 3). However, a greater number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were found in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice relative to controls when compared to Gba-nKO mice relative to controls (increased: 725 vs. 530 genes, decreased: 175 vs. 20 genes). Only two genes, Prnp and Socs2, were significantly differentially expressed in hSNCAA53T mice relative to controls (Supplementary Fig. 11 A). As such, DEGs in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice vs. hSNCAA53T mice demonstrated a very similar profile to that of Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice vs. controls (Supplementary Fig. 11B).

Analysis of dysfunctional pathways common to GD and synucleinopathies

To explore autophagy-lysosomal dysfunction, a common link between PD and GD (Klein and Mazzulli, 2018), we assessed autophagy markers p62 and LC3, which showed no significant differences among the groups (Fig. 8A-B). Since ER stress is also a shared feature of PD and GD (Colla, 2019; Pornsukjantra et al., 2024), we also investigated levels of CHOP and XBP1. While XBP1 levels were not significantly different among any groups, CHOP levels were increased in both Gba-nKO and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice compared to controls (Fig. 8C-D) as shown previously in Gaucher disease neuronal cells (Pornsukjantra et al., 2024). We also examined levels of STING and phosphorylated STING, as STING signaling is also a common feature of GD and PD (Hinkle et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024a, 2024b). STING levels were significantly elevated in both Gba-nKO mice and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice, while phosphorylated STING levels were not altered among any of the groups (Fig. 8E-F).

Discussion

The current study investigated how pathogenic α-syn affects the neuron-specific loss of GBA in vivo. Using a previously established Gba-nKO mouse model that mimics the neuronopathic form of Gaucher disease, we demonstrate that introducing the A53T human variant of SNCA significantly accelerates disease progression. These double variant mice exhibited an earlier onset of disease, shortened lifespan, elevated phosphorylated α-syn and GlcCer, and subtle changes in gene expression, providing insights into how pathogenic α-syn can intensify the course of GBA-related neurodegenerative disease.

The relationship between brain sphingolipid levels and PD in the context of heterozygous GBA variants (PD-GBA) is complex. While there has been one report of GlcCer elevation in the substantia nigra of a PD cohort, which included some PD-GBA patients (Huebecker et al., 2019), post-mortem studies of PD-GBA patients have generally provided little evidence of GlcCer accumulation in the brain (Blumenreich et al., 2022; Gegg et al., 2015; Leyns et al., 2023). However, GlcCer accumulation does appear to be positively correlated with phosphorylated α-syn pathology (Leyns et al., 2023). Our study investigated the effect of pathogenic α-syn on sphingolipid levels in a neuron-specific Gba knockout model, which differs from PD-GBA in that both alleles are mutated. In this model, pathogenic α-synuclein expression exacerbated GlcCer accumulation without significantly altering GlcSph levels, suggesting an impairment in GlcCer turnover. Potentially, pathogenic α-syn could also stimulate de novo sphingolipid synthesis, further augmenting the GlcCer burden. However, other metabolites in the sphingolipid pathway, including ceramides and gangliosides, were largely unaffected by α-synuclein in this model. This contrasts with PD-GBA, where ganglioside levels have been found to be elevated (Blumenreich et al., 2022).

Studies have indicated that α-syn may directly interact with the GBA protein and impede its enzyme activity (Yap et al., 2011; Yap et al., 2013). However, such an interaction is unlikely to occur in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice due to the neuron-specific deletion of Gba, which removes the possibility of a GBA protein interaction with α-syn. More plausible is that GlcCer, the primary substrate for GBA, and α-syn influence each other within the lysosomal environment to alter the status and accumulation of each component. GlcCer can be converted to GlcSph through deacylation by acid ceramidase (Ferraz et al., 2016), which removes the fatty acid moiety from GlcCer. We hypothesize that pathogenic α-syn may hinder GlcCer clearance by blocking its conversion to GlcSph (Fig. 9).

Schematic depicting lysosomal processing of GlcCer in Gba-nKO and Gba-nKO/SNCAA53T mice. Gba-nKO/SNCAA53T mice exhibit a greater accumulation of GlcCer but not GlcSph relative to Gba-nKO mice. This lipid pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that pathogenic α-syn interferes with the conversion of GlcCer to GlcSph, thereby amplifying pre-existing GBA-dependent pathology and driving greater disease severity. Figure created with biorender.com.

Both GlcCer and α-syn are known to accumulate within neuronal lysosomes (Xie et al., 2022), supporting the possibility of a functional interplay within this organelle. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that GlcCer directly influences α-syn fibril assembly (Mazzulli et al., 2011; Taguchi et al., 2017; Zunke et al., 2018). Partial colocalization of GlcCer and α-syn has also been reported (Zunke et al., 2018). Although GlcSph has also been found to interact with α-syn (Taguchi et al., 2017), results from two Gaucher mouse models indicate that GlcSph accumulation and α-syn levels do not necessarily coincide (Xu et al., 2011). Additionally, GlcSph and α-syn pathology had no correlation in postmortem PD subjects (Leyns et al., 2023). These results support the concept that GlcCer itself may play a critical role in processes that influence α-syn status within lysosomes. Notably, while phosphorylated α-syn levels were elevated in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice, thioflavin S staining revealed no evidence of protein aggregates near the end stage of their disease, suggesting that GBA deficiency promotes α-syn phosphorylation but is insufficient to drive fibrillar aggregation. Thus, the absence of thioflavin S-positive aggregates in the double mutant mice suggests that α-syn aggregation is not a major driver of disease pathogenesis in this model.

The greater severity of disease in the double mutant Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice likely reflects an exacerbation of the already rapidly progressing phenotype caused by neuronal GBA deficiency, potentially due to enhanced GlcCer accumulation. While α-syn toxicity may contribute, the earlier onset and severity are more consistent with an amplification of Gba-nKO pathology rather than a fundamentally new disease mechanism. Notably, the major pathophysiologic features and brain transcriptional profiles of Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice were largely similar to Gba-nKO mice. Markers of autophagy appeared unchanged in both groups, though more sensitive methods such as electron microscopy or flux assays may be needed to detect subtle defects. Measures of neurodegeneration, ER stress, and STING signaling were also comparable between Gba-nKO and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice. Despite the similar overall transcriptional profiles between Gba-nKO and Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice, some genes that were differentially expressed between these two groups may be biologically relevant to PD. For example, Cybrd1 expression is increased in the caudate nucleus of PD patients (Bossers et al., 2009). Neuropeptide Y (NPY) is thought to be protective in PD, and NPY levels are reportedly altered in the disease (Zheng et al., 2021). The neuropeptide Cortistatin, encoded by Cort, has shown therapeutic potential in PD (Serrano-Martinez et al., 2024). Further resolution of cell-specific transcriptional responses, potentially through single-cell or single-nucleus RNA-seq, may help clarify these findings and reveal subtle disease-relevant changes.

Since the discovery of the association between GBA and PD, other lysosomal enzyme gene variants involved in sphingolipid degradation have also been implicated in PD susceptibility (Chang et al., 2017; Robak et al., 2017). Examples include SMPD1, which encodes acid sphingomyelinase (deficient in Niemann-Pick disease) and GALC, which encodes galactosylceramidase (deficient in Krabbe disease). These variants are thought to disrupt the autophagy-lysosomal system impairing the clearance of misfolded α-syn and enhancing its accumulation and dispersion. However, we find that hSNCAA53T expression leads to a more pronounced reduction in lifespan in Gba-nKO mice compared to Hexa-KO or Hexb-KO mice, models for Tay-Sachs and Sandhoff diseases, respectively, which also affect glycosphingolipid degradation. This disparity suggests a unique detrimental synergy between GBA deficiency and pathogenic α-syn, leading to increased GlcCer accumulation and a cellular environment that promotes α-syn toxicity.

This study has some limitations. While ataxia scoring captured gross motor impairment, additional behavioral assessments such as rotarod or open-field testing could provide more granular insight into functional deficits. Furthermore, although the use of a neuron-specific Cre driver supports a CNS origin for the observed phenotype, we cannot fully exclude contributions from peripheral systems without direct histopathological or metabolic analyses of non-CNS tissues.

In conclusion, our model uniquely combines neuron-specific Gba deletion with expression of mutant α-synuclein. In contrast to previous models our approach specifically targets neurons, enabling the study of neuron-intrinsic disease mechanisms. We show that pathogenic α-synuclein accelerates disease progression in the setting of complete neuronal GBA loss, even in the absence of overt α-syn aggregation. This is accompanied by GlcCer accumulation and minimal transcriptional and cellular pathology differences between Gba-nKO and double mutant mice, suggesting that α-synuclein amplifies preexisting GBA-related pathology rather than initiating a distinct molecular pathway. Together, these findings establish a new in vivo model for studying early neuronal events in GBA-associated synucleinopathies.

Materials and methods

Mouse generation

All animal procedures were approved by the National Institutes of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases’ Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were originally obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME) and further bred in-house at the National Institutes of Health (Bethesda, MD).

Gbafl/fl mice (JAX#021329)(Sinclair et al., 2007) and Thy1-CreERT2,-EYFP mice (JAX#012708) (Caroni, 1997; Young et al., 2008) were crossed to generate neuron-specific Gba-KO (Gba nKO) mice as previously described (Duffy et al., 2024). In order to generate Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice, B6.Cg-2310039L15RikTg(Prnp-SNCA*A53T)23Mkle/J (hSNCAA53T) mice (JAX#006823) (Lee et al., 2002) were crossed with Gbafl/fl mice. Heterozygous offspring were backcrossed to Gbafl/fl mice. Gbafl/fl/hSNCAA53T mice were then crossed with Gbafl/fl/Thy1-CreERT2,-EYFP (Gba-nKO) mice to produce Gbafl/fl/Thy1-CreERT2,-EYFP/B6.Cg-2310039L15RikTg(Prnp-SNCA*A53T)23Mkle/J (Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T) mice, Gbafl/fl/Thy1-CreERT2,-EYFP (Gba-nKO) mice, Gbafl/fl/B6.Cg-2310039L15RikTg(Prnp-SNCA*A53T)23Mkle/J (hSNCAA53T) mice, and Gbafl/fl (control) mice.

Gbafl/fl mice (JAX#021329) (Sinclair et al., 2007) and Tmem119 -CreERT2 mice (JAX# 031820) (Kaiser and Feng, 2019) were crossed to generate microglia-specific Gba KO (Gba-mKO) mice as previously described (Duffy et al., 2024). Using the same scheme as described for Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T mice, Gba-mKO/hSNCAA53T mice were also generated.

Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T, Gba-nKO, Gba-mKO/hSNCAA53T, Gba-mKO, hSNCAA53T, and control mice were fed a tamoxifen citrate diet (500 mg/kg) ad libitum from weaning (3 weeks of age) for 5 weeks. Mouse brains were collected at approximately 13–14 weeks for additional experiments. All “hSNCAA53T” mice used in Gba-nKO/hSNCAA53T or Gba-mKO/hSNCAA53T experiments are Gbafl/fl/hSNCAA53T mice.

Hexa- and Hexb-KO mice were generated as previously described (Sango et al., 1995; Yamanaka et al., 1994). These mice were crossed with B6.Cg-2310039L15RikTg(Prnp-SNCA*A53T)23Mkle/J mice and then backcrossed to generate Hexa-KO/hSNCAA53T and Hexb-KO/hSNCAA53T mice. Hexa-KO and Hexb-KO mice and control mice were not fed a tamoxifen citrate diet. “hSNCAA53T” mice used in Hexa or Hexa experiments are GbaWT/hSNCAA53T mice.

Ataxia tests

A battery of ataxia tests was performed to assess hindlimb clasping, stance, ledge walking, gait/tremor, and kyphosis as previously described (Duffy et al., 2024; Guyenet et al., 2010).

Neurodegeneration silver staining

Mice were anesthetized with Avertin (250 mg/kg) and perfused with a saline flush (9.0 g/L NaCl). Brains were collected and post-fixed in 4 % PFA for 24 h. Brains were then cryoprotected in 20 % sucrose for a further 24 h, until sinking. 40 μm fresh, frozen sections were cut on a Leica Cryostat. FD NeuroTechnologies, Inc. further fixed brain sections in 4 % PFA before performing silver staining with the FD NeuroSilver™ Kit. The National Heart Lung and Blood Institutes’ Light Microscopy Core imaged stained sections, which were further analyzed using NDP. view2 (Hamamatsu Photonics K.K.) and thresholding with Fiji/ImageJ. Some sections were excluded due to poor quality (e.g. overwhelming background staining). For quantification of the thalamus and brainstem, 3 40× images per section were collected from each brain region. 2–6 sections from 2 to 3 mice per group were quantified. For quantification of the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), 1 5× image per section was aligned to the mouse atlas provided online by Matt Gaidica (https://labs.gaidi.ca/mouse-brain-atlas/) (Franklin and Paxinos, 1997). 2–6 sections from 2 to 3 mice per group were quantified. The SNc was traced, and thresholding was performed within the traced region. For quantification of the whole brain, the cerebellum was excluded due to the high level of counterstaining in that region. 5–6 sections from 3 mice per group were quantified. For each region of interest, the mean for each mouse sample was the input for statistical testing.

Fluoro-Jade C staining

Mouse brain sections were stained with Fluoro-Jade C (FJC) Ready-to-Dilute Staining Kit (BioSensis #TR-100-FJT). The company-provided protocol was followed with small modifications. In brief, sections were permeabilized with 0.2 % Triton X-100/ PBS for 30 min at room temperature. After 3 washes with PBS, sections were briefly rinsed with distilled water and fluorescent background was blocked with potassium permanganate for 10 min. Sections were rinsed in water for 5 min and incubated with FJC/DAPI working solution for 20 min. After 3 rinses with water, sections were dried at 55 ◦C for 20 min. Sections were cleared by xylene for 1 min and mounted with VectaMount. Images were taken under a LSM 780 confocal microscope (Zeiss).

Nissl staining

Samples were stained with DAPI (working concentration 1 mg/mL) and NeuroTrace 530/615 red fluorescent Nissl stain (ThermoFisher Scientific #N21482, 1:300). Sections were washed 3–4 times with PBS and mounted with Prolong Diamond Antifade Mountant (ThermoFisher Scientific #P36961). Images of the brainstem were taken under a LSM 780 confocal microscope. Nissl-positive cells were counted blind from 3 images per sample using Fiji/Image J.

TUNEL staining

TUNEL assay was performed using In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit, TMR red (Roche, # 12156792910) according to manufacturer’s manual. Briefly, mouse brain sections were permeabilized with 0.1 %Triton X-100 in freshly prepared 0.1 % sodium citrate for 10 min on ice, then incubated with TUNEL reaction mixture for 60 min at 37 ◦C. The positive control section was treated with DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich, #DN25) of 2000 U/mL in 50 mM Tris-HCL, pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mg/mL BSA for 10 min at room temperature prior to TUNEL labeling procedure. The negative control section was incubated with TUNEL label solution without the enzyme solution. Sections were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL), washed 3 times with PBS, and mounted with Prolong Diamond Antifade Mountant. Tile images were taken under a LSM 780 confocal microscope.

Thioflavin-S staining

Mouse brain sections were air-dried at 37 ◦C overnight and rehydrated in 50 % ethanol for 5 min. Sections were stained with 0.1 % Thioflavin-S (Millipore Sigma #T1892) in 50 % ethanol for 5 min, rinsed in 80 % ethanol for 5 min, and mounted with VectaMount (Vector Laboratories #H-5700–60). Images were taken under a LSM 780 confocal microscope.

Western blotting and antibodies

Mouse brain tissue from one hemisphere was homogenized and lysed in RIPA Lysis and Extraction buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and treated with HALT protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 30 μg of protein per sample were separated on a NuPAGE 4–12 % Bis-Tris gel or a NuPAGE 12 % Bis-Tris gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under denaturing conditions. Protein was then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane using the iBlot2 Blotting System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were blocked with 5 % non-fat dry milk and incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 ◦C. Samples were then washed in 5 % non-fat dry milk three times and incubated with secondary antibody for one hour at room temperature. Membranes were washed twice more prior to chemiluminescent detection using the ECL prime Western blotting system (Millipore Sigma). β-actin was used as a loading control. Blots were imaged on the Amersham Imager 680 (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) and quantified with Fiji/ImageJ. Relative band intensity was calculated by taking the intensity of each band (primary antibody and actin) relative to the first control band, dividing that value by the relative intensity of each sample’s actin band, and calculating the fold change of each sample.

Antibodies used in this study are as follows: GBA (Cell Signaling Technology #88162, 1:1000), α-synuclein (Cell Signaling Technology #2642, 1:10000), phospho-α-synuclein (Cell Signaling Technology #23706, 1:10000), SQSTM1/p62 (Sigma Aldrich #P0067, 0.5μg/mL), LC3B (Novus Biologicals #NB100–2220, 0.5 μg/mL), XBP-1 (ABClonal #A1731, 1:1000), CHOP (Cell Signaling Technology #5554, 1:5000), STING (Cell Signaling Technology #50494, 1:1000), phospho-STING (Cell Signaling Technology #72971, 1:1000), peroxidase conjugated antibody (Millipore Sigma #AP132P, 1:3000), β-actin (Abcam #Ab49900, 1:20000), β-actin (Sigma Aldrich #A3854, 1:20000), and β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology #5125, 1:20000).

Glucocerebrosidase assays

GBA activity was quantified fluorometrically using fluorophore 4-methylumbelliferone (4-MU) assays. Brain tissue was homogenized in water and diluted to 1 mg protein per mL. For GBA enzyme assays, 10 μl samples (equivalent to 10 μg protein) were treated with 1 mM of the GBA2 inhibitor N-(n-butyl)deoxygalactonojirimycin (NB-DGJ; Toronto Research Chemicals) for 1 h on ice. Samples were then incubated with 4 mM 4-MU β-D-glucopyranoside (Sigma Aldrich) in GBA assay buffer (0.1 M Citrate/0.2 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 5.2), 0.1 % Triton-X100, 0.25 % sodium taurochlorate, 1.25 mM EDTA) at 37 ◦C for 2 h. Fluorescent intensity was measured with a CLARIOstar plate reader (BMG Labtech) at Ex./Em. = 360/450 nm.

Mass spectrometry

One hemisphere of brain tissue was homogenized in RIPA Lysis and Extraction buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with HALT protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Lipids were then extracted and identified via mass spectrometry through the Lipidomics Shared Resource at the Medical University of South Carolina. High-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS) and supercritical fluid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (SFC-MS/MS) were used to identify GlcCer, GlcSph, Sph, and S1P as previously described (Bielawski et al., 2009).

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was performed to quantify glycosphingolipids as previously described (Neville et al., 2004; Priestman et al., 2024) with minor modifications as detailed below. Brain tissue was homogenized in water and 0.75 mg were diluted into 0.5 mL aqueous solution. Briefly, lipids were extracted in a final volume of chloroform: methanol: water (4:8:3, v/v/v) and purified in C18 columns (telos, Kinesis, UK). Lipids were treated with recombinant ceramide glycanase. Cleaved oligosaccharides were then labeled with anthranilic acid and run on a normal-phase HPLC machine (Waters). In order to identify peaks, glucose units were calculated by comparing sample retention times to a 2AA-labeled homopolymer dextran ladder. Peak areas were quantified using a known amount of 2AA-labeled chitotriose standard.

RNA sequencing

RNA was isolated from one hemisphere of brain tissue using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Inc) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Novogene Inc. then performed quality control, library preparation, and bulk sequencing. RNA was sequenced with NovaSeq 6000 PE150 technology (Illumina) and paired-end reads were generated. Analysis was carried out on the Galaxy platform. High quality clean reads were mapped to the mm10 mouse reference genome using Hisat2 (Kim et al., 2015). Counts were generated with featureCounts and differential expression was determined with limma (Liao et al., 2014; Robinson et al., 2010; Smyth, 2005). The Benjamini and Hochberg approach was used to calculate false discovery rates (FDR). Gplot and ggplot2 R packages were used to generate heatmaps and volcano plots and to perform principal component analysis. The VennDiagram R package was used to generate Venn diagrams. Gene ontology enrichment analysis was performed using The Gene Ontology Resource (geneontology.org) with the PANTHER (18.0) classification system. KEGG analysis was performed using ShinyGO 0.82.

qPCR

Five differentially expressed genes (Npy, Ugt8a, Cryab, Vegfa, Socs2) revealed by RNAseq were selected for qPCR validation. cDNA was synthesized with Superscript IV VILO Master Mix without DNase (Invitrogen). TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) was used in combination with TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Applied Biosystems, assay IDs: Mm01410146_m1, Mm00495930_m1, Mm0051 5567_m1, Mm00437306_m1, Mm00850544_g1, Mm00607939_s1) to perform qPCR. Expression was normalized to actin and is presented as fold change relative to the control group unless otherwise stated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism v9 (GraphPad Software). Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) tests were used to analyze survival curves. The two-tailed unpaired t-test, Welch’s t-test, one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA, Sidak’s multiple comparisons test, Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, and simple linear regression were used where applicable and as indicated in figure legends. Concentrations of sphingolipid subspecies (of varying chain lengths) were square root transformed to align with test assumptions and back transformed for data presentation. Significance was set at p < 0.05 or, in the case of RNAseq analysis and multiple comparisons tests, padj < 0.05. Significant fold change was set to |log2FC| > 0.5.
