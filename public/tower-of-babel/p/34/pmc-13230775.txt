Halving global road traffic deaths through targeted mitigation practices by 2050
Authors: Shengyong Zou, Fengjun Lv, Yinhua Qian, Chao Han, Jinlei Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRoad traffic deaths (RTD) are the leading cause of mortality and represent a serious threat to global health and sustainable development. By analyzing data from more than 190 countries covering the period from 2000 to 2021, we assessed the historical trends and key influencing factors of RTD across different regions and income groups. Global RTD rose from 1.16 million in 2000 to 1.28 million in 2012, then slightly declined to 1.17 million by 2021. Africa and low-income countries continue to bear the highest burdens. GDP per capita emerged as the most influential factor, reflecting the role of economic growth in improving road safety infrastructure, healthcare access, and public awareness. Targeted mitigation practices including stronger education, expanded emergency care, coupled with the adoption of WHO best practices could potentially halve global RTD from 1.43 million to 0.74 million by 2050 under SSP2 scenario. The greatest reductions are anticipated in lower-middle-income and low-income countries, where the need for intervention is most urgent. These findings highlight the critical importance of integrated, evidence-based mitigation strategies that promote safe mobility, particularly in resource-constrained settings, and emphasize road safety as a fundamental component of global sustainability and public health efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42076-z.Keywords: Road safety, Influencing factors, Mitigation, Projections, Sustainable developmentSubject terms: Developing world, Environmental social sciences, Health care, Scientific community, Social sciences

Introduction

Road traffic deaths (RTD) constitute a serious global health challenge, claiming more than one million lives each year and ranking among the leading causes of mortality for individuals aged 5 to 291,2. In many regions, RTD impose profound burdens on families, communities, and national economies. The consequences extend far beyond public health, hindering socioeconomic progress through reduced productivity, increased healthcare costs3, and the disruption of social structures4. In addition to the direct health impacts, the psychological toll on families and society is also considerable. Despite the scale of the problem, the intricate interplay of factors that associated with variations RTD is still not fully understood, and the development of effective global strategies remains difficult. This urgency is underscored by the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), which include a target to halve global road traffic fatalities by 2030 under SDG 3.6.

The relationship between socioeconomic development and RTD is multifaceted. Existing research highlights how economic conditions, road safety regulations, vehicle standards, traffic management systems, and behavioral factors such as speeding, alcohol use, and seatbelt compliance shape RTD patterns5,6. While evidence suggests traffic fatalities often rise with initial economic growth and later decline7, countries benefit from better infrastructure and enforcement, offsetting risks from higher vehicle ownership8. In contrast, low-income regions including sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia suffer disproportionately from poor infrastructure and limited investment in road safety9. Social measures such as stricter laws10,11, education12, and improvements in healthcare13,14 also play key roles, while environmental factors like smog and adverse weather can worsen risks by reducing visibility and road quality15–17. Additionally, driver behaviors including consistent seatbelt use, speed control and psychological responses can also exert influence on traffic accident rates5,6,18.

However, substantial gaps remain in the global understanding of RTD. Existing studies predominantly focus on high-income settings, offering limited insights into the complex influencing factors associated with RTD in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in regions like Africa and Southeast Asia19. Besides, many investigations often focus on isolated factors, such as infrastructure or legislation, without capturing how economic, social, and behavioral elements interact, which can obscure true causal pathways. Furthermore, interventions often overlook local institutional capacities and socioeconomic constraints, resulting in limited effectiveness. For instance, although Europe and North America have achieved remarkable progress in road safety through comprehensive measures, many developing regions continue to face challenges in implementing even fundamental regulations and infrastructure improvements due to financial limitations, governance gaps, and competing political priorities20.

Addressing these issues requires a more integrated approach that can reveal the influencing factors linked to RTD across diverse contexts and inform tailored, scalable strategies. In this study, we bridge these gaps by compiling extensive panel data from over 190 countries spanning 2000 to 2021. We first systematically analyze the spatial and temporal patterns of RTD across countries, regions, and income groups. Using fixed-effects regression and structural equation modeling (SEM), we identify key economic, social, and environmental determinants of RTD rates and quantify their direct and indirect effects. We then develop country-specific mitigation practices, incorporating improvements in education, healthcare, legislation, and individual safety behaviors, and project future RTD trends under multiple Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) scenarios. By contrasting scenarios with and without targeted interventions, our study provides a robust evidence base to support the operationalization of the UN Road Safety Action Plan. This work enhances understanding of how different influencing factors relate to RTD in varied socioeconomic settings and guides more effective policies and resource allocation to reduce global traffic fatalities.

Results and discussion

Spatiotemporal variability of RTD

Globally, both the number and rate of RTD display substantial spatial heterogeneity (Fig. 1a and Fig. S1). Between 2000 and 2021, Europe consistently reported the lowest absolute RTD and the lowest RTD rate, with an average of 0.1 million deaths and 11.2 deaths per 100,000 population, reflecting the region’s relatively advanced road safety systems. In contrast, the South-East Asia and Western Pacific regions together accounted for approximately 0.63 million annual RTD, representing more than half of the global total. Although Africa contributed only about 20% of global RTD, it recorded the highest RTD rate at 27.2 deaths per 100,000 population (Fig. 1e). This figure is more than twice the European rate, underscoring the acute road safety challenges faced by countries in the African region.

Temporally, the global average RTD rate declined from 19.0 deaths per 100,000 population in 2000 to 14.8 in 2021, amounting to a 22.2% reduction. Over the same period, the total number of RTD showed an initial rise, from 1.16 million in 2000 to a peak of 1.28 million in 2012, followed by a gradual decline to 1.17 million in 2021. Europe demonstrated the most pronounced improvement, achieving a 55% reduction in RTD rates over the past two decades (Fig. 1d). In contrast, other regions saw more modest declines that fell well short of the World Health Organization’s (WHO) target to halve road traffic deaths during the Decade of Action for Road Safety 2011–2020, highlighting the continued need for focused interventions and robust safety strategies.

Analysis by income levels reveals marked disparities in RTD rates. A clear negative relationship exists, where low-income countries experience RTD rates that are on average 2.5 times higher than those in high-income countries (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2a). Significant differences are also observed across WHO regions. African and Eastern Mediterranean countries generally exhibit RTD rates above the global average, while most European countries fall below this benchmark (Fig. 2b), reflecting comparatively stronger road safety policies and infrastructure in these higher-income contexts.

Mechanisms underlying observed associations

This study identified the primary factors associated with RTD rates from economic, social, and environmental perspectives, and employed linear regression to quantify these relationships. From an economic standpoint, higher per capita GDP (PGDP) is generally associated with lower RTD rates, a pattern that is particularly pronounced among high-income countries (Fig. 2c). This association plausibly reflects the coevolution of economic prosperity with safer road infrastructure, stronger regulatory enforcement, and improved emergency and healthcare capacity. Notably, our results differ from earlier work that framed the development-RTD relationship as an inverted U-curve, with fatalities rising during early motorisation before declining at higher income levels7,21,22. A key distinction is that much of the evidence supporting the inverted U-shape was derived from earlier historical periods, whereas our analysis focuses on the contemporary era in which vehicle technologies, road design standards, trauma care, and safety governance have broadly advanced since the turn of the 21st century. Consistent with this interpretation, supplementary fixed-effects analyses show that adding a quadratic PGDP term neither improves model fit nor yields a plausible turning point, and robustness checks using low income countries (LICs) and Africa subsamples and the longer time series data (1980–2023) likewise provide little evidence for an inverted U-shape (Supplementary Text). Together, these results support the linear specification adopted here as a parsimonious representation of the development-RTD association in the 21st century. Under current global conditions, rising income is associated with sustained gains in institutional capacity and safety systems across development stages: earlier growth may coincide with rapid structural transformation and upgrading of transport and governance, while later-stage development tends to consolidate these gains through mature safety programmes, standardized infrastructure, and more effective enforcement.

From a social perspective, increased school enrollment rates correlate with lower RTD rates, most notably in high-income countries (Fig. 2d). This relationship likely arises because greater educational attainment enhances safety awareness and fosters more responsible driving behavior: individuals with higher levels of education exhibit better risk perception, stronger compliance with traffic regulations, and more consistent use of protective measures such as seat belts. These behavioral improvements collectively reduce the likelihood of fatal accidents and help explain the observed negative association between education and RTD rates.

Regarding environmental influences, elevated concentrations of PM2.5 are linked to more frequent and severe smog events. The association between smog and increased accident risks is robustly supported by a dual-mechanism framework23,24. First, reduced visibility under smog can impair drivers’ ability to perceive road conditions and judge distances25, thereby increasing accident risk and, in turn, RTD rates (Fig. 2e). Second, acute exposure to PM2.5 may adversely affect cognitive performance, including slower reaction times and reduced attention, which can increase the likelihood of errors even when visibility is not markedly compromised23,26. However, in a macro-level regression framework, PM2.5 may also proxy broader, time-varying contextual factors that are difficult to observe directly, such as urban density, congestion intensity, and industrial or travel activity. We therefore interpret the estimated PM2.5 coefficient as a contextual association rather than a stand-alone causal policy lever. Consistent with this interpretation, the relationship is more apparent in high-income settings where strong infrastructure and enforcement reduce variation in other risk factors, allowing environmental and activity-related signals to be more readily detected (Fig. 2e). Overall, these findings suggest that environmental conditions can amplify traffic risk, but they should be understood as part of the broader risk environment rather than a direct intervention target inferred from the regression model.

To control for unobserved differences across countries, we applied a fixed effects model to examine the associations between key indicators and RTD rates. While traditional views suggest that economic growth and rising urbanization may exacerbate RTD rates by outpacing improvements in transport systems27, our results indicate the opposite. PGDP exhibits a clear and statistically significant negative relationship with RTD rates, with an increase of 0.152 thousand USD per year linked to a reduction of one death per 100,000 population (Table 1). Although the urbanization level also shows a negative association with RTD rates, this finding lacks statistical significance, possibly due to uneven urban development that fails to adequately expand transport infrastructure. Similarly, school enrollment demonstrates a negative but non-significant relationship, which may reflect its collinearity with PGDP (Table 1). Nevertheless, separate regressions using urbanization and school enrollment individually yield statistically significant coefficients with directions matching those in the full model, indicating that their explanatory effects are partially absorbed when correlated socioeconomic indicators are included simultaneously (Supplementary Text). These robustness checks support that the associations reported in Table 1 reliably reflect how major socioeconomic factors relate to RTD rates. Air pollution indicators, including PM2.5 and SO2, are positively associated with RTD rates, suggesting that higher levels of these pollutants contribute to reduced visibility and impaired driver cognition, thereby increasing accident risks28(Table 1). However, reverse causality must also be considered, since increased driving contributes to higher emissions29.

To further explore direct and indirect pathways, we employed a structural equation model (SEM) (Fig. 3). This analysis reveals that the effect of PGDP on RTD rates is partly mediated by school enrollment and urbanization. The direct effect of PGDP on RTD rates is represented by a standardized path coefficient of -0.23, while indirect effects through school enrollment and urbanization are -0.27 and -0.03, respectively. Together, these yield a total effect of -0.53, with direct impacts accounting for 44% and indirect effects contributing 56% (Supplementary Table 1). Among all influencing factors examined, school enrollment exerts the strongest direct influence on RTD rates, with a standardized path coefficient of -0.69. This suggests that improvements in education not only elevate safety awareness but also significantly increase seat belt usage30, which is confirmed by a standardized path coefficient of 0.46 linking higher education to seat belt adoption. This greater use of seat belts subsequently contributes to a reduction in RTD rates.

Urban development plays a crucial role in advancing healthcare infrastructure and increasing hospital density, which together strengthen emergency response systems after traffic accidents. As a result, a larger share of injured individuals can receive timely medical care13, ultimately lowering mortality from traffic injuries (Fig. 3). In addition, stricter laws and effective enforcement represent another key influencing factor in reducing RTD rates. Our analysis shows that regulatory measures, such as setting urban speed limits for passenger cars and imposing Blood Alcohol Concentration (BAC) limits for drivers, meaningfully help decrease RTD rates. The standardized path coefficients for these factors are 0.20 for speed limits and 0.08 for BAC restrictions, underscoring their important contributions to mitigating traffic death risks by promoting safer driving behaviors and reducing the severity of accidents.

Mitigation pathways towards 2050

To quantitatively assess future trends in RTD, we incorporated projections of per capita GDP (PGDP) and demographic shifts under the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs). This analysis covered SSP1 (Sustainability), SSP2 (Middle of the Road), SSP3 (Regional Rivalry), and SSP4 (Inequality)31. We excluded SSP5 (Fossil-fueled Development) due to its emphasis on investments in health, education, and institutions, which closely resemble the mitigation scenarios already examined. Under expected economic growth and associated improvements in road infrastructure, healthcare services, and public safety awareness, global RTD rates are projected to decline under these pathways (Fig. 4).

However, despite differences in development assumptions, the absolute number of global RTD is projected to rise across all scenarios, mainly due to population growth. By 2050, global RTD are expected to peak at 1.62 million under SSP3, with the other scenarios ranging between 1.27 and 1.46 million. These figures indicate an increase from current levels, underscoring the persistent threat that RTD pose to public health and social stability without stronger interventions. This trend suggests that achieving the WHO targets in the Global Plan for the United Nations Decade of Action for Road Safety 2021–2030 may remain out of reach, hindering progress toward broader sustainable development goals.

Enhanced interventions, including improvements in education, healthcare and legislation, have the potential to substantially offset the projected increase in RTD rates. Although the WHO has actively promoted road safety through multiple initiatives, their impact remains limited, particularly in low and lower-middle income countries (Fig. 4e-f). In response, we developed region-specific mitigation practices aligned with the WHO best practice criteria, considering current national conditions. These practices assume that by 2050, countries will fully implement policies promoting seat belt use, enforcing speed limits, and maintaining strict BAC controls, while also achieving improvements in education and healthcare systems (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 4). When these measures are incorporated, RTD rates across all SSP scenarios drop markedly. Compared with the Business-as-Usual (BAU) scenario, global RTD in 2050 are projected to decline by approximately half, ranging from 46.8% reduction under SSP4 to a 49.0% reduction under SSP1, with an average reduction of 48.0%. Specifically, under the representative SSP2 scenario, global RTD could decrease from 1.43 million to 0.74 million by 2050. These results highlight the substantial impact and necessity of targeted governance measures to improve road safety worldwide.

Among all the targeted interventions evaluated, adopting stricter speed-limit standards is projected to yield the most substantial benefits. If all countries align their urban speed limits with the WHO-recommended maximum of 50 km/h, global RTD rates are expected to decline by 15.8% (Fig. 4b), likely because lower speeds reduce braking distance, extend driver reaction time, and limit crash energy, thereby substantially lowering fatality risk. Improvements in educational attainment also play a critical role: higher education levels are empirically linked to better risk perception, greater adherence to traffic regulations, and safer driving behaviors32. Under the enhanced enrollment scenario, RTD rates fall by 14.8% (Fig. 4b), illustrating that strengthening school enrollment is a highly effective pathway for reducing traffic fatalities. Universal seat belt compliance further contributes significantly, as seat belts prevent occupant ejection and distribute collision forces more safely, yielding an estimated 11.0% reduction in RTD rates (Fig. 4b). By contrast, improvements in medical system capacity and stricter BAC limits result in smaller yet meaningful reductions of 2.7% and 0.7%, respectively (Fig. 4b); while their marginal effects are modest, both remain essential components of a comprehensive safety system given their roles in post-crash survival and alcohol-related crash prevention. Under limited fiscal resources, governments should prioritize interventions that offer high impact and low implementation barriers, such as speed-limit enforcement, educational expansion, and universal seat belt usage, to maximize immediate reductions in RTD rates. Once these measures are in place, more resource-intensive or lower cost-effectiveness interventions can be strengthened to achieve additional long-term improvements in road safety.

Analysis across income levels reveals that high-income and upper-middle-income countries already exhibit relatively low RTD rates under the baseline scenario, supported by strong individual safety awareness and robust regulatory frameworks. Therefore, the marginal benefits of additional interventions appear less pronounced in these contexts (Fig. 4c-d). In contrast, lower-middle-income and low-income countries, where baseline RTD rates are significantly higher, experience more substantial reductions under intervention scenarios. This underscores the particular effectiveness of these measures for countries with fewer existing safeguards. However, even under these improved conditions, low-income countries still face major barriers to further progress. Due to limited economic resources, underdeveloped infrastructure, and challenges in cultivating widespread safety awareness, average RTD rates in these countries are projected to remain above 20 deaths per 100,000 population in 2050 under all scenarios (Fig. 4f). This underscores the urgent need for continued targeted investment in road infrastructure, education initiatives, and expanded international support to secure lasting improvements.

Moreover, the benefits of these mitigation strategies vary considerably by region. Compared to the BAU pathway, Southeast Asia, Africa, and South America stand out for their potential to achieve large absolute reductions in RTD rates, typically exceeding 10 deaths per 100,000 population by 2050 (Fig. 5a-c and Fig. S1-S3). Among these, Southeast Asia demonstrates the highest relative potential, with RTD rates projected to decline by nearly 89.3% compared to 2020 levels (Fig. 5d), indicating a strong opportunity for transformative improvements. Conversely, Africa continues to face significant challenges, including persistent economic stagnation, inadequate road networks, and obstacles to expanding road safety education. As a result, RTD rates remain relatively high even after interventions are implemented (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, the rapid population growth observed in these regions, which outpaces the global average, contributes to maintaining elevated global RTD numbers. Together, these findings reinforce the necessity of long-term, region-specific strategies that directly address the underlying socioeconomic and infrastructural barriers to safer mobility.

Challenges and opportunities

Addressing the persistent global burden of RTD stands among the most complex yet critical challenges facing public health and sustainable development. Despite decades of targeted interventions, evolving national policies, and international action plans, RTD continue to inflict severe health, economic, and social costs, with the burden falling disproportionately on LMICs. These countries often grapple with rapid motorization that outpaces regulatory oversight, fragile infrastructure systems, under-resourced emergency services, and limited institutional capacity2,33. As a consequence, while many high-income countries (HICs) have achieved notable progress in reducing RTD, LMICs disproportionately shoulder the human and economic costs of road crashes.

A striking indication of these structural disparities emerges in the realm of governance. Despite clear guidance from the WHO advocating for comprehensive frameworks to tackle road safety, only seven countries worldwide have enacted legislation that simultaneously addresses all five key behavioral risk factors: speeding, driving under the influence, motorcycle helmet use, seat belt adherence, and child restraint systems. In sharp contrast, approximately 80 countries still lack even the most basic laws concerning vehicle safety standards34. This leaves large populations exposed to preventable harms and underscores the fragility of protective systems where political resolve or fiscal capacity to implement robust legislation remains insufficient. In many LMICs, the absence of well-resourced, centralized agencies dedicated to road safety further undermines the ability to design, execute, and evaluate effective interventions. Without such institutional anchors, efforts to introduce or enforce safety policies often stall, and opportunities for evidence-based course corrections are lost.

Looking ahead, the urgency of these gaps becomes even more pronounced. The global vehicle fleet is expected to double by 2030 compared to 2010 levels34. This dramatic growth threatens to overwhelm existing transport systems, especially in LMICs, intensifying congestion, worsening air quality, and amplifying health and economic vulnerabilities. Against this backdrop, our analysis suggests that while targeted interventions can meaningfully reduce RTD, achieving the WHO target of halving road traffic fatalities by 2030 appears overly ambitious within the current decade. Persistent barriers such as uneven economic development, slow educational progress, delays in modernizing infrastructure, and fragmented legislative adoption continue to constrain meaningful progress.

Our projections indicate that realizing a 50% reduction would require transformative advances in socioeconomic conditions, infrastructure resilience, and individual safety awareness on a scale more aligned with levels anticipated by 2050. This sobering assessment highlights the depth of investment and systemic change needed. It also underscores the imperative of fostering stronger political commitments, increasing financial allocations to road safety, and building institutional capacities that can sustain coordinated, data-driven interventions35. Without such integrated approaches, the global community risks perpetuating cycles where the most vulnerable populations continue to pay the highest price in lives lost and communities destabilized36,37.

Deficiencies in physical infrastructure add a second critical dimension to this challenge. In numerous LMICs, road networks remain poorly engineered, often without basic protective features for pedestrians and cyclists, and rarely conform to design standards necessary to minimize severe collisions33. Rapid urban growth and accelerating population pressures exacerbate these vulnerabilities, creating congested environments where traffic risks escalate sharply, especially in settings that lack affordable and efficient public transportation alternatives38. Financial constraints compound these problems, as many LMICs struggle to prioritize investments in safer road systems, widespread educational outreach, and well-equipped emergency services33. While HICs benefit from sustained, well-funded road safety initiatives embedded in broader development agendas, LMICs frequently rely on sporadic external aid or private investment streams that may be misaligned with long-term national priorities. Corruption and inefficiencies in the allocation of limited funds can further dilute the impact of such investments. Even when resources are mobilized, they are too often directed toward narrowly scoped or reactive projects rather than comprehensive infrastructure upgrades and enduring safety programs that systematically reduce RTD35,39.

Data limitations pose another formidable barrier to effective road safety management33,40. Reliable, standardized data on RTD and associated interventions form the backbone of any evidence-based strategy, enabling countries to monitor progress, allocate resources, and refine policies. Yet, profound disparities exist in how data are collected, with many nations grappling with underreporting of fatalities, inconsistent definitions of injuries, and fragmented record-keeping systems. These inconsistencies not only hamper rigorous cross-country comparisons but also obscure emerging patterns that might inform timely policy responses. The situation is especially acute in LMICs, where integrated national databases capable of systematically tracking RTD trends are often absent. Without such foundational data systems, it becomes exceedingly difficult to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions or pinpoint high-risk areas that demand urgent, targeted attention. This pervasive data gap further complicates international coordination efforts, leading to uneven application of safety measures and diluted accountability.

Despite these structural and institutional challenges, substantial opportunities exist to advance global road safety. One particularly compelling strategy is the the Safe System approach, which has proven effective in numerous high-income contexts, especially across Europe.41 This comprehensive model does not place the burden of safety solely on individual road users; rather, it systematically integrates safer infrastructure design, rigorous vehicle safety standards, and robust enforcement protocols to create an environment where mistakes do not automatically result in severe harm. Countries such as Norway and the Netherlands have demonstrated remarkable success under this paradigm, achieving significant reductions in RTD and setting benchmarks for what is possible through coordinated, multi-faceted action42. Translating these successes to LMIC settings will require adaptive strategies that are sensitively aligned with local infrastructural realities, governance capacities, and socioeconomic contexts43.

Beyond the governance and behavioral interventions analyzed in this study, technological innovation represents a rapidly expanding frontier with the potential to transform road safety landscapes worldwide. Continued economic growth facilitates the deployment of intelligent transport systems (ITS), which leverage tools such as automated traffic enforcement, real-time flow optimization, and dynamic vehicle-to-infrastructure communication44–46. These systems can identify violations instantaneously, manage congestion proactively, and reduce collision risks through data-driven interventions47. Moreover, the anticipated proliferation of connected and autonomous vehicles (CAVs), equipped with sophisticated safety technologies, promises to dramatically reduce human error, which has long been recognized as the predominant cause of road crashes48. Complementary tools, including mobile applications and AI-enabled traffic management platforms, can provide authorities with actionable insights on emerging hazards, enabling swifter, more precise deployment of safety resources.

At the international level, collaborative frameworks and financing mechanisms will be critical to scaling these advances equitably. Initiatives like the Global Plan for the Decade of Action for Road Safety (2021–2030) underscore the importance of shared responsibility among governments, global institutions, and private sector actors in building sustainable, scalable road safety systems49. Increased investments directed toward infrastructure, local capacity building, and harmonized policy regimes hold considerable promise, especially in LMICs and African regions where high RTD rates converge with constrained resources. Strategic international assistance in these contexts can produce disproportionately large gains, delivering meaningful reductions in RTD at comparatively modest costs. Additionally, well-structured public-private partnerships (PPPs) offer avenues to harness technological expertise and unlock capital flows that might otherwise remain inaccessible for road safety initiatives50,51. By aligning commercial incentives with public health imperatives, these partnerships can foster the adoption of cost-effective, scalable solutions that accelerate progress toward safer, more resilient transport systems.

Finally, strengthening public awareness and fostering deep community engagement are essential pillars for sustaining long-term improvements in road safety. Well-designed educational campaigns that reach all categories of road users, including drivers, pedestrians, and cyclists, can cultivate safer behaviors and reinforce adherence to traffic laws. Community-based initiatives such as Safe School Zones52 and the promotion of pedestrian-friendly urban environments have already demonstrated substantial success in lowering RTD risks and should be systematically expanded. Moreover, fostering stronger collaborations among local governments, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and research institutions can facilitate the broader implementation of evidence-based interventions, thereby enhancing the overall impact and ensuring that mitigation strategies are effectively tailored to local contexts.

In conclusion, although major significant challenges continue to hinder the attainment of global road safety targets, there is significant scope for achieving meaningful reductions in RTD through strategic policy reforms, technological progress, and enhanced international cooperation. The Global Plan for the Decade of Action for Road Safety (2021–2030) clearly articulates the need to transition from unsafe, congested streets to mobility systems that are fundamentally safe, inclusive, and environmentally sustainable49. Realizing this vision requires a comprehensive and multi-sectoral approach that integrates rigorous policy frameworks, innovative technological solutions, and robust global partnerships to establish resilient and effective road safety systems across all nations. It is crucial to acknowledge the profound interdependence between safe mobility and sustainable development. A transport system that fails to protect its users undermines broader development objectives, just as meaningful progress in sustainability cannot be achieved without prioritizing road safety. The urgency of this agenda demands immediate and coordinated action. Only through the prompt and concerted implementation of holistic measures can the international community fulfill its commitment to secure and sustainable mobility, ensuring a healthier, more equitable, and resilient future for all.

Methods

Data source

This study employed a comprehensive dataset encompassing socioeconomic indicators from more than 190 countries for the period 2000 to 2021, together with panel data on road traffic death (RTD) rates, to examine the spatiotemporal dynamics and underlying drivers of RTD. The primary data sources were the World Bank (https://data.worldbank.org/indicator) and the World Health Organization (WHO) (https://www.who.int/data/gho/publications/world-health-statistics), both of which provided extensive time-series records on RTD rates at the country level.

Key socioeconomic variables, including national income classifications, per capita gross domestic product (PGDP), urbanization level, and school enrollment ratio, were extracted from the World Bank. Data on environmental pollutants, specifically PM2.5 concentrations and sulfur dioxide (SO2) emissions, were compiled from the World Bank, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/), and the Emissions Database for Global Atmospheric Research (EDGAR) (https://edgar.jrc.ec.europa.eu/). To ensure data integrity and minimize gaps, we systematically integrated information from these multiple sources, enhancing both the completeness and consistency of our dataset.

Additionally, data on road safety policies and national legislative measures were gathered from WHO records. This included metrics such as seat-belt wearing rates, maximum speed limits, blood alcohol concentration limits, and the total density of hospitals per 100,000 population. These factors are critical for elucidating how regulatory frameworks and healthcare infrastructure influence RTD rates. A detailed description of all variables is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

For the projection of future RTD trends, we utilized anticipated population and PGDP trajectories extending to 2050, derived from the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP) database developed by the IIASA Energy Program (https://tntcat.iiasa.ac.at/SspDb). This database offers scenarios that go beyond simple population estimates, incorporating dimensions such as age structure, gender distribution, educational attainment, and broader patterns of socioeconomic development across generations. By applying multidimensional demographic modeling techniques, we were able to project national populations and related socioeconomic indicators under each of the five SSP narratives53, thereby facilitating a robust assessment of prospective RTD trends across diverse global contexts.

To complement the fixed-effect MRM, which quantifies the ceteris paribus associations between key indicators and RTD rates while controlling for unobserved national heterogeneity, we further employed a structural equation model (SEM) to examine the plausibility and structure of the hypothesized pathways linking socioeconomic conditions, policy environments, and RTD rates. In our framework, the two models serve related but distinct roles: the MRM provides longitudinal estimates of the overall association from each indicator to RTD rates under time variation, whereas the SEM is used to assess the existence and direction of direct and indirect pathways that remain a “black box” in the MRM.

A single MRM can only accommodate one dependent variable at a time and thus yields direct associations conditional on a large set of covariates and regional effects. However, this structure makes it difficult and inadvisable to infer indirect effects by comparing coefficients across different MRMs, because the units, scales, and explained variance of predictors (for example, PGDP versus school enrollment) differ across models and influence coefficient magnitudes. Even when coefficients are standardized, deriving coherent indirect effects through MRMs becomes complicated given the multiple controls and fixed effects embedded in the regressions. By contrast, the SEM incorporates multiple linked relationships simultaneously and reports standardized path coefficients, allowing the direct and indirect impacts of each socioeconomic indicator on RTD rates to be represented in a comparable manner. The SEM also explicitly addresses partial interdependence among predictors, such as correlations between PGDP, urbanization, educational attainment, and seat belt usage, thereby clarifying intermediate pathways that underlie the broader associations identified in the MRM.

Due to data availability constraints, the SEM was estimated using nationally representative data for 2017, the only year for which a complete global dataset exists for essential predictors such as seat belt usage, alcohol limits, hospital density, speed regulations, and enforcement indicators. Importantly, we do not use the SEM to generate the coefficients for forecasting. Rather, the SEM provides a system-level consistency check on pathway structure and direction, while the MRM supplies the time-varying associations. Although coefficients from the SEM and MRM are not directly comparable in magnitude, both models assess the influence of each predictor on RTD rates in a consistent manner. In practice, we emphasize directional concordance between the SEM pathways and the MRM estimates (that is, consistent signs and qualitative relationships), rather than one-to-one equality of coefficients. Together, the combined use of the MRM and SEM yields a more complete interpretation of cross-country variation in RTD rates: the MRM quantifies robust longitudinal associations, while the SEM provides an integrated, system-level representation of interdependencies and the direct and indirect pathways that cannot be reliably derived from MRM results alone. By integrating insights from both approaches, we obtain a more comprehensive and mechanistic understanding of the drivers of RTD outcomes across countries. Robustness assessments, tests of alternative predictors, and detailed multicollinearity diagnostics supporting the stability and validity of the SEM are provided in the Supplementary Text.

The SEM exhibited acceptable model fit, with a Comparative Fit Index (CFI) above 0.95 and a Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) below 0.08. Future scenario development

We developed future projections of RTD rates up to 2050 by leveraging the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs), which provide consistent narratives of demographic and economic trajectories. As a foundation, we established a baseline scenario, referred to as “Business As Usual” (BAU), in which all demographic and economic parameters strictly follow the SSP projections. Under this scenario, improvements in education levels and hospital density are incorporated to reflect general socioeconomic improvements associated with economic growth. However, no additional policy interventions or behavioral changes are introduced specifically to reduce RTD. Traffic laws, regulatory frameworks, and seat belt usage rates remain fixed at their current levels throughout the projection period.

To assess the potential benefits of more proactive approaches, we designed a suite of targeted mitigation practices that estimate the impacts of deliberate interventions on future RTD rates. Within these scenarios, per capita GDP and urbanization trajectories continue to follow the SSP assumptions. Future improvements in school enrollment rates are incorporated by using projections from the Barro-Lee Educational Attainment Dataset as the baseline, after which school enrollment rates are adjusted upward according to each country’s economic development level. Under this approach, enrollment rates rise approximately 15 to 30% above the baseline trajectory by 2050, reflecting continued economic expansion and global educational trends. Hospital density is also projected to increase progressively from current levels, with emphasis on strengthening post-crash emergency care capacity. Based on income group patterns and urbanization trends, we estimate hospital density will grow by 25 to 50% relative to 2020 levels by 2050. In terms of governance and individual behaviors, we assume that all countries will reach the WHO’s best practice standards for road safety legislation by 2050, following a linear progression beginning in 2020. Due to data constraints, our analysis focuses on three specific legislative components. First, we assume that urban speed limits will be standardized at 50 km per hour worldwide by 2050. Second, in countries where the current legal blood alcohol concentration (BAC) threshold exceeds 0.05 g per deciliter, this limit is assumed to decrease to 0.05 g per deciliter. Concurrently, strengthened safety education and enhanced national legislation are projected to raise seat belt usage rates to 100% globally by 2050. A detailed description of the mitigation-scenario variables and projected key indicator values for 2050 is provided in Supplementary Table 5.

It is important to note that environmental variables, such as PM2.5 concentrations, were not included as predictors in the future projections. This choice reflects both the substantial heterogeneity in their determinants across regions and the fact that, in macro-level panel regressions, such variables can act as proxies for broader, time-varying contextual conditions (for example, urban density, congestion, and industrial or travel activity) rather than representing stable, directly policy-actionable levers. Moreover, projecting future environmental conditions would require additional scenario-specific assumptions and coupled atmospheric and activity models that are beyond the scope of this study and would introduce considerable uncertainty. We therefore restricted the projection model to socio-economic and safety-system determinants with clearer interpretability and more consistent scenario pathways, ensuring that the forecasts remain robust and comparable across countries.
