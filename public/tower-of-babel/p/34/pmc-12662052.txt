Pod photosynthesis: a new frontier for developing stress-resilient and high-yielding crops
Authors: Jing Zhang, Hendry Susila, Sadia Majeed, Gonzalo M Estavillio, Harsh Raman, Barry J Pogson, Robert T Furbank
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBurgeoning global demand for crop products and the negative impact of climate change on crop production are driving the need to improve yield by developing new elite crop varieties without expanding planted area or increasing agronomic inputs. Improvement in photosynthesis is critical for enhancing crop productivity. Even though leaf photosynthesis is well-studied, the photosynthetic potential of non-foliar green tissues like pods in Brassicaceae and Fabaceae species remains underexplored. This review emphasizes pod photosynthesis in determining seed yield and quality in Brassicaceae and Fabaceae crops. At present, accurate and efficient phenotyping methods are unavailable, limiting understanding and genetic improvement of pod photosynthesis. Novel approaches like chlorophyll fluorescence and hyperspectral reflectance are promising for high-throughput phenotyping of pod photosynthetic traits. This review further discusses genetic targets and regulatory mechanisms for enhancing pod photosynthesis, including transcription factors like GOLDEN2-LIKE and GATA that may regulate photosynthetic capacity in pods, suggesting potential genetic manipulation strategies to boost crop productivity. In conclusion, unlocking the genetic and physiological bases of pod photosynthesis offers opportunities for advancing crop breeding to ensure sustainable food security amidst climate change and increasing global population pressures. Future research should focus on developing high-throughput phenotyping tools and elucidating genetic pathways to maximize pod photosynthesis in crops.Keywords: Brassicaceae, genetic improvement, Fabaceae, methodological limitations, pod photosynthesis, yield

Introduction

Over the past half-century, selective breeding for high-yielding cultivars combined with advanced agronomic management has significantly increased the yields of major crops to meet global demand. However, the demand for food, feed, fibre, and biofuel continues to rise due to the ever-growing global population which will reach 9.7 billion by 2050 (Lal 2016). At the same time, climate change and the degradation of natural resources are negatively impacting crop productivity. These challenges highlight the urgent need to enhance yields by developing new elite crop cultivars without expanding agricultural land or increasing agronomic inputs in order to achieve food security (Godfray et al. 2010, Ingram and Porter 2015). Despite past gains, yield improvements driven largely by advances in traditional breeding and agronomic management have now plateaued (Grassini et al. 2013, Gerber et al. 2024). This trend on farm may be further exacerbated by the banning of glyphosate herbicide and certain types of pesticides in several countries in the world (Donley 2019, Camargo et al. 2020). To overcome these bottlenecks, innovative approaches are required to develop high-yielding crop varieties to feed the growing population (Simkin et al. 2019, Furbank et al. 2020).

Photosynthesis, the process by which CO2 is assimilated into organic compounds powered by the energy of sunlight, forms the basis of all food chains, and its efficiency is considered a key determinant of crop productivity. Therefore, improving photosynthetic efficiency has become a focus of intensive research (Simkin et al. 2019, Long et al. 2022, Sharwood et al. 2022, Leister 2023, Prywes et al. 2023, Smith et al. 2023, Croce et al. 2024). Several recent studies have provided strong support demonstrating that improving specific steps of the photosynthetic process through genetic engineering can provide an avenue to improve yield potential (Simkin et al. 2015, Kromdijk et al. 2016, Shen et al. 2019, South et al. 2019, Wu et al. 2019, Chen et al. 2020, Lopez-Calcagno et al. 2020, Perveen et al. 2020, Wang et al. 2020b, De Souza et al. 2022, Wei et al. 2022, Chen et al. 2025). Meanwhile, theoretical approaches with cross-scale modelling predict that photosynthetic manipulation will be effective in increasing crop yield (Wu et al. 2019, García-Rodríguez et al. 2022, Yin et al. 2022). Leaves are considered to be the primary photosynthetic organ in plants. Therefore, most research focuses on leaf photosynthesis with little understanding of nonfoliar green tissue photosynthesis and its contribution to yield and seed quality. The non-foliar green tissues include reproductive structures such as pods of Fabaceae species and Brassicaceae species, ears of wheat (Molero and Reynolds 2020), husks of maize (Pengelly et al. 2011), green floral organs (Bertolino et al. 2022), green seeds (Shackira et al. 2022) as well as other green nonfoliar tissues, such as stems (Tokarz et al. 2021), which all perform photosynthesis to provide an additional important source of photoassimilates for the plant. Recently, several review papers pointed out the importance, controversies and perspectives of non-foliar photosynthesis and how it is measured (Brazel and O'Maoileidigh 2019, Simkin et al. 2020, Lawson and Milliken 2023), particularly in C3 cereals (Tambussi et al. 2021). Among non-foliar tissues, the reproductive structures play an important role in contributing to yield (Hetherington et al. 1998, Aschan and Pfanz 2003, Hu et al. 2012). Because functional green leaf area declines in many crops due to leaves senescing during reproductive stages, photosynthesis of the reproductive structures can take over the load and become the major sources of CO2 assimilation to support seed growth, which is particularly apparent in Brassicaceae species (Zhang and Flottmann 2016, Kirkegaard et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2023).

However, in contrast to leaves, there is limited information available on natural variation in photosynthetic parameters and biochemical pathways in reproductive structures due to methodological limitations and the difficulty of assessing their contribution to overall carbon assimilation and yield (Molero and Reynolds 2020). Among reproductive structures, pods (also termed ‘siliques’) play an essential role in provisioning a carbon supply for seed development and yield production in Fabaceae and Brassicaceae species (Bennett et al. 2011) and have increasingly attracted researchers’ attention in recent years (Koley et al. 2022, Cho et al. 2023).

In this review, we will focus on the pivotal role of pod photosynthesis in final yield, particularly in members of the Brassicaceae family and the opportunity to drive a step-change in yield enhancement targeting these issues. Additionally, we will discuss the challenges of developing high-throughput methods to measure pod photosynthesis, the knowledge gaps to be filled to utilize this novel trait for yield improvement in breeding programs and the targets for genetic improvement of pod photosynthesis.

Pod Photosynthesis Largely Determines Yield and Seed Quality in Brassicaceae

During podding and seed-filling, there is a rapid decline in canopy photosynthetic activity as leaves senesce, and the canola (Brassica napus) pods themselves undertake photosynthesis which largely determines 1000-seed weight, seed size, seed yield and seed oil content (Hua et al. 2012). Therefore, pod photosynthesis plays a significant role in determining seed yield and quality in Brassicaceae. To date, its contribution has largely been assessed through indirect methods such as isotopically labelled CO₂ feeding, whole plant/organ shading, and herbicide application (Sanchez-Bragado et al. 2016). In Camelina sativa, in planta 13C labelling studies showed that proximal pod photosynthesis contributed to 33 to 45% of seed carbon. Remarkably, in the absence of leaves, the latent photosynthetic capacity of the pod wall alone could support up to 79% of the total seed biomass (Koley et al. 2022).

A recent review summarized the contribution of pod photosynthesis to yield evaluated by shading treatment in oilseed rape, soybean and alfalfa (Wingler and Soualiou 2025). For example, the impact of pod and seed photosynthesis on seed weight and composition were evaluated under field conditions by covering developing soybean pods with aluminium foil for 6 to 8 weeks. While recognizing that covering pods may have pleiotropic effects on plant performance, the results suggest that soybean pod and seed photosynthesis contributed up to 14% of the mature seed weight, while the effects of shade treatment on seed composition were not clear (Cho et al. 2023). The previous field shading experiments showed that pod photosynthesis can contribute up to 86% of yield depending on the duration and incoming light intensity under shading, which indicated that the factors determining yield and seed quality in canola are not tightly linked to leaf photosynthesis during vegetative development (as it is in most crops) but to leaf and/or pod photosynthetic capacity during the reproductive stage (Kirkegaard et al. 2018, Zhang and Flottmann 2018, Wang et al. 2023) (Fig. 1). Furthermore, in contrast to soybean (Cho et al. 2023), the canola seed oil content and composition were all affected by shading treatment. For example, shading the silique during podding resulted in a 45% reduction in seed oil content and changes in fatty acid profiles (Wang et al. 2016a). Similar findings have been observed in alfalfa (Medicago sativa), where shading studies revealed that leaf photosynthesis contributed only 26%–48% to seed weight per pod (Wang et al. 2016b). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that photosynthesis in the pod wall, endocarp, seed, and embryo, rather than in leaves, is the major determinant of seed dry matter for these crops. Further physiological and molecular experiments in canola (Hua et al. 2012) and Arabidopsis (Zhu et al. 2018) support the conclusion that pod wall photosynthesis is a key determinant of seed oil and seed yield (Liu et al. 2022). Moreover, the seed, endocarp and embryo of developing pods are actively photosynthetic structures during oil accumulation (King et al. 1998). In developing embryos of canola, Rubisco (ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) acts in a metabolic context in addition to producing sugars and starch via the Calvin cycle, to increase carbon efficiency by supplying carbon skeletons during oil synthesis. Although pod shading experiments offer a straightforward approach to assess the contribution of pod photosynthesis to seed yield, the results may be confounded by changes in pod microenvironment (e.g. increased humidity), changes to signalling by light and metabolic imbalances due to light exclusion. These potential side effects should be considered when interpreting outcomes.

Unique Aspects of Photosynthesis in Pods versus Leaves

The pods are significantly different from, and more complex than, those of leaves in anatomical structure, which have multiple photosynthetically active tissues, including the pod wall, seed coat, and embryo. The pod wall exhibits unique anatomical specializations as shown in Fig. 2a-j, with larger but fewer stomata, more tightly packed cross-sectional arrangements with larger cells, fewer intercellular air spaces, and fewer, smaller chloroplasts with thin grana lamellae compared to leaves (Lu et al. 2017). These anatomical traits might be linked to the reduced light capture and CO2 diffusion, leading to lower stomatal conductance, maximum velocity of Rubisco carboxylation (Vcmax), maximum rate of electron transport (Jmax) and photosynthetic rate of the pod wall. Although the photosynthetic capacity differs between the pod wall and the leaf, both utilize a similar photosynthetic pathway, where CO2 is fixed by Rubisco as part of the Calvin cycle (Fig. 3a and b). Apart from pod wall, canola embryos are green and contain chloroplasts with thylakoid structures and enzymes of the typical photosynthetic machinery, with 20% of incident light transmitted through the pod wall (King et al. 1998). Schwender et al. (2004) reported that Rubisco in embryo was active in fixing CO2 by the usual carboxylation reaction but not operating as part of the Calvin cycle to produce sugars as in the leaf (Fig. 3a). The alternative pathway could convert carbohydrates to oil more efficiently, with 20% more acetyl-CoA and 40% less CO2 lost from the seeds (Schwender et al. 2004). Meanwhile, the embryos demonstrated appreciable photosynthetic electron transport rates, generating NADPH and ATP to be utilized for energy-expensive fatty acid synthesis (King et al. 1998, Ruuska et al. 2004, Goffman et al. 2005).

Unlike leaves, where atmospheric CO2 diffuses through stomatal pores is fixed by Rubisco, internal recycling of respiratory CO2 produced in mitochondria occurred in pods of chickpea (Fig. 3c) (Furbank et al. 2004) and canola (Fig. 3d) (Mogensen et al. 1997, King et al. 1998). In chickpea, although both the embryo and seed coats are photosynthetic, the pod wall plays the predominant role in this refixation which was supported by the evidence that more than 80% of isotopically labelled CO2 applied to the internal pod airspace was fixed by the pod wall (Fig. 3c) (Furbank et al. 2004). Indeed, a comparison of chickpea pod electron transport rates estimated from chlorophyll fluorescence in the presence and absence of externally provided CO2 suggest that the majority of pod wall photosynthetic capacity is used for recycling CO2 not fixing de novo from the atmosphere (Furbank et al. 2004). This will of course depend on developmental age of the pod and seed tissues. In contrast, in canola pods, while developing seeds and pod wall endocarp can potentially refix respired CO2, the embryo chloroplasts play a critical role in this refixation while the pod largely fixes atmospheric CO2 (King et al. 1998). The seed coat was shown to be a substantive barrier to CO2 diffusion out of the seed (King et al. 1998), retaining respiratory CO2 within the green embryo (Fig. 3d). In all, the presence of glycolysis, oxidative pentose-phosphate pathway, Rubisco and photosystems in green seeds provides alternative metabolic routes that allow adaptation to different environments and thereby maximize the use of both the carbon provided by the mother plant and the light available to the embryo (Schwender et al. 2004).

Methodological Limitations in Quantifying Pod Photosynthesis

Despite the pivotal contribution of pod photosynthesis to crop seed yield, there is no information about genetic vatiation and its potential as a breeding target due to lack of efficient high-throughput methods. Pod photosynthetic capacity have been typically investigated using gas exchange systems based on infrared gas analysis (IRGA) i.e. LI-6400XT or LI-6800 (LI-COR Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA).

IRGA chambers with top and bottom illumination of the pod have been used for canola (Lu et al. 2019) and soybean pods (Cho et al. 2023) (Fig. 4a). The limitations of this approach for measuring pod photosynthesis include (i) the long duration of the measurement i.e. ~ 20 minutes for each measurement; (ii) the need to harvest the pod and to scan the projected area to normalize the measurement; (iii) the difficulties in measuring the pod temperature with the thermocouple to allow calculation of intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci). In addition, as pods are inevtiably fleshy organs and IRGA gas exchange systems and algorithms for calculating transpiration and stomatal conductance are designed for thin leaves with uniform properties across mesophyll cells adjacent to intercellular air space. The uncertainty of this method in providing an estimate of photosynthetic capacity is also concerning as net CO2 fixation does not account for the refixation of respiratory CO2 in the internal tissue of pod where atmospheric C-fixation is restricted (i.e. internal pod wall and seeds) (Lawson and Milliken 2023). As demonstrated in chickpea (Furbank et al. 2004) and canola (Mogensen et al. 1997, King et al. 1998) pods, atmospheric CO2 can diffuse into the cell through stomatal pores and be fixed by Rubisco, but CO2 produced by mitochondrial respiration can also be re-fixed by the pod wall and seeds; the relative contributions of these processes will depend on pod age, stage of development, internal barrier to CO2 diffusion, and light reaching the internal parts of the pod (King et al. 1998). With these limitations, it is impractical to screen germplasm diversity panels and mapping populations across multiple seasons and multiple locations to get reliable results for determining genetic variation in pod photosynthetic traits. Hence, robust methods need to be developed to accurately quantify pod photosynthesis and screen for variation of pod photosynthesis at a large scale.

Chlorophyll fluorescence is a rapid, direct and non-invasive, high-resolution technique to measure photosynthetic efficiency through monitoring the fluorescence emission of photosystem II (PSII), which is positively and directly correlated with chloroplast electron transport rates and leaf net CO2 fixation (Evans et al. 2017, McAusland et al. 2019). One widely used technique is pulse amplitude modulation (PAM) fluorometry which can measure the PSII and even PSI efficiency of dark-adapted and light adapted leaves and potentially pods in situ (Mizzotti et al. 2018). While PAM fluorometry cannot directly predict gross CO2 assimilation rates from atmospheric sources, it can be used to estimate pod wall photosynthetic capacity, since linear electron flow would capture light use in both fixation of atmospheric CO2 by pod wall and any internal refixation, plus energy use in photorespiration. To screen for genetic variation in pod photosynthetic capacity either in germplasm panels or transgenic plants, spot measurements of linear electron flow (LEF) can be made without dark adaptation in less than one minute. Several handheld, field portable PAM devices are now commercially available such as the MultispeQ (https://photosynq.org/) (Kuhlgert et al. 2016), which is widely used for assessing multiple photosynthetic traits in leaves including chlorophyll, LEF, quantum yield of PSII (ΦPSII), non-photochemical quenching (Ben-Jabeur et al. 2020, Deva et al. 2020, Khan et al. 2021). As shown in Fig. 4b, we have demonstrated that this device can also be used to measure LEF and ΦPSII of pods. Capturing canopy level estimates of photosynthetic capacity is challenging with PAM but recently a light-induced fluorescence transient sensor has been shown to be a reliable and high throughput measurement for plant photosynthetic performance in the field (Zendonadi Dos Santos et al. 2021). While technically challenging for field or glasshouse measurements of reproductive structures attached to plants without excision, chlorophyll fluorescence imaging is also becoming an increasingly popular screening tool for photosynthetic traits because it allows multiple samples to be monitored simultaneously under identical conditions. In wheat, > 500 samples day−1 can be measured to determine photosynthetic and photo-protective parameters on leaf segments in a dynamic light environment under controlled gaseous conditions (McAusland et al. 2019). This technique has been applied to the reproductive structures of wheat with success using detached organs (Furbank et al. 2020; Lawson and Milliken et al. 2022) which appeared to be photosynthetically robust for several hours if kept humidified, potentially useful for intact detached pods or even seeds (Fig. 4c and d).

Leaf photosynthesis-related traits have been predicted from leaf hyperspectral reflectance measurements (350 to 2500 nm) following training of models on validated data sets. During this training, reflectance data collected using instruments such as a full range Field Spectroradiometer (Fig. 4e) is coupled with ground-truth measurements of the target traits. Predictive models are then developed on spectral reflectance data using machine/deep learning techniques to predict physiological properties. For example, robust predictions methods are now available for wheat leaf photosynthetic traits using deep learning models which allow simultaneous prediction of a variety of traits (Furbank et al. 2021). These traits include leaf dry mass per area, nitrogen per unit leaf area (Narea), Vcmax maximum velocity of carboxylation at 25 °C (Vcmax25), electron transport rate (J), CO2 assimilation rate (A), and with relatively high Pearson Correlation Coefficient between the predicted and observed above traits, ranging from 0.82 to 0.98 (Silva-Perez et al. 2018, Furbank et al. 2021). Compared to traditional leaf gas exchange measurements, the hyperspectral reflectance prediction method is extremely rapid, taking only 1 to 2 min per measurement. This technique has been demonstrated to predict photosynthetic traits in maize (Yendrek et al. 2017), tobacco (Sexton et al. 2021), soybean (Kumagai et al. 2022) and various other species (Heckmann et al. 2017, Boshkovski et al. 2020, Fu et al. 2020). While this technique has not yet been applied to reproductive organs such as pods, it has strong potential. If sufficiently large training datasets combining traditional low-throughput gas exchange data with hyperspectral reflectance data can be generated, pod-specific analytical models could be developed. This would enable high-throughput screening of pod photosynthetic traits in Brassicaceae and Fabaceae feasible.

Pod angle may be an important determinant of light interception for pod photosynthesis. While light interception in cereals and canopy architecture has been a focus of breeding and thought to be largely optimized (Furbank et al. 2019), pod angle has recently become a topic of investigation in breeding programs of crops where pods are major photosynthetic organs (Yang et al. 2022). Phenotyping silique morphology in canola using skeletonization with hierarchical segmentation has been reported with a 3D handheld scanner (Ma et al. 2023). Multispectral 3D scanners (e.g. PlantEye F600 multispectral 3D scanner for plants - PHENOSPEX) could be used in screening for biomass, leaf and pod area, normalized difference vegetative index measurements, and potentially pod angle of canola (Fig. 4f and g).

Pod Photosynthesis under Water Deficit Conditions

Photosynthesis is a complex physiological and metabolic process sensitive to temperature and water availability, and as such, a target of intensive research for developing stress-resilient high-yielding crops (Hatfield and Dold 2019, Lawson and Vialet-Chabrand 2019, Leakey et al. 2019, Simkin et al. 2019, Ahmad et al. 2020, Nguyen et al. 2023). Terminal drought can severly limit yield potential in grain crops and most commonly occurs during podding and seed filling when most of the photoassimilates are derived from pod to support and fuel seed growth. Therefore, pod photosynthesis is particularly important under water deficit conditions (Ma et al. 2001). Pod walls have stomata, but their behaviour under drought has not been extensively studied as in other non-foliar organs. In canola, the growth rate of seeds was not influenced by intermittent periods of water stress that occurred during seed filling (Mogensen et al. 1997). This may be due to pod succulence and low stomatal conductance, which resulted in only small changes in water potential per day (Ψ day −1) during soil drying, thereby maintainining high relative water content during severe drought (Mogensen et al. 1997). In chickpea, 13CO2 labelling experiments showed that pods had higher gross photosynthetic rates than leaves; the possible explanation was that the internal recycling of CO2 protected from photodamage and assisted in maintaining seed filling under water stress (Ma et al. 2001). In cotton, the bracts showed significantly lower stomatal conductance which contribute to significantly higher water use efficiency (Hu et al. 2013). In alfalfa, the pod wall had been proposed to support a facultative C4 pathway, providing drought tolerance (Wang et al. 2020a). However, as with early reports of C4 photosynthesis in Brassica pods (Singal et al. 1987), this has never been proven by isotopic labelling experiments showing movement of labelled carbon from C4 acids to sugars (Hibberd and Furbank 2016). Therefore, the topic of pod photosynthesis presents the community with a challenge to integrate the fields of photosynthesis, development, and stress responses (Brazel and O'Maoileidigh 2019). Similarly, in C3 cereals, the importance and contribution of ear or spike photosynthesis to yield are considered even more important when foliar tissues are damaged or stressed (Ntakirutimana and Xie 2020) and wheat ear photosynthetic efficiency appears to be less affected by drought compared to that of leaves (Tambussi et al. 2021). Under water stress conditions, wheat ears exhibited a relatively higher water content with stronger osmotic adjustments, delayed senescence, and great surface area compared to leaves, which favours its role as a major photosynthetic organ during grain filling under water stress (Tambussi et al. 2005, Jia et al. 2015, Vicente et al. 2018, Tambussi et al. 2021). Given the loss of leaf photosynthetic area in canola after podding, it is likely that the photosynthesis in reproductive structures under drought will be even more important than that of wheat.

Master Regulators as Potential Targets for Genetic Improvement of Pod Photosynthesis and Accelerating Genetic Gain

To our knowledge, characterization of potential gene targets or genetic allelic variants contributing to the more unique aspects of pod photosynthetic performance remains limited. Currently, there are no generalized mechanistic photosynthetic models for pods, apart from those for soybean and camelina (Koley et al. 2022, Cho et al. 2023) and given the complexity of the organ, these are difficult to parameterize. However, given the difficulty of choosing the most high priority single gene target for modification, photosynthetic trait leaf-level modelling of C3 and C4 crops suggests that substantial yield gains could be obtained by manipulating several photosynthetic traits/genes in unison (Wu et al. 2019, Wu et al. 2023). Given that photosynthetic capacity of pods is commonly only ~ 50% of leaves on an area basis, improvement pod photosynthetic capacity starts from a relatively low baseline. Therefore, increasing the number and size of photosynthetic organelles in pods may be more beneficial than in leaves, where excessive chloroplast abundance could reduce intercellular airspace and limit CO₂ diffusion (Evans 2021). Rather than targeting individual photosynthetic proteins in a gene-by-gene approach, manipulating photosynthetic capacity by targeting global regulators is an attractive approach. This is supported by leaf-level modelling which suggest that increasing levels of several photosynthetic proteins in unison is likely to have the largest impact on yield (Wu et al. 2019, Sharwood et al. 2022). Transcription factors (TFs) that regulate many aspects of photosynthesis, including chloroplast biogenesis, light-harvesting, and carbon fixation, are considered good targets for improving photosynthesis. In rice, manipulating these TFs has led to significant increases of chloroplast populations in reproductive structures (Wang et al. 2017, Wang et al. 2018b, Sharwood et al. 2022). In this section, we will focus on several TFs that are involved in leaf photosynthesis as possible targets for improving pod photosynthesis.

Transcriptional regulation of photosynthesis is a complex process involving several internal and external cues, including light, temperature, and phytohormones (Wang et al. 2017, Hernandez-Verdeja and Strand 2018, Cackett et al. 2022) and few specific global regulators of photosynthetic capacity have been identified. Among these, several TFs have been implicated as master regulators of photosynthesis, as they directly activate the expression of genes involved in the light-harvesting complex, chlorophyll biosynthesis, and chloroplast biogenesis (Cackett et al. 2022). The GOLDEN2-LIKE (GLK) proteins, which are members of the GARP family of MYB transcription factors, have been identified in diverse plant species as essential components for coordinating the activation of the photosynthetic apparatus, including the light-harvesting complex, chlorophyll biosynthesis enzymes, and the assembly of chloroplast thylakoids and grana (Waters et al. 2008, Hernández-Verdeja and Lundgren 2023). Another RR-type MYB-related TF has also recently been identified as a broad regulator of photosynthesis, coordinating CO₂ fixation, photorespiration, photosystem assembly, and repair (Frangedakis et al. 2024). Two GATA-type TFs, GATA, nitrate-inducible, carbon-metabolism-involved (GNC) and CYTOKININ-responsive GATA factor1 (CGA1) are responsible for the development of chloroplasts from proplastids and control chloroplast growth and division (Wang et al. 2017, Zubo et al. 2018).

The ectopic overexpression of GLK and/or GATA synergistically leads to the activation of chloroplast biogenesis and photosynthesis activity even in the root, suggesting their central role in the development of photosynthetic machinery (Kobayashi et al. 2013, Ohnishi et al. 2018). The constitutive expression of GLK boosts chlorophyll content, photosynthetic rate, biomass, and grain yield in multiple plant species (Wang et al. 2017, Li et al. 2020, Yeh et al. 2022, Zhang et al. 2023). Additionally, the overexpression of PdGATA19 in the tree species poplar increases chlorophyll content, photosynthesis rate, and biomass (An et al. 2020). In contrast, the overexpression of MYB-related TFs, MpRR-MYB2 and MpRR-MYB5 in Marchantia does not lead to an increase in chlorophyll content or chloroplast size, albeit the mutants have reduced chlorophyll content and underdeveloped chloroplast, suggesting an intricate mechanism that regulates optimization of this process (Frangedakis et al. 2024).

Computational approaches suggest that control of expression of photosynthetic genes is likely due to the combinatorial interaction of several TFs (Wang et al. 2017, Cackett et al. 2022, Halpape et al. 2023, Hernández-Verdeja and Lundgren 2023). Recently, a Dof TF, COGWHEEL1, has been found to be a direct activator of several light-harvesting complex genes. Overexpressing this gene could increase photosynthesis by up to 29%, biomass by up to two-fold, and starch accumulation by up to 26% compared to wild-type Arabidopsis (Wei et al. 2023). Other TFs, such as growth regulating factor 5, promote chloroplast division and chlorophyll biosynthesis indirectly through the phytohormone cytokinin, which act as upstream regulators of GLK1, GLK2, GNC, and CGA1 to promote chloroplast development and chlorophyll biogenesis (Kobayashi et al. 2013, Vercruyssen et al. 2015, Kobayashi et al. 2017, Ohnishi et al. 2018, Doroshenko et al. 2022). Cytokinin has a positive effect on yields, as reduced expression of OsCKX2, a gene for cytokinin oxidase/dehydrogenase, causes cytokinin accumulation in inflorescence meristems and increases the number of reproductive organs, resulting in enhanced grain yield (Ashikari et al. 2005).

Nuclear Factor Y (NF-Y) is another TF candidate that act upstream of photosynthesis genes. The silencing of rice NF-YB (OsHAP3a, OsHAP3b, and OsHAP3c) results in pale green leaves and degenerate chloroplast formation (Miyoshi et al. 2003). In wheat, overexpression of Nuclear Factor Y-B3 (TaNF-YB3) and TaNF-YB4 significantly increased chlorophyll content, photosynthetic rate and early growth rate from elevation of expression of several photosynthesis genes, suggesting that enhancing photosynthesis through transcriptional regulation can increase grain yield (Stephenson et al. 2011, Azhar et al. 2024). Furthermore, in maize, ZmNF-YB16 played an important role in drought resistance by regulating the expression of a number of genes involved in photosynthesis, the cellular antioxidant capacity and the endoplasmic reticulum stress response, eventually maintaining the yield under drought conditions (Wang et al. 2018a).

However, most of the TFs involved in photosynthesis also modulate other physiological and developmental processes, including flowering time which can confound the phenotypic effects on photosynthesis and growth (Adachi et al. 2019, Sharwood et al. 2022). For example, overexpression of GLK and GATA TFs also results in late flowering (Wang et al. 2017, Susila et al. 2023). Similarly, several TFs that regulate chloroplast development, including phytochrome-interacting factor (PIFs) and HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5), are part of photomorphogenesis genes that also modulate hypocotyl elongation, leaf expansion, and seed germination (Hernandez-Verdeja and Strand 2018, Jing and Lin 2020). Moreover, enhancing photosynthesis by increasing the light-harvesting capacity in the leaves may also reduce light transmission to the leaves in the lower canopy, thereby reducing the whole-plant photosynthetic output-particularly under high-density cropping systems (Walker et al. 2018, Slattery and Ort 2021, Leister 2023). Therefore, an alternative strategy is to target TFs regulating photosynthesis specifically in reproductive tissues such as pods, thereby potentially boosting yield without affecting plant phenology and canopy-level photosynthesis. Interestingly, a previous report suggests that the mis-expression of GLK1 specifically in the pod wall could increase the thousand-seed weight and oil content of Arabidopsis thaliana (Zhu et al. 2018), reinforcing the feasibility of this approach. Similarly, overexpression of GLK transcription factors in rice has been shown to significantly increase grain yield and chlorophyll content (Yeh et al. 2022). The increase in chlorophyll was substantially greater in panicle than in leaves, indicating a greater margin for improvement in reproductive tissues (Yeh et al. 2022). Other approaches, such as modifying promoter regions by adding pod-specific enhancer elements through gene editing (Claeys et al. 2024), or by reconstituting allelic variation with superior pod photosynthesis through conventional breeding or genome editing (Garcia-Fernandez et al. 2021), could also be further explored.

Nevertheless, given the unique aspects of pod photosynthesis, which are still underexplored, simply boosting the expression of TFs without modifying specific pathways and anatomy may not be sufficient to improve pod photosynthesis and yield. Further studies on the limiting processes for flux in pod photosynthesis are necessary. These insights will provide a foundation for strategies aimed at enhancing photosynthesis in pods.

Future Directions

Enhancing pod photosynthesis is promising for the improvement of drought resilience and seed yield. However, pod photosynthesis has not been used as a breeding target trait in Fabaceae and Brassicaceae species due to the phenotyping methodological limitations and lack of extensive study. To achieve yield improvement by engineering pod photosynthesis, the researchers should focus on following future directions.

Understanding the rate limitations in pod photosynthetic flux

Pod photosynthesis is an unexplored avenue for the improvement of yield potential and drought resilience in Fabaceae and Brassicaceae species. However, there are several key knowledge gaps which limit the capacity to explore this trait. Firstly, the fleshy pod structure is physically much more complicated than thin leaves; is photosynthesis diffusion limited and is increasing stomatal density or pod wall conductance a key approach to improving photosynthetic flux? Secondly, there is limited understanding of the regulatory network controlling photosynthesis in pod wall, endocarps, and embryo, and the mechanism of carbon partitioning in different tissues. Thirdly, the effects of abiotic stress (e.g. drought and heart) on photosynthetic efficiency and mechanism are not well understood. Finally, natural variation of pod photosynthesis across genotypes and its genetic basis remains largely unexplored, and given the dynamic changes of photosynthetically active tissues across developmental stages is poorly characterized, when should photosynthetic performance be measured and compared? To address these gaps, fundamental studies are needed across scales e.g. morphology, physiology, biochemistry and molecular biology to identify the factors limiting pod photosynthetic performance leading to a more detailed understanding of the gene regulatory networks involved.

Developing high-throughput methods for phenotyping pod photosynthesis

Phenotyping techniques present a bottleneck for utilizing this trait in rebreeding and breeding programs where thousands of individuals must be compared. High-throughput methods for measuring pod photosynthesis accurately and effectively at scale, need to be developed to exploit genetic variation for pod photosynthetic traits. Estimation of the variation of pod photosynthesis and related parameters in large populations may be feasible by adapting some of the methods mentioned above. In addition, proximal remote sensing at the canopy scale may offer a powerful tool for much higher throughput phenotyping of plants for assessing crop traits. To effectively evaluate pod photosynthesis at scale, it is critical to develop field-based high-throughput phenomics for pod photosynthetic traits, utilizing the aerial and mobile platforms equipped with hyperspectral reflectance, thermal imaging, LIDAR and RGB imaging at canopy level (Furbank et al. 2020). For pods, this may be simpler than in cereal crops as in Brassica for example, the sole photosynthetic organ presenting to a top-down drone image not long after pollination will be the pod, rather than a complex collection of leaves at different depths in a crowded canopy.

Determining key regulators of pod photosynthesis

Genome-wide association studies are powerful tools for the discovery of the genetic basis of complex phenotypes due to the advent of next-generation sequencing technologies (Liu and Yan 2019, Zhang et al. 2024). By linking genome and phenome, candidate genes controlling pod photosynthesis could be identified to fill the knowledge gap about photosynthetic pathways of pod to prioritize gene targets (Furbank et al. 2019). Thereafter, allelic variation in novel genes/TFs and known TFs discussed above that regulate photosynthesis could be identified to develop new elite lines by either genome editing and/or overexpression or introgression of novel alleles from related species via wide hybridization.

Identifying temporal, spatial, or tissue-specific promoters and enhancer elements

Even though there are some promoters available for temporal, spatial, or tissue-specific expression of target genes in some model and crop species (Kummari et al. 2020), there are few such promoters designed for genetic engineering in Fabaceae and Brassicaceae species. However, with rapid advances in single-cell transcriptomics and stereomics data, future research should be focused on identifying temporal/development, spatial or tissue specific promoters or enhancer elements to be utilized in genetic engineering of pods (Xia et al. 2022).

Strategizing ways to improve pod photosynthesis

Given our immature understanding of the limiting steps in pod photosynthesis, overexpressing the master TF regulators in the pod using a pod-specific promoter could be the most straightforward way to improve pod photosynthesis given the results from other species (Zhu et al. 2018). However, unless this could be achieved by editing the promoter of the endogenous genes to activate expression, this would result in a genetically modified (GM) crop. The process for release and commercialization of GM crops can be long and complicated. But, in many countries, GM oilseed crops such as canola (or indeed the fibre/oil crop cotton) containing several stacked transgenes are commonplace as extracted oil is not viewed as a risk due to the absence of transgene DNA or protein in the final product. Photosynthetic improvement by traditional breeding methods would take considerable time and are likely not feasible for crops with a limited gene pool, requiring a large number of wide crosses and backcrosses to produce an elite variety. The application of new plant breeding technologies using genome editing offers a solution for crop improvement, especially in the current policy environment, where SDN-1 (small changes at the target site) and SDN-2 (template-guided repair by homologous recombination to introduce a specific DNA sequence replacement in the genome) after transgene removal may be considered non-GM and thus not fundamentally different from lines that carry natural variants (Buchholzer and Frommer 2023). Several strategies that could be implemented using genome editing to improve pod photosynthesis include: (i) removing repressive regulatory elements in the promoter to increase the gene expression of master regulators of photosynthesis genes, especially in the pod; (ii) gene or allele replacement via homology-directed repair for superior pod photosynthesis alleles; (iii) adding pod-specific enhancer elements to increase the expression of master regulators of photosynthesis (Claeys et al. 2024); and (iv) modifying protein domains via base editing or prime editing to improve the activity of proteins, including TFs and enzymes in photosynthetic reactions, especially in the pod. In the future, the application of new plant breeding technologies using genome editing targeting pod photosynthesis could potentially lead to a massive increase in crop productivity, especially in Fabaceae and Brassicaceae crops.
