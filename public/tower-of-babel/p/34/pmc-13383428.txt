Early versus delayed weight-bearing after ORIF of isolated AO/OTA 44-B1 lateral malleolar fractures: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: İsmail Güzel, Halil Saraç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal timing of weight-bearing after surgical fixation of isolated lateral malleolar fractures remains debated. Although delayed mobilization is commonly practiced, early weight-bearing has been proposed as a strategy to improve functional recovery without increasing complications. This study compared early versus delayed weight-bearing following operative treatment of AO/OTA 44-B1 fractures using a homogeneous fracture cohort.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 89 adults who underwent open reduction and internal fixation for isolated lateral malleolar fractures between January 2018 and December 2021. Patients were managed with either early weight-bearing initiated within two weeks postoperatively (n = 37) or delayed weight-bearing after six weeks (n = 52). All patients had a minimum follow-up of 24 months. Outcomes included postoperative complications, radiographic union time, and final functional status using the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) score. Statistical analyses included chi-square tests, t-tests, and exploratory logistic regression.ResultsOverall complication rates did not differ significantly between the early and delayed groups (3/37 [8.1%] vs. 6/52 [11.5%], p = 0.61). Time to radiographic union was comparable (7.1 ± 1.2 vs. 7.3 ± 1.5 weeks, p = 0.43). Final AOFAS scores were similar between groups (91.8 ± 5.4 vs. 90.9 ± 6.2, p = 0.47). AOFAS ≥ 90 was achieved in 32/37 (86.5%) patients in the early group and 41/52 (78.8%) in the delayed group (p = 0.38). In exploratory multivariate analysis, early weight-bearing was not significantly associated with postoperative complications.ConclusionEarly weight-bearing after operative fixation of isolated lateral malleolar fractures was not associated with increased complication rates or delayed union in this retrospective cohort. Functional outcomes were comparable between groups. These findings suggest that early weight-bearing may be considered in appropriately selected patients with stable fixation; however, prospective randomized studies are required to confirm these results.Keywords: Ankle Fractures; Fibula Fractures; Weight-Bearing; Early Ambulation; Fracture Fixation, Internal; Recovery of Function; Postoperative Complications

Introduction

Ankle fractures are among the most common injuries encountered in orthopedic trauma practice, accounting for approximately 9% of all fractures in adults [1]. The lateral malleolus is the most frequently involved structure, and isolated lateral malleolar fractures—classified as AO/OTA type 44-B1—constitute a relatively stable subset of ankle fractures [2, 3]. While conservative management may be an option for nondisplaced fractures, displaced or unstable lateral malleolar fractures are generally treated with open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) to restore anatomical alignment and joint congruity, thereby enabling early mobilization and reducing the risk of post-traumatic arthritis [4, 5]. However, postoperative rehabilitation strategies are often applied uniformly across different ankle fracture patterns, despite substantial differences in fracture stability and biomechanical behavior.

Despite advances in surgical techniques and implants, the optimal postoperative rehabilitation protocol for ankle fractures, particularly with respect to the timing of weight-bearing(WB), remains a topic of ongoing debate. Traditional protocols recommend delayed weight-bearing—typically initiated at 6 weeks postoperatively—to avoid potential complications such as implant failure, loss of reduction, and delayed union [6, 7]. However, prolonged non-weight-bearing may lead to functional limitations and patient dissatisfaction, prompting a shift in recent literature toward investigating early mobilization strategies [8, 9]. Studies suggest that early weight-bearing may improve functional recovery without increasing the risk of adverse outcomes, particularly in fractures with inherently stable biomechanics like isolated lateral malleolar injuries [10, 11]. Importantly, many of these studies include heterogeneous fracture patterns, such as bimalleolar or trimalleolar injuries, which may limit the applicability of their findings to more stable, isolated fracture subtypes [3, 6–8].

One of the proposed benefits of early weight-bearing is the potential enhancement of neuromuscular function. Prolonged immobilization may negatively affect proprioception, muscle strength, and joint mobility, whereas early mechanical loading may support sensorimotor recovery and functional rehabilitation [12–19]. In addition, early mobilization may reduce muscle atrophy, joint stiffness, and other complications associated with extended non-weight-bearing [20–22]. However, these mechanisms were not directly evaluated in the present study.

Early weight-bearing protocols may also improve patient satisfaction and facilitate earlier return to daily activities, while potentially reducing indirect socioeconomic costs [23, 24]. Moreover, prolonged immobilization has been associated with an increased risk of complex regional pain syndrome type I (CRPS-I), and early mobilization may contribute to reducing this risk by promoting peripheral circulation and sensorimotor activation [25]. As a result, there remains a need for fracture-specific evidence to determine whether early weight-bearing can be safely implemented based on fracture stability rather than generalized postoperative protocols.

While the potential benefits of early weight-bearing are well recognized, most studies addressing this topic involve heterogeneous patient populations that include bimalleolar or trimalleolar fractures, often requiring syndesmotic or posterior malleolar fixation. These injury patterns differ biomechanically and may not be suitable for early mobilization, thereby limiting the generalizability of existing findings.

In contrast, isolated lateral malleolar fractures (AO/OTA 44-B1) represent a more stable and biomechanically distinct subgroup. Evaluating early weight-bearing specifically within this homogeneous fracture population may provide more precise and clinically relevant evidence, minimizing confounding factors related to fracture complexity.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the clinical outcomes and complication rates associated with early versus delayed weight-bearing following ORIF in patients with isolated AO/OTA type 44-B1 fractures, using a homogeneous fracture cohort. We hypothesized that early weight-bearing would not increase complication rates and would provide comparable functional outcomes compared to delayed protocols.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single tertiary care university hospital and included patients who underwent open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) for isolated lateral malleolar fractures between January 2018 and December 2021. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the institutional review board (Approval No: 2024/432). All included patients had a minimum follow-up duration of 24 months after surgery.

Patient groups

A total of 89 patients met the eligibility criteria and were divided into two groups based on their early postoperative rehabilitation protocol:

Early weight-bearing group (n = 37): Allowed to begin weight-bearing as tolerated within the first two postoperative weeks.

Delayed weight-bearing group (n = 52): Maintained non-weight-bearing status for 6 weeks before progressive loading.

Group allocation reflected the standard practice of the treating orthopedic surgeon and was not randomized. The decision regarding early or delayed weight-bearing was made according to the surgeon’s clinical judgment at the time of postoperative follow-up. This non-randomized allocation introduces a potential risk of selection bias, which should be considered when interpreting the study findings.

This approach may also introduce confounding by indication, as patients deemed suitable for early weight-bearing may have had more favorable early postoperative conditions.

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed by board-certified orthopedic trauma surgeons using a standard lateral approach under spinal or general anesthesia. Fracture fixation was achieved using a one-third tubular or locking compression plate. No cases required syndesmotic screw fixation or additional posterior malleolar fixation. Postoperative antibiotics and thromboprophylaxis were administered according to hospital protocol [26].

Postoperative rehabilitation protocol

Early group: Encouraged to bear weight as tolerated from postoperative days 7–14, using crutches for support. Weight-bearing was performed as tolerated using a walking boot and crutches during the initial phase.

Delayed group: Remained strictly non-weight-bearing for 6 weeks, followed by partial and then full weight-bearing under supervision.

Progression to full weight-bearing was performed gradually under clinical supervision.

Both groups received physiotherapy focused on range of motion, proprioceptive training, and progressive strength exercises [27].

The decision to initiate early weight-bearing was made during the first postoperative outpatient visit and was based on a structured clinical and radiographic assessment by the treating surgeon, rather than a fixed timeline; however, no strict predefined protocol was applied. Patients were evaluated for surgical wound integrity (absence of wound complications or signs of infection), pain control and tolerance to loading, radiographic evidence of stable fixation without loss of reduction, tolerance to active ankle range of motion, and regression of soft-tissue swelling. Patients who met these criteria earlier were allowed to commence weight-bearing closer to postoperative day 7, whereas those who required a longer recovery period initiated weight-bearing between postoperative days 10 and 14. Thus, the 7–14 day interval reflects individual variability in early postoperative recovery while adhering to a consistent clinical decision framework.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome: Incidence of postoperative complications (wound infection, implant failure, loss of reduction, nonunion).

Secondary outcomes: Time to radiographic union, Final functional status assessed using the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) Ankle-Hindfoot score, The AOFAS score is a clinician-based scoring system combining pain, function, and alignment domains, with a total score ranging from 0 to 100, where higher scores indicate better function. A “successful outcome” was defined as an AOFAS score ≥ 90 at the final follow-up. Patient-reported pain using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS, 0–10) at final visit.

Functional outcomes were assessed only at final follow-up, and no early postoperative functional evaluations (e.g., within the first 12 weeks) were performed. Complications were recorded throughout the follow-up period and were not subclassified as early or late complications.

In addition to radiographic and functional outcomes, all patients were assessed for late-onset complications, including CRPS-I. Diagnosis of CRPS-I was based on clinical evaluation according to Budapest criteria, including disproportionate pain, sensory disturbances, vasomotor and sudomotor changes, and motor dysfunction [28].

Sample size and power analysis

An a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 software. Assuming a 15% difference in complication rates between groups (5% in early vs. 20% in delayed), a power of 80%, and α = 0.05, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 35 patients per group.

This assumption was based on complication-rate differences reported in prior ankle fracture rehabilitation studies, which demonstrated clinically relevant differences between early and delayed mobilization protocols [8].

With 37 patients in the early group and 52 in the delayed group, the sample size exceeded the minimum required [29]. However, the study was primarily powered to detect large differences in complication rates and may have been underpowered to detect smaller but clinically meaningful differences.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations and compared using the independent-samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test based on normality (Shapiro-Wilk test). Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

A secondary analysis was performed to compare the proportion of patients achieving AOFAS ≥ 90. An exploratory binary logistic regression analysis was performed to assess potential associations between selected variables and postoperative complications. Given the limited number of complication events, this analysis should be interpreted with caution, as the estimates may be imprecise and underpowered. Because the observed difference in complication rates (3.4%) was smaller than the assumed 15%, the study may be underpowered to detect smaller yet clinically meaningful effects.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 89 patients were included in the final analysis, with 37 patients (41.6%) in the early weight-bearing group and 52 patients (58.4%) in the delayed weight-bearing group. The mean follow-up duration was 29.3 ± 4.6 months, with no significant difference between the groups (p = 0.52).

Complications

Postoperative complications occurred in 3/37 patients (8.1%) in the early group and 6/52 patients (11.5%) in the delayed group (p = 0.61).

Complication details were as follows: superficial wound infection in 1 patient in the early group and 2 in the delayed group; loss of reduction (> 2 mm) in 1 patient in each group; and symptomatic implant irritation requiring removal in 1 patient in the early group and 3 in the delayed group. No deep infections, implant failures, or nonunions were observed in either group.

During the minimum 24-month follow-up period, no patient in either cohort developed clinical signs of CRPS-I.

Radiological union

All fractures united within 12 weeks. The mean time to union was: Early group: 7.1 ± 1.2 weeks, Delayed group: 7.3 ± 1.5 weeks (p = 0.43).

Radiographic union was defined as the presence of bridging callus across at least three cortices on standard radiographs.

Discussion

This study evaluated the impact of early weight-bearing on clinical and functional outcomes following ORIF in isolated AO/OTA 44-B1 lateral malleolar fractures. The findings indicate that early weight-bearing was not associated with an increased rate of postoperative complications or delayed radiographic union in this retrospective cohort. Functional outcomes were comparable between groups, with no statistically significant difference in the proportion of patients achieving excellent AOFAS scores (≥ 90). Importantly, the present study focuses exclusively on a homogeneous cohort of isolated AO/OTA 44-B1 lateral malleolar fractures treated with stable lateral plating. By limiting biomechanical variability and ensuring a minimum follow-up of 24 months, these findings provide fracture-specific insight without extrapolating conclusions from heterogeneous ankle fracture populations.

These results align with previous studies reporting that early mobilization does not adversely affect fracture healing or increase complication rates in surgically treated ankle fractures [30–32]. However, unlike these heterogeneous or shorter-term analyses, the present study adds focused evidence derived from a strictly homogeneous cohort of isolated 44-B1 fractures with a minimum follow-up of 24 months, allowing clearer interpretation of early loading in fractures traditionally considered biomechanically stable.

A key strength of this study is the use of a homogeneous cohort of isolated AO/OTA 44-B1 fractures treated with stable lateral plating. This reduces biomechanical variability and allows more precise interpretation of early weight-bearing in a relatively stable fracture pattern. Additionally, the minimum follow-up of 24 months strengthens the reliability of the findings.

One of the most clinically relevant observations was the high proportion of patients achieving excellent functional outcomes (AOFAS ≥ 90) in both groups. Although a slightly higher rate was observed in the early mobilization cohort, this difference was not statistically significant. Previous studies have suggested that early mechanical loading may stimulate joint mechanoreceptors and contribute to neuromuscular activation, proprioceptive input, and postural control [11, 33, 34]. These mechanisms may be particularly relevant for the ankle joint, where dynamic stabilization plays a key role in functional recovery. In line with this, Cimino et al. and Keene et al. reported improved proprioceptive recovery following early functional rehabilitation after ankle fracture surgery [35, 36]. However, proprioceptive function was not directly assessed in the present study, and these mechanisms should therefore be interpreted as theoretical rather than outcome-based findings.

Prolonged immobilization is also associated with muscle atrophy, joint stiffness, and delayed functional recovery [18, 19, 37]. Permitting earlier weight-bearing encourages muscle activation, helps maintain joint mobility, and may facilitate more efficient restoration of gait mechanics [38–40]. These physiological effects may explain the slightly higher proportion of excellent functional outcomes observed in the early group. Early weight-bearing may contribute to maintaining joint mobility and muscle activation; however, these effects were not directly evaluated in this study.

Another relevant consideration is the risk of complex regional pain syndrome type I (CRPS-I). Prolonged immobilization has been associated with increased CRPS-I incidence in lower extremity fractures [25, 41, 42]. In this cohort, no cases were observed. However, because CRPS-I was assessed solely through standard clinical follow-up without dedicated quantitative testing, mild or early-stage presentations may have been missed.

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk is another concern following lower extremity immobilization. Although no VTE events were identified in our sample, early ambulation remains an important consideration in postoperative rehabilitation [22, 43].

Early mobilization has been associated with improved patient satisfaction and reduced dependence on assistive devices in previous studies [24, 44–46]; however, these outcomes were not directly evaluated in this cohort.

456This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and non-randomized group allocation based on surgeon preference introduce a risk of selection bias that may have influenced the results, despite comparable baseline characteristics between groups. Second, while the sample size was adequately powered to detect differences in complication rates, the observed effect size was smaller than the expected 15%, and the study may be underpowered to detect small but clinically meaningful differences in functional outcomes. Third, CRPS-I was not evaluated using standardized or quantitative assessment tools, and mild cases may have gone unrecognized. Additionally, early postoperative functional outcomes (e.g., within the first 12 weeks) were not assessed, which may limit the ability to detect short-term benefits of early weight-bearing. Furthermore, complications were not subclassified as early or late events, which may affect the interpretation of their clinical timing. Lastly, although all patients had at least 24 months of follow-up, long-term outcomes such as return to sport or reinjury were not evaluated. In addition, unmeasured confounding factors such as fracture displacement, soft-tissue condition, and surgeon-specific decision-making may have influenced group allocation and outcomes. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution when generalizing to broader patient populations.

Conclusion

In patients undergoing surgical fixation for isolated lateral malleolar fractures, early weight-bearing initiated within the first two postoperative weeks was not associated with an increased risk of complications or delayed union compared to delayed protocols in this retrospective cohort.

Functional outcomes were comparable between groups at final follow-up. These findings suggest that early weight-bearing may be considered as part of postoperative rehabilitation in appropriately selected patients with stable fixation.

However, given the retrospective design, potential for selection bias, and limited sample size, these results should be interpreted with caution. Prospective randomized controlled trials are required to confirm these findings and to establish standardized, evidence-based postoperative weight-bearing protocols.
