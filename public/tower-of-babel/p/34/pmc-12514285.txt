AI-based modality-agnostic classification system for vascular calcifications
Authors: Mehdi Ramezanpour, Anne M. Robertson, Yasutaka Tobe, Xiaowei Jia, Juan R. Cebral
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The importance of vascular calcification in major adverse cardiovascular events such as heart attacks or strokes has been established. However, calcifications have heterogeneous phenotypes, and their influence on diseased tissue stability remains poorly understood. Precise classification of calcification phenotypes is therefore essential for determining their impact on tissue stability through clinical and basic science studies. Here, we introduce a new classification system for phenotyping calcification along with a semi-automatic, non-destructive pipeline that can distinguish these phenotypes in imaging datasets. This pipeline covers diverse calcification phenotypes characterized by their size-related, morphological, spatial, and environmental phenotypes. We demonstrated its applicability using high-resolution micro-CT images of five arterial and aneurysmal specimens. The pipeline comprises an annotation-efficient, semi-automatic deep learning-based segmentation framework for the segmentation of sample and lipid pools in large noisy μ-CT stacks, an in-house 3D reconstruction tool, and advanced unsupervised clustering techniques for calcification classifications. The segmentation framework achieved high accuracy, with mean Dilated Dice Similarity Coefficients (dilation radius: 2 pixels) of 0.998 ± 0.003 (95% CI 0.997–0.999) for sample segmentation and 0.961 ± 0.031 (95% CI 0.955–0.967) for lipid pool segmentation across all samples using only 13 manually marked slices for each stack. Relying on 3D models rather than input images makes our classification system applicable to any imaging technique allowing 3D reconstructions, such as micro-CT and micro-OCT. This provides a common language across studies to communicate findings on the role of each calcification phenotype and potentially paves the way toward identifying novel biomarkers for accurate cardiovascular risk assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19422-8.

Introduction

Arterial calcifications are linked to major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) that are categorized as the leading causes of mortality and morbidity worldwide1–4. For instance, in silico studies have shown calcifications in fibrous caps within atherosclerotic plaques can impact cap vulnerability5,6. The rupture of these fibrous caps can lead to serious health issues, including ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction. Calcifications are also believed to impact failure in other vascular tissues, including cerebral aneurysms3 and aortic valves1,7. Vascular calcifications display a wide range of physical characteristics (phenotypes) with regard to their size, spatial distribution, morphology, and surrounding tissue properties. Studies have shown that these phenotypes can modulate calcification impact on diseased tissue vulnerability, with some calcifications being deleterious and others even being protective. However, the exact role of each phenotype on tissue failure and their protective or deleterious nature has not yet been well understood, and in some cases, there remain substantial controversies8, which have important implications for the accuracy of risk assessment tools for MACE.

In terms of calcification size, in clinical studies, small calcifications were often categorized as either microcalcifications (0.5–50 um)9 or spotty calcifications (< 3 mm), while for large calcifications, there is no unique definition among publications3,8,10. These findings show that small or spotty calcifications are often associated with inflammation11,12, positively correlated with plaque rupture10, are more prevalent in patients with acute coronary syndrome, and are correlated with reduced collagen synthesis13. In contrast, larger calcifications are typically found in more stable asymptomatic plaques14,15. Biomechanical computational investigations corroborate these findings by suggesting that small calcifications can significantly increase cap intramural stresses while larger calcifications can be protective5,9,16–18.

The impact of calcification morphology has also been investigated clinically. Calcifications with irregular shapes—characterized as dotted or arcuated—are more likely to cause intraplaque hemorrhage (IPH) than patchy calcifications—large calcifications that occupy more than 50% of plaque19. Another study reported that thin calcifications (max. thickness < 2 mm) are linked to IPH20. A computational study also suggests that the jagged edges of large calcification may induce aberrant intramural stresses to the cerebral aneurysms, promoting tissue rupture21.

Calcification spatial distribution can be defined with respect to their location within the wall or with respect to each other, i.e., distribution density. With respect to the distribution within the wall, clinical findings suggest that calcifications close to the luminal surfaces are more likely to cause IPH20,22,23 and rupture in lipid-rich plaques23.

Regarding calcification distribution density, an in-vivo experimental study on mice showed an association between regions of plaque with degraded collagen fiber network and the aggregation of small calcification24. It is also demonstrated that these small calcifications increase in number and gradually coalesce to form larger calcifications24. Clinical studies have shown a positive correlation between the higher number of calcifications in plaques and plaque rupture22,25,26. Computational investigations have also shown that the presence of two densely distributed small calcifications can increase plaque stress by orders of magnitude6, which is consistent with the higher prevalence of IPH in plaques with multiple calcifications22.

With respect to the surrounding environments of calcifications, biomechanical studies have shown that the presence of lipid pools or necrotic cores near or around calcifications can modulate their impacts on tissue intramural stresses and stability. It has been shown that soft lipid pools can amplify the stress-inducing impact of small calcifications in caps5. In contrast, a computational investigation indicates that the presence of a soft lipid pool around large calcifications can attenuate aberrant stress concentrations in cerebral aneurysms21, which is consistent with experimental findings3.

Despite these general trends, there are important inconsistencies among clinical findings. For instance, some clinical studies link larger calcification volume to a greater rate of IPH27 and disease progression28, while in other studies, higher calcification volume is found in asymptomatic patients8,14,15, suggesting a protective role. These discrepancies may be related to some inconsistencies with coronary artery calcium score (CACS)29–33, which ignores small calcifications (< 1 mm2)34. Patients with low CACS may still be at risk of MACE32, and an elevated CACS has been reported among statin users despite the established benefits of statin in lowering cardiovascular risk35,36. Furthermore, a clinical study has reported a reduction in the rate of the symptomatic carotid artery in patients with a calcium score greater than 800 despite increased calcification volume15.

Considering these controversies and the phenotypic diversity of calcifications, it is evident that vascular calcification is a complex, multifaceted pathological condition with significant implications for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). The classification of the characteristics of complex pathological conditions such as cancer has shown promising outcomes in effective treatment and management strategies37–39. However, presenting a classification system for vascular calcifications is challenging, given the diversity in the commonly implemented image acquisition techniques, such as IVUS, OCT, CT, and micro-CT. A single classification system for calcifications that can be applied to all imaging modalities can serve as a common language among professionals to communicate their findings in the context of calcification phenotypes. This is essential for gaining a better understanding of the impact of each calcification phenotype in diseased tissue failure and resolving the existing controversies. However, to date, there is no such classification system for vascular calcification.

In this study, we present a novel modality-agnostic classification system for calcifications within vascular specimens. This system performs quantifications on the 3D reconstructions of calcifications and their surrounding tissues rather than the 2D input images. The system operates using a pipeline that employs advanced artificial intelligence-based frameworks to meticulously assign each calcification particle to one of eight phenotypes illustrated in Fig. 1.

Figure 2 illustrates the overall workflow of calcification classification, which can be divided into two major parts: data acquisition and pipeline implementation. The first part (Fig. 2A–D) represents the data acquisition process, including tissue collection, preparation, and μ-CT imaging, which generates grayscale image stacks of vascular specimens. The pipeline is then applied to the generated stack, which comprises three main components: 1—Segmentation: a semi-automatic deep-learning-based segmentation algorithm enables precise segmentation of samples and lipid pools (Fig. 2E,G,H), and image calibration performs calcification segmentation in the input stacks (Fig. 2I). 2—Reconstruction: an in-house 3D reconstruction tool generates 3D models of tissue components from the segmentation results (Fig. 2J). 3—Classification: Unsupervised clustering algorithms categorize calcified particles based on their spatial distribution, morphology, and location with respect to lipid pools (lipidic: inside and non-lipidic: outside lipid pools) (Fig. 2K–N).

Classification is carried out by automatically identifying each particle in the calcification domain and measuring its volume. Small and large calcifications are differentiated based on volume, with a threshold equivalent to the volume of a spherical particle with a diameter of 500 um3 and are labeled as micro and macrocalcifications, respectively (Fig. 2L). Microcalcifications are further categorized as clustered or isolated (Fig. 2M), while macrocalcifications are subcategorized based on their topology as dense or sparse (Fig. 2N). Sparse regions are distinguished by their thin, dotted, or porous nature, whereas the remaining portions are categorized as dense. The distinction between dense and sparse regions draws inspiration from the in-vivo experimental analysis of Hutcheson, et al.24, who identified a progressive evolution of macrocalcifications from microcalcifications. Furthermore, it is supported by clinical studies reporting that this distinction is important in plaque stability19,20. Finally, each of the four phenotypes (micro-isolated, micro-clustered, macro-dense, and macro-sparse) is further classified as lipidic or non-lipidic based on its colocation with regions of lipid pool domains, leading to eight distinct phenotypes in total (Fig. 1).

The pipeline introduced here can be used for clinical and scientific investigations into the role of calcification in tissue failure/stability that drives MACE. Moreover, it can be used to leverage the wealth of clinical data on calcification from both traditional and emerging imaging technologies. Hence, this pipeline lays the foundation for a more sophisticated analysis of calcification phenotypes and potentially identifying better biomarkers of adverse cardiovascular events. Furthermore, this pipeline can be used to provide a more sophisticated and quantitative analysis of the patient-specific effects of treatments, such as statin therapies, on intramural calcification. In the subsequent section, we present the outcomes of applying our proposed pipeline to five representative atherosclerotic samples (Results). The detailed description of each component of the framework that forms the backbone of our pipeline is delineated in the ’Methods” section.

Results

Application of the proposed pipeline for phenotyping vascular calcifications

The proposed pipeline was applied to five atherosclerotic vascular samples, and the resulting 3D reconstructions enabled detailed quantification of calcifications and their surrounding tissue composition, including sample and lipid pools (Fig. 3A and in silico generated cross sections in Fig. 3B). The classification framework identified a diverse range of calcification phenotypes (Fig. 1), such as isolated or densely packed clusters of microcalcifications, and dense or sparse macrocalcifications. These were distributed both within lipid-rich and non-lipid regions of the samples.

Figure 3C, presents the measured volumetric ratios of lipid pools to tissue and calcifications to tissue, enabling quantitative assessments of atherosclerosis and calcification extent, respectively. Across samples with macrocalcifications (samples with non-zero yellow bars), the proportion of calcifications (gray bars) located within lipid pools varied, but in most cases, a significant fraction (often > 60%) of calcifications were found inside lipid-rich areas. Other variables, including the proportion of macrocalcifications or clustered microcalcifications to the overall calcification volume, have also been measured. Among the five samples analyzed, there was a strong, statistically significant inverse correlation between the presence of clustered microcalcifications and the ratio of macrocalcification to overall calcification volume (Pearson r = − 0.999, p = 0.00003; Spearman r = − 0.9, p = 0.037), suggesting these calcification phenotypes are likely mutually exclusive. Additionally, a moderate negative correlation was observed between the ratio of lipidic calcifications to total calcification volume and amount of clustered microcalcifications (Pearson r = − 0.575; Spearman r = − 0.8), indicating a potential trend where samples with greater atherosclerotic involvement show lower clustered calcification, though this relationship did not reach statistical significance. Conversely, the ratio of lipidic calcifications to total calcification volume showed a moderate positive correlation with macrocalcification ratio (Pearson r = 0.58; Spearman r = 0.6), hinting that larger atherosclerotic regions may be more likely to contain macrocalcifications. While these latter correlations were not statistically significant due to the limited sample size, they suggest biologically meaningful trends that warrant further investigation in a larger dataset.

Further categorizing calcifications into macro and micro domains (Fig. 3D,E, respectively) and then breaking each group into subcategories based on their morphological and spatial features allowed more detailed analysis of the potential correlations between the phenotypic diversity of calcifications and their surrounding tissue properties (categories illustrated in Fig. 1). Among the five samples analyzed, a strong and statistically significant positive correlation was observed between Macro-Sparse-Lipidic calcifications and Micro-Isolated-Lipidic calcifications (Pearson r = 0.97, p = 0.005; Spearman r = 0.89, p = 0.04), suggesting a potential phenotypic continuum between isolated microcalcifications and early-stage sparse macrocalcifications in lipid-rich environments. Additionally, Macro-Sparse-Lipidic calcifications showed a moderate negative correlation with Micro-Lipidic-Clustered calcifications (Pearson r = − 0.88, p = 0.048), indicating these phenotypes may represent distinct or even opposing calcification pathways. Other intra- and inter-group correlations were not statistically significant but revealed several trends worth exploring, such as possible inverse relationships between dense and sparse macrocalcification phenotypes. These findings suggest a structured phenotypic organization within vascular calcifications and may inform mechanistic interpretations of their development.

The time required to complete each step of the pipeline after obtaining the μ-CT images for each specimen (Fig. 2D) is presented in Fig. 3F. The image segmentation time was calculated by combining the required training and application time of the proposed segmentation framework for sample and lipid pool segmentation with the time needed to perform calcification segmentation via image thresholding. The required time for segmentations using the proposed framework has also been detailed in Table 1. Among the five samples analyzed, 3D reconstruction time and particle identification time exhibited extremely strong and statistically significant positive correlations with the total runtime (Pearson r = 0.99 and 0.99, p = 0.0011 and 0.0021; Spearman r = 1.0 for both), indicating they are the most time-consuming components of the pipeline. Segmentation time also showed a strong positive correlation with total runtime (Pearson r = 0.96, p = 0.0099), highlighting its substantial but slightly lesser contribution. A moderate correlation was observed between total microcalcification count and runtime (Pearson r = 0.87), though this did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.056), suggesting a possible trend where larger particle counts may increase processing time. These results suggest that optimizing each of the image segmentation, 3D reconstruction, and particle identification steps may yield the greatest reduction in the total runtime.

A surrogate model for estimating microcalcification impact on collagen network density

The pipeline utilizes an unsupervised clustering algorithm to evaluate the detrimental effects of microcalcification distribution density on the distribution density of collagen fibers and, subsequently, on tissue strength. Discussion

Arterial calcifications have a significant correlation with major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), encompassing conditions like stroke and heart attack, and are linked to a range of vascular diseases, including intra and extracranial aneurysms and aortic valve diseases2,3,31,40–49. Identifying patients at risk for MACE is crucial for effective monitoring and treatment.

The Coronary Artery Calcium Score (CACS), the prevailing clinical risk stratification tool34, operates on the premise that a higher total calcification area correlates with an increased risk of MACE50. However, this assumption is challenged by findings indicating that large calcifications (macrocalcifications) may actually have a protective effect16,17,51. In contrast, it has been reported that small calcifications (microcalcifications), which can be invisible in images acquired by traditional CT scanners due to their resolution limits (2.5–3.0 mm sections)34, have detrimental impacts on plaque stability5,9–18. Additionally, statins, despite lowering the risk of MACE, are associated with increased calcification and higher CACS35,36,52. These contradictions highlight the need to expand CACS to account for more sophisticated calcification characteristics/phenotypes. Based on the reported impacts on diseased tissue stability in several clinical or biomechanical studies, these characteristics can be categorized with respect to factors, including size, spatial distribution, topology, and surrounding tissue properties3–6,9–26,51,53,54. Fundamental studies are needed to understand the diverse mechanistic roles of these calcification phenotypes and their complex interactions, which can subsequently inform the identification of clinical measures of calcification relevant to the risk for MACE. However, such studies require both a classification system for the diverse types of calcification and effective tools for its application to imaging data sets.

Therefore, in this work, we introduced a new system for categorizing calcification phenotypes, motivated by both direct (mechanistic) and indirect (associative) evidence of the role of calcification in adverse cardiovascular events3–6,9–26,51,53,54. We identified four main categories resulting in eight distinct phenotypes, distinguishing calcifications by size, spatial distribution, topology, and association with lipid pools (Fig. 1).

Having identified these categories, we then developed a pipeline that enables precise phenotyping of thousands of individual calcification particles across vascular specimens and provides a powerful means of distinguishing all 8 phenotypes (Fig. 1) in diseased tissues, which can have significant implications for the study and diagnosis of calcification-related diseases. The pipeline uses the following two artificial intelligence frameworks:

In the first framework, we developed a semi-automatic deep-learning-based image segmentation approach that is capable of discerning sample and lipid pool regions in thousands of high-resolution micro-CT images after training on only a few hand-labeled slices for each stack. This feat is achieved by a blend of techniques, including transfer learning. By harnessing the capabilities of UNet to discern common characteristics of regions of interest (ROIs) within the training slices and leveraging neural network classifiers to incorporate pixel spatial coordinates, we devised a mechanism that mitigates the impact of CT artifacts on model performance and delivers reliable segmentations. The high-performance scores observed in our evaluations are an expected outcome of the intended interpolation-based workflow: the model is designed to propagate a few user annotations to neighboring unseen slices within the same specimen’s stack. In this setting, high inter-slice correlation is not a source of information leakage but the primary signal enabling accurate label propagation. In sample 1, for example, the total number of input slices was 3269; only 13 slices were used for training/validation, and the remaining 3259 were considered as unseen slices. The model was then trained on the 13 seen slices, and then the trained model was applied to all unseen slices. Finally, the model performance was measured on a selected subset of 12 unseen slices, which were distributed to have maximum distance from the seen slices. For instance, in this particular case, there were 136 slices between each test slice and its adjacent seen (training/validation) slices, and the distance between each two consecutive seen slices (training/validation) was 272 slices. Given the small number of seen slices, the model could achieve a remarkably high score (> 96% for lipid pool and > 99% for sample regions). The same evaluation protocol was applied to all other subjects in this study.

In the second framework, we employed an unsupervised density-based clustering algorithm to perform microcalcification classification based on spatial distribution and to categorize macrocalcifications based on their topology. As an example, this framework was used to evaluate the relationship between collagen fiber density and micro-calcification cluster distribution density in a vascular specimen (Fig. 4). In the future, this surrogate model could be applied to a larger sample set, to determine a global density threshold above which the presence of microcalcifications is associated with local degradation or even voids in the collagen network. A second application of this framework could be to determine the relationship between the density of microcalcifications and tissues mechanical strength. For example, as discussed in section S5 of Supplementary Information (SI) (Fig. S5), comparison of the cluster maps with tissue failure location and mechanical strength could be used to identify a critical density threshold (defined using DBSCAN parameters through Eq. S2 of SI) above which microcalcifications can diminish tissue structural integrity and mechanical strength. Additionally, this approach could support clinical studies using high-resolution imaging modalities such as IVUS or OCT, combined with clinical outcomes, to identify spatial density thresholds associated with plaque instability and adverse cardiovascular events (SI section S5). Such analyses can be extended to other calcification phenotypes to investigate potential correlations between their presence and tissue rupture.

The classification framework presented in this study is designed to operate on 3D reconstructions of calcified regions rather than raw image data, rendering it inherently modality-agnostic. Once calcifications are segmented and reconstructed into 3D models, their geometric and spatial characteristics become independent of the imaging modality used for acquisition. Segmentation techniques for modalities such as optical coherence tomography (OCT) and intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) have already been well established and can produce suitable inputs for our pipeline55–61. As such, our method complements existing modality-specific segmentation tools by enabling downstream phenotypic classification of calcifications on any pre-segmented 3D dataset. This feature facilitates the aggregation of data across studies using different imaging systems (e.g., micro-CT, OCT, or IVUS) and allows for meaningful phenotypic comparisons that were previously infeasible due to modality-specific imaging characteristics. For instance, OCT and IVUS typically characterize calcifications using 2D metrics such as arc length, maximum thickness, and longitudinal extent along the vessel wall, while CT-based approaches like coronary CT angiography (CCTA) rely on calcification area and density measurements8, making direct comparison difficult. By shifting phenotyping to the 3D reconstruction space, our pipeline introduces a unified framework to overcome this limitation.

While the classification framework is theoretically applicable to modalities such as OCT and IVUS, its performance on reconstructions derived from these systems has not yet been experimentally validated. Differences in image resolution, contrast, and artifact profiles across modalities may influence the detectability of small or sparsely distributed calcifications. However, this variability also presents an opportunity. Because the proposed tool can be applied across any imaging modality that produces 3D reconstructions, it enables comparative studies to determine which modalities can reliably detect clinically relevant phenotypes. As proposed in Supplementary Section S5, high-resolution ex vivo imaging (e.g., micro-CT), combined with mechanical testing, can be used to identify calcification phenotypes associated with tissue failure. These phenotypes can then serve as benchmarks. By scanning specimens containing such features using multiple imaging platforms (e.g., OCT, IVUS), and applying the same pipeline uniformly to the resulting 3D reconstructions, it becomes possible to determine whether a given modality provides sufficient resolution and contrast to capture deleterious phenotypes. This strategy enables the tool not only to standardize phenotype classification but also to serve as a cross-modality evaluation platform—a novel capability that addresses a major barrier in the field and defines an important direction for future research.

Clinical imaging technology has advanced considerably since the time the Agatston coronary artery calcium score (CACS) was developed in 199062. These newer technologies enable in-vivo detection of microcalcifications and, therefore, have the potential to provide the necessary data to improve the calcium scoring index. For example, modern CT scanners like ultrahigh-spatial-resolution cardiac PCD CT have a resolution down to 100 microns63. Another area of emerging technology is intravascular micro-optical computed tomography (μ-IVOCT), which has the capability to image the inner wall of arteries in vivo with a resolution of 10 um64,65. Since our high-throughput quantification tool relies on 3D reconstructions of tissue components, it could be easily implemented for these data sets to provide the corresponding calcification phenotypes along the vasculature.

Such integration has the potential to improve upon the CACS. As an example, in our study, sample 3 exhibited a greater calcification extent than sample 1, as evidenced by their calcification to tissue volumetric ratios (11.5% vs. 2.1%), while sample 1 had substantially more microcalcifications (5509 vs. 104) (Fig. 3E). The current CACS measurement method, sensitive only to macrocalcifications, would disregard the role of microcalcifications, potentially underestimating tissue vulnerability in sample 1. Our pipeline also measures the proportion of clusters of microcalcifications to the overall volume of calcifications and the ratio of macrocalcification volume to the entire calcification domain (Fig. 3C). Previous findings indicate that macrocalcifications are more found in stable plaques4,14,15, while clusters of microcalcifications may induce aberrant stresses that can lead to rupture6,10. Moreover, the ratio of macrocalcifications to the total calcification volume can serve as a measure of the CACS reliability as CACS primarily focuses on macrocalcifications. A lower ratio suggests a higher presence of microcalcifications, which CACS cannot account for, potentially resulting in inconsistent assessments of the specimens’ risk levels by CACS. Furthermore, the quantitative information provided by the pipeline within each macro and microcalcification domain (Fig. 3D,E, respectively) is particularly valuable for studying the potential connections between macrocalcification topology or microcalcification distribution density and the type of the milieu in which they are embedded (lipidic and non-lipidic).

The pipeline introduced in this study provides an important tool for basic science studies, the development of more sophisticated risk assessment tools, and the design of medical treatments. For example, the possibility of improving cardiovascular health by removing calcification from artery walls is an area of active investigation66. In this regard, it is important to note that not all calcification phenotypes are harmful, and eliminating all calcifications may not necessarily improve tissue stability. For example, the removal of macrocalcifications may not only remove a potentially stabilizing structure, but it may also create voids in the extracellular matrix that increase stress on fibrous caps, inadvertently contributing to their failure. The proposed pipeline would make it possible to quantify the pace of calcification elimination by these treatments and its effect on different calcification phenotypes in either basic science or clinical studies.

With regard to the role of calcification in tissue failure, the pipeline supports both computational and experimental analyses aimed at mechanistic studies designed to understand the complex interplay of calcification phenotypes on intramural stress within vascular tissues. For example, for computational studies, the pipeline’s accurate 3D reconstructions can be deployed to perform further biomechanical computational studies to investigate the role of each calcification phenotype on tissue vulnerability (Fig. 3A). For experimental analyses, it facilitates the selection of regions of interest for mechanical testing and enables the quantification of dissected vascular tissue samples based on calcification phenotypes. Studies of this kind are needed for improving risk assessment and assessing novel therapies (36).

Future adaptations of this classification system may include additional categories and modifications based on scientific advancements and findings. In this regard, future releases may utilize three or more size groups for calcification size3, and may also involve the spatial location of calcifications within the wall or their orientations as additional phenotypes. While we have motivated the present work with applications to MACE, calcification is also found in heart valves, peripheral vessels, and many other soft tissues across the circulatory system2,67. The approach introduced here is equally applicable to calcification in other areas and can be tailored to the relevant phenotypes for a particular disease.

In conclusion, the presented classification system for vascular calcifications and the novel quantification tool enable analysis of calcification in diseased tissues and represent an important step towards improving risk assessment tools for MACE and other calcification-related diseases. Specifically, the proposed pipeline quantifies specimens based on type, size, topology, and spatial distribution of calcifications. It also simplifies information extraction for objective analysis of numerous specimens, aiding studies exploring the relationships between calcification phenotypes and disease progression. High-throughput quantification combined with mechanical testing can be used to identify the role of each calcification phenotype in the failure process. Applying these insights to emerging imaging modalities capable of capturing microcalcifications, such as ultrahigh-spatial-resolution cardiac PCD CT or μ-IVOCT, will enhance the diagnostic value of risk assessment tools such as calcium scoring, potentially reducing the economic burden of cardiovascular disease.

Deep learning framework for sample and lipid pool segmentations in the stack of high-resolution micro-CT images

Segmenting lipid pool regions in μ-CT images of calcified vascular tissues presents challenges due to diminished contrast and indistinct boundaries, in contrast to other regions (Fig. 1). Although a prior study68 introduced an automatic segmentation method using UNet, achieving a dice similarity coefficient of 0.41 for lipid pool segmentation, unresolved issues such as CT artifacts and overlapping macrocalcifications persisted, and no segmentation maps were provided. Despite employing 1601 training slices, the modest segmentation score for the lipid pools underscores the necessity of implementing a semi-automatic approach for the noise-prone micro-CT images of atherosclerotic calcified tissues. Semi-automatic methods prioritize efficiency over complete automation, allowing users to refine segmentation accuracy by manually annotating a few slices per stack for training and then applying the model to the remaining slices. This approach permits users to position training slices where regions of interest (ROIs) are clearly discernible (i.e., where micro-CT slices are minimally impacted by artifacts) to perform objective segmentations in other locations, including where artifacts affect ROI visibility through interpolations. After reviewing the segmentation results, users can later add more training slices at locations where higher segmentation accuracy is needed. In the following section, we describe a semi-automatic segmentation tool that minimizes the manual effort and model training time to ensure applicability while presenting reliable segmentations across all specimens within a stack.

Model description

Our method employs a hybrid neural network framework to semi-automatically segment both sample and lipid pools from μ-CT datasets of highly heterogeneous vascular tissues (Fig. 5A–Q). The framework combines UNet, a deep learning model tailored for biomedical image segmentation, with two sequentially connected neural networks to address the limitations of 2D UNet in discerning the gradual volumetric variations of ROIs and to reduce the required number of manually marked slices for each stack to perform reliable segmentations.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, the developed semi-automatic segmentation model trains on a few manually marked slices provided by users for each stack to perform segmentations on the remaining slices from the same stack. The proposed framework utilizes transfer learning, employing UNet as a feature extractor to train the initial neural network (sample classifier) responsible for distinguishing the foreground (sample domain) from the background. Details of the UNet architecture, including parameter choices, and the statistical distribution of manually annotated slices used for training and validation are provided in the Supplementary Information Section S3. During the training process, the kernel weights in the convolutional layers of UNet are optimized to assign higher probability values to pixels belonging to sample regions compared to other regions. This 2D feature map is then converted to a tall vector and is utilized as an input feature along with other features, including pixel 3D spatial information (inter and intra-slice coordinates) and their grey-scale intensity, to train the sample classifier. The inclusion of the pixel 3D spatial coordinates enables the model to discern the gradual volumetric evolution of the ROIs among the manually annotated slices. This, in turn, requires the manually annotated slices to be distributed in the input stack in a way that they can span the entire ROIs. Upon completion of training, the first neural network (sample classifier) serves to classify the pixels of the input slices into the foreground (sample regions) and background, which encompasses the dark background and any undesired regions, such as sample holders or CT artifacts. The resulting segmentation maps are then used to generate a foreground-pass filter that excludes background pixels from the training set of the second neural network classifier (lipid classifier). This exclusion significantly reduces the required predictive power to accurately classify lipid and non-lipid pixels and shortens the training time.

The second neural network is trained on a comprehensive set of 30 features, which are derived from both direct extraction and synthetic generation. The direct features consist of the 3D spatial coordinates of each pixel, indicating their positions within each slice and across the μ-CT image stack. Synthetic features are created by applying a 3 × 3 convolutional layer with 27 filters to the training slices, detailed in Appendix Table S4. These filters are fine-tuned by testing various filter weights on the training slices to identify those that best capture inter-slice features, aiding in the detection of lipid pool regions. The selected filters include Gaussian, median, blurring, sharpening, brightness adjustment, and edge detection filters. The resulting feature maps are then processed through a foreground-pass filter to exclude background pixels. The filtered dataset is subsequently fed into the second neural network (lipid classifier) to classify pixels as either lipid pool or non-lipid pool.

The serial fashion of the proposed segmentation tool makes it possible to optimize the segmentation of the sample and lipid separately. In particular, in cases without significant micro-CT artifacts, we can replace the slow deep learning-based sample segmentation model with a faster image thresholding approach, which can result in a substantial reduction in the sample segmentation time from 100 to 1 min (Table S2). The serial segmentation process also allows the efficient refinement of the results after the initial training, if needed. This can be achieved through retraining each model independently to refine the results by adding additional hand-labeled slices in locations where higher segmentation accuracy is desired.

To mitigate the risk of overfitting and prevent information leakage, we implemented a structured slice selection strategy. For each stack, a 25-slice reference grid with equal axial spacing was defined, and all 25 slices were manually annotated. From this set, 13 evenly spaced slices were designated as seen slices (7 for training and 6 for validation), arranged such that at least one validation slice separated any two training slices. The remaining 12 annotated slices were designated as the test set. By design, these test slices coincided with the axial midpoints between consecutive seen slices, ensuring maximum possible z-separation. All test slices, together with the rest of the stack (3256, 2528, 1588, 148, and 273 slices for samples 1–5, respectively), were considered unseen during training and validation. This arrangement ensured that training, validation, and test slices were spatially separated within the stack. Since the intended application of the semi-automatic segmentation tool is interpolation within a specimen’s stack, this test slices selection protocol also places test slices at the maximum possible z-distance from any seen slices (training and validation), and ensures that the test slices were not involved in the model training process.

Additionally, we integrated a convergence criterion during the training phase of the neural network classifiers, monitoring validation accuracy to halt training if no improvement was observed over a number of consecutive epochs (45 and 15 epochs for sample and lipid pool segmentation, respectively). This approach, coupled with the inclusion of a dropout layer within the UNet architecture and an early stopping criterion to stop the training process if the validation loss showed no improvements for five consecutive epochs, served as further safeguards against overfitting, enhancing the model’s generalization capabilities and ensuring robust performance across varied datasets. Finally, we evaluated the model’s generalizability on test slices located far from the training and validation slices, ensuring at least one test slice between each two consecutive seen slices during the training process.

Performance assessment-reliable segmentation of thousands of high-resolution μ-CT images by training on only a few hand-labeled images

The performance of the deep learning framework was evaluated on specimens from three intracranial arteries and two cerebral aneurysms. These five samples (denoted 1–5) were scanned using high-resolution μ-CT with a three-micron spacing with volumes ranging from 4 to 83 mm3 (Table S3), generating a range of stack sizes (3269, 2541, 1601, 161, and 286 images in Samples 1–5, respectively).

Two experts selected 25 uniformly distributed slices throughout each stack and manually marked both sample and lipid pools from each sample. This manual segmentation is performed based on the histologically validated characteristics of the sample and lipid pools visible in micro-CT images70. The model was trained and validated on just 13 manually marked slices from each stack. By selecting 13 training/validation slices for each sample, our primary objective was to compare the model performance across samples using a fixed number of training/validation slices and to showcase that the proposed model effectively streamlines the annotation of Regions of Interest (ROIs) in stacks of thousands of micro-CT images by merely requiring manual annotations on a few slices from users for each stack. This choice of 13 training/validation slices was informed by a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Materials, section S1), which demonstrated that increasing the number of slices beyond 13 did not result in statistically significant improvements in segmentation accuracy. The remaining manually marked slices (12 slices) were used for testing purposes. At least one test slice was positioned between every two consecutive training/validation slices, and there was at least one validation slice between every two consecutive training slices.

This sensitivity can be mitigated by using the dilated forms of performance scores (Eq. 3) rather than their standard forms71. The Dilated Dice Similarity Coefficient (DDSC), for example, ignores non-overlapping pixels located within a small neighborhood around the boundaries of the true and predicted masks. The size of this neighborhood, also known as dilation radius, is a user-defined parameter for calculating the DDSC. In this study, we used a dilation radius of 2 pixels to measure DDSC values. We achieved high performance scores, with mean Dilated Dice Similarity Coefficients of 0.998 ± 0.003 (95% CI 0.997–0.999) for sample segmentation and 0.961 ± 0.031 (95% CI 0.955–0.967) for lipid pool segmentation across all samples using only 13 manually marked slices in each stack. Supplementary Information Section S6 presents example slices, highlighting the differences between standard and dilated DSCs in evaluating model performance.

Average training times for each sample were less than 45 min (Table 1), with significantly more time spent on the sample compared with the embedded lipid pool (Expert 1: lipid pool mean of 331s versus sample mean 2291s, P = 0.009; two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test; Expert 2 lipid pool mean of 333s versus sample: mean 2309 s, P = 0.009; two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test). The average training time for the two experts was not significantly different (Expert 1: mean of 2623s, Expert 2: 2642s, P = 0.754; two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test).

Technical challenges for segmenting μ-CT data with calcification and embedded lipid pools

In general, μ-CT images of calcified vascular tissues have several categories of imaging artifacts72. We briefly review these artifacts and how the segmentation framework was designed to address these challenges.

Low border contrast between lipid pool and surrounding tissue: While the lipid pool regions are darker than the surrounding sample, the interface between the lipid pool and surrounding tissue is not always distinct (Fig. 6 Sample 1: A1, C1; Sample 2: E1; Sample 5: Q1–T1), hindering accurate segmentation of this boundary in some images. The lipid pool occupies a 3D volume with gradual morphological changes across the sequential 2D μ-CT slices (not shown). The shape continuity is leveraged through the inclusion of the z position as a feature in the neural network classifier, enabling linking information in neighboring images with sharper boundaries to improve segmentation in those with hazy boundaries (Fig. 6A3,C3,E3,Q3–T3), yielding average DSC scores above 0.8 for the lipid pools segmentations (Table 1).

Overlap of macrocalcifications and lipid pools: The overlap of macrocalcification with the lipid pool borders challenges the automatic identification of the lipid pools in some slices (Fig. 6J1–P1). The continuity of the lipid pool across the sample was again leveraged to address this issue and enabled accurate segmentation of the boundary (e.g., Fig. 6J3–P3).

Streak artifacts: Large calcifications, due to their denser material compared to the adjacent tissues, can induce intense streak artifacts, creating dark regions around the calcifications such as those identified with red arrows in Sample 3 (Fig. 6K1,L1), as well as dark/bright ray-like artifacts such as those in Sample 4, (Fig. 6M1–P1). Although these artifacts could severely impact the visibility of tissue or lipid pools in the affected regions in some cases, the proposed framework was able to effectively identify the sample and lipid pool regions (Fig. 6K2–P2 and K3–P3, respectively) Table 1. Further improvement of the model performance in these regions is feasible by adding more training/validation slices in the stack in the proximity of slices that are significantly impacted by these artifacts.

Ring artifacts: The most common artifacts present in μ-CT images that can significantly reduce their quality are ring artifacts72. Their presence hinders accurate segmentation of boundaries of ROIs. Samples 3 and 4 are severely affected by ring artifacts (center marked by the cyan arrow), (Fig. 6K1,L1), and (Fig. 6M1–P1), respectively. Despite the overlap of ring artifacts and both tissue and lipid pool regions, the segmentation results for the sample and lipid pool boundaries are in good agreement with the manual markings (Fig. 6K2–P2) and (Fig. 6K3–P3), respectively. This demonstrates the capability of the proposed framework for handling such cases without the need to perform any additional post-processing steps to mitigate the ring artifacts.

Undesired presence of sample holder: Sample holders are required to constrain samples inside the sample container during scans using μ-CT. In some cases, these holders may appear in μ-CT images at a greyscale similar to that of the sample, green arrows in (Fig. 6A1–E1,Q1), hindering segmentation of ROIs using the image thresholding approach. Our segmentation algorithm effectively leverages the spatial coordinates associated with each pixel so that pixels outside the ROI can be removed, e.g. (Fig. 6A2–E2,Q2).

Calcification classification

A novel multi-step framework was developed to classify calcifications based on size, spatial distribution, and topology, Fig. 8. First, each calcification particle in the domain was segmented using image thresholding, and then 3D models were reconstructed. In the next step, each particle is categorized as macro or micro based on a predefined volume threshold (Fig. 8A). In prior work, microcalcifications were defined as having an equivalent diameter of less than 500 um (e.g., Gade, et al.3). Here, we use this same critical diameter and prescribe all particles with a volume greater than that of a sphere with 500 um as macrocalcifications. In the next step of this study, the micro-calcification domain was inspected for the existence of any clusters based on their distribution densities using the DBSCAN algorithm, which is a density-based clustering method (Fig. 8B)73. The DBSCAN algorithm takes two input parameters, Eps and MinPts, which denote the range of the neighborhood within which vertices distribution density is computed and the minimum number of vertices required within that neighborhood to designate a region as dense, respectively. These parameters jointly establish the local density of the microcalcification distribution (Eq. S2). If the computed density for a particular region surpasses a pre-defined threshold, that region was identified as a cluster of microcalcifications; otherwise, the calcified particles within that region were classified as isolated microcalcifications. The values considered for Eps and MinPts were equal to 0.5 and 8000, respectively.

In the final step of the classification framework, macrocalcifications are examined based on their topology. The developed method in this step measures the volumetric integrity of the macrocalcifications and identifies parts of each macrocalcification that are thin and sparse with sharp and irregular surfaces (Fig. 8C). To perform this analysis, the volume of each macrocalcification is discretized using 3D cubic elements with equal length scales (0.02 mm) via an in-house code. Then, the unsupervised clustering algorithm implemented in the previous step is applied to the nodes connecting the cubic elements with Eps = 0.1 and MinPts = 400 to identify the dense parts of the volume of each macrocalcification. The subtraction of the 3D model of the input macrocalcification from the identified dense parts marks the sparse parts of each macrocalcification.

As shown in Fig. 8, the objective analysis of the studied specimens using the developed calcification classification framework revealed that the first specimen had two macrocalcifications, each with sparse and dense parts, and seven clusters of microcalcifications (Fig. 8A–C). The second specimen showed no macrocalcifications but had three clusters of microcalcifications (Fig. 8D). The third sample exhibited two macrocalcifications, each with sparse and dense parts, along with two clusters of microcalcifications (Fig. 8E). In the fourth sample, one macrocalcification, with both dense and sparse parts, was observed, and this sample did not have any clusters of microcalcifications (Fig. 8F). The fifth sample displayed one entirely sparse macrocalcification and three clusters of microcalcifications (Fig. 8G).

It is important to note that the current classification framework operates without ground-truth labels as determining which microcalcification cluster densities are biomechanically relevant depends on the application and, in fact, can be considered an output of the study. For example, when applied to mechanical testing data, this approach can be used to identify a critical density threshold of spatial clustering for diminished tissue strength (Supplementary Information section S5). Such an analysis is part of our planned future work. Without such data, standard supervised evaluation tools such as confusion matrices cannot be constructed. The classification categories are generated solely based on geometric and spatial features derived from 3D reconstructions. The set of DBSCAN parameters utilized in this study has been determined through a comprehensive sensitivity analysis for a range of DBSCAN parameters (Supplementary Information section S5). This set used here has been chosen to demonstrate the applicability of DBSCAN to such a classification task.

Calcification-collagen spatial distribution analysis

A multiphoton microscopy (MPM) dataset for Sample 1 was used for this analysis, as this modality can image both collagen fibers and calcification (3), (Fig. 4A). In the first step, each of the collagen and calcification channels was extracted (Fig. 4B,C, respectively) from the input dataset, and their 3D models were reconstructed (Fig. 4D,E, respectively).

The 3D model of the collagen channel was first divided into regions of low and high fiber density using the DBSCAN algorithm (Fig. 4D). Next, the 3D model of calcifications was superimposed on that of the collagen channel (Fig. 4E), and calcifications were labeled C1 and C2 based on their co-location with the low and high-density collagen domains, respectively. The DBSCAN clustering algorithm was then applied to the calcifications to cluster them into regions of low and high density. The calcifications within these regions were labeled as D1 and D2, respectively (Fig. 4F). The DBSCAN parameters were iteratively adjusted to obtain an accuracy of 80% or more for the match between D1–C1 and D2–C2 particles, collectively providing a threshold for calcification density corresponding to diminished collagen content.

This analysis was performed on the 3D point cloud models of each of the calcifications and collagen domains, and vertices represent the points located on the surfaces of the point cloud geometries. The density values are calculated by dividing the number of vertices by the volume of a sphere with a radius equal to the Eps parameter of the clustering analysis reported in Fig. 4.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons of model performance in this study were conducted using either the two-sample t-test or the two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test, depending on the sample size and distribution characteristics. Correlations between calcification phenotypes and model runtime were assessed using both Pearson and Spearman correlation analyses. Unless otherwise specified, results were deemed statistically significant at a P-value threshold of < 0.05.
