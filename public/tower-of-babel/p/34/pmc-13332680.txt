Body Image Following Ostomy Surgery for Cancer: A Systematic Review
Authors: Emma Hanlon, Lucy Hayden, Johannes Karl, Simon Dunne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOstomy surgery, often required in the treatment of colorectal, bladder, prostate and gynaecological cancers, has profound effects on body image and psychosocial well‐being. While previous reviews have examined body image in colorectal cancer patients, no synthesis to date, to our knowledge, has focused on body image across cancer‐related ostomy populations.MethodsA systematic review methodology was employed, following PRISMA guidelines. Four databases (PsycINFO, MEDLINE, CINAHL, and Web of Science) were systematically searched for peer‐reviewed, quantitative studies assessing body image in cancer survivors with ostomies. Inclusion criteria required validated body image measures and separable ostomy data. Ten studies met eligibility criteria, comprising 1454 participants.ResultsAcross studies, the presence of a stoma was consistently associated with poorer body image compared to non‐stoma groups. Permanent stomas were linked with the greatest body image disturbance, while reversal of temporary stomas was associated with improvements. Body image was associated with psychosocial outcomes including quality of life, anxiety, depression, self‐efficacy, and social support, and many of these psychosocial variables mediated the relationship between stoma status and psychological wellbeing. However, evidence regarding differences across cancer types was limited, and definitions and measures of body image varied considerably, with some studies relying on instruments not specifically designed for cancer populations.ConclusionsBody image distress is a significant yet under‐recognised concern for cancer survivors with ostomies. Greater conceptual clarity, standardised and ostomy‐sensitive measurement, and longitudinal research across cancer diagnoses are needed.Implications for cancer survivorsMore refined ostomy‐sensitive measurement, routine assessment of body image concerns and access to tailored psychosocial interventions may improve body image and related outcomes for cancer survivors with ostomies.Keywords: body image, cancer, cancer‐related ostomy, cancer survivorship, neoplasms, quantitative research, systematic review

Introduction

As cancer survival rates continue to rise globally, increasing numbers of people are living with the long‐term consequences of treatment [1]. An estimated one in four survivors will experience at least one physical or psychological consequence daily, linked to their treatment. One such impact is living with a stoma, following ostomy surgery for cancer, a life‐altering procedure often required in the treatment of colorectal, bladder, prostate and gynaecological cancers [2, 3, 4].

Across Europe, approximately 700,000 people are living with an ostomy [5]. Studies suggest that individuals with a stoma may experience lower self‐esteem, feelings of shame, and avoidance of social and sexual interactions, along with a reduced overall quality of life [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Ostomy formation involves creating a surgical opening (stoma) between a hollow organ and the body surface, used to divert the flow of stool or urine [11, 12]. Stomas can involve the gastrointestinal tract (colostomies, ileostomies) or the urogenital tract (urostomies) and may be temporary or permanent depending on the underlying condition and treatment needs.

Ostomy surgery can significantly affect how patients perceive their body's appearance and functionality [6, 7], and are often associated with disrupted sleep patterns [13] as well as adverse impacts on sexuality [8]. Although often essential for survival, ostomy surgery can deeply alter a person's relationship with their body and sense of self. Body image refers to an individual's perceptions, thoughts, and feelings about their own body, which may encompass internal representations (mental, emotional, and personal) and perceived societal or interpersonal views [14, 15]. Ostomy surgery can disrupt body image, leading to body image distress, which has been linked to psychosocial distress, anxiety, depression, and changes in bowel function [16, 17, 18].

To date, we are not aware of any systematic review examining body image concerns among cancer survivors with ostomies, particularly across varying cancer or ostomy types. A scoping review exploring body image in colorectal cancer patients found that individuals with a current stoma, or a history of a temporary stoma, reported poorer body image compared with those who never had a stoma [9]. Complications that delayed ostomy surgery were also consistently linked to greater body image distress.

As ostomy surgery is performed for various clinical reasons depending on the cancer site or ostomy function, its psychosocial impact may likewise vary by diagnosis [10, 19] highlighting the need for a comprehensive synthesis of the literature on body image following ostomy surgery across cancer types. This review focuses on the “stoma effect” collectively in this way because research has found that an abdominal stoma is a shared, conspicuous signifier that can disrupt body image and social identity regardless of cancer type [20, 21]. Ostomy creation causes major alterations to physical appearance and function, requiring patients to adapt both physically and psychologically. Moreover, although rare, certain gynaecologic/urologic cases (e.g., pelvic exenteration, fistulas or complications) do lead to faecal diversion, so excluding these based on diagnosis alone would omit individuals facing the same visible ostomy burden [21]. For these reasons, we opted to conduct a systematic review on all individuals where the presence of an ostomy itself (as a visible marker on the abdomen) is arguably the critical factor affecting body image. In this context, this review aims to: (1) synthesise existing evidence on body image concerns in cancer survivors who have undergone ostomy surgery; (2) examine variables associated with body image outcomes in cancer populations following ostomy surgery; and (3) understand how body image is defined and measured in the current literature on ostomy and cancer.

Materials and Methods

This systematic review was conducted and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [22]. It was registered with the following PROSPERO protocol registration number: CRD420261281194.

Search Strategy

A systematic search of the literature on body image in individuals with stomas was conducted across four electronic databases: PsycINFO, MEDLINE, CINAHL, and Web of Science (Social Sciences Citation Index only). Searches were conducted on 12th June 2025. The search strategy combined free‐text and controlled vocabulary terms related to body image and ostomy (see Supporting Information S1: Table 1 for full list of search terms). Search terms related to body image were developed with reference to previous reviews [23], as were ostomy‐related terms [3, 24]. No date restrictions were applied to the search.

Data Extraction

After duplicates were removed, titles and abstracts from the database search were independently screened by two reviewers (E.H. and S.D.) to determine eligibility. The full‐text articles identified as potentially eligible were sourced and independently reviewed for inclusion by E.H. and L.H., as S.D. was unavailable, using the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

For the screening process, any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion. The following data were extracted from each included study: (1) author(s), (2) country of study, (3) study design, (4) sample size, (5) number and type of recruitment or data collection sites, (6) participant demographics (age and gender), (7) cancer type (if reported), (8) body image assessment tool used, and (9) key findings related to body image concerns in individuals with cancer‐related ostomies.

Statistical Analysis

Due to heterogeneity in the analyses of the included studies, a meta‐analysis was not conducted. Consequently, we undertook a narrative synthesis of the included studies using the synthesis without meta‐analysis (SWiM) approach [36]. The SWiM framework was initially developed for intervention reviews but has now been widely recommended as an appropriate reporting framework for quantitative systematic reviews where meta‐analysis is not feasible, including observational and descriptive quantitative evidence. Importantly, our review questions allowed for the inclusion of intervention studies, as our research questions allowed for examination of both correlates and interventions related to body image in cancer‐related ostomy populations. Therefore, SWiM was both relevant and appropriate for transparent and structured reporting of our quantitative synthesis. The framework provides guidance on key aspects of synthesis, including the grouping of studies, methods used to synthesise findings, investigation of heterogeneity, presentation of results, assessment of the certainty of evidence, and reporting of the limitations of the synthesis. Use of the SWiM framework supports a systematic and transparent approach to narrative synthesis and facilitates interpretation of findings by readers.

Results

A total of 651 articles were identified through database searches (see Figure 1). After removing 160 duplicates, 491 articles remained for screening. Abstract review excluded 376 articles that did not meet the inclusion or exclusion criteria, leaving 89 for full‐text assessment. Of these, only 10 studies met all eligibility requirements. Study Characteristics

The included articles described a total of 1454 participants across 10 studies, 855 of whom had a cancer‐related stoma (CRO). Sample sizes ranged from 45 [30] to 282 [32]. Of the studies included for which gender was reported, 642 were male and 513 were female. Two studies included participants of unspecified gender [18, 31], and one study reported two gender diverse participants [29], while another failed to report the gender split at follow‐up [33].

All studies reported participants' mean age. In samples including only participants with a CRO, this ranged from 61.3 to 75.1 years [26, 27, 30]. Four studies that included individuals without a CRO reported only total mean ages ranging from 56.0 to 64.6 years [25, 28, 29, 33]. Additionally, three studies that included non‐CRO participants, disaggregated the mean age for CRO participants: 56.75 (SD = 11.15) [31], 58.85 (SD = 10.3) [32], 62.21 (SD = 15.33) [18].

Colorectal cancer (including colon and rectal cancer) was the most frequently reported cancer diagnosis in participants, appearing in 7 studies [18, 26, 27, 28, 31, 32, 33]. Other cancers included bladder cancer [26, 30] and unspecified bowel cancers [30]. Two studies did not specify any cancer type [25, 29].

Study designs included cross‐sectional (n = 5) [25, 26, 27, 28, 32], prospective (n = 2) [18, 31], prospective longitudinal (n = 1) [33], one‐group repeated measures (n = 1) [30], and randomised controlled trial (n = 1) [29]. Studies were conducted in a range of countries, including Turkey (n = 1) [25], Israel (n = 1) [26], Thailand (n = 1) [27], Portugal (n = 1) [28], Australia (n = 1) [18], Australia and New Zealand (n = 1) [29], the USA (n = 2) [30, 31], and China (n = 2) [32, 33]. Settings included hospitals, cancer centres, and community/online contexts.

Recruitment settings varied across studies. Three studies drew participants from multiple sites, including combinations of hospitals, clinics, and support organisations, with some also using webpages, online platforms, and tumour registry mailings to reach participants [26, 29, 31]. Among the remaining seven studies, those conducted in hospital settings drew participants from multiple sites [17, 33, 37, 38, 39], while studies based in cancer centres recruited from a single site [26, 28].

Quality Assessment

Overall, the included quantitative studies were of acceptable to good methodological quality, with total quality scores ranging from 11 to 22 out of a possible 24 (See Supporting Information S1: Table 2). Most studies clearly stated their aims, adequately described their populations and study designs, and employed appropriate and well‐described statistical methods. Measures were generally relevant and validated, and selective reporting was not evident across studies.

However, several recurring sources of potential bias were identified. Few studies included a control group, limiting causal inference, and justification of sample size was frequently absent. Reporting of non‐responders and non‐participants was inconsistent, raising concerns about selection bias. While most authors acknowledged study limitations, the depth of these discussions varied. Taken together, these issues suggest that while the evidence base is methodologically sound overall, findings should be interpreted with caution due to limitations in study design and reporting transparency.

Body Image Definitions

The included studies varied considerably in how they defined body image. Four studies provided a clear definition of body image [25, 30, 32, 33]. One of these studies [25], which examined body image perceptions, drew on nursing and psychology literature to define body perception as “the perception of an individual toward the dimensions, appearance, and function of his/her body” [37] and further described it as the mental depiction of how one views oneself and imagines others to view them [40]. Similarly, another study [30] conceptualised body image as perceptions of physical appearance and function, explicitly emphasising its multidimensional nature, which includes physical and subjective components, like feelings and attitudes toward the body [38]. One study [32] likewise defined body image as a component of self‐concept encompassing perceptions and evaluations of appearance and bodily function, citing cancer care guidelines [39]. In the final study with a clear definition [33], body image was defined as a multidimensional construct, which involves cognitive, behavioural, and emotional elements related to the whole body and its functioning [41].

Other studies emphasised body image distress rather than offering a conceptual definition of body image itself. For instance, one study [29] framed body image in terms of distress associated with changes to appearance and bodily function in ostomy patients. They noted that some individuals may come to appreciate their body more following ostomy surgery, while others experience significant psychological impairment when changes are perceived as disfiguring or alienating. Similarly, three other studies [18, 27, 31] focused on body image disturbance or distress as measured by the Body Image Scale (BIS), but did not explicitly define the underlying construct. The two remaining studies [26, 28] also omitted a definition altogether, instead relying on measurement tools to operationalise the construct of body image.

Body Image Measurement Tools

To examine body image measurement across the included studies, we explicitly examined and extracted information on each measurement instrument used, comprising four different measures across the 10 studies (Table 3). Only one of these instruments, the BIS, was specifically developed for use with cancer survivors [41]. Three measures provided a single overall body image score without separate domains: the BIS [41], the Body Cathexis Scale (BCS) [42], and the Draw‐a‐Person (DAP) quantitative adaptation [47]. The remaining measure, the Body Investment Scale—short version [46], assesses four general domains of body image (body image feelings and attitude; body care; body protection; comfort in touch), although it was not designed specifically for cancer populations. An analysis of these individual instruments revealed that none had been developed specifically for individuals with ostomies, nor did the instruments comprehensively address stoma‐related concerns (e.g., appliance visibility, leakage anxiety, bodily function changes).

Factors Associated With Body Image

Stoma Status and Bowel Symptoms

Four studies highlighted the association between stoma status and body image following CRO surgery [30, 31, 32, 33]. Two studies found that individuals with a temporary or permanent stoma were more likely to experience body image issues than those without one [32, 33]. One study [32] found that substantive body image issues (BIS ≥ 10) were most common in the permanent stoma group (47.7%), compared with the temporary stoma group (29.8%) and the non‐stoma group in colorectal cancer patients (p < 0.001). Mean BIS scores also differed significantly by stoma status (p < 0.001), with permanent stomas associated with the poorest body image (M = 9.93, SE = 0.96), followed by temporary stomas (M = 6.93, SE = 0.63) and then non‐stoma colorectal cancer patients (M = 4.64, SE = 0.56). The permanent stoma group also reported significantly poorer body image than the temporary stoma group (p = 0.009). In fact, stoma status emerged as the strongest predictor of body image (β = −0.19, p = 0.001), followed by age (β = −0.14, p = 0.018), and clinical stage (β = 0.15, p = 0.009). Furthermore, body image fully mediated the effect of stoma status on anxiety, depression, and global quality of life (QoL) in individuals with permanent and temporary stomas but mediated the effect on distress only in permanent ostomates.

Contrastingly, another study [33] observed no significant differences in mean body image scores across the three colorectal cancer groups (permanent stoma, temporary stoma, non‐stoma) from baseline (1–2 weeks post‐surgery) to follow‐up (6 months post‐surgery). However, participants in the temporary stoma group who underwent reversal within 6 months of surgery reported significant improvements in body image (p < 0.05). One study [31] developed on this idea further, establishing that, after adjusting for covariates, current ostomy patients reported significantly worse body image distress (M = 12.26) compared to those who never had an ostomy (M = 6.48) and those with a past ostomy (M = 8.67) (F (2, 138) = 11.68, p < 0.001, partial η 2 = 0.15).

Longitudinal analyses in one study [33] further demonstrated that both temporary and permanent stomas were associated with poorer body image, with odds ratios of 2.70 and 6.17, respectively, compared to non‐stoma colorectal cancer patients. Mediation analyses revealed that body image had a significant indirect effect on distress at follow‐up for participants with a temporary stoma compared to those without a stoma (indirect effect = 0.167; 95% CI: 0.024, 0.403, remaining direct effect = 1.535, p < 0.05) and for participants with a permanent stoma compared to those without a stoma (indirect effect = 0.319; 95% CI: 0.407, 0.712, remaining direct effect = 2.173, p < 0.05), indicating partial mediation. However, body image did not have a significant indirect effect on anxiety at follow‐up for participants with a temporary stoma compared to those without a stoma (indirect effect = 0.172; 95% CI: −0.104, 0.531, remaining direct effect = 2.427, p < 0.05) or for participants with a permanent stoma compared to those without a stoma (indirect effect = 0.329; 95% CI: −0.212, 0.983, remaining direct effect = 2.883, p < 0.05). Similarly, body image did not have a significant indirect effect on depression at follow‐up for participants with a temporary stoma compared to those without a stoma (indirect effect = 0.083; 95% CI: −0.197, 0.378, remaining direct effect = 2.365, p < 0.05) or for participants with a permanent stoma compared to those without a stoma (indirect effect = 0.158; 95% CI: −0.365, 0.750, remaining direct effect = 3.767, p < 0.05.

One study [30], which referred to temporary or permanent stomas as “stoma type” and continent or incontinent stomas as “stoma status,” also found no significant differences in body image outcomes based on these variables (all p‐values > 0.005 after Bonferroni correction), although their small sample may have limited statistical power.

Finally, one study [27] reported on the relationship between bowel symptoms themselves and body image among individuals with a stoma. Bowel symptoms were significantly associated with poorer body image, with greater symptom burden predicting more negative body image (significant negative path coefficient, p < 0.05; effect size not explicitly reported).

Quality of Life

Three studies investigated the relationship between body image and QoL in individuals with CRO. The first of these studies [28] found that body image dissatisfaction interacted with stoma presence to predict both emotional QoL (R2 = 0.477, β = −0.572, p < 0.001) and physical QoL (R2 = 0.372, β = −0.360, p < 0.001), indicating that colorectal cancer patients with stomas and greater body image dissatisfaction reported significantly poorer emotional and physical well‐being than non‐stoma colorectal cancer patients. However, body image dissatisfaction was not a significant standalone predictor of either emotional or physical QoL.

Meanwhile, another study [26] revealed perceived body image as a robust predictor of QoL (p < 0.001), accounting for 73.6% of the variance, after controlling for covariates. They also established a positive significant association between body image and QoL scores (r = 0.76, p < 0.01), with men reporting significantly higher QoL than women.

The final study [27] revealed significant negative correlations between body image disturbance (BID), as measured by the BIS, and Health‐Related QOL (HRQoL; r = −0.50, p < 0.01). BID had the strongest direct negative relationship to HRQoL (β = −0.44, p < 0.001), and was also a significant mediator of the relationship between gender, religion and HRQoL (β = −0.16, p < 0.001).

Mental Health Variables

Two studies examined the association between body image and mental health in CRO. The first study [27] reported a significant negative correlation between body image disturbance (BID), assessed using the Body Image Scale (BIS), and depressive symptoms (r = 0.78, p < 0.01). The second study [26] found that perceived body image, measured with the Body Investment Scale, was positively correlated with anxiety (r = 0.597, p < 0.05).

Social Support

Two studies explored the relationship between social support and body image among CRO populations. One study [27], identified significant negative associations between body image disturbance (BID) and levels of social support (r = −0.29, p < 0.01). The second study [26] reported a positive correlation between perceived body image, assessed via the Body Investment Scale, and social support (r = 0.490, p < 0.05).

Self‐Related Variables

In one study [26] perceived body image was positively correlated (p < 0.05) with self‐care skills (r = 0.335), self‐efficacy (r = 0.695).

Changes in Body Image Over Time

Three studies examined body image changes over time among individuals with CROs. One of these studies [30] reported statistically significant changes in body image across three assessment points: preoperative (1–7 days before the ostomy surgery), postoperative 1 (1 month following surgery), and postoperative 2 (6 months following surgery) (F (2, 44) = 3.13, p = 0.049). There were significant increases in mean scores from the preoperative assessment (M = 3.62, SD = 0.40) to postoperative 2 assessment (M = 3.76, SD = 0.53), as well as between postoperative 1 (M = 3.65, SD = 0.60) and postoperative 2, suggesting an overall improvement in participants' satisfaction with their bodies over time following surgery. No significant difference was observed between preoperative and postoperative 1 mean scores, indicating that body image may remain relatively stable immediately after surgery before improvement occurs later. However, no statistically significant changes were observed in the Draw‐a‐Person (DAP) scores across the three time points (p = 0.59).

Conversely, another study [18] found that colorectal cancer patients with a stoma reported significantly greater BID at baseline compared to those without (t 1,98 = −3.525, p = 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.57). This difference remained at follow‐up (following adjuvant or neoadjuvant treatment, or approximately six months later), with stoma patients continuing to report significantly greater BID (t 1 , 78 = −4.156, p < 0.0005, Cohen's d = 1.32). Further analysis regarding BID revealed a significant main effect for time (F1,78 = 7.669, p = 0.007) and stoma (F1,78 = 8.907, p = 0.004), and a significant time and stoma interaction (F1,78 = 4.378, p = 0.04), indicating that BID was higher for those with a stoma at baseline and that it worsened over time for these individuals, while remaining stable for those without a stoma. However, when specifically examining initial stoma status, no main effect was found (F1,78 = 0.081, p = 0.777), whereas stoma status at Time 2 was a significant predictor of body image disturbance at follow‐up (F1,75 = 13.48, p < 0.0005). No significant interaction was observed (F1,78 = 0.218, p = 0.642), suggesting that having a stoma at follow‐up, regardless of baseline status, was associated with greater body image disturbance at that time point.

Consistent with this variability, as aforementioned, a more recent study [33] demonstrated that body image scores remained largely unchanged over 6 months post‐surgery for both permanent and temporary stoma groups, with improvements only occurring following stoma reversal (p < 0.05). An important caveat to this study is that participants were free to drop out, resulting in some comparisons in a ∼14% dropout rate.

Differences in Body Image Between CRO and Other Groups

Four studies compared body image in CRO groups to non‐stoma comparison groups, finding significantly poorer body image in the former in all cases [18, 31, 32, 33]. Two further studies examined differences in body image in CRO patients to those with ostomies related to conditions other than cancer [25, 29]. The first study [25] found that CRO patients reported significantly higher body image perceptions (M = 135.6) than those with non‐cancer‐related stomas (111.0; p = 0.026). The second study [29] demonstrated an increased negative association between body image distress and ostomy as a result of cancer (ρ = −0.13) compared to inflammatory bowel disease (ρ = −0.07), though differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this systematic review synthesised evidence on body image concerns among cancer survivors who have undergone ostomy surgery for the first time. The convergence of quantitative findings across multiple independent samples in this review supports the characterisation of body image distress as a core and recurring concern within this population. This review also highlights that body image following CRO is closely linked to QoL, mental health, social support, and self‐related variables such as self‐care and self‐efficacy. Stoma status, particularly permanence and reversibility, is another key determinant, influencing body image both directly and indirectly through psychosocial outcomes. However, the overall evidence base is limited, with most studies focusing on colorectal cancer, only two on bladder cancer, and none on gynaecological or prostate cancers, highlighting a clear need for research in underrepresented populations.

Stoma status appears to play a central role following CRO surgery, exerting both direct and indirect effects on body image. Across the 10 included studies, findings consistently demonstrated that having a CRO was associated with significantly poorer body image, exceeding those reported by non‐stoma patients [18, 31, 32, 33] and those with non‐cancer‐related ostomies [25, 29]. Those with a permanent stomas appeared to experience greatest disturbance in body image, followed by temporary stomas and then no stoma [30, 31, 32, 33]. In one study, stoma status emerged as the strongest predictor of body image, surpassing age and disease stage, and clinically significant body image concerns were most prevalent among individuals with permanent stomas [32]. Importantly, body image functioned not only as an outcome but also as a key mechanism linking stoma status to broader psychosocial adjustment, with mediation analyses indicating that body image fully or partially explained the effects of stoma status on distress, anxiety, depression, and global quality of life, particularly among permanent ostomates [32, 33]. Longitudinal evidence further suggests that expectations of reversibility are critical, as improvements in body image were observed following stoma reversal and current ostomy patients reported significantly greater distress than those with past or no ostomy [31, 33]. The overall pattern highlights stoma permanence and reversibility as central determinants of body image adjustment. Taken together, these findings underscore the need for future research to move beyond direct associations and more systematically examine indirect pathways, particularly the mediating role of body image, in order to clarify mechanisms of psychological adjustment and inform targeted interventions for individuals living with cancer‐related ostomies.

Nonetheless, some null findings were reported in relation to stoma status, likely reflecting limited statistical power [30]. In particular, while body image was found to partially mediate the relationship between stoma presence (both temporary and permanent) and general distress at follow‐up in one longitudinal study [33], it did not exert a significant indirect effect on specific measures of anxiety or depression. This nuance suggests that the psychological impact of altered body image following stoma creation may manifest more readily as a broader evaluative distress or adjustment difficulty rather than discrete clinical affective disorders. Post‐surgical anxiety and depression in this group may be driven by direct factors independent of body image, such as structural changes in bowel function, somatic symptoms, fear of cancer recurrence, or overarching lifestyle disruptions. Further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the pathways through which stoma‐related experiences affect different dimensions of psychological well‐being.

In addition to stoma status, across the reviewed studies, body image was closely related to a range of psychosocial outcomes, underscoring its central role in adjustment following CRO surgery. More positive body image was consistently associated with better overall QoL, including emotional, physical, and health‐related domain [26, 27], as well as with enhanced self‐care skills and greater self‐efficacy [26]. Individuals with more positive body image also reported lower levels of anxiety [26], emphasizing both the relational and self‐regulatory dimensions of body image in this population. Conversely, body image dissatisfaction was linked to poorer emotional and physical QoL [28], reduced health‐related quality of life [27], diminished social support [27], higher depressive symptoms [27], and greater bowel‐related symptom burden [27]. These associations illustrate that body image is embedded within broader mental health, social, and self‐related processes, influencing both emotional adjustment and functional coping following ostomy surgery. Collectively, the evidence underscores the importance of assessing and addressing body image as a fundamental component of comprehensive cancer survivorship care given the range of psychological outcomes it appears to be related to, with the potential for interventions which target body image to enhance QoL, mental health, social integration, and self‐management for individuals living with CROs.

While these studies highlight important findings relating to body image in CRO, the review also underscores the inconsistencies in how body image is conceptualised and measured in extant research with these groups. Fewer than half of the included studies provided an explicit definition [25, 30, 32, 33], with some adopting multidimensional perspectives emphasising perceptual, emotional, and functional components [30, 33], while others defined it as an element of self‐concept [32], focusing on evaluations and perceptions of one's physical appearance and functions [25, 32]. Many studies, however, described body image distress or disturbance rather than body image as a standalone construct [18, 27, 29, 31]. Notably, over half of the studies relied exclusively on measurement tools, particularly the Body Image Scale (BIS), to operationalise the construct without offering a conceptual definition [18, 26, 27, 28, 29, 31]. While the BIS was validated in cancer populations, it was originally designed for breast cancer survivors [41] and may not fully capture the unique concerns of individuals living with a stoma, with no items dealing with changes to the body relating to a stoma. Other instruments, such as the Body Cathexis Scale [42, 43] (assessing satisfaction with specific body parts and functions), Body Investment Scale [46] (measuring emotional attitudes toward the body related to appearance, care, protection, and comfort), and Draw‐a‐Person quantitative adaptation [47, 48] (analyzing spontaneous human figure drawings based on features such as area, height, and centredness), further varied in their focus and clinical utility, with limited validation in oncology populations. Taken together, these findings highlight the need for future research to establish a clear, theoretically grounded conceptualisation of body image specific to individuals living with a CRO, alongside the development and validation of measurement tools that capture stoma‐related bodily changes, functional challenges, and psychosocial concerns. Greater conceptual clarity and measurement consistency are essential to advancing research quality and informing targeted clinical assessment and intervention in this population.

As previously highlighted, only a small number of studies addressed body image beyond colorectal cancer, with just two studies focusing on bladder cancer and colorectal cancer together [26, 30], two not report the underlying cancer type [25, 29], and none explicitly examining gynaecological or prostate cancers. This restricted focus constrains the ability to understand how body image experiences may vary according to cancer site, despite clear differences in anatomical location, treatment pathways, and the functional and psychosocial consequences of ostomy formation. The absence of research addressing gynaecological or prostate cancers is particularly striking, given the potential implications for sexuality, gender identity, and embodied self‐perception following pelvic cancer treatment. Together, these findings point to a substantial gap in the evidence base and highlight the need for future research to examine body image in CROs among gynaecological and prostate cancer survivors, to inform more tailored and equitable psychosocial care.

Implications (Clinical and Research)

The findings of this review have important implications for cancer survivorship research and clinical practice. Given the consistent findings that individuals with CROs are vulnerable to negative body image concerns, particularly when compared to non‐stoma survivors with equivalent cancers and where stomas are more permanent, and the consistent negative associations between body image and QoL, mental health, social support, and self‐related variables, addressing body image distress should be considered a core component of care for individuals living with a cancer‐related stoma. Regular screening for body image concerns in clinical settings could facilitate early identification of distress and promote timely psychosocial support. However, the implementation of routine screening is currently constrained by the lack of stoma‐specific, validated body image measures; developing and standardizing such tools should be a priority for future research.

Furthermore, there is a clear need to develop and rigorously evaluate interventions aimed at reducing body image distress in this population. Existing evidence suggests that body image is closely tied to psychosocial adjustment, self‐care, and functional outcomes, indicating that interventions targeting body image may have wide‐reaching benefits. Future research should explore both the efficacy of general psychosocial interventions and the potential for tailored approaches for individuals at higher risk of body image concerns, such as those with permanent stomas, complex functional challenges, or limited social support. Research indicates that interventions such as self‐compassion writing [49, 50] and body image–focused cognitive behavioural therapy [51] may mitigate body image distress. Yet, such programmes remain underdeveloped and under‐evaluated in CRO populations [29]. Evaluating intervention effectiveness across diverse cancer types, including underrepresented populations such as bladder and gynaecological cancers, will be critical to ensure equitable care. By combining routine screening with targeted, evidence‐based interventions, clinicians can more effectively support individuals with cancer‐related stomas to maintain positive body image, enhance QoL, and improve broader psychosocial outcomes.

Limitations and Conclusions

Several methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this review. Many included studies were characterised by small sample sizes (e.g., 29), limiting statistical power and the ability to detect meaningful differences across cancer types and stoma subgroups. Relatedly, few studies sufficiently differentiated between temporary and permanent stomas, and only a small number examined body image trajectories over time. Evidence regarding longitudinal change was inconsistent, with some studies reporting gradual improvement [30], others indicating deterioration [18], and one suggesting that body image remains largely stable unless stoma reversal occurs [33]. Interpretation of longitudinal findings is further complicated by methodological issues within individual studies; for example, one study allowed unrestricted participant dropout, resulting in attrition rates of approximately 14% for some comparisons [33]. Improvements observed in this context may therefore reflect selective dropout rather than true change, underscoring the importance of systematically examining and statistically accounting for attrition in future research. Additionally, potential reporting irregularities were identified in some included papers, such as unusually large effect sizes or implausible confidence intervals. Although these issues were not formally analysed or reported within this review, they warrant caution in interpreting the magnitude of reported effects.

In addition to methodological limitations, the current review highlights gaps in the literature in relation to certain important disease‐related variables that restrict a comprehensive understanding of factors influencing body image outcomes. Specifically, no included studies examined the relationship between disease stage or prognosis and body image, limiting our ability to assess how illness severity or trajectory may shape these experiences, despite their likely interaction with body image and stoma status in particular. In addition, it was not possible to disentangle the relative contributions of stoma status and gastrointestinal symptom burden. Although one study examined the relationship between symptom burden and body image [27], it did not differentiate between the effects of stoma presence and symptoms, nor include a comparison group of non‐stoma survivors. Consequently, the independent and interactive effects of these variables remain unclear, highlighting the need for future research using comparative and methodologically robust designs.

Beyond these quantitative limitations, the exclusion of qualitative and mixed‐methods research in this review represents an important gap. Such approaches could provide critical insights into how cultural context, gender, sexuality, and identity shape body image following CRO surgery. Future research should therefore prioritise definitional and methodological consistency, including the development of validated, stoma‐specific body image measures, while also incorporating qualitative and mixed‐methods designs to capture lived experiences and inform person‐centred care.

Moreover, adequately powered, methodologically rigorous longitudinal studies are needed to track changes in body image over time, across cancer types, stoma status, and treatment pathways, and to account for missing data systematically. Addressing these gaps will be essential for advancing understanding and supporting tailored psychosocial interventions for individuals living with CROs. Investment in tailored psychosocial interventions, enhanced research, and routine integration of body image screening into survivorship services may be essential to improving outcomes for this growing population of cancer survivors with ostomies.
