Impairment of Cardiovascular Functional Capacity in Mild-to-Moderate Kidney Dysfunction
Authors: Kenneth Lim, Matthew Nayor, Eliott Arroyo, Heather N. Burney, Xiaochun Li, Yang Li, Ravi Shah, Joseph Campain, Douglas Wan, Stephen Ting, Thomas F. Hiemstra, Ravi Thadhani, Sharon Moe, Daniel Zehnder, Martin G. Larson, Ramachandran S. Vasan, Gregory D. Lewis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) in patients with CKD is a global epidemic and major public health priority.1 Individuals with only mild decrements in eGFR to <60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 have a two-fold increased risk of cardiovascular mortality, and this risk increases up to 20-fold by ESKD.2 Traditional atherosclerotic risk factors, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes mellitus, only partially explain the increased risk of CVD in patients with CKD, thereby significantly limiting the predictive ability of traditional risk estimate tools.3 The development of left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy and diastolic dysfunction are well-recognized predictors of worse cardiovascular outcomes in patients with CKD.4–7 However, LV geometric changes do not fully account for modulations in cardiovascular risk in patients with impaired kidney function.8,9 In fact, imaging the cardiac structure at rest is insufficiently sensitive to detect subclinical CVD in CKD.8 Despite these limitations, cardiovascular risk stratification in patients with CKD and end points for cardiorenal interventional trials have traditionally relied on resting measures of LV geometry, such as LV mass index.9

As CKD progresses, the cardiovascular system is exposed to complex stressors, such as uremic, metabolic, proinflammatory, hemodynamic, and mineral dysregulation.10 This leads to impairment of multiple organ systems that form the oxygen transport chain, including the heart, lungs, vascular, and peripheral musculoskeletal reserve capacity and widespread ultrastructural and molecular changes.11–13 These alterations collectively contribute to impairment of the cardiovascular system to fulfill its primary function as an effective oxygen transport system, as captured by the Fick principle: oxygen uptake at peak exercise (VO2Peak)=HRmax×SVmax×(arterial−venous) O2 difference.9,14 The assessment of VO2Peak by cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) is therefore a powerful integrative index of these alterations and reflective of overall cardiovascular functional capacity. In addition, CPET allows the ascertainment of submaximal indices of O2 uptake that are independent of volitional effort and a host of other cardiac-specific and ventilatory parameters that enables detailed phenotyping of cardiovascular alterations.

Few, if any, studies have examined abnormalities in cardiovascular functional capacity as reflected by VO2Peak and complementary CPET measures across the spectrum of mild-to-moderate decrements in kidney function. Furthermore, alterations in ventilatory and hemodynamic exercise response patterns in CKD are likely fundamentally different from the general heart failure population, and their precise alterations and the relative contribution of each Fick variable to VO2Peak impairment in CKD remains unknown. The overall aim of this study was to define multidimensional exercise response patterns related to cardiovascular functional capacity in patients with mild-to-moderate decrements in kidney function who had undergone CPET. We examined two unique cohorts: (1) participants from a large community-dwelling cohort in the Framingham Heart Study (FHS) who underwent noninvasive CPET evaluations15,16 and (2) participants who had preserved ejection fraction, the predominant phenotype in CKD-associated cardiomyopathy, from the Massachusetts General Hospital Exercise Study (MGH-ExS).17

Methods

Cohort Description

We performed a cross-sectional study of participants from the FHS and MGH-ExS cohorts.17 FHS participants from the Generation Three, multiethnic Omni Generation Two, and New Offspring cohorts15,16 underwent CPET at a routine study visit (2016–2019) as previously described.17 A total of 3075 participants who underwent CPET as part of their examination three visit were included in this analysis. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed as part of FHS Exam 1 of Generation Three (2002–2005), Omni Two (2003–2005), and New Offspring (2003–2005) cohorts. The Boston University medical center and Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) institutional review boards approved all study protocols, and all participants provided informed written consent.

The MGH-ExS cohort consisted of individuals referred to a single tertiary care center for evaluation of exertional dyspnea.17 For this investigation, a total of 451 participants who had preserved ejection fraction defined as an left ventricular ejection fraction ≥50% and underwent CPET with hemodynamic monitoring at MGH between 2006 and 2017 were included. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed at MGH, and results of a participants' scan closest to their CPET within a 3-year span were analyzed. All participants provided informed consent, and the study was approved by the MGH Institutional Review Board. In both the FHS and MGH-ExS cohorts, patients with a respiratory exchange ratio <1.0 were excluded from the analysis.

CPET

Noninvasive CPET was conducted in the FHS cohort, and invasive CPET was conducted in the MGH-ExS cohort. Invasive CPET enables the assessment of direct Fick cardiac output (CO) in parallel with standard CPET metrics. This was achieved using a 7F balloon-tipped triple-lumen pulmonary artery catheter inserted through the internal jugular vein before CPET as previously described.18 CPET was conducted as previously described using harmonized protocols in both cohorts and identical approaches to gas exchange measurements and analysis (see Supplemental Item 1).17,19 The VO2 at the point of anaerobic threshold (VO2AT) was assessed using the V-slope method as the average of measurements by two experienced readers. Ventilatory efficiency (the ratio of minute ventilation [VE] to carbon dioxide [CO2]: VE/VCO2) was assessed as the lowest VE/VCO2 ratio during exercise using 30 seconds of averaged breaths with a mid-5-of-7 breath averaging algorithm (VE/VCO2 nadir) as previously described.17

eGFR Determination

Serum creatinine was measured using the modified Jaffe method in the FHS cohort. Because these measures can vary widely across different laboratories, we calibrated serum creatinine values for all participants included in this study through the FHS two-step procedure: First, we standardized creatinine to the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey III (NHANES III) creatinine values that were calibrated to the Cleveland Clinic Laboratory, requiring a correction factor of 0.23 mg/dl. Second, mean creatinine values from the FHS were then aligned with the corresponding corrected NHANES III values by age- and sex-specific means.20 We then calculated eGFR using these standardized creatinine values using the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration equation.21 In the MGH-ExS cohort, creatinine levels were assessed within 14 days of invasive CPET as mandated by preprocedural policies.

Statistical Analyses

In this study, an eGFR ≥90 ml/min per 1.73 m2 was defined as normal eGFR and those with an eGFR <90 ml/min per 1.73 m2 were defined as having reduced kidney function. The normal eGFR group was used as our reference group. Participants were stratified according to eGFR (normal eGFR ≥90, eGFR 60–89, and eGFR 30–59 ml/min per 1.73 m2). Baseline characteristics and cardiorespiratory measures were summarized using means with standard deviation or median with 25th and 75th percentiles or frequencies (percentage). For each cohort, ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis tests, and chi-square tests were performed as appropriate for comparisons across the eGFR groups. Our primary outcomes of interest were VO2Peak and submaximal indices, including VO2AT and VE/VCO2 nadir (dependent variables). Correlation matrices and bivariate correlation analysis were used to reflect associations between eGFR, age, height, weight, and the outcomes. Multiple linear regression models were fitted to further evaluate associations between eGFR groups and each outcome variable adjusted for sex, height, and weight (model 1). To assess additional effects of age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and β-blocker use, three additional regression analyses were performed with age (model 2); age and both comorbidities (model 3); and age, both comorbidities, and β-blocker use (model 4) added to model 1. All covariates were selected on the basis of a combination of biological plausibility and known predictors from published studies and those that were significantly different between participants with reduced eGFR compared with those in normal eGFR groups.8 Given the exploratory nature of the analysis, nominal P values are reported without multiple comparison adjustment. Statistical analysis was conducted using SAS (version 9.4).

Results

Characteristics of the Study Samples

FHS Cohort

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Of the 3075 FHS participants included, 1712 had an eGFR ≥90 ml/min per 1.73 m2, 1271 had an eGFR of 60–89 ml/min per 1.73 m2, and 92 had an eGFR of 30–59 ml/min per 1.73 m2. Participants with reduced eGFR were older (P < 0.001) and had higher systolic BP (P < 0.001). There were no significant differences in diastolic BP or sex between the groups. The eGFR 30–59 group had a greater frequency of hypertension (P < 0.001), diabetes mellitus (P < 0.001), and ischemic heart disease (P = 0.002) and had a higher body mass index (P = 0.02). Among participants with eGFR ≥90 ml/min per 1.73 m2, 8% were smokers compared with 4% in those with eGFR 60–89 ml/min per 1.73 m2, and zero reported smokers in those with eGFR 30–59 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (P < 0.001). The median (Q1–Q3) eGFR in the eGFR 30–59 group was 55.7 (49.7–58.1) ml/min per 1.73 m2.

MGH-ExS Cohort

Of the 451 MGH-ExS participants included, 247 had an eGFR ≥90 ml/min per 1.73 m2, 154 had an eGFR of 60–89 ml/min per 1.73 m2, and 50 had an eGFR of 30–59 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (Table 1). Participants with reduced eGFR were older (P < 0.001) and had higher systolic BP (P = 0.02). The eGFR 30–59 group had lower diastolic BP (P = 0.007) and lower hemoglobin (P = 0.001) compared with the other groups. The two lower eGFR groups had a greater prevalence of hypertension and dyslipidemia (P < 0.001). There were no significant differences in body mass index, sex, smoking status, or prevalence of diabetes mellitus or heart failure (P values > 0.05) between the groups. Those in the MGH-ExS had lower median (Q1–Q3) eGFR in the eGFR 30–59 group (49.3 [40.3–53.3] ml/min per 1.73 m2) compared with the FHS cohort.

CPET and Resting Echocardiographic Measures

FHS Cohort

Functional and structural cardiovascular measures are presented in Figure 1 and Supplemental Table 1. The eGFR ≥90 group had a VO2Peak of 22.2 (17.9–27.8) ml·min−1·kg−1 compared with 21.6 (17.4–26.4) ml·min−1·kg−1 in the eGFR 60–89 group, with a much lower level of 16.5 (13.3–20.4) ml·min−1·kg−1 (P < 0.001) in the eGFR 30–59 group in unadjusted analysis. We found a similar pattern of changes with VO2AT (P < 0.001). However, these differences in VO2Peak and VO2AT were lost after adjustment for covariates (Table 2). The eGFR 30–59 group had a higher percentage of predicted VO2Peak at which VO2AT occurs compared with the other groups in both unadjusted (P < 0.001) and adjusted (P = 0.04) models, suggesting relatively greater impairment in post-AT exercise capacity. When comparing the eGFR 60–89 versus eGFR 30–59 groups, we found in unadjusted comparisons that the VO2AT differs by approximately 1.4 ml·min−1·kg−1; however, there was a greater difference of 5.1 ml·min−1·kg−1 in VO2Peak. The eGFR 30–59 group had higher VE/VCO2 nadir compared with the other groups (P = 0.001) on adjusted analysis.

MGH-ExS Cohort

Participants in the MGH-ExS cohort had generally greater impairment in VO2Peak across eGFR groups compared with those in the FHS, with a similar pattern of incrementally lower VO2Peak with worsening kidney function (Figure 2). Participants in the eGFR ≥90 group had a VO2Peak of 17.7 (14.7–21.7) ml·min−1·kg−1 compared with 14.4 (12.3–18.3) ml·min−1·kg−1 in the eGFR 60–89 group, with a further decline to 12.8 (10.9–14.8) ml·min−1·kg−1 (P < 0.001) in the eGFR 30–59 group on unadjusted analysis. We found a similar pattern in VO2AT (P < 0.001). These differences in VO2Peak and VO2AT remained significant even after adjusting for covariates. The two lower eGFR groups also showed a lower CO (eGFR 30–59: 11.4±0.4 L/min; eGFR 60–89: 11.9±0.2 L/min versus eGFR ≥90: 12.6±0.2 L/min; P = 0.002) even after adjusting for covariates (Figure 3 and Table 2). These participants had higher VE/VCO2 nadir on unadjusted analysis (Supplemental Table 1), but this was lost after adjustment (P = 0.77).

Discussion

This is the first epidemiologic study to comprehensively describe alterations in cardiovascular functional capacity in participants with mild-to-moderate CKD using noninvasive and invasive CPET and echocardiography. We describe ventilatory exercise response patterns in two complementary cohorts, a large cohort of community-dwelling adults (FHS) and a dyspneic referral cohort (MGH-ExS) with preserved ejection fraction (the phenotype characteristic of CKD-associated cardiomyopathy). In the FHS cohort, a small decrement in VO2Peak was detectable in participants with even mildly reduced eGFR of 60–89 ml/min per 1.73 m2 compared with participants with an eGFR ≥90 ml/min per 1.73 m2, whereas a much greater reduction in VO2Peak was observed in the eGFR 30–59 group. However, these differences were lost in adjusted modeling, and this is postulated to be due to age-associated declines in kidney function because participants in these groups were significantly older. In addition, there were substantially fewer participants in the FHS eGFR 30–59 group compared with the other groups. However, in the MGH-ExS cohort, an already impaired VO2Peak in participants with an eGFR ≥90 ml/min per 1.73 m2 was observed, and both VO2Peak and CO were incrementally lower in the lower eGFR groups in adjusted models. Participants in the MGH-ExS cohort differed from those in the FHS, having been selected from a referral cohort with known preserved ejection fraction and, importantly, those in the eGFR 30–59 group had worse kidney function (median 6.4 ml/min per 1.73 m2 lower eGFR) compared with the eGFR 30–59 group in the FHS cohort.

In the Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing in Renal Failure and After Kidney Transplantation study8—a prospective, three-arm, controlled cohort study that recruited 81 patients with stage 5 CKD who underwent kidney transplantation, 85 nontransplanted patients with stage 5 CKD, and 87 hypertensive controls—we showed that VO2Peak significantly declined in the nontransplanted group, whereas the transplanted group exhibited improved VO2Peak at 1-year follow-up. These changes contrasted with the absence of detectable alterations in LV geometry by echocardiography. The present study builds upon this narrative and suggests that VO2Peak may provide superior assessment of cardiovascular alterations at least in symptomatic patients with mild-to-moderate CKD in the MGH-ExS.

In addition, we found that submaximal exercise-derived indices of cardiovascular functional capacity, including VO2AT, also were lower in parallel with VO2Peak beginning with participants with an eGFR <90 ml/min per 1.73 m2. Reduced kidney function was associated with greater impairment in the anaerobic phase of exercise compared with the aerobic phase. These results highlight that exercise testing can detect cardiovascular impairment that would not otherwise be evident at rest. Impairment of cardiovascular functional capacity was associated with a blunted heart rate at peak exercise, suggesting that chronotropic incompetence occurs early in CKD and contributes to impairment in exercise capacity. We also found incremental reductions in maximal CO with reduced kidney function in the MGH-ExS cohort. These findings suggest that lower VO2Peak observed in mild-to-moderate CKD is in part driven by lower CO.

Our study has several strengths. It is the largest study to date investigating the role of CPET in patients with reduced kidney function. In addition, our study includes a well-validated community-based cohort (FHS) and referral cohort (MGH-ExS) that were not selected for CKD, thereby reducing the risk of selection bias for CKD. Our findings should be interpreted against the limitations of the study. First, the study population is represented by a predominantly White population and the prevalence of reduced kidney function in this study may underestimate the true prevalence in Black individuals or Native American individuals who are at increased risk of kidney disease. Second, substantially fewer participants had an eGFR between 30 and 59 ml/min per 1.73 m2 in both cohorts. Because only 92 participants in the FHS cohort had an eGFR between 30 and 59 ml/min per 1.73 m2 compared with 1271 participants in the eGFR 60–89 group, it was likely underpowered to detect differences in VO2Peak. Finally, a limitation of this study is the gap in timing between when echocardiography and CPET was performed in both the FHS and MGH-ExS cohorts. Therefore, alterations in echocardiography measures compared with CPET measures by level of eGFR should be cautiously interpreted.

In summary, this study is the first large epidemiologic study to examine cardiovascular functional capacity using CPET. Incremental declines in ventilatory exercise responses in symptomatic referral patients from the MGH-ExS cohort occurred in the absence of alterations in LV geometric indices, providing evidence that assessment of VO2Peak and submaximal indices may better detect cardiovascular changes during early stages of kidney function decline at least in patients with exercise intolerance. Further appropriately powered studies are needed to examine comprehensive CPET-derived global indices of cardiovascular health in asymptomatic patients with mild-to-moderate CKD. The emergence of CPET technology in the field of cardionephrology offers the potential of reliably detecting subclinical CVD in CKD.
