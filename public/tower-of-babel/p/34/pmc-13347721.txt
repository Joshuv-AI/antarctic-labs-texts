Minimal Invasive Approach in Severe Tracheobronchomalacia: Türkiye's First Case of Vats Tracheobronchoplasty
Authors: Melike Ulker, Aysegul Ciftci, Ali Cevat Kutluk, Volkan Erdogu, Muzaffer Metin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tracheobronchomalacia is an expiratory collapse of the posterior wall of the trachea and at least one of the main bronchi. It may be idiopathic or may develop due to secondary causes. It results in a slowing of dynamic airflow and clinically presents with dyspnea, cough, and pneumonia. Although tracheal stents provide temporary relief in treatment, considering the problems encountered during the chronic phase, endobronchial therapy does not offer a long-term solution. Tracheobronchoplasty is a surgical treatment option. Examination of the few cases reported in the literature shows that, in centers with adequate video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) experience, this procedure, which is frequently performed via right thoracotomy, can be effectively performed using minimally invasive techniques. A 66-year-old male patient presented with dyspnea. Severe tracheomalacia was detected, and tracheoplasty was performed using VATS. The present study aims to describe the first VATS tracheobronchoplasty procedure in Türkiye.

Tracheomalacia is the expiratory collapse of the posterior membrane, with a reduction of at least 50% in the anteroposterior caliber of the airway diameter.[1] If collapse of one or both main bronchi is added to tracheal collapse, it is called tracheobronchomalacia.[2] It may be idiopathic or may develop due to secondary causes such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and regular use of inhaled corticosteroids. Attenuation of dynamic airflow is a consequence of airway collapse, which manifests as dyspnea, cough, and pneumonia.[3] The most objective tests used in diagnosis are dynamic computed tomography and dynamic bronchoscopy.[4] Tracheal stents are used to stabilize the airway during therapy. Nevertheless, although stents offer temporary relief, they are not a long-term solution because of recurrent problems such as blockage and infection. Another treatment method is tracheobronchoplasty (TBP), a surgical technique.[1-4]

TBP aims to restore stability to the posterior membrane through the use of polypropylene mesh. This procedure is often performed via right posterolateral thoracotomy.[5,6] In this case report, we aimed to present a case of TBP performed with triportal VATS. Informed consent was obtained from the patient.

Case Report

A 66-year-old male patient presented to the hospital with dyspnea and wheezing. Over the past three years, he had reported persistent shortness of breath during physical activity. The patient was diagnosed with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and Buerger’s disease. He had a smoking history of 50 pack-years and had quit smoking five years earlier. Computed tomography (CT) of the thorax showed a diffuse mosaic perfusion pattern in both lungs. Bronchoscopy performed for diagnostic purposes revealed diffuse purulent secretions and pronounced malacia of the trachea and the membranous wall of the right main bronchus. Dynamic findings with >90% expiratory airway collapse indicated severe tracheobronchomalacia (Fig. 1).

After one year of stability, the patient experienced recurrent exacerbations with episodes of desaturation (<88% SpO2 during exertion) and was subsequently readmitted to the hospital with complaints of diffuse secretions and dyspnea. Thoracic CT revealed diffuse pneumonic infiltrates in the right lung. After the pneumonic infiltration regressed with medical treatment, bronchoscopy was performed again, and malacia persisted. After discussion by the multidisciplinary council, the decision was made to perform VATS tracheobronchoplasty. Informed consent was obtained from the patient.

Surgical Technique

Following single-lumen intubation, the patient was positioned in the lateral decubitus position. Partial lung collapse was achieved through CO2 insufflation. Single-lumen intubation was preferred to enable continuous fiberoptic bronchoscopy. The thorax was entered through a 2-cm utility incision in the anterior axillary line at the fourth intercostal space. The thorax was explored with a 30° video-thoracoscopic camera. A camera port was inserted through the seventh intercostal space in the posterior axillary line. The azygos vein was sacrificed with an endo-stapler to visualize the tracheal area more easily. The posterior end of the azygos vein and the vagus nerve were fixed posteriorly to the chest wall for proper exposure. The trachea was released by blunt and sharp dissection. After the trachea, carina, and right and left main bronchi were exposed, an impression was taken with a sterile plate for the polypropylene mesh planned for suturing to the membranous part of the trachea. Under the guidance of this plate, the mesh was prepared according to the dimensions of the membranous part of the trachea. Another port was opened in the axillary line posterior to the fifth intercostal space. The camera was moved to this port, and the inferior port was used to exclude the lung tissue. The polypropylene mesh was sutured to the membranous region in both transverse (3 rows) and longitudinal planes (6 rows), starting from the junction of the membranous part and the proximal cartilage ring of the trachea. During suturing, the intubation tube was pulled proximally, and fiberoptic bronchoscopy (FOB)-guided suturing was performed without touching the tube. After the mesh was fixed from the distal part of the trachea along the right main bronchus, a new 2×1 cm mesh was prepared for suturing to the left main bronchus and was sutured to the left main bronchus using the same technique. Post-procedure leakage control showed no leakage in the tracheal suture lines. Stability was increased by applying tissue adhesive over the entire polypropylene mesh area. After stabilization of the membranous structure along the trachea, carina, and main bronchi was observed by FOB, the procedure was terminated by placing a single thoracic drain. The operative time was 210 minutes, and perioperative bleeding was 200 mL.

The patient was followed up in the intensive care unit on the first postoperative day, and intermittent non-invasive mechanical ventilation was applied. On the second postoperative day, the patient was followed up in the ward, and the drain was removed. Bronchoscopy performed on the fourth day showed that posterior membrane stabilization had been achieved. The patient was discharged on the seventh postoperative day and stated that dyspnea on exertion had significantly decreased. Postoperative first-day and seventh-day chest radiographs are shown in Figure 6. The patient received inhaled corticosteroids, long-acting bronchodilators, and antibiotics during exacerbations and was followed monthly in the outpatient clinic. Postoperative spirometry at 3 months showed that FEV1 improved from 1.12 L to 1.36 L (21% increase). The patient reported reduced inhaler use and improved exertional tolerance. No additional emergency room admissions occurred during that year, and no complaints were noted at the postoperative follow-up visit.

Discussion

Tracheobronchomalacia (TBM) is a challenging clinical entity characterized by expiratory airway collapse, often resulting in significant respiratory symptoms and recurrent infections. Since Nissen’s first description of surgical management in 1954,[7] the primary surgical approach has traditionally involved right posterolateral thoracotomy.[5,6] Over time, the development of minimally invasive thoracic surgery has expanded treatment options, with video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and robotic surgery increasingly being used in selected patients.[2,8,9]

In a 2011 study by Gangadharan et al.,[6] a review of 63 patients showed significant improvements in performance scale, quality of life, and 6-minute walk test measurements, whereas no improvement or significant deterioration was observed in postoperative FEV1 values. The mean operative time was 373 minutes. The median length of hospitalization was 8 days, while the median length of intensive care unit stay was 3 days. Mortality due to pneumonia and massive pulmonary embolism was observed in two cases in the series. In our case, there were no comorbidities, and no complications or mortality were observed. The operative time was 210 minutes, despite this being the first VATS case. The shorter operative time was attributed to the triportal VATS approach and the expertise of our VATS team in extended resections.

Minimally invasive approaches have also been explored in the literature. Tse et al.[8] reported two cases in which tracheobronchoplasty was successfully performed using a triportal VATS technique, with no postoperative complications or mortality. Our surgical method bears similarities, particularly in the use of triportal access and intraoperative fiberoptic bronchoscopy to guide mesh placement. Unlike Tse et al.’s[8] report, airway stenting was not necessary in our case, suggesting that sufficient airway stabilization was achieved intraoperatively.

Robot-assisted techniques have also shown promise. Lazar et al.[9] published the first robot-assisted TBP case in 2015, and the first robotic series of 42 cases was subsequently published by Lazzaro et al.[2] in 2019. Forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), and peak expiratory flow rate all improved when preoperative and postoperative pulmonary function tests were compared. Although robotic surgery offers advantages such as enhanced dexterity and better visualization, VATS remains a viable alternative, particularly in centers with extensive experience in thoracoscopic procedures.

Correct patient selection and preoperative assessment are crucial for surgical success. Dynamic computed tomography and flexible bronchoscopy remain the gold standards for diagnosing TBM and assessing its severity.[4] In our case, persistent symptoms despite optimal medical management and dynamic bronchoscopic findings consistent with severe malacia supported the decision for surgical intervention, aligning with current recommendations.[1,3]

Intraoperative guidance with fiberoptic bronchoscopy allowed accurate mesh placement along the posterior membranous wall, which likely contributed to the satisfactory stabilization achieved. Early postoperative management strategies, including non-invasive ventilation and prompt mobilization, may have further facilitated the patient’s smooth recovery. Close follow-up and further studies with larger patient populations are needed to better define long-term outcomes and ideal surgical strategies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this report represents Türkiye’s first case of VATS tracheobronchoplasty for severe TBM. The favorable perioperative results suggest that VATS tracheobronchoplasty can be a safe and effective alternative to open surgery in centers with appropriate expertise. However, continued experience and further clinical studies are essential to confirm these promising early results.
