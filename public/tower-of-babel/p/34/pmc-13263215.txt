Guillain–Barré Syndrome Following Recombinant Zoster Vaccine in US Medicare Adults Age 65 Years or Older
Authors: Susan dos Reis, Yueh-Yi Chiang, Alejandro Amill-Rosario, Haeyoung Lee, Abree Johnson, Kathleen Ryan, Driss Oraichi, Harry Seifert, Huifeng Yun, Susan Gamble, O’Mareen Spence
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivePre- and post-marketing studies detected Guillain–Barré syndrome following recombinant zoster vaccine. This study evaluated new-onset Guillain–Barré syndrome following recombinant zoster vaccine in older US adults.MethodsUsing the Medicare Chronic Condition Warehouse administrative data, we implemented a self-controlled risk interval case-only design with a 1- to 42-day risk window and a 43- to 84-day control window following recombinant zoster vaccine. Participants were Medicare beneficiaries aged ≥65 years who received at least one dose of the recombinant zoster vaccine two-dose regimen from 2018 to 2020. New-onset Guillain–Barré syndrome was identified using an algorithm requiring an International Classification of Disease, Tenth Revision diagnosis code of G61.0 in the primary position in an inpatient setting or in any other setting followed by an inpatient encounter within 7 days, with no Guillain–Barré syndrome in the preceding 365 days. Conditional Poisson regression models estimated the relative risk and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We estimated the attributable risk and 95% CI. Sensitivity analyses examined dose-specific effects, 60- to 183-day dose spacing, and coronavirus disease 2019 and seasonality.ResultsOf 3,383,799 eligible participants, 75 experienced new-onset Guillain–Barré syndrome. The primary analysis detected a relative risk of 3.15 (95% CI 1.82, 5.68) and an attributable risk of 6.59 per 1,000,000 doses (95% CI 4.35, 7.96). The dose-specific analysis detected a relative risk of 5.33 (95% CI 2.59, 12.36) for dose 1. The coronavirus disease 2019 and seasonality analyses revealed an increased risk. Dose 2 and the 60- to 183-day dose spacing were not significant.ConclusionsThe elevated risk of new-onset Guillain–Barré syndrome among US Medicare beneficiaries aged ≥65 years was detected after recombinant zoster vaccine dose 1 but not dose 2.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40264-026-01658-6.

Key Points

In this study of adults aged ≥65 years and enrolled in Medicare fee-for-service, there were 75 new-onset Guillain–Barré syndrome cases out of 3,383,799 who received recombinant zoster vaccine in the 2018–2020 study period.A statistically significant elevated risk of new-onset Guillain–Barré syndrome following recombinant zoster vaccine was detected after recombinant zoster vaccine dose 1 but not dose 2.There is an increased risk of new-onset Guillain–Barré syndrome following recombinant zoster vaccine among adults aged ≥65 years.

Introduction

An estimated one million cases of herpes zoster (HZ), caused by reactivation of the latent varicella zoster virus, occur in the USA annually [1]. Herpes zoster contributes to US$5 billion in annual healthcare expenditures among adults aged ≥50 years [2]. Risk factors for HZ include older age and immunocompromising conditions [3–5]. Postherpetic neuralgia is a complication of HZ, the risk of which increases with age and is difficult to treat [4]; thus, HZ prevention is critical.

Preventive vaccination reduces the risk of HZ and associated complications. The recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV; Shingrix, GSK) is a subunit adjuvanted two-dose series vaccine first approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for HZ prevention in adults aged ≥50 years, and later approved use for those aged ≥18 years who are or will be at increased risk of HZ because of immunodeficiency or immunosuppression caused by a known disease or therapy. In pivotal trials, its efficacy was 97.2% in adults aged ≥50 years [6] and 89.8% in adults aged ≥70 years [7]. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices recommends RZV in adults aged ≥50 years [8] and adults aged ≥19 years who are immunodeficient [9].

Pre- and post-licensure safety monitoring of RZV detected Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) [10]. Guillain–Barré syndrome is an immune-modulated condition that affects the peripheral nervous system [11], and can also be triggered by the HZ infection [12, 13]. Case reports revealed two GBS cases within 10 days after the first RZV dose [14, 15] and another 2 weeks after the second RZV dose [16]. A population-based study using claims data for 849,397 Medicare beneficiaries aged ≥65 years detected a relative risk (RR) of 2.84 (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.53, 5.27) and an attributable risk (AR) per million RZV doses of 3.13 (95% CI 0.62, 5.64) for GBS [17]. Two studies conducted in adults aged ≥50 years, one using electronic health records from an integrated healthcare system for 647,307 adults [18] and the other using signal detection methods in a commercial claims database of over one million vaccinations [19], did not find an increased risk of GBS following RZV. Post-marketing safety surveillance of RZV based on the Vaccine Adverse Events Reporting System from October 2017 through April 2024 found GBS among the most reported serious adverse events; however, the disproportionality analysis did not detect a significant safety signal [20].

Robust data on the risk of GBS following RZV among older US Medicare beneficiaries are limited, as most evidence is from US adults with commercial insurance [18, 19]. This study investigated the risk of new-onset GBS following RZV using a comprehensive database that represents US Medicare beneficiaries aged ≥65 years.

Methods

Study Design

This observational study using secondary data employed a self-controlled risk interval (SCRI) design to assess the risk of new-onset GBS. An SCRI design is particularly well suited for safety monitoring of acute outcomes [21]. Following the Brighton Collaboration recommendation for a 6-week risk window [22], this case-only design included only those RZV vaccinees who had evidence of GBS in the pre-defined risk window (RW), days 1–42, or the control window (CW), days 43–84, following each respective RZV dose.

Recombinant zoster vaccine doses should be administered 2–6 months apart, but dose 2 may be administered 1–2 months after dose 1 for those who are or will be immunocompromised [23]. As a result, we considered scenarios where the dose 1 CW did not exist (i.e., dose 2 received 1–42 days after dose 1) or was shortened (i.e., dose 2 received 43–84 days after dose 1) [see Fig. S1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM]). We excluded post-dose 1 GBS cases if dose 2 was received in the 1- to 42-day RW as no dose 1 CW would exist. The index date of the RZV vaccination was not included in the RW.

Data Source

This study used the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Chronic Condition Warehouse databases, which represent US adults enrolled in the Medicare program. The data include all claims paid for the provision of healthcare services in inpatient, institutional outpatient, and provider settings, skilled nursing facilities, hospice, and home health services, and for the provision of durable medical equipment and prescription drugs.

Study Cohort

The GBS analytic cohort comprised US Medicare beneficiaries aged ≥65 years on receipt of at least one RZV dose in calendar years 2018 through 2020, were enrolled in the Medicare fee-for-service (FFS) program because of age or end-stage renal disease, and were continuously enrolled in Medicare FFS Parts A, B, and D in the month of RZV vaccination and in 11 of the 12 months preceding through day 84 following RZV. As Medicare Part C (i.e., Advantage plans) is not included in the Chronic Condition Warehouse data, we excluded those enrolled in Medicare Part C at any time in 11 of the 12 months preceding and including the month of RZV receipt. To avoid confounding by a disability, we excluded those with this qualifying reason for Medicare enrollment. Of beneficiaries who met all eligibility criteria, the final GBS analytic cohort included those who had GBS in the RW or CW after either RZV dose 1 or dose 2 and who did not have GBS in the 365 days prior to the event.

Exposure

The exposure definition was at least one RZV dose any time during calendar years 2018 through 2020. The national drug code on a prescription claim or a Current Procedural Terminology code of 90750 for RZV administration on a physician claim identified RZV exposure.

Outcome Ascertainment

Guillain–Barré syndrome was identified using a validated claims-based algorithm with a positive predictive value (PPV) of 56% of detecting GBS in claims data [24]. We ascertained a GBS case as a G61.0 International Classification of Disease, Tenth Revision diagnosis code in an inpatient setting in the primary diagnosis position or in any setting in any position followed by an inpatient claim within 7 days. The GBS case date was the date of the first occurrence that met ascertainment criteria in either the day 1–42 RW or the day 43–84 CW following RZV dose 1 or dose 2. We determined new-onset GBS if there was no GBS in an inpatient setting, primary position in the 365 days preceding but not including the GBS case date. Those with GBS in this 365-day period, i.e., prevalent cases, were excluded.

Covariates

Demographic, chronic conditions, and health service utilization covariates were measured in the 365 days preceding RZV dose 1 to characterize the study cohort. Age, sex, and race were measured on the date of RZV dose 1. Evidence of Medicare FFS Parts A, B, and D coverage in the month of and for a minimum of 11 of 12 months preceding RZV dose 1 defined continuous enrollment. Chronic medical conditions identified in the 365 days preceding receipt of RZV dose 1 included diabetes mellitus, liver, kidney, and lung disease, cardiovascular conditions (e.g., congestive heart failure, ischemic heart disease, and stroke), and Alzheimer’s disease and related dementia. Solid organ and stem cell transplant and cancer with chemotherapy captured treatments that induce immunodeficiency. Immunocompromising conditions measured prior to receipt of RZV dose 1 included human immunodeficiency virus, rheumatoid and psoriatic arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease (i.e., Crohn’s disease, colitis), lupus, and multiple sclerosis. Hospitalizations, emergency department visits, dermatology and ophthalmology visits, and use of durable medical equipment, skilled nursing facilities, hospice, and home healthcare captured prior health service utilization. Administration of influenza, pneumococcal, and tetanus-diphtheria and pertussis preventive vaccines were captured on the date of RZV.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analyses used means, standard deviations, frequencies, and proportions. We identified the number of GBS cases over the follow-up. Since prior infection may increase the risk of GBS [11], we assessed the number of GBS cases without a respiratory (including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2) or gastrointestinal infection in an inpatient or outpatient setting 60 days prior to the GBS diagnosis. A priori sample size estimation for an SCRI design [25] to detect a RR of 3.0 with 80% power and a two-sided type 1 error of 0.05 was 30 GBS events and 2.05 million vaccinated individuals.

We used a conditional Poisson regression model that conditions on the events in the RW or CW, as the dependent variable [26]. The model accounts for within-participant dependence using generalized estimating equations for robust variance estimation. The log of the time spent in the interval is the offset term. The model generates the RR estimate with a 95% CI. We used SAS® PROC GENMOD with the Poisson distribution and a log link function.

Attributable risk determined how many new-onset GBS cases would be due to RZV, quantified per 1,000,000 doses administered. We applied the formula [27]:

To compute a 95% CI of the AR, we used the formula 1,000,000 × number of cases in the RW × [1 − (1 ÷ exp(RR upper or lower CI))] ÷ [number of vaccine doses].

A temporal scan statistic was obtained testing multiple time intervals to ascertain whether there was significant clustering of cases in the RW or CW.

The primary analysis estimated the risk of GBS without distinguishing between doses. Two secondary analyses evaluated the potential impact of informative censoring, due to a shortened dose 1 CW. We conducted a fixed 3-week (i.e., 21 days) and a fixed 6-week (i.e., 42 days) CW length, without distinguishing between doses. If a GBS case occurred in a period of overlap between the dose 1 CW and the dose 2 RW, we attributed this to the dose 2 RW.

Sensitivity analyses tested the dose-specific effects, dose spacing, the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, and the seasonality of GBS. The dose spacing sensitivity analysis restricted the analytic cohort to the subset who received the two-dose regimen 60–183 days apart, per the US label, to test if dose timing affects GBS risk. The COVID-19 pandemic could have reduced the ability to identify new-onset GBS. We excluded any RZV exposures on or after 8 November, 2019 to allow a follow-up before the 1 February, 2020 pandemic onset. To account for informative censoring, two sensitivity analyses assessed the dose-specific risk with 6-week fixed CW lengths. To assess the impact of GBS seasonality, we adapted methods used in other SCRI studies of vaccine safety [27–29] (see Table S1 of the ESM). Finally, we evaluated GBS risk after excluding cases with a respiratory or gastrointestinal infection 60 days prior to the GBS diagnosis. We conducted two post-hoc analyses; one corrected for 50% PPV of the claims-based algorithm and the second estimated the risk of GBS in those aged 65–74 years separately from those aged 75 years and older.

To comply with the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services data use agreement, cell sizes <11 were suppressed. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Studio® 9.4, and statistical significance was determined using a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Maryland, Baltimore (HP-00083509). The study is listed on the European Medicines Agency Real World Data Catalogue (EUPAS37133) and the GSK registry (209696).

The RR for the sensitivity analyses for the primary and secondary analyses, respectively, are in Fig. 2 and Fig. S2 of the ESM. The seasonality-adjusted sensitivity analysis was consistent with the primary analysis (RR = 3.17; 95% CI 1.84, 5.72; AR = 6.61; 95% CI 4.41, 7.97). Sensitivity analyses for COVID-19 and the exclusion of those with a prior respiratory or gastrointestinal infection showed a statistically significant increased risk of GBS (Fig. 2). The dose 1 fixed 6-week CW sensitivity analysis was statistically significant (RR = 5.33; 95% CI 2.59, 12.36; and AR = 11.83; 95% CI 8.94, 13.38). The dose 2 fixed 6-week CW analyses were not statistically significant (see Fig. S2 of the ESM).

The PPV-adjusted post-hoc analysis supported a statistically significant increased risk. The age-stratified analysis revealed the risk of GBS was significantly elevated for adults aged 65–74 years and adults aged 75 years and older, but the risk estimate was higher in the oldest age group (see Table S3 of the ESM). Of the 45 scan windows tested in the temporal scan analysis, the observed-to-expected cases revealed statistically significant clustering of GBS cases in the 42-day RW (see Table S4 of the ESM).

Discussion

This study found an elevated risk of new-onset GBS within 42 days following RZV, relative to days 43–84, among US Medicare beneficiaries aged ≥65 years. The elevated risk was primarily after RZV dose 1. Guillain–Barré syndrome is rare, with an incidence of 1.85–2.66 per 100,000 person-years among adults aged ≥60 years [30]. This study detected 75 new-onset GBS cases among the 3,383,799 US Medicare beneficiaries aged ≥65 years who met the study inclusion criteria and received at least one RZV dose in calendar years 2018–2020. Most cases (n = 57; 76%) occurred in the 42-day RW. The fixed 3- and 6-week CW secondary analyses also generated elevated risk estimates that corresponded with the primary analysis. The primary analysis was robust to the COVID-19- and the seasonality-adjusted sensitivity analyses. There was no statistically significant increase in the risk of GBS after dose 2 or within the dose compliant subgroup.

Our findings offer new insights on the risk of GBS following RZV vaccination. We used population-level secondary data to enrich the understanding of RZV safety in older adults. Goud et al. [17] used Medicare claims data from 1 October, 2017 through 29 February, 2020 and identified 44 GBS cases and a statistically significant increased risk of GBS (RR = 2.84; 95% CI 1.53, 5.27) following RZV. Similar to our findings, they also reported a higher risk of GBS after RZV dose 1 and a non-significant risk after RZV dose 2 [17]. It is difficult to speculate the reasons for a statistically significant association with dose 1 but not dose 2, as there are few published papers distinguishing between doses. The underlying hypothesis for the onset of GBS is that vaccines may trigger an immune-modulated reaction, but the specific biological mechanism through which RZV may trigger GBS is unclear.

Despite using the same Medicare data source as the study by Goud et al. [17], our study offers important differences that contribute new information on the association between RZV and new-onset GBS. For one, the study design was distinctly different than Goud et al. [17]. Our design had a 214-day longer assessment period (i.e., 1 January, 2018 through 31 December, 2020) and identified 31 more GBS cases. Second, Goud et al. [17] required participants to be aged 65 years or older and have continuous Medicare enrollment from the date of RZV approval in the USA in October 2017 through the vaccination date. This excluded individuals who turned 65 years of age after RZV approval, where our cohort included these individuals. The different design factors contributed to a sample size in our study that was approximately three times larger than in the Goud et al. study [17]. The larger sample size is preferable for detecting rare outcomes, such as GBS.

Studies have reported fewer cases of GBS following RZV than reported here or by Goud et al. [17]. Yih et al. [19] investigated GBS cases following RZV using a commercial insurance claims database of over one million vaccinations in US individuals aged ≥50 years and found nine cases of GBS, of which only five were validated by a clinician review [19]. Another study in adults aged ≥50 years detected six GBS cases using the Vaccine Safety Datalink, of which three were confirmed through a medical chart review [18]. Most individuals in these studies were aged <65 years, unlike this study where all were aged ≥65 years. The risk of GBS increases with age [11], and may explain the different findings. Our post-hoc analysis revealed that the risk of GBS was highest in adults aged 75 years and older, which offers new insight on the potential age-related differences in risk. An ongoing study in adults aged 50 years and older may offer new insights on an age-related risk of GBS following RZV. Finally, the populations in the prior studies may differ from the present study in susceptibility to GBS.

Guillain–Barré syndrome is thought to be an immune-modulated condition [31]. Respiratory or gastrointestinal infections are strong risk factors for GBS [32], but we found that most of the GBS cases included in our analyses did not have an infection in the 60 days preceding the GBS diagnosis. The sensitivity analysis in the subgroup without prior respiratory or gastrointestinal infections generated risk estimates similar to the primary analysis, suggesting that confounding by prior infection was unlikely to explain away the results [32].

The study has several notable strengths. First, the SCRI design is a rigorous study design commonly used in vaccine safety studies [27–29]. Second, the Chronic Condition Warehouse Medicare data do not rely on participant recall, mitigating reporting bias. Medicare requires several demographic measures at enrollment, so missing data on age, sex, and race is minimal (i.e., <5%). Third, we employed a validated claims-based algorithm. While the algorithm was developed in a different database, it shares common variables with the Medicare data, so we do not expect appreciable differences in its performance in this study. To strengthen the case identification, we sought expert clinical consultation. Fourth, several planned sensitivity analyses addressed the potential for bias due to external factors and informative censoring. Finally, Medicare is the most comprehensive data on older US adults, so the findings will generalize to US adults aged ≥65 years. Appreciable differences between those in the study and those aged ≥65 years who are not enrolled in Medicare FFS Parts A, B, and D that would affect the GBS risk following RZV are not expected.

This study has limitations. Exposure misclassification is possible, as we were unable to identify RZV administered in residential facilities. A major limitation is the inability to validate the GBS diagnoses by chart review because the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services does not share the beneficiaries’ identities. While case-finding algorithms using administrative data are rarely sensitive and specific, we used claims-based algorithms with established PPV [24]. Further, a post-hoc analysis adjusting for the 50% PPV supported the finding of an increased risk of GBS. The COVID-19 pandemic may have led to detection bias, which we attempted to mitigate with our sensitivity analysis. Continuous enrollment requirements may exclude those who die or lose eligibility before the end of the follow-up. However, the relatively short follow-up (i.e., 84 days) should not produce any appreciable bias. An SCRI design does not control for time-varying confounders, but minimal time-varying confounding is expected in the 84-day follow-up. Individuals who receive two doses may introduce selection bias if they differ on measured and unmeasured factors. Finally, despite efforts to minimize random error, it is possible that this may still occur in this observational study using secondary data.

Conclusions

This study revealed a statistically significant higher risk of GBS within the 42 days following RZV among US Medicare beneficiaries aged ≥65 years. This elevated risk was observed following dose 1 but not dose 2.
