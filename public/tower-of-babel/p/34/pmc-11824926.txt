Translation and Validation of the Tamil Version of the Menopause Rating Scale
Authors: Chanil Ekanayake, Sakshara Ranasinghe, Arunasalam Pathmeswaran, Kopalasuntharam Muhunthan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:The Menopause Rating Scale (MRS) is a widely used tool to assess the health-related quality of life (HRQOL) and severity of menopausal symptoms in postmenopausal women. The objective of the present study was to validate a Tamil translation of the MRS, as a Tamil translation is not available.Methods:The Tamil translation of MRS was developed following standard methodology, and a validation study to assess psychometric properties was done at the community level in Mannar, Sri Lanka. Reliability was assessed through internal consistency and test–rest reliability. Validity was assessed using content, discriminatory, and construct validity.Results:Internal consistency using Cronbach’s α coefficient ranged from 0.74 to 0.78 and test–retest reliability assessed by weighted kappa (k) ranged from 0.51 to 0.71. Content validity was evaluated by the acceptability of items for a sample of women and gynecologists conversant in Tamil during the translation process. Discriminant validity between symptomatic and asymptomatic women was significant for all three domains: somato-vegetative, sexual, and psychological (P < 0.05). Factor analysis with exploratory principal component analysis revealed three factors that explained 55.9% of the cumulative variance, demonstrating satisfactory construct validity. A MRS total score ≥6 had a sensitivity of 81% and a specificity of 84.7% to identify symptomatic menopausal women.Conclusion:Tamil translation of MRS retained the psychometric properties of the original questionnaire and will be an invaluable tool to assess menopausal symptoms and HRQOL among postmenopausal tamil-speaking women.

INTRODUCTION

Favorable socioeconomic factors and improved health care have increased the life expectancy of women with almost a third of their life being spent in a menopausal state, and as such, quality of life is an important issue in these women.[12] The Menopause Rating Scale (MRS) is a widely used tool to assess both the health-related quality of life (HRQOL) and severity of menopausal symptoms in postmenopausal women.[34] In addition, MRS can be used as a screening tool to identify those who need referral for further care.[5]

Potthoff et al. categorized the 11 items in the self-administered MRS scale into three independent domains; somato-vegetative symptoms, psychological symptoms, and urogenital symptoms.[6] Subsequently, the MRS has been translated into 28 different languages.[7] A Tamil version of the MRS has hitherto not been done.

The availability of a widely accepted, concise, and easy-to-use questionnaire would be helpful for Tamil-speaking women as there are approximately 70 million Tamil-speaking people in South Asia alone. Therefore, this study was designed to translate the English MRS to Tamil and validate its psychometric properties for it to be used among Tamil-speaking postmenopausal women.

METHODS

This validation study included the translation and cross-cultural adaptation of the questionnaire to the Tamil language and the analyses of the Tamil questionnaire’s psychometric properties (validation).

Cross-cultural adaptation of Menopause Rating Scale to Tamil language (translation)

The standard guidelines for cross-cultural adaptation by Beaton et al. and principles of psychometric testing for reliability and validity by Streiner were followed.[8910] The MRS questionnaire was translated from English to Tamil (forward translation) by an independent translator fluent in both English and Tamil. Following this, a discussion was held with Tamil-speaking gynecologists, community physicians, and patients to ascertain whether the questionnaire measured what it was designed to measure and that clinically meaningful aspects were not deficient in the Tamil version. Afterward, the Tamil template of the MRS was translated from Tamil to English (backward translation) by another independent translator fluent in both English and Tamil. The final version was produced after modifications where necessary to resolve problems and minimize further discrepancies. It was also pretested to ensure face validity among 10 postmenopausal women.

Study design, setting, and participants

A community-based validation study was conducted in the Nanattan Medical Officer of Health area; in Mannar district in Northern Sri Lanka, a predominantly Tamil-speaking community. The study participants were healthy postmenopausal women in the community who had natural menopause. The exclusion criteria were as follows: women with severe medical or surgical disability, mental illnesses, endocrine disorders (diabetes, thyroid dysfunction, etc), musculoskeletal impairment, women who had undergone hysterectomy, and women on hormone replacement therapy (HRT). Postmenopausal status was determined based on a self-reported menstrual history with cessation of menstruation within the previous 12 months after the last menstruation.[11]

Administration of Menopause Rating Scale Tamil questionnaire

The MRS-Tamil questionnaire was self-administered at the community level during the Primary Health Care System Strengthening Project (PSSP) in Mannar. Two research assistants fluent in Tamil were available for assistance during data collection. For sample size calculation, a subjects-to-variables ratio of 10:1 was considered adequate, and therefore, a minimum of 110 women were required.[12]

Analyses of psychometric properties

Validity

Content validity was evaluated by the acceptability of items for a sample of women and gynecologists conversant in Tamil as described above during the translation process. Discriminant validity was measured by comparison of menopausal symptomatic and asymptomatic women for each item, compared using Mann–Whitney U-test.[13] Asymptomatic and symptomatic women in this study were based on the severity of menopausal symptoms; absence of symptoms and mild symptoms in five-point Likert scale (0–1) were considered asymptomatic and other severities (2–4 in Likert scale) were taken as symptomatic women and for the three domains.

Construct validity was assessed using exploratory factor analysis (FA) with principal component analysis (PCA) keeping Varimax with Kaiser normalization as the rotation method to determine whether the latent item structure mirrored the three domains specified in the original questionnaire. The Keiser–Mayer–Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett’s tests for sphericity were used along with the correlation matrix to determine whether exploratory FA was appropriate, for sampling adequacy (KMO >0.7), multicollinearity assessment (most coefficients in correlation matrix >0.3), Bartlett’s test of sphericity (P < 0.001) and commonalities coefficient being more than 0.6. The number of extracted components was determined by the scree plot, percentage of variance explained by each component, number of eigenvalues over one (Kaiser–Guttman rule), and consideration of prior psychometric analyses of MRS. Items were considered representative of a component if their individual loading was >0.4, and in the cross-loading items, the factor with the higher value was selected.[13]

Reliability

Assessment of the homogeneity of the items in the study instrument was done using Cronbach’s α for each question. According to Nunnally’s criteria, internal consistency was considered satisfactory if the scores were more than 0.7.[14] For test–retest reliability, the translated instrument was re-administered to a subsample (n = 87) after 4 weeks by the same interviewer and assessed using the weighted kappa (k) statistic.[15]

Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS 22.0 version (IBM statistics, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Correlation values between 0.10 and 0.29 were considered weak, between 0.30 and 0.49 were considered moderate, and between 0.50 and 1.00 were considered strong.[13] Ethical clearance for the study was obtained and informed written consent was obtained from each participant.

RESULTS

The basic characteristics of the study participants are shown in Table 1. Age of the subjects ranged from 47 to 92 (median, interquartile range [IQR] = 62.0, 58.0–67.0) years, whereas age at menopause and duration since menopause ranged from 35 to 59 (median, IQR = 50.0, 45.0–52.0) years and 0–52 (median, IQR = 13.0, 6.0–20.0) years, respectively. Clinical assessment found that 73 (65.8%) women were symptomatic for menopausal symptoms [Table 1]. The most common symptoms were joint and muscular complaints (41.4%), irritability (27%), physical and mental exhaustion (25.2%), and sleep disturbances (24.3%). Sexual problems and vaginal dryness were rare [Table 2]. Only 4 women did not have any symptoms (3.6%). The median (IQR) scores of somato-vegetative, psychological, urogenital domains, and total MRS scores were 3 (2–5), 2 (1–4), 1 (0–2), and 6 (4–11), respectively [Table 2].

Construct validity was assessed by FA and exploratory PCA showed that 55.9% of the cumulative variance was explained by grouping the items into three domains [Figure 1 and Supplementary File 1]. Item 1 (hot flushes and sweating), item 3 (sleep disturbances), item 4 (depressive mood), and item 5 (irritability) were saturated in domain 1, and therefore, it was named “psychosomatic problems.” Item 8 (sexual problems), item 10 (vaginal dryness), and item 6 (anxiety) were saturated in domain 2, and therefore, it was named “sexual problems.” Item 2 (heart discomfort), item 7 (physical and mental exhaustion), item 9 (bladder problems), and item 11 (joint and muscular discomfort) were saturated in domain 3 and it was named “physicourological problems.” Item 9 (bladder problems) cross loaded in domains 2 and 3 but was considered to be in domain 3 due to a higher loading value in domain 3 [Table 5].

DISCUSSION

The psychometric properties of the MRS-Tamil questionnaire were satisfactory in terms of internal consistency, test–retest stability, discriminant validity, content, and construct validity. The findings of this study are comparable to previous validation studies on the MRS questionnaire.[371617]

A three-factor model accounted for 55.85% of the total variance which was similar to the first FA where a three-domain model accounted for 58.8% of the total variance.[6] However, a few exceptions were noted where certain items were saturated in domains different from the original domains in the MRS scale. In this study, there were five such variations compared to the Sinhala validation which had thee variations.[7] Similar variations were observed in other validation studies done for Czech, Chinese, and Serbian languages as well.[41617]

Limited access to HRT due to the local economic crisis in the country and the coronavirus pandemic precluded assessment of responsiveness of the scale. Discriminant validity, being the ability of the scale to distinguish symptomatic women from asymptomatic women was present for all items including the domains except for anxiety (item 6), sexual problems (item 8), and vaginal dryness (item 10). The low prevalence of symptoms in these items may have contributed for the insignificant difference among symptomatic and asymptomatic women.

Criterion validity could not be done as there was no tool to assess menopausal quality of life in Tamil at the time of the study. Therefore, we believe this tool in the Tamil language can be a screening tool to assess menopause-related health issues in Tamil-speaking women in Sri Lanka, which will be an eye opener to an area that is often neglected. A lack of funding necessitated the study to be combined with the PSSP survey of the Ministry of Health, enabling it to be done at the community level strengthening the validity of the study.

Incidentally, this is also the first version of MRS in Tamil gaining access to approximately 70 million women in the South Asian region, although with local variations in the dialects. It also showed a high sensitivity and specificity for MRS total score ≥6 which could be used as cutoff for referral for specialized care when the questionnaire is used as a screening tool in primary care settings.

CONCLUSION

The MRS-Tamil questionnaire demonstrated satisfactory content, discriminant and construct validity, test–retest reliability, and internal consistency and is equivalent to the original English version. It will be a valuable tool for assessing menopausal symptoms and HRQOL of Tamil-speaking postmenopausal women in the South Asian region.
