Cellulose-mediated ionic liquid crystallization enables tough-stiff switchable ionogels
Authors: Siheng Wang, Huayu Liu, Zhengyang Yu, Xinle Ren, Qi Hua, Mahyar Panahi-Sarmad, Pu Yang, Chuhang Liu, Scott Renneckar, He Liu, Feng Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nature has inspired to fabricate mechanically switchable materials for applications in various aspects, which is, however, unique but challenging to achieve reversible phase transitions using common ionic liquids in ionogels with ambient temperature-triggered crystallization feature. Here, we develop a tough-stiff switchable ionogel through a reversible solvent crystallization design. Cellulose acts as a chemical regulator, competitively binding with polymers to promote the formation of ionic liquid crystals. This results in a tough ionogel with a bulk toughness of 25.7 MJ m−3 and a fracture toughness of 47.1 kJ m−2, which can switch into a stiff ionogel with a tensile modulus of 134.6 MPa and a compressive modulus of 48.9 MPa. Upon heating, the crystallized ionogel reverts to its unconfined as ionic liquid crystals melt. This phase-driven structural and rigidity transition enables dynamical programming, with rapid, reversible and repeatable shape recovery through heating. Our study demonstrates solvent crystallization in ionogels, offering a strategy for creating intelligent, reconfigurable, and performance-switchable materials with customizable functions.

Introduction

In nature, living organisms can tune their mechanical properties to accommodate complex environments and exert functions1–3. For instance, sea cucumbers rapidly harden as a protective mechanism with stiffness increasing from ~5 to 50 MPa4,5. Muscles can also reversibly stiffen and relax for precise control over movement6,7. These dynamic self-regulating behaviors arise from the reversible transformation of organizational structure. Inspired by these natural mechanisms, polymeric materials featuring switchable performance have been developed using techniques such as phase separation-miscibility process8,9, crystallization-melting transition10–13, hydration-dehydration effect14–16, rubber-glass switching17–19, solvent stimulation20,21, and entropy change22,23. Among these, the crystallization-melting transition stands out for its facile and reversible switchability of mechanical properties24–26. Moreover, other than tunable mechanical properties, the crystallization-melting transition strategy can also introduce additional features such as shape recovery and enhanced toughness27–30. Currently, reversibly switching properties are typically achieved by incorporating supersaturated solute salts (e.g., sodium acetate13,31,32, calcium chloride33, and sodium thiosulfate34) into a polymer system. However, the crystallization transition in these materials is highly dependent on the salt-to-water ratio within the system31. This requires precise control over the salt concentration, and often reduces the polymer’s compatibility, resulting in degraded mechanical properties34. Therefore, utilizing solvents rather than solutes for crystallization-melting transition is expected to bypass these limitations, but this has not been widely studied.

Ionic liquids (ILs) are salts with relatively low melting points, which consist of organic cations and inorganic or organic anions. On account of the unique physicochemical properties of ILs, such as non-volatility, high ionic conductivity, and non-flammability, they have been widely used to synthesize tough ionogels35–38. Interestingly, ILs can crystallize when the temperature is reduced to below their crystallization temperature39–41, realizing switchable mechanical properties. However, the strong interactions between the cation and anion species of the ILs and the dissolved polymer chains can inhibit crystallization due to the disruption of the ordered stacking of ILs42–44. In order to realize crystallization in ionogel, specifically designed ILs with highly symmetrical anions (e.g., tetrafluoroborate ([BF4]−), hexafluorophosphate ([PF6]−), and bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide ([TFSI]−) anions) and cations (e.g., 1,3-dimethylimidazolium ([MMIM]+) and 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium ([EMIM]+) cations) species, and cations with short side chain alkanes (<C4) are needed42,45. Such ionogels, typically based on [EMIM][PF6] and [MMIM][TFSI], require cooling to −20 °C to induce crystallization. They also involve complex synthesis processes, and rely on fluorinated chemicals, which restrict their practical applications and sustainability12,46,47. The synthesis of mechanically switchable ionogels using common ILs (e.g., 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride ([BMIM]Cl)), with crystallization triggered at ambient temperature, has not been studied and presents significant potential.

In this study, we demonstrate that a tough-stiff switchable ionogel can be constructed through reversible crystallization in the [BMIM]Cl-dissolved cellulose system, in which N,N-dimethylacrylamide (DMAA) and hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA) are copolymerized within the network. Cellulose, a bio-based polymer rich in hydroxyl, readily dissolves in [BMIM]Cl and serves as an ideal candidate for developing tough ionogels due to its strong interfibrillar interactions15,20,48–53. Here, we leverage the polar nature of cellulose to facilitate interactions with PDMAA and PHEMA segments, resulting in an elastic and stretchable ionogel, referred to as Cel gel (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). The resulting Cel gel can crystallize at room temperature when seeded with IL crystals, transforming into a strong and rigid ionogel, referred to as Crystal-Cel gel. Importantly, benefiting from the reversible crystallization-melting transition of ILs, the ionogel features switchable mechanical properties, transitioning from a tough and stretchable Cel gel (Fig. 1b, c) to a stiff and rigid Crystal-Cel gel (Fig. 1d, e). A mechanical property change of over eightfold is observed, closely resembling the mechanical transition seen in sea cucumbers54. Benefiting from its structurally and mechanically reversible switching, the Cel gel demonstrates the ability to undergo complex morphing and rapid, reproducible shape recovery. Results

Switchable solvent crystallization design in performance-changing ionogel

As a salt with strong electrostatic interactions between the cations ([BMIM]+) and anions (Cl−), the [BMIM]Cl ionic liquids undergo reversible melting-crystallization with a phase transition temperature of 57.9 °C (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). It is reported that within the [BMIM]Cl crystals, each Cl− anion is doubly ionic hydrogen-bonded to three [BMIM]+ cations55,56. However, when DMAA and HEMA monomers are added to ILs (with a mass ratio of monomers to ILs of 0.575), the ILs cannot crystallize at −25 °C with and without seeding (Supplementary Fig. 3). Such non-crystallization observation remains even after copolymerizing DMAA and HEMA into the ionogel (IL gel). This can be ascribed to the strong bonding between PDMAA/PHEMA segments and ILs that inhibits the binding of anion-cation pairs into crystals.

In order to understand the crystallization behavior of ILs with different polymers, a homogeneous cellulose/ILs solution (3 wt% cellulose) is prepared (Supplementary Fig. 4). For the dissolution of cellulose in [BMIM]Cl, the Cl− anion acts as a highly active hydrogen bond (H-bond) acceptor, binding to the hydroxyl group of cellulose. This disrupts the original inter- and intramolecular H-bonds within cellulose, leading to the destruction of cellulose’s crystal structure. The hydrophobic interaction between [BMIM]+ cation and cellulose further stabilize the dissolved cellulose molecules in ILs, thereby obtaining a homogeneous cellulose solution57,58. Interestingly, the resulting cellulose/ILs solution can still crystallize when stored at −25 °C for 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 5), which is consistent with previous studies59,60. The crystallization behavior of the ILs remains even adding the same amount of DMAA and HEMA (mass ratio of monomers to ILs of 0.575) and after polymerization into the Cel gel. As captured from Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Movie 1, the [BMIM]Cl ionic liquids in the Cel gel can rapidly nucleate, grow and aggregate to form ionic liquid crystals after seeding. The formed Crystal-Cel gel appears opaque with a transmittance drop from around 85% to 2% (Supplementary Fig. 7).

The resultant Cel gel with an unconfined structure easily lifts 500 g weight without breaking, and can be fixed during crystallization and reconfigurable into the Crystal-Cel gel (Fig. 1b). The confined Crystal-Cel gel is able to support 500 g weight without noticeable deformation, indicating its excellent load-bearing capacity (Fig. 1d). After crystallization, the tensile and compressive moduli of the Crystal-Cel gel increase 8.9 folds from 15.2 to 134.6 MPa and 6.1 folds from 8.0 to 48.9 MPa, respectively (Fig. 1e). When the crystals melt, the Cel gel shows enhanced toughness of 25.7 kJ m−3 (improved by 4.4 times) and high strain of 1514% (improved by 27.8 times) compared to the Crystal-Cel gel (Fig. 1c).

Evolution of structures and interactions

The SEM images in Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 8 show that both the IL gel and Cel gel present similarly smooth, flat surfaces, consistent with their non-crystalline states. In contrast, once crystallization is triggered in the Cel gel, dense crystalline nanoparticles appear, reducing the amorphous regions. The resulting Crystal-Cel gel contains numerous crystalline nanoparticles due to the formation of ionic liquid crystals. The small angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) profile further supports this observation, revealing a distinct scattering peak at qmax ≈ 0.024 Å−1 for the Crystal-Cel gel, in contrast to the IL gel and Cel gel, which lack significant peak signals (Fig. 2b). This indicates that the amorphous Cel gel undergoes a transformation into a crystalline structure in the Crystal-Cel gel, with an average crystalline domain spacing (d) of 26.2 nm, as calculated using the Bragg equation61. To investigate these interactions, we employed attenuated total reflection-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy to analyze both solution and ionogel systems. The characteristic peaks for the C2 − H stretching vibration of the BMIM imidazole ring in the molten ILs and ILs/cellulose (3 wt% cellulose) are observed at 3039 and 3049 cm−1, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9)62. While in the crystallized counterpart, this peak shows a significant blue shift to 3062 and 3060 cm−1, respectively, which is due to the increased interaction between [BMIM]+ and Cl− caused by crystallization59,60. When DMAA and HEMA are copolymerized within ILs to form the IL gel, the C2 − H stretching vibration shifts from 3039 to 3064 cm−1, and the stretching vibration assigned to the carbonyl group (C = O) of PDMAA shifts from 1629 to 1641 cm−1 (Fig. 2c, d). These obvious band shifts suggest the formation of strong H-bond interactions between the C = O of PDMAA segments and C2 − H of BMIM10,12,63. In the Cel gel system, these two characteristic peaks shift to 3057 and 1634 cm−1, respectively. This indicates that cellulose competitively binds to the C = O of PDMAA segments via H-bonding, thus weakening the binding with BMIM64,65. Therefore, the Cel gel can crystallize to the Crystal-Cel gel, which can also be confirmed by a notable blue shift of the C2 − H stretching to 3075 cm−1. This blue shift is attributed to enhanced ionic pairing between [BMIM]+ and Cl− and increased molecular ordering upon crystallization. Furthermore, when the mass ratio of HEMA to ILs in this system is 0.064, neither the C = O band of HEMA nor the C2 − H band of BMIM shows a significant peak shift, suggesting that their mutual interaction is negligible under this condition (Supplementary Fig. 10).

In addition to ATR-FTIR, we carried out 1H NMR spectroscopy to gain further insights into the molecular interaction between polymers and ILs (Fig. 2e). In the 1H NMR spectrum of ILs, the peak at 9.5 ppm is assigned to the C2 − H proton of BMIM, while the peaks at 7.9 and 7.8 ppm correspond to the C4,5 − H protons of BMIM, respectively66. Compared to the ILs, the spectrum of ILs/cellulose (3 wt% cellulose) shows negligible chemical shifts, and no cellulose bands are observed, which is due to the relatively low concentration of cellulose and the additional effect of 1H − 1H dipolar broadening67. In sharp contrast, the spectrum of ILs/DMAA (33.8 wt% DMAA) shows a noticeable increase in the chemical shifts of the C2 − H and C4,5 − H protons of BMIM, indicating enhanced deshielding and reduced electron density around these protons, which is mainly attributed to the affinitive H-bond interaction between the C = O of DMAA and the protons of BMIM68. However, in the presence of cellulose, the peak positions of these protons shift slightly upfield, suggesting that cellulose contributes to mitigating the deshielding effect of DMAA on these protons. This is ascribed to the competitive H-bonding between the −OH of cellulose and the C = O of DMAA, which increases the electron density around the C2 − H and C4,5 − H protons of BMIM. As a result, comprehensive spectroscopic analysis demonstrates that cellulose acts as a chemical regulator to competitively bind with PDMAA segments, which promotes the binding of [BMIM]+ and Cl−, ultimately enabling crystallization within the Cel gel.

The reversible crystallization-melting observation is determined by the rheology of a temperature sweep. As shown in Fig. 2f, the storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G”) of the Crystal-Cel gel remain high and stable below 50 °C, indicating a solid-like, crystallized state. At approximately 52 °C, an abrupt decrease in G’ and G” is observed, accompanied by a pronounced peak in tan delta (G”/G’), which signifies a transition from a glassy/crystalline regime to a rubbery, amorphous state69. This thermal response corresponds to the melting of the ionic liquid crystalline domains formed within the gel network. Above this transition temperature, the moduli stabilize at lower values, reflecting the enhanced chain mobility and loss of crystalline order. The phase transition temperature of the Crystal-Cel gel is further determined by DSC curves, delivering a distinct exothermic melting peak at 55.2 °C during heating, while the IL gel and Cel gel show no obvious peak signals due to their non-crystalline feature (Fig. 2g). These results indicate that the Crystal-Cel gel can transition into the Cel gel by heating to melt ionic liquid crystals. During the cooling process, no significant crystallization peaks are observed in any of the samples, indicating that rapid cooling alone does not induce phase transitions. This result supports the conclusion that the formation of ionic liquid crystals in ionogels requires the presence of seed crystals, rather than being driven by a short-term cooling process.

To deepen the understanding of the evolution of polymers-ILs molecular interactions, time-dependent FTIR curves of the Crystal-Cel gel are recorded at 60 °C. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 11, the stretching vibration bands around 3144 and 3078 cm−1 belong to C4,5 − H and C2 − H of the BMIM imidazole ring, respectively70. These bands demonstrate an obvious red shift with accumulated heating time, accompanied by a dramatic intensity reduction. The changes in peak intensity and wavenumbers reveal the dissolution of aggregated ionic liquid crystals due to the decoupling of doubly ionic H-bonds between C2 − H and Cl and the weakened stacking of ILs39. Meanwhile, it can be found that the stretching vibration bands of bonded C = O from PHEMA segments at 1718 cm−1 and PDMAA segments at 1633 cm−1 experience a blue shift and gradually decreasing peak intensity during heating (Supplementary Fig. 12). The changes in these bands are attributed to the overall increase in molecular mobility associated with the release of spatial confinement by the dissolved ionic liquid crystals upon heating, resulting in the weakened H-bond interactions between −OH of cellulose and C = O of PDMAA and PHEMA segments22,71. Additionally, the band assigned to the stretching vibration of free C = O of PDMAA segments at 1651 cm−1 also decreases accordingly, which is due to the enhanced solvation effect caused by the melting of ionic liquid crystals72.

Two-dimensional (2D) correlation spectra (2DCOS) of the Crystal-Cel gel are further extracted to determine the thermal-responsive sequence of all the species. Based on the Noda’s judging rule73,74, both synchronous and asynchronous spectral signals are considered to compare the influence order of different groups with increasing heating time as follows (→ denotes prior to or earlier than): In the interval 3175 − 3000 cm−1, it appears as 3078 → 3144, i.e., v(C2 − H) → v(C4,5 − H), indicating that C2 − H is more susceptible to temperature effects than C4,5 − H (Fig. 2h, i and Supplementary Table 2); in the case of 1750 − 1585 cm−1, it shows 1651 → 1633 → 1718, i.e., v(C = O) (free, PDMAA segment) → v(C = O) (bonded, PDMAA segment) → v(C = O) (PHEMA segment), proving that PDMAA segment is more sensitive to heating compared to PHEMA segment (Fig. 2j, k and Supplementary Table 3). The above favorable results jointly demonstrate that the formation of ionic liquid crystals within the Crystal-Cel gel is primarily dominated by doubly ionic H-bond interactions between C2 − H of BMIM and Cl75,76. Additionally, cellulose significantly contributes to promoting confined crystallization by competitively binding PDMAA segments.

Tough and damage-tolerant mechanics with reversible switching

As shown in Fig. 3a, the three types of ionogels with an initial gauge length of 1.5 cm elongate differently when hanging the same 500 g weight. The IL gel is stretched to 4.5 cm and the Cel gel is extended to 2.0 cm, while the Crystal-Cel gel presents negligible elongation, demonstrating enhanced mechanical strength and rigidity by crystallization. The mechanical properties of ionogels were further quantified with tensile stress-strain tests (Fig. 3b). The Cel gel exhibits a tensile strength of 2.2 MPa with a breaking strain of 1561.6%, which is 2.8 and 2.0 times that of the IL gel respectively. The Crystal-Cel gel displays significantly enhanced tensile strength and reduced strain as compared to the Cel gel. Notably, the Crystal-Cel gel has a flow-plastic yield plateau region reaching a total elongation of over 70% (inset in Fig. 3b), indicating its good malleability. This plastic-like yield plateau arises from the intercrystalline regions between ionic liquid crystals, as confirmed by SAXS (d = 26.2 nm), which allow localized deformation and energy dissipation during stretching. While the overall elongation (~70%) is moderate, it is significantly higher than that of many previously reported stiff gels, which typically fracture at relatively low strains, usually below 20%, and exhibit brittle failure behavior11,17. These comparisons highlight the improved deformability of the Crystal-Cel gel. This is further supported by the comparable work of fracture between the Crystal-Cel gel (5.8 MJ m−3) and the IL gel (5.0 MJ m−3) (Fig. 3c). The Cel gel shows the highest work of fracture of 25.7 MJ m−3, exhibiting a 5.1-fold increase compared to the IL gel, which highlights its superior toughness. The Crystal-Cel gel achieves the highest tensile modulus of 134.6 MPa, which is 8.9 times that of the Cel gel and 37.4 times that of the IL gel, indicating a transition from tough to rigid states. Ultra-high tunability ((Valuehigh−Valuelow)/Valuehigh) is realized, with 88.7% (from 15.2 to 134.6 MPa) of the stiffness in the Crystal-Cel gel attributed to crystallization, and 77.4% (from 5.8 to 25.7 MJ m−3) of the toughness in the Cel gel attributed to melting (Fig. 3d). In order to investigate the toughening of the IL gel with cellulose, we compared the damage tolerance and fracture toughness mechanics of the IL gel and Cel gel using pure shear test and trouser tear test (Supplementary Fig. 14)77. The Cel gel with pre-crack exhibits a critical strain for crack propagation of 796.8%, which is 3.3 times higher than that of the IL gel (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 15). The fracture energies of ionogels are further calculated by the Rivlin and Thomas models78 to quantify the insensitivity of crack propagation (Fig. 3g). The fracture energy of the Cel gel is as high as 47.1 kJ m−2, showing an enhancement by a factor of 9.8 compared to 4.8 kJ m−2 for the IL gel. In addition, due to the plastic-like yield plateau feature of the Crystal-Cel gel, it exhibits a fracture energy of 5.3 kJ m−2 with a critical propagation strain of 20.1% (Supplementary Fig. 16). Tear loading deformation provides additional insights for evaluating the damage tolerance of ionogels (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 17). The Cel gel exhibits significantly higher normalized force (applied force/thickness of the gel) and displacement during tearing, indicating its strong resistance to crack propagation. This is visually evident in the inset in Fig. 3h, which shows that the Cel gel extending to 70 mm. The tearing energy of the Cel gel is 7.8 times (0.77 kJ m−2 vs. 6.0 kJ m−2) higher than that of the IL gel (Fig. 3i). This enhanced fracture resistance is attributed to H-bonding between cellulose and the copolymer network, which promotes energy dissipation under crack-tip stress. These results imply that the Cel gel possess excellent fracture toughness and insensitivity to crack propagation.

To further highlight the contribution of cellulose to the mechanical properties and crystallization behavior of the gel system, we prepared ionogels with various cellulose contents (mass ratios of 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2% to the total mass of the gel system) (Supplementary Figs. 18−21). In these investigations, the contents of DMAA, HEMA, and ILs are kept the same. In the presence of cellulose, all ionogels can crystallize by seeding (Supplementary Fig. 18). Simultaneously, the wide tunability in mechanical properties are realized for both crystallized and melted ionogels (Supplementary Figs. 19 and 20). Moreover, the increased cellulose content can strengthen and toughen the ionogels due to H-bonding interactions between cellulose and the copolymer network (Supplementary Fig. 21). Note that ionogels with high cellulose content (over a mass ratio of 1.5%) exhibit slightly reduced mechanical properties due to the aggregation of cellulose (Supplementary Fig. 22 and Supplementary Movie 2). Interestingly, the additional H-bonding introduced by cellulose (a mass ratio of 1.5%) does not significantly compromise the ionic conductivity and the specific capacitance, indicating that the electrochemical performance is well preserved (Supplementary Fig. 23). Additionally, HEMA also contributes to mechanical reinforcement. Compared to the brittle and easily fractured gels without HEMA, the work of fracture of the IL gel and Cel gel is increased by 16.7-fold and 21.4-fold, respectively, upon the incorporation of HEMA (Supplementary Fig. 24).

Integrating these mechanical features, our Cel gel stands out among most reported strong and tough hydrogels and ionogels (Supplementary Table 4), and is positioned in the middle-upper range of the Ashby diagram in terms of work of fracture versus tensile strength and fracture energy versus tensile strength (Fig. 3j, k). Notably, the resultant Cel gel exhibits a comparable work of fracture but a remarkably higher fracture energy compared to typical phase-separated ionogels79. Moreover, the prepared ionogel exhibits an ultrahigh stiff-stretchable switchability, surpassing most reported switchable ionogels in terms of tensile modulus versus maximum strain (summarized data in Supplementary Table 5). This positions our Cel gel in the upper right corner of the Ashby diagram of tensile modulus vs. strain (Fig. 3l).

Stiff and load-bearing mechanics with reversible switching

Figure 4a compares the ability of the different ionogels (4 cm × 1 cm × 0.3 cm) in holding a 200 g weight. It is evident that the IL gel is too soft to support the weight, whereas the Cel gel exhibits a slight bending deflection but remains stable, and the Crystal-Cel gel shows robust support with negligible deformation. These observations indicate that the three ionogels exhibit significantly different stiffness. The flexural stress-strain curves derived from three-point bending test are used to quantify the stiffness of ionogels (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 25). The Crystal-Cel gel exhibits a flexural strength of 7.0 MPa, which is 2.7 and 41.2 times of the Cel gel and IL gel, respectively. Notably, the Crystal-Cel demonstrate a substantial linear elastic region extending up to 15% strain, followed by a plastic region with relatively stable stress under further deformation. This observation indicates that the ionogel, after crystallization, gains increased stiffness without exhibiting the brittleness typically associated with traditional rigid materials20. The compressive stress-strain curves of the ionogels reveal a progressive increase in ultimate compressive stress at 80% compressive strain, with values of 39.0 MPa for the IL gel, 94.9 MPa for the Cel gel, and an ultra-high 148.5 MPa for the Crystal-Cel gel (Fig. 4d). Additionally, we demonstrate the load-bearing capacity of the Crystal-Cel gel, showing that four cylindrical specimens, each with a cross-sectional area of ~0.15 cm2 and a height of 1.5 cm, can stably support a 75 kg load–approximately 13,000 times of their combined weight (Fig. 4f, g, and Supplementary Movie 3). No noticeable cracks or deformation are observed in the Crystal-Cel gel after the demonstration. The cyclic compressive modulus changes between the Cel gel and the Crystal-Cel gel are monitored over five consecutive melting-crystallization cycles, showing a stable and reversible shift from 8 MPa to 50 MPa (Fig. 4h). The 50 MPa compressive modulus of the Crystal-Cel gel far exceeds that of recently reported physically strong hydrogels, organogels, and ionogels (Fig. 4i and summarized date in Supplementary Table 6), making it a suitable candidate for applications that require high compressive stiffness.

Tailored programming and shape recovery functions

Leveraging the reversible stiffness transition and controllable triggering of the ionogel, it shows promise for dynamically programmable materials. As a proof-of-concept, we tune the Cel gel to transform into the Crystal-Cel gel with customized shapes through on-demand fixation during crystallization. As shown in Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 26, the programmed Crystal-Cel gels can be shaped into various geometries, including tunable bending angles and extreme twisting, while maintaining structural integrity. We also configure notched Cel gels for spatial programming, creating complex three-dimensional (3D) architectures like pinwheel and cubic bowl shapes (Fig. 5b, c). The reconfigurable Crystal-Cel gel demonstrates excellent load-bearing capacity by supporting mechanical loading with negligible shape deformation (Supplementary Fig. 27). This reconfigurable ionogel shows a rapid shape recovery within 9 min upon melting of ionic liquid crystals at 60 °C (Fig. 5e). During recovery, all three angles gradually increase, ultimately reaching 160°, 165°, and 160° for angles I, II, and III, respectively (Fig. 5f). The programmable ionogel exhibits a high shape recovery of 89.8% (calculated as θ/180° × 100%, where θ is the recovered bending angle), even after five consecutive programming-recovery cycles, maintaining over 80% recovery (Fig. 5g). Discussion

In conclusion, we demonstrate the fabrication of a tough-stiff switchable ionogel with reconfigurable structures and programmable functions by reversibly crystallizing-melting ionic liquids in a [BMIM]Cl-dissolved cellulose system integrated with polymers. Cellulose acts as a regulator by competitively binding with polymers, enhancing doubly ionic hydrogen bonds between BMIM and Cl, and thus facilitating ionic liquids crystallization. This process creates a stiff Crystal-Cel gel with a tensile modulus of 134.6 MPa and a compressive modulus of 48.9 MPa. Upon heating, the ionic liquid crystals melt, switching into a tough Cel gel with a work of fracture of 25.7 MJ m−3 and a fracture toughness of 47.1 kJ m−2. The ionogel can be programmed into complex shapes with high load-bearing capacity and achieves rapid, reversible shape recovery upon heating. These features are unique in ionogels. We anticipate that this versatile ionogel, combining mechanical switchability, reconfigurability, programmability, and customizability, could be applied to areas such as device programming and stimulus-responsive elements. The solvent crystallization strategy outlined here provides a comprehensive framework for designing performance-switchable, intelligent, and responsive materials.

Method

Materials

Northern bleached softwood kraft pulp (NBSK) was provided by Pulp and Paper Center at University of British Columbia. N,N-dimethylacrylamide (DMAA, 99%), 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA, 97%), poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA, average Mn 575), 4,4’-azobis(4-cyanovaleric acid) (ACVA, ≥98%), 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride ([BMIM]Cl, 98%), and (methyl sulfoxide)-d6 (DMSO-d6, 99.9 atom % D) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). All reagents were used as received without further purification unless otherwise stated.

Fabrication of the ionogels

A homogeneous ILs-dissolved cellulose solution was prepared by dissolving 3 g of cellulose in 97 g of molten [BMIM]Cl at 80 °C for 3 h. PEGDA (0.1 wt% of the monomers) and ACVA (0.5 wt% of the monomers) were dissolved in a mixture containing 2 g of DMAA, 0.25 g of HEMA, and 1 g of molten [BMIM]Cl, followed by mechanically stirred until a uniform solution was obtained. This mixed solution was then added dropwise to 3 g of cellulose solution under continuous mechanical stirring to prepare a homogeneous pre-polymerized mixture. After degassing, the mixture was poured into a mold and irradiated in a UV crosslinker chamber (Model 234100, Boekel Scientific, USA) with two 6-watt UV lamps for 1 h. The heat generated by UV irradiation raised the temperature to approximately 60 °C, which thermally initiated polymerization and yielded the ionogel referred to as Cel gel. The Cel gel crystallizes at room temperature when touched by [BMIM]Cl ionic liquid crystals, forming a crystallized ionogel referred to as Crystal-Cel gel. Notably, molten [BMIM]Cl transforms into a crystalline state after being placed at −25 °C for 24 h. The crystallized [BMIM]Cl is then used as a seed crystal to trigger gel crystallization. Placing a small seed crystal on the surface of a prepared Cel gel induces the formation of a Crystal-Cel gel at room temperature. As a control, PEGDA (0.1 wt% of the monomers) and ACVA (0.5 wt% of the monomers) were dissolved in a mixture of 2 g of DMAA, 0.25 g of HEMA, and 3.91 g of molten [BMIM]Cl under mechanical stirring to prepare a homogeneous pre-polymerized solution. After degassing, this solution was poured into a mold and polymerized under same conditions, fabricating an ionogel referred to as IL gel (Supplementary Fig. 28). Additionally, by adjusting the cellulose content in systems, Cel gel_x and Crystal-Cel gel_x samples were prepared, where x represents the cellulose mass as a ratio of the total system mass. For instance, x = 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2% correspond to cellulose masses of 0.03, 0.06, 0.09, and 0.12 g, respectively.

Characterizations

The microstructure of the ionogels were observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis (Zeiss XB350, Germany). The optical properties of the ionogels were analyzed using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (UV-3600, Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with an integrating sphere across the visible wavelength range of 200 − 800 nm. SAXS measurements were performed with a SAXSpoint 2.0 apparatus (Anton Paar, Germany), and the resulting SAXS profiles were collected for analysis. The rheological properties of the ionogels were conducted using a Thermo Fisher Scientific MARS25 rheometer with cone-plate geometry. ATR-FTIR spectra of the ionogels were collected using a spectrometer (INVENIO S, Bruker, Germany) equipped with an ATR module, capturing data in the wavenumber range of 4000 − 400 cm−1. Time-dependent ATR-FTIR spectra of the Crystal-Cel gel at 60 °C were analyzed with 2D Shige software (version 1.22) in predetermined wavelength ranges. All ATR-FTIR spectra were collected at a resolution of 4 cm−1. All time-evaluated spectra were normalized using the C–H stretching peak at ~2875 cm−1. Liquid-state 1H NMR spectra of DMAA, ILs, ILs/cellulose, ILs/DMAA, and ILs/cellulose/DMAA systems were recorded using a Bruker Avance (300 MHz) spectrometer in DMSO-d6. Rheologic tests for the Crystal-Cel gel were performed using an AR2000 rheometer (TA Instruments, USA). Disc-shaped specimens (25 mm diameter, approximately 0.1 mm thickness) underwent temperature sweeps from 20 to 100 °C at an angular frequency of 10 rad s−1, with a heating rate of 3 °C min−1 and a 20 s wait time between steps for temperature equilibration. Data on the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) and the delta (G”/G’) were collected over the temperature ranges. DSC analysis of the ionogels were conducted using a TA Instruments DSC Q2000, with a continuous heating and cooling cycle from 0 to 150 °C and then cooling to −40 °C. The heating and cooling rates were both 5 °C min−1, with a sample purge flow rate of 50 mL min−1. The process of cellulose dissolution was recorded by optical microscope (OT 13 181, Opti-Tech, Canda).
