Small Extracellular Vesicles in Gestational Diabetes Mellitus: Current Landscape and Emerging Diagnostic Horizons
Authors: Mai Ahmed, Dada Oluwaseyi Temilola, Mushi Matjila, Manogari Chetty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common metabolic disorder of pregnancy associated with significant maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality. Current diagnostic strategies, particularly the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), are limited by late detection, variability in diagnostic criteria, and patient burden, highlighting the need for earlier and more reliable biomarkers. Small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) nanosized vesicles released by maternal, placental, and adipose tissues have emerged as promising candidates for liquid biopsy approaches in GDM. Their molecular cargos, including proteins, miRNAs, circRNAs, and lncRNAs, reflect the physiological or pathological state of the cell of origin and show distinct alterations in pregnancies complicated by GDM. These changes are linked to insulin resistance, inflammation, and placental dysfunction, suggesting a strong diagnostic potential. This review summarizes current knowledge on sEVs cargos as biomarkers for GDM and other pregnancy‐related complications and highlights emerging perspectives on their role in the early detection and management of GDM.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is major global public‐health concern across all healthcare settings, affecting a substantial proportion of pregnant women and contributing to adverse maternal and foetal outcomes. GDM is defined as any degree of glucose intolerance with onset or first recognition during pregnancy (American Diabetes Association 2015).

Additionally, a systematic review has identified a prevalence range between 2% and 24.5%, further emphasizing the continuous correlation between maternal glucose levels and pregnancy complications (Nugroho 2025; Lee et al. 2025). The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) reports that GDM affects approximately 21 million live births annually, with prevalence estimates varying between 5% and 25% depending on regional, genetic, and socioeconomic factors (Mora‐Ortiz and Rivas‐García 2024). While risk factors such as maternal obesity, advanced maternal age, and disparities in healthcare accessibility play a role in disease incidence, the variability in GDM diagnosis remains a critical issue due to differences in diagnostic criteria set by organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO), the American Diabetes Association (ADA), and the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Group (IADPSG) (Mora‐Ortiz and Rivas‐García 2024; Divakar et al. 2025; Hod et al. 2015).

The Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) is the gold standard for GDM diagnosis, involving fasting glucose measurement followed by a glucose load and subsequent blood glucose testing. Although widely used, the OGTT presents limitations, including the need for prolonged clinical visits, patient discomfort, and variability in results due to differences in testing protocols (Richards et al. 2021; Gu et al. 2023). Alternative screening methods such as Haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), Random Plasma Glucose (RPG), and Fasting Plasma Glucose (FPG) were explored during the COVID‐19 pandemic to minimize hospital visits and reduce the need for in‐person testing. Although these methods are generally cheaper and more accessible than the OGTT, their lower sensitivity, higher risk of false negatives, and lack of standardized diagnostic thresholds limit their reliability and widespread clinical adoption (Sun et al. 2024; Panaitescu et al. 2021). Emerging diagnostic technologies, including Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM), vibrational spectroscopy, and machine learning models, offer promising advancements in early detection; however, these approaches remain costly and require further validation to improve their accuracy and feasibility in clinical settings (Richards et al. 2021; Ko and Choi n.d.; Bhuria et al. 2024). Given these diagnostic challenges, there is an urgent need for novel, non‐invasive, and highly sensitive biomarkers for GDM (Panaitescu et al. 2021).

sEVs have emerged as potential biomarkers for various diseases, offering a minimally invasive approach to disease detection and monitoring. These vesicles, typically ranging from 30 to 150 nm in size, are released by cells into the extracellular space and serve as carriers of bioactive molecules, including proteins, lipids, mRNA, and microRNA (miRNA) (Garcia‐Garcia et al. 2024; Vučemilović 2024). Their biogenesis occurs through the endosomal pathway, where intraluminal vesicles form within multivesicular bodies before being released into circulation (Garcia‐Garcia et al. 2024). However, unless the endosomal origin is experimentally demonstrated, such vesicles are operationally referred to as sEVs in accordance with MISEV 2023 guidelines (Welsh et al. 2024). sEVs exhibit high heterogeneity, with their composition reflecting the physiological or pathological state of the organism and the specific cell type of origin (Sani et al. 2024). This unique characteristic makes sEVs promising candidates for use as biomarkers in diseases such as cancer, cardiovascular disorders, and metabolic conditions (Vučemilović 2024; Sani et al. 2024; Huang et al. 2025; Sergazy et al. 2025; Zabeti Touchaei et al. 2024; James et al. 2024). Although many studies cited in this review originally referred to these vesicles as “exosomes,” the term “sEVs” is used throughout this paper unless endosomal origin was explicitly demonstrated, consistent with the MISEV 2024 recommendations (Welsh et al. 2024).

In recent years, sEVs have gained attention for their potential to overcome the diagnostic challenges of GDM, primarily due to their stability in bodily fluids, disease‐specific cargo, and involvement in key metabolic processes (Chen and Liu 2022). In the context of GDM, sEVs play a crucial role in intercellular communication, facilitating the transfer of bioactive molecules between maternal and foetal tissues. Their involvement in key biological processes, including immune modulation, angiogenesis, and metabolic regulation, suggests that sEVs cargo could provide valuable insights into GDM pathophysiology and progression (Zabeti Touchaei et al. 2024; James et al. 2024). Clinically, GDM develops through excessive insulin resistance, driven by placental hormones and inflammatory mediators that impair insulin signalling (Al‐Noaemi and Shalayel 2011; Cursaru et al. 2024). Beta‐cell compensation is often inadequate, resulting in persistent hyperglycaemia (Kühl et al. 1985; Gajera et al. 2023). Placental factors including cytokine secretion, epigenetic modifications, and altered PI3K/Akt signalling further disrupt glucose metabolism and contribute to structural changes such as increased syncytial knots (Xu et al. 2022; Luo et al. 2024). Endothelial dysfunction, mediated by oxidative stress and inflammation, links GDM to maternal complications like pre‐eclampsia (Portelli and Baron 2019). Beyond pregnancy, women with GDM face an elevated risk of type 2 diabetes, while offspring exposed to chronic hyperglycaemia are predisposed to macrosomia, foetal distress, and later‐life metabolic disorders (Gajera et al. 2023; Sarfaraz et al. 2025). This review is focused on exploring the role of sEVs cargoes as liquid‐based diagnostic biomarkers for GDM and the challenges that should be addressed for their clinical implementation.

Literature Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed and Google Scholar databases to identify relevant studies published between 2015 and 2025. The search terms included combinations of keywords such as “small extracellular vesicles,” “extracellular vesicles,” “exosomes,” “microvesicles,” “gestational diabetes mellitus,” “pregnancy,” “biomarkers,” “miRNA,” “circRNA,” and “lncRNA.” Only peer‐reviewed articles that investigated the molecular cargo, biological roles, or diagnostic potential of small extracellular vesicles and related vesicle subtypes in GDM or associated pregnancy complications were included. Reference lists of key publications were also screened to identify additional relevant studies. Despite a structured search strategy, it is possible that some relevant manuscripts particularly those focused on specific EV cargos (e.g., miRNA‐ or mtDNA‐associated signatures) or using alternative EV terminology were not captured; this is a limitation of the present review. The literature was reviewed and synthesized to provide a comprehensive and up‐to‐date overview of the current landscape and emerging diagnostic prospects.

Small Extracellular Vesicles (sEVs) and GDM

Mechanism of sEVs Formation and Release

sEVs formation begins with the inward budding of endosomal membranes, resulting in the formation of intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) within multivesicular bodies (MVBs). These ILVs are then released as sEVs when MVBs fuse with the plasma membrane, a process primarily regulated by Ras‐related in brain (Rab) GTPases, particularly Rab35 (Hsu 2010; Saha et al. 2024). sEVs biogenesis follows both Endosomal Sorting Complex Required for Transport (ESCRT)‐dependent and ESCRT‐independent pathways. The ESCRT machinery sorts cargo into ILVs, but alternative mechanisms involving ceramide formation and, in some contexts such as central nervous system–derived vesicles, proteolipid protein (PLP), have also been implicated in ESCRT‐independent cargo loading (Hsu 2010). Lipids such as ceramides are essential for vesicle release, and their inhibition markedly reduces sEVs secretion (Hsu 2010).

Tetraspanins (CD9, CD82, CD63) also support ESCRT‐independent formation, highlighting the role of lipid rafts and protein clustering (Hessvik and Llorente 2018). Only certain MVBs, particularly those rich in cholesterol, successfully release sEVs (Hessvik and Llorente 2018). The cytoskeleton also contributes, as actin remodelling, microtubules, and proteins like cortactin assist in MVB trafficking and fusion (Hessvik and Llorente 2018; Ji et al. 2025). Rab proteins (Rab27a, Rab27b, Rab35) and associated molecules (TBC1D10A‐C) regulate MVB docking and mobility (Hsu 2010; Hessvik and Llorente 2018). Furthermore, Soluble NSF Attachment Protein Receptor (SNARE) proteins (e.g., VAMP7, YKT6) and synaptotagmin‐7, a calcium sensor, are involved in the final membrane fusion steps, highlighting the role of Ca2 +‐dependent regulation in sEVs biogenesis (Hessvik and Llorente 2018).

Role of sEVs in Pregnancy

sEVs are believed to play an important role in pregnancy by mediating intercellular communication between the mother and foetus, contributing to maternal foetal immune tolerance, and supporting placental development. These sEVs transport essential biomolecules, including miRNAs, which regulate gene expression and cellular interactions necessary for implantation and pregnancy maintenance (Fazeli and Godakumara 2024). Placental‐enriched sEVs are released into the maternal circulation as early as 6 weeks of gestation, with their levels progressively increasing throughout pregnancy in association with the growth of the placenta and enhanced placental blood flow (Mitchell et al. 2015)​. Placental‐enriched sEVs contribute to vascular regulation and immune modulation, carrying proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids that mirror the placental microenvironment and support normal pregnancy progression (Nishi and Modi 2024). In addition, placental sEVs modulate maternal immunity by carrying HLA‐G, an immunomodulatory molecule that promotes maternal immune tolerance to foetal antigens, preventing foetal rejection (Czernek and Düchler 2020). Furthermore, C19MC miRNAs, exclusively expressed in the placenta, play a crucial role in immune regulation by modulating maternal natural killer (NK) cell and T‐cell activity, further ensuring immune tolerance (Ghafourian et al. 2022). sEVs also contribute to maternal metabolic adaptation, influencing insulin sensitivity, lipid metabolism, and vascular homeostasis, which are essential for maintaining a healthy pregnancy (Mitchell et al. 2015)​. Beyond the placenta, adipose tissue‐derived small extracellular vesicles (sEV‐AT) contribute significantly to metabolic adaptations and complications during pregnancy. sEV‐AT levels are significantly elevated in pregnancies complicated by GDM, with proteomic profiles enriched in proteins linked to insulin resistance, lipid metabolism, and inflammatory pathways (Jayabalan et al. 2017). These sEVs also modulate placental glucose metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, and the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signalling pathway, thereby influencing foetal growth and maternal energy balance (Salomon et al. 2015). Moreover, visceral fat thickness has been associated with elevated levels of sEV miRNA‐148 family, which are involved in regulating insulin resistance, further reinforcing their contribution to the pathogenesis of GDM (Yang and Wu 2022).

sEVs can be isolated from various maternal biological fluids, including blood, saliva, and urine. Among these, plasma‐derived sEVs are widely studied due to their abundance and systemic relevance in pregnancy. Urinary sEVs also offer a valuable, non‐invasive window into maternal physiology during pregnancy. Proteomic analysis of urinary sEVs from GDM patients has identified over 600 differentially expressed proteins, particularly alterations in S100 calcium‐binding proteins and inflammatory mediators (Mitra et al. 2023). In particular, S100A9 is significantly upregulated in both GDM and pre‐gestational diabetes (PGD), correlating with maternal obesity and increased risk of macrosomia in newborns (Ramachandrarao et al. 2016). Additionally, urinary sEV miRNAs such as sEV‐miR‐122 and sEV‐miR‐16‐5p have been implicated in beta‐cell dysfunction and insulin resistance, further underscoring their potential as prognostic markers for GDM‐related complications (Mitra et al. 2023).

The Role of sEVs in Pregnancy‐related Complications

However, sEVs alterations have been linked to pregnancy complications, including preeclampsia, gestational diabetes mellitus, and foetal developmental disorders. In GDM, sEV miRNAs, including miR‐210 and miR‐136‐5p, have been implicated in disease pathogenesis, suggesting their potential as early diagnostic markers (Popova et al. 2024). GDM‐associated sEVs exhibit an altered cytokine profile and affect insulin signalling pathways, contributing to maternal metabolic dysregulation (Czernek and Düchler 2020).

The Role of sEVs in GDM

Evidence suggests that sEVs contribute to GDM pathogenesis by disrupting glucose homeostasis and insulin signalling. Experimental studies have shown that sEVs derived from GDM patients can induce glucose intolerance in healthy pregnant mice by attenuating islet glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) and aggravating insulin resistance effects not observed with sEVs from normal pregnancies (James‐Allan et al. 2022). In vitro investigations using pancreatic β‐cell lines further demonstrate that GDM‐derived sEVs reduce cell proliferation, alter glucose metabolism, and impair insulin secretion, thereby contributing to β‐cell dysfunction (Zabel et al. 2023). Mechanistically, sEVs originating from adipose stem cells in GDM have been shown to transport thrombospondin 1 (Thbs1), a protein that promotes insulin resistance through activation of the Tgfβ/Smad2 signaling pathway, leading to decreased hepatic insulin sensitivity (Li et al. 2024). Consistent with these findings, placental‐enriched sEVs classified within the sEVs population modulate insulin resistance and glucose uptake in skeletal muscle cells, directly influencing maternal insulin sensitivity (Li et al. 2024, Liu and Qiu 2022). Furthermore, interventions using antagonistic peptides against Thbs1 have been reported to restore insulin sensitivity, underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting sEVs ‐mediated pathways (Li et al. 2024). Collectively, these findings indicate that sEVs act as active mediators in the pathophysiology of GDM by impairing insulin secretion, promoting insulin resistance, and altering cellular metabolism, thereby highlighting their potential as both biomarkers and therapeutic targets for GDM progression (James‐Allan et al. 2020).

sEVs Biomarkers Associated With GDM

Recent studies have revealed that the molecular cargo of sEVs including proteins, miRNAs, circRNAs, and lncRNAs exhibits distinct alterations in pregnancies complicated by GDM, supporting their potential as early, non‐invasive diagnostic biomarkers (Mitra et al. 2023, Ye et al. 2022, Zhao and Wang 2025).

sEVs Proteins as Biomarker

Proteomic analysis has identified differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in total plasma small extracellular vesicles and placental‐enriched sEVs in GDM patients. These proteins are involved in complement pathways, immunity, inflammation, and coagulation, suggesting their role in the progressive development of GDM (Lin et al. 2024). Proteins such as Complement C3, C5, and others are dysregulated in GDM, indicating alterations in coagulation and lipid metabolism pathways. These changes are associated with clinical parameters of GDM, highlighting their potential as biomarkers (Bernea et al. 2022). Notably, the concentration of placental‐enriched sEVs is significantly higher in GDM pregnancies compared to normoglycemic pregnancies, increasing 2.2‐fold in early pregnancy, 1.5‐fold in mid‐pregnancy, and 1.8‐fold in late pregnancy (Salomon et al. 2016). These sEVs have been shown to induce pro‐inflammatory cytokine release from endothelial cells, contributing to vascular dysfunction in GDM (Salomon et al. 2016). However, contrasting findings were reported by Kandzija et al. (2024), who performed proteomic profiling of placental‐enriched sEVs and found no significant differences in protein expression between GDM and normal pregnancies. The authors attributed these inconsistencies to variations in study design, gestational age at sampling, and EV isolation methods (Kandzija et al. 2024).

sEV‐associated miRNA

sEV‐associated miRNAs are key regulators of insulin sensitivity, glucose metabolism, and inflammation. Dysregulated sEV‐associated miRNAs in GDM include miR‐423‐5p, miR‐122‐5p, miR‐148a‐3p, miR‐192‐5p, and miR‐99a‐5p, all of which are linked to AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) and insulin signalling (Ye et al. 2022). Additionally, hsa‐miR‐149‐3p and hsa‐miR‐455‐3p play critical roles in insulin secretion and beta‐cell function, while hsa‐miR‐3665 and hsa‐miR‐6727‐5p are involved in immune tolerance and placental dysfunction (Razo‐Azamar et al. 2024). Placenta‐derived sEV‐associated miRNAs such as miR‐125b and miR‐144 show differential expression in GDM; miR‐125b is downregulated, whereas miR‐144 is upregulated, suggesting their contribution to glucose metabolism dysregulation and inflammatory responses (Maligianni et al. 2022)​.

Biologically, miR‐125b regulates insulin signalling and supports glucose homeostasis, placental development, and trophoblast invasion processes essential for a healthy pregnancy (He et al. 2024, Dinesen et al. 2023, Owen et al. 2024, Du et al. 2021). Its downregulation in GDM may impair insulin sensitivity and compromise placental function, thereby contributing to the disease pathophysiology. Conversely, miR‐144 plays a role in lipid metabolism, insulin signalling, oxidative stress regulation, and inflammation (He et al. 2024; Poirier et al. 2017; Masete et al. 2022; Novakovic et al. 2019). Upregulation of miR‐144 may promote insulin resistance and inflammatory changes, which are hallmark features of GDM. Both miRNAs influence glucose metabolism and the PI3K/Akt pathway, and their aberrant expression has been linked to placental dysfunction and GDM progression (He et al. 2024; Dinesen et al. 2023; Owen et al. 2024; Du et al. 2021). Notably, their presence in maternal circulation offers potential as early diagnostic biomarkers for GDM (Dinesen et al. 2023; Guarino et al. 2018).

Additionally, miR‐144 levels in circulating sEVs correlate negatively with pre‐pregnancy BMI and delivery weight, and positively with postprandial glucose levels, indicating its potential as an early diagnostic biomarker for GDM (Maligianni et al. 2022).

Further studies have shown that C19MC sEV‐associated miRNAs, including miR‐516b‐5p, miR‐517‐5p, and miR‐518a‐3p, are exclusively expressed in placental‐enriched sEVs and are upregulated in GDM particularly miR‐518a‐5p, miR‐518b, miR‐518c, and miR‐520c‐3p, the latter being linked to placental oxygenation (Cuffe et al. 2017). C19MC miRNAs are also key regulators of maternal immune tolerance, acting through extracellular vesicle‐mediated transfer to maternal immune cells, where they suppress cytotoxic responses and promote T‐cell tolerance, thereby preventing foetal rejection and supporting continued pregnancy (Chaiwangyen et al. 2020). Other sEV‐associated miRNAs, such as miR‐16‐5p and miR‐222‐3p, regulate key pathways including PI3K/Akt, Wnt, insulin, and mTOR signalling by targeting IRS1 and IRS2. Their dysregulation in GDM contributes to insulin resistance and abnormal placental signalling (Geng et al. 2014; Zhu et al. 2015; Oostdam et al. 2020; Kwon et al. 2014; Cao et al. 2017). Interestingly, miR‐222‐3p is downregulated in serum but upregulated in adipose tissue of GDM patients (Zhao et al. 2011; Pheiffer et al. 2018; Shi et al. 2014). miR‐222‐3p has also been shown to modulate glucose uptake and insulin sensitivity in adipocytes, further linking tissue‐specific expression to metabolic dysfunction in GDM (Shi et al. 2014). Moreover, sEV‐associated miRNAs such as miR‐103, miR‐107, miR‐132, miR‐29a, miR‐17‐5p, miR‐19a/b‐3p, and miR‐20a‐5p exhibit significant changes in serum or plasma levels in GDM (Zhu et al. 2015; Zhao et al. 2011; Trajkovski et al. 2011). Several of these miRNAs, such as miR‐29a and miR‐103, are known to impair insulin signalling by targeting key mediators in the insulin receptor pathway, while others, like miR‐17‐5p, affect mitochondrial function and cell proliferation in trophoblasts, influencing placental development (Li et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2023). These findings underscore the broad regulatory impact of sEV‐associated miRNAs on insulin signalling, immune modulation, and trophoblast function. Experimental evidence further supports the functional role of sEVs in modulating glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity. James‐Allan et al. (2020) demonstrated that sEVs isolated from the plasma of healthy pregnant women enhanced glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) in pancreatic islets and induced physiological insulin resistance when infused into non‐pregnant mice, mimicking normal metabolic adaptations of pregnancy. In contrast, sEVs derived from women with GDM failed to promote insulin secretion and instead exacerbated insulin resistance and glucose intolerance. Mechanistically, GDM‐derived sEVs reduced phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate‐1 (IRS‐1) and Akt in skeletal muscle, indicating impaired insulin signalling. Proteomic and miRNA analyses revealed dysregulated pathways involved in oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, and myogenesis, suggesting that altered sEVs cargo contributes to metabolic dysfunction characteristic of GDM. These findings highlight that sEVs serve not only as biomarkers but also as functional mediators influencing maternal insulin sensitivity and β‐cell function in GDM (James‐Allan et al. 2020).

sEV‐associated circRNAs

sEV‐associated circular RNAs (circRNAs) have recently emerged as important regulatory molecules in GDM. circRNAs can bind to RNA‐binding proteins, alter mRNA stability, or act as miRNA sponges, affecting gene expression at the post‐transcriptional level (Trajkovski et al. 2011; Liu and Chen 2021; Chen et al. 2021; Cao et al. 2020; Hansen et al. 2013). Studies have shown that in umbilical cord blood sEVs from GDM patients, circ_0008285 is significantly upregulated, while circ_0001173 is downregulated, indicating a role in foetal development and glucose regulation (Chen et al. 2021). In total, 507 circRNAs were differentially expressed, with 229 upregulated and 278 downregulated in GDM (Cao et al. 2020). Many of these circRNAs contain binding sites for miRNAs involved in GDM, such as miR‐330, miR‐23a, and miR‐16‐5p, which interact with circ_0092108, a circRNA found to be reduced in GDM (Zhu et al. 2015; Pfeiffer et al. 2020; Sebastiani et al. 2017; Yoffe et al. 2019). This supports the mechanism whereby sEV‐associated circRNAs regulate gene expression indirectly through miRNA sequestration.

A novel circRNA, circ‐PNPT1, derived from the polyribonucleotide nucleotidyltransferase 1 (PNPT1) gene, is highly expressed in GDM placental tissue and in high glucose‐induced trophoblast cells. It exerts its function by downregulating miR‐889‐3p and upregulating PAK1, leading to reduced trophoblast proliferation, migration, and invasion. Silencing circ‐PNPT1 alleviates high‐glucose‐induced trophoblast dysfunction, highlighting its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target (Wu et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2021).

Beyond these, sEV‐associated hsa_circRNA_0039480 and hsa_circRNA_0026497 have also been found to be significantly upregulated in GDM, correlating strongly with oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) levels across different trimesters (Jiang et al. 2022). These circRNAs regulate key metabolic and inflammatory pathways, further supporting their role as early indicators of GDM risk (Jiang et al. 2022).

sEV‐associated lncRNAs and GDM

Long non‐coding RNAs (lncRNAs) in sEVs have been shown to modulate insulin signalling and β‐cell function, both of which are central to GDM pathophysiology. A transcriptomic analysis identified 256 lncRNAs differentially expressed in umbilical cord blood sEVs from GDM patients compared to healthy controls (Cao et al. 2020). These lncRNAs frequently contain miRNA binding sites and may regulate gene expression through lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA axes (Cao et al. 2020; Qu et al. 2016; Zheng et al. 2018).

In particular, lnc‐ZNF800‐1:1 is downregulated in GDM and interacts with miR‐362‐5p, which is also dysregulated in the placenta (Li et al. 2015). Meanwhile, lnc‐COX17‐2:3 is upregulated and may interact with miR‐508, another miRNA involved in GDM (Li et al. 2015). These interactions suggest a complex regulatory network involving sEV‐associated lncRNAs in β‐cell function and foetal growth.

Additionally, sEV‐associated lncRNAs have been found to modulate genes involved in insulin resistance, placental function, and glucose homeostasis, further supporting their diagnostic potential (Maligianni et al. 2022). The modulation of transcription and translation via lncRNAs emphasizes their emerging importance as both biomarkers and targets in GDM management (Filardi et al. 2020).

These findings illustrate the diagnostic promise of sEVs cargo in GDM. Further standardization and validation in diverse populations are necessary for their integration into early, non‐invasive screening tools (Yuan et al. 2022). Path to Clinical Translation of sEVs Biomarkers in Gestational Diabetes Mellitus

The translation of candidate biomarkers for GDM requires a defined pathway from discovery to diagnostic application. The OGTT, typically performed at 24–28 weeks’ gestation, remains the clinical standard for diagnosis (Richards et al. 2021; Gu et al. 2023). However, its late timing, patient burden, and variability in diagnostic thresholds across guidelines limit its value for early risk stratification (Richards et al. 2021; Gu et al. 2023). These limitations underscore the need for earlier, non‐invasive, and reliable diagnostic tools that can identify GDM before clinical onset. In this context, sEVs nano‐sized vesicles released by multiple cell types and abundant in biological fluids such as maternal blood and urine have emerged as highly promising candidates for novel GDM biomarkers (Mitra et al. 2023; Liu and Qiu 2022; Zhao and Wang 2025). sEVs act as mediators of intercellular communication, transporting a stable cargo of biomolecules such as miRNAs, proteins, and lipids that reflect the physiological or pathological state of their originating cells (Mitra et al. 2023; Liu and Qiu 2022; Almaghrbi et al. 2023). Studies employing various sEVs isolation methods (ultracentrifugation, precipitation, size‐exclusion chromatography) and molecular analyses (miRNA sequencing, proteomics) have reported significant alterations in both sEVs concentration and cargo in GDM pregnancies compared with healthy controls (Ye et al. 2022; Zhao and Wang 2025; Almaghrbi et al. 2023; Sibiak et al. 2020). Importantly, these molecular alterations particularly in miRNA and protein profiles related to metabolic, inflammatory, and coagulation pathways have been observed as early as the first trimester, suggesting their potential utility for early GDM prediction and diagnosis (Ye et al. 2022; Zhao and Wang 2025; Bernea et al. 2022; Almaghrbi et al. 2023; Sibiak et al. 2020).

Specifically, several studies have identified dysregulated sEV‐associated miRNAs such as miR‐423‐5p, miR‐122‐5p, miR‐148a‐3p, miR‐192‐5p, and miR‐99a‐5p, which are significantly associated with GDM and are believed to influence key metabolic pathways, including insulin and AMPK signalling, thereby contributing to insulin resistance and beta‐cell dysfunction controls (Ye et al. 2022; Zhao and Wang 2025; Sibiak et al. 2020). Proteomic analyses have further demonstrated alterations in proteins linked to coagulation, complement activation, and lipid metabolism, reinforcing their role in the pathophysiology of GDM and their potential as early biomarkers (Bernea et al. 2022; Almaghrbi et al. 2023). Detecting these changes in early pregnancy offers the opportunity for proactive monitoring and intervention, improving both maternal and foetal outcomes (Ye et al. 2022; Zhao and Wang 2025; Sibiak et al. 2020). The use of sEVs as biomarkers presents multiple advantages non‐invasive sampling, cargo stability, and mechanistic relevance offering a more comprehensive molecular insight than traditional glucose measurements (Mitra et al. 2023; Liu and Qiu 2022; Ye et al. 2022).

Challenges in Clinical Translation of sEVs Biomarkers

Despite these promising findings, the translation of sEV‐based diagnostics into clinical practice faces significant challenges. Biological variability among individuals and across pregnancy stages affects the consistency of sEVs cargo signatures, which include proteins, microRNAs, and circular RNAs (Mitra et al. 2023; Lin et al. 2024). The expression of certain markers, such as hsa_circRNA_0039480, may fluctuate dynamically, complicating the establishment of reliable diagnostic thresholds (Yang and Wu 2022; Liu and Qiu 2022). Technical limitations also persist, as isolation and analysis of sEVs require advanced tools such as proteomics and bioinformatics pipelines that are not yet standard in clinical laboratories (Lin et al. 2024; Jiang et al. 2022). Variability across isolation protocols underscores the need for methodological standardization to ensure reproducibility and comparability (Liu and Qiu 2022).

Clinical validation remains another key barrier. Large, well‐designed, multicentre studies are urgently needed to confirm the diagnostic reliability and predictive value of specific sEVs biomarkers across diverse populations and gestational stages (Ye et al. 2022; Zhao and Wang 2025; Sibiak et al. 2020). Understanding how sEVs profiles change over pregnancy, in both normal and GDM conditions, is crucial to define optimal sampling windows and ensure accurate clinical interpretation (Liu and Qiu 2022; Ye et al. 2022). Finally, regulatory, cost, and quality‐control considerations must be addressed to enable large‐scale implementation of sEV‐based diagnostics (Zhao and Wang 2025).

Future Directions

The future of sEVs biomarkers in GDM research holds immense promise, with multiple avenues for advancement. Mechanistic studies are needed to clarify how sEVs mediate cell‐to‐cell communication and modulate metabolic and inflammatory pathways central to GDM pathogenesis (Floriano et al. 2020). A key priority is the standardization and validation of isolation, characterization, and detection methods across laboratories to ensure consistent, reproducible results (Sibiak et al. 2020; Nguyen et al. 2023). Longitudinal studies that monitor sEVs molecular profiles across gestation could identify biomarkers predictive of disease onset and severity (Floriano et al. 2020).

Technological innovation will also play a vital role. Integration of AI‐based algorithms, multi‐omics approaches, and microfluidic detection technologies can improve diagnostic precision and scalability (Mitra et al. 2023; Nguyen et al. 2023). Future biomarker discovery should emphasize the identification of sEVs cargoes particularly miRNAs and proteins that correlate with insulin resistance and glucose dysregulation, as these molecules offer insight into disease mechanisms and early detection potential (Sibiak et al. 2020). Maternal physiological and lifestyle factors, as well as fetal sex, should be incorporated into sEVs studies to better understand biological variability (Oostdam et al. 2020; Palma et al. 2022).

Validation of sEVs biomarkers in easily accessible biofluids such as plasma, urine, and gingival crevicular fluid will be essential for early, non‐invasive GDM risk prediction, even among low‐risk women (Salomon et al. 2016). Cost‐effectiveness and feasibility studies will support their adoption in routine clinical screening (Sibiak et al. 2020; Monteiro et al. 2018). Determining optimal testing timepoints and establishing robust concentration cut‐offs for sEVs quantification will improve diagnostic reliability (Salomon et al. 2016; Arias et al. 2019). In parallel, exploration of sEV‐based therapeutic strategies including delivery of functional vesicles or inhibition of pathogenic sEVs cargo may open new avenues for GDM treatment (Razo‐Azamar et al. 2024; Nair et al. 2023).

Large‐scale, multi‐centre validation studies of sEV miRNA profiles, especially in early gestation, will further define their predictive value and clarify dynamic changes during disease progression (Oostdam et al. 2020; Rathnayake et al. 2024). Investigating how these vesicles interact with maternal and fetal tissues will deepen our understanding of sEVs biology and its role in GDM pathophysiology (Oostdam et al. 2020; Nair et al. 2023). Finally, standardizing sEVs biomarker measurement and reporting across populations, as emphasized by the MISEV 2023 guidelines, is essential for reproducibility and clinical translation (Welsh et al. 2024; Rathnayake et al. 2024).

In conclusion, integrating validated sEVs biomarkers with traditional risk factors and applying multi‐omics frameworks such as proteomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics will enhance diagnostic precision and personalize GDM management (Rathnayake et al. 2024). Combining cost‐effectiveness evaluation with regulatory standardization will accelerate translation into early pregnancy screening programs, improving accessibility and patient outcomes (Rathnayake et al. 2024).

Conclusion

sEVs are gaining increasing attention as potential diagnostic biomarkers for GDM, given their involvement in key physiological processes such as metabolic regulation, immune signalling, and intercellular communication. Nonetheless, significant challenges remain. The lack of consensus on protocols for sEVs isolation and characterization continues to limit reproducibility across studies. Inconsistent methodologies can obscure meaningful biological signals, highlighting the pressing need for standardized workflows and more refined analytical approaches. Innovations such as microfluidic isolation systems and machine learning‐assisted biomarker discovery may help address these limitations and bring sEVs diagnostics closer to clinical application. However, robust, well‐powered studies across diverse populations are still needed to confirm the reliability of candidate biomarkers and to establish appropriate diagnostic thresholds. As the field progresses, sEVs‐based technologies hold considerable potential to advance non‐invasive, early‐stage detection of GDM, offering new avenues for personalized care and improving maternal and neonatal outcomes.
