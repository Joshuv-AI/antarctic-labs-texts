Medications for opioid use disorder, recovery supports, and overdose risk -- a database linkage study of a rural cohort of people who inject drugs and use opioids
Authors: Rachel E Gicquelais, Hailey K Ballard, Mikaela Becker, Sara Doyle, Erika Bailey, Ryan P Westergaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) are the gold standard treatment for opioid use disorder, but rural-dwelling people who inject drugs (PWID) may have lower access. PWID may also receive outpatient or residential treatments, attend mutual help groups, or reside in recovery housing. We aimed to investigate which supports were associated with time to first fatal or nonfatal opioid-involved overdose in a cohort of PWID who used opioids in the past 30 days.MethodsPWID who used opioids (N = 751) in the past 30 days were recruited in Wisconsin using respondent-driven sampling and completed a cross-sectional survey in 2018–2019. Fatal and nonfatal opioid-involved overdose incidence was estimated through 2022 via linkage with vital records, emergency department, and hospitalization data. Cox proportional hazards models summarized associations of reporting MOUD, outpatient counseling, residential treatment, mutual help group participation, and living in recovery housing in the 30 days before enrollment with time to first fatal/nonfatal opioid overdose adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics, health insurance, drug use frequency, substance use severity, criminal legal system involvement, and overdose history. Self-reported barriers to MOUD were summarized among those who had never used each MOUD.ResultsApproximately 18.1% (N = 136) used MOUD, 22.0% (N = 165) had outpatient counseling, 7.2% (N = 54) had residential treatment, 19.7% (N = 148) participated in mutual help, and 7.2% (N = 54) lived in recovery housing in the 30 days before enrollment. During follow-up, 10.7% of participants experienced ≥ 1 opioid-involved overdose, including 114 nonfatal overdoses (incidence: 3,291.6 per 100,000 person-years) and 26 fatal overdoses (incidence: 784.5 per 100,000 person-years). Past 30-day MOUD was associated with a 78% reduction in risk of future opioid-involved fatal/nonfatal overdose (95% CI: 0.08, 0.57). Past 30-day counseling, residential treatment, mutual help, and recovery housing were not associated with fatal/nonfatal overdose. The top barrier to MOUD reported was being unable to afford MOUD (reported among 33.2% who never used buprenorphine, 27.7% who never used methadone, 22.7% who never used naltrexone).ConclusionsRecent MOUD was highly protective against experiencing fatal or nonfatal overdose in a cohort of PWID who used opioids in the past 30 days, reinforcing the need for widespread MOUD access in rural communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28376-z.Keywords: Medications for opioid use disorder, People who inject drugs, Overdose, Opioids, Rural opioid initiative

Background

The ongoing surge in U.S. overdose deaths [1] is largely attributable to highly potent synthetic opioids (i.e., illicitly manufactured fentanyl and fentanyl analogs) [2, 3]. Continued progress in reducing overdose mortality [1] will rely in part on increasing use of the gold standard treatment for opioid use disorder, which includes three FDA-approved medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD), methadone, buprenorphine, and naltrexone [4–7]. Use of MOUD, particularly methadone and buprenorphine, is associated with reductions in opioid use [4, 5], nonfatal overdose [8], and overdose mortality [9, 10], and is particularly protective of mortality following a nonfatal overdose [11].

Current U.S. clinical guidelines recommend offering psychosocial treatments, including residential substance use disorder treatment and outpatient treatments, alongside MOUD [12]. Importantly, guidelines also emphasize that MOUD should not be withheld if patients decline psychosocial treatments. These clinical guidelines are based on mixed evidence suggesting that a variety of therapeutic techniques (e.g., cognitive behavioral therapy, motivational interviewing, 12-step facilitation) provided in both inpatient and outpatient clinical settings may enhance the effectiveness of MOUD to varying degrees [13]. However, other reviews of the literature question whether psychosocial therapy adds clinically meaningful benefits beyond MOUD [14–16]. Little is known about the association of psychosocial treatments with overdose occurrence or mortality, as endpoints such as opioid abstinence are more commonly studied [16].

People who inject drugs (PWID) may additionally engage in community-based supports, such as recovery housing [17, 18] and community-based mutual help groups (e.g., Narcotics Anonymous or SMART Recovery) [19]. Recovery housing is defined by the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration as an abstinence-based living environment, and encompasses sober housing, therapeutic communities, and self-governed residences [17, 18]. While recovery housing is associated with increased opioid abstinence after opioid detoxification [20] and provides important social and structural supports for building recovery skills in an environment that can minimize triggers, little is known about its effectiveness in the context of MOUD [17]. Findings on the association of mutual help group attendance with long-term improvement in opioid use disorder (OUD) are mixed [19]. In one study, weekly or more frequent attendance at a mutual help group was associated with opioid abstinence five years after completing a residential treatment program without MOUD [21]. However, other studies question this finding among patients attending mutual help groups who are also taking MOUD, and patients may be reticent to share that they are using MOUD in mutual help group settings that prioritize total abstinence from using drugs and alcohol [22, 23].

Numerous individual and organizational-level barriers prevent PWID from initiating or sustaining OUD treatment, including withdrawal symptoms, stigma, lack of access to treatment programs, and lack of information about treatment options available [24]. Rural-dwelling PWID face additional challenges that have created persistently lower access to MOUD in rural compared to urban or suburban communities [25–27]. For example, rural communities have fewer providers and specialized Opioid Treatment Programs that provide methadone [28]. Rural-dwelling patients often travel longer distances to get to treatment and have more limited transportation options [24]. Providers in rural communities may also have negative attitudes towards MOUD [29, 30] and novel models of treatment, such as “on demand” treatment that makes MOUD available with no wait time, appear to be more slowly implemented in rural communities [31]. These barriers are part of a larger ecosystem of social and structural challenges that PWID face across geographic settings, including higher rates of poverty and homelessness, lack of health insurance, and criminal legal system involvement [32], which in turn exacerbate fatal and nonfatal overdose risk.

In this paper, we examine whether five self-reported exposures—MOUD use, outpatient counseling, residential treatment, mutual help group participation, and residence in recovery housing—are associated with time to first fatal or nonfatal opioid-involved overdose. Our analysis leverages a sample of 751 rural-dwelling PWID who used opioids in Wisconsin who participated in a cross-sectional survey as part of the Rural Opioid Initiative in 2018–2019. Identifiers reported in the study were used to identify fatal and nonfatal opioid-involved overdose events through 2022 via linkage to hospitalization, emergency department, and vital records administrative data. Among those not receiving MOUD, we also describe self-reported barriers to access to illuminate opportunities to widen access to MOUD in rural communities.

Methods

Data sources and linkage with hospital and vital records data

Data for this analysis were from the Wisconsin node of the federally funded Rural Opioid Initiative (ROI) [33]. As part of ROI, 991 Wisconsin residents aged 15 years or older who had injected any drug in the past 30 days were enrolled between 2018 and 2019. They provided informed consent and completed a cross-sectional survey, were tested for HIV, viral hepatitis, and syphilis. Participants received remuneration of $20. Per the study protocol approved by the University of Wisconsin-Madison Health Sciences Institutional Review Board (Study 2017 − 0866), eligible minors aged 15–17 years were able to enroll without permission from a parent or legal guardian (see Ethics approval and consent to participate section). For the present study, these baseline data were linked to statewide hospital and vital records registries to identify fatal and nonfatal opioid-involved overdoses occurring through 2023.

Respondent-driven sampling (RDS), a peer-driven recruitment technique that aims to recruit representative samples of hard-to-reach populations, was used to recruit ROI participants [34–36]. Index participants (i.e., “seeds”) were provided 3–5 coupons and asked to invite other PWID to screen for eligibility for the study. If these coupons resulted in a new enrollment, the participant who provided the coupon received remuneration of $10 for each referred participant. The same coupon referral process was used for each newly enrolled participant until the target sample size was reached. Participants were enrolled at one of six syringe services program offices located in the Northern and Central parts of Wisconsin and run by community partner Vivent Health; each office serves a rural-dwelling clientele.

In 2024, the research team collaborated with the Wisconsin Department of Health Services and the Wisconsin Hospital Association to obtain data on deaths, hospitalizations, and emergency room visits occurring after study participation. Participant identifiers reported at the time of enrollment (i.e., name, date of birth, and sex) were used to query hospital and vital records data for events among participants. Of the 991 participants, 971 had sufficient identifier information available, including name, date of birth, and sex. Vital records analysts identified candidate matches by querying vital records from January 1, 2018, through December 31, 2023 that met one of the following criteria: (a) exact match of name, date of birth, and sex (N = 47 matches); (b) exact match in last name, date of birth, and sex and match in the first three letters of the first name (N = 3 matches); (c) exact match in first name, date of birth, and sex and match in the first three letters of the last name (N = 7 matches), or (d) match in first three letters of first and last name, sex, and birth year (N = 0 matches). Analysts provided the underlying and multiple cause of death codes to the research team for all matches.

In a separate linkage, hospital data analysts queried data from all initial encounters involving a hospitalization or emergency room visit in a Wisconsin-based facility from January 1, 2018, through June 30, 2022 that documented a complete patient first name, last name, date of birth, and sex (N = 9,913,160 visits). For the 971 survey participants, the SQL SOUNDEX procedure [37] was used to generate a unique patient identification code that was then hash tokenized and matched to eligible hospital records, which were subject to the same process to generate unique identifiers. Among 5,629 linked hospitalizations and emergency department visits, records documenting ICD-10-CM codes for substance use (F codes) or drug overdose (T codes) were provided to the research team (described further below).

For the present manuscript focused on opioid-involved overdose and MOUD, the sample was restricted to participants from the original sample of PWID to those who also self-reported using an opioid (i.e., heroin, fentanyl, prescription medications taken not as prescribed) in the past 30 days, resulting in a sample of N = 751. This excluded 219 participants who injected a drug other than an opioid and did not report use of opioids by any other route of administration (e.g., oral, sniffing, smoking) in the 30 days before completing the cross-sectional survey. Among the N = 751 PWID included, N = 638 injected opioids and N = 113 did not inject opioids but reported using them another way (i.e., snorting, sniffing, smoking, or orally) in the 30 days before the survey.

Measures

The primary exposure variables captured self-reported participation in MOUD, outpatient counseling, residential treatment, recovery housing, and mutual help groups on the cross-sectional survey. Participants reported whether they had received methadone, buprenorphine, or naltrexone in the past 30 days as separate measures. Those who said yes to receiving at least one form of MOUD were considered to have received MOUD and coded as “1” in a binary exposure variable (vs. “0” for those who did not). Similarly, participants who self-reported receipt of outpatient counseling in the past 30 days were coded as having outpatient counseling, and those who self-reported participation in an inpatient or residential program in the past 30 days were coded as having residential treatment. Finally, participants who endorsed that they “stayed overnight at a sober house” in the past 30 days were coded as having been in recovery housing, and those who endorsed attending a “group like Narcotics Anonymous, Alcoholics Anonymous, Celebrate Recovery, or Rational Recovery, etc?” in the past 30 days were coded as having participated in a mutual help group.

The primary outcomes included fatal overdose and nonfatal overdose that involved care in an emergency department or hospital. Fatal and nonfatal overdoses were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) and ICD-10-CM codes indicating opioid-involved events (Supplementary Methods) [1]. Deaths from other causes among participants were also described using underlying and multiple cause of death codes (Supplementary Table 1).

Confounding variables measured in the cross-sectional survey included sociodemographic characteristics (i.e., age, race, ethnicity, education, income, enrollment in health insurance), substance use behaviors (i.e., substances used in the past 30 days, frequency of using opioids, frequency of injecting drugs), whether they had experienced an overdose in the past 30 days, and criminal legal system involvement (i.e., number of days spent in jail or prison in the past 6 months, whether they were stopped or searched by police, arrested, or on probation in the past 6 months). Substance use severity for heroin, cocaine, and methamphetamine use disorders was quantified using the severity of dependence scale [38], which captured whether use was perceived as out of control, whether missing a dose caused anxiety or worry, general worry about use, wish to stop use, and difficulty stopping use.

Finally, the subset of participants who had never used each MOUD was asked whether they had ever attempted to obtain but were unable to start each medication, and whether they considered but never started each medication. Participants who said yes to either question then reported reasons for lack of access to each medication in a select all that apply question. Response choices included being unable to afford MOUD, that insurance would not pay for it, lack of providers, lack of transportation, wait lists for treatment, program rules, and anticipated stigma (i.e., fear of provider disrespect, fear of being seen at the clinic).

Analysis

We described opioid-involved overdose events by type (nonfatal vs. fatal) and by drug involvement (opioids only vs. opioids with stimulants). Weighted incidence rates and 95% confidence intervals were calculated in R using the package “survey” [39] to extrapolate incidence rates from the observed number of events divided by person-time after incorporating RDS-II weights that account for the use of RDS as well as clustering by recruitment seed. As linkage to the hospital and death data used different end dates of June 30, 2022, and December 31, 2023, respectively, person-time was calculated separately for nonfatal and fatal overdose incidence rates from the date of survey completion through each corresponding end of data availability. Unweighted incidence rates were also quantified by dividing the observed number of events by person-time in follow-up.

Subsequently, the associations of self-reported MOUD use, outpatient counseling, residential treatment, participation in mutual help groups, or recovery housing in the 30 days prior to the cross-sectional survey with time to first overdose event (fatal or nonfatal) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Person-time was calculated from the date of survey completion to the date of the first overdose (fatal or nonfatal) after survey completion, date of death from other causes, or June 30, 2022 (i.e., end of the shorter of our two follow-up windows). Unadjusted models and those adjusted for confounders reported in the cross-sectional survey included substance use severity scores [38] for heroin, cocaine, and methamphetamine, criminal legal system involvement, frequency of drug injection, age, gender, race/ethnicity, health insurance status, income, education, experiencing an overdose in the past 30 days, and days used opioids in the past 30 days. Models incorporated all five primary exposure variables simultaneously, as participants could receive MOUD and other supports concurrently.

A complete characterization of the RDS process reporting on characteristics such as convergence and homophily published previously [34] suggests that presenting both weighted (using the RDS-II estimator) and unweighted results may be useful in characterizing how potential biases from recruitment create variability in estimated associations. Thus, we present main analyses weighted by the RDS-II estimator and including clustering by seed and compare them to a sensitivity analysis with unweighted results. Missing data patterns were examined and multiple imputation with 10 imputations was used to impute missing covariate data. A complete case analysis, dropping those who had missing covariate data from models, was conducted as a sensitivity analysis. Finally, among those who had attempted to obtain each MOUD medication or considered starting each medication but had never received MOUD, the prevalence of each barrier to access was described. Analyses were completed using SAS 9.4 (Cary, NC) and R 4.5.1 (Vienna, Austria).

Results

Sample characteristics and treatment at baseline

The original sample of N = 991 participants involved recruitment by 277 “seeds,” of whom 153 recruited at least one other participant and 124 recruited no additional participants. The longest chain was 10 waves. In the analytic sample of N = 751 PWID who used opioids (Table 1), most identified as male (N = 441, 58.7%), White (N = 592, 78.8%), and Non-Hispanic (N = 715, 95.2%). The mean age at the time of the survey was 34 years (standard deviation: 10 years). Most participants used stimulants and opioids in the past 30 days (93.1%), injected drugs on a daily basis (66.4%), and had previously experienced an opioid-involved overdose (52.2%). Nearly three-quarters (71.8%) reported being searched by police, arrested, or on probation or parole in the prior 6 months and spent a mean of 23 days in jail or prison in the prior 6 months. Approximately one-fifth (18.1%, N = 136) reported receiving MOUD in the 30 days before completing the survey; 22.0% (N = 165) reported receiving outpatient counseling and 7.2% (N = 54) reported receiving residential treatment in the past 30 days. Among those who received MOUD, 78 (10.4%) reported receiving methadone, 67 (8.9%) received buprenorphine, and 16 (2.1%) received naltrexone. A total of 19.7% (N = 148) reported participating in a mutual help group and 7.2% (N = 54) reported residing in recovery housing in the past 30 days. Overdose experiences during follow-up

A total of 80 participants (10.7% of the sample) experienced at least one opioid-involved overdose event in the follow-up period, including a total of 114 nonfatal opioid-involved overdoses (weighted incidence rate: 3,291.6 per 100,000 person-years) and 26 fatal opioid-involved overdoses (weighted incidence rate: 784.5 per 100,000 person-years; Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 2). Among all fatal and nonfatal opioid-involved overdoses detected, 18 involved both an opioid and a stimulant drug, and 122 involved only an opioid. The specific opioids identified in ICD-10 codes included heroin/opium (N = 91, 65.0%), semi-synthetic opioids (N = 20, 14.3%), methadone (N = 6, 4.3%), and synthetic opioids (N = 29, 20.7%); 11 (7.9%) listed undetermined opioids (Supplementary Table 3). All the stimulants involved were psychostimulants of abuse potential, which includes methamphetamine and ecstasy but excludes cocaine. Additional substances involved in overdoses (fatal or nonfatal) included cannabis (N = 1, 0.7%), alcohol (N = 2, 1.4%), antidepressants (N = 2, 1.4%), and sedatives (N = 7, 5.0%), which included three overdoses including benzodiazepines.

The weighted all-cause mortality rate, including fatal overdoses, was 1,291.1 per 100,000 person-years (Supplementary Table 2). A total of 22 participants died of causes other than opioid-involved fatal overdose during the follow-up period, with the most common underlying causes being suicide (N = 7, 31.8%) and major cardiovascular diseases (N = 3, 13.6%) (Supplementary Table 1). Four deaths had ICD-10 codes involving substance use disorders or substance use listed as multiple causes of death, including one death each from other stimulant use, unspecified (F15.9), opioid abuse (F11.1), other psychoactive substance dependence with intoxication (F19.2), and other psychoactive substance use (F19.1).

Association of treatment, mutual help groups, and recovery housing with subsequent overdose

Receipt of any MOUD in the 30 days before enrollment was associated with a 78% reduction in the hazard of future overdose (fatal or nonfatal) (adjusted HR [95% CI]: 0.22 [0.08, 0.58], p-value: 0.0027) after adjusting for confounders (Table 2). Other exposures, including outpatient counseling (adjusted HR [95% CI]: 0.87 [0.34, 2.23], p-value: 0.77), residential treatment (adjusted HR [95% CI]: 1.97 [0.67, 5.78], p-value: 0.22), mutual help group participation (adjusted HR [95% CI]: 0.51 [0.17, 1.51], p-value: 0.23), and living in recovery housing (adjusted HR [95% CI]: 0.73 [0.20, 2.66], p-value: 0.64) were not significantly associated with subsequent opioid-involved fatal or nonfatal overdose. While interaction terms between MOUD, outpatient counseling, residential treatment, mutual help group participation, and recovery housing were considered, there were no overdose events among the subsample of participants who only received MOUD and did not participate in counseling or residential treatment, leaving models unable to be fit with interactions between treatment types.

Sensitivity analyses

Covariates included in the final model were missing data in 0.1%-10% of the sample. Each variable in the final, imputed model had > 100 degrees of freedom, suggesting that 10 imputations used to estimate associations was sufficient [40]. A complete-case sensitivity analysis dropping those who had missing covariate data (Supplementary Table 4) did not significantly change the estimated HRs (MOUD adjusted HR [95% CI]: 0.29 [0.13, 0.67]; other exposures remained nonsignificantly associated with overdose). The unweighted sensitivity analysis excluding the RDS-II weights (Supplementary Table 5) also did not materially change the estimated HRs (MOUD adjusted HR [95% CI]: 0.33 [0.15, 0.72]; all other associations nonsignificant).

Barriers to MOUD

The top barrier to receiving each type of MOUD was that participants perceived that they could not afford each medication (buprenorphine: 33.2%, methadone: 27.7%, naltrexone: 22.7%, Table 3). Other common barriers reported differed by MOUD type. Among 208 participants unable to start buprenorphine, other commonly reported barriers included not having a prescribing provider in their area (21.6%) and encountering wait lists for treatment (16.3%). Among 166 unable to start methadone, other commonly reported barriers included not having transportation to an Opioid Treatment Program (15.7%) and wait lists for treatment (13.3%). Finally, among 128 participants unable to start naltrexone, other commonly reported barriers were that insurance would not pay for treatment (11.7%) and wait lists for treatment (7.8%).

Discussion

Receiving MOUD in the 30 days before baseline was highly protective against future fatal or nonfatal opioid-involved overdose, regardless of concurrently receiving outpatient counseling, residential treatment, mutual help group participation, or recovery housing in the same 30-day period, among a cohort of rural-dwelling PWID who used opioids in Wisconsin. The most common reason for lack of access to MOUD across medication types was cost. Other common reasons included a lack of buprenorphine providers, transportation barriers to access Opioid Treatment Programs, lack of insurance coverage for naltrexone, and wait lists to access all three medications. These findings reinforce the need for widespread MOUD access, including all medication options (i.e., methadone, buprenorphine, and naltrexone) in rural communities.

Our finding that MOUD was associated with a nearly 80% reduction in risk of subsequent opioid-involved overdose is in line with prior estimates that MOUD reduces risk of subsequent overdose mortality during periods of treatment receipt by 53%-88% [10, 41]. Nonfatal overdose is a well-established, strong risk factor for future, fatal overdose [42, 43] and therefore an important opportunity to initiate MOUD [11, 42]. Accordingly, we modeled a composite outcome that estimated the association of MOUD with either a nonfatal overdose involving an emergency department visit or hospitalization or a fatal opioid-involved overdose. Unfortunately, rates of MOUD initiation after a nonfatal overdose are low; for example, a study of Medicare beneficiaries who experienced a nonfatal overdose in 2020 found that only 4.1% received MOUD over the next year [43]. To improve MOUD initiation, the extant literature suggests that new care models are needed, including initiation of MOUD in emergency departments [44], a focus on outpatient (rather than inpatient) care models for delivery of MOUD [45], enhanced flexibility for patients to receive take-home doses of MOUD [46], and the introduction of new care models to distribute methadone outside Opioid Treatment Programs, such as through mobile treatment units [47] and community pharmacies [48].

In alignment with current treatment recommendations for opioid use disorder [12], we examined outpatient counseling and residential treatment, two common forms of psychosocial treatments, to determine if they were associated with reduced overdose risk independently of MOUD. Our finding that they were not associated with fatal or nonfatal overdose in bivariate nor adjusted analysis is suggestive that these treatments were not strong enough to independently impact overdose risk and/or that treatments were not continued through follow-up. Our results therefore agree with prior studies and reviews questioning the additional impact of psychosocial treatments beyond MOUD [14–16]. This highlights a need to isolate the most beneficial components of psychosocial treatments, including treatment formats (e.g., outpatient, inpatient), timing, and techniques (e.g., contingency management, 12-step facilitation, cognitive behavioral therapy), that improve outcomes for people receiving MOUD. We found similar results for a lack of independent impact of participation in mutual help groups and residence in recovery housing on overdose risk. Collectively, our results support current treatment recommendations stating that need for psychosocial treatments should be assessed with patients, but not made a requirement for obtaining MOUD [12].

When asked about barriers experienced to accessing MOUDs, patients reported that being unable to afford treatment was a top barrier for all medications. This may be related to the high proportion (29%) without insurance or who did not know if they were insured at the time of survey participation in 2018–2019. Importantly, Wisconsin did not expand Medicaid coverage up through the Affordable Care Act [49], which would have provided coverage for MOUD and other health services for individuals with incomes up to 138% of the federal poverty level. Instead, Wisconsin provides subsidies to assist individuals with incomes at ≥ 100% of the federal poverty level to purchase insurance [49]. Medicaid is a key source of coverage for MOUD in Wisconsin and nationally [50], though the specific details of MOUD coverage varies by insurer and can be overly restrictive to covering specific medication types or care models [51].

Treatment wait lists and a lack of access to transportation or providers were also cited as top reasons for not accessing MOUD. These largely agreed with a review of the literature on access to MOUD in rural areas, which found that a lack of availability of clinics or prescribing providers, travel hardships, and cost were key barriers [52]. These challenges amplify existing economic, structural, social, and political factors that contribute to increased overdose risk in rural communities and set up a unique ‘risk environment’ for overdose that lowers access to and likelihood of treatment [53]. For example, closely-knit communities in rural areas may make people who use opioids in rural areas reticent to be seen at treatment facilities for fear of being identified [53]. Further, our results highlighting the negative impact of wait times for treatment are confirmed by recent research suggesting that access to MOUD with no wait time (i.e., ‘treatment on demand’) is about half as likely to be available for rural-dwelling residents relative to those living in metropolitan or micropolitan areas [54]. To implement the novel treatment models highlighted above and expand MOUD access, research will need to continue to characterize how these treatment models can be adapted for rural communities.

Limitations

Participation in MOUD, outpatient counseling, residential treatment, mutual help groups, and recovery housing were measured at one point in time based on a self-reported measure with a 30 day recall period prior to completing a cross-sectional survey. As such, our exposure variables may not strongly indicate sustained treatment or program engagement over the follow-up period, introducing measurement error into our approach. Future studies could leverage insurance claims data to further examine these results. Relatedly, time-varying confounders beyond baseline (e.g., substance use severity, criminal legal system interactions) were not adjusted for, which may introduce residual confounding. Taken together, the association of MOUD at one point in time with reduced risk of subsequent fatal or nonfatal overdose suggests it is strongly associated with improved outcomes; however, the lack of association for outpatient counseling, residential treatment, mutual help, and recovery housing may be impacted by these biases. Relatedly, recovery housing is identified using many terms [17, 18] and thus our survey question assessing “sober housing” may under-capture participation. The null findings are likely also affected by limited power and small subgroups receiving these treatments and experiencing the outcome, as evidenced by imprecise confidence intervals. Participants who reported Hispanic ethnicity had significantly higher risk of fatal/nonfatal overdose only in the adjusted models from our main analysis (but not in sensitivity analyses); however, the exceedingly small number of participants who reported Hispanic ethnicity and experienced an opioid-involved fatal/nonfatal overdose in follow-up (N = 4) as well as our treatment of ethnicity as a confounder limits our ability to interpret this result. Additionally, study enrollment was completed at syringe service programs, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability, for example if individuals seeking syringe service program services were more likely to enroll and less likely to be engaged in treatment. Further, our modest sample size and lack of fatal or nonfatal overdose events for participants with some treatment combinations (e.g., there were no overdose events among those who received MOUD without counseling or residential treatment) precluded us from examining combinations of treatment types with an effect modification approach, which should be pursued in future work to determine if there are synergistic effects of various treatment modalities or community programs. Finally, our use of hospital and vital records data to capture overdose events during follow-up excludes nonfatal overdose events that were reversed by peers or emergency medical services that did not receive further medical follow-up. People with greater access to healthcare (e.g., those receiving treatment) may be differentially captured in hospital-based overdose event data, potentially introducing differential misclassification of outcomes based on exposure status. Accordingly, we anticipate that the true incidence of overdose is higher than reported here and that our results pertain to medically severe or fatal overdose events. With respect to barriers to MOUD, being unable to afford treatment was the most common, but we were unable to identify the drivers of cost-related barriers (e.g., insurance co-pays, true cost of medication, etc.) and the survey measure may have excluded other key barriers such as participant beliefs about MOUD.

Conclusion

MOUD is an essential tool for rural-dwelling PWID and people who use opioids to reduce the risk of fatal and nonfatal overdose. Barriers to access, including cost, insurance, wait lists, and provider or treatment program availability, must be addressed to ensure continued reduction in overdose risk in rural communities.
