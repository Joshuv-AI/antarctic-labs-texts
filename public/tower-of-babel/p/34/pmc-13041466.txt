Different arts-based interventions for depression and anxiety in college students: a network meta-analysis of rigorous randomized controlled trials
Authors: Peng Chen, Shurong Jia, Yufeng Deng, Jiahao Cheng, Tingting Tang, He Li, Hao Li, Hailin Ma, Tao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArts-based interventions are increasingly employed to alleviate anxiety and depression among college students; however, their relative effectiveness across modalities remains unclear. This study aimed to compare the efficacy of diverse arts-based approaches for improving mental health outcomes in this population.MethodsThis study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and was preregistered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (ID: CRD420251141163). A Bayesian network meta-analysis was conducted including randomized controlled trials published up to 10 September 2025, identified from the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and APA PsycNet. Standardized mean differences (Hedges’ g; negative values indicate symptom reduction) were synthesized under a random-effects model, and treatment rankings were estimated using the Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking curve (SUCRA). The certainty of evidence was assessed using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) framework, potential publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s regression test, and the node-splitting method was applied to assess inconsistency.ResultsTwenty-eight trials involving 2,654 participants met the inclusion criteria. For anxiety, cognitive-behavioral therapy combined with music therapy showed the greatest improvement (SUCRA = 92.4%), followed by sandplay therapy (SUCRA = 82.4%). For depression, music therapy ranked highest (SUCRA = 82.4%), followed by cognitive-behavioral therapy combined with dance therapy (SUCRA = 79.8%). No evidence of publication bias was detected (Egger’s p > 0.05), and the node-splitting analysis revealed no significant inconsistency across comparisons.ConclusionsAmong arts-based interventions for college students, integrating music into cognitive-behavioral therapy may offer the greatest benefit for anxiety, while standalone music therapy appears most effective for depression. These findings support the adoption of music-based programs in university settings and underscore the need for large-scale, rigorously designed trials to confirm these effects and optimize intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04224-y.Keywords: Arts-based interventions, Depression, Anxiety, College students, Network meta-analysis

Introduction

Anxiety and depression are among the most prevalent mental health problems in college populations, undermining well-being, academic performance, and persistence to graduation [1–3]. In parallel with the rising demand for scalable psychosocial care on campus, arts-based interventions—such as music therapy, dance movement therapy, sandplay, drama, and creative writing—have attracted increasing attention because they combine emotional expressiveness with comparatively low barriers to delivery and acceptance [4–6]. However, despite growing uptake in student support programs, the comparative effectiveness of distinct arts-based modalities remains uncertain, and evidence syntheses that can inform prioritization and implementation in resource-limited settings are still limited.

Anxiety often co-occurs with depressive symptoms, contributing to sleep disturbances, substance misuse, and increased suicidal risk, yet it remains underrecognized as students may normalize their distress or attribute it to competitive environments [3]. Although campus mental health services are available, many students postpone seeking help until their difficulties become severe, highlighting the need for early, accessible, and engaging intervention strategies [7]. Together, depression and anxiety represent significant and interrelated threats to the mental health and academic success of college students. Despite the demonstrated efficacy of conventional treatments such as cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) and pharmacotherapy, multiple barriers—including stigma, limited accessibility, and variable treatment adherence—prevent many young individuals from receiving adequate care [8, 9]. Their high prevalence, early onset, and functional consequences highlight the urgent need for effective and scalable preventive and therapeutic strategies [10]. In response, arts-based interventions have gained growing recognition as scalable and engaging approaches to mental health promotion [11].

Arts-based interventions, such as music therapy, dance therapy, sandplay therapy, expressive writing, and related forms of psychological support, have been increasingly applied to promote emotional expression and mental well-being [12–15]. These approaches provide a non-verbal means of emotional regulation, which is especially advantageous for adolescents who may have difficulty expressing their emotions verbally [16]. For example, a randomized controlled trial (RCT) showed that music therapy improved emotional regulation and reduced depressive symptoms among college students [17]. Similarly, combining music and art therapy with cognitive-behavioral strategies has been found to enhance treatment comprehension, emotional adjustment, and overall therapeutic outcomes [18]. Music therapy has been associated with moderate improvements in mood and anxiety; dance movement therapy (DMT) has shown benefits for stress regulation and body awareness; and sandplay and drama-based approaches have demonstrated promising, albeit heterogeneous, effects on internalizing symptoms [19–21]. Collectively, these findings suggest that arts-based interventions may have potential benefits for emotional and psychological well-being.

Several systematic reviews and pairwise meta-analyses have reported that arts-based interventions can reduce anxiety and depressive symptoms relative to inactive controls or usual care in youth and young adults [22–24]. A recent systematic review demonstrated that music therapy effectively alleviates symptoms of depression and anxiety among children and adolescents [25]. Moreover, a meta-analysis evaluated the efficacy of dance movement therapy in reducing depressive symptoms [13]. However, these studies predominantly focus on single interventions or specific symptom domains, resulting in a fragmented evidence base that makes it difficult to directly compare the comprehensive effects of different arts-based interventions and limits comparability across interventions [26, 27].

To systematically synthesize and compare the available evidence, we conducted a Bayesian network meta-analysis to evaluate the efficacy of different arts-based interventions in reducing anxiety and depression symptoms among college students. Network meta-analysis allows for the integration of both direct and indirect evidence, enabling the simultaneous comparison of multiple interventions within a single model [28]. This approach accounts for heterogeneity across studies and provides policymakers with actionable insights into which interventions are most effective in specific populations [29]. Additionally, network meta-analysis enhances the precision of effect estimates and enables the ranking of interventions based on their relative efficacy. The certainty of evidence for key comparisons and rankings was appraised with the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA), considering within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence [30, 31]. Network meta-analysis provides an ideal framework for comparing the relative efficacy of multiple arts-based interventions simultaneously.

To date, limited research has systematically compared the effectiveness of arts-based interventions for depression and anxiety in college students. This study aims to evaluate the relative effectiveness of various arts-based interventions, including music therapy, dance therapy, mandala coloring, and self-drawing therapy. By synthesizing evidence from rigorous randomized controlled trials, we seek to answer a pivotal question: which specific arts-based interventions are most effective for alleviating depressive and anxiety symptoms in college students?

Methods

Protocol and registration

This network meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [32, 33]. The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD420251141163) [34]. The protocol is available at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251141163.

Search strategy

We conducted a systematic search of the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and APA PsycNet databases (including PsycInfo, PsycArticles, PsycBooks, PsycTests, and PsycExtra) to identify eligible studies up to 10 September 2025. The search strategy combined indexed and free-text terms using Boolean operators across four tiers: (1) college students; (2) arts-based interventions; (3) RCTs; and (4) anxiety or depression. The search strategy was developed in consultation with relevant professionals experienced in systematic reviews and meta-analyses to enhance search sensitivity and minimize the risk of selection and publication bias. The complete search strategy is contained in Supplementary Methods 1.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies met the following criteria: (1) Study Population: college students; (2) Intervention: arts-based interventions, including music therapy, dance movement therapy, painting therapy, mandala therapy, sandplay therapy, and other arts-based interventions; (3) Study Design: RCTs; (4) Outcome Measures: primary outcomes assessing changes in anxiety and depression symptoms post-intervention. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) included participants with a prior clinical diagnosis of anxiety, depression, or any other mental disorder (e.g., autism spectrum disorder); (2) focused on special populations (e.g., severe developmental disorders); (3) utilized non-peer-reviewed sources (e.g., conference abstracts); or (4) involved participants using concurrent medications or receiving standard non-pharmacological interventions (e.g., counseling or routine care).

Study selection

Following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, the study selection process was conducted by a research team comprising Peng Chen, Shurong Jia, Tingting Tang, and Jiahao Cheng. Duplicate entries were removed first. Each team member independently screened titles and abstracts according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to identify potentially eligible studies. Full-text articles were then independently reviewed by each team member to confirm eligibility. Disagreements were resolved through discussion between Peng Chen and Shurong Jia, with final inclusion decisions made by Peng Chen to ensure transparency and consistency. For full-text articles or unpublished data that could not be obtained, the corresponding authors or study investigators were contacted to request the necessary information. Studies were excluded if the requested data could not be obtained. The selection process was documented using a PRISMA flow diagram [35].

Data extraction and management

In this network meta-analysis, data extraction was conducted using a modified version of the Cochrane Public Health Group’s extraction form to ensure a standardized and systematic approach. To minimize bias and errors, two independent reviewers performed the extraction process. Key extracted variables included study characteristics (author, publication year, and country), participant demographics (age range and gender distribution), intervention details (content, duration, delivery method, and personnel involved), control group type (e.g., waitlist control or inactive control), and primary outcome measures (anxiety and depression scores). The scales used to measure the outcome indicators are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Any discrepancies arising during the extraction were resolved through discussion between the reviewers to maintain consistency and accuracy, thereby ensuring the reliability of the empirical foundation for subsequent analyses.

For outcome data, we extracted continuous measures, including means and standard deviations, to quantify intervention effects. Effect sizes were subsequently calculated as standardized mean differences (Hedges’ g) to allow comparability across studies with different measurement scales. The formulas used for the calculation can be found in Supplementary Methods 2. Our methodological approach adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews [36].

Categorization of arts-based interventions

The categorization of arts-related interventions in this study was primarily based on the established therapeutic frameworks described in the Handbook of Art Therapy [37], combined with the actual intervention content reported in the included studies. According to the therapeutic medium and intervention approach, the interventions were categorized into distinct types. Although these categories share overlapping techniques and expressive components, they differ in their dominant modality and underlying therapeutic mechanisms.

DMT emphasizes movement and body expression as primary therapeutic media, using rhythm and motion to facilitate emotional release and body–mind integration. Music therapy (MT) involves listening, singing, or instrumental playing to promote relaxation, emotional regulation, and interpersonal connection. CBT combined with music (CBT+Music) integrates CBT techniques with music engagement to enhance emotional regulation and cognitive processing. CBT combined with dance (CBT+Dance) combines CBT strategies with movement-based interventions to promote emotional expression and cognitive restructuring. Expressive writing (EW) encourages participants to explore and articulate emotions through free writing, aiming to improve self-reflection and emotional processing. Mandala therapy (Mandala) employs structured circular drawing or coloring of mandalas to enhance mindfulness, concentration, and emotional balance. Painting therapy (PT) involves free or guided two-dimensional artistic creation using tools such as pencils, brushes, or pigments to promote self-expression and emotional release. Sandplay therapy (SPT) utilizes symbolic play with miniature figures and sand scenes to externalize inner experiences and facilitate psychological integration through a nonverbal and imaginative process.

Although these interventions share creative and expressive elements, they are grounded in distinct therapeutic pathways, ranging from bodily expression and cognitive restructuring to symbolic representation and mindfulness enhancement. Across categories, the hypothesized active ingredients and therapeutic mechanisms differ in their primary targets. Dance movement therapy and music therapy primarily engage emotion regulation and physiological arousal through embodied movement, rhythm, and auditory stimulation, thereby facilitating nonverbal emotional expression and body–mind integration [38, 39]. CBT-integrated interventions (CBT combined with music or dance) explicitly incorporate cognitive restructuring and behavioral skill training alongside experiential and affective engagement, aiming to address both maladaptive cognitions and emotional processes [40]. Expressive writing emphasizes narrative construction and cognitive–emotional processing through structured self-reflection [41]. Mandala- and painting-based interventions focus on visual–spatial expression and attentional regulation, promoting mindfulness, emotional balance, and self-awareness [42]. Sandplay therapy relies on symbolic representation and imaginative play to externalize internal experiences and support psychological integration through nonverbal mechanisms [43]. Further details regarding the operational definitions of the interventions and the control condition are available in Supplementary Table S2.

Assessment of risk of bias

To ensure the scientific rigor and reliability of this network meta-analysis, we systematically and independently assessed the risk of bias in the included studies. This process was carried out by two researchers (the second and third authors), who evaluated the studies based on the criteria outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. A standardized tool was used in the evaluation to ensure consistency and objectivity. We assessed the risk of bias using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) [44].

Network meta-analysis

We conducted a network meta-analysis using R version 4.2.2, applying a random-effects model to account for heterogeneity across studies [45]. This approach enabled us to compare multiple interventions simultaneously by considering differences in participants, intervention types, and outcome definitions. Bayesian inference was performed using the gemtc package, enhancing result credibility while managing hierarchical data structures [46]. Outcome data were formatted as relative effect estimates to be compatible with the gemtc package in R, enabling standardized comparison across studies. Effect sizes were expressed as standardized mean differences (SMD, Hedges’ g) with 95% credible intervals (CrI) to summarize the effects of interventions and facilitate comparability across studies, focusing on differences between intervention and control groups in anxiety and depression outcomes [47].

To ensure model convergence and parameter estimate stability, four Markov chains were run with 20,000 adaptation iterations, followed by 50,000 sampling iterations per chain. A network visualization illustrated intervention comparisons, using the blank control group as a reference point to evaluate the relative effectiveness of various interventions. The blank control, which refers to a no-treatment or usual-care group, is commonly recommended as the reference in network meta-analyses to establish a neutral baseline for comparison. This approach avoids the potential bias introduced by using an active comparator and allows for a clearer interpretation of the absolute effectiveness of each intervention [47]. We assessed network heterogeneity using the I² statistic, which measures the proportion of variation across studies due to differences in treatment effects (I² < 50% indicates low heterogeneity) [48].

Additionally, we used the Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Curve (SUCRA) values to rank the interventions based on their effectiveness. SUCRA is a metric used to rank treatments in network meta-analysis, indicating the probability that a given intervention is the best option compared to others. Higher SUCRA values reflect better overall effectiveness and a greater likelihood of being the best intervention. In this study, the SUCRA rankings provided a clear visual representation of the relative effectiveness of each intervention. Interventions with higher SUCRA values reinforce our findings from the other statistical tests. Explanations of terms including SMD, credible intervals, and other relevant concepts can be found in Supplementary Methods 3.

Network meta-regression analysis

To explore potential effect modifiers, we extended our analysis using a Bayesian network meta-regression framework, specifying the intervention as a covariate and including the control group as a reference. This approach allowed us to examine how study-level covariates might influence the effectiveness of arts-based interventions. The selected covariates included the proportion of female participants, publication year, number of intervention sessions, and sample size. The model was run using 20,000 adaptation steps and 50,000 iterations to estimate the parameters, with thinning set to 1 to reduce autocorrelation between iterations. This approach was designed to account for study heterogeneity while refining the intervention effect estimates based on the selected covariates. We applied a random-effects model to capture study heterogeneity while incorporating relevant covariates. These variables were included to evaluate moderating effects, ensuring a comprehensive evaluation of intervention outcomes [49].

Inconsistency analyses

To assess the consistency between direct and indirect evidence in the network, we conducted a node-splitting analysis. This method separates evidence for each comparison into direct and indirect components and tests their agreement using a Bayesian p-value. A p-value greater than 0.05 indicates no significant difference between the two sources of evidence, suggesting that the consistency assumption holds across the network [50].

Publication bias analyses

To evaluate potential publication bias, we conducted Egger’s regression test and generated funnel plots for visual inspection. Egger’s test examines the relationship between effect sizes and their standard errors, where a significant result (p < 0.05) suggests possible small-study effects or publication bias. In addition, the symmetry of the funnel plots was inspected—symmetrical plots indicate low risk of publication bias, while noticeable asymmetry may reflect selective reporting or other biases [51].

Credibility assessment

The credibility of each comparison was evaluated using the CINeMA online application (https://cinema.ispm.unibe.ch/), which is based on the GRADE approach for network meta-analysis [30, 31]. We assessed six domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. Evidence confidence was downgraded when concerns were identified in any domain: (1) Within-study bias: Comparisons were downgraded when most of the studies providing direct evidence for comparisons were unclear or high risk; (2) Reporting bias: Publication bias was assessed by conducting Egger’s regression test and visually inspecting funnel plots for all pairwise comparisons. Evidence of asymmetry in the funnel plots or significant results in Egger’s test was interpreted as potential publication bias; (3) Indirectness: Indirectness was judged according to whether studies met the target population criteria. Studies involving special populations or lacking direct comparisons with the control group were downgraded; (4) Imprecision: Using a clinically important difference of Hedges’ g = 0.2 as the equivalence margin, comparisons were considered to have “some concerns” when the 95% credible interval crossed the equivalence zone, and “major concerns” when it extended beyond both sides; (5) Heterogeneity: The degree of heterogeneity was assessed by comparing prediction and credible intervals relative to the equivalence zone. If the prediction interval crossed the zone once, “some concerns” were assigned; if it crossed twice, “major concerns” were assigned; (6) Incoherence: Using a global design-by-treatment interaction and node-splitting approach to compare direct and indirect estimates, with inconsistencies interpreted as potential violations of transitivity.

Results

Study identification and selection

Overall, we identified 5,913 citations from six databases: the Cochrane Library (n = 321), Embase (n = 286), CNKI (n = 627), APA PsycNet (n = 316), Web of Science (n = 2,689), and PubMed (n = 1,674). After removing 2,064 duplicates, 3,849 records were screened by title and abstract. Additionally, 36 studies from reference lists were identified for full-text evaluation. Of these, 265 studies were assessed for full-text eligibility. Ultimately, 28 studies (2,654 participants) were included in the quantitative synthesis, with 11 on depression and 26 on anxiety (see Supplementary Methods 4). For depression symptoms, Fig. 3b shows the comparisons between interventions, and the forest plot (Fig. 4b) reveals a broadly similar trend. Music Therapy emerged as the most potent intervention (SMD = -1.76, 95% CrI: [-3.16, -0.36]), followed by CBT+Dance (SMD = -1.70, 95% CrI: [-3.50, 0.08]) and DMT (SMD = -0.59, 95% CrI: [-1.56, 0.36]). Sandplay Therapy (SMD = -0.99, 95% CrI: [-3.36, 1.37]) and Expressive Writing (SMD = 0.01, 95% CrI: [-1.67, 1.68]) showed comparatively weaker effects. Figure 5 displays the rankings based on SUCRA values, with Music Therapy (SUCRA: 82.4%) at the top, demonstrating the strongest effect, and CBT+Dance (SUCRA: 79.8%) ranking second. Heterogeneity was minimal (I² = 11%), and no significant inconsistency between design and treatment was observed (p > 0.05). Similarly, a league table was also developed (see Table 2) to provide an intuitive overview of the comparative efficacy of the different interventions on depression.

To assess the convergence of Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling, we used the Gelman-Rubin diagnostic. The Gelman-Rubin diagnostic evaluates the convergence of the overall chain to the target distribution by comparing the differences between multiple independent MCMC chains. In our analysis, the point estimates and the 97.5% upper bounds of the Gelman-Rubin diagnostic for all parameters were 1, indicating that the model has successfully converged to the target distribution. We also plotted trace plots and density plots, which show the trajectories of the MCMC chains and the posterior distribution of the parameters. No specific patterns or anomalies were observed in these plots, further supporting the convergence of the results (See Supplementary Figures S1 and S2).

Bayesian network meta-regression

To further explore whether study-level characteristics could account for the heterogeneity in treatment effects, Bayesian random-effects network meta-regression analyses were conducted. Each model included one covariate at a time, with the control group serving as the reference treatment. The examined covariates included the proportion of female participants, publication year, total sample size, and number of intervention sessions. All models were estimated under the random-effects assumption with shared coefficients, and convergence was achieved for all parameters. The regression coefficients (β) represent the average change in treatment effect associated with a one-unit increase in each covariate, holding other factors constant. Detailed results for each covariate are presented in Supplementary Figure S3 for anxiety and Supplementary Figure S4 for depression.

In the node-splitting analysis, we examined potential inconsistencies between direct and indirect comparisons in both the anxiety and depression networks. Credibility assessment

Despite the relatively large network, confidence in the estimated effects varied: five comparisons were rated as high confidence, three as moderate, and five as low, as shown in Fig. 7. Downgrading mainly resulted from imprecision and heterogeneity, as several credible prediction intervals were wide enough to influence conclusions about clinical significance. In addition, some intervention comparisons were informed by only a single study, which may affect confidence in the corresponding effect estimates. There were no major concerns regarding within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, or incoherence, as all interventions had direct comparisons with the control group and consistent evidence across studies.

Discussion

This network meta-analysis elucidates the relative efficacy of arts-based interventions in alleviating anxiety and depression symptoms among college students, providing an evidence-based foundation for campus mental health initiatives. Across the 28 included RCTs, we comprehensively evaluated multiple arts-based modalities, including music therapy, sandplay therapy, dance movement therapy, and hybrid forms integrating CBT with arts-based interventions. Using a Bayesian model, we assessed and ranked the effectiveness of each intervention. The results indicate that in terms of alleviating anxiety, CBT+Music ranks first (SUCRA = 92.4%), followed by sandplay therapy (SUCRA = 82.4%). Regarding depressive symptoms, music therapy ranks first (SUCRA = 82.4%), followed by CBT+Dance (SUCRA = 79.8%). These rankings are derived from standardized mean differences under a Bayesian random-effects model, with evidence certainty assessed via the CINeMA framework, yielding moderate-to-high confidence for most comparisons in efficacy. Our findings highlight the pivotal role of music therapy in depression interventions while emphasizing the potential of CBT-arts integrations for anxiety management, advocating for the adoption of these low-cost, accessible non-pharmacological strategies in university settings to complement traditional psychotherapies.

In this Bayesian network meta-analysis of college students, one of the key findings is that arts-based interventions significantly alleviate anxiety and depression symptoms. For anxiety symptoms, the results show that CBT+Music ranked highest for anxiety (SUCRA = 92.4%), followed by sandplay therapy (SUCRA = 82.4%). This hybrid approach, integrating structured cognitive strategies with artistic and experiential elements, may be particularly effective in reducing anxiety by enhancing emotional regulation and bolstering coping mechanisms [79, 80]. CBT-integrated arts-based interventions, by integrating art therapy with CBT techniques, aim to create a space where people can express themselves openly and accept a wide range of human expression [81]. Sandplay therapy provides a structured, symbolic environment that promotes emotional processing and regulation, which may contribute to its strong efficacy in alleviating anxiety symptoms and is supported by observed normalization of metabolic activity in key brain regions such as the hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex [82]. For depressive symptoms, the results show that music therapy (SUCRA = 82.4%) achieved the highest SUCRA ranking, followed by CBT+Dance (SUCRA = 79.8%). The efficacy of music therapy may be attributed to its unique ability to facilitate emotional expression, improve mood, and support physiological regulation through rhythm, melody, and interpersonal engagement [83]. Neuroimaging evidence further suggests that engagement in music activates key brain regions, including the limbic system and prefrontal cortex, thereby facilitating emotional regulation and reward-related processing [84]. CBT-integrated arts-based interventions appear to be particularly effective for both depression and anxiety. These approaches integrate art-based therapeutic practices with the theoretical models of cognitive behavioral therapy [85]. Their efficacy may be attributed to enhanced skill development in critical areas including social connection, non-verbal communication, creative problem-solving, cognitive flexibility, and emotion regulation [86]. The heterogeneity was low, with I² = 9% for anxiety outcomes and I² = 11% for depression outcomes. While these statistics suggest that the model fit the data well, low residual heterogeneity does not preclude the presence of important clinical and methodological differences across studies [87, 88]. Variations in intervention fidelity, duration, therapist expertise, cultural context, and participant characteristics may still influence effect estimates [89, 90]. Moreover, some interventions, such as CBT+Dance, showed wide credible intervals, reflecting potential variability, particularly for comparisons supported by a limited number of studies or a single trial. Therefore, although the Bayesian model indicates consistent effects, some uncertainty related to potential clinical and methodological heterogeneity remains.

Our findings align with prior meta-analytic evidence, reinforcing the therapeutic value of arts-based interventions for youth anxiety and depression [91, 92]. The effectiveness of arts-based interventions aligns with contemporary neurodevelopmental understanding of adolescence—a period characterized by heightened limbic system reactivity concurrent with maturing prefrontal regulation, creating vulnerability to emotional dysregulation [93]. Arts-based interventions uniquely engage both emotional and sensorimotor networks, offering distinct therapeutic mechanisms [94]. For instance, the rhythmic components of music therapy can synchronize amygdala-prefrontal oscillations, enhancing top-down regulation while providing non-threatening emotional expression [95]. DMT modulates autonomic arousal through movement, promoting interoceptive awareness—a mechanism that may underlie its anxiety-reducing effects [96]. Neuroimaging evidence further supports these interventions, showing enhanced default mode network connectivity following art therapies, particularly in self-relevant processing and emotional integration [4]. Collectively, these results indicate that multimodal, experiential interventions—particularly those rooted in the arts—may be especially well-suited for addressing the complex emotional needs of youth, offering both therapeutic depth and high acceptability.

Moreover, the Bayesian network meta-regression did not identify any statistically significant moderating effects of the examined study-level covariates. However, exploratory trends were observed that may provide hypotheses for future research. Specifically, a higher proportion of female participants appeared to be directionally associated with greater improvements in anxiety outcomes, whereas an opposite tendency was observed for depression [97]. In addition, more recent studies tended to report slightly stronger effects for both outcomes, which may reflect improvements in study design or intervention implementation over time [5]. Regarding sample size, larger studies showed a weak tendency toward greater improvements in depressive symptoms, which may warrant further examination in adequately powered future studies. Overall, these observations should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of confirmed moderating effects.

In the context of college mental health, the findings of this study highlight the distinct value of arts-based interventions as a feasible and engaging approach to promoting emotional well-being among students. College students often experience heightened stress related to academic demands, social transitions, and identity formation, which collectively increase their vulnerability to anxiety and depression [98]. Unlike traditional talk-based therapies, arts-based interventions such as music therapy and DMT provide non-stigmatizing and experiential pathways for emotional expression and self-regulation [99]. Their participatory and creative characteristics enhance students’ intrinsic motivation to engage in psychological support activities, thereby reducing resistance and improving adherence. Furthermore, these interventions may be readily implemented in campus settings, as they require minimal specialized equipment and allow for flexible formats ranging from individual sessions to group workshops integrated into existing counseling or wellness programs [100]. From a practical perspective, applying the top-ranked interventions identified in this network meta-analysis to real-world college settings entails several considerations. Brief group music therapy sessions could be delivered by trained mental health educators or counseling staff rather than specialized music therapists. This approach may enhance scalability and reduce resource requirements [101]. Sandplay therapy, while effective, typically requires more specialized training and dedicated space, which may limit immediate scalability; creative adaptations such as portable or abbreviated formats could mitigate these constraints [102]. Compared with more resource-intensive interventions, expressive arts activities (e.g., painting, mandala creation) may offer flexible, low-cost alternatives that maintain therapeutic benefits while being easier to implement in large student populations [103]. Taken together, careful planning regarding staffing, session duration, space availability, and integration with existing mental health services is critical to ensure both feasibility and fidelity when translating these findings into practice.

Meanwhile, the reliability of the network meta-analysis was supported by consistency and publication bias assessments. The node-splitting analysis showed no significant inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence, and Egger’s test showed no statistically significant evidence of small-study effects. In addition, the credibility of the evidence was further evaluated using the CINeMA framework, which demonstrated moderate to high confidence in most treatment comparisons. However, visual inspection of the funnel plots suggested some asymmetry and the presence of a small number of outliers, which may have influenced the estimated effects. Together, these findings suggest that the present results are generally robust while acknowledging some remaining uncertainty.

Limitations

Although we found some interesting results, our study also has some limitations. First, due to our strict inclusion criteria requiring RCTs, some potentially effective interventions were excluded because of insufficient RCT evidence. Additionally, the included interventions were unevenly represented—some, such as music therapy and DMT, have been studied extensively, while others, like mandala drawing or CBT combined with music/dance, were supported by only a single study or a limited number of trials. This imbalance may have influenced the weighting of interventions in the network meta-analysis and the resulting SUCRA rankings. These findings underscore the need for further research on promising yet underexplored approaches to strengthen the evidence base and confirm their relative efficacy.

Second, the heterogeneity of the included studies, particularly in terms of intervention duration, intervention characteristics, and assessment tools, may have an impact on the general applicability of our research results [104]. Differences in regional culture, therapeutic modalities, and therapist expertise may lead to variations in how the same intervention is applied across studies. These contextual factors can affect both the consistency and effectiveness of the intervention. Future research should account for such variability through appropriate cultural adaptation and standardized therapist training to enhance comparability and implementation fidelity.

Third, the regression analyses were limited by a lack of covariate data. Network meta-analysis typically adjusts for confounders or effect modifiers to capture differences in intervention effects. However, the limited availability of data on factors like socioeconomic status, family environment, and baseline mental health may have led to biased estimates.

Finally, the diversity of assessment tools across studies introduced inconsistencies. While we standardized effect sizes for comparability, differences in scale range, sensitivity, and scoring methods may have introduced bias or data loss, affecting the conclusions.

Recommendations

Based on the findings, future research should focus on six key areas: (1) integrating cognitive-behavioral techniques with arts-based therapies to leverage the theoretical rigor of CBT and the accessibility and engagement advantages of artistic interventions, thereby enhancing emotional regulation and coping skills; (2) continuing to expand and evaluate arts-based interventions, given their demonstrated efficacy in alleviating anxiety and depressive symptoms, and exploring their long-term impact and sustainability; (3) applying machine learning to optimize intervention selection based on individual characteristics (e.g., sensory processing preferences, emotional sensitivity), advancing personalized mental health care [105]; (4) utilizing neuroimaging techniques to elucidate the neural mechanisms underlying arts-based interventions, providing potential biomarkers for personalized and mechanism-informed treatment; (5) conducting mixed-method implementation trials to test scalable delivery models, such as school- or campus-based programs, ensuring interventions are feasible and broadly accessible; (6) examining cultural adaptability and cost-effectiveness across diverse populations and settings, ensuring interventions are equitable, generalizable, and suitable for widespread implementation [106, 107]. This approach aligns with the World Health Organization’s 2023 Mental Health Action Plan, which emphasizes “developmentally appropriate, culturally rooted interventions” [108].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this network meta-analysis of 28 randomized controlled trials evaluated the effectiveness of arts-based interventions in treating anxiety and depression in college students. In terms of alleviating anxiety, CBT+Music was ranked highest (SUCRA = 92.4%), followed by sandplay therapy (SUCRA = 82.4%). Regarding depressive symptoms, music therapy ranks first (SUCRA = 82.4%), followed by CBT+Dance (SUCRA = 79.8%). The integration of artistic and cognitive approaches appears to be a particularly effective strategy for managing both conditions simultaneously. These findings have important implications for clinical practice, suggesting that multimodal interventions may offer a comprehensive, accessible, and engaging approach to college students mental health care. Further research is needed to refine these interventions and explore their long-term impacts and cross-cultural applicability. Collectively, these findings provide a comprehensive evidence base supporting the integration of arts-based modalities into university mental health services, highlighting their therapeutic potential and practical feasibility for addressing depression and anxiety among college students.
