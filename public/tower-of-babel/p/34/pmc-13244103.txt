What Will Define the Next Era of Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement
Authors: Juri Iwata, Kentaro Hayashida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Lee et al1 evaluated the valuable insights into the evolving landscape of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) in South Korea, focusing on nationwide procedure strategies and case volumes, clinical outcomes, and economic implications in a real-world setting. As the indication for TAVR continues to expand beyond high-risk patients to intermediate- and low-risk populations worldwide,2,3 understanding regional practice patterns and health care resource utilization has become increasingly important.

One of the notable strengths of their analysis lies in its comprehensive evaluation of nationwide data, which allows for an overview of how TAVR treatment has progressed within the Korean health care system from 2015 to 2021. Such population-level analyses are essential for capturing procedural trends, institutional variability, and real-world outcomes that may not be fully reflected in randomized clinical trials. The findings appear to confirm the growing acceptance of TAVR as a standard therapeutic option for patients with severe aortic stenosis, reflecting global trends observed over the past decade.

In addition to documenting the increase in case and hospital numbers of TAVR in South Korea, the study provides important insights into clinical outcomes not only immediately after TAVR, but also during outpatient follow-up. The reported outcomes, such as a 2.9% in-hospital mortality or 99% procedural success rate, seem consistent with those observed in international registries and landmark trials, supporting the safety and effectiveness of contemporary TAVR practice in Korea.4, 5, 6, 7

Improvements in procedural techniques, device technology, and patient selection likely contribute to these favorable results. Because the Asian region exhibits distinct anatomical characteristics, such as a higher prevalence of small aortic annuli compared with Western countries, it is likely important to understand the differences in patient profiles across regions to further improve clinical outcomes after TAVR.8,9

Another important contribution of this work is its focus on economic considerations. As health care systems worldwide struggle with rising costs associated with advanced structural heart interventions, cost analyses are increasingly relevant for policymakers and clinicians alike. The authors’ evaluation of procedural costs and health care utilization provides a useful framework for understanding the financial implications of TAVR within a national insurance system. Regarding TAVR, given that the procedural materials cost the highest, such information may help guide future reimbursement strategies and resource allocation decisions. However, as the authors note in the limitations section, there is a global lack of cost comparisons between TAVR and surgical aortic valve replacement, and comparing the costs of these 2 procedures will be a key challenge as TAVR indication expands in the future.10

Lee et al1 mention the increase in the number of hospitals performing TAVR. The number of TAVR hospitals in Japan also continues to rise. However, since TAVR became reimbursed in Japan in 2013, high-volume centers performing TAVR since its introduction boast stable safety profiles (with a 30-day mortality of 0.5%) based on their high technical expertise, and their case numbers are increasing despite the increasing number of hospitals performing TAVR.11 Naturally, reducing complications directly leads to lower medical costs for each individual patient, and progress is expected not only in TAVR devices but also in the dedicated TAVR techniques of the operators and hospitals. Furthermore, the continued decline in procedural complications reported in many real-world datasets underscores the impact of operator experience and institutional learning curves.12 It goes without saying that it is essential for operators to master the necessary technical skills to provide patients with high-quality TAVR.

Despite these strengths, several considerations should be taken into account when interpreting the results. As with most retrospective or patient-level data from the registry-based analyses, the potential for unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Detailed clinical variables, such as frailty, anatomical complexity, or technical differences during the procedures, were not fully included in administrative datasets. Additionally, long-term follow-up remains critical, particularly as TAVR is increasingly offered to younger and lower-risk populations in whom valve durability and lifetime management strategies are of growing importance.13

Looking ahead, continued surveillance of real-world outcomes will be essential as TAVR technology evolves and indications expand. Future studies integrating clinical, procedural, and imaging data may further clarify patient selection strategies and optimize outcomes. Furthermore, as Lee et al1 note, the global population is aging, including South Korea, and the number of patients eligible for TAVR is expected to increase. For this reason, it is crucial to consider how to make the most effective use of limited medical resources and insurance systems, and cost-effectiveness analyses will be more essential.

In conclusion, by examining procedural trends, clinical outcomes, and cost considerations simultaneously, more patients will benefit from TAVR, and this will contribute to further advancements in treatment devices and techniques. These findings will be of interest not only to clinicians involved in structural heart interventions, but also to health care administrators and policymakers seeking to balance innovation with sustainability in modern cardiovascular medicine.
