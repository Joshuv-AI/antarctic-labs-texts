Assessing the albumin–bilirubin score’s role in predicting the mortality rate of severe acute pancreatitis: A multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Baiyan Xu, Huizhong Chen, Tingting Xu, Qilin Yang, Jianchuan Zhang, Guibao Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The albumin–bilirubin (ALBI) score serves as an effective alternative for assessing patients with hepatocellular carcinoma, sepsis, and acute kidney injury. Its relevance to clinical outcomes in acute pancreatitis (AP) is not well defined. A retrospective multicenter analysis of critically ill patients with AP was conducted using the medical information mart for intensive care (MIMIC) IV (v3.1) and eICU collaborative research database (eICU-CRD) (v2.0) databases. The association between ALBI score and mortality was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves, smooth curve fitting, and both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. A subgroup analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the results. This study analyzed 529 AP patients from the MIMIC-IV database and 410 from the eICU-CRD database. ALBI scores were categorized into 2 groups: <‐1.546 and ≥‐1.546. The higher-ALBI-scores group was associated with higher in-hospital (adjusted hazard ratios [HRs]: 2.34, 95 % confidence intervals [CIs]: 1.45–3.79, P < .001) and intensive care unit mortality (adjusted, MIMIC-IV HR: 2.17, 95 % CI: 1.23–3.84, P = .008 vs eICU-CRD HR: 2.22, 95% CI: 1.08–4.56, P = .029) compared to the lower-ALBI-scores group. Curve-fitted analysis revealed a linear relationship between ALBI scores and mortality was observed (P for nonlinearity > .05). The ALBI score did not interact with most subgroups. In critically ill patients with AP, an elevated ALBI score is associated with worse clinical outcomes.

1. Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a common gastrointestinal disorder characterized by localized injury, systemic inflammation, and potential organ dysfunction.[1] Even with standardized diagnostic and therapeutic methods, >25% of AP patients develop complications like pancreatic necrosis and organ failure, frequently requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission.[2–4] Severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) patients face a high mortality rate, ranging from 30% to 50%, and typically endure hospital stays exceeding 1 month.[3,5–7] The disease causes acute inflammation of the pancreas and nearby tissues, potentially leading to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome and heightened mortality risk, with septicemia as a primary cause of death.[8] Thus, there is a clear need for simpler and more reliable markers to identify mortality risk in AP patients, which could help reduce both complication rates and fatalities.

Several scoring systems are currently employed in clinical practice to assess AP severity, including the Glasgow score, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (II), Bedside Index of Severity in AP, Ranson criteria, sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA), and the Modified Computed Tomography Severity Index. These tools incorporate clinical, biochemical, and radiological parameters, yet their sensitivity and specificity for identifying SAP vary widely, from 55% to 90%.[8–13] They often require considerable time and resources to complete. Thus, there is a clear need for simpler and more reliable markers to identify mortality risk in AP patients, which could help reduce both complication rates and fatalities. Their complexity impairs the capacity for timely and accurate assessment of disease severity at an early stage.

The albumin–bilirubin score (ALBI) score, determined by serum albumin and bilirubin levels, was initially created to forecast outcomes in hepatocellular carcinoma. It has since been applied to assess outcomes in conditions such as liver injury, sepsis, and acute kidney injury. These studies indicate that the ALBI score correlates with indices of inflammation.[14–16] Due to inflammatory processes play a key role in the development and progression of AP. We propose that the ALBI score could be a valuable biomarker for critically ill AP patients. Its ability to predict mortality in this specific population remains unconfirmed. This study assess the correlation between the ALBI score and the mortality in critically ill AP patients.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data source

Datas from the medical information mart for intensive care (MIMIC-IV) (v3.1) and eICU collaborative research database (eICU-CRD) (v2.0) databases were utilized for this retrospective study. The MIMIC-IV database comprises comprehensive and high-quality data from 65,366 patients admitted to the intensive care units at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2022.[17] The eICU-CRD (v2.0) database comprises data from 200,859 patients across 208 U.S. hospitals collected in 2014 and 2015.[18] Guibao Jiang received authorization to access the MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD database (record number: 66796235), and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). Given that the datasets are fully anonymized and do not contain protected health information, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. The study’s design, conduct and reporting conformed fully to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in epidemiology statement.[19]

2.2. Study population

Within the Postgre Structured Query Language environment (version 14.6), we utilized Structured Query Language for data extraction. In our study, patients who was diagnosed with AP both in hospital and in ICU were included from these 2 databases.[20] Patients diagnosed with AP were identified using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 9th Revision (ICD-9), and 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, specifically 5770, K8590, K8510, K8520, K859, K8580, K859, K851, K8521, K852, K8500, K8592, K8511, K8530, K8581, K858, K8522, K8512, K853, K8582, K8501, K8502, K850, and K8531, from the MIMIC-IV database, as well as codes 577.0 and K85.9 from the eICU-CRD database.[21] The exclusion criteria are: non the first admission to ICU; under the age of 18-years old; lacking recorded admission-day data of bilirubin and albumin levels; ICU hospital stays shorter than 24 hours.

2.3. Data collection

The ALBI score served as the main variable in this investigation. We collected the data within the initial 24 hours after the patient was admitted to the ICU. From the database, we extracted demographic data (age, gender, and weight), clinical measurements (vital signs, serum creatinine, bicarbonate, platelet counts, white blood cell count, blood urea nitrogen, total bilirubin, albumin, serum sodium, and serum potassium), medical history (myocardial infarction, chronic heart failure, severe liver disease, chronic pulmonary disease, and diabetes), treatments (vasoactive medications, mechanical ventilation, and continuous renal replacement therapy), and assessment scores (simplified acute physiology score II [SAPS II], SOFA). Records were kept of the ICU stay length and the hospital stay length. The survival time and status were also collected, such as in-hospital mortality, ICU mortality, mortality rates at 28, 60, 90, and 360 days were also calculated.

2.4. Calculation of ALBI score

The ALBI score was determined using the following formula: ALBI = (log10 bilirubin [μmol/L] × 0.66 − 0.085 × albumin [g/L]).[22] We stratified patients with AP into 2 groups using the ALBI score (50% quantile): <‐1.546 and ≥‐1.546.

2.5. Outcome variables

The primary outcome was the hospital and ICU mortality. Mortality rates at 28 days, 60 days, 90 days, and 365 days were also sourced from the MIMIC-IV database as secondary outcomes.

2.6. Statistical analysis and model building

In our study, continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are shown as number (percentage). Student t test or chi-square test was used to compare different ALBI score groups. The k-nearest neighbors algorithm was applied to handle incomplete covariate data. We adjusted for covariates including age, sex, respiratory rate, peripheral oxygen saturation, and white blood cell count. Multivariate Cox regression models were utilized to determine the relationship between the ALBI score and the mortality, calculating the related hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI). Kaplan–Meier curves was applied to create survival curves, and the log-rank test the log-rank test was used to assess statistically significant differences. Curve-fitting procedures were applied to delineate the relationship between the ALBI score and mortality. Subgroup analysis was exhibited by the forest plot. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2 (https://www.Rproject.org) and the Free Statistics software, version 2.2 (https://www.clinicalscientists.cn/freestatistics). Statistical significance was considered for a P value <.05.

The study population had a median age of 58.2 years and was predominantly male (55.6 %). 410 patients were enrolled in the eICU-CRD database in totally. The median age was 54.1 years, and 252 (61.5%) patients were female. 3.2. Primary outcome

The rate of mortality for patients in the hospital was 17.60% (93/529) in the MIMIC-IV database and 11.00% (45/410) in the eICU-CRD database, ICU mortality was 12.10% (64/529) and 9.00% (37/410), respectively. Both in-hospital and ICU mortality rates were significantly higher (P < .05) in the high ALBI score group compared to the low ALBI score group (P < .05). We also found significant differences between the 2 groups, as detailed in Table 1.

3.3. Cox regression analysis

The high ALBI score group experienced increased in-hospital and ICU mortality rates (P < .05), as well as increased lengths of stay in both the ICU and the hospital overall (Table 1).

Multivariate Cox regression analyses assessed the potential link between ALBI score and mortality in AP patients. The ALBI score was significantly correlated with an elevated risk of in-hospital mortality in both databases. The data findings were observed in-hospital mortality: in unadjusted models: (MIMIC-IV: HR: 2.21, 95% CI: 1.58–3.10, P < .001 and eICU-CRD: HR: 2.41, 95% CI: 1.50–3.85, P < .001), in adjusted model: (MIMIC-IV: HR: 2.30, 95% CI: 1.62–3.26, P < .001 and eICU-CRD: HR: 2.56, 95% CI: 1.52–4.31, P < .001). Compared with the group of lower ALBI scores, patients exhibiting high ALBI levels were at a substantially increased risk of dying in the hospital (adjusted MIMIC-IV: HR: 2.34, 95 % CI: 1.45–3.79, P < .001 vs eICU-CRD: HR: 2.34, 95% CI: 1.45–3.79, P = .001). Similar results for ICU mortality were found:in unadjusted models: (MIMIC-IV: HR: 2.83, 95% CI: 1.81–4.43, P < .001 vs eICU-CRD: HR: 2.40, 95% CI: 1.43–4.03, P < .001), in adjusted model: (MIMIC-IV: HR: 3.13, 95% CI: 1.96–5.02, P < .001 vs eICU-CRD: HR: 2.71, 95% CI: 1.52–4.84, P < .001). In the high ALBI score group, ICU mortality was 47% in MIMIC-IV and 26% in eICU-CRD (adjusted HR: 2.17, 95 % CI: 1.23–3.84, P = .008 vs HR: 2.22, 95% CI: 1.08–4.56, P = .029). 4. Discussion

This study shows that elevated ALBI scores are significantly associated with an increased risk of mortality in critically ill patients with AP. The ALBI score serves as an independent predictor of clinical outcomes in this patient population. This highlights its potential utility as a practical prognostic tool in the intensive care setting.

Earlier research indicates that AP patients admitted to the ICU experience ICU and hospital mortality rates of 16% and 20%, respectively.[23] The mortality rate was slightly higher than that observed in our study, likely due to the sample size limitations of the research. Early risk stratification is crucial for identifying patients in need of intensive care monitoring. Several guidelines recommend the use of established scoring systems to guide clinical decision-making.[24] Tools such as SOFA and SAPS II require the collection of 12 to 17 variables which can delay timely intervention. Shi et al conducted a retrospective study utilizing the MIMIC-III database and found that the ALBI score was more effective than SAPS II and SOFA in predicting in-hospital mortality among AP patients.[25] Our study builds upon this work by utilizing larger, multi-database cohorts (MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD) and adjusting for a broader set of covariates. A nomogram developed based on the ALBI score also exhibited strong predictive performance for survival outcomes. We also assessed the relationship between the ALBI score and ICU mortality, which produced concordant results. Yang X et al analyzed data from the MIMIC-IV database and found that elevated total bilirubin-to-albumin ratio (TBAR) levels were significantly associated with increased mortality in patients with AP at 7, 14, 21, 28, 90, and 365 days (P < .05).The high TBAR group exhibited lower survival rates at all time points (P < .05).[26] In this study, patients with ALBI levels (≥‐1.546) had higher mortality at 28 days, 60 days, 90 days, and 365 days intervals (P < .0001). Although the TBAR and ALBI scores are distinct variables, both are derived from albumin and bilirubin levels. It can be inferred from the scoring formula that lower albumin and higher bilirubin levels are strongly associated with more SAP.

The prognostic value of bilirubin and albumin in AP is well established. Cholecystitis and biliary tract stones are the primary causes of acute biliary pancreatitis.They lead to elevated serum total bilirubin levels, reflecting the extent of liver damage.[27] Logistic regression analysis determined that total bilirubin independently predicts poor outcomes in the bile reinfusion group of AP.[28] A separate cohort study identified a significant positive correlation between lower bilirubin levels and the severity of hyperlipidaemia-induced AP.[29] Biliary stones and cholecystitis can induce bile reflux into the biliary and pancreatic ducts, which directly elevates serum bilirubin and may trigger pancreatic inflammation. During this process, inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and TNF-α are released; these cytokines can damage hepatocytes and impair bilirubin metabolism and excretion. The consequent hyperbilirubinemia can aggravate oxidative stress and disrupt microcirculation, thereby contributing to progression toward organ dysfunction.[30–32] The ALBI score may be able to reflect the inflammatory response of the system. The study on the relationship between mortality rates of patients with acute kidney injury and acute sepsis also showed a positive correlation with the ALBI score.[15,16,33] In recent years, this scoring system has been particularly widely used in the assessment of liver function and liver diseases. Han L et al indicates that a significant correlations were observed between arterial iodine fraction, extracellular volume fraction, and iodine washout rate, with ALBI grades (r = .873, .908, and ‐.846, respectively; all P < .001) in patients with cirrhosis.[34] A study shows a lower ALBI score is associated with improved treatment efficacy and increased survival period in unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma with transarterial chemoembolization.[14]

Hypoproteinemia is relatively common in patients with AP. Albumin contributes to colloid osmotic pressure and exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects by binding and neutralizing inflammatory mediators (such as cytokines, endotoxins, and reactive oxygen species) released during SAP. A decline in albumin levels may precipitate an uncontrolled systemic inflammatory response and exacerbate capillary leakage, promoting disease progression. Additionally, the chemical structure of albumin is affected by certain specific processes (oxidation and glycosylation), which leads to its rapid clearance and catabolism. As inflammation intensifies, albumin consumption increases.[35,36] Thus, albumin levels correlate with disease severity and mortality.[37,38] Furthermore, previous studies that aimed to predict the prognosis of AP and acute myocardial infarction have also reached the same conclusion.[39–42]

A multivariate Cox analysis demonstrated that TBIL (HR = .768,) and albumin (HR = .962) were independent risk factors for in-hospital mortality. They also found that the low CumALB group had a higher proportion of biliary causes, suggesting that bile reflux may induce albumin depletion by triggering bile-acid-mediated Ca2+ signaling in pancreatic acinar cells, thereby amplifying both local and systemic inflammatory responses.[43,44] We infer that elevated bilirubin together with reduced serum albumin may synergistically worsen prognosis during the course of AP. Utilizing bilirubin or albumin levels alone to forecast AP prognosis has its limitations. Both indices are readily obtainable at low cost, and calculating the ALBI score provides clinicians with a simple and efficient means for rapid prognostication in AP. We further conducted subgroup analyses, which consistently demonstrated that both continuous and categorized ALBI scores were robustly associated with mortality in AP. The ALBI score consistently predicted both short- and long-term all-cause mortality, underscoring the robustness and applicability of our findings. In recent years, composite indices derived from these routine parameters have been developed to improve prognostic accuracy. This thereby strengthens the evidence for its reliability. The conclusion drawn from the ALBI scoring formula is also consistent with the clinical prognosis of AP. This score simultaneously reflects liver function, inflammatory status, and oxidative stress levels. It can quickly identify the prognosis of AP. This enables clinicians to take more proactive treatment strategies, such as early fluid resuscitation and enhanced nutritional support.

Some limitations are also present in this retrospective study. Despite employing multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression and stratified analyses, residual confounding from unmeasured or unknown variables may persist, a challenge inherent in observational studies. Second, this study employed a retrospective design with a limited sample size. Heterogeneity in geographic location, population demographics, and healthcare environments may have introduced bias. Third, approximately 35% eligible patients had missing bilirubin or albumin data, which may introduce selection bias. Therefore, larger multicenter prospective studies are necessary in the future to further validate the results of this study.

5. Conclusion

Evidence from multicenter database, this study identified a linear connection between the ALBI score and clinical outcomes in AP patients. An elevated ALBI score could correlate with a heightened risk of adverse clinical outcomes in critically ill AP patients.
