A High-Precision Geo-Spatial-Techno-Economic Framework for CO2 Source-Sink Pairing: Land-Sea Synergy and Cross-Regional Coalitions in China
Authors: Ning Wei, Keyao Lin, Shengnan Liu, Dalin Jiang, Wendong Wang, Zunsheng Jiao, Robert T. Dahowski, Xiaochun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
China suffers from a severe geospatial mismatch between industrial and energy CO2 emission sources and geological storage sinks, while existing carbon storage assessments lack national-scale high-resolution and land-sea synergy analysis. This study develops a high-resolution (1 km × 1 km) techno-economic framework integrating hierarchical storage capacity, site suitability, and full-chain carbon capture and storage (CCS) source-sink matching to clarify China’s geological carbon storage characteristics. The results show nearly balanced onshore-offshore theoretical (2.5 trillion tons) and P50 effective (1.5 trillion tons) storage capacities, with distinct cost gaps: onshore storage costs of 3–10 USD/t and offshore costs of 10–60 USD/t. Benefiting from regional capacity and transportation economic heterogeneity, 60–75% of China’s coal-based captured CO2 achieves a levelized cost below 90 USD/t. This fine-scale framework maps national CCS distribution heterogeneities across multiple scales. We further propose cross-regional CCS coalitions via infrastructure sharing, land-sea storage integration, and cross-region carbon corridors to mitigate imbalance and could manage about 80% of coal-sector emissions, providing a scalable solution for Global South countries with unbalanced carbon development.

Introduction

Carbon capture, utilization, and geological storage (CCUS) emerges as a linchpin for the decarbonization odyssey of fossil-intensive industries and energy sectors, including thermal power, cement, chemical, and steel production, in their pursuit of carbon neutrality. − At the heart of successful CCUS scaling is the meticulous evaluation of storage sites, a prerequisite for garnering stakeholder confidence, particularly for CO2 deep saline aquifer storage, which accounts for more than 90% of the total contribution. However, the intricate geological heterogeneity of these formations, coupled with diverse trapping mechanisms operating across disparate temporal and spatial scales, exacerbates the challenge. − Unlike oil and gas reservoirs, which benefit from decades of comprehensive site characterizations and operational data, deep saline aquifers suffer from a dearth of comparable data sets. This severely limits empirical insights and hinders stakeholders’ efforts to accurately quantify storage capacities, assess costs, bridge source-sink mismatches, and evaluate associated costs. ,

Although significant advancements in capacity evaluation have been made, inconsistencies and uncertainties persist, stemming from divergent technical assumptions, variable data quality, and fragmented assessments of basins. In China, the large-scale deployment of onshore carbon capture and storage (CCS) faces unique hurdles, primarily due to the pronounced spatial disparity between CO2 emission sources and viable geological storage reservoirs. − This geographical mismatch imposes very high cost of transport and storage, e.g., >60 USD/t CO2, and creates substantial regional cost gaps between northern/inland storage hubs and southeastern/eastern emission-intensive centers in the Southeastern Coastal and South China regions. Relying solely on onshore storage in the distant Three Norths (Northwest, North, and Northeast) regions renders mismatched projects economically unfeasible. ,, Although scholars have conducted disjointed evaluations of capacity and site suitability in selected onshore (e.g., Junggar, Songliao) and offshore basins (e.g., Pearl River Mouth, Bohai Basin, Beibuwan), inconsistencies in data quality (geological, geographical, and socio-economic) and methodologies impede their integration and comparison. −

Various qualitative and quantitative algorithms and methods are utilized for capacity assessment, site suitability evaluation and selection, techno-economic analysis, source-sink matching, and quantification of mitigation potential. Conventional source–sink matching primarily aligns the theoretical storage capacity with CO2 emission sources, while techno-economic evaluation mostly relies on empirical or oversimplified models. A critical limitation in existing studies lies in the poor integration of geological storage potential, techno-economic performance, source–sink configuration, and macro policy constraints. − Furthermore, the lack of high-resolution, standardized multidimensional assessment frameworks that couple capacity, site suitability, cost, and policytogether with ambiguous data quality specificationsprevents robust national-scale holistic evaluation. ,, This gap impedes coordinated CCS planning across sectors, regions, and land–offshore boundaries.

Site assessment for CO2 geological storage is commonly conducted at basin, sub-basin/zone, and individual site scales, depending on data type, accuracy, and methodological algorithms. ,− Storage capacity evaluation generally covers four hierarchical dimensions: theoretical, effective, practical, and matched capacity, with assessment approaches evolving from theoretical extrapolation to refined actuarial estimation for improved accuracy. , The site suitability assessment and theoretical capacity evaluation of offshore CO2 geological storage in China primarily focus on individual basins or theoretical capacity assessments at the basin scale, , without considering the synthetic effects of capacity, suitability, cost, and policy. These high-resolution assessments are data-driven by a high-quality database (data type and resolution). The tiered geological-economic assessment with various algorithms and data are shown in Figure . Notably, zone- and sub-basin-scale evaluations of site selection and tiered storage capacity are still limited, especially for China’s offshore regions.

These shortcomings are further exacerbated by China’s incomplete onshore–offshore geological data sets, basin-isolated and fragmented site assessment methodologies, generalized one-size-fits-all techno-economic models, assumption-dependent source–sink matching schemes, and emission inventories confined to individual sectors or limited regions. Collectively, these prevailing limitations constrain a systematic understanding of national CCS development potential and industrial deployment pathways, hinder integrated evaluation of storage capacity, economic feasibility, source–sink optimization and policy adaptability across multiscale administrative and geographic units, and may ultimately mislead stakeholder decision-making and strategic CCS layout.

This study establishes a nationwide 1 km × 1 km high-resolution evaluation framework that systematically integrates zone-level onshore–offshore storage suitability, hierarchical capacity quantification, techno-economic models of CCS projects, enterprise-level carbon emission accounting across multiple industries, and source–sink matching across the full CCS chain (capture, pipeline transportation, and geological storage) based on pipeline cost surfaces, and carbon reduction contribution forecasting, enabling comprehensive analysis at the kilometer scale and beyond. Figure illustrates the schematic of the zone-level framework. A database of CO2 sources, geological features, geographic characteristics, and related socioeconomic data in China and adjacent areas was established utilizing a wider range of high-precision data. Then, algorithms of CO2 source screening, site suitability assessment, hierarchical storage capacity, techno-economic assessment, source-sink pairing, and pipeline network generation algorithms were selected and integrated into a software named “Integrated Techno-Economic Assessment Model of Carbon Capture, Geological Utilization, and Storage with hub, ITEAM-CCS hub”. ,, The framework addresses inherent limitations of conventional basin-scale and sector-specific assessments by supporting national-scale cross-regional and cross-sectoral comparative analysis. It further quantifies spatial heterogeneities across basins, geographical regions, industrial sectors, and administrative regions, and delivers practical pathways for CCS collaboration, integrated land-sea management, and cross-regional carbon corridor planning to facilitate evidence-based policy formulation and large-scale CCS deployment. Additional details regarding the ITEAM-CCS model can be found in the papers by Wei, et al. , It unlocks the intricacies of China’s CCS landscape, providing a comprehensive understanding across sectors, regions, basins, and land-sea boundaries, heralding a new era in CCS planning and implementation.

Method and Data

High-precision evaluations of geological structures, techno-economic factors, and carbon-neutrality targets are crucial steps toward achieving large-scale deployment of carbon geological storage and achieving neutrality goals. However, there is currently a scarcity of publicly available assessment reports on site selection and storage capacity for CO2 geological storage at the sub-basin and zone scales. This is particularly true for zone-scale assessments and studies on onshore/offshore storage capacities in China. Through data compilation and algorithm development, we propose a high-resolution framework for site selection, hierarchical capacities, economic analysis, and source-sink pairing. This involves a multiple-stage assessment integrating technical scheme definition, suitability, algorithms, data compilation, and data-driven analysis at ∼1 × 1 km2 grid resolution (Shown in Figure ). This zone-scale methodology complements site-scale tools (e.g., Petrel, TOUGH2, MRST, et al.) by geospatially integrating storage capacity, site suitability, and economic viability via stratigraphic analysis, cost-surface modeling, and multiobjective optimization. Unlike traditional meter-to-hundred-meter-resolution single-reservoir models, our kilometer-gridded system enables national-scale CCS infrastructure planning by identifying strategic storage hubs and optimizing cross-basin source-sink pairings. This shift from isolated project analysis to systematic evaluation addresses critical scale-resolution mismatches in carbon management policies, providing both technical outputs (site suitability, capacity hierarchies, cost curves) and policy guidance for regional potential distribution and transitions.

Schematic figure of the zoning evaluation framework and analysis.

When evaluating the suitability of onshore and offshore storage sites, we use two independent criteria systems because of differences in technical schemes and data sets. Techno-economic analyses for onshore and offshore storage are also conducted independently. Additionally, all analyses are grounded in the current understanding of large-scale carbon storage technologies, database resources, and the analytical algorithm system. It should be noted that this assessment serves only as a reference for macro-assessment and preliminary feasibility studies and cannot be used as a basis for screening specific sites and project design.

Technical Schemes for Large-Scale CO2 Geological Storage

The performance evaluation of CO2 geological storage depends on multiple factors, including various CO2 trapping mechanisms and the spatial distribution of sedimentary facies, structures, lithologies, and related properties. Meanwhile, regional hydrodynamic conditions and the technical design of the CO2 storage system impact site performance. −

Among these factors, the technical scheme for the CO2 storage project can be meticulously designed to guarantee successful execution and performance. The key characteristics of large-scale storage include a near-mass-balance strategy, the use of multiple CO2 injection and geofluid production wells, and the use of numerous geological strata to maximize underground space and mitigate geological risk. These other technical features include well-field design, well structure, multiple-layer optimization, and risk mitigation strategies, all of which are crucial for the safe storage of CO2 and the maximization of underground space utilization. Subsequently, key site performance can be evaluated using hypothesized technical schemes and relevant algorithms for storage capacity, injectivity, site suitability, and economic evaluation.

Algorithms of Site Performance Evaluation

Based on previously established site assessment methods, site performance assessment can be conducted using GIS-based raster analysis or numerical simulation. The relevant GIS data are rasterized with a 1 km × 1 km spatial resolution. Subsequently, algorithms for site suitability, capacity, economic evaluation, and source-sink pairing are used to analyze and summarize the raster data set, yielding outcomes of “hierarchical capacities-costs-geospatial matching”.

Sites Suitability

Any potential storage site must meet numerous criteria to ensure the effectiveness, safety, and economic feasibility of storage operations. , The site suitability assessment of CO2 geological storage should consider multiple indices across three key aspects: (1) storage site performance (storage capacity, injection, and techno-economic performance); (2) storage safety or risk management; and (3) social-economic and other constraints (economic, legal, and environmental factors). A systematic classification of CO2 storage indices is proposed and presented in Supporting Information Tables S2 and S3. This classification integrates geological performance, safety, and socio-environmental constraints, enabling high-resolution site suitability assessments for onshore and offshore aquifer sites. We used multisource data assimilation to normalize and classify assessment indices for geological site suitability. Then, we generated individual and summary index layers using GIS spatial analysis algorithms to conduct a high-resolution site-suitability assessment. This process helps analyze key factors affecting geological storage suitability and their influence levels for scientific site selection.

Capacity for Storage Sites

The mathematical theories, evaluation procedures, and data requirements for the above-capacity algorithms differ substantially. The existing algorithms and tools for storage capacity estimation can be categorized into several major classes, including empirical methods, semianalytical methods, reduced-order methods, numerical simulations, and other methods. Most methodologies rely on the theoretical and geo-cellular volumes of storage reservoirs, integrating specific storage mechanisms over defined periods, spanning from the commencement of CO2 injection to its termination or the final state of the injected CO2. The precise and detailed comparisons of existing algorithms depend heavily on the quality of the data set (in terms of various types and resolution). − Based on data quality, the algorithm should be chosen from empirical or statistical algorithms, analytical or analytic methods, reduced-order numerical simulation, numerical simulation, general artificial intelligence, and hybrid algorithms. − More detailed information on capacity algorithms can be found in the algorithm realization section of the Supporting Information.

CO2 Emission Sources

Carbon emission source assessment involves conducting investigations and analyses of carbon-emitting enterprises, such as the coal-fired power, steel, cement, and chemical industries. It aims to understand: (1) basic information on the enterprise, including its location, products, output, and processes; (2) energy consumption of coal, oil, and gas, water, electricity, etc.; (3) carbon emission situations, such as carbon emission intensity, main emission links and concentrations, and emission amounts. A carbon-emission source database is established, and enterprise screening indicators are developed to assess whether carbon-emitting enterprises are suitable for implementing CCS projects. Different regions and industries need to develop screening criteria for their CO2 sources. More detailed information about P10 and P90 results can be found in the capacity algorithms in the Supporting Information.

Techno-Economic Assessment of Full-Chain CCS Project

The costs of CO2 storage operations are critically contingent upon an integrated set of strategies. , Techno-economic models, which incorporate algorithms, are composed of two main components: technical and economic models. Numerous economic models of onshore and offshore CO2 geological storage have been developed globally. − An algorithm considering a broader range of technical schemes and economic parameters can yield cost estimates with higher resolution and reduced uncertainty. This study employs a technical-economic module at the prefeasibility scale for assessment. The economic viability of the storage site depends on the site performance assessment and the engineering implementation process. This encompasses factors such as capacity and injectivity, the type of offshore platform, the well pattern and layout, injection strategies, and cost parameters. In terms of technical details, economic models can be categorized into empirical, statistical, budgetary, and accounting models. More detailed parameters and assessments can be found in the techno-economic models in Supporting Information (key parameters shown in Tables S5–S10). All costs and revenues are escalated to 2022 USD.

Source-Sink Pairing Assessment

Source-sink pairing algorithms aim to pair CO2 emission sources (e.g., power plants, industrial facilities) with suitable geological sinks (e.g., depleted oil reservoirs, saline aquifers, chemical utilization) while minimizing costs and risks 62, 63 The source-sink pairing model is developed based on different models of integrated CCS projects. The model includes point-to-point source-sink pairing (P2P) and multiple-source-to-multiple-sink pairing (M2M) scenarios. Pipeline optimization algorithms were employed to identify suitable routes that considered technical features, socioeconomic factors, environmental impact, and other relevant factors. The costs of each source-sink pairing or of integrated CO2 sources and sinks within the search radius can be assessed using reservoir properties, regional coefficients, technical and economic parameters, and the techno-economic model. In this assessment, all modeled CO2 sources are assumed to promptly initiate CCS retrofitting and commence operations at their assumed capacity levels. This assumption focuses on defining the key attributes of matched CCS projects to understand the commercialization potential of CCS technology across China. More detailed information about the full description and parameter settings can be found in the Supporting Information.

Data Collection and Integration

The data supporting the evaluation method includes various data types and levels of precision, as well as data transition or assimilation methods, which jointly support the operation of the evaluation model.

Data Set

Geological surveys generate data sets taxonomically divided into subsurface and surface domains, each characterized by distinct spatial–temporal resolutions and scientific utilities. , Subsurface data sets exhibit hierarchical organization across nested scales: (1) basin-scale frameworks integrate stratigraphic architecture, sedimentary facies, litho-physical property distributions, and geodetic observations to construct reservoir-caprock system models; (2) regional-scale attributes synthesize reservoir-seal integrity, boundary system typology (open/semiopen/closed), fault activity regimes, and facies continuity to constrain basin-scale fluid migration; (3) site-scale data sets combine petrophysical measurements, borehole lithology, 2D/3D seismic data, and high-permeability conduit delineation (sparse in oil fields and CO2 storage projects).

Surface-derived data sets, critical for anthropogenic-environmental-social-economic analyses, include: (1) surface geological archives (outcrop lithology, stratigraphic contouring, structural morphology); (2) geospatial-geomorphic data sets (digital elevation models, river and lake system, landform classifications, climatic parameters like precipitation/insolation/wind regimes, and vegetation indices via satellite remote sensing); (3) Socio-environmental metrics (CO2 emission hotspots, transportation networks, urban clusters, industrial zones, protected areas, and economic indicators), enabling sustainability-focused spatial planning.

Data sources include institutional repositories (Chinese Academy of Sciences, Ministry of Natural Resources, National Basic Information Center), requiring continuous updating to support zone- and site-scale research. Subsurface geological/resource data, as well as surface-derived data sets (both geological and nongeological), are detailed in the Supporting Information Table S13. Techno-economic parameters incorporate current petroleum industry and CO2 storage project metrics, spanning site exploration, drilling, oil and gas fields, and pipeline operations. This hierarchical data set architecture, underpinned by rigorous scale-transition methods, forms a foundational toolkit for addressing complex challenges in the Earth system, particularly in energy, environmental, and engineering geosciences.

The data implementation framework establishes hierarchical fusion strategies spanning core-scale to regional-scale data sets, with subsurface data resolutions and types detailed in Table . Hierarchical Types and Resolutions of Zone-Scale Geological Data

The high-resolution (1 km × 1 km) spatial analysis presented in this study is based on geological source data with a much coarser resolution (well spacing at 20–50 km). While we employed a data interpolation tool and other downscaling approaches to generate the high-resolution grid, it is crucial to acknowledge the inherent uncertainties in this process. The fidelity of the resulting model is fundamentally constrained by the quality and density of the underlying input data. Although the downscaling method enhances visualization precision and introduces spatial variability consistent with the source data, it cannot resolve subgrid geological heterogeneities (e.g., small-scale faults, channels, or permeability barriers) that are not captured by the sparse well data. Therefore, the model represents the best possible interpretation given the available data, and the results should be interpreted with the understanding that the resolution of fine-scale features is limited by the original data density. These methods are suitable for evaluating the general technical economy and suitability of sites at the zone scale, but a more refined assessment of site-specific heterogeneities would require denser, higher-quality input data, such as 3D seismic surveys or additional well logs.

Evaluation Framework

Building on the multidimensional data set, a four-pillar evaluation system is constructed, forming a zone-scale framework within the Resources-Engineering-Economics-Policy Integrated Assessment Framework for CCS. This platform visualizes zone-level suitability indices, hierarchical capacities, and levelized costs, enabling stakeholders to screen storage sites and design CCS projects under different policy scenarios. This tool also addresses a critical need for actionable, spatially explicit analytics in global road-mapping for carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCS).

Basic Geological Setting

China’s sedimentary basins exhibit distinct structural and stratigraphic characteristics across terrestrial and marine realms, shaped by diverse tectonic histories and depositional environments. The large sedimentary basins in China and its adjacent regions are shown in Figure .

China’s basinsextensional in the east, compressional in the west, and rifted-marine offshoreoffer diverse carbon storage potentials. Onshore basins vary in reservoir properties and caprock stability. In contrast, offshore basins feature thick, continuous sealing strata, which are critical for optimizing onshore-offshore sequestration strategies to meet decarbonization goals efficiently.

Eastern onshore basins, such as the Songliao and Bohai Bay basins, are fault-depression/extensional types formed under Mesozoic–Cenozoic tension, characterized by thick sequences of Mesozoic and Cenozoic clastics. Reservoirs exhibit moderate porosity and permeability due to diagenesis, posing challenges for large-scale carbon storage, despite the presence of favorable structural traps for hydrocarbons. Central basins (Ordos, Sichuan), governed by compressional-shear forces, host Paleozoic–Mesozoic sediments, including low-porosity and low-permeability (5–15% and 0.1–10 mD) sandstones. While challenging for conventional oil and gas recovery, their stable cratonic settings with sealed strata benefit storage safety. Western basins (Tarim and Junggar), formed by compressional tectonics, span Paleozoic to Cenozoic deposition and contain up to 10,000 m of sedimentary rocks. Target strata with suitable sandstones (12–20% porosity) that benefit from robust structural traps and regional seals, making them promising for CO2 sequestration. Offshore basins (East China Sea Shelf, Bohai, Pear River Mouth Basins et al.) formed during the Late Mesozoic–Cenozoic, via early continental rifting and late marine depression. Neogene-Quaternary marine sediments exceed 8000 m in thickness, creating clastic-carbonate reservoir-caprock associations (e.g., high-porosity continental sandstones sealed by marine muds) to favor carbon storage.

The site assessment methodology and fundamental data were utilized to conduct the site suitability assessment for CO2 storage in saline aquifers. Figure shows the assessment results of site suitability for CO2 saline aquifer storage in the sedimentary basins of China and adjacent areas. Generally, saline aquifers in sedimentary basins in China and neighboring regions exhibit relatively high site suitability. The assessment outcomes reveal numerous suitable sites for saline aquifer storage. Among the offshore basins, the Bohai Basin, the Yellow Sea Basin, the East China Shelf Basin, the Pearl River Mouth Basin, and the Yinggehai Basin stand out for their relatively high site suitability for CO2 storage. These basins boast abundant exploration data, large storage capacity density, good injectivity, high safety, and low risk. Storage sites with higher suitability can be strategically combined with emissions sources from coastal cities to initiate demonstration projects, thereby laying the foundation for technological advancement of offshore CO2 storage in saline aquifers.

Storage Cost

Figure illustrates the average levelized cost of CO2 geological storage in China and its adjacent regions. Basins such as the Bohai Bay Basin, East China Sea Basin, Pearl River Mouth Basin, Yinggehai Basin, Zengmu Basin, and some basins in the South China Sea exhibit favorable injectivity. This is attributed to the thick Cenozoic sedimentary rocks and their good physical properties in these regions. The risk and cost associated with saline aquifer storage are low in areas with good injectivity. The costs of most offshore storage are generally below 60 USD/t. Offshore sites range from 10 to 60 USD/t. In contrast, onshore storage ranges from 3 to 10 USD/t, driven by factors such as offshore platforms, related offshore infrastructure, heterogeneous geological features, capacity intensity, and injectivity. Large-scale CO2 injections can lead to high-pressure build-up, limiting storage capacity and potentially increasing long-term risks. Therefore, during site performance evaluation, it is essential to establish a comprehensive injection or storage strategy that considers the layout of the well field, the number of wells to be drilled, and the design of pressure-control (water-recovery) wells.

CCS Cost Curves and Various Heterogeneities

Accurate assessment of source-sink pairing and proximity for CCS in key regions is critical to accelerate large-scale deployment of CCS projects and hubs. Analysis shows that >99% of CO2 sources (coal power, steel, cement, and coal chemical sectors) in China can be paired with suitable onshore or offshore storage within 800 km, though mitigation costs exhibit strong distance and spatial dependency. Cost curves for CCS mitigation potential (Figure ) reveal that at 85% capture rate, achievable abatement rises from 4.2 Gt/a (150 km pairing) to 6.0 Gt/a (800 km) with full-chain levelized costs <90 USD/t, covering 59–79% of annual CO2 emissions captured from target industries. Slight upticks in avoidance and levelized cost curves reflect CCS project-induced emissions and reliance on coal-fired energy. The plateaus in levelized costs and carbon abatement cost curves stem from transitions between onshore/offshore storage systems and sedimentary basin heterogeneities. Onshore reservoirs offer lower infrastructure and operational costs, making them economically preferable for an increasing number of sources as pairing distances expand, thus shortening the cost plateau. Beyond critical thresholds (e.g., 300 km), distance-dependent transport logistics dominate cost gradients. This is driven by high single-pipeline costs that reduce onshore viability, particularly in high-GDP regions with steep land acquisition costs, thereby incentivizing phased offshore integration.

Cost curves of CCS mitigation potential in China for various pairing distances.

Regional heterogeneity in costs arises from distributions of storage costs (Figure ) and gaps in transport infrastructure, including urban, hydrological, railway, and conservation area impacts. This necessitates backbone pipeline and cluster strategies for economic deployment. Globally, China’s CCS cost curves are low: full-process costs for >69% of CO2 capture lie in 30–60 USD/t, and >79% (about 4/5th) of cases cost <90 USD/t. However, a higher fraction of projects exceeds 60 USD/t than in other nations, ,, attributed to source-sink imbalances necessitating long-distance transportation and offshore storage. Future policies must prioritize long-distance transport and cluster development to align with China’s unique CCS landscape.

Carbon abatement cost curves for various matched basins with source-sink pairing conditions in China (300 km).

The site suitability levels, storage capacities, levelized costs, CCS project costs, and regional characteristics presented in this analysis were compared with the prefeasibility study results of multiple large-scale CCS demonstration projects in China, including the Ordos coal-to-liquids, Yulin coal chemical, and CNOOC Enping projects, as well as the Bohai Bay project. Under the P50 probability level, the site suitability, capacity, and costs are generally consistent with the actual full-process project costs.

Cost Curve Characteristics: Administrative and Basin Perspectives

When categorized by storage sites (Figure ), cost curves are shaped by site-specific attributes, proximity to emission sources, and source-sink dynamics. Bohai Bay, Ordos, Subei, Songliao, Junggar, Jianghan-Dongting, and East China Sea Shelf Basins showcase CCS curves with substantial emission reductions and gradual cost transitions. Marine basins incur significantly higher abatement costs than their continental counterparts due to multistep profiles, which arise from heterogeneous storage capacities across intrabasin zones, leading to tiered transportation and storage expenses.

Regional and Sectoral Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity in geological storage capacity and matching costs for CCS emerges across basins, regions, industries, and administrative boundaries, shaped by physical attributes, economic landscapes, and policy frameworks. Basin-wise, continental basins (e.g., Bohai Bay, Ordos) exhibit gradual cost transitions with high emission reduction potential. In contrast, marine basins (e.g., East China Sea Shelf) incur stepwise higher costs due to intrabasin storage heterogeneity, which drives tiered transport and storage expenses. Regionally, low-GDP coal-rich zones (Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia) offer cost-competitive onshore sequestration via favorable subsurface properties and low land/energy costs. At the same time, high-GDP coastals leverage marine basins to halve abatement costs through proximal source-sink pairing, despite elevated offshore infrastructure investments. Sectoral disparities stem from CO2 stream characteristics: coal chemical industries benefit from high-concentration streams, reducing capture costs; coal-fired power/steel plants adopt pithead models for moderate costs with proximal storage; cement sectors face the highest costs due to fragmented scales and geographic isolation. Administratively, cost curves vary from smooth transitions in provinces with centralized basin storage (Shandong in Bohai Bay) to stepwise profiles in multibasin regions (Inner Mongolia), reflecting source-sink pairing efficiencies and intrabasin storage heterogeneities. Such heterogeneities necessitate spatially tailored policiescross-regional carbon corridors and sector-industry integrationto align CCS deployment with resource endowments and optimize decarbonization costs.

Regional CCS coalitions classified by source-sink pairing form independent CO2 reduction hubs, exhibiting marked cost and emission reduction scale heterogeneities. Twelve coalitions are proposed based on sequestration priority and resource endowment, with Junggar-Tuha, Tarim, Qaidam, Ordos, Songliao, Jianghan-Dongting, Erlian, Subei-onshore/South Yellow Sea-offshore, Bohai Bay-onshore/Bohai-offshore, East China Sea Shelf, Pearl River Mouth, and Beibuwan identified as prime CCS cluster candidates. Distinct zone- and site-level storage evaluations inform optimal site selection and cross-sector/regional collaboration modules, with tailored strategies: coastal regions like Subei prioritize integrated land-sea storage due to limited onshore capacity. Large-scale, low-cost “cross-regional carbon corridors” could facilitate CCS deployment and industrial relocation to lagging regions.

Cross-industry and regions collaboration accelerates CCS scaling, but heterogeneities necessitate phased strategies: short-term enterprise alliances (e.g., China Energy, Huaneng Group, China Offshore Oil Company et al.) for pipeline sharing; midterm provincial-level carbon mitigation agreements; long-term statutory “carbon corridors” integrated into cross-regions and national spatial planning. This roadmap transitions from enterprise-led infrastructure sharing to policy-governed coordination to address scaling challenges. Competition from a Global Perspective

The product offers economic viability, with lower overall costs and greater competitiveness compared to counterparts in the U.S., Central Asia, and Europe. This competitiveness stems from synergies in infrastructure deployment, economies of scale, and integrated industrial networks. Concurrently, it highlights the resilience of China’s traditional coal-based industries under the carbon neutrality agenda, illustrating how these industries can leverage carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies to advance decarbonization while mitigating stranded asset risks, ensuring a smooth low-carbon transition, and complying with regulatory requirements. The country’s unique position in balancing low-cost CCS implementation with industrial transition highlights its potential to shape global carbon management strategies.

Limitations and Future Work

This study presents a novel zone-scale methodological framework at 1 km grid resolution, complementing existing site-scale tools. By integrating capacity-suitability-economic triads through geospatial analytics, it surpasses conventional single-reservoir models, enabling national CCS infrastructure planning with strategic hub identification, such as the Ordos Basin’s $8 USD/t geological storage cost. This systemic approach bridges scale and resolution gaps in carbon management, shifting CCS deployment from isolated projects to integrated networks.

However, limitations exist in both methodology and assessment. Potential double-counting of coal-based power emissions across thermal and captive power plants may overestimate China’s CO2 emissions in four sectors, requiring careful consideration. Uncertainty analysis of the capacity-suitability-economics-cost curve is constrained by limited parameter uncertainty ranges in existing geological data sets and by high computational costs for multidimensional analysis. Current results primarily rely on P50 values, with only capacity providing P10–P90 ranges, as the public focuses on P50-level triad outcomes.

This paper primarily focuses on saline aquifer storage. Even if EOR generates profits, its trading price still needs to be set at the standard price (mainly derived from saline aquifer storage with the most supply). Meanwhile, this paper conducts assessments targeting large-scale saline aquifer storage, thus excluding oil-field-related content.

All quantitative source-sink matching results rely on P50 median estimates; actual outcomes may be affected by cost uncertainties, primarily stemming from transportation and reservoir properties 25. The findings serve only for strategic planning and policy support rather than for final project design, and future work will enhance robustness through a comprehensive P10–P90 full-range uncertainty analysis.

Additionally, even with mitigated CCS operational emissions, coal-based industries and power systems cannot achieve 100% net CO2 mitigation solely through CCS. This necessitates integration with complementary technologies like biomass energy, direct air capture with geological storage, and low-carbon power sources. Future improvements will involve a comprehensive uncertainty analysis across P10–P90 ranges for capacity, suitability, and economic contribution. Additionally, refining technical parameters, assessment indices, and weights will be pursued as engineering experience, data quality, and techno-economic understanding advance, potentially significantly enhancing the accuracy of results.

The framework integrates hierarchical capacity assessment, multicriteria site screening, techno-economic modeling, and source-sink pairing algorithms to optimize the deployment of onshore and offshore storage.
