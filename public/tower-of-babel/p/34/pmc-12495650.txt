The impact of systemic inflammatory markers on EGFR-mutant non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Supagone Wangsubtawee, Thanaporn Thamrongjirapat, Narumol Trachu, Pimpin Incharoen, Natini Jinawath, Passaworn Cheyasawan, Nanamon Monnamo, Dittapol Munthum, Nattanan Reungwetwattana, Salin Amponnavarat, Pimtip Sanvarinda, Arthit Chairoungdua, Montien Ngodngamtaweesuk, Khantong Khiewngam, Ekaphop Sirachainan, Phichai Chansriwong, Thitiya Dejthevaporn, Thanyanan Reungwetwattana, Putthapoom Lumjiaktase
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh prevalence of EGFRm lung cancer was found in the Asian population. Preclinical data suggest that inflammatory cytokines activated by PM2.5 affected EGFRm clone expansion. Here, we explored the correlation between inflammatory markers and EGFRm NSCLC.MethodsResected NSCLC patients (2016–2023) were enrolled. Tumor tissues and blood serum were retrieved from Ramathibodi tumor biobank. EGFR 19del and L858R mutations were performed by rt-PCR in cancerous tissue and dPCR in normal tissue in the same patient. NF-Kb and STAT3 protein signaling were measured by ELISA in both cancerous and normal tissue. Cytokines (IL-1ß, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12 and TNF-α) were explored in serum by flow cytometry.ResultsAmong 140 patients, EGFRm prevalence was 58% in cancerous tissue but only 5% in normal tissue. NF-kB and STAT3 were statistically higher in cancerous tissue than normal tissue [NF-kB median O.D.=0.82 (IQR; 0.07–2.82) vs. 0.32 (IQR; 0.05–2.48), P < 0.001; STAT3 median O.D.=0.32 (IQR; 0.10–1.58) vs. 0.17 (IQR; 0.06–1.29, P < 0.001]. STAT3 was significantly increased in EGFRm compared to EGFRwt [median O.D.=0.36 (IQR; 0.234–0.592) vs. 0.23 (IQR; 0.158–0.409), OR = 11.09 (95% CI; 2.17–56.58), P = 0.004]. TNF-α, IL-10, and STAT3 in cancer cells were higher in EGFRm than EGFRwt (P = 0.003, 0.008, and < 0.001, respectively). None of cytokines was statistically different between EGFRm and EGFRwt patients. However, only STAT3 in cancer cells and non-smoker were associated with EGFRm NSCLC in multivariable analysis.ConclusionInflammation could be one of the pathogenesis of both NSCLC and EGFRm lung cancer as we demonstrated in our pilot study. STAT3 is a potentially inflammatory-predictive biomarkers. Larger cohort is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14915-1.Keywords: Lung cancer, EGFR, Cytokines, STAT3, Inflammatory marker

Introduction

Adenocarcinoma is the most common subtype in NSCLC which is now known to have oncogenic driven mutations especially epidermal growth factor receptor mutation (EGFRm) [1–3]. However, we cannot explain the reason of higher prevalence of EGFRm NSCLC in Asian patients compared to Caucasian patients, as well as between smokers and non-smokers [4–9].

Particulate matter with diameter of less than 2.5 microns (PM2.5) is the major air pollution problem in Asia. In Thailand, PM2.5 has an average concentration of 22 mcg/m3, which exceeds standard recommendation from the WHO [10, 11]. A meta-analysis of European cohorts showed a trend toward an increased risk of lung cancer, reaching statistical significance for the adenocarcinoma subtype (HR 1.55; 95% CI, 1.05–2.29) with increasing exposure to PM2.5 [12]. Furthermore, multi-countries study demonstrated an association between increasing exposure to PM2.5 and the incidence of EGFRm NSCLC [13]. Several pre-clinical studies reported of PM2.5 inhalation in mice led to increased clonal expansion of EGFRm and inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β [13, 14]. In addition, an in-vitro study demonstrated that PM2.5 can induce human alveolar epithelial cells to increase EGF receptor protein levels, NF-kB, and IL-8 [15, 16]. These are shown as the dynamic interactions within the tumor microenvironment that facilitate tumor progression in lung cancer [17]. The complex and diverse interplay among these components contributes to intratumoral heterogeneity and may underlie mechanisms of acquired resistance to targeted therapies [18]. The differences in inflammatory cytokine levels observed in this study.

In Thailand, many reports indicate a prevalence of EGFRm NSCLC was approximately 50-60% [19–22]. In Ramathibodi cohort, comprising 441 early-stage resected NSCLC patients, the prevalence of EGFRm was found to be approximately 58%, with a predominance of non-smoker patients [23]. Taking all of this into account, given high local incidence of EGFRm NSCLC and high exposure rate of PM2.5, we hypothesize that PM2.5 is associated with EGFRm NSCLC by inducing pre-existing EGFRm clones expansion via inflammation pathways. This retrospective study aims to explore the pathogenesis and the correlation between inflammatory markers (cytokines, protein signaling) and EGFRm NSCLC.

Methods

Patients and study design

The Datas were collected from electronic medical records (EMRs) of resected NSCLC patients at Ramathibodi Hospital from January 2016 to December 2023. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18, diagnosed of adenocarcinoma of the lung, availability of fresh frozen tumor tissue and blood serum which was collected before surgery. Patients with other cell types such as squamous cell carcinoma and adenosquamous, were excluded from this study. Demographic information such as age, sex, ECOG performance status, history of smoking, AJCC 8th edition staging, and pathological subtype were collected from EMRs. We prepared each fresh frozen tumor tissue for protein signaling testing and EGFRm testing in both the cancerous and normal regions. Blood serum was tested for inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 1). This study, alongside all protocols, received comprehensive approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee of Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand (IRB number COA. MURA2023/928) and was conducted in alignment with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study flow chart

Detection of protein signaling

Fifty milligrams of five-micrometer-thick sections of frozen lung tissue from both cancerous and normal regions were homogenized using a syringe with an 18-gauge needle in lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The homogenate was then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min to obtain tissue lysates. The total protein concentration of tissue lysates was measured by the Bradford protein assay. Tissue lysates were used at 50 µg/mL and 400 µg/mL total protein to detect total NF-kB and total STAT3 levels, respectively. InstantOne ELISA assay kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were used to measure the levels of NF-kB (Cat. No. 85-86081-11), and STAT3 (Cat. No. 85-86101-11) according to the manufacturer.

Measurement of inflammatory cytokines

Human inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12, and TNF-α were measured in patient serum using a Human Inflammatory Cytokines Cytometric Bead Array (CBA) kit (Cat. No. 551811, BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer. The BD FACSLyric™ flow cytometer (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA, USA) was used to determine individual cytokine levels. The data were analyzed with BD FACSuite™ and BD™ Cytometric Bead Array Software (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA, USA).

EGFR testing

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections, each with a thickness of 10 μm, underwent deparaffinization. Genomic DNA was extracted from the FFPE tissue by the High Pure FFPET DNA Isolation Kit (Roche Molecular Systems, Inc.). EGFR del19 and L858R mutations in cancerous regions were analyzed using the Super-ARMS® EGFR Mutations Detection Kit (Amoy Diagnostics, Xiamen, China) in combination with the SLAN-96 S real-time PCR system (Shanghai Hongshi Medical Technology Co., Ltd, China), both of which are approved by the National Medical Products Administration for clinical use in mainland China. Results were interpreted as either positive or negative based on the manufacturer’s instructions. For testing EGFR mutation in normal regions from frozen tissue blocks underwent genomic DNA (gDNA) extraction utilizing the Dneasy Blood & Tissue (Qiagen, Valencia, CA). The gDNA was then analyzed using primer/probes to detect EGFR del19 and L858R mutations on the digital polymerase chain reaction (dPCR) QIAcuity platform from QIAGEN. The QIAcuity Software Suite was employed to analyze mutation frequencies in the target samples, applying a 0.1% cutoff for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were reported as descriptive variable. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test, the chi-square test and Kruskal-wallis test. The prevalence of EGFR mutation was summarized as percentages. To compare difference of inflammatory cytokines and protein signaling, We reported as median and IQR, and reported graph as scatter plot. Association between inflammatory cytokines, protein signaling, and EGFR status was evaluated using univariate and multivariate analyses, and tested through logistic regression with a significant level of < 0.05. Data were analyzed using Stata version 18 and graphs were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 5.

Baseline clinicopathological characteristics

EGFRm epidermal growth factor receptor mutation, EGFRwt epidermal growth factor receptor wild-type, COPD Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, PL1 visceral pleura, PL2 parietal pleura, PD-L1 programme death ligand1

Resected adenocarcinoma of lung in Ramathibodi Hospital during January 2016 to December 2023 were include in total of 140 patients. Fresh frozen from resected tissue and blood serum of each patient were stored in the Ramathibodi tumor biobank. The cohort was predominantly female (65%), with high proportion of non-smokers (74%), favorable permomance status (ECOG 0–1 in 89%) and underwent lobectomy (91%). The average patient age was 65. In terms of tumor features, early-stage disease (stage I) was most common (64%), with nodal involvement seen in 15% of cases. Surgical margin were negative in 93% of patients. Lymphovascular invasion was not present in 57% and 61% showed no pleural invasion. Histologically, the acinar subtype was most frequent (54%), followed by the lepidic pattern (19%). Less than 10% of tumors showed micropapillary, papillary, or solid features. Tumor grade distribution included 15% well-differentiated, 36% moderately differentiated, and 5% poorly differentiated tumors. Out of 82 patients tested for PD-L1 IHC, 24 were PD-L1 positive.

The prevalence of EGFR mutation was 58% with 30% of del19 and 28% of L858R mutation. Baseline characteristics between the EGFRm and EGFR wild-type (EGFRwt) were similar, except for smoking status, predominant histology, and tumor grading. In the EGFRm group, significantly more patients were non-smoking, had acinar histology, and had no poorly differentiated grading.

Protein signaling

A total of one hundred and forty resected tumor tissues underwent protein signaling testing. In the cancerous regions, the NF-kB protein level was significantly higher compared to the normal regions (median O.D. 0.823 (IQR 0.339, 1.278) vs. 0.321 (IQR 0.167, 0.553), p < 0.0001). Similarly, the STAT3 protein level in the cancerous region was also higher compared to the normal region (median O.D. 0.316 (IQR 0.201, 0.556) vs. 0.174 (IQR 0.125, 0.316), p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2). Furthermore, both NF-kB and STAT3 were significantly increased in the cancerous region across all stages, tumor grades, and PD-L1 status (Supplement Table 1).

Protein signaling in cancerous and non-cancerous regions

Regarding EGFRm status, the NF-kB protein displayed a statistically significant increase in the cancerous region compared to the normal region, irrespective of whether it was EGFRm or EGFRwt [median O.D. of cancerous region in EGFRm 0.91 (IQR 0.514, 1.406) vs. normal region in EGFRm 0.33 (IQR 0.179, 0.654), p < 0.0001, and median O.D. of cancerous region in EGFRwt 0.79 (IQR 0.294, 1.135) vs. normal region in EGFRwt 0.287 (IQR 0.16, 0.474), p < 0.0001]. Likewise, STAT3 protein levels exhibited a significant increase in the cancerous region compared to the normal region, with median levels of 0.359 (IQR 0.234, 0.592) and 0.186 (IQR 0.129, 0.335) for cancerous region and normal region in EGFRm, respectively (p < 0.0001), and median levels of 0.23 (IQR 0.158, 0.409) and 0.159 (IQR 0.12, 0.27) for cancerous region and normal region in EGFRwt, respectively (p = 0.0002) (Fig. 3).

Protein signaling according to EGFR status

However, when comparing EGFRm to EGFRwt, only the STAT3 protein in the cancerous region showed a statistically significant increase in EGFRm compared to EGFRwt, with median O.D. of 0.359 (0.234, 0.592) vs. 0.23 (0.158, 0.409), respectively (p = 0.0007), while the NF-kB protein demonstrated a numerically higher level in the cancerous part of EGFRm compare to the cancerous part in EGFRwt (median O.D. 0.91 (0.514, 1.406) vs. 0.79 (0.24, 1.135) p = 0.068) (Fig. 3).

Inflammatory cytokines

We analyzed inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12, and TNF-α in 140 blood serum samples. Inflammatory cytokines demonstrated a trend of increase in EGFRm patients compare to EGFRwt patients in IL-6 (8.06 pg/ml vs. 5.41 pg/ml, p 0.3778), IL-8 (10.26 pg/ml vs. 9.56 pg/ml, p = 0.6592), IL-10 (2.63 pg/ml vs. 1.94 pg/ml, p = 0.0806), and TNF- α (1.26 pg/ml vs. 1.19 pg/ml, p = 0.3695), though none reached statistical significance (Supplement Table 2). However, we found a statistically significant increase in TNF-α levels in the del19 subgroup compared to EGFRwt patients (1.375 pg/ml vs. 1.19 pg/ml, p = 0.0033), whereas IL-10 levels were significantly elevated in L858R EGFRm patients compared to EGFRwt patients (3.3 pg/ml vs. 1.94 pg/ml, p = 0.0082) (Supplement Table 3).

Univariate and multivariate analysis

Univariate analysis revealed that being a non-smoker and an increase in STAT3 protein in cancerous tissue were risk factors for EGFRm NSCLC, with odds ratio of 2.99 (95%CI: 1.33–66.66) and 11.09 (95%CI: 2.17–56.58), respectively. Controversely, PD-L1 positivity was associated with a decreased risk of EGFRm NSCLC, with an odds ratio of 0.38 (95%CI: 0.14–0.99) (Table 2). Due to lack of a standard cut-off level for STAT3 protein, we selected a cut-off point of ≥ 0.354 O.D. to determine the risk of EGFRm NSCLC, achieving 52% sensitivity and 71% specificity. Multivariate analysis identified only STAT3 protein in cancerous tissue, using the 0.354 O.D. cut-off, and non-smoking status as significant risk factors for EGFRm NSCLC, with odds ratio 2.518 (95%CI: 1.15–5.49, p = 0.02), and 2.671 (95%CI: 1.17–6.08, p = 0.019), respectively (Table 3).

EGFRm in normal region of NSCLC patients

EGFR mutation testing was performed on the normal regions of resected tumor tissue. Due to the minimal presence of mutation clones in normal regions, we decided to use dPCR for EGFRm testing to increase sensitivity. The prevalence of EGFRm in normal regions was 5% among EGFRm patients. The type of mutation detected was del19, which was similar to the detection in cancerous regions. However, only 2 from 59 (3%) EGFRwt patients were found to have EGFRm in non-cancerous regions: one was detected with del19 and the other with L858R. (Supplement Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the association between inflammatory cytokines and protein signaling associated with adenocarcinoma of the lung, together with EGFRm status. Our results demonstrated a significant increase in STAT3 and NF-kB protein in cancer cells compared to normal cells in all patients regardless of EGFRm status. Regarding EGFRm patients, we also found a significant elevation of STAT3 protein compared to EGFRwt population while other inflammatory cytokines did not reach statistical significance. The prevalence of EGFRm in our population was 58%, and the baseline characteristics were predominantly with female, non-smoker, and acinar histology, which similar as previous reports [23–25]. In this study, we also tested for EGFRm status in normal cells in resected tumors, and interestingly, we found EGFRm rate of only 5% and 3% in EGFRm patients and EGFRwt patients, respectively. Tang et al., reported 43% (9 of 21) of EGFRm in normal respiratory epithelium with EGFRm adenocarcinoma, and none of EGFRm was detected in EGFRwt tumor [26]. In a Japanese study from Ueno et al., it was reported of 8% (1 of 12) of EGFRm were found in CT-guided needle biopsies from lung tumors initially diagnosed as non-malignant based on pathological reports [27]. These two studies concluded that EGFRm might be indicative of early detection of NSCLC. However, our study showed a similar EGFRm rate in normal regions from resected tumors in both EGFRm patients and EGFRwt patients. Due to the lack of a standard technique or cut point for detecting EGFRm in normal tissue, the prevalence of EGFRm varies between studies. Further research to calibrate testing methods and establish appropriate cut points is necessary to validate the concept and facilitate clinical application.

Signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) 3 is a transcription factor in the Janus kinase (JAK)/STAT pathway, playing a role in cell proliferation and apoptosis. This pathway can be activated by receptor tyrosine kinases such as epidermal growth factor and cytokine receptor such as IL-6 [28, 29]. Reports indicated STAT3 activation approximately 22–65% of NSCLC [30, 31]. Jiang et al. studied correlation of STAT3 protein in lung carcinoma and normal lung, demonstrating a higher rate of phosphorylation STAT3 (p-STAT3) expression in lung cancer compared with normal lung (64.6% vs. 37% p 0.001). Furthermore, they found that p-STAT3 significantly correlated with female gender (p 0.004), non-smoker status (p 0.006), and EGFRm NSCLC (p 0.003) [32]. Our results also found similar baseline characteristics of the patients and significant increase STAT3 protein in tumor cells compared to normal cells, with a higher expression in EGFRm NSCLC. This correlation supports the hypothesis of STAT3 as a potential biomarker for predicting lung cancer carcinogenesis and EGFRm clonal expansion in NSCLC.

Another protein signaling tested in the study was nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-kB), a proinflammatory transcription factors involved in inflammation, proliferation, and apoptosis pathways. Additionally, TNF-α and IL-1β, inflammatory cytokines associated with tumorigenesis, have been reported to activate and upregulate of NF-kB [33, 34]. Several studies have reported of a significant elevation of NF-kB in lung cancer tissue compared with adjacent normal tissue, consistent with our findings of a significant elevation of NF-kB in cancer cells [35, 36]. Furthermore, several studies have reported a significant association of TNF-α was significant being higher in EGFRm patients and IL-1β levels being significantly elevated in adenocarcinoma [37–39]. However, both TNF-α and IL-1β did not show significant increase in cancer in our study, although TNF-α showed a numerical increase in the EGFRm population compared to EGFRwt population. Other inflammatory markers such as IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, and IL-12 have been reported to be associated with tumor growth in NSCLC [40–45]. However, our results did not demonstrate of strong correlation with these cytokines except for IL-10 which is more significantly elevated in L858R than del19 subgroup and EGFRwt subgroup. Evidence has shown a positive feedback regulatory pathway between IL-10 upregulation of subsequent signaling cascades (JAK1/STAT3, Src, PI3k/Akt, and Erk) to promote EGFR expression, whereas EGF expression in turn enhances IL-10 expression via increasing its mRNA stability [46].

Our study demonstrated of the pathogenesis of lung cancer probably being associated with the inflammation process, especially the development of EGFRm clones which were found at a high rate in Asian population. STAT-3 protein might be the most potential protein for predicting lung cancer together with EGFRm subtype as proven by multivariate analysis. Further molecular epidemiological analysis may be needed to better understand the distribution of potential causative agents—such as air pollution—and their association with molecular subtypes of lung cancer, which could have significant clinical implications, including differences in treatment strategies and resistance mechanisms.

Limitations of this study include its retrospective nature, which raises a concern about the quality of tissue and serum samples. Additionally, establishing standard cutoffs of inflammatory cytokines and protein signaling was challenging, making comparison with previous studies difficult. Lastly, our comparison was limited to cancer patients only, without non-cancer patients for the comparison.

Conclusion

The pathogenesis of both NSCLC and EGFRm lung cancer is intricately associated with inflammatory processes, as demonstrated in our pilot study. STAT3 protein emerges as a potentially crucial inflammatory predictive biomarkers for both NSCLC and also EGFRm patients. Prospective study involving a larger cohort is warranted for further analysis and validation. If it is confirmed in the future that inflammation is one of the pathogenesis of NSCLC, especially in oncogenic-driven lung cancer, then the air pollution might be the cause of chronic inflammation for the airway and leading to lung cancer, thus the air pollution controlled policy should be rigorous encouraged in the society.
