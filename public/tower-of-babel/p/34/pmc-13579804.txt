Reversible coma and elevated central venous pressure: warning signs of acute superior vena cava graft thrombosis following Warden procedure: a case report
Authors: Deming Liu, Leilei Zhou, Bingqian Hu, Lin Shi, Yuzhang Yan, Xingguang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Warden procedure effectively corrects partial anomalous pulmonary venous connection (PAPVC) draining into the superior vena cava (SVC), yet superior vena cava obstruction remains a serious potential complication. This report describes a rare case of acute prosthetic graft thrombosis in the SVC following Warden procedure, presenting primarily with rapid neurological deterioration and initially lacking typical signs of superior vena cava syndrome.Case presentationA 51-year-old female patient underwent a Warden procedure with autologous pericardial tube reconstruction of the superior vena cava. Postoperatively, her central venous pressure (CVP) progressively increased from 12 mmHg to a peak of 28 mmHg. Concurrently, her level of consciousness deteriorated from mild coma to deep coma with brainstem reflex suppression. Emergency cranial CT revealed no hemorrhage or significant edema. The combination of elevated CVP and deteriorating neurological function suggested acute SVC obstruction, prompting emergency exploration. During the perioperative period, even in the absence of facial/neck edema or positive cranial CT findings, immediate investigation of superior vena cava anastomotic patency is warranted upon detection of abnormally elevated central venous pressure (CVP) accompanied by altered mental status.Keywords: Warden procedure, Superior vena cava obstruction, Coma, Cerebral perfusion pressure, Partial anomalous pulmonary venous return, Case report

Background

Surgical correction of superior vena cava-type partial anomalous pulmonary venous connection (PAPVC) poses significant technical challenges. The Warden procedure has been widely adopted due to its ability to effectively reduce the risk of sinus node dysfunction and avoid superior vena cava (SVC) inlet stenosis [1, 2]. However, SVC anastomotic stenosis or obstruction remains a significant clinical concern [3], with reported incidence rates ranging from 0% to 10% in the literature. Most cases manifest as classic “SVC syndrome”—facial, neck, and upper limb edema [4]. Rarely, acute complete obstruction causes a sudden surge in cerebral venous pressure, severely impairing cerebral perfusion [5, 6]. This report describes a case of acute prosthetic graft thrombosis in the SVC following a Warden procedure. The patient presented with rapidly progressive coma due to a sharp decrease in cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP), which was successfully reversed after emergency vascular replacement.

Case presentation

A 51-year-old woman was admitted for evaluation of an atrial septal defect (ASD) initially detected during a routine physical examination. On further clinical inquiry, the patient reported mild chest tightness after physical activity, which was relieved by rest. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated enlargement of the right atrium and right ventricle, suggesting significant right heart volume overload. Computed tomography angiography confirmed PAPVC: the left superior pulmonary vein drained directly into the left brachiocephalic vein, while the right superior pulmonary vein drained into the upper segment of the SVC (Fig. 1). The pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio (Qp: Qs) was measured at 2.1, confirming a hemodynamically significant left-to-right shunt. In addition, the patient had moderate pulmonary hypertension, with a systolic pulmonary artery pressure of 57 mmHg. Based on the presence of symptoms, significant shunting, and right heart enlargement, surgical correction was considered indicated.

The patient underwent a Warden procedure under cardiopulmonary bypass. The surgeon transected the SVC above the heterotopic venous drainage site. Due to the high transection position of the SVC, direct anastomosis would have resulted in excessive tension and risked stenosis. A tubular graft (8 cm long, 1.5 cm diameter) was constructed from autologous pericardium (soaked in 0.6% glutaraldehyde and rinsed). The serous (visceral) surface of the pericardium was oriented toward the luminal side to provide a smoother inner surface. The graft circumference was tailored according to the native superior vena cava diameter to ensure appropriate size matching and minimize flow disturbance. The pericardium was folded and sutured longitudinally using a continuous technique to create a uniform tubular conduit. The graft was positioned along the anatomical course of the superior vena cava to avoid angulation or kinking and to facilitate optimal venous return, reconstructing systemic venous return. The proximal end of the SVC was closed. Ectopic pulmonary venous blood flow was diverted into the left atrium through an ASD. Concurrently, the ectopic left pulmonary vein was transected and transplanted into the left atrial appendage.

The patient received sufentanil citrate injection during anesthesia. Standard intraoperative monitoring included invasive arterial pressure monitoring and central venous catheterization. Transesophageal echocardiography was also used intraoperatively to assess cardiac structure and function. After surgery, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit without continuous sedative infusion, and no sedative agents were administered during the early postoperative period, allowing for reliable neurological assessment. Initial hemodynamics were stable, with CVP fluctuating around 12 mmHg. Due to bleeding risk, intravenous heparin infusion (500 IU/h, targeting an activated partial thromboplastin time 1.5–2.0 times the control value) was initiated after chest drainage decreased to less than 50 mL/h, indicating adequate hemostasis. The patient’s condition deteriorated rapidly (Fig. 2). At 0–2 h after surgery, the patient was hemodynamically stable, with a CVP of approximately 12 mmHg and a MAP of around 70 mmHg. No sedative agents were administered. At 2 h postoperatively, the patient developed sluggish pupillary light reflexes, although pupil size remained bilaterally equal (4 mm). Between 3 and 6 h postoperatively, CVP began to rise progressively. At 6 h, after a reduction in surgical drainage, intravenous heparin infusion was initiated (500 IU/h, targeting an activated partial thromboplastin time 1.5–2.0 times the control value). At 8 h postoperatively, the patient’s neurological status deteriorated significantly, progressing to deep coma with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 3, absence of response to painful stimuli, and loss of cough reflex. At this time, CVP had risen markedly, while MAP remained relatively stable at approximately 70 mmHg. Chest X-ray confirmed the central venous catheter tip was positioned near the proximal SVC (Fig. 3), with unobstructed aspiration. An emergency non-contrast head CT was performed and showed no evidence of intracranial hemorrhage, infarction, or significant cerebral edema (Fig. 4). At the time of imaging, the patient remained intubated and mechanically ventilated, ensuring a secure airway. A dedicated CT angiography with venous-phase imaging was not performed due to the need for rapid exclusion of intracranial hemorrhage and to avoid delays in definitive management. Between 9 and 12 h postoperatively, CVP further increased to 26–28 mmHg. Meanwhile, progressive facial and bilateral upper limb edema became apparent. Given the combination of rapidly rising CVP and severe neurological deterioration despite stable MAP and negative CT findings, acute obstruction of the superior vena cava pathway was strongly suspected. Emergency re-exploration was initiated at 12 h postoperatively. Upon observation of increased tension in the autologous pericardial graft, incision revealed extensive thrombus formation within the lumen, completely occluding the SVC. No mechanical torsion, rotation, or external compression was identified. The original graft was resected and replaced with a bovine pericardial tubular graft with diameter widened to 2 cm. CVP normalized following revision. The patient regained consciousness 8 h postoperatively, with gradual improvement in muscle strength. At 24 h postoperatively, neurological function was fully restored, and the endotracheal tube was successfully removed. Follow-up echocardiograms at 5 and 10 days showed patent SVC without obstruction, no recurrence of cephalic edema, no upper limb swelling, and no headaches or visual blurring. The patient was discharged and initiated on oral warfarin anticoagulation for six months, with a target international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.5–1.8. This relatively lower target range was selected to balance the risk of thrombosis and postoperative bleeding following recent superior vena cava reconstruction.

Discussion and conclusions

Superior vena cava (SVC) obstruction following reconstruction is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication that may result in acute neurological deterioration [4, 5]. In the present case, the patient developed rapid-onset coma in the early postoperative period, which was ultimately attributed to impaired venous return and cerebral venous congestion.

Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) is classically defined as the difference between mean arterial pressure (MAP) and intracranial pressure (ICP) [7, 8]. Although ICP was not directly measured in this case, elevated central venous pressure (CVP) may contribute to increased intracranial venous pressure in the setting of impaired venous drainage. In conditions such as SVC obstruction, elevated CVP can be transmitted retrogradely to the cerebral venous system, leading to venous congestion and a secondary increase in intracranial pressure [5, 6]. This mechanism differs fundamentally from arterial hypoperfusion, in which reduced MAP is the primary determinant of decreased cerebral blood flow.

In our patient, the combination of markedly elevated CVP, relatively preserved MAP, and rapid neurological deterioration strongly supports a venous congestion–dominant mechanism rather than primary arterial insufficiency. Importantly, the rapid and complete neurological recovery following surgical correction of the obstruction further reinforces the reversibility of this hemodynamic disturbance [9].

A structured differential diagnosis is essential when evaluating postoperative coma [10]. Residual anesthetic or sedative effects are a common cause; however, this was unlikely in our patient, as no sedative agents were administered during the postoperative period. Respiratory causes, such as hypercapnia, were also unlikely, as the patient remained intubated and mechanically ventilated. Metabolic disturbances, including hypoglycemia and electrolyte imbalance, were excluded based on laboratory findings. Acute intracranial events, such as hemorrhage or ischemic stroke, were considered; however, emergency non-contrast cranial CT revealed no evidence of intracranial hemorrhage, and the rapid neurological recovery argues against structural brain injury. Taken together, these findings support a reversible hemodynamic mechanism.

This case also highlights important anesthetic and critical care implications. Comprehensive intraoperative monitoring, including invasive arterial pressure monitoring, central venous pressure monitoring, and transesophageal echocardiography, is essential in patients undergoing complex vascular reconstruction [11]. Equally important is vigilant postoperative surveillance in the intensive care unit. Dynamic changes in CVP, particularly a marked elevation in the presence of relatively stable MAP, may represent an early warning sign of impaired venous return rather than primary cardiac dysfunction. In addition, rapid neurological deterioration in the absence of sedative agents should prompt immediate evaluation for hemodynamic or vascular causes.

Advanced imaging modalities may provide further insights into cerebral perfusion abnormalities [12, 13]. CT perfusion imaging can directly assess cerebral blood flow and identify regions of hypoperfusion, while CT venography may help visualize venous outflow obstruction. Magnetic resonance imaging techniques, including MR venography and diffusion-weighted imaging, may further characterize venous congestion. However, in the setting of acute neurological deterioration, particularly in the early postoperative period, non-contrast CT remains the most rapid and practical modality to exclude life-threatening intracranial hemorrhage. The use of advanced imaging techniques may be limited by time constraints and patient stability.

Bedside neuromonitoring techniques may also provide valuable information [11]. Electroencephalography (EEG) can detect global cortical dysfunction, transcranial Doppler (TCD) allows real-time assessment of cerebral blood flow velocity, and near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS)-based cerebral oximetry enables continuous monitoring of regional cerebral oxygen saturation. However, these modalities have limitations, including limited specificity, operator dependency, and susceptibility to extracranial signal interference. In this case, these techniques were not utilized due to the emergency nature of the clinical deterioration and limited availability during nighttime hours.

The timing of postoperative anticoagulation represents a critical clinical challenge [14]. Early initiation may reduce the risk of graft thrombosis but must be balanced against the risk of postoperative bleeding. In this case, anticoagulation was initiated only after mediastinal drainage decreased to less than 50 mL/h, indicating adequate hemostasis. While this cautious approach reduces bleeding risk, it may contribute to thrombus formation in high-risk settings. Therefore, individualized anticoagulation strategies should be considered.

In summary, this case illustrates a rare but reversible cause of postoperative coma due to impaired venous return following SVC reconstruction. Recognition of the characteristic hemodynamic pattern—markedly elevated CVP with relatively preserved MAP—may facilitate early diagnosis and timely intervention.
