Academic burnout and internet gaming disorder in Chinese adolescents: The chain mediating roles of depressive symptoms and negative attentional bias
Authors: Yalei Li, Yanyu Wang, Jinghua Zhu, Liping Jia, Guohua Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImportanceAcademic burnout is prevalent among Chinese adolescents and could increase their susceptibility to internet gaming disorder (IGD) by influencing both affective and cognitive factors. However, the underlying mechanisms, particularly the roles of depressive symptoms and attentional bias, remain unclear.ObjectiveThe present research investigated how academic burnout influences IGD among adolescents and examined whether depressive symptoms and attentional bias mediate this association.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey was conducted using cluster sampling, recruiting 2042 adolescents aged 11–17 years old. Scales were used to assess academic burnout, IGD, depressive symptoms, and attentional bias. Descriptive statistics and correlation analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0, and the chain mediation model was tested using the PROCESS 4.1 model 6.ResultsAcademic burnout positively predicted IGD among the adolescents (β = 0.218, P < 0.001). Depressive symptoms and negative attentional bias independently mediated this association, and significant chain mediation via depressive symptoms, followed by negative attentional bias, was observed. Positive attentional bias showed no significant mediating effect.InterpretationThese findings highlight the affective and cognitive pathways associated with academic burnout and IGD, underscoring the interplay between depressive symptoms and attentional bias. By integrating these mechanisms, this study advanced the interaction of person–affect–cognition–execution model and provided potential targets for prevention and intervention in adolescent IGD.

INTRODUCTION

Rapid advancements in digital technology have made internet gaming one of the most popular recreational activities for adolescents in China. 1 However, some adolescents exhibit loss of control, compulsive use, and addictive behaviors, which may develop into internet gaming disorder (IGD). 2 , 3 Defined in the International Classification of Diseases, 11th revision (ICD‐11) as a subtype of gaming disorder, IGD is characterized by impaired control over gaming, increased priority given to gaming, and continued use despite adverse consequences. 4 , 5 The global prevalence of adolescent IGD ranges from 3% to 8.8%, 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 with studies in China reporting higher rates of 10%–20%. 10 , 11 , 12 IGD is associated with a range of adverse psychological and social outcomes. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Therefore, understanding the psychological mechanisms involved in adolescent IGD is of great significance for developing effective intervention approaches and safeguarding adolescent mental health.

Under China's highly competitive, examination‐oriented education system, adolescents are exposed to sustained academic pressure and evaluative stress, which substantially increases the risk of academic burnout. 18 , 19 , 20 Academic burnout refers to a psychological state characterized by emotional exhaustion, learning‐related cynicism, and reduced academic efficacy resulting from prolonged academic stress. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 According to the compensatory internet use theory, individuals tend to engage in online activities (e.g., gaming) to compensate for negative emotions or dissatisfaction experienced in real life. 25 Consequently, adolescents experiencing persistent academic burnout may rely on internet gaming for emotion regulation and psychological compensation, thereby increasing the risk of IGD. 26 Based on previous literature, we propose Hypothesis 1: Academic burnout may positively predict adolescent IGD.

Previous studies have primarily focused on the association between academic burnout and generalized behavioral addictions. For instance, Männikkö et al. 27 conducted a large‐scale cross‐sectional study among Finnish higher education students and found a significant positive correlation between academic burnout and problematic internet use (PIU), including gaming‐related behaviors. Similarly, Tomaszek and Muchacka‐Cymerman 28 found that burnout (particularly academic burnout, loss of interest in school activities, and academic pressure imposed by parents) significantly and positively predicted PIU among Polish adolescents. Moreover, a systematic review revealed that academic burnout tended to foster excessive internet use over time among Finnish adolescents. 29 However, the specific psychological mechanisms underlying the relationship between academic burnout and IGD remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

According to the interaction of person–affect–cognition–execution (I‐PACE) model, the development of IGD results from the interaction between situational predisposing factors and individual predisposing variables, including affective, cognitive, and executive factors. 30 , 31 External stressors serve as a situational predisposing factor of IGD, and academic burnout, as a typical response to academic stress, may influence IGD through affective and cognitive processes. Depressive symptoms are characterized by persistent sadness, loss of interest or pleasure, reduced energy, and cognitive impairments. 32 Numerous studies have demonstrated a strong association between academic burnout and depressive symptoms. Prolonged exposure to excessive academic demands often results in emotional exhaustion and feelings of helplessness among adolescents, which may in turn trigger sustained depressive symptoms. 33 , 34 , 35 Moreover, previous studies have indicated that depressive symptoms significantly predict the development and maintenance of adolescent IGD. 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 Consistently, a longitudinal study of 1121 Chinese adolescents found that baseline depressive symptoms significantly predicted later IGD incidence. 41 Internet gaming may serve as a means of emotional regulation to cope with negative emotions such as depression, anxiety, and loneliness. However, prolonged reliance on gaming as a coping strategy may lead individuals to perceive it as a stable and effective way to relieve stress, thereby weakening behavioral control and gradually increasing the risk of IGD. 25 , 42 Thus, we propose Hypothesis 2: Depressive symptoms may mediate the relationship between academic burnout and adolescent IGD.

In addition to affective factors, the I‐PACE model also emphasizes that cognitive components play an equally important role in IGD. 30 , 31 Among these, attentional bias, an automatic cognitive processing pattern, reflects an individual's selective attention tendency toward specific types of stimuli during information processing. 43 Negative attentional bias refers to the preferential processing of negative information. 43 According to the mood‐congruent hypothesis, negative emotional states may enhance attentional bias toward mood‐congruent information. 44 Among adolescents, both emotional exhaustion caused by academic burnout and depressive symptoms can increase negative attentional bias. 44 , 45 , 46 However, research on IGD and attentional bias toward emotional information remains limited. Previous research has demonstrated a robust association between behavioral addictions and negative attentional bias. 46 For example, Hu et al. 46 found that college students with mobile phone addiction exhibited a preferential processing of negative stimuli. Similarly, Tsumura and Kusunoki 47 reported that college students with internet addiction showed bias toward negative and internet‐related words, but not positive information. These findings suggested heightened sensitivity to negative cues in behavioral addictions. Such preferential processing of negative information may intensify psychological distress, reinforce addictive behaviors, and thereby increase the risk of developing addictive disorders.

However, prior studies exhibited several limitations. First, most previous studies have focused on gaming‐related attentional bias, while relatively neglecting emotional attentional bias, particularly the preferential processing of negative information. This limitation may underestimate adolescents’ cognitive processing tendencies when experiencing academic burnout or emotional distress (e.g., depression), as well as the potential role of such tendencies in the development and maintenance of IGD. Second, most prior studies have been based on adult samples. Adolescence represents a peak developmental window for IGD onset, 41 characterized by rapid neurobiological maturation alongside still‐developing executive control systems. 48 Immaturities in prefrontal regulatory mechanisms may compromise inhibitory control in high‐pressure contexts, increasing vulnerability to maladaptive gaming behaviors. Moreover, developmental differences in emotion regulation and cognitive processing suggest that attentional mechanisms in adolescents may operate differently from those in adults. Based on these considerations, we propose Hypothesis 3: Negative attentional bias may mediate the relationship between academic burnout and adolescent IGD.

In addition, academic burnout may influence adolescent IGD through its effects on depressive symptoms and subsequent attentional bias. According to the I‐PACE model, both individual factors and situational triggers jointly contribute to IGD. 30 , 31 When experiencing high levels of academic burnout, adolescents often report persistent depression. 49 , 50 Such negative emotional states could weaken reward sensitivity and executive control, thereby leading to negative attentional bias. 51 , 52 This process makes adolescents more likely to focus on negative stimuli while ignoring positive stimuli. To cope with these aversive emotional experiences, adolescents may turn to internet games for short‐term reward and emotional compensation, gradually developing maladaptive gaming patterns and increasing the risk of IGD. Thus, we propose Hypothesis 4: Depressive symptoms and negative attentional bias may jointly mediate the relationship between academic burnout and adolescent IGD.

In summary, adolescent IGD is influenced by situational predisposing factors as well as internal emotional and cognitive factors. Based on the I‐PACE model and the compensatory internet use theory, the present study investigated the relationship and underlying mechanisms between academic burnout and adolescent IGD. METHODS

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Second Medical University (Approval No. 2023YX118). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, their legal guardians, and the school authorities before data collection.

Participants

Participants were recruited in May 2024 using a cluster random sampling procedure from four ordinary public middle schools in Shandong Province, China, including two urban schools and two rural schools. Intact classes served as the clustering units, with three to four classes randomly selected from Grades 7 to 9 within each school. Paper‐based questionnaires were administered in classrooms by trained psychology graduate students and collected immediately upon completion. Of the 2112 questionnaires distributed, 70 were excluded due to missing data (n = 30), completion time under five minutes (n = 15), uniform or patterned responses (n = 18), or failure on attention‐check items (n = 7). A total of 2042 valid responses were retained. Inclusion criteria required that participants be registered students in the selected classes and be able to complete the questionnaires independently. As the study aimed to examine gaming behaviors in a community‐based adolescent sample, no additional exclusion criteria based on prior clinical diagnoses of mental disorders or IGD were applied. The final sample comprised 610 students in Grade 7 (29.9%), 671 students in Grade 8 (32.8%), and 761 students in Grade 9 (37.3%). The sample included 1014 females and 1028 males, with a mean age of 13.2 years (SD = 1.0), ranging from 11 to 17 years.

Assessments

Adolescent student burnout inventory

Academic burnout was assessed using the adolescent student burnout inventory, adapted from the Maslach burnout inventory 53 and specifically developed for use with Chinese adolescents. 54 The scale comprised 16 items covering three dimensions: emotional and physical exhaustion, academic detachment, and diminished academic efficacy. Each item was rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all true) to 5 (completely true). Higher total scores reflected more severe academic burnout. This scale has been widely used and has demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity among Chinese adolescents. 55 , 56 , 57 In the present study, Cronbach's alpha was 0.870.

IGD scale–short form

The Chinese version of the IGD scale–short form (IGDS9‐SF) was used to assess IGD symptoms and severity over the previous 12 months. 58 Each item was rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (very often), with higher total scores reflecting more severe IGD. The IGDS9‐SF has been widely used in studies of Chinese adolescents and has shown satisfactory reliability and validity. 59 , 60 In the present study, Cronbach's alpha was 0.882.

Center for epidemiologic studies depression scale

The Chinese version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale, originally developed by Radloff 61 and revised for use with Chinese adolescents by Chen et al., 62 was used to assess depressive symptoms and severity. The scale comprised 20 items. Each item used a 4‐point Likert scale, scored from 0 (rarely or none of the time) to 3 (most or all of the time), and higher scores corresponded to more severe depressive symptoms. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity across Chinese clinical studies. 63 , 64 In the present study, Cronbach's alpha was 0.894.

Attention to positive and negative information scale

The revised Chinese version of the attention to positive and negative information scale (APNI) was used to assess attentional bias toward emotional information. 65 , 66 The scale comprised two subscales: attention to positive information (API) and attention to negative information (ANI). It included 40 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale, with options from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores reflected a greater bias in the corresponding direction. The scale has been widely used in Chinese adolescent research and has shown good reliability and validity. 67 , 68 In the present study, Cronbach's alpha was 0.860 for the ANI and 0.910 for the API.

Data analysis

All data processing and statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.0. Descriptive statistics and Pearson's correlation analyses were first performed. Mediation analyses were subsequently conducted using Model 6 of the PROCESS macro 4.1 with bootstrap resampling (5000 samples) to test whether academic burnout was indirectly associated with IGD through depressive symptoms and attentional bias (negative or positive). 69 Within the PROCESS macro procedure, all path coefficients were estimated using ordinary least squares regression. Specifically, a series of regression equations were fitted to estimate each component path of the chain mediation model, including: (a) the effect of academic burnout on depressive symptoms; (b) the effects of academic burnout and depressive symptoms on attentional bias (negative or positive); and (c) the effects of academic burnout, depressive symptoms, and attentional bias (negative or positive) on IGD. Sex and age were entered as covariates in all regression equations. Indirect effects were considered statistically significant when the 95% bias‐corrected confidence interval did not include zero. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

RESULTS

Common method bias test

Harman's single‐factor approach was applied to assess potential common method bias. 70 The analysis identified 11 factors with eigenvalues exceeding 1, and the first factor explained 20.376% of the overall variance, which was below the 40% threshold. These findings indicated no serious common method bias.

Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis

Descriptive statistics and correlation results were presented in Table 1. Correlational analysis revealed that IGD was positively associated with academic burnout, emotional and physical exhaustion, academic detachment, diminished academic efficacy, depressive symptoms, negative attentional bias, and age (all P < 0.001), and negatively associated with sex and positive attentional bias (all P < 0.001). Academic burnout was positively correlated with depressive symptoms, negative attentional bias, sex, and age (all P < 0.001), and negatively correlated with positive attentional bias (P < 0.001). Depressive symptoms were positively associated with negative attentional bias and sex (all P < 0.001), and negatively associated with positive attentional bias (P < 0.001). Negative attentional bias was positively correlated with sex and age (all P < 0.001). Positive attentional bias showed a weak positive correlation with age (P < 0.001). Based on these results, IGD showed moderate associations with academic burnout, depressive symptoms, and negative attentional bias, highlighting their relevance for examining the underlying mechanisms of IGD. Positive attentional bias, as a distinct component of attentional bias, remained theoretically relevant. Accordingly, subsequent analyses employed a chain mediation model with sex and age controlled.

DISCUSSION

This study examined the effect of academic burnout on IGD and explored the mediating roles of depressive symptoms and attentional bias in this association. The results showed that academic burnout positively predicted IGD among adolescents, suggesting that greater academic burnout was associated with higher IGD severity. This finding supported the notion that academic burnout is a major predisposing factor in IGD, consistent with previous research. 34 , 71 According to the compensatory internet use theory, 25 adolescents often seek psychological compensation through online activities such as gaming. Those with high academic burnout typically experience emotional exhaustion and reduced efficacy, whereas internet games can provide a sense of control, achievement, and autonomy, thereby enhancing perceived self‐worth. 72 , 73 Adolescents with high burnout also face multiple stressors, especially academic pressure. 74 Prior studies have shown that academic stress is positively associated with gaming addiction. 75 Adolescents frequently use internet games as a stress‐coping strategy due to their low barriers and immediate rewards. 76 From a neurodevelopmental perspective, the adolescent prefrontal cortex has not yet fully matured, resulting in weaker cognitive control. Under conditions of high stress or burnout, this limited control makes it more difficult for adolescents to inhibit impulsive gaming, increasing the likelihood of excessive use and dependence and, in turn, elevating the risk of IGD. 77 , 78

The present results showed that depressive symptoms mediated the relationship between academic burnout and adolescent IGD, consistent with prior findings 34 and with the I‐PACE model. 30 , 31 According to this framework, academic burnout activates internal psychological pathways in adolescents, with affective factors, particularly depressive symptoms, playing a central mediating role in IGD. Previous studies have shown that academic burnout increases depressive symptoms. 35 , 49 Adolescents experiencing burnout are more likely to make negative attributions about their learning and self‐worth, thereby triggering or exacerbating depressive symptoms. A systematic review of 407 620 adolescents and young adults across 33 countries further identified depression as a risk factor for IGD. 7 Longitudinal studies also demonstrated that baseline depressive symptoms predicted later IGD. 41 , 79 , 80 , 81 Excessive internet gaming can itself serve as a maladaptive emotion‐regulation strategy, as adolescents facing real‐life stress, academic burnout, and related negative experiences may seek positive emotions or a sense of belonging through gaming, thereby reinforcing gaming behavior. 80 , 82

In addition to the affective factor, the cognitive factor is also critical in the mechanisms underlying IGD. The present study found that negative attentional bias mediated the relationship between academic burnout and adolescent IGD. Specifically, higher levels of academic burnout were associated with stronger negative attentional bias, which in turn predicted greater IGD severity. A plausible explanation is that academic burnout, as a chronic state of psychological exhaustion, weakens attentional control and enhances the processing of negative information and attentional bias, rendering individuals more sensitive to failure‐related or aversive cues. 83 , 84 This heightened sensitivity may, in turn, elicit stronger avoidance motives and increase reliance on virtual environments. Kaczmarek and Drążkowski 85 found that individuals who used gaming to escape real‐life difficulties were more likely to blur the boundary between virtual and real worlds. Consequently, adolescents might repeatedly rely on internet gaming to avoid or regulate real‐life problems, forming a vicious cycle that exacerbates IGD. 46 , 86 Meanwhile, the present study found that positive attentional bias was not a mediating pathway between academic burnout and IGD. David and Magurean 87 reported that training individuals to direct attention toward positive cues significantly improved emotion regulation and adaptive functioning. A meta‐analytic study also demonstrated that attentional preference for positive emotional stimuli was associated with more favorable emotional outcomes. 88 Therefore, positive attentional bias may, to some extent, buffer the adverse effects of academic burnout on IGD. However, this interpretation requires further validation.

The present study further found that depressive symptoms and negative attentional bias sequentially mediated the relationship between academic burnout and adolescent IGD. This indicated that higher levels of academic burnout increased depressive symptoms, which in turn exacerbated negative attentional bias, ultimately contributing to IGD. These findings supported the I‐PACE model, 30 , 31 which posits that IGD results from the interaction of affective, cognitive, and situational factors. Adolescents experiencing high levels of academic burnout are more likely to undergo emotional exhaustion and diminished self‐efficacy, thereby rendering them more vulnerable to depressive symptoms. Depression not only reflects heightened emotional dysregulation but also significantly alters information‐processing tendencies, making individuals more attentive to negative information, less responsive to positive information, and more likely to experience intensified psychological distress in daily life. Under such a maladaptive emotion–cognition processing pattern, adolescents may increasingly rely on internet games to obtain immediate emotional relief or psychological compensation, allowing gaming to gradually become a dominant strategy for avoidance and affect regulation. Furthermore, Brand 89 proposed that the development of IGD is jointly driven by positive reinforcement mechanisms (e.g., pleasure, a sense of achievement) and negative reinforcement mechanisms (e.g., alleviation of distress, escape from reality). When adolescents repeatedly rely on gaming for self‐regulation in the context of severe burnout and depression, these reinforcement processes continuously strengthen the behavior, causing initially coping‐motivated use to consolidate into compulsive, habitual, and ultimately pathological gaming patterns, thereby contributing to IGD. 90 , 91

The findings of this study offered important theoretical and practical implications for understanding and intervening in adolescent IGD. First, the results supported the I‐PACE model, highlighting the combined influence of situational predisposing factor, affective factor, and cognitive factor on IGD. By incorporating negative attentional bias into the cognitive component of the model, this study elucidated a key pathway through which academic burnout contributed to IGD, thereby enriching the understanding of IGD mechanisms. Second, this study extended prior research by focusing on adolescents, whose cognitive control and emotion‐regulation capacities were still developing and who faced substantial academic pressure. Under high burnout, adolescents were more likely to exhibit sequential increases in depressive symptoms and negative attentional bias, thereby elevating their risk of IGD. These findings underscored the importance of developmentally sensitive prevention and intervention strategies. Third, in terms of practical implications for IGD intervention, schools could establish routine screening mechanisms for academic burnout and emotional difficulties, such as questionnaire‐based assessments and mental health evaluations. Academic burden could be reduced by optimizing curricula and assessment systems while reinforcing the intrinsic value of learning. Evidence‐based programs, including stress‐management and positive psychology courses, could further strengthen students’ resilience and emotion‐regulation abilities. High‐risk students could benefit from brief cognitive behavioral therapy‐based interventions or group‐based stress‐reduction programs. Importantly, attentional bias represents a promising intervention target, and attentional bias modification training could help redirect adolescents’ attentional resources toward positive information, thereby reducing the influence of negative affect and maladaptive cognition on gaming behavior and lowering the risk of IGD.

Several limitations should also be noted. First, the cross‐sectional design prevented causal inference. Future longitudinal studies are needed to validate the temporal ordering of the variables. Second, attentional bias was assessed using a self‐report measure, which might be subject to subjective bias. Future research should integrate cognitive‐behavioral experimental paradigms (e.g., dot‐probe tasks and eye‐tracking), informant reports (e.g., parent or teacher ratings), objective behavioral data (e.g., passive usage logs), and ecological momentary assessment to enhance ecological validity.
