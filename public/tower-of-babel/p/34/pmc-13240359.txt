Integration of Raman Spectroscopy and Metabolomics for Early Breast Cancer Detection and Classification
Authors: Xue Li, He Ren, Yuhan Deng, Yang Li, Fangjie Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBreast cancer, now the fourth leading cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide, necessitates early detection for improved clinical outcomes. Conventional histopathology, though widely used, is invasive and subjective, limiting its utility in early‐stage diagnosis. Here, we integrated confocal Raman spectroscopy with metabolomics to analyze biochemical and morphological features of breast tissues, including normal, fibroadenoma, ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), and invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC). Using spectral analysis and spectral unmixing, we mapped key biochemical components—proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids—across distinct tissue types. Cancerous tissues displayed heightened signals for proteins and nucleic acids but reduced lipids and carotenoids, reflecting profound metabolic alterations. Validation through metabolomic profiling revealed upregulated glycolytic and lipid synthesis pathways in tumor regions. Raman imaging further enabled precise classification of breast cancer subtypes, such as mucinous carcinoma and phyllodes tumors. These findings underscore the potential of Raman spectroscopy as a non‐invasive diagnostic modality for early detection and classification of breast cancer. The integration of Raman imaging with machine learning presents a promising avenue for advancing precision oncology.Keywords: biochemical composition, breast tumors, metabolites, pathological states, Raman spectroscopy, tissue imaging

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy among women globally, with its incidence rising annually, posing a significant public health challenge [1, 2]. In‐depth research on early‐stage breast cancer is academically crucial, as it holds the potential to enhance patient treatment outcomes and develop effective strategies for preventing recurrence and metastasis [3, 4]. The pathological progression of breast cancer is notably complex, with the prognosis and treatment intricately linked to the pathological characteristics of the lesions [5]. Current diagnostic and pathological grading approaches predominantly depend on histological examination, which involves obtaining tissue specimens and conducting detailed analyses to ascertain the type, location, and degree of lesion invasion. However, these conventional methods are invasive, limited in scope, and subject to observer bias. Moreover, traditional histological staining methods, which focus on morphological analysis, are inadequate for detecting lesions during the latent stages of the disease and fail to provide critical biochemical information about the tumor [5, 6, 7].

As cancer progresses, tissue biochemical components undergo significant changes that reflect tumor development, underscoring the importance of early detection and intervention. Traditional methods often fail to capture the dynamic evolution of precancerous lesions [8]. Recently, Raman spectroscopy has emerged as a non‐invasive technique capable of providing high‐resolution molecular information. Beyond its established utility in materials science, confocal Raman spectroscopy has been widely investigated in biomedical applications, demonstrating significant diagnostic potential in differentiating malignant from benign tissues in skin, prostate, and gastrointestinal cancers, as well as characterizing non‐cancerous inflammatory conditions [9, 10]. During the rapid proliferation of early‐stage tumors, the DNA content in tumor cells increases significantly, and Raman spectroscopy enables the detection of subtle variations in molecular vibrational modes, offering quantitative insights into biochemical alterations through shifts in spectral peaks [11, 12, 13, 14].

In breast cancer research, confocal Raman spectroscopy integrates biochemical and morphological analysis, offering a comprehensive view of biomarker changes and tissue heterogeneity [15, 16]. However, the complexity and variability of breast cancer tissues present significant challenges. By applying dimensionality reduction and clustering techniques to Raman spectroscopic data, it becomes possible to mitigate these challenges, enhancing the understanding of pathological processes [17].

This study aims to utilize Raman spectroscopy imaging to investigate the pathological characteristics of human breast tissues and improve early diagnostic efficiency. Key biochemical components—including lipids, nucleic acids, proteins, and carotenoids—were visualized throughout breast cancer progression, providing insights into abnormal molecular expression in tumor tissues. Additionally, Raman imaging reduced the need for repeated biopsies in healthy individuals and those with pre‐tumor lesions. Combined with metabolomics and single‐cell sequencing, this approach demonstrated high reliability, positioning Raman spectroscopy as a robust diagnostic tool for breast cancer classification and metabolic analysis (Figure 1). Raman spectroscopy imaging offers the potential to replace traditional pathological biopsies, providing a non‐invasive, real‐time method for the detection and classification of breast cancer. The findings of this study contribute significantly to understanding the molecular mechanisms driving breast cancer progression and hold promise for advancing early diagnosis and personalized treatment strategies.

Result

Raman Spectroscopy Reveals the Distribution of Major Biochemical Components in Breast Tissue

To elucidate the primary biochemical components of various breast tissue types, Raman spectroscopy was employed using a 532 nm laser system with enhanced spatial resolution and signal‐to‐noise ratio. Raman spectra of normal breast tissue (NBT), fibroadenoma tissue (FAT), ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), and invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) were acquired, focusing on the fingerprint region (200–1800 cm−1), the silent region (1800–2700 cm−1), and the high‐wavenumber region (2700–3300 cm−1). These regions provide critical molecular insights, encompassing characteristic vibrations of proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids. Analysis of 34 breast tissue samples revealed significant spectral differences among tissue types. Tumor tissues exhibited increased signals corresponding to proteins and nucleic acids and decreased signals for lipids and carotenoids. These changes reflect profound biochemical alterations associated with tumorigenesis [18]. Comparative analysis of Raman spectra showed a progressive weakening of peak intensity correlating with the pathological severity (Figures 2 and S1). Biochemical shifts identified through spectral features are detailed in Table 1. While the peak assignments are based on established consensus, the core innovation of our study lies in mapping the relative intensity shifts of these specific peaks directly to spatial multi‐omics data (AFADESI‐MSI and scRNA‐seq) across diverse breast cancer subtypes, a comprehensive validation not present in the referenced article.

Visualization of Different Types of Breast Tissue Using Raman Spectroscopy Imaging

To correlate tissue optical properties with precise pathological features, we performed confocal Raman spectroscopic imaging on specific regions of interest (ROIs) directly guided by adjacent H&E‐stained reference sections. Three‐dimensional Raman imaging was employed to visualize the spatial and wavenumber distribution of key biochemical components across distinct breast tissue types (Figure 3A–D). To quantitatively interpret the hyperspectral images, True Component Analysis (TCA) was applied to deconvolute the spectral matrix. This chemometric blind source separation successfully isolated the distinct spectral contributions of cellular nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids, enabling precise spatial monitoring of biochemical alterations driven by carcinogenesis. Subsequently, unsupervised K‐means clustering reconstructed the pathological architecture based purely on these biochemical signatures, demonstrating clear structural differentiation among normal, benign, and malignant breast tissues (Figure 3E–H). We further utilized univariate imaging to map the spatial distribution of specific biochemical constituents by integrating signature Raman bands: proteins (2942 ± 13 cm−1), lipids (1355 ± 77 cm−1), carotenoids (1128 ± 42 cm−1), collagen (1010 ± 71 cm−1), nucleic acids (767 ± 18 cm−1), and cytochromes (1560 ± 31 cm−1) (Figure 4). Spatial analysis revealed that collagen, phenylalanine, nucleic acids, and carotenoids predominantly localized within fibrous and proliferative regions, whereas lipid‐rich adipose tissues exhibited robust, distinct characteristic peaks. Crucially, as tissues transitioned from normal to malignant states, the spatial expression of proteins and nucleic acids markedly intensified, concomitant with a significant depletion in lipid spatial distribution, particularly fatty acids.

Differential Changes in Breast Cancer Tissue and Adjacent Non‐Cancerous Tissue

To reliably distinguish breast cancer (CA) from adjacent non‐cancerous tissues (PY), average Raman spectra and corresponding difference spectra were extracted from paired surgical specimens (Figure 5A). Comparative spectral analysis revealed a pronounced depletion of lipid content in CA tissues, evidenced by significant intensity reductions in the CH2 deformation peak (1440 cm−1) and the prominent lipid band at 2854 cm−1. Furthermore, the phenylalanine peak (1002 cm−1) exhibited reduced intensity in CA tissues. Conversely, CA tissues demonstrated significantly elevated signals for proteins and nucleic acids. This was characterized by enhanced intensities of the nucleic acid band (785 cm−1), protein‐related amide III (1240–1300 cm−1) and amide I (1650–1680 cm−1) peaks, as well as the C=C (1650–1680 cm−1) and C–H (2800–3000 cm−1) stretching vibrations. Pseudocolor Raman imaging based on these targeted peaks visually corroborated the spatial dichotomy of lipid and protein/nucleic acid distributions between CA and PY tissues (Figure 5B), accurately reflecting the biochemical hallmarks of rapid tumor proliferation.

Histometabolomic Validation of Raman Spectral Signatures

To validate the biochemical alterations captured by Raman spectroscopy (RS), we integrated high‐throughput targeted metabolomics with AFADESI‐MSI on 46 paired breast tissue samples (cancer [CA] vs. paracancerous [PY]). Multivariate analyses of 27 key differential metabolites robustly discriminated CA from PY tissues (Figures 6A–C and S4), confirming profound metabolic reprogramming within the tumor microenvironment. Crucially, the spatial and quantitative metabolomic data directly corroborated our RS findings. The enhanced Raman signal at 1085 cm−1, indicative of upregulated glycolysis, precisely matched the elevated spatial accumulation of lactic acid (m/z 124.0057) visualized via AFADESI‐MSI (Figure S2). Furthermore, characteristic lipid shifts observed in RS—such as the phospholipid peak at 716 cm−1 and fatty acid synthesis signals between 2845 and 2890 cm−1—strongly correlated with significant alterations in phosphatidylinositol (PI‐38:4), specific fatty acids (FA‐20:0, FA‐24:1), and triglycerides (TG‐54:11) (Figure S3). The MSI detection of multiple carnitine species (e.g., propionylcarnitine, tetradecanoylcarnitine) further substantiated the RS‐detected restructuring of fatty acid metabolism (Figure 7). Additionally, RS‐identified alterations in protein and nucleic acid content aligned directly with the MS‐detected enrichment of key amino acids (valine, glycine, phenylalanine, and glutamate) and nucleosides in tumor‐infiltrated regions. Our integrated analysis also revealed stage‐dependent metabolic dynamics: early‐stage tumors exhibited heightened lipid and lactate accumulation with depleted branched‐chain amino acids, whereas advanced stages demonstrated progressive lipid consumption alongside upregulated pyruvate and glutamate pathways. Collectively, this multi‐modal cross‐validation confirms that RS imaging reliably captures the complex, spatiotemporal metabolic reprogramming inherent to breast cancer progression (Tables S1 and S2).

Sc‐RNA Sequencing Analysis

To delineate the cellular heterogeneity and molecular basis of the RS‐detected biochemical shifts, scRNA‐seq analysis was performed (Figures 8A,B, S5A,B). Unsupervised clustering identified 21 distinct subpopulations, which were annotated into nine major cell lineages: epithelial cells, T/NK cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs), B cells, mast cells, and plasma cells. Crucially, pathway enrichment analysis of the transcriptomic data provided direct genomic validation for the metabolic shifts observed in RS. Specifically, the transcriptomic upregulation of glycosphingolipid biosynthesis and glycerophospholipid metabolism strongly correlated with the elevated lipid synthesis signals captured by RS at 2845–2890 cm−1. Similarly, enhanced pyrimidine metabolism at the single‐cell level corresponded to the increased nucleic acid Raman signals. The robust upregulation of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis pathways structurally supported the variations in protein‐related Raman peaks. By correlating these transcriptomic profiles with spatial spectral features, this dual‐modal analysis mechanistically anchors the RS diagnostic signatures to specific transcriptomic alterations within the tumor microenvironment.

Raman Spectral Imaging of Special Types of Breast Tumors

Mucinous breast carcinoma (MBC) and mesenchymal‐derived tumors are relatively rare subtypes of breast cancer. MBC is characterized by a significant presence of extracellular mucin, within which clusters of tumor cells are suspended [20]. MBC can be further categorized into pure mucinous breast carcinoma (PMBC) and mixed mucinous breast carcinoma (MMBC). PMBC typically exhibits a favorable prognosis, whereas MMBC is associated with a poorer outcome. The clinical, pathological, and prognostic heterogeneity between these MBC subtypes, including differences in mucin characteristics and treatment responses, necessitates a comprehensive approach to diagnosis and management. Although the molecular subtypes and mechanisms underlying MBC require further investigation, Raman spectral imaging has shown promise in mapping mucin distribution within tumor tissues, offering insights into the tumor's progression (Figure 9). On the other hand, phyllodes tumors (PT) of the breast, which account for only 0.3% to 0.8% of all breast tumors, are mixed neoplasms composed of stromal and epithelial components [21]. PTs can be classified as benign, borderline, or malignant based on their growth patterns and stromal characteristics. Most commonly found in women aged 40–50, benign and borderline PTs are primarily treated surgically, with careful resection of the tumor capsule to minimize recurrence. Malignant PTs may require adjunctive radiotherapy. Although the prognosis is generally favorable, there is a significant risk of recurrence, particularly for borderline and malignant subtypes. For these rare breast tumors, Raman spectral imaging can provide detailed information on the distribution and concentration of phyllodes tissues within the tumor. By analyzing the Raman spectra, it is possible to distinguish stromal tissues from other components, generating detailed spectral images that illustrate the tumor's structure (Figure 9). These images are crucial for understanding the development and spread of phyllodes tumors. In conclusion, while mucinous breast carcinoma and phyllodes tumors are rare, their distinct biological behaviors warrant thorough investigation. The integration of Raman spectral imaging into the diagnostic workflow could significantly enhance the early diagnosis and treatment of these uncommon breast cancer subtypes, underscoring the need for multidisciplinary approaches in their management.

Discussion

The integration of confocal Raman spectroscopy (RS) with spatial metabolomics and single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) presents a robust, multi‐modal paradigm for the early detection and molecular subtyping of breast cancer. Our findings demonstrate that RS imaging possesses the requisite sensitivity to capture dynamic biochemical shifts during tumorigenesis without the need for destructive sample processing. A significant innovation of this study is the cross‐validation of spectral features through multi‐omics profiling. Rather than merely observing spectral peak variations, our integrated approach confirms that the biochemical alterations detected by RS are directly driven by underlying metabolic reprogramming. For instance, the transition from lipid‐dominated spectra in normal tissues to protein and nucleic acid‐rich spectra in cancerous tissues (as seen in the Raman signals for lactate at 1085 cm−1 and specific amino acids) seamlessly aligns with the upregulated glycolysis and amino acid metabolism pathways characteristic of the Warburg effect, which was corroborated by both AFADESI‐MSI and scRNA‐seq data. Furthermore, the capacity of RS to image rare and complex subtypes, such as mucinous breast carcinoma and phyllodes tumors, underscores its potential clinical utility, particularly for rapid intraoperative margin assessment where traditional MRI is cost‐prohibitive. To navigate the inherent heterogeneity, high dimensionality, and noise of RS datasets, advanced chemometric and machine learning frameworks are indispensable. In this study, our preliminary supervised models (SVM and PLS‐DA) successfully established diagnostic boundaries between normal and neoplastic tissues. However, recognizing that the integration of raw Python code and exhaustive algorithmic iteration steps into the main text detracts from the biological narrative, we have provided the comprehensive coding pipelines, raw spectral libraries, and extensive model optimization parameters in the Supporting Information (Figure S6 and Pdf S1). Future computational efforts should transition toward non‐linear dimensionality reduction algorithms (such as UMAP) and deep learning networks equipped with attention mechanisms, which are better suited to preserve complex spatial‐spectral relationships while enhancing biological interpretability. Despite the promising diagnostic potential demonstrated, this study has notable limitations that must be addressed before clinical translation. A critical limitation is the restricted sample size for specific clinical categories, notably for DCIS (n = 3) and the rare subtypes (n = 1 for mucinous carcinoma and phyllodes tumors). While the inclusion of these rare entities highlights the broad applicability of our RS approach, this limited cohort size significantly elevates the risk of statistical overfitting within our machine learning classification models. Consequently, the current diagnostic algorithms may lack the robustness and generalizability required for immediate clinical implementation. In conclusion, the synergistic application of RS, metabolomics, and single‐cell transcriptomics offers profound insights into the spatial and metabolic architecture of breast cancer. Future investigations must prioritize the recruitment of large‐scale, multicenter patient cohorts to rigorously validate these spectral signatures, refine the predictive models, and ultimately facilitate the translation of this technology into routine oncological diagnostics.

Material and Methods

Experimental Design

This study systematically characterized the biochemical features of various breast tissue types, including normal breast tissue (NBT), fibroadenoma tissue (FAT), ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), and invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC). Confocal Raman spectroscopy imaging was employed to analyze cancerous tissues and adjacent normal tissues obtained from human breast samples. Specific details regarding the types of breast tissues analyzed are provided in Table 2. Tissue samples underwent Raman spectroscopy imaging, with data processing and visualization conducted using Project FOUR software (version 5.3, WITec). Additionally, a spatiotemporal correlation between Raman imaging, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and metabolomics was explored. Certified pathologists annotated the H&E staining results to identify clusters of cancer cells and surrounding stromal regions.

Breast Tissue Sample Preparation

Fresh breast tissue samples were collected from 43 patients who underwent surgical excision or Mammotome biopsy at the Breast Surgery Department of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. Among the 43 patients, the majority provided a single characteristic lesion tissue. However, to facilitate robust comparative analysis, multiple distinct tissue specimens (e.g., paired tumor and adjacent paracancerous tissues, or distinct multifocal lesions) were successfully obtained from a subset of these patients, resulting in a total cohort of 51 distinct breast tissue samples. Detailed patient information can be found in Excel S1. Pathological diagnoses were confirmed by the hospital's pathology team. As this was a retrospective study, formal consent for sample collection after lesion removal was not required. The study adhered to the ethical guidelines of the People's Republic of China and received approval from the hospital's Ethics Committee (Ethical Number: KY2023‐73). Collected tissues were immediately frozen at −80°C. Four adjacent sections, each 6 μm thick, were prepared from each specimen using a cryostat (LEICA‐CM3050S, Germany) set at −20°C. One section was stained with H&E for routine histopathological analysis by three experienced breast pathologists, while the remaining sections were fixed in 4% formaldehyde, air‐dried, and stored at −20°C for Raman imaging analysis. Before imaging, frozen samples were thawed, rehydrated in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), and rinsed with distilled water. Multiple spectra and Raman spectral images were subsequently collected from each sample.

Confocal Raman Measurements and Raman Spectroscopy Imaging

Raman spectral data were acquired using an alpha 300R WITec system (WITec GmbH, Germany) equipped with a 532 nm laser source at 50 mW and a 100× objective lens (EC Epiplan‐Neofluar, Zeiss, Germany) with a numerical aperture of 1.25. The instrument was operated under ambient conditions, and system calibration was performed using the silicon peak at 521 cm−1. The laser power was maintained at 20 mW during measurements to ensure accuracy. Raman spectra were collected over a range of 100–3600 cm−1, with an integration time of 50 milliseconds per spectrum. Each breast tissue sample was scanned over a 50 × 50 μm area; instead of scanning the entire macroscopic tissue sample, specific regions of interest (ROIs) were carefully selected to represent the most characteristic pathological features. This selection was guided by the morphological evaluation of adjacent H&E‐stained sections by an experienced pathologist. Within these precisely targeted ROIs, a representative 50 × 50 μm area was scanned, yielding 10,000 spectra per sample. This targeted approach ensured that the spectral data accurately reflected the core pathological microenvironment rather than peripheral or non‐representative stroma.

Raman Data Structure, Processing, and Statistical Analysis

A total of 51 breast tissue samples representing five distinct pathological types were analyzed. The raw RS dataset comprised a high‐dimensional hyperspectral data cube (spatial dimensions x, y, and spectral dimension λ), which was subsequently unfolded into an aggregated 2D data matrix of N rows (total acquired spectra) by M columns (Raman shift wavenumbers). To ensure robust statistical evaluation, raw spectral data were preprocessed using Project Plus software (WITec). The established processing pipeline included cosmic ray removal, polynomial baseline subtraction to mitigate autofluorescence, and Savitzky–Golay filtering for noise reduction and signal smoothing [22]. Post‐preprocessing, spectra were vector‐normalized. The statistical analyses were designed to achieve three primary goals: (1) Dimensionality Reduction and Feature Extraction: Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Non‐negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) were applied to simplify the massive dataset matrix, identifying the orthogonal spectral vectors that represent distinct biochemical components (e.g., lipids vs. proteins). (2) Unsupervised Tissue Classification: K‐means clustering analysis utilizing cosine similarity was employed to group similar spectral patterns, effectively reconstructing the pathological structures based purely on biochemical signatures without prior labeling. (3) Diagnostic Model Construction: Supervised algorithms, specifically Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS‐DA), were utilized to establish classification boundaries between different tissue types. Model performance was evaluated using cross‐validation and Area Under the Curve (AUC) metrics.

Extraction and Analysis of Tissue Metabolomics

Human breast cancer tissue samples, divided into two groups—CA (Cancer, 24 pieces) and PY (Paracancerous, 22 pieces)—underwent high‐throughput targeted metabolomics analysis, totaling 46 samples. Samples were weighed, mixed with pre‐cooled methanol–water extraction solution (3:1) and steel beads, followed by multiple cycles of grinding and sonication. After standing at −40°C for 1 h, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (4°C) for 15 min. The supernatant was dried under vacuum, reconstituted with pure water, filtered, and subjected to LC–MS/MS analysis. Standard solutions were prepared by diluting 10 mmol/L stock solutions. Metabolite analysis was performed using Thermo Scientific Dionex ICS‐6000 HPIC for ion chromatography separation, and a 6500 QTrap+ mass spectrometer for quantitative analysis in MRM mode. Data were processed with AB SCIEX software, with calibration curves generated through regression analysis and precision and accuracy evaluated. This HPIC‐MRM‐MS/MS method was used to detect metabolites in 46 breast cancer tissue samples, followed by statistical analysis.

Air flow‐assisted desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry imaging (AFADESI‐MSI) platform equipped with a Q‐Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Bremen, Germany) and an AFADESI ion source was used for tissue metabolite imaging. Samples were mounted on a 2D moving stage, and MSI data were acquired in negative ion mode, with an m/z range of 100–1200. The spray solvent was acetonitrile and water (80:20, v/v) at a flow rate of 5.0 μL/min, with a spray voltage of −4500 V. The AFADESI ion source gas flow was set to 45 L/min, and the nebulizing gas (N2) flow rate was set to 0.7 MPa. Tissue sections were scanned continuously in the X direction at 100 μm/s, with 100 μm separated Y‐direction steps. MassImager ProTM software was used for background subtraction, image reconstruction, and calculation of average metabolite expression in regions of interest.

Single‐Cell RNA Sequencing Data Collection, Quality Control, Clustering, and Metabolic Activity Analysis

Single‐cell transcriptomic data were collected from 12 breast cancer samples derived from six donors diagnosed with BRCA at the Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital, along with paired adjacent normal tissues. An additional six DCIS samples were obtained from the external dataset GSE195861, resulting in a total of 18 samples. Quality control measures included the exclusion of cells with fewer than 500 detected genes and those with a mitochondrial gene content exceeding 30%, leaving 111,888 cells for further analysis. To integrate the data and address batch effects, we employed the Harmony algorithm, setting the resolution parameter to 0.3 to achieve optimal clustering. We examined the expression of canonical markers (PTPRC, CD3D, CD8A, CD4, CD79A, MZB1, LYZ, TPSAB1, VWF, PECAM1, COL3A1, LUM, EPCAM, and KRT19) on UMAP plots, which allowed us to classify cells into 21 subpopulations. These subpopulations were then annotated into nine major cell types: Epithelial, T/NK, Myeloid, Fibroblast, Endothelial, CAF, B, Mast, and Plasma cells. For the analysis of cellular metabolic activity, we utilized the R package scMetabolism, referencing the KEGG dataset and employing AUCell as the computational method, with all other parameters set to default.
