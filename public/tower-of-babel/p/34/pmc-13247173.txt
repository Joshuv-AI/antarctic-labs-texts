Morphological and pomological diversity of fig (ficus carica l.) germplasm in North-Western India
Authors: Tsering Lanzes, Arti Sharma, Mahital Jamwal, Romesh Kumar Salgotra, Parshant Bakshi, Stanzin Khenrab, Vikas Gupta, Prabhdeep Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fig (Ficus carica L.) represents one of the most ancient and economically important fruit crops with significant nutritional and medicinal properties. The study evaluated the morphological and pomological variability of 40 fig genotypes during 2023–2025 at Sher-e-Kashmir University of Agricultural Sciences and Technology of Jammu, India. The germplasm included 39 indigenous varieties from different regions of Jammu and Kashmir, alongside a commercial variety “Brown Turkey’. A total of 35 morpho-pomological parameters including tree, leaf, fruit characteristics, were evaluated. Most of the genotypes exhibited significant variability with coefficient of variations (CV) above 20.00% ranging from 11.40% to 136.10%, revealing exceptionally high phenotypic diversity. Fruit weight showed strong and positive correlation with fruit width (r = 0.97) and fruit flesh thickness (r = 0.97), moderately with fruit shape (r = 0.48) and ostiole width (r = 0.44), but negatively with total soluble solids (r = -0.30) and ease of peeling (r = -0.39). Principal component analysis revealed five components explaining 80.12% of cumulative variance, with PC1 (26.20%) primarily associated with fruit and leaf size-related traits. Ward cluster analysis using Euclidean distance grouped genotypes into two main clusters, each with two sub-clusters, with the maximum genetic distance observed between Cluster I and Cluster II. Genotypes such as SKJFRa (Sel-14) and SKJFRa (Sel-17) emerged as particularly promising. SKJFRa (Sel-14) excelled in fruit size, flesh thickness and resistance to cracking for better storability, while SKJFRa (Sel-17) offered higher sugar content (TSS) with greater tree vigour. The results demonstrate that Himalayan fig germplasm possesses a rich phenotypic diversity and identified genotypes represent a valuable genetic resource for conservation and climate-resilient breeding programs targeting morpho-pomological improvements and adaptation to challenging environmental conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42056-3.

Introduction

Fig (Ficus carica L.) is among the most ancient, domesticated1 fruit crops and continues to hold substantial economic, nutritional and cultural significance from Mediterranean to the Himalayan region. The global centre of fig production has shifted dramatically from the Mediterranean basin to Asia and Africa. While the Mediterranean region including Turkey, Egypt, Morocco, Algeria and Iran dominated with 61.50% of global output in 1962, by 2022 Asia became the leading producer (50.44%) with Africa growing significantly to 35.87%, while Europe’s share declined to just 8.46%2. Figs demonstrate remarkable adaptability to diverse climatic and soil conditions, enabling widespread cultivation across various geographical regions3. Despite their Mediterranean origins, figs thrive in subtropical to temperate climates, including the Sub-Himalayan tract of Jammu and Kashmir. The species exhibits exceptional resilience, tolerating alkaline, rocky and shallow soils while withstanding temperature extremes from − 12 °C to 38°C3,4. Fruits are valued for both fresh consumption and dried preservation, distinguished by their extensive reservoir of bioactive phytochemicals that confer medicinal and therapeutic properties5. Over the past three decades, figs have drawn increasing scientific attention due to their elevated levels of phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity that are associated with human health promotion5,6. Numerous studies have demonstrated that regular fig consumption can mitigate oxidative stress and lower the risk of chronic degenerative diseases, including cardiovascular ailments and certain cancers7,8. These attributes situate the fig not only as an economically valuable fruit crop but also as a nutritionally functional food with recognized roles in dietary health management9,10. Despite extensive research across Mediterranean and subtropical environments, North-Western India’s mid-Himalayan region remains underexplored for fig adaptation and diversity, even as the Himalaya is recognized for distinctive plant diversity patterns and strong environmental filtering across cold and elevation gradients. The region’s episodic frosts, low winter chill and erratic rainfall present selective pressures likely to foster cold-resilience and stress tolerance traits of strategic value for climate adaptation and expansion of fig cultivation beyond traditional belts. Investigating genetic diversity and identifying native varieties are crucial first steps in preventing genetic erosion and conserving these valuable resources. Without baseline information, these genetic resources risk being underutilised in developing resilient cultivars adapted to local stresses, including cold, drought, rain during ripening and fluctuating phenology. This study assesses the diversity of fig cultivars and local accessions from the mid-Himalayan zones of North-Western India by analyzing morphological, pomological and phytochemical traits. It quantifies variability, identifies key discriminant characters for genotype evaluation and examines trait correlations. and highlights candidate genotypes combining superior fruit quality with cold resilience for genetic conservation and climate-resilient breeding programs.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

In the present investigation, a total of 40 fig genotypes were selected from major fig growing districts, Rajouri, Reasi and Poonch in the Jammu province of Jammu and Kashmir’s Northwestern Himalayas. India. Rajouri, situated at coordinates 33°25’ N, 74°21’ E, has an average elevation of 1,038 m (3,406 feet). Reasi, located at coordinates 32°53’ N, 75°05’ E, has an average elevation of 981 m (3,218 feet). Poonch, situated at coordinates 33°43’ N, 74°08’ E, has an average elevation of 1,205 m (3,953 feet) (Fig. 1). Plant material was collected in compliance with applicable institutional, national (National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources, New Delhi) and international guidelines. Ficus carica L. is assessed as “Least Concern” on the IUCN Red List and is not CITES listed11.

The study area features mountainous terrain with a temperate climate receiving 1225 mm annual precipitation (including 461 mm snowfall). To ensure data traceability, the geographic coordinates and elevations of all sampled trees are provided in the supplementary files along with their corresponding genotype codes. Fig genotypes at these altitudes are subject to abiotic pressure, including frost prone winter with temperatures typically below − 2 °C, orographic rainfall that increases monsoon saturation and elevation-driven gradients that put significant selection pressure on cold-hardy phenology, rain-tolerant fruit characteristics and freeze-thaw resilience. The observed genetic diversity is probably shaped by these abiotic filters, which favour locally adapted accessions. The objective of the survey was to identify promising accessions among a diverse range of fig genotypes and evaluate the variability in their morpho-pomological and biochemical traits.

Morphological and pomological characterization

For the assessment of morpho-pomological traits, samples were systematically surveyed and collected from 40 fig genotypes, with one tree representing each genotype. The experiment was laid out in a Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) with three replications, established by sampling from three canopy directions (East, West, and North-South) on each tree, which served as the replications. Within each replication, 15 fully expanded leaves and 10 mature fruits were collected from the designated tree to comprehensively capture within-tree variability. Fruit harvest was conducted over a period of 60–110 days, corresponding to the main summer crop. The crop initiates ripening in June, peaks from July to August and continues until September. It was harvested at a uniform stage of physiological maturity, determined visually to ensure comparability. The trunk girth (cm) was measured 50 cm above ground level using a measuring tape, while the tree spread (m) was determined as canopy diameter (East-West) during the active growth period, also using a measuring tape. On each selected tree, 10 shoots were tagged with an even distribution across three directions. Variables including terminal bud length (mm), petiole thickness (mm), fruit width (mm), fruit length (mm), ostiole width (mm), fruit stalk length (mm), fruit neck length (mm) and fruit flesh thickness (mm) were measured using a digital vernier caliper (Mitutoyo Inc., Japan). Ostiole form was assessed visually and tactilely, classifying it as flat (ostiolar region level with or slightly recessed below the surrounding fruit surface) or embossed (ostiole noticeably protruding from the fruit surface). Leaf length (cm) was recorded from the petiole base to the lamina tip, while leaf width (cm) was measured at the widest point perpendicular to the leaf length axis13. Both measurements were taken on fully developed leaves using a ruler and average values were calculated. Petiole length (mm) was measured from its point of attachment on the stem to the base of the leaf blade with the help of a scale. Fruit length (mm) was recorded from the fruit base to the tip of the ostiole and fruit width (mm) was measured at the equatorial region of the fruit using a digital vernier caliper (Mitutoyo Inc., Japan). Fruit flesh thickness (mm) was determined by cutting the fruit longitudinally and measuring at multiple points along the cut surface. Fruit weight was recorded at harvest using a digital electronic balance with a precision of 0.01 g (Precisa XB 120 A) and the mean fruit weight was calculated accordingly. The total soluble solids (TSS) content of the pulp was determined using a digital refractometer (Hanna Instruments). For titratable acidity (TA), 10 g of the fruit sample was homogenized, filtered through Whatman No. 1 paper and the clarified juice was titrated against 0.1 N NaOH using phenolphthalein as an indicator. The titration endpoint was identified by the appearance of a persistent light pink colour lasting for at least two seconds14. The observation on qualitative traits was evaluated according to the fig descriptor of International Plant Genetic Resources Institute (IPGRI) and International Centre for Advanced Mediterranean Agronomic Studies (CIHEAM)15.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative parameters were analyzed using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) for a Randomized Complete Block Design, which was performed in IBM-SPSS (Version 26.0) to evaluate variation among genotypes. Descriptive statistics, including minimum, maximum, mean, standard error (SeM), standard deviation (SD), and coefficient of variation (CV%) were computed for all traits. The relationships between genotypes and traits were investigated using multivariate analyses and a scatter plot was constructed based on the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) to visualize genotype distribution using Windowstat version 9.50. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess associations between traits. Finally, cluster analysis was performed using the Ward method on a phenotypic distance matrix to elucidate patterns of variability among genotypes using R-Software (Version 4.5.0, stats package).

Result and discussion

Morpho-pomological

The morphological and pomological traits of 40 fig genotypes exhibited variation ranging from 11.4% to 136.1%, indicating a wide diversity among the individual samples and implying a high breeding potential within the fig population studied. Exceptionally high variation (CV > 100%) was observed in number of lobes (136.1%), the shape of lobes (132.6%) and fruit rib (107.8%), indicating substantial morphological diversity in these leaf and fruit characteristics. High variation (CV 50–100%) was particularly pronounced in fruit-related traits, including fruit pulp colour (57.8%), fruit weight (57.4%), leaf margin dentation (57.0%), fruit width (50.2%), fruit flesh thickness (49.1%) and fruit skin colour (49.0%) (Table 1). These results align with previous studies16–18 confirming that fruit characteristics are the major discriminating traits in fig diversity studies. Moderate variation (CV 20–50%) was observed in the majority of traits, including fruit apex shape (45.5%), fruit neck length (44.19%), fruit stalk length (38.2%), terminal bud colour (37.4%), fruit lenticels quantity (37.5%), ostiole form (36.9%), shape of fruit stalk (35.4%), fruit shape (34.0%), leaf width (31.5%), leaf shape (29.7%), total soluble solids (28.6%), ostiole width (27.7%), tree vigour (27.5%), number of leaves per shoot (26.5%), resistance to ostiole-end cracks (25.8%), petiole length (25.2%), petiole thickness (24.0%), ease of peeling (23.9%), acidity (22.3%), relative degree of branching (22.8%) and terminal bud length (21.7%). Low variation (CV ≤ 20%) was exhibited by tree spread E-W (20.3%), tree height (20.0%) and trunk girth (11.4%), indicating relatively uniform tree architectural characteristics across genotypes. Reports from Moroccan and Eastern Mediterranean collections indicate that structural tree metrics are comparatively uniform, whereas fruit and leaf descriptors drive most phenotypic differentiation19,20.

Variation in morpho-pomological traits among 40 fig genotypes revealed significant variation in tree architecture with tree heights ranging from 2.30 to 5.76 m and trunk girths between 32.83 and 56.47 cm. Tree spread (E-W) varied from 2.41 to 4.97 m (Table 1). These dimensions obtained fall within the range of tree dimensions previously for fig germplasm20,21. Of the observed degree of branching, an intermediate degree was found in 67.50% genotypes including ‘Brown Turkey’, a dense degree in 17.50% genotypes and a sparse degree in 15.00% genotypes (Table 2). Tree vigour was intermediate in 47.50% of genotypes (including ‘Brown Turkey’), high in 32.50% and low in 20.00%, aligning with the moderate vigour and branching reported for Slovenian Figs22. Terminal bud length displayed a moderate range, varying from 5.70 to 11.84 mm. Bud colour was pinkish brown in 47.50% of genotypes, green in 25.50% and light green in 25.00% (including ‘Brown Turkey’).

Leaf dimensions varied considerably, with leaf length ranging from 8.90 to 25.43 cm and leaf width from 7.53 to 23.81 cm. Petiole length demonstrated substantial variation, from 32.63 to 98.20 mm. Petiole thickness varied between 2.43 and 5.45 mm (Table 1). Leaf traits are crucial for evaluating landraces and play a significant role in taxonomic studies17. Our findings are consistent with established ranges for Mediterranean and Irano-Turanian fig germplasm9,23,24. Leaf shape analysis revealed that 57.5% of genotypes possessed unlobed leaves, while 30.0% (including ‘Brown Turkey’) exhibited a base decurrent shape. The base cordate, five-lobed, lobes spatulate forms and base calcarate, lobes linear were each found in 5.0% of genotypes, while base calcarate, lobes latate were found in 2.5% genotypes. Heterophylly was evident in the number of lobes. Unlobed leaves were predominant (57.50% of genotypes), followed by three-lobed leaves in 27.50% (including ‘Brown Turkey’). The five-lobed and seven-lobed leaves were equally represented, each in 7.50%. The observed heterophylly and diversity in leaf shape, with a majority of genotypes possessing unlobed leaves, align with the known morphological plasticity of Ficus carica16,24. Similar ranges in lobe number and shape have been documented as key varietal descriptors in other germplasm collections17,25. Latate was the predominant lobe shape (27.5% of genotypes), followed by spatulate (12.5%) and linear (7.5%) (Table 2). Notably, the lyrate lobe shape often a distinctive marker in other Ficus subgenera was absent, suggesting a degree of morphological convergence within this specific germplasm.

Leaf density analysis revealed that the majority of genotypes (62.50%, including ‘Brown Turkey’) possessed > 12 leaves per shoot, while 27.5% had 9–12 leaves per shoot, closely aligning with the reported average of 11.2 leaves per shoot26. Only 10% of genotypes exhibited < 8 leaves per shoot, indicating a strong tendency toward high leaf density in this germplasm collection. Completely dentate margins were most prevalent (45.0%, including ‘Brown Turkey’), followed by upper-margin dentation (22.5%), entire margins (20.0%) and fully dentate lobes (12.5%) (Table 2). These patterns correspond with the finding that documented predominantly completely dentate margins in wild fig accessions27.

Fruit traits exhibited high variability, reflecting a broad genetic base. The fruit weight ranged from 6.80 to 45.20 g (Table 1). These values are consistent with the diversity found in Mediterranean landraces, though they represent a smaller-fruited range compared to specific cultivars like ‘Zidi’ (96.45 g)28. Fruit width exhibited a wide spectrum (13.02 to 55.93 mm), aligning with previously reported dimensions for both wild and cultivated populations20,21,28. When categorized, the germplasm was predominantly small (28–38 mm, 40.00%), followed by very small (< 28 mm, 22.50%), medium (38–49 mm, 20.00%), and large (50–60 mm, 17.50%); no accessions were classified as very large (> 60 mm). Internal quality traits also showed substantial variation. Flesh thickness varied from 5.62 to 27.57 mm. These findings align with previous researches29,30 and reflect the broad phenotypic diversity observed in North African populations, particularly in Tunisia18,31. Variation in flesh thickness is a critical determinant of fruit physiology and commercial utility. Consequently, thick-fleshed genotypes are prioritized for the fresh market due to their superior textural firmness. Additionally, this robust structure acts as a mechanical buffer, enhancing postharvest shelf-life and reducing susceptibility to transport-related damage and microbial infection32.

Furthermore, no fruit ribs were observed in 50.0% genotypes including ‘Brown Turkey’, intermediate ribs in 32.5% genotypes and prominent ribs in 17.5% genotypes. The truncated fruit apex form was most frequent (47.5%), followed by the rounded form (32.5%, including ‘Brown Turkey’). For fruit size, small (28–38 mm) was the most prevalent (37.5%), followed by medium (38–49 mm) (25.0%), large (50–60 mm) (22.5%) and very small (18–28 mm) (15.0%). For fruit skin colour, purple was the most prevalent (22.5%), followed closely by red (20.0%). Yellow-green and black were each observed in 15.0% genotypes. Brown occurred in 12.5%, while green (10.0%) and light green (5.0%) were less common (Table 2). The dominance of dark-coloured skins (purple, red, black) reflects anthocyanin accumulation, a pigment enhanced by sun exposure and physiological maturity9. The predominance of dark-colored skins (purple, red and black) is indicative of increased anthocyanin accumulation, a stress-responsive pigment that is brought on by exposure to monsoon rain, high-altitude UV radiation and intermittent frosts in mid-Himalayan locations. These anthocyanins improve fruit resilience and indicate superior adaptation in locally selected genotypes by protecting against oxidative damage from freeze-thaw cycles and excessive rainfall during ripening33. Amber dominated pulp colouration (37.5%), followed by red (20.0%). White and pink each account for (17.5%) and (15.0%) respectively, while dark red appeared in the least frequent (10.0%) (Table 2). This distribution, particularly amber prevalence, aligns with a previous report34, though it differs from another study, which reported yellow as predominant in Iranian accessions, suggesting geographic or varietal influences35. Overall, fruit skin and pulp colour, combined with other morphological features, serve as key criteria for identifying promising genotypes in breeding programs and indicate fruit quality and desirability as noted in earlier studies on edible Fig36,31.

The scarce fruit lenticels were observed in 55.00% genotypes, intermediate quantity in 20.00% genotypes and numerous lenticels in 12.50% genotypes including ‘Brown Turkey’, while a complete absence was observed in 12.50%. Of the observed fruit shapes, globose was predominant (55.00%, including ‘Brown Turkey’), while oblong and oblate shapes each occurred in 22.50% of genotypes. The dominance of globose fruit aligns with previous studies reporting 70% prevalence and is considered optimal for efficient handling, packaging and shipment due to their compact geometry and balanced symmetry30,37. The shape of fresh fig fruit plays a crucial role in both canning and fresh market distribution, as it directly affects packing efficiency and mechanical stability during transit. Varieties bearing spherical or oblong fruits are preferred for canning because of their compact, neckless structure, while oblate types are well suited for standardized packaging systems used in long-distance shipments26.

Concerning the fruit stalk shape, long and slender fruit stalk in 57.5% genotypes including ‘Brown Turkey’, variously enlarged stalk in 22.5% genotypes and a short and thick stalk in 17.5% genotypes. Fruit stalk length ranged from 5.47 mm to 36.73 mm, while fruit neck length varied from 0.07 mm to 11.27 mm (Table 1). The stalk-to-neck length ratio (stalk: neck), which ranges from 0.76 to 26.89, is essential for minimizing fruit damage during harvest because longer ratios reduce tearing stress28. Short stalks paired with moderate necks increase the risk of neck cracking and skin rupturing. Such damage facilitates latex exudation and creates entry points for post-harvest pathogens. High-ratio genotypes, on the other hand, provide better protection, which aligns with findings on Iranian fig accessions35,38.

Ostiole width, a critical trait for fruit quality and storability, ranged from 2.12 to 6.21 mm (Table 1). Narrower ostioles are preferred because they enhance storage potential and reduce susceptibility to infectious agents32. Narrower ostioles physically restrict fungal ingress by pathogens such as Botrytis cinerea and Aspergillus smut, as well as moisture accumulation within the syconium cavity. This substantially lowers decay incidence during cold storage compared to wider openings39. Ostiole size correlates strongly with fungal decay incidence and poor fruit quality40. Among the evaluated genotypes, 72.5% exhibited an embossed ostiole form, while 27.5% showed a flat form. The majority of genotypes (65.00%) demonstrated a medium ease of peeling, while 20.00% were easy (including ‘Brown Turkey’) and 15.00% were difficult. Ostiole-end crack resistance emerges as a key selection criterion in fig breeding for improved fruit quality and storability. Among the evaluated genotypes, 55.0% demonstrated resistance, 27.5% showed intermediate resistance and 17.5% (including ‘Brown Turkey’) exhibited susceptibility (Table 2). Even slight splits serve as entry points for postharvest pathogens, accelerating decay and consequently reducing postharvest shelf-life under cold storage conditions. This vulnerability intensifies under rain-prone ripening conditions common in mid-Himalayan sites, where regulated deficit irrigation has proven effective in minimising splits via reduced fruit turgor39.

Fruit quality parameters exhibited substantial variation among genotypes. The majority of genotypes (37.50%) demonstrated a medium TSS level (13.1–16.0°Brix), followed by low (32.5%, 10.0–13.0 °Brix), high (27.0%, 16.1–20.0 °Brix) and very high (2.5%, > 20.0 °Brix). Total soluble solids (TSS), an indicator of fruit sweetness, ranged from 7.01 to 21.03 ºBrix, while acidity levels varied from 0.10 to 0.40%, contributing to diverse flavor profiles. These findings align with similar TSS ranges reported in previous studies20,21,37,41,42(Table 1). The ratio of TSS acidity is a key determinant of fruit palatability and consumer acceptance, as it reflects the sugar-acid balance influencing taste30. The wide variation indicates potential for selecting genotypes with optimal sugar-acid balance for varied market and processing needs. Correlation between variables

The Pearson correlation analysis revealed significant positive and negative associations among the 35 morphological and pomological traits studied (Fig. 3). Leaf length was strongly positively correlated with leaf width (r = 0.92) and petiole thickness (r = 0.71), while moderately correlated with petiole length (r = 0.52) and terminal bud length (r = 0.34). Correlation of leaf width with the petiole thickness aligns with the previous finding35. The number of lobes per leaf (NuLo) exhibited a moderate negative correlation with leaf margin dentation (LfMgDn) (r = -0.49), consistent with coordinated patterning of lobation and marginal features in fig leaves43. The number of leaves per shoot (NuLfSt) was positively correlated with the tree vigour (r = 0.50) and relative degree of branching (ReDBr) (r = 0.33). The trees with a more branched architecture present a greater number of nodes, which are the sites of leaf initiation, which is in agreement with previous studies in edible and caprifig Figs. 31. The existence of close positive correlation among traits indicates that more leaf expansion leads to stronger aerial growth18. The relative degree of branching was moderately positively correlated with terminal bud length (r = 0.46), tree spread (r = 0.35). The correlation of branching density with tree spread aligns with the finding that increased lateral bud activity directly promotes canopy expansion and overall vegetative volume30. Fruit weight demonstrated exceptionally strong positive correlations with fruit width (r = 0.97) and fruit flesh thickness (r = 0.97), moderate correlations with fruit shape (r = 0.48) and ostiole width (r = 0.44), but was negatively correlated with total soluble solids (r = − 0.30) and ease of peeling (r = -0.39), consistent with previous findings in Fig. 26. The positive correlation of resistance to ostiole end cracking with TSS (r = 0.65) and fruit neck length (r = 0.55), which may be accompanied by osmotic buffering in which high soluble solid lowers the osmotic potential of the pulp. This acts as a physiological buffer that prevents rapid water influx during high humidity events30,43,44. Total soluble solids (TSS) showed a strong negative correlation with acidity (r = -0.73), reflecting the classic sugar-acid trade-off during fruit ripening. This antagonistic relationship is physiologically explained by the fact that, as ripening progresses, soluble sugars accumulate while organic acids (primarily malate and citrate) are progressively metabolized or diluted within the expanding fruit tissues, leading to concomitant increases in sweetness and decreases in acidity45,46. TSS was also negatively correlated with fruit weight (r = − 0.30), fruit width (r = − 0.26) and fruit flesh thickness (r = 0.28), indicating the yield-quality dilution effect. This pattern is consistent with the dilution mechanism described for fleshy fruits, where a finite assimilate supply is distributed into progressively larger, more water rich fruits so that sugar concentration per unit fresh weight declines as fruit size increase, as reported for multiple species47. This trade-off suggests that selecting genotypes with larger fruits may reduce sweetness, so balancing fruit size and TSS should be a key breeding goal for high quality. These results demonstrate that the evaluated traits play a consistent role in identifying cultivars with planting potential and in germplasm assessment. Correlation coefficients provide valuable guidance for selecting the most informative traits in genotype evaluation31.

Principal component analysis

The principal component analysis (PCA) revealed ten principal components that explained 80.12% of cumulative variance (Table 3). PC1 accounted for 26.16% of total variance and was primarily associated with fruit and leaf size-related traits, including fruit weight (0.308), fruit width (0.301), fruit flesh thickness (0.306), leaf length (0.297), leaf width (0.296), petiole length (0.241), petiole thickness (0.241) and total soluble solids (0.234). PC2 explained 12.59% of variance and loaded mainly on vegetative architecture traits, with strong negative contributions from leaf margin dentation (-0.351), relative degree of branching (-0.269), number of leaves per shoot (-0.260) and tree height (-0.213). These characteristics were the most useful for distinguishing and identifying the genotypes under study. Based on the scatter plot generated using PC1 and PC2, the genotypes were distributed across four distinct quadrants, revealing clear morphological groupings among the fig genotypes (Fig. 4). Specifically, Quadrant I was characterized by elevated TSS, alongside greater tree height (Tr Ht) and increased petiole length (PlLe) with genotypes Sel-17, Sel-11, Sel-10, Sel-25 and ‘Brown Turkey’. Quadrant II included genotypes Sel-34, Sel-30 and Sel-32, which were distinguished by traits including high fruit lenticel quantity (FrLcQ) and long, slender fruit stalk morphology (ShFrSk). While elevated lenticel density facilitates gas exchange, it heightens the risks of russeting and microcracking. Research in pome fruits indicates that lenticel hydration can significantly increase their susceptibility to pathogen invasion48. The third Quadrant had Sel-36, Sel-37, Sel-22 and Sel-19 genotypes, associated with higher fruit acidity (Ac) and an embossed ostiole form (OtFm). Quadrant IV was dominated by genotypes Sel-14, Sel-15, Sel-38 and Sel-24, showing the strongest positive loadings on fruit weight (FrWt), fruit width (FrWi) and fruit shape (FrSh). These genotypes also associate with narrower ostiole width (OtWi) and elevated resistance to ostiolar end-cracking (RtOtEdCr), indicating that tighter ostioles confer tolerance to pre and post-harvest splitting for improved marketability. The first two principal components explained 38.75% of the variance, positioning Sel-10, Sel-11, Sel-14 and Sel-17 as elite multi-trait selections. For a targeted breeding program, high-sugar, vigorous genotypes from Quadrant I (Sel-17) and large-fruited, crack-resistant genotypes from Quadrant IV (Sel-14) present a promising approach to pyramid these desirable traits. Consistent with Algerian Fig37, the multivariate structure captured most variability (61.80% in the first three PCs) and clustering resolved compact cultivar groups, indicating a relatively narrow genetic base in some subsets. These results corroborate earlier findings that pomological traits serve as the primary discriminators in fig diversity and breeding assessments25,49. Similar applications of PCA have been previously reported in Moroccan Fig.30, Turkish Fig28 and Iranian Fig50. The selection of highly discriminant variables is essential for optimizing resources in morphological assessment51,52.

Ward cluster analysis

The application of PCA and cluster analysis revealed significant variation among the fig genotypes. This observed phenotypic diversity may be attributed to factors such as heterogeneity, genetic background of the populations, environmental and developmental traits16,32. In the present study, the Ward method was used to perform a cluster analysis of 35 morphological and pomological traits across 40 fig genotypes. The analysis grouped the genotypes into two main clusters (Fig. 5) as evidenced by the maximum pairwise distance between Cluster I and Cluster II. Cluster I comprised 22 genotypes, while Cluster II consisted of 18 genotypes. The Cluster I was further subdivided into two sub-clusters. Sub-cluster IA containing 12 genotypes (including genotypes with red and pink colouration patterns) and Sub-cluster IB with 10 genotypes (primarily characterized by purple and black fruit skin colour). Cluster II also showed subdivision into Sub-cluster IIA with 8 genotypes (mainly green and yellow-green types) and Sub-cluster IIB containing 10 genotypes (including ‘Brown Turkey’ and genotypes with intermediate morphological characteristics). The clustering pattern reflects a biologically meaningful divergence primarily driven by anthocyanin expression, which is often linked to adaptive responses to high altitude stressors like UV-B radiation53,54. The clear separation between the dark-skinned and the green-skinned genotypes suggests a strong genetic divergence in the secondary metabolite pathways, which influences the skin pigmentation. Crossing parents from the highly distinct Cluster I and Cluster II could facilitate maximize genetic recombination55, the development of hybrids that combine the robust phytochemical profile of dark skinned figs with the superior fruit geometry of commercial types in Cluster II. Comparable clustering patterns have been documented in other fig germplasm studies. For instance, Moroccan fig accessions were grouped into two main clusters using morphological traits, highlighting strong structuring based on fruit and leaf characters56. Similarly, high morphological variability has been reported among Tunisian figs, where cluster analysis separates cultivars largely according to fruit size and peel colour17. More recently, the PCA and Ward clustering on fruit characters of Iranian figs resolved two principal clusters, confirming that fruit geometry and colour are among the most discriminating traits in fig diversity analyses35.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of morphological and pomological diversity among the fig genotypes collected from Jammu and Kashmir, revealing broad phenotypic variation with substantial breeding potential. The study identified elite genotypes for distinct breeding objectives. SKJFRa (Sel-14) excelled in fruit quality and storability, with superior fruit size, flesh thickness, a narrow ostiole and high resistance to ostiole end cracking. In contrast, SKJFRa (Sel-17) was a high sugar, vigorous type with significantly higher soluble solids and greater tree vigour. Analyses of trait relationships provided crucial insights for selection strategies, notably revealing a key trade-off between fruit size and sugar content that must be navigated in future breeding programs. Furthermore, the prevalence of traits like dark fruit skin in the collection suggests local adaptation to high altitude stress, marking this germplasm as a valuable source of resilience alleles. Given its rich variability and adaptive potential of this germplasm, establishing an ex-situ collection is crucial for genetic conservation. Future efforts should prioritize molecular characterization through marker analysis to validate phenotypic diversity, understand genetic structure, estimate true genetic distances and identify marker-trait associations. This must be complemented by multi-location trials to verify trait stability and targeted validation of cold tolerance to support the breeding of climate-resilient, high-quality cultivars, enabling the expansion of fig cultivation into temperate Himalayan regions.
