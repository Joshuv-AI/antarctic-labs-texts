Lutein augments the hypoglycemic effect of metformin through the gut microbiota-imidazole propionate axis in type 2 diabetic model mice
Authors: Kai Liu, Yue Han, Jingbin Zhang, Zhongwei Yin, Hongjia Yan, Wei Gao, Huixin Tong, Haiwei Zhu, Suocheng Hui, Linlang Liang, Hao Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur objective was to investigate the potential of lutein in enhancing the efficacy of metformin in diabetes treatment and the underlying mechanisms involved. Here, the study was conducted with type 2 diabetic mice, which was induced by a high-fat diet with streptozotocin. Twenty-four diabetic model mice were divided into four groups: model control group, metformin group, lutein group, and lutein combined with metformin (Lut_Met) group. Six C57BL/6 mice were assigned to the normal control group and provided a normal chow diet. First, we found that the blood glucose concentration, area of the curve of the oral glucose tolerance test, and homeostasis model assessment for insulin resistance and imidazole propionate (ImP) levels of the Lut_Met group were significantly lower than those of the metformin group at the 8th week. Additionally, a significant positive correlation was observed between the reduction in blood glucose and the ImP levels in the Lut_Met group. The Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio in the Lut_Met group was significantly lower than that in the model control and metformin groups. In addition, the abundance of Eggerthella lenta in the Lut_Met group was significantly lower than that in the metformin group and showed a positive relationship with ImP levels. Moreover, compared with the metformin treatment, Lut_Met intervention had a significant impact on the AMPK signaling pathway. Further western blot analyses revealed that the ratio of phosphorylated AMPK/β-Actin to AMPK/β-Actin in the Lut_Met group was significantly greater than that in the metformin group. In conclusion, lutein has the potential to enhance the favorable effects of metformin on blood glucose metabolism and insulin resistance in a mouse model of diabetes. Metformin is currently the most commonly prescribed oral medication for lowering blood glucose levels in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)1. However, metformin monotherapy has a high rate of treatment failure due to individual variations in responses to the therapy2. Recent studies have shown that elevated levels of imidazole propionate (ImP) may contribute to the reduced efficacy of metformin in diabetes treatment3,4. The circulating levels of ImP in patients with T2DM are significantly greater than those in individuals without T2DM4. Furthermore, metformin treatment further promotes the production of ImP4. An increase in ImP levels not only leads to increased insulin resistance but also diminishes the blood glucose-lowering impact of metformin3,5. ImP has been identified as an inhibitor of metformin treatment3,6. Thus, ImP could serve as a target for improving metformin action to provide potential therapeutic opportunities for individuals with diabetes who exhibit an inadequate response to metformin treatment.

ImP is a microbially produced histidine derived metabolite5. Histidine degradation pathways leading to the production of urocanate is well conserved in bacteria6. The synthesis of ImP is primarily dependent on the activity of the bacterial enzyme UrdA, which catalyzes the production of ImP from urocanate5,7,8. In addition, the production of ImP is closely associated with dietary factors4. Antonio Molinaro et al. conducted a study to examine the relationship between typical dietary factors and the levels of ImP4. The study findings indicated that insufficient nut consumption in the diet is linked to elevated ImP levels4. Previous evidence in humans suggests that pistachios have the ability to decrease the bacteria positively associated with ImP and increase the bacteria negatively associated with ImP9. Thus, we assume that pistachio can decrease ImP. However, other nuts do not show a regulatory impact on the gut microbiota that may affects ImP levels10,11. The beneficial nutrients in nuts mainly include unsaturated fatty acids, dietary fiber, and phytochemicals12. Pistachios do not significantly differ from other nuts in terms of unsaturated fatty acid and dietary fiber content12. Therefore, the regulatory effect of pistachios on the ImP-related gut microbiota may be attributed to the presence of phytochemicals in pistachios. Lutein is the specific phytochemical responsible for distinguishing pistachios from other nuts. The lutein content in pistachios is 31 to 2900 times greater than that in other nuts12. The above information suggests that lutein has the potential to reduce the levels of ImP, and may in turn improve the hypoglycemic efficacy of metformin.

Lutein, a dietary carotenoid belonging to the xanthophyll group, cannot be synthesized by the human body, and the primary source of lutein intake is through the diet or dietary supplements13. Lutein is abundant in plant-based sources, such as dark green leafy vegetables and marigold flowers14,15. Marigold flowers are the main natural source of commercial lutein supplements14. Research studies involving human subjects have revealed a negative correlation between blood lutein levels and the risk of T2DM16. Furthermore, experimental animal and in vitro studies have shown that lutein can ameliorate impaired glucose tolerance17–20. Given the glucose-lowering effect of lutein and its potential to reduce the levels of ImP, it is hypothesized that the combination of lutein and metformin may lead to improved therapeutic outcomes for individuals with T2DM. Notably, there is currently a lack of research on the combined effects of lutein and metformin for the treatment of T2DM. Here, we conducted an animal study to investigate the potential of lutein to enhance the efficacy of metformin in diabetes treatment and to elucidate the underlying mechanism involved.

Materials and methods

Animals and treatments

Male C57BL/6 mice, aged 4 weeks, were obtained from HuaFuKang Bioscience (Beijing, China). The mice were individually housed and acclimated to the experimental conditions for seven days before the study began. The mice were housed in an environment within a temperature range of 22 to 24 ℃, with a relative humidity of 50 to 65%. They were maintained under a 12-hour light/dark cycle and given unrestricted access to food and water. Metformin hydrochloride with purities exceeding 97% was acquired from Macklin Biochemical Technology (Shanghai, China). Lutein, with a purity exceeding 90%, was purchased from Jingzhu Biotechnology (Nanjing, China). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The animal experiments were reported following the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Additionally, the protocol was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of General Hospital of Northern Theater Command.

Experimental design

Determination of metformin, lutein, and their combined effects on high-fat diet and streptozotocin-induced T2DM in mice.

The T2DM mouse model was established following a previously reported protocol with slight modifications21. Three doses of lutein have been tested and detailed experimental design is presented in Supplementary Methods. Both medium dose and high dose of lutein had more significant beneficial effects on the diabetic model mice compared with low dose of lutein (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, there were no significant differences in treatment effects of lutein between the medium dose group and high dose group (Supplementary Fig. 1). Therefore, we used the medium dose of lutein (4 mg/kg/day) for our latter experiments. In addition, the intervention doses of lutein and metformin were also evaluated based on the surface area of the mice which calculated by the Meeh-Rubner formula22. The doses of lutein (4 mg/kg/day) and metformin (200 mg/kg/day) for mice used in this study were approximately equivalent to 20 mg and 1000 mg daily intake of lutein and metformin in a 60 kg person, respectively22. Six C57BL/6 mice in the NC group were orally administered a control vehicle and provided a normal chow diet (containing 10% kcal from fat; D12450B, Research Diets) throughout the experimental period. Thirty-two C57BL/6J mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD; 60% kcal from fat; D12492, Research Diets). After 4 weeks of HFD treatment, all mice were fasted overnight and then administered a single injection of 100 mg/kg streptozotocin (STZ; Sigma Aldrich, MO, U.S.) to induce the T2DM mouse model. After a 72-hour period post injection, only mice displaying a glucose level equal to or exceeding 11.1 mmol/L were included in the study (n = 24).

Twenty-four diabetic mice induced by a combination of HFD and STZ were divided into four groups. Over a period of 8 weeks, the mice were orally administered various agents in addition to the HFD. The groups were as follows: (1) Model Control (MC) group, where mice (n = 6) received a control vehicle; (2) Metformin (Met) group, where mice (n = 6) were given metformin at a dosage of 200 mg/kg/day; (3) Lutein (Lut) group, where mice (n = 6) were administered lutein at a dosage of 4 mg/kg/day; and (4) Lutein combined with metformin (Lut_Met) group, where mice (n = 6) were given lutein and metformin at dosages of 4 mg/kg/day and 200 mg/kg/day, respectively. At the end of study, tissue samples were collected promptly after the mice were euthanized by administering sodium pentobarbital. These samples were then rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and preserved at −80 °C until further analysis. Additionally, fecal samples were collected for the purpose of sequencing the gut microbiota.

Determination of the contribution of the gut microbiota to the antidiabetic effects of metformin combined with lutein.

Eighteen HFD + STZ co-induced T2DM mice were randomly divided into three groups, namely, the MC, Met, and Lut_Met groups, with six mice in each group. The treatment protocols followed those outlined in the initial phase of the experiment. Following an 8-week treatment period, all mice received antibiotic cocktails (Abx; Solarbio, Beijing, China) consisting of vancomycin (0.5 g/L), ampicillin (1 g/L), metronidazole (1 g/L), and neomycin sulfate (1 g/L) for 4 weeks to eliminate gut microbiota.

Glucose and insulin tolerance tests

The glucose and insulin tolerance tests were conducted following the methodologies outlined in our previous study with minor modifications23,24. After a 6-hour fasting period, the mice were orally administered glucose at a dosage of 2.0 g/kg body weight. Blood samples were collected from the tail vein at specific time intervals (0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min) for the measurement of blood glucose levels using a blood glucose meter. Mice that had fasted for 6 h were injected with insulin (2.0 units/kg body weight for the diabetic mice, and 1.0 units/kg body weight for the normal mice) into the peritoneal cavity. Blood glucose levels were assessed in tail blood samples at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min post insulin administration. We assessed the area under the curve (AUC) of the glucose or insulin tolerance test at the end of the treatment period.

Biochemical analysis

Fasting insulin levels were measured using enzymatic assay kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Homeostasis assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated using the following formula: HOMA-IR = [glucose (mmol/L) × insulin (µIU/mL)]/22.5. ImP was quantified using ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry, following previously reported methods with minor modifications5. Plasma samples were extracted using four volumes of ice-cold methanol/acetonitrile mixture (v/v = 1:1). The samples (5 µL) were injected onto an Agilent Poroshell 120 HILIC-Z column (100 mm × 2.1 mm with 2.7 μm particles; Waters, MA, U.S.) and separated using a gradient consisting of acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (A-phase) and a 10 mmol/L ammonium acetate solution with 0.1% formic acid (B-phase). Mass spectrometric analysis was performed using a QTRAP 5500 system (Sciex, MA, U.S.). ImP was quantitatively determined using multiple reaction monitoring in positive electrospray ionization mode (m/z: 141.3/81.0).

Metagenomic sequencing, and taxonomic and functional annotation

The gut microbiota was sequenced and analyzed using established bioinformatic protocols23. Specifically, bacterial genomic DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the PF Mag-Bind Stool DNA Kit (Omega Biotek, GA, U.S.). Metagenomic sequencing was conducted using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc, CA, USA), and taxonomic classification of nonredundant genes was achieved by alignment with the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) NR database using DIAMOND software (version 2.0.13). Functional annotation of nonredundant genes was also conducted. The metagenomic sequencing data associated with this study have been archived in the NCBI Short Read Archive database (accession number: PRJNA1093369).

Western blot analysis

Protein extraction and western blot analyses were conducted following the methodology outlined in a previous study23. In summary, proteins were extracted utilizing tissue lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) supplemented with proteases and the PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitor (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, the proteins were separated on SDS‒PAGE gels, transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore Corporation, MA, U.S.), and subjected to overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C. Densitometric analysis was carried out using Quantity One software (BIO-RAD, CA, U.S.). The primary antibodies used in this study were anti-AMPK (Cell Signaling Technology, MA, U.S.) and anti-phospho-AMPK (T172) (Cell Signaling Technology, MA, U.S.).

Statistical analysis

The differences in fasting insulin, AUC of oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), AUC of insulin tolerance test (ITT), HOMA-IR, ImP, and protein expression among the different groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s test. The analysis of datasets such as fasting glucose and body weight data was conducted using two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test. Bioinformatic analysis of the gut microbiota was conducted using the Majorbio Cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). Based on the read number information, alpha diversity indices were calculated using Mothur v1.30.2. The microbial community resemblance across various samples was assessed through principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) using Bray‒Curtis dissimilarity as the metric with the assistance of the Vegan v2.4.3 software package. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was used to identify taxa that were significantly more abundant across different groups. Differential analysis was conducted at various levels‒taxonomic, functional, and genewise‒using the Kruskal‒Wallis test with false discovery rate (FDR) corrections for comparisons involving three or more groups. In cases where two groups were compared, the Mann‒Whitney U test was employed. The correlations between gut microbiota and clinical parameters (glucose and ImP) were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation. Statistical analyses and graphical representations were generated using SPSS (v18.0, IBM, USA) and GraphPad Prism (v10.0, GraphPad, USA).

Results

The combination of Metformin and lutein had a superior effect on glucose homeostasis compared to either treatment alone

Figure 1A shows the schema of groups and treatments of the mice. No significant intergroup differences in body weight were observed during the study period (Fig. 1B). The fasting glucose levels are shown in Fig. 1C. The initial blood glucose levels of the NC group were significantly lower than those of the other groups at the beginning of the study. There were no statistically significant differences in fasting blood glucose levels between the MC group and the treatment groups at week 0. After 4 weeks, the blood glucose levels of the Met, Lut, and Lut_Met groups were significantly lower than those of the MC group. However, at week 4, the blood glucose levels of the Lut_Met group did not significantly differ from those of the Met and Lut groups. By week 8, the blood glucose levels of the Met, Lut, and Lut_Met groups were significantly lower than those of the MC group. Notably, the blood glucose levels of the Lut_Met group were also significantly lower than those of the Met and Lut groups at this time point.

As shown in Fig. 1D, at week 8 of the treatment phase, the MC group had the highest AUC, while the NC group had the lowest total AUC. Comparisons of the between-group differences revealed that at week 8, the AUC in the MC model group was significantly greater than that in the NC group. Furthermore, the total AUC was significantly lower in the Met, Lut, and Lut_Met groups than in the MC group. While no significant difference was found between the Met and Lut groups, the Lut_Met group exhibited a significantly lower total AUC than both the Met and Lut groups.

The combined use of Metformin and lutein had a more significant effect on insulin resistance than either treatment alone

Figure 1E shows the fasting insulin levels of each group in the eighth week. The MC group exhibited significantly greater fasting insulin levels than did the NC group. Conversely, the Met, Lut, and Lut_Met groups exhibited significantly lower fasting insulin levels than did the MC group. However, there were no significant differences in fasting insulin levels among the Met, Lut, and Lut_Met groups.

The results of the ITT in the eighth week for each group are shown in Fig. 1F. Inter-group difference analysis revealed that in the eighth week, the AUC of the MC group was significantly greater than that in the NC group. Furthermore, the total AUC of the Met, Lut, and Lut_Met groups was significantly lower than that of the MC group. No significant intergroup differences were detected in the Met, Lut, or Lut_Met groups.

Figure 1G shows the HOMA-IR index values for each group in the eighth week. The HOMA-IR value was significantly greater in the MC group than in the NC group. In contrast, the HOMA-IR values of the Met, Lut, and Lut_Met groups were significantly lower than those of the MC group. Additionally, the HOMA-IR value of the Lut_Met group was significantly lower than that of both the Met and Lut groups.

Different treatments modified the dysbiosis of gut microbiota

The fecal samples were collected and sequenced at the end of the treatment phase. Figure 2A shows that there was no significant difference in the Chao1 index among the groups at week 8. Cluster analysis of the species-level gut microbiota in each group revealed significant differences in beta diversity, as shown in Fig. 2B. Analysis of the gut microbiota at the phylum level indicated that the abundances of Firmicutes and Bacteroidota were the greatest and had greater functional contribution in each group, as shown in Fig. 2C and D. Furthermore, Fig. 2E shows that the abundance of Firmicutes was significantly greater in the MC group than in the NC group, and the abundance of Firmicutes in the Lut and Lut_Met groups was significantly lower than that in the MC group. Additionally, the abundance of Firmicutes in the Lut_Met group was significantly lower than that in the Met and Lut groups. Moreover, Fig. 2F shows that the abundance of the phylum Bacteroidota in the Lut and Lut_Met groups was significantly greater than that in the MC group. Additionally, the abundance of the phylum Bacteroidota in the Lut_Met group was significantly greater than that in the Met and Lut groups. Finally, as shown in Fig. 2G, the Firmicutes/Bacteroidota (F/B) ratio in the Lut_Met group was significantly lower than that in the MC and Met groups.

Lutein enhanced the beneficial effects of Metformin on glucose control through the gut microbiota-ImP axis and AMPK signaling pathway

The levels of ImP for each group in the 8th week are shown in Fig. 5A. The ImP level in the MC group was significantly greater than that in the NC group. Furthermore, the Met group had significantly greater ImP levels than did the MC group. Conversely, the levels of ImP in the Lut and Lut_Met groups were lower than those in the MC and Met groups. Additionally, there was no statistically significant difference in ImP levels between the Lut and Lut_Met groups. A correlation analysis was conducted to investigate the relationship between changes in blood glucose and ImP levels (Fig. 5A). The analysis revealed a significant inverse correlation between the decrease in blood glucose and the levels of ImP in the Met group (R2 = 0.85, P < 0.05). Additionally, a significant positive correlation was observed between the reduction in blood glucose and the ImP levels in the Lut and Lut_Met intervention groups (Lut group: R2 = 0.74, P < 0.05; Lut_Met group: R2 = 0.69, P < 0.05).

Discussion

This study revealed that both lutein and metformin have beneficial effects on fasting blood glucose levels, glucose tolerance, and HOMA-IR in a diabetic mouse model. The combined intervention of lutein and metformin had a more potent effect on reducing blood glucose levels and alleviating insulin resistance in T2DM model mice than the individual interventions of lutein and metformin. This may be related to the combined intervention of lutein and metformin altering the gut microbiota structure and ImP levels. Further analyses revealed that the reduction in ImP levels caused by the combined intervention of lutein and metformin may be associated with a decrease in the abundance of the bacterium Eggerthella lenta, which is known for its ability to generate ImP. Additionally, combined treatment with lutein and metformin had a more significant beneficial effect on the AMPK pathway and AMPK phosphorylation levels than treatment with metformin alone.

We found that lutein has the potential to reduce elevated blood glucose levels, alleviate impaired glucose tolerance and insulin resistance, and increase insulin sensitivity in a mouse model of diabetes. A previous study involving 5488 individuals revealed a significant negative correlation between blood lutein levels and the risk of developing T2DM16. Various animal studies have indicated that lutein can ameliorate the imbalance in blood glucose metabolism and impaired glucose tolerance in T2DM mice17,19,20. Furthermore, in vitro studies have shown that lutein can decrease high blood glucose concentrations in HepG2 cells by influencing mitochondrial biogenesis18. Consistent with previous research findings25,26the current study demonstrated a notable therapeutic impact of metformin in a mouse model of diabetes. During the 8-week treatment period, a slight decreasing trend in blood glucose reduction was observed in the metformin group over the latter 4 weeks compared to the initial 4 weeks. This outcome indicates a potential decline in the blood glucose-lowering efficacy of metformin toward the end of the intervention period, consistent with findings from previous animal studies that have also reported a decrease in the effectiveness of metformin over time27. Conversely, the combined treatment group receiving both lutein and metformin did not exhibit a decrease in efficacy; instead, it showed enhanced effects in reducing blood glucose levels and ameliorating insulin resistance in diabetic mice compared to metformin monotherapy. Notably, there is a lack of research on the combined administration of lutein and metformin, highlighting the necessity for additional validation of the effect of the combined intervention on blood glucose regulation through larger-scale randomized controlled trials.

Additionally, this study indicated that the combined administration of lutein and metformin may offer a more effective therapeutic outcome than metformin alone. This effect is potentially attributed to their influence on the gut microbiota composition in diabetic mice. This study indicated that metformin, lutein, and Lut_Met treatments can induce significant changes in the beta diversity of the gut microbiota in T2DM model mice. Analysis of the gut microbiota revealed that the reduced beta diversity may be associated with the composition of the Firmicutes and Bacteroidota microbiota. Integration of the findings from blood glucose analysis suggest that the enhanced blood glucose-lowering effect observed in the Lut_Met group could be linked to its higher beta diversity and lower Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio. A decrease in beta diversity and an increase in the Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio may contribute to the onset and progression of T2DM. Previous studies in humans have demonstrated significantly lower gut microbiota beta diversity in individuals with diabetes than in individuals without diabetes28. Several animal studies have also revealed a significantly greater Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio in diabetic mice than in control mice29,30. Furthermore, our study revealed a lower abundance of Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus saprophyticus in the Lut_Met group than in the MC group. Staphylococcus aureus is commonly identified as the predominant pathogen in diabetic foot infections31. Untreated Staphylococcus saprophyticus infections may progress to pyelonephritis, which can subsequently lead to renal insufficiency32. Moreover, we observed greater levels of various species, including Firmicutes bacterium and Bacteroides sp. (data not shown), in the Lut_Met group than in the Met group. Previous research has indicated that individuals with diabetes exhibit gut microbiota dysbiosis characterized by reduced levels of Firmicutes bacterium33.

Moreover, the current study revealed that compared with NC mice, T2DM model mice exhibit elevated levels of ImP. Following metformin intervention, the ImP levels in T2DM model mice are expected to increase further, and lutein may ameliorate the increase in ImP levels induced by metformin treatment and the progression of T2DM. An increase in ImP levels not only contributes to increased insulin resistance but also diminishes the glucose-lowering efficacy of metformin3,5. ImP has been reported to inhibit the effectiveness of metformin therapy3,6. Further analyses of the gut microbiota data revealed that the concurrent administration of lutein and metformin may lead to a decrease in ImP expression, potentially linked to a reduction in the abundance of Eggerthella lenta. Previous research has indicated that individuals with T2DM exhibit gut microbial dysbiosis characterized by elevated levels of Eggerthella lenta34a known producer of ImP, as demonstrated in in vitro studies5. Moreover, our study demonstrated that combined lutein and metformin intervention had a more pronounced regulatory effect on the AMPK pathway and on AMPK phosphorylation levels than metformin intervention alone. Previous studies have suggested that metformin primarily elicits its hypoglycemic effects through the AMPK pathway6. The influence of ImP on the hypoglycemic efficacy of metformin is closely tied to AMPK activity3. Cellular experiments have shown that lutein can effectively bind to AMPK, thereby significantly enhancing AMPK phosphorylation18. The results of the antibiotic clearance experiment indicate that the enhanced hypoglycemic effects of the Lut_Met intervention may be contingent on its impact on the gut microbiota-ImP axis.

The combined use of lutein and metformin has some potential advantages. Cost, as a barrier to wider adoption, has been identified as an issue that limits compliance35. The cost of typical lutein products is more economical, resulting in lower annual treatment expenses than those of metformin and commonly prescribed oral hypoglycemic medications1,36,37. Thus, the cost of this combination therapy is less than that of other combination therapies. A population study conducted over a one-year period validated that a daily intake of 20 mg of lutein does not yield any notable adverse effects38. Due to its relatively low cost and safety, lutein supplements are recommended as an attractive option for preventing T2DM39. Diabetic retinopathy is a significant complication associated with T2DM, affecting approximately 30 to 40% of individuals diagnosed with diabetes40. Lutein, a dietary carotenoid, is known to accumulate in the retina, especially in the macular region41. Within the macula, lutein can selectively accumulate and function as a potent biological antioxidant42. Additionally, previous research has indicated that incorporating a diet rich in lutein may reduce the risk of diabetic retinopathy in individuals diagnosed with T2DM43. Besides, lutein may also have certain beneficial effects on diabetic nephropathy and heart disease. The concentration of serum lutein is negatively correlated with the incidence of diabetic nephropathy44. Additionally, increased dietary intake of lutein are generally associated with improved cardiometabolic health45. Moreover, lutein has been demonstrated to prevent cardiac and renal injury in diabetic rats by modulating polyol pathway activity and oxidative stress markers20.

The present study has some limitations. Although the HFD + STZ model has been used in several previous animal studies of T2DM46,47it may be better to use genetic models. We conducted the western blot analyses of the AMPK and p-AMPK protein in the liver and found Lut_Met intervention had a significant impact on the AMPK signaling pathway. However, we cannot clearly explain the mechanism because of the complicated situation of animal model used in this study. Additionally, the sample size of only six mice per group is relatively small for microbiota and metabolomic analysis. These findings require further investigation of animal studies with larger sample size and genetic models such as db/db mice. Besides, although we conducted the antibiotic clearance experiment to diminish the microbiota, it may be better to conduct fecal microbiota transplantation experiments. Furthermore, although we found that lutein showed a similar hypoglycemic effect as metformin, no large randomized controlled trials have directly shown that lutein supplementation reduces blood glucose in humans. Whether the observed effects could be extrapolated to humans remains uncertain and requires further investigation of well-designed randomized controlled trials.

Conclusion

Both lutein and metformin exhibit beneficial effects on the regulation of blood glucose and insulin levels in diabetic mice. Furthermore, lutein has the potential to enhance the favorable effects of metformin on blood glucose metabolism and insulin resistance in T2DM model mice. The underlying mechanism of these effects is likely associated with the modulation of the composition of the gut microbiota and the abundance of ImP-producing bacteria. Consequently, this leads to a reduction in ImP concentrations in the bloodstream and an increase in AMPK phosphorylation levels.
