Field scale evaluation of agronomic and malting quality traits in hybrid barley
Authors: R Karatas, N Kandemir, I Saygılı, I Karatas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHybrid breeding has been used for a long time to enhance yields in crops including self-pollinating ones such as cereals. Its grain yield advantage is known and has been started to be used in barley production. However, its advantages in malting barley, a specialty crop, with its own quality characteristics has not been well documented under field conditions. Therefore, the aim of the current study was to determine the agronomic and malt quality characteristics of hybrids produced from malting barley cultivars. Seeds of ten F1 hybrids were produced by hand-pollination of malting barley cultivars Zeynelağa, Aydanhanım, Efes-3, Tokak 157/37 and Harrington. Evaluations were made in two field trials i.e., fall-planted and rainfed field trials in Tokat, Türkiye in 2013-14 and 2014-15 growing seasons. Agronomic and malt quality traits were examined in hybrids as well as in parent cultivars.ResultsThe hybrids had similar phenological and plant characteristics as the parent cultivars. As average, they had improvements of 7.5 and 4.5% for grain yield and 3.2 and 0.8% for malt extract percentage. The variation for seed size was greater compared to the parent cultivars, which could somewhat lower the heterosis advantages.ConclusionsAlthough the average grain yield and malt quality advantages of the hybrids produced from only five cultivars were not striking, remarkable improvements could be expected in individual hybrids produced between a multitude of genotypes as typical in commercial programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08703-7.Keywords: Alpha amylase, Diastatic power, Grain yield, Seed weight, Malt extract, Test weight

Background

Hybrid breeding has been used for a long time to increase yield by utilizing heterosis in crops. Hybrids also have good performance under abiotic and biotic stress conditions [1, 2]. Due to high cost of seed production with the current technology of cytoplasmic male sterility, their use has been traditionally confined to some cross-pollinated field crops and vegetables. However, with the emerging technologies such as apomixis, their widespread use could be feasible in near future for crops such as cereals and pulses which have large acreages but low unit prices. Therefore, potential of the technology should be evaluated for specialty crops such as malting barley.

Hybrid seeds of self-pollinating crops feature agronomic advantages albeit somewhat lower compared to those of cross-pollinating ones. For instance, yield increases of 15–20% in rice [3] and 10–20% in wheat [4] have been achieved. Moreover, it was reported that hybrid cultivars have improved quality characteristics [4] and greater adaptability due to higher stability and tolerance to environmental stresses [5, 6]. For this reason, there is a major interest in developing hybrid cultivars in self-pollinating crops such as cereals.

Barley is a major cereal crop used in feed and in malting industry. There were some studies with hybrid technology in feed barley mainly regarding the agronomic traits. For barley grain yields, improvement of 7.5–20.0% were reported compared to their parent cultivars [1, 2, 7–9]. In terms of yield components, 5.9–8.3% improvements were observed for 1000-seed weight [10] and 3.9% for number of tillers/plant [11]. Gupta and Singh [12] stated that grain yield increase in barley hybrids was mainly due to increased 1000-seed weight and ear number/plant. Indeed, Khan [13] observed that in nine of the 10 crosses examined, the seed weight was enhanced notably over the best parent. These results point to the potential of hybrid cultivars in terms of improved agronomic performance in barley if the seed production costs could be lowered through novel technologies such as apomixis.

Malting barley is a crop with specific quality characteristics such as moderate protein and high starch contents that differ from feed barley. Studies on the heterosis for quality characteristics of malting barley are limited. In a study conducted with hybrids produced from five barley cultivars, it was shown that the malt extract percentage was improved by 0.55% compared to the parent cultivars [13]. Gupta and Singh [12] evaluated 26 hybrids produced using three male sterile barley lines and 11 cultivars, and found that they had up to 2.1% higher malt extract percentage compared to a standard cultivar. However, 25 of the 26 hybrids had lower values for diastatic power and 19 had higher values for protein content than the standard cultivars. Studying a total of 21 hybrids produced between seven cultivars, Carleton [11] observed an average of 9.1% increase in terms of diastatic power and 4.8% increase for malt extract in two-rowed barley hybrids compared to their parents. The fact that these values were the averages of all hybrids examined and they could be higher in individual hybrids indicates that the use of hybrid cultivars in malting barley can improve malt quality characteristics.

Thanks to apomixis technology which does not require male sterility systems and allows propagation of hybrid seeds [14], hybrid seed production is expected to be employed in all major crops. Currently, this technology has been introduced into rice by genome editing [15]. It would be beneficial to examine in detail the effects of this technology in crops with special quality needs such as malting barley. To the best of our knowledge, no study has been conducted so far on malting quality of hybrids produced from malting barley cultivars under field conditions with commercial seeding rates. Thus, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the agronomic and malt quality characteristics of hybrids produced from crosses between malting barley cultivars.

Methods

Plant material and seed production

Malting barley cultivars Zeynelağa, Aydanhanım, Efes-3, Tokak 157/37 and Harrington were used as parent cultivars to produce the hybrids in the study. Origin and pedigrees of all cultivars used are given in Supplementary Material Table S1. Harrington is a Canadian malting barley cultivar. Its pedigree is Klages/3/Gazelle/Betzes//Centenial [16]. Efes-3, Zeynelağa and Aydanhanım are malting barley cultivars developed and used for malt production in Türkiye. The pedigree of cv. Zeynelağa is Antares/Ky63-1294//Lignee131 and pedigree of cv. Aydanhanım is GK Omega/Tarm 92 [17] while the pedigree of Efes-3 is unknown [18]. Especially cv. Aydanhanım is widely used in malt production. Cv. Tokak 157/37, hereafter Tokak, is preferred in malt production because of its large grain size rather than its superior malting properties. It is a selection from barley landraces [18]. Hybrid seeds were produced by hand crossing in a half-diallel crossing scheme [19] in field-grown plants at rows 80 cm apart in 2013 and 2014. Emasculations and crossings were made in April using the twirl method in at least 200 ears isolated with paper bags [20]. Seeds of cvs. Zeynelağa, Aydanhanım, Efes-3 and Tokak 157/37 seeds were obtained from Field Crops Central Research Institute of Türkiye while seeds of cv. Harrington was from Washington State University, Department of Crop and Soil Sciences through North American Barley Genome Mapping Project.

Field trials

Hybrids and parent cultivars were evaluated in two fall-planted, rainfed field trials (Latitude: 40°19′ N, Longitude: 36°26′ E, and Altitude: 578 m) in the experimental land of Middle Black Sea Transition Zone Agricultural Research Institute in Tokat Province of Türkiye in the consecutive growing seasons of 2013-14 and 2014-15. The design of field trials was randomized complete blocks with three replications. Each plot consisted of three rows of 3-meter length with a row spacing of 20 cm. Seeding rate was 500 seeds/m2 and planting was done by hand. Two wheat rows (cv. Bezostaja-1) were planted at the ends of the blocks and between the plots to prevent the lodging of one plot to interfere with the lodging of another. The plots were given 75 kg/ha of phosphorus and 100 kg/ha of nitrogen as fertilizers. All of the phosphorus fertilizer and half of the nitrogen fertilizer were applied at planting, and the remaining nitrogen was applied before the stem elongation period. Soil characteristics and planting dates in the experimental area are given in Table 1, and monthly average temperatures and amounts of precipitation are given in Fig. 1. Weed control was carried out with an herbicide containing 800 g/ha of 2,4-D. The matured plants were harvested manually and threshed after drying.

Of the agronomic characteristics examined in the study, days to heading, plant height (cm), grain yield (t/ha) and test weight (kg/hl) were determined as explained by Kandemir et al. [21] while number of grains/ear, number of ears/m2, 1000-seed weight, biological yield and harvest index were determined based on Aisawi et al. [22]. Seed weight variances were determined in 100 random seeds in each genotype by weighing each of the 100 seeds separately and calculating their variance [23].

Malt was produced using the method described by von Korff et al. [24] with some modifications as explained by Saygili et al. [25]. Forty grams of grain was immersed in water for nine hours, drained for 16 h and immersed in water again for another nine hours. Seeds were germinated at 14 °C for five days. Moisture level was maintained at 45% of the original seed weight through spraying the seeds with water. Upon reaching of plumula to the 75% of grain length, germination process was ended. Kilning was performed by consecutive heat treatments of seeds at 60 °C for eight hours, 70 °C for six hours and 80 °C for five hours. The rootlets were mechanically separated from the seeds.

The malt extract percentage was determined based on the method described by Fox and Henry [26]. Diastatic power analysis was performed using the PAHBAH method [27]. Results were expressed as Windisch-Kolbach unit (°WK). Alpha amylase activity was assayed using the K-CERA commercial kit based on the manufacturer’s instructions (Megazyme Int. Ireland Ltd). Alpha amylase activity results were expressed as CU/g.

Statistical analyses

The obtained data were subjected to analysis of variance using the MSTAT-C software [28]. Variance analysis results are given in Supplementary Material Table S2. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and differences among the means were compared using Duncan’s multiple range test. In order to compare the parent cultivars with their hybrids, one-degree of freedom Post hoc analysis was conducted [29].

Results

The hybrids had an average of one day late heading in both years (Supplementary Material Table S3). All hybrids of cv. Zeynelağa had significantly late heading compared to cv. Zeynelağa in both years (Fig. 2). On the other hand, two hybrids of cv. Aydanhanım in 2013-14 and three hybrids in 2014-15 had significantly early heading compared to cv. Aydanhanım.

Plant height had considerable variations in the two experimental years. The average plant height across all genotypes was 79.0 cm in the first year and 114.1 cm in the second year, and this difference seemed to be due to the markedly higher precipitations in November-April period in the second year (Supplementary Material Table S3). The hybrids were 1.8 cm shorter in the first year but 7.2 cm taller in the second year compared to the parents.

The parents had 22.5–30.4 seeds/ear in 2013-14 trial and 27.0-35.3 in 2014-15 trial while the hybrids had 24.5–29.6 seeds/ear in 2013-14 and 29.6–33.3 in 2014-15. Thus, hybrids had 4.1 and 4.2 more grains/ear in the first and second experimental year, respectively (Supplementary Material Table S3). Tokak, Zeynelağa, and Efes-3 hybrids produced higher seeds/ear compared to their parents (Fig. 2). These increases were significant in Efes-3 x Aydanhanım and Zeynelağa x Aydanhanım in both years.

The average number of ears/m2 was similar in 2013-14 (311.8) and 2014-15 years (332.5) (Supplementary Material Table S3). Two of the Efes-3 hybrids in the first year and all hybrids in the second year produced fewer ears than their parents in the second year (Fig. 2). In terms of the individual hybrids, the Efes-3 x Harrington produced fewer ears than either parent. Some other hybrids of parents had higher values in the first year but lower values in the second year for number of ears/m2 compared to their parents.

The average grain yield was higher in 2014-15 (5.32 t/ha) than in 2013-14 year (4.47 t/ha) (Supplementary Material Table S3). Parents Harrington and Zeynelağa had significantly lower grain yields in both years (3.74 and 3.20 t/ha in 2013-14 and 4.35 and 4.54 t/ha in 2014-15, respectively) while cv. Aydanhanım had higher yields in both years (5.47 and 5.95 t/ha, respectively). The average grain yield of hybrids was higher in both 2013-14 (4.58 t/ha) and 2014-15 years (5.40 t/ha) compared to the parents (4.26 and 5.17 t/ha in the first and second years, respectively). In cv. Zeynelağa hybrids, grain yields of three hybrids in 2013-14 and four hybrids in 2014-15 were significantly higher than this parent (Fig. 2). Similarly, two Harrington hybrids in the first year and three hybrids in the second year had significantly higher grain yields than this parent. One hybrid of cv. Aydanhanım had significantly higher grain yield than itself in 2014-15. Two hybrids of Efes-3 and three hybrids of Tokak in 2013-14 and one hybrid each in 2014-15 years had significantly higher grain yields than their parents.

Malt extract average of the hybrids was higher than that of the parents in the first (75.5 vs. 72.3%, respectively) and second year (76.7 vs. 75.9%, respectively) (Supplementary Material Table S3). In terms of individual hybrids, all four hybrids of cv. Harrington had significantly lower malt extract percentage in 2014-15 while two hybrids had higher values in 2013-14 compared to this parent. Among the hybrids of cvs. Aydanhanım, Zeynelağa, Tokak and Efes-3, all had similar or significantly higher malt extract percentages compared to themselves in any year (Fig. 3). However, 12 hybrids of these parents in 2013-14 and 9 hybrids in 2014-15 had significantly higher malt extract percentages than their respective parents.

Average thousand-seed weight of all genotypes was similar in both years (Supplementary Material Table S3). Average 1000-seed weight of all hybrids was 1.8 g higher in 2013-14 (53.4 vs. 55.2 g) and 4.2 g higher in 2014-15 (50.1 vs. 54.3 g) compared to the average of all parents. All hybrids of cv. Harrington had higher 1000-seed weight than this parent in both years (Fig. 3). On the other hand, two hybrids of cv. Zeynelağa in 2013-14 and all hybrids in 2014-15 had significantly higher 1000-seed weight compared to this parent. In terms of the individual hybrids, Tokak x Aydanhanım had higher 1000-seed weight than both parents in the two years.

The parents had a variance range of 66.5-103.3 (average: 76.8) for seed weight in 2013-14 trial and 63.7–89.7 (average 74.5) in 2014-15 trial while the hybrids had a seed weight variance range of 54.6-125.1 (average: 91.0) in 2013-14 and 64.6–117.0 (average 95.1) in 2014-15 (Supplementary Material Table S3). Thus, the variances of hybrids for grain yield were about 18.5 and 27.7% higher in the first and second trial, respectively (Fig. 4).

Average test weight was lower in 2014-15 (64.5 kg/hl) compared to 2014-15 (66.4 kg/hl) (Supplementary Material Table S3). The average test weight of hybrids was 65.2 kg/hl in 2013–14 kg/hl and 67.0 kg/hl in 2014-15. Average test weight of the parents was 63.2 and 65.2 kg/hl in the first and second years, respectively. Thus, the hybrids had higher test weights than parents in both years. Except for Zeynelağa hybrids, other hybrids produced either higher than or similar test weights to their respective parents (Fig. 3). All hybrids of Efes-3 had higher test weight than this parent in both years. Similarly, two Harrington and three Tokak hybrids had higher test weights than their parents in both years.

Average alpha amylase activity of the hybrids (29.8 CU/g) was lower than that of parents (34.0 CU/g) in 2013-14 but similar in 2014-15 (Supplementary Material Table S3). The alpha amylase activity of the parents varied from 23.3 to 55.2 CU/g in 2013-14 and from 31.4 to 74.6 CU/g in 2014-15. The hybrids, on the other hand, had alpha amylase activities between 18.5 and 55.3 CU/g (average: 29.8) in 2013-14 and between 33.8 and 75.6 CU/g (average: 54.1) in 2014-15. Three of the Efes-3 hybrids had higher alpha amylase activity than this parent (Fig. 3). As individual hybrids, Efes-3 x Aydanhanım provided higher alpha amylase activity than both parents. Some other hybrids of parents had higher alpha amylase activity in the first year but lower in second year compared to their parents.

Diastatic power had considerable variation among parents as well as among hybrids (Supplementary Material Table S3). Hybrids had clearly higher diastatic power than parents in in 2013-14 (248.0 vs. 211.0 °WK) and in 2014-15 (279.2 vs. 205.4 °WK). All hybrids of cv. Efes-3 and three hybrids of cv. Harrington and Tokak had significantly higher diastatic power than those of their parents in both years (Fig. 3). On the other hand, three hybrids of cv. Aydanhanım in 2013-14 had significantly high diastatic power compared to this parent.

Discussion

Malting barley is a specialty crop with many quality and agronomic criteria to be met to satisfy both producers and consumers. Hybrid technology has long been used to increase the agronomic performance in many crops. However, agronomic and especially quality characteristics of field-grown hybrid barley are not well known at plot level. Therefore, 10 barley hybrids produced from crosses between five malting barley cultivars were evaluated for both agronomic and quality traits.

The hybrids had 7.50 and 4.45% higher grain yields in the first and second years compared to their parent cultivars. The yield increases in the barley hybrids observed in the present study were somewhat lower than the 7.5–20.0% increases reported in the previous hybrid barley studies which evaluated the hybrids produced from six row or feed barley cultivars [1, 2, 7–9]. The lower yield increases produced from malting barley cultivars in the present study could be due to much lower diversity in malting barley compared to feed barley [30] since low heterosis levels in autogamous crops was reported to be due to low genetic diversity between the parental lines [2].

Hybrids did not have markedly different plant characteristics compared to their parents. They had about one day of late heading, and were about 2 cm shorter or 7 cm taller than their parents depending on the growing year, but individual hybrids had varying results in terms of plant height. In terms of yield components, the hybrids had similar number of ears/m2 to the parent cultivars. Tiller number was reported to be among the major causes of yield increases in barley hybrids [13] and 3.9% of increases were reported for number of tillers/plant [12]. However, lack of improvement in hybrids for tiller number in the present study could be due to the relatively low variation for number of tillers/m2 among our parent cultivars (about 16 and 38% in the first and second years, respectively) compared to the variation among the lines (80%) used by Carleton [12]. The hybrids had 1.3–1.4 more seeds/ears than their parents. Besides, their 1000-seed weights were clearly higher than the parents. Thus, grain yield increases of the barley hybrids in the present study seemed to be mainly through 1000-seed weight increases. Similarly, Khan [13], Gupta and Singh [12] and Zhang et al. [10] reported that the grain yield increases in hybrid barley were principally due to increased 1000-seed weight. It should be kept in mind that unlike most plant characteristics which are a result of F1 nature of hybrids, seed characteristics such as seed size are a result of F2 nature. Thus, a heterogeneity in seed size was hypothesized to be a pitfall for heterosis in malting barley [31]. To test this hypothesis, the seed size variances of hybrids and parent cultivars were analysed in the current study, and 18.5 and 27.7% higher variances were calculated in hybrids compared to their parent cultivars in the first and second experimental years, respectively. Similarly, despite the seed weight improvements, proportions of small and medium sized-grains were reported to be significantly higher in wheat hybrids compared to parent cultivars, indicating higher variance for seed weight in hybrid plants [32]. This could be a drawback compromising the malt quality improvements achieved as a result of heterosis in the F1 plant.

The hybrids had about 3.2 and 0.8% higher malt extract percentages than their parents in the first and second years, respectively. Higher malt extract percentages of the hybrids were partly due to their higher test weights (2.0 kg/hl higher in the first year and 1.8 kg/hl higher in the second year) compared to the parents since test weight is known to be positively associated with malt extract percentage [33, 34]. Another factor that might have contributed to the improved malt extract percentages in the hybrids could be the 1.8 and 4.2 g higher 1000-seed weights in the first and second years, respectively. Seed size is known to contribute to malt extract percentage [35, 36]. Even the high variation for the seed size in hybrid seeds due to their F2 nature could not prevent the seed size increases from contributing to malt extract improvements. One final factor that could be effective in better malt extract could be higher activity levels of starch digesting enzymes. The hybrids had 37.0 and 73.8 °WK unit higher diastatic power than their parents in the first and second years, respectively. Conflicting results were observed for diastatic power in hybrid malting barley. Gupta and Singh [12] reported lower diastatic activity in hybrid barley while Carleton [11] reported higher activity. In sorghum hybrids, on the other hand, Wenzel and Pretorius [37] observed different level of diastatic power increases depending upon the parents, indicating the strong genomic background effects of parents. Diastatic power represents the total activity of starch digesting enzymes [38], and this activity is associated with higher malt extract percentages [34, 39]. Good quality malting barleys should have a DP level of 110–150 °L (369–509 °WK) [40]. Alpha amylase is one of the four major starch digesting enzymes in barley and good quality malting cultivars should have alpha amylase levels of 40–70 DU [40]. However, alpha amylase activities of the hybrids were not better than their parents in either year. Thus, activities of other enzymes might have been responsible for the higher diastatic power, and consequently malt extract percentages, of the hybrids.

Future hybrids to be produced between cultivars with high malt extract levels could have even higher malt extract percentages due to heterosis for this trait. Indeed, Hockett et al. [31] found that the best malting quality was achieved in F1’s produced from the parents with the best malting quality. Considering the confounding effects of F2 nature of hybrid seeds which was mentioned to be a possible drawback for enzyme activities in the hybrid seed [41], use of parents with superior malting characteristics for the production of malting barley hybrids seems to be necessary to prevent the segregation of genes affecting the malting quality in hybrid seeds.

Conclusions

Heterosis has been shown to confer superior performance in numerous crops. Similarly, average grain yields of malting barley hybrids in the present study were 7.5 and 4.5% higher than the average of parent cultivars in the first and second experimental years. The present study also showed 3.2 and 0.8% malt extract improvements. Efes-3 x Tokak and Tokak x Zeynelağa in 2013-14 and Aydanhanım x Efes-3 and Aydanhanım x Zeynelağa in 2014-15 appeared to have better grain yield and malting quality performance compared to other hybrids. The F2 nature of hybrid seed produced on F1 plants might have prevented even higher improvements for malt quality traits. The results of the study showed that hybrid cultivars of malting barley could improve malting quality. Therefore, malting barley hybrids merit further research in terms of quality characteristics.
