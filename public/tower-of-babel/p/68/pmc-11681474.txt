Longitudinal Impacts of High Versus Low Greenness on Cardiovascular Disease Conditions
Authors: Scott C. Brown, William W. Aitken, Joanna Lombard, Abraham Parrish, Julius R. Dewald, Maria I. Nardi, Alejandro M. A. Mantero, Sarah W. Metalonis, José Szapocznik
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGreenness—or vegetative presence—has been identified as a factor in chronic disease. The present study examines the longitudinal relationship between objective measures of greenness at the residential block level and incidence of 6 cardiovascular disease conditions.Methods and ResultsAnalyses examined the impact of consistently high versus consistently low “precision” greenness at the Census block level on the 5‐year incidence of cardiovascular disease conditions, including acute myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, ischemic heart disease, stroke/transient ischemic attack, and hypertension, among 229 034 US Medicare beneficiaries in Miami‐Dade County, Florida, USA. Zero‐inflated Poisson regression was used to model the odds of developing any new cardiovascular disease and number of new cardiovascular disease conditions based on greenness tertiles computed across 2011 and 2016 Normalized Difference Vegetation Index values, adjusting for individual age, sex, race, ethnicity, baseline cardiovascular disease conditions, neighborhood income, and walkability in 2011 and 2016. When compared with individuals consistently in the low greenness tertile in 2011 and 2016, those consistently in the high greenness tertile in 2011 and 2016 had a 9% lower odds of having any new cardiovascular conditions (odds ratio [OR], 0.91 [95% CI, 0.84–0.99]; P=0.021).ConclusionsOver a 5‐year period, consistently high greenness, when compared with consistently low greenness, was associated with lower odds of any new cardiovascular disease conditions. Identifying the role of greenness exposure in such a small geographic area, the Census block on which the older adult resides, allows for more precise, strategic decisions on where additional trees can be added—by selecting at‐risk blocks rather than entire neighborhoods for tree‐planting interventions.

Environmental determinants of health play an increasing role in our understanding of human disease. It is estimated that 13.7 million deaths yearly (or 24% of all deaths) globally are attributable to modifiable environmental factors. 1 Cardiovascular disease has been documented to be affected by environmental factors across the lifespan. 2 , 3 It is estimated that a full third of all deaths globally are attributable to cardiovascular disease. Worldwide, ischemic heart disease is the leading cause of death and stroke is the third leading cause of death. 4 , 5 Cardiovascular disease encompasses a broad group of conditions, including acute myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, ischemic heart disease, stroke/transient ischemic attack (TIA), and hypertension. Over the past 50 years, reductions in rates of cardiovascular disease have largely been a result of medical interventions including smoking cessation, cholesterol lowering medications, and blood pressure control. 6 More recently, it has been hypothesized that further reductions in cardiovascular disease could be achieved by interventions aimed at optimizing environmental factors, 7 such as air pollution, 8 , 9 , 10 noise pollution, 11 organic and inorganic pollutants, 12 , 13 the built environment, 14 and environmental greenness, 15 , 16 which have been shown to have an impact on cardiovascular health.

Greenness—or vegetative presence such as tree canopy—has been associated with reduced rates of cardiovascular disease. 15 , 16 Greenness has also been found to be a protective factor for various health outcomes in addition to cardiovascular disease, including cardiometabolic conditions (eg, hyperlipidemia, diabetes), 17 depression, 18 Alzheimer's disease, 19 and age‐related cognitive decline. 20 Although the precise mechanism linking greenness and reduced rates of cardiovascular disease has not yet been clearly delineated, there is evidence of vegetation's mitigation of air pollution, 21 , 22 , 23 among other pathways, including increased physical activity, 24 stress reduction, 25 noise pollution reduction, 26 and temperature regulation. 27

Our prior population‐based, cross‐sectional research investigated the association between greenness and 4 forms of heart disease (acute myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, and ischemic heart disease), as well as stroke/TIA and hypertension, in 249 405 US Medicare beneficiaries residing in Miami‐Dade County, Florida, USA. In that research, we computed greenness at the Census block level as the average across all pixels in the Census block of the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), an objective measure of greenness from satellite imagery. We defined the term “precision greenness” to refer to greenness measured by NDVI at the Census block level, the smallest available geographic unit in the Census. The findings revealed that beneficiaries residing in the highest tertile of greenness had 19% lower odds of any heart disease and 20% lower odds of stroke/TIA, compared with those residing in the lowest tertile of greenness, adjusting for age, sex, race, ethnicity, and neighborhood income (P<0.001 for both). 1 Similarly, in earlier research, an increase in greenness from 1 SD below the mean to 1 SD above the mean was associated with 13% reduced odds of hypertension in the same sample, adjusting for the same variables (P<0.0001). 17 One of the important contributions of these studies was the use of a small geographic area, the Census block on which the older adult resides, in computing greenness exposure, which was associated with significantly lower rates of cardiovascular health outcomes cross‐sectionally. Hence, an important contribution to this area of research is the empirical evidence that greenness at the Census block in which an older adult resides, which we have termed “precision greenness,” is a major contributor to health. 28 Identifying the role of greenness exposure in such a small geographic area, the Census block, allows for strategic decisions on where additional trees can be added—by selecting at‐risk blocks rather than entire neighborhoods for tree‐planting interventions. It is for this reason that in the current study, building on this prior work, we also use greenness at the Census block as our predictor of cardiovascular conditions.

Our findings and those of others 15 , 21 notwithstanding, additional longitudinal research is needed to test the relationship of greenness to cardiovascular health at the Census block level. Although there have been findings on the longitudinal relationship between greenness and cardiovascular disease, 29 , 30 , 31 the public policy implications of greening initiatives at relatively precise geographic areas, such as the Census block, could allow for more precise interventions with improved intervention cost effectiveness. 32 The American Heart Association has identified neighborhood environment—including neighborhood greenness—as a determinant of cardiovascular health and has recommended further research to better understand this relationship. 33 The current study builds on prior cross‐sectional findings with a population‐based sample, to investigate the impact of block‐level greenness on new cardiovascular conditions over time.

In this study we investigated the impact of consistently high greenness on cardiovascular conditions among older US adults residing in Miami‐Dade County, Florida, USA, between 2011 and 2016. Specifically, the purpose of this study was to longitudinally and prospectively investigate the impact of greenness (mean NDVI at the Census block level) on the 5‐year incidence of new cardiovascular conditions available from the US Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) Chronic Conditions segment of US Medicare beneficiaries' records. Chronic conditions contained in the CMS records include acute myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, and stroke/TIA. 34 All Medicare beneficiaries aged ≥65, residing in Miami‐Dade County from 2011 through 2016, were used to derive the cohort in which the study was conducted. We hypothesized that Medicare beneficiaries residing in Census blocks consistently high in greenness in 2011 and 2016, when compared with those residing in blocks consistently low in greenness in 2011 and 2016, would have reduced risk of new cardiovascular conditions from 2012 to 2016.

METHODS

Data Availability Statement

The Medicare data underlying this article cannot be shared publicly due to the privacy of beneficiaries of CMS, a federal agency within the US Department of Health and Human Services. The data, however, can be obtained with the assistance of the CMS Research Data Assistance Center (ResDAC), available at https://resdac.org/. NDVI data are publicly available from US Geological Survey's Earth Resources Observation and Science (EROS) Science Processing Architecture On Demand Interface (US Geological Survey, 2016).

Study Design

To test the hypothesis, a prospective, longitudinal study was conducted to compare US Medicare beneficiaries in Miami‐Dade County, Florida, USA, living in consistently high versus consistently low naturally occurring residential greenness micro‐environments, defined at the Census block level. In this regard, the current study intends to test whether a very small geographic area surrounding an individual's home can have an impact on incidence of cardiovascular conditions. Hence, in our use of “micro‐environment” we refer to the block in which the older adult resides (not time–space activity as has been used in other studies 35 ). Census blocks are identified through the 9‐digit ZIP code (ie, ZIP+4). 36 , 37 Ajayakumar et al concluded that spatial analyses performed on ZIP+4 data suggest a far more insightful geographic output than if using larger geographic units. 37 The Census block, which is determined using ZIP+4 data from CMS files, provides precision for the measurement of greenness in the immediate environment to which the beneficiary is exposed. According to the US Census, “Census blocks [are] the smallest geographic area for which the Bureau of the Census collects and tabulates decennial census data.” 38 In urban areas, the Census block typically reflects a city block bounded by streets. To illustrate the smallness of a Census block, it is useful to note that Census blocks denoted by ZIP+4 are contained within Census block groups, which in turn are contained within Census tracts, which in turn are contained with ZIP codes, defined with 5 digits only.

To determine greenness levels (low, middle, high), all blocks where Medicare beneficiaries reside were assessed for greenness level in both 2011 and 2016. All greenness scores from Census blocks in which beneficiaries lived in 2011 and 2016 were used to calculate tertiles. The threshold established for the lower and upper tertiles based on 2011 and 2016 values were used to label blocks. Blocks were labeled as Low only if greenness was in the low tertile, and as High if greenness was in the high tertile, with Low‐Low blocks consistently in the low tertile and High‐High blocks consistently in the high tertile both, in 2011 and 2016. For post hoc analyses exploring the impact of increasing greenness (Low‐High blocks), blocks with low greenness in 2011 and high greenness in 2016 were compared with Low‐Low blocks on the risk of new cardiovascular conditions.

To investigate the impact of consistently high greenness on cardiovascular conditions, beneficiaries' incidence of any new cardiovascular condition, and number of new cardiovascular conditions (if ≥1), were compared for beneficiaries living in consistently high greenness blocks (ie, High‐High blocks) with beneficiaries living in consistently low greenness blocks (ie, Low‐Low blocks) in both 2011 and 2016. The number of total new cardiovascular conditions was obtained by summing the number of new cardiovascular conditions across acute myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, and stroke/TIA that appeared for the first time in 2012 through 2016.

The study included Medicare beneficiaries who resided in Miami‐Dade County in 2011 through 2016, for whom we are able to identify Census blocks with consistently high versus consistently low greenness in 2011 and 2016 and cardiovascular outcomes in 2012 to 2016. The 2016 CMS Master Beneficiary Summary File was used to identify who was still living in Miami‐Dade County in 2016. The third CMS Current Enrollment Data Base File with change of addresses was needed to obtain ZIP+4 for moves data for both 2011 and 2016.

Variables/Measures

Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Data: Cardiovascular Conditions and Location

The CMS' Master Beneficiary Summary File provided person‐level data for location (ZIP+4 for 2011 and 5‐digit ZIP code for 2016), health outcomes, age, sex, race, and ethnicity. CMS' Enrollment Database obtained in 2020 provided the history of known ZIP+4s, based on the “Beneficiary Residence Change Date” and “Beneficiary Mailing Contact Zip.” This was used to identify ZIP+4 for movers in both 2011 and 2016.

Cardiovascular conditions were identified from the 2011 CMS data obtained in 2013 and 2016 CMS data obtained in 2020. The CMS Master Beneficiary Summary File Chronic Conditions Segment, using CMS' Chronic Conditions Algorithms based on Medicare claims of all types for each beneficiary in a calendar year, yielded 6 cardiovascular conditions (acute myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, and stroke/TIA). For each calendar year, CMS's Chronic Conditions Algorithm determines the presence or absence of each chronic condition using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) codes for that condition across multiple claim types (Table S1). Two outcomes were used for the current study: any cardiovascular conditions occurring in 2012 to 2016 (0 or 1) and the sum across all new cardiovascular conditions (ranging zero to 6) occurring in 2012 to 2016. In addition, individual age, sex, race, and ethnicity were obtained from the 2011 Master Beneficiary Summary File to use as covariates.

Normalized Difference Vegetation Index

NDVI has been previously established as a well‐validated measure of neighborhood greenness for use in epidemiological research 40 and is a commonly used indicator of vegetation greenness. 41 Many previous studies investigating the relationship between greenness and health have used NDVI. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 42 NDVI is computed as the ratio of difference between red and near‐infrared light reflected from the earth's surface divided by the sum of red and infrared light (Equation 1). 43 In areas with high levels of healthy vegetation, more red light tends to be absorbed for use in photosynthesis, whereas in areas with low levels of healthy vegetation less red light is absorbed and proportionally more red light tends to be reflected. 40 , 41 This study relies on Landsat 5 imagery for all of Miami‐Dade County acquired on November 11, 2011, and Landsat 8 imagery for all of Miami‐Dade County acquired on October 22, 2016—obtained from the US Geological Survey's EROS Science Processing Architecture On Demand Interface (US Geological Survey, 2016). 44 Because both images were obtained in the fall, we provide from our prior research the correlation of NDVI from sensors in Landsat 5 and 8 for Miami‐Dade County in the fall, 0.964. 45 For both Landsat images, Level 2 data were obtained (for surface reflectance, NDVI correction). Cloud and cloud‐shadow pixels were removed from the images using the pixel QA band. 46 , 47 Mean NDVI values were derived for each of the 23 856 Miami‐Dade County Census blocks on which Medicare beneficiaries resided for both 2011 and 2016, resulting in a total of 47 712 NDVI values. These 47 712 NDVI values across 2011 and 2016, in turn, were categorized into 3 tertiles to create meaningful exposure categories of block‐level greenness: the Low greenness tertile had an upper threshold of 0.376; the High greenness tertile had a lower threshold of 0.489. (1) NDVI=NIR−RedNIR+Red NIR indicates near‐infrared.

Neighborhood Income

The neighborhood median household income, calculated in thousands of dollars, uses 5‐year American Community Survey estimates at the Census block group level. Specifically for the 2011 location, neighborhood income from 2006 to 2010 was used; and for the 2016 location, the 2011 to 2015 estimate was used.

Walkability

Walk Score was used as a measure of walkability. Walk Score awards points based on distance to the nearest destination of each type (eg, retail, recreational) using multiple data sources (eg, Google, OpenStreet‐Map). 48 , 49 Points are summed and normalized to produce a score of 0 to 100. Reliability and validity are acceptable. 48 , 49 , 50 For the current study, we obtained Walk Score in 2014 from WalkScore.com, which had been calculated in 2013 for the longitude and latitude of the area centroid of every Census block in Miami‐Dade County. Because Walk Score did not archive data for Miami‐Dade County, we applied the 2013 Walk Score values for the participant's 2011 Census block and separately for the participant's 2016 Census block, with the recognition that walkability is slow to change. 51

To test the hypothesis comparing beneficiaries living in HH versus LL greenness conditions, we conducted a multivariable analysis using a nonlinear mixed effects zero‐inflated Poisson model. First, we ran a zero‐inflated Poisson model using generalized linear mixed models (fit in SAS PROC GENMOD) to obtain the initial estimates for the fixed effects and second, we ran a generalized linear mixed model (fit in SAS PROC GLIMMIX) to obtain the initial estimates for the random effects. Lastly, we used SAS PROC NLMIXED to fit a mixed effects zero‐inflated Poisson model by incorporating both the fixed and random effect estimates from the PROC GENMOD and GLIMMIX procedures, respectively. Once model convergence was achieved, we used the Logit to calculate the odds of any new cardiovascular condition (Yes/No), and a Poisson regression to calculate the count of the total number of new cardiovascular conditions if ≥1. To compare the HH and LL conditions, a sequential modeling approach was used (Model 1: adjusting for individual variables of age, sex, race, ethnicity, number of cardiovascular conditions, and neighborhood income for 2011 and 2016; and the main model, Model 2, additionally adjusting for Walk Score in 2011 and 2016).

RESULTS

Table 1 presents descriptive statistics for the sample used to test of the main hypothesis and the post hoc analyses. Beneficiaries in the HH greenness condition, when compared with the LL greenness condition, were significantly younger in 2011 (mean age: 73.8 versus 74.7), more likely to be male (43.4% versus 40.7%), had higher neighborhood median household income (2011: $79 500 versus $40 900; 2016: $82 100 versus $40 200), were more likely to be non‐Hispanic White (44.0% versus 14.8%), were less likely to be Hispanic (42.1% versus 82.5%), and had a lower Walk Score on average (2011: 42.2 versus 62.2; 2016: 44.5 versus 61.0) (all P<0.0001), suggesting the importance of controlling for these variables in the multivariable analyses.

Contrary to our hypothesis, in the univariate analyses in Table 1, there was a significantly higher likelihood (21.2% versus 17.5%, P<0.0001) of having at least 1 new cardiovascular condition in the consistently High than in the consistently Low greenness condition (P<0.0001). However, as presented in Table 2, this relationship changed when we controlled for the variables noted.

Test of the Hypothesis on High‐High Versus Low‐Low Greenness

In contrast to the univariate results, as presented in Table 2, in the multivariable analysis in Model 2, when controlling for age, sex, race, ethnicity, baseline number of cardiovascular conditions, 2011 and 2016 for both neighborhood income and Walk Score values, the relationship between greenness level and cardiovascular conditions was statistically significant (odds ratio [OR], 0.91 [95% CI, 0.84–0.99]; P=0.021). That is, beneficiaries were 9% less likely to have any new cardiovascular conditions in the consistently high versus consistently low greenness Census blocks. However, among beneficiaries with any new cardiovascular conditions, there were no significant differences between greenness conditions in the total number of new cardiovascular conditions. Full statistical results including covariates are provided in Tables S2 through S5.

Post Hoc Analysis: Low‐High Versus Low‐Low Greenness Blocks

To determine if changes in greenness levels over 5 years had an impact on cardiovascular conditions, we compared LH versus LL greenness blocks. LH blocks were in the low tertile in 2011 and in the high tertile in 2016 and were compared against LL blocks that were consistently in the low tertile in 2011 and 2016. This was of interest because ~200 000 trees were planted in Miami‐Dade County between 2011 and 2016. 52 Using the same methods described above, greenness measured at the block level was categorized into 2 groups by NDVI scores in 2011 and 2016 (LL and LH greenness conditions). In all other respects, the analyses are the same as previous described. There were no significant differences between the LH and the LL greenness conditions in either the odds of new cardiovascular conditions or the total number of cardiovascular conditions in either model. It should be noted that the OR for the LH versus LL comparison (OR, 0.85, P=0.114) is comparable to the OR for the HH versus LL comparison (OR, 0.91, P=0.021). However, a difference between the 2 statistical comparisons is the sample size, with 24 995 beneficiaries in the HH condition but only 2114 in the LH condition. Hence, power was greater for the main analysis than the post hoc analysis, particularly because of the small number of Census blocks that increased from Low to High tertiles in the 5‐year period.

Sensitivity Analyses

We conducted 2 sensitivity analyses. The first was to determine the moderating effects of having a preexisting cardiovascular condition in 2011 on the relationship between greenness and cardiovascular conditions (Tables S6 through S8). The second was to determine the moderating effects of moves between 2011 and 2016 on the relationship between greenness and cardiovascular conditions (Tables S9 through S11). For the first sensitivity analyses, we analyzed the interactions between presence/absence of preexisting cardiovascular conditions in 2011 and HH versus LL greenness's impacts on new cardiovascular conditions from 2012 to 2016. We found 2 significant interactions: one for the odds of any new cardiovascular (0 or 1) condition (β=0.366 [95% CI, 0.131–0.602]; P=0.0023), and a second for the total number of new conditions for those beneficiaries who had any new cardiovascular condition (β=0.148 [95% CI, 0.065–0.231]; P=0.0005). To examine the results of the interactions we stratified the analyses by presence/absence of preexisting cardiovascular conditions in 2011, and found that, for those who did not have a preexisting cardiovascular condition by 2011, the impact of HH when compared with LL on the odds of any new cardiovascular conditions was statistically significant. Specifically, (1) those without any preexisting cardiovascular condition in 2011 had a 10% lower odds of any new cardiovascular conditions in 2012 to 2016 in the HH condition, compared with the LL condition (OR, 0.90 [95% CI, 0.82–0.98]; P=0.017); and (2) there was not a significant difference in the total number of new cardiovascular conditions when comparing HH versus LL (Exp[Coefficient]=1.02 [95% CI:,0.95–1.10]; P=0.566). For those individuals with any new preexisting cardiovascular conditions, (1) there was not a statistically significant difference between greenness conditions on odds of any new cardiovascular condition (OR, 1.13 [95% CI, 0.78–1.62]; P=0.519); and (2) surprisingly, for beneficiaries with a preexisting condition in 2011, there was a significant 12% increase in the total number of new cardiovascular conditions (if >=1) in the HH greenness blocks compared with the LL greenness blocks (estimate=1.12 [95% CI, 1.01–1.24]; P=0.033).

Consistently high greenness appears to have prevented the incidence of cardiovascular conditions among those who did not have preexisting conditions (universal prevention) with 10% reduced odds of developing any new cardiovascular conditions in this group. However, for those with a preexisting cardiovascular condition, consistently high greenness did not prevent further deterioration, and in fact, would appear to have worsened the progression to additional cardiovascular conditions. One interpretation of these findings is that beneficiaries with preexisting cardiovascular conditions in 2011 were more likely to show an increase in the number of new cardiovascular conditions when residing consistently in high greenness blocks, compared with consistently low greenness blocks. High greenness in this study is associated with lower walkability. In another study on disablement, there is a somewhat parallel finding in which older adults, despite declining physical functioning, report greater independence in instrumental activities when they live in environments with higher land‐use diversity. 53 In fact, older adults with multiple functional limitations were nonetheless less likely to have Instrumental Activities of Daily Living disabilities if they lived in a neighborhood with high land‐use diversity. The result of the Clarke and George study as well as the present study suggest the importance of person–environment fit. 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 It would then appear that if there are preexisting conditions, there may be a benefit of living in a walkable environment, even if that environment is low in greenness. This further specifies “for whom and under what conditions” consistently high greenness benefits the prevention of cardiovascular conditions.

The second set of sensitivity analyses looked at the moderating effect of moves on the relationship between greenness conditions (HH versus LL) and new cardiovascular conditions from 2012 to 2016. We analyzed the interactions between moves and HH versus LL greenness's impacts on cardiovascular conditions and found only 1 highly statistically significant interaction—for the odds of any new cardiovascular (0 or 1) condition (β=0.272 [95% CI, 0.128–0.416]; P=0.0002), but not for the number of new conditions for those beneficiaries who had any new cardiovascular condition (β=−0.041 [95% CI, −0.117 to 0.036]; P=0.298). In stratifying the analyses by those beneficiaries who did not move, the impact was 10% lower odds of any new cardiovascular conditions in the HH condition, when compared with the LL condition (OR, 0.90 [95% CI, 0.82–0.99]; P=0.025). However, for those who moved, there was not a statistically significant difference between greenness conditions on odds of any new cardiovascular conditions (OR, 0.96 [95% CI, 0.82–1.13]; P=0.631). Consistently high greenness appears to have prevented the incidence of cardiovascular conditions among those who did not move (universal prevention) with 10% reduced odds of any new cardiovascular conditions, among HH when compared with LL.

DISCUSSION

The present study examined the impact of consistently high versus low greenness (2011, 2016), measured by the Census block level NDVI, on the 5‐year incidence of cardiovascular conditions, including acute myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, ischemic heart disease, stroke/TIA, and hypertension among 229 034 US Medicare beneficiaries in Miami‐Dade County, Florida, USA. Even after adjusting for individual age, sex, race, ethnicity, 2011 number of cardiovascular conditions, and both neighborhood income and Walk Score in 2011 and 2016, results revealed significant impact of a consistently high level of greenness over a consistently low level of greenness, on the odds of developing any new cardiovascular conditions over a relatively short period of 5 years. When compared with individuals with consistently low greenness, those individuals with consistently high greenness had a significant 9% lower odds of having any new cardiovascular conditions. However, greenness level did not affect the total number of cardiovascular conditions among those developing any new condition in the 5‐year period. Hence, consistently high greenness may be likely to prevent the development of a cardiovascular condition but not in protecting against further progression in the number of cardiovascular conditions, once a single condition appears.

People who had low greenness in 2011 and high greenness in 2016 did not show significant differences in new cardiovascular conditions when compared with those exposed to consistently low greenness. However, the OR for any new cardiovascular conditions in the LH versus LL greenness conditions (OR, 0.85; P=0.114) was similar to the OR for consistently high versus consistently low greenness (OR, 0.91; P=0.021), suggesting that the lack of significant findings for LH versus LL may have resulted from the much smaller sample size: In fact, the LH group had one‐tenth the sample size of the HH group. Stated another way, of the beneficiaries who were Low in greenness in 2011 (the possible universe for increase to High greenness), only 1.6% of these participants who lived in a block that was in the low greenness tertile in 2011 lived in a block that was in the high greenness tertile in 2016. Hence, increasing greenness was a rare event in Miami‐Dade, suggesting that achieving better cardiovascular health at the population level might require a different initiative that not only includes vigorous tree planting but also protection of existing canopy. 57 If the OR for the comparison of LH to LL greenness remains the same, an increase in beneficiaries living in blocks that go from low to high greenness over 5 years should yield significant results.

One of the major contributions of this study and of our program of research is the empirical evidence of the impact of greenness at the residential Census block on cardiovascular health. Consistent with our assumption about the role of the Census block in cardiovascular health, this study found that the residential Census block greenness scores were predictive of decreases in the incidence of cardiovascular conditions. In prior work, we define the term “precision greenness” as the greenness measured by NDVI at the Census block level. 28 Hence, an important contribution to this area of research is the empirical evidence that precision greenness (ie, at the Census block in which an older adult resides) is a major contributor to cardiovascular health. Identifying the role of greenness exposure in such a small geographic area, the Census block, allows for more precise strategic decisions on where additional trees can be added—that is, selecting for tree‐planting interventions those Census blocks with low greenness, rather than entire neighborhoods.

There are several unique contributions of this study to increasing precision in determining what impacts cardiovascular conditions and who is affected. The first contribution is, as noted, the precision in the geographic unit, the Census block, which we used to investigate the longitudinal relationship of greenness to cardiovascular health. This is the smallest available geographic area provided by the US Census. Most research conducted on greenness outside of our team has used larger geographic units, ranging from a 250‐meter buffer to entire counties. 16 , 20 , 22 , 26 , 27 , 58 The second contribution of this study is the identification of the conditions under which high greenness has an impact at a population level. This study suggests that only nonmovers benefited from the impact of consistently high greenness on new cardiovascular conditions. Even movers who were exposed to high greenness both in 2011 and 2016 did not appear to benefit. However, much more information would be needed about why and where movers are locating before it is possible to establish that moving reduces the benefit of consistently living in high greenness blocks. The third contribution of this study is the identification of which Medicare beneficiaries appear to benefit from consistently high greenness, those without preexisting conditions at baseline. For those beneficiaries with preexisting conditions, those living in consistently high greenness blocks appear to have gotten worse. Given that consistently high greenness blocks tend to be less walkable than low greenness blocks, if there are preexisting conditions, there may be a benefit of living in a walkable environment, even if that environment is low in greenness. 53 Thus, this study contributed in 2 ways to increase the specificity or precision of the relationship between greenness and cardiovascular outcomes, with important implications for planning interventions: (1) the smallest geographic area studied to date that can affect cardiovascular health—the Census block; and (2) for whom and under what conditions—nonmovers and individuals without a preexisting cardiovascular condition (universal prevention).

What is the cost of not moving the needle on greenness? If all 79 375 beneficiaries residing in blocks that were low in greenness in 2011 and remained low in greenness in 2016 had instead lived in blocks that were high in greenness in 2011 and 2016, the odds of developing any new cardiovascular condition would be reduced by approximately 1.8%, or 1464 fewer people with new cases of cardiovascular conditions (95% CI, 1391–1541), all other variables being equal. Although our study did not include a cost analysis, considering estimates of lifetime expenditures for cardiovascular disease of approximately $100 000 per condition in the United States, 59 even in the event of preventing a single condition for 1464 people, might have resulted in a savings of as much as $146 million—which may have paid for a more vigorous tree‐planting/tree preservation intervention and reduced human suffering.

Although greenness and other neighborhood environmental conditions are recognized in a recent scientific statement by the American Heart Association as variables that can affect the prevention of cardiovascular disease, 33 the most recent American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines on the primary prevention of cardiovascular disease only refer to clinical interventions. 60 In contrast, the European Society of Cardiology points to the importance of avoiding exposure to pollution, an environmental variable, in the prevention of cardiovascular disease. 61 Future consideration should be given to developing guidelines for environmental conditions, including greenness, that can contribute to the primary prevention of cardiovascular disease at a population level.

There are several limitations of the study that merit consideration. First, the sample includes US Medicare beneficiaries residing in a single large US county in which a majority of the residents are of Latin American origin. Therefore, the results require replication in other populations and settings. Second, we use a single image for calculating NDVI for a single date in each 2011 and 2016 (using images in the fall when images were mostly cloud‐free; and within 2 weeks of each other [5 years apart]). Third, for this initial study with a follow‐up of only 5 years, to maximize power, we summed the number of new cardiovascular conditions across the 6 possible conditions and thus did not test the impact of greenness on any single cardiovascular condition. In fact, it was beyond the power of this study to test the effect of consistently high versus consistently low greenness on each cardiovascular condition separately. Fourth, the outcomes are based on a single indicator for each cardiovascular condition: the algorithm indicating presence or absence for each of the 6 cardiovascular conditions provided by CMS. Fifth, because the archival data used in this study (ie, Medicare and US Census) do not include information on stress, physical activity, diet, sleep, or smoking and other potential mediators and moderators, we were not able to include these in our analyses. Similarly, the CMS data did not include individual‐level data on potential confounders of the relationships of greenness to health, such as individual income or education. Sixth, the design of the study took advantage of naturalistic phenomena, and thus we are not able to establish causality. Seventh, we measured greenness only at the Census block in which the older adult resides. Others have suggested that another way to define the micro‐environment is by including everywhere the individual spends their time. 35 Nevertheless, it is significant to have established that greenness levels in the block in which the older adult lives, in and by itself, can have such an impact on cardiovascular health. Eighth, a limitation of our Walk Score analysis is that data were not archived by Walkscore.com for Miami‐Dade County for 2011 or 2016, and thus Walk Score data from 2013, which we had obtained in 2014, were used for beneficiaries' walkability scores for their locations in 2011 and 2016, respectively. Because walkability changes slowly, county‐wide changes in 5 years are likely to be small to negligible. 51 Ninth, it was beyond the scope of the current study to examine other important environmental factors such as crime and air and noise pollution at the individual level as mediators/moderators of the relationship of greenness and cardiovascular conditions. 7 , 16 , 22 , 26 , 27 , 62 Finally, with regard to social determinants of health, such as sociodemographic variables, there is strong evidence that these individual sociodemographics play an important role in cardiovascular outcomes. 62 Our own research, however, reveals that in Miami‐Dade County, the relationship between Census block NDVI scores and neighborhood level income, as used in the current study, is highly statistically significant (P<0.0001) as is the relationship between neighborhood‐level income and number of chronic conditions per beneficiary (P<0.0001). 17 Although neighborhood income is not a perfect proxy for individual income, research by Brown et al in 2016 suggests that as a predictor of greenness and of chronic conditions, it is a meaningful covariate for the relationship of greenness to chronic disease. 17

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, over a 5‐year period, consistently high greenness, when compared with consistently low greenness, is associated with lower odds of any new cardiovascular conditions. This research also suggests that beneficiaries who remained in green neighborhoods, and either did not move during the period of the study or did not have a preexisting cardiovascular condition, benefitted from the consistently high greenness, whereas others did not. Further research is needed to determine the mechanisms by which the moderation occurs and whether there are other constraints that limit the beneficial impact of consistently high greenness on cardiovascular conditions. To better determine what is needed for intervention, it is also important for future research to determine the minimum period of greenness exposure required to obtain beneficial effects.

From a policy perspective, it is important to note that many countries around the world have tree‐planting, greening initiatives to ameliorate the impact of climate change (see for example the European Green Deal where the focus is on improving the quality and quantity of European forests). 63 Similar tree‐planting initiatives are being carried out in US cities such as Miami, New York, Portland, and San Diego. 52 , 64 , 65 , 66 The focus of these initiatives, however, has been on planting trees to reverse climate change, without sufficient attention to where tree planting could also have the maximum impact on the health of human populations. We propose that greening of the residential micro‐environment, that is, the Census block, of individuals can not only revert the trajectory of climate but could also improve the health of citizens in an efficient manner.
