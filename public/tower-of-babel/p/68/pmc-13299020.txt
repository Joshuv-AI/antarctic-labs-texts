Renal Cell Carcinoma Metastasis to Cervical Lymph Node: An Unusual Phenomenon Diagnosed by Fine-Needle Aspiration
Authors: Lokeswari Srinivasan, Soundarya Ravi, Debasis Gochhait, Neelaiah Siddaraju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe fine-needle aspiration (FNA) technique has emerged as a rapid diagnostic modality in diagnosis of metastatic tumors of unknown primary. Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is well known for its potential to metastasize to remote sites. However, RCC primarily presenting with cervical lymph node metastasis is an unusual phenomenon. Herein, we describe a case of RCC with cervical lymph node metastasis as the initial clinical presentation, which was diligently diagnosed in FNA smears by appreciating the classic cytomorphological features and with wise use of an immunocytochemical panel.

CASE REPORT

A 55-year-old male presented to our fine-needle aspiration (FNA) clinic with chief complaints of loss of weight and loss of appetite for the past 1 month. There was no history of fever, chronic cough, difficulty in swallowing/breathing, and change of voice. He also did not report any urinary complaints. On clinical examination, there were multiple palpable, nontender, firm-to-hard lymph nodes in both the left supraclavicular region and left posterior cervical region, with a maximum dimension of 2 cm. There were no lesions in the oral cavity. No visible enlargement of the thyroid gland was observed on clinical examination. Per abdominal examination revealed hepatomegaly, measuring 5 cm from the right costal margin.

Serum markers were performed, which were within the biological reference range—AFP: 2.3 ng/mL (0–40 ng/mL), CEA: 1.8 ng/mL (0–2.5 ng/mL), and CA19-9: 14.9 U/mL (0–37 U/mL). FNA was performed from both left supraclavicular and left posterior cervical lymph nodes. Two passes were performed from both sites, and the material was collected for cell block preparation also. Both air-dried smears as well as alcohol-fixed smears were prepared and stained using May Grunwald Giemsa and Papanicolaou stains.

Microscopic examination of the aspirate smears revealed several neoplastic epithelial cells arranged predominantly in sheets, loosely cohesive clusters, as well as discrete cells [Figure 1]. Some of the clusters showed delicate fibrovascular cores. These cells had abundant polygonal granular cytoplasm with distinct cell borders and presence of fine cytoplasmic vacuoles in some of the cells. Nuclear atypia was mild to moderate with low N:C ratio, an eccentrically placed round nucleus, vesicular chromatin, and prominent nucleoli. In addition, some of the clusters showed tumor cells adhered to the metachromatic basement membrane like matrix material. These cells were seen along with few stripped nuclei in a blood-mixed tigroid background. The cell block preparation showed tumor cells with clear cytoplasm, arranged in nests and alveolar pattern, with a delicate vascular network in between the nests of tumor cells [Figure 2a].

Based on the clinical presentation and cytomorphological findings, we had three differential diagnoses, with metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma (RCC) topping the list, followed by hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and the hepatoid variant of adenocarcinoma. In addition, since the patient presented with cervical lymphadenopathy, we wanted to exclude the possibility of metastasis from a thyroid or lung primary tumor. Therefore, we proceeded with testing using a panel of immunocytochemical (ICC) markers, on the cell block—PAX8, CK7, TTF-1, and HepPar-1. These tumor cells expressed strong nuclear positivity for PAX-8 and were negative for CK7, HepPar-1, and TTF-1 [Figure 2b and c]. In correlation with the cytomorphology and ICC findings, a diagnosis of metastatic clear cell RCC was rendered.

In our report, we had suggested the clinicians to detect primary tumor in the kidneys. As per our suggestion, ultrasonography of the abdomen and contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen were performed, which revealed a 4 cm × 4 cm renal mass in the upper pole of the left kidney [Figure 2d]. In addition, multiple well-defined hypoechoic lesions were noted in both lobes of the liver, the largest measuring 4 cm × 3 cm in segment 7, indicating a possibility of liver metastasis. The patient was then referred to the Oncology department, where he underwent a left radical nephrectomy followed by adjuvant chemotherapy. Histopathological examination of the resected specimen confirmed clear-cell RCC—WHO/ISUP Grade 1 [Figure 2e and f], corroborating our cytological diagnosis.

DISCUSSION

RCC constitutes 2% of the entire adult malignancies.[1] While RCC most commonly metastasizes to the lungs, bones, and liver via the hematogenous route, metastases to the head and neck region are relatively uncommon, occurring in about 14% of cases. Among these, the thyroid, parotid gland, cervical lymph nodes, and paranasal sinuses are the most frequent sites of involvement.[2,3,4] However, head and neck involvement as the primary presentation is documented in only about 7.5% of RCC patients, as observed in our case.[3,4]

FNA plays a pivotal role in diagnosis of primary tumors in patients clinically presenting with metastasis of unknown primary tumors. In our case, key cytomorphological features provided valuable clues, enabling a precise diagnosis of RCC and facilitating the exclusion of other differential diagnoses.

The cytological soft pointers that favored our diagnosis were the presence of a classical metachromatic basement membrane-like material adherent to the tumor cell clusters, fine cytoplasmic vacuoles, and tigroid background. A tigroid background is most commonly observed in tumors with high glycogen content, such as seminoma, Ewing sarcoma, and clear cell tumors of the female genital tract.[5] However, many of these tumors are not common among elderly male patients. In addition, the cytomorphological features of these tumors were not observed in our case and therefore were excluded from our list of differential diagnosis.

HCC and the hepatoid variant of adenocarcinoma were considered in our differential diagnosis due to the presence of polygonal tumor cells with granular cytoplasm, prominent nucleoli, and fine capillaries traversing through the clusters.[6] However, trabecular pattern, intranuclear inclusions, and many singly scattered stripped nuclei in the background were not identified in the smears examined and helped us rule out these possibilities. Some of the tumor cells had plasmacytoid morphology with prominent macro-nucleoli reminiscent of metastatic amelanotic melanoma. But the tigroid background and cohesiveness of the tumor cells were less likely to be seen in melanoma.

Our cytological diagnosis of clear cell RCC was corroborated by ICC findings. The tumor cells showed nuclear positivity for PAX8, a transcription factor essential for kidney development.[7] Furthermore, the tumor cells were negative for HepPar-1 and CK7, effectively ruling out the possibility of HCC and the hepatoid variant of adenocarcinoma. TTF-1 staining was negative, further excluding metastases from lung or thyroid origins.

CONCLUSION

This case highlights the need to include RCC in the differential diagnosis for elderly patients presenting with cervical lymph node metastasis of unknown primary origin. It substantiates the diagnostic utility of FNA as a rapid, minimally invasive, and effective technique for identifying metastatic tumors, particularly in cases where the primary site remains elusive. By recognizing classic cytomorphological features and employing appropriate ICC markers, FNA can play a pivotal role in guiding timely diagnosis and management.
