Genetically tractable embryonic cell lines from sea urchins Lytechinus variegatus and Strongylocentrotus purpuratus
Authors: Kate R. Castellano, Carl J. Manner, Riss M. Kell, Reanna M. McAtee, Nicole M. Capozzi, Gregory A. Wray, Andrea G. Bodnar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sea urchins have been valuable research models for over a century, advancing our understanding of fundamental biological processes like cell cycle regulation and developmental gene regulatory networks. While an increasing number of functional genomic resources are being developed for sea urchins, in vitro cell culture tools and methods for scalable transgenesis have remained elusive. Here, we successfully established embryonic cell lines from sea urchins (Lytechinus variegatus and Strongylocentrotus purpuratus) which recapitulated aspects of the developmental program in vitro, generating 3D structures and diverse cell types representing all three germ layers. Single-cell transcriptomics revealed signatures of naive stem-like cells and distinct cell clusters expressing markers of neurons, myocytes, skeletogenic, endodermal, and pigment cells. Integration of the single-cell RNA sequencing data from L. variegatus cell cultures and the single-cell developmental atlas revealed that, while most cultured cells were notably distinct from cells in vivo, some cell-types were remarkably similar to their corresponding in vivo counterparts. The ability to genetically manipulate the cultured cells was demonstrated using a lentiviral vector, providing a scalable platform for mechanistic studies. These advances expand the utility of this classic model organism and provide a framework for developing similar tools for other non-model marine invertebrates.

Introduction

For more than 150 years, sea urchins and other echinoderms have served as valuable research models, driving key discoveries in cell cycle regulation, fertilization, egg activation, and the gene regulatory networks that direct cell lineage specification and morphogenesis in the early embryo1,2. The ease of gamete collection, rapid development, and the ability to experimentally perturb embryos has provided a powerful platform for reproductive and developmental biology and their unique phylogenetic position offers critical insights into deuterostome evolution. Beyond development, echinoderms have been used to study diverse questions such as tissue regeneration3, biomineralization4, neurobiology5, aging6, immunity7, ecotoxicology8 and response to environmental stress9. However, tools to study processes beyond the early life stages are limited, and genetic perturbations are constrained to low-throughput microinjection approaches.

To expand the utility of echinoderms as research models, there have been many attempts to culture cells from embryos and adult tissues for in vitro studies. Early studies used sea urchin primary mesenchyme cells to study biomineralization and spicule formation10. Primary embryonic cells have also been used to study pigment cell differentiation and the production of bioactive naphthoquinone pigments with therapeutic potential11,12. Cultured ovary cells have been proposed as a model to study oogenesis and gonad maturation13. Coelomocyte cell cultures have been used to study innate immunity14–16, cellular response to genotoxicants17,18, and as biosensors for environmental monitoring19. Although these primary cells could be maintained in culture for weeks or months, they exhibited limited (or no) proliferative capacity and no continuously dividing cell line has been established for echinoderms, severely limiting in vitro investigations.

The availability of genetically tractable continuous cell lines from sea urchins or other marine invertebrates would be transformative across a myriad of applications from developmental biology to environmental research. Embryonic cells can recapitulate aspects of development in vitro allowing for interrogation of factors controlling pluripotency and signals that guide cell lineage specification20–22. Such cells would serve as a resource to augment in vivo investigations, facilitate year-round work on species with seasonal breeding cycles, and enable investigation of the function of genes that are embryonic lethal when perturbed. Additionally, cultured cell lines provide a controlled and scalable platform to investigate disease etiology and identify pathogens that can have devastating impacts on ecosystems and aquaculture23. The ability to genetically manipulate cells in culture enables functional testing of target genes, including unannotated (or “dark”) genes, and facilitates high-throughput functional studies such as genome-scale CRISPR screens for unbiased discovery of genotype-to-phenotype relationships24–26.

Despite the importance and versatility of cell culture tools, marine invertebrate cell culture has proven difficult to develop, posing many challenges throughout decades of effort across at least six different phyla (e.g. Porifera, Cnidaria, Crustacea, Mollusca, Echinodermata, and Urochordata)27. In this study, we have overcome this barrier by generating the first continuous cell lines from sea urchins and demonstrating the ability to genetically manipulate cells with lentiviral transduction to facilitate mechanistic studies of gene function in vitro. These cell culture tools will enable new avenues of investigation to fully exploit the important properties of echinoderms as research models and provide a framework for extending these approaches to other non-model marine invertebrates.

Results

Establishing L. variegatus embryonic cell cultures

The sea urchin species L. variegatus was selected as the initial target for our studies due to its long history as a model for developmental biology with well-characterized rapid development at room temperature28,29. We selected late-stage blastulae as a starting point for establishing continuous cell lines to capture cells with high proliferative potential and the ability to differentiate into cell-types representing all three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm). Blastulae were mechanically disrupted (Fig. 1A) and seeded into three different growth media previously developed for marine cell culture: Urchin Media (UM) containing 3% fetal bovine serum (FBS)30, Supplemented Oyster Media (SOM)31, and Marine Media M199 (M1) developed for sponge cell culture32. There was active cell proliferation in all media conditions, with all cultures achieving 1-2 population doublings in the first 3 days of culture (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Data 1). However, only cells maintained in UM with 3% FBS showed sustained growth and viability over time, while the viability of cells in M1 and SOM rapidly declined over the first 2 weeks in culture (Fig. 1B, C, Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Data 1). Cultures in UM with 3% FBS gave rise to a variety of cell types, both adherent and suspension (Fig. 1D), in addition to 3D structures resembling embryoid bodies (Fig. 1E). Some cell types were easily identified, such as red pigmented cells, and many of the embryoid bodies were covered with cilia and motile, while others exhibited active contractions indicating muscle cell activity which was sustained over the culture period (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Videos 1–6). When additives were systematically removed from the UM (insulin, transferrin, FGF and EGF), it had detrimental effects on cell growth (Supplementary Fig. 2A) indicating that the sea urchin cells were responsive to these growth factors. Over time, there was an increase in the size of adhered colonies, number of suspension cells, and number and size of embryoid bodies in UM cultures. The number of suspension cells was used as a proxy for evaluating population doublings for the culture, with a continuous increase in cell proliferation over the entire culture period of 600 days (average of 0.6–1.1 population doubling per week) (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Data 1). Suspension cell counts were used as a proxy for overall growth due to the heterogeneous nature of the cultures and the observation that the adherent cells did not recover well from traditional trypsinization. While we recognize that this approach underestimates total growth, applied consistently, it provided a reliable proxy for culture growth over time. At about 130 days in culture the embryoid bodies became a more regular spheroid shape and it appeared that new spheroids were budding from existing spheroids (Fig. 1F) increasing their numbers over time such that by 250 days in culture, most 3D structures were spheroids (Fig. 1G). To determine whether the individual suspension cells in the cultures were capable of dividing or whether they were being generated from the 3D structures, we passed the cultures through a 40 µm filter to separate suspension cells from the embryoid bodies/spheroids and each fraction was used to seed a new culture (at Day 152). The suspension cells did not undergo any cell divisions and cell viability declined steadily (to <1%) over the next 4 weeks in culture (Supplementary Fig. 3). In contrast, the cultures seeded with the embryoid bodies/spheroids gave rise to new suspension cells, adhered cell colonies, and additional embryoid bodies/spheroids, indicating that they are driving growth of the cultures (Supplementary Fig. 3).

To determine the cell-type composition of the 3D structures, we conducted single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on dissociated spheroids collected at Day 278 in culture. This identified eight transcriptionally distinct cell clusters which were assigned cell-type identities based on the expression of selected marker genes and differential gene expression profiles between clusters (Fig. 1H, Supplementary Data 2). Marker genes were selected based on previous knowledge of cell types in sea urchins and genes with well-characterized functions in sea urchins and other animals29,34,35. We identified clusters expressing stem cell markers, cell proliferation markers, and markers representing all three germ layers (Fig. 1H, Supplementary Data 2). While all cell clusters showed some expression of cell proliferation markers, cluster 4 showed the highest overall expression of genes involved in cell cycle and DNA replication (e.g. cyclins, DNA polymerases, proliferating cell nuclear antigen (pcna)) relative to all other clusters (Fig. 1H, Supplementary Data 2). Cluster 6 showed the highest expression of the stem markers seawi and vasa, and also nanos2, a germ cell marker. Cluster 7 showed the highest expression of mesodermal markers indicative of skeletogenic, immune, and muscle cells, whereas cluster 5 had the highest expression of endodermal markers (i.e. endo16, foxa2). Clusters 1 and 2 expressed many ectodermal markers including neuronal genes and had the highest expression of cilia-related genes, supporting visual identification of ciliated cells in the cultures. The scRNA-seq results support the observation that isolated spheroids contain dividing cells that can seed actively growing new cultures and are suggestive of a stem cell population that gives rise to the other cell types.

At 250 days in culture there was a decrease in cell viability for two of the three replicate cell lines (decreasing to 24% and 56% viable cells) and we increased the concentration of FBS in the UM from 3% to 5% which resulted in increased viability and continued cell proliferation (Fig. 1B, C, Supplementary Data 1). Given this observation, we seeded new flasks of cells into UM with higher amounts of FBS (10% and 15%) on Day 292. Cultures in UM with 5–15% FBS showed faster growth and higher viability than those in 3% FBS, with the cultures in 10% FBS showing the fastest growth over the culture period (Fig. 1B, C, Supplementary Data 1). Throughout the culture period (Day 3, Day 82, Day 110, Day 173, Day 229, Day 369), the cultures were determined to be free of prokaryotic contamination using 16S rDNA PCR and confirmed to be L. variegatus cells using 18S rDNA targeted amplicon sequencing (Supplementary Data 3).

Increasing FBS concentration affected L. variegatus embryonic cell culture growth and composition

Given that our cultured sea urchin embryonic cells responded to increased concentrations of FBS with faster growth and higher viability, we started new cultures from L. variegatus blastulae from a different mating pair and seeded the newly derived cells into UM with different concentrations of FBS (3%, 5%, 10%, and 15%). In addition, we compared two different dissociation techniques: mechanical dissociation with a Dounce homogenizer and chemical dissociation using Hyaline Extraction Media (HEM)36. The HEM-dissociated embryonic cell cultures contained more single cells and only a few small aggregates, whereas the mechanically disrupted (dounced) cultures had many clumps of fragmented embryos in addition to single cells (Supplementary Fig. 4). All cultures contained a mixture of single cells in suspension, adhered colonies and cell aggregates; however, there were fewer 3D structures (embryoid bodies and spheroids) in higher FBS concentrations (10−15%) (Fig. 2A). Over the first year in culture, there was a clear pattern of increased cumulative population doublings with increasing concentration of FBS in the growth media (Fig. 2B, C, Supplementary Data 4). Around the end of the first year in culture the cells maintained in UM with 10% FBS showed accelerated growth, surpassing those maintained in 15% FBS (Fig. 2B). Cell proliferation over the first 2 years in culture averaged 0.9–2.7 population doublings per week depending on the concentration of FBS (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Data 4), and the cells have now surpassed 3 years in continuous culture and >100 population doublings (Supplementary Fig. 5) clearly establishing them as immortal cell lines. Cultures were determined to be free of prokaryotic contamination by 16S rDNA PCR and validated to be L. variegatus cells by 18S rDNA targeted amplicon sequencing on Day 22, Day 112, Day 334, Day 487, Day 543, Day 857, Day 1012 (Supplementary Data 3).

For long-term preservation of the sea urchin embryonic cell lines, we tested a variety of different cryopreservation conditions (slow freezing, vitrification, and different cryoprotective agents)37–39 using cell viability and proliferation post-thaw as our metric of success. In total, twenty-eight freezing conditions were tested (Supplementary Data 5). Post-thawing viability was highly variable (23.5%–100%) and cell viability decreased over the next several weeks in culture with only limited cell proliferation post-thaw noted in a few conditions (Supplementary Data 5). Therefore, further optimization is required to define cryopreservation conditions for banking these cell lines.

Single-cell sequencing of cultured L. variegatus embryonic cells identified stem-like cells and differentiated cell types representing all three germ layers

To identify cell types present in the cultures, we collected L. variegatus embryonic cells (derived from the second mating pair) maintained in UM with 5%, 10% and 15% FBS on Day 169 in culture and conducted scRNA-seq. For these experiments, all suspension cells, aggregates, embryoid bodies/spheroids and adhered colonies were pooled and dissociated into single cells for analysis on the 10x Genomics platform. Clusters of cells were assigned identities based on the expression of marker genes29,34,35 and differential gene expression between clusters (Fig. 3A–C, Supplementary Data 6). In all FBS conditions, we identified clusters expressing stem markers, cell proliferation markers, and markers representing cell types from all three germ layers including neurons, myocytes, pigment cells, skeletogenic cells, and endodermal cells (Fig. 3A–C, Supplementary Data 6). This indicates that some aspects of the developmental program were preserved in sea urchin embryonic cells in all media conditions over the culture period. Consistent with our prior observation of increased cell proliferation in cultures maintained in higher concentrations of FBS (Fig. 2B), we observed a higher percentage of cells expressing markers of cell division and a higher percentage of cells expressing stem cell markers with increasing concentrations of FBS (5% < 10% < 15%) (Fig. 3D, E). Not all cell clusters could be definitively identified based on marker gene expression, but for those clusters that could be identified, cultures maintained in UM with 5% FBS had the highest percentage of mesodermal cells (e.g. muscle and pigment cell markers), while those maintained in UM with 10% FBS had the highest percent of endodermal cells, and those in UM with 15% FBS had the highest percent of ectodermal cells (e.g. nerve cell markers) (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Data 6). To validate these observations, we compared the number of pigment cells estimated from single-cell analysis (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Data 6) with actual cell counts performed on the day of analysis (Day 169) (Supplementary Data 4). The percentage of red cells in the cultures calculated by these two methods was similar, with cell counts estimating 7.73%, 0%, and 0.79% red cells, and scRNA-seq estimating 5.77%, 1.4%, and 1.08% red cells for cultures grown in media with 5%, 10%, and 15% FBS, respectively. Taken together, these results suggest that components of serum were providing signals that favor differentiation or selection of particular cell types; however, the results represent only a snapshot in time for one replicate of each condition and therefore additional replication is needed to validate this conclusion.

Integration of the scRNA-seq data from in vitro cultures (at 169 days in culture) and the L. variegatus single-cell atlas of development29 revealed that while most cultured cells were notably distinct from cells in vivo, there are several tight clusters overlapping in UMAP space (Fig. 4A). Of the 35 clusters identified in the integrated analysis, nine clusters were composed of cells from both sources, three clusters were unique to the cultures, and 23 clusters were exclusive to the developmental atlas (Fig. 4B, C, Supplementary Data 7). Several cell types that were very rare in vivo were abundant in vitro, and vice versa (Fig. 4C), which likely reflects the fact that culture conditions select for actively dividing cells, while most cells in the organism tend toward a non-dividing, terminally differentiated fate. The three clusters unique to the cell cultures (clusters 0, 28, 30) may represent cell types from later stages of development (the atlas only represents cells from the first 24 h of development) or a cell state unique to the culture system. The nine shared clusters were assigned identities based on the expression of marker genes (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Data 7) and include stem-like cells and terminally differentiated cell types (e.g. pigment and skeletogenic cells). For each cluster, we compared the expression of 51 genes described as part of the sea urchin developmental gene regulatory network or genes involved in stemness (Supplementary Fig. 7). Cultured cells overlapping with pigment, skeletogenic, and blastocoelar cells from the atlas show robust expression of the expected marker genes (pks1, alx1, and ese, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 8). The expression of genes associated with stemness and maintenance of primordial germ cell (PGC) fate (pumilio, seawi-1, and nanos2, respectively) were enriched in cultured cells, where the expression of seawi-1 and pumilio far exceeded that seen in cells from the developing embryo. k-nearest-neighbor (knn) distance-weighted pseudotime for cultured cells was calculated using a projection of cultured cells onto a reference manifold generated from atlas cells (Fig. 4D). This revealed a sizeable cluster of cells that most closely resemble totipotent 2- and 4-cell blastomeres, a cluster resembling PGCs, and several areas of overlap with distinct differentiated cell types, consistent with the integrated analysis in Fig. 4A. This result suggests that the majority of cultured cells are stem-like, and that they continually generate terminally differentiated cells that are remarkably similar to their corresponding in vivo cell types. Timecourse of RNA sequencing revealed transcriptional changes in the L. variegatus embryonic cell cultures over time

To evaluate transcriptional changes throughout the culture period we conducted bulk RNA-seq at different time points (Day 20, 182, 313, 445, and 738) for representative cultures grown in 5%, 10% and 15% FBS (Fig. 5, Supplementary Data 8). Multidimensional scaling (MDS) analysis of the transcriptomic data demonstrated that, over time in culture, cells maintained in 5%, 10%, and 15% FBS became more dissimilar, especially notable after 445 days in culture (Fig. 5A). As in the scRNA-seq data, we observed the expression of stem markers, cell proliferation markers, and markers representing cell types from all three germ layers throughout the culture period (Supplementary Data 8). Gene expression patterns within the bulk RNA seq data were visualized as a heatmap with k-means clustering identifying two main clusters (Fig. 5B), with 922 genes in Cluster 1 and 1466 genes in Cluster 2. Genes in Cluster 1 showed an increased expression trend over time and pathway enrichment analysis revealed 53 GO terms and 3 KEGG terms (Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate [FDR] corrected p-value < 0.05) (Supplementary Data 8). The top term in each GO category was “Translation” (GO:0006412, Biological Process), “Structural constituent of ribosome” (GO:0003735, Molecular Function) and “Ribosome” (GO:0005840, Cellular Component) and the top KEGG term was “Ribosome” (spu03010) suggesting increased expression of translational machinery over time in culture. Genes in Cluster 2 showed a decreased expression trend over time in culture and pathway enrichment analysis revealed 14 GO Cellular Component terms with the most significant term being “Membrane-bounded organelle” (GO:0043227) and 3 KEGG terms with the most significant being “Metabolic pathways” (spu01100) (Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate [FDR] corrected p-value < 0.05) representing a wide range of cellular processes (Supplementary Data 8). We could validate some of the trends observed within the enrichment analysis with morphological observations. For example, expression of the pigment cell marker polyketide synthase (pks1) decreased over time in all conditions, which is consistent with our visual observations of declining red pigmented cells after the first year in culture (Supplementary Data 8 and Supplementary Data 4, “Red Cells Summary”). We also observed increased expression of skeletogenic marker genes at times which coincided with the appearance of spicules in the cultures (Supplementary Data 8 and Supplementary Fig. 10). The observed spicules resemble those produced by sea urchin primary mesenchyme cells in vitro10, and indicate that our embryonic cells maintained biomineralization capabilities even after prolonged time in culture (at least 425 days in culture as shown in Supplementary Fig. 10). Genetic manipulation of cultured sea urchin embryonic cells was achieved with lentiviral transduction

The ability to genetically manipulate cells is critical to test gene function and therefore a variety of commonly used transfection methods were tested for their ability to transfect our L. variegatus embryonic cell lines. In vitro transcribed mRNA encoding red fluorescence protein (RFP)40, a plasmid encoding cerulean fluorescent protein (CFP) behind the L. variegatus polyubiquitin-C gene promoter (LvPolyUb::H2B-CFP)41, and fluorescent-labeled dextran (tetramethylrhodamine dextran) were used as reporters. We tested ten different conditions using Lipofectamine MessengerMAX with RFP mRNA, ten different conditions using polyethylenimine (PEI) with LvPolyUb::H2B-CFP, 18 different electroporation conditions using a BioRad Gene Pulser Xcell Electroporation System, and 42 different conditions using the Lonza 4D Nucleofector Transfection System (Supplementary Data 9); however, none resulted in a measurable fluorescent signal above background. We note that previous attempts to transfect sea urchin immune cells (coelomocytes) were also unsuccessful, but the underlying reason remains unknown42.

We next turned our attention to lentiviral transduction, a widely used approach for introducing exogenous nucleic acids into mammalian cell cultures and a critical enabling technology for forward genetic screening43. We first sought to generate expression cassettes that were compact and capable of driving robust transgene expression using the promoters of three L. variegatus protein coding genes: elongation Factor 1α (ef1α), cyclin B, and metallothionein A (mta). We synthesized DNA corresponding to the 270 base pairs immediately upstream of the annotated transcription start sites and cloned these upstream of luciferase in the pGEM-Luc vector. Promoter activity was analyzed by microinjecting the reporter constructs into L. variegatus zygotes and harvesting the embryos at 24 h post-fertilization for luciferase assays. The embryos injected with EF1α-Luc and Cyclin B-Luc constructs exhibited significantly higher luminescence versus the control construct (Supplementary Fig. 11A, Supplementary Data 10). Embryos injected with the Mta-Luc construct produced robust luminescence in the presence of seawater supplemented with 5 nM CuSO4 at 1-h post-fertilization (Supplementary Fig. 11B, Supplementary Data 10).

For lentiviral transduction, we constructed a codon-optimized dual reporter vector encoding mNeonGreen-P2A-Luciferase under control of the L. variegatus EF1α promoter with nearby putative enhancer sequence identified from ATAC-seq experiments33 (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Data 10). The resulting vector was used to produce VSV-G pseudotyped lentivirus. Successful transduction of the sea urchin cells with reporter virions was achieved using a high virus-to-cell ratio and briefly acidifying the media to pH 5.5, which corresponds to the pH at which VSV-G envelope fusogenic activity is triggered44. These conditions maintained cell viability, and bright fluorescence was observed compared to cultures transduced with heat-killed virus (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Fig. 12). The transduction experiments were repeated six times using cell lines generated from the two distinct matings maintained in UM with different FBS concentrations (5%, 10%, and 15%). The cells transduced with the reporter virions showed both increased fluorescence (Fig. 6C) and increased luminescence (Fig. 6D) compared to cells transduced with heat-killed control virus (p = 3.156 × 10−5 (n = 20) and p = 9.002 × 10−9 (n = 54), respectively) (Supplementary Data 10). Cells derived from adult L. variegatus tissues showed limited proliferative capacity in our culture conditions

Given the success of establishing embryonic cell lines, we attempted to culture primary cells from a variety of adult L. variegatus tissues (ovary (n = 7), coelomocytes (n = 5), Aristotle’s lantern muscle (n = 4), radial nerve cord (n = 2), tube feet (n = 1) and spines (n = 1)) using UM with 5−15% FBS (Fig. 7, Supplementary Data 11). In all cases, total cell numbers increased over the first few weeks in culture as determined by counting cells in suspension and evaluating density of the attached cells by microscopy; however, most of the cells showed limited proliferative potential over the longer term (3−12 weeks) (Fig. 7A, Supplementary Data 11). Only ovary cell cultures showed appreciable levels of proliferation, achieving up to 30 population doublings and maintaining high viability over an extended time in culture (7−20 weeks); however, there was a large range in proliferative capacity of ovary cell cultures derived from different individuals (1−30 population doublings) (Fig. 7A, Supplementary Data 11). The range in proliferative capacity may be due to different stages of ovary development in the individuals used for deriving cultures. Previous work culturing cells from ovaries of the sea urchin Paracentrotus lividus found higher survival rates in cells cultured from early stages of gametogenesis; however, this previous study did not observe any cell proliferation over the 1-month culture period13. The embryonic cell culture approach extends to other sea urchin species

To demonstrate that our cell culture approach extends to other sea urchin species we selected the purple sea urchin, Strongylocentrotus purpuratus, a species widely used as a model organism that diverged from L. variegatus about 30−50 million years ago45,46. We used both late blastulae and early gastrulae as starting material for deriving embryonic cell cultures (Supplementary Fig. 13); however, the blastulae-derived cells were lost to contamination after 10 days in culture, and therefore only the gastrulae-derived cells were used for long-term observations. The S. purpuratus gastrulae-derived cells showed sustained proliferation in culture and displayed many of the same attributes as the L. variegatus cultures with attached colonies, suspension cells, and formation of embryoid bodies and spheroids (Fig. 8, Supplementary Data 12). Like the L. variegatus cultures, the S. purpuratus cultures contained pigmented cells, contracting muscle cells, and ciliated embryoid bodies in all conditions (Supplementary Videos 7–8); however, there were fewer embryoid bodies/spheroids present in cultures maintained in UM with 5% FBS compared to 10% or 15% FBS. The growth rate was faster for cultures in media with higher amounts of serum and showed a similar trend as the L. variegatus cultures with an initial dose response (15% > 10% > 5% FBS) and UM with 10% FBS ultimately supporting the most robust growth over the 357 days in culture (Fig. 8). The overall rate was slower than for cells derived from L. variegatus, achieving only 0.59-1.84 population doublings per week (Fig. 8). This slow growth may be related to culturing the cells at colder temperatures (16.5–17 °C) to align with the environmental temperature the parent animals experienced at the time of collection. S. purpuratus can experience a wide range of temperatures due to its broad geographic range (4–20 °C on average), with the upper limit of thermal tolerance for embryos reported to be 25 °C47. To test if the cultured embryonic cells could tolerate increased temperature, we split the cells maintained in UM with 10% and 15% FBS into replicate flasks at room temperature (22 °C) on Day 51 of culture. The cultures held at room temperature showed limited growth, declining cell viability, and dissociation of embryoid bodies over a 195 day period (Supplementary Fig. 14, Supplementary Data 12) indicating that temperature is a key parameter to consider when deriving cell cultures from cold-water species. Discussion

The successful establishment of continuous cell lines from sea urchins represents a major breakthrough, providing a new in vitro platform to advance research using this model organism. The embryonic cell cultures demonstrated continuous long-term proliferation, the presence of stem-like cells, and the ability to generate a variety of different cell types representing all three germ layers. The distinct growth characteristics and cell-type composition of cultures maintained in different concentrations of FBS (5%, 10%, and 15%) suggests that components of serum are providing signals that favor differentiation or selection of particular cell types. However, further investigations are needed to systematically test additional media components and matrices for optimized cell growth and to define signals that specifically direct cells along different lineages. One of the challenges of conventional monolayer cell culture is that it lacks the cellular architecture to truly recapitulate biological processes in vivo. The observation that our sea urchin embryonic cultures spontaneously generated 3D structures may be particularly important as a model in a developmental context as they may more closely resemble the cell heterogeneity, structure and function of tissues. Integrated scRNA-seq analysis between the cultures at Day 169 and the L. variegatus developmental atlas revealed nine clusters with overlap between in vitro and in vivo cells. Pseudotime analysis identified stem-like cells resembling totipotent 2- and 4-cell blastomeres and primordial germ cells, as well as several terminally differentiated cell types. That the stem-like cells are transcriptionally distinct from most cells in the atlas and have a relatively early pseudodevelopmental time is suggestive of a self-renewing and persistent naive stem-like state. Interestingly, the cultures continually generate terminally differentiated cells that are remarkably similar to their corresponding in vivo counterparts while having few other similarities, suggesting that alternative paths to those terminal cell fates exist. Context-dependent transdifferentiation events have been observed in sea urchin embryos previously48. Our results suggest that many more such events are possible given an appropriate context, and position these in vitro cultures as a uniquely valuable model for discovering alternative cell fate determination pathways.

Some clusters from the scRNA-seq analysis of cultured cells demonstrated the co-expression of markers for distinctly different cell types. This result is consistent with studies on sea urchin embryos and larvae that have also noted unexpected patterns where genes thought to be specific to a particular cell type show broad distribution patterns throughout many other cell states when analyzed by scRNA-seq34,35. For example, similar to our cultured cells, cells from sea urchin larvae show overlapping mesodermal and ectodermal markers35. The expression of ‘cell-specific’ genes outside of the cells of interest has been observed across widely disparate cells and organisms using scRNA-seq, which suggests that this finding is of biological relevance and may represent alternative cell states34,49,50. The time course for bulk RNA-seq demonstrated that the cultures changed their gene expression profiles over time and that cultures maintained in different concentrations of FBS become increasingly distinct over time. It is well known that cells adapt to the culture environment and experience genetic drift and selection over time51,52. We did not conduct karyotype analysis on our cultures to investigate genome stability, but this will be an important validation to implement in future studies to determine the window of time where no significant genetic alterations or loss of function are detected to ensure meaningful and reproducible results for the questions under investigation.

Sea urchins exhibit indeterminate growth and high regenerative capacity, and the expression of stem-marker genes (seawi and vasa) has been detected in somatic tissues, suggesting robust stem-cell-like properties beyond embryonic stages53. Therefore, we attempted to culture cells from a variety of adult tissues using the same culture conditions that successfully established embryonic cell cultures. Cells derived from L. variegatus adult tissues demonstrated limited growth in culture apart from ovary-derived cells which underwent up to 30 population doublings over 20 weeks in culture. Additional investigations are required to understand the constraints on the growth of adult primary cells, and to design culture media that supports optimized growth. Further, it may be possible to immortalize these cells through genetic manipulations that disrupt cell cycle checkpoint pathways (i.e. p53, Rb), promote telomerase expression, or upregulate select oncogenes54–56.

Lentiviral transduction has been reported across several taxa, including some invertebrates57–60, but it has not been widely adopted beyond mammalian systems. Our results, in which transduction was achieved by acidifying media containing cells and high-titer virus, hints at a possible mechanism for transduction which exploits the pH-responsive fusogenic properties of VSV-G to bypass the requirement for receptor mediated endocytosis. While this hypothesis requires further investigation, it differs fundamentally from the receptor-specific pseudotype, offering the tantalizing possibility of using lentiviral vectors across a diverse range of organisms. Alternatively, the generation of novel pseudotypes leveraging engineered or natural envelope proteins specific to sea urchin receptors offers a path to improve transduction efficiency. Further technological advances to expand the catalog of expression cassettes to drive transgenes and short RNA expression represent an important area for future development. Such efforts hold great promise for, among other things, enabling genome-scale CRISPR screens for interrogating genome-to-phenome relationships in sea urchins and other ‘non-model’ organisms.

In addition to expanding the toolkit for genetically manipulating cultured sea urchin cells, future work will focus on developing cryopreservation protocols so that the sea urchin cells can be banked as a resource for the research community. It is conceivable that a library of banked cell lines with defined genetic alterations could serve as a repository for nuclear transfer to produce transgenic animals and serve as a convenient and cost-effective alternative to maintaining colonies of live animals. The cell culture tools developed for sea urchins represent a significant advance to enable new research directions using this historic model animal. Further, the demonstration of feasibility across two different sea urchin species, L. variegatus and S. purpuratus, that diverged from a common ancestor about 30–50 million years ago45,46, holds great promise to extend this approach to other more divergent echinoderms and potentially other marine invertebrates.

Methods

L. variegatus spawning, fertilization, and embryonic development

Adult L. variegatus were obtained from Pelagic Corporation (Sugarloaf Key, Florida) and housed in aquaria with recirculating artificial seawater (Instant Ocean) at room temperature (22 °C). Before spawning, individual sea urchins were “cleaned” by incubating in sterile filtered artificial sea water (ASW) for two washes of 15 min each before transferring to an antibiotic wash containing 2 mg/mL penicillin G, 2 mg/mL streptomycin sulfate, and 0.1 mg/mL metronidazole in sterile ASW for 30 min, followed by a final wash in sterile ASW for 5 min. The “clean” urchins were transferred to a sterile tissue culture hood, where all subsequent work was conducted. To induce spawning, 0.5–1.0 mL of 0.1 M acetylcholine was injected through the peristomial membrane. Eggs were collected into a beaker with sterile ASW and settled eggs were transferred to a fresh 500 mL beaker containing sterile ASW. Sperm was collected dry, diluted 1:9 in sterile ASW and passed through a 40 μm cell strainer to remove any large contaminants. For fertilization, 100 µL of diluted, filtered sperm was added to the eggs with gentle stirring (60 rpm on a stir plate). Fertilization was assessed after 5 min for the formation of the fertilization membrane and embryos were left to develop for 18 h at 18 °C with constant gentle stirring (60 rpm on a stir plate).

Establishment and maintenance of L. variegatus embryonic cell cultures

Late-stage blastulae embryos (18 h post-fertilization) were collected on a 40 μm cell strainer and washed twice with ASW supplemented with 1X Antibiotic-Antimycotic Gibco™ (Anti-Anti) (Thermo Fisher Scientific 15240062). Embryos were then collected in 25 mL of ASW with 1X Anti-Anti and centrifuged at 1400 × g for 5 min. The embryo pellet was resuspended in 8 mL of Basic Urchin Media (BUM: 10% L15, 7% DMEM, 3% F-12, 0.18 g/L sodium bicarbonate, 15 mM HEPES and 1X Anti-Anti in ASW)30 before transferring to a 15 mL Dounce homogenizer. The embryos were homogenized with 16−20 gentle strokes with pestle B which resulted in single cells and clumps of fragmented embryos. Alternatively, the embryos were dissociated in Hyaline Extraction Media (HEM)36. The cells were counted on a hemocytometer with trypan blue exclusion using a 1:1 dilution with 0.4% (w/v) trypan blue in ASW. Three million cells were plated in 5 mL of growth media in T25 tissue culture flasks. Urchin media (UM)30 was comprised of BUM supplemented with 10 µg/mL insulin (Sigma I0516), 10 µg/mL transferrin (Sigma T8158), 50 ng/mL fibroblast growth factor (FGF) (Thermo Fisher Scientific RP-8628), 50 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF) (Thermo Fisher Scientific PHG0311), 55 µM β-mercaptoethanol, 0.1% Chemically Defined Lipid Concentrate Gibco™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific 11905031), 10 nM selenium and 200 µM L-glutamine with the specified concentration of heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (3%, 5%, 10% or 15% v/v). Supplemented oyster media (SOM) was modified from Chen and Wang (1999)31 and contained 40% L15 in ASW with 10 µg/mL insulin (Sigma I0516), 1X Amino Acid Solution (Sigma R7131), 0.1% Chemically Defined Lipid Concentrate Gibco™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific 11905031), 1X Anti-Anti Gibco™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific 15240062), and 1% (v/v) FBS (Sigma F2442). Marine Media M199 (M1)32 was composed of 9.4 g/L M199 (Sigma M3769), 17.25 g/L sodium chloride, 10.04 g/L magnesium chloride hexahydrate, 0.88 g/L sodium sulfate, 0.4 g/L calcium chloride, 0.25 g/L potassium chloride, 4.02 g/L Tris hydrochloride, 2.97 g/L Tris base, 1X Amino Acid mix (Sigma R7131), 2 mM L-glutamine, 0.56 mM alanine, and 1X Anti-Anti Gibco™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific 15240-062). Cells were maintained at 22 °C and evaluated for proliferation and viability once per week by counting a sample of suspension cells on a hemocytometer using the trypan blue exclusion method described above. If less than 3 million cells were present, all cells were reseeded into 5 mL fresh media, otherwise 3 million cells were reseeded into fresh media and population doublings were calculated using the following equation: PDL = 3.32*(log(final cell number)-log(initial cell number)). An additional media change was conducted 3–4 days after counting throughout the first year in culture; after which the media was changed only once per week. Cells were imaged on the Olympus IX83 inverted microscope using the Olympus cellSens Dimension software v1.18.

We refer to our cell cultures as “embryonic cell lines” according to terminology defined by the Society for In Vitro Biology61,62 and the American Type Culture Collection. We use the term “continuous cell line” for our cultures that have surpassed 100 population doublings, as defined in “The Guidelines for the Use of Cell Lines for Biomedical Research”62.

Cryopreservation of cultured sea urchin cells

A variety of cryopreservation protocols were tested for our sea urchin embryonic cultures (slow freezing, vitrification, different cryoprotective agents), detailed in Table S5. Aliquots of 2–10 million cells (along with any spheroids and embryoid bodies in the culture) were centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min. The cell pellets were gently resuspended in 1 mL of the appropriate freezing media (see Table S5) and placed in a 2 mL cryovial in a Mr. Frosty™ freezing container at −80 °C to cool at a controlled rate (~ 1 °C/min). After 24 h, the cells were transferred to the vapor phase of a liquid nitrogen freezer (Taylor Wharton 10K K-Series Cryogenic System) for at least 24 h before reviving. To revive cells, the cryovial was quickly warmed in a 25 °C water bath until just thawed (<1 min) and 1 mL of room temperature urchin media with 15% FBS was added to the cryovial before transferring into 10 mL of urchin media with 15% FBS to dilute the cryoprotectants. The sample was then centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min. The cell pellets were gently resuspended in 5 mL urchin media with 15% FBS and transferred to a T25 tissue culture flask maintained at room temperature. Cells were counted and viability assessed on a hemocytometer using trypan blue exclusion post-thaw and once per week thereafter, and the media was changed two times per week until the experiment was terminated.

Vitrification, or fast freezing, was also tested using two different cryoprotectant solutions38. Vitrification solution A (VSA) contained 40% (v/v) Ethylene Glycol and 0.6 M sucrose while vitrification solution B (VSB) contained 20% (v/v) Ethylene Glycol, 0.6 M sucrose and 16% (v/v) 1,2 Propanediol. Embryoid bodies and spheroids, single cells and adhered colonies were collected and centrifuged for 2 min at 100 × g. First the cells were equilibrated in equilibration solution 1 (ES1; 10% ethylene glycol in basic urchin media) for 3.5 min. Cells were then centrifuged for 2 min at 100 × g, ES1 was removed and equilibration solution 2 (ES2; 25% ethylene glycol in basic urchin media) was added and incubated for 3.5 min. The cells were centrifuged for 2 min at 100 × g, ES2 was removed and 500 µL of VSA or VSB was added. The cells were equilibrated for 3.5 min in cryovials at room temperature before being placed directly in a liquid nitrogen freezer for a minimum of 24 h. To revive cells, the cryovial was quickly warmed in a 25 °C water bath (<1 min) before adding 1 mL of room temperature 1 M sucrose. The cells were left to equilibrate for 5 min before sequentially diluting with sucrose to 0.7 M by 0.175 M with each step, equilibrating for 2.5 min after each addition. Then cells were spun for 2 min at 100 × g and resuspended in 5 mL of urchin media with 15% FBS and plated on a T25 flask. Cells were counted using a hemocytometer with trypan blue exclusion to determine growth and viability post-thaw.

Spawning, development and culture of embryonic cells from S. purpuratus

S. purpuratus were obtained from the Monterey Abalone Company (Monterey, CA) (permit ID: S-200160001-20016-001) and maintained in aquaria with recirculating ASW (Instant Ocean) at 15 °C. Embryos were generated following the procedure detailed above for L. variegatus, except that 0.5 M potassium chloride (~1 mL) was injected through the peristomial membrane to induce spawning. The S. purpuratus embryos were maintained at 15 °C for the first 16 h (until early blastulae stage), after which the temperature was increased to 16.5 °C until embryos reached late-blastula stage (20 h post-fertilization) and gastrula stage (40 h post-fertilization). Cell cultures were generated from S. purpuratus embryos as described above for L. variegatus, except using 52 and 32 strokes of the Dounce homogenizer to dissociate blastulae and gastrulae, respectively. S. purpuratus embryonic cell cultures were initially maintained at 16.5 °C before adjusting to 17 °C after 30 days in culture.

Establishing cell cultures from adult tissues of L. variegatus

Primary cell cultures were initiated from adult L. variegatus tissues including Aristotle’s lantern muscle, radial nerve cord, ovary, tube feet, spines, and coelomocytes. L. variegatus adults were cleaned as described above prior to tissue collections. Coelomic fluid (CF) was collected in a syringe fitted with a 21-gauge needle inserted through the peristomial membrane and mixed with an equal volume of UM with either 5% FBS or 15% FBS. An aliquot of cells was counted on a hemocytometer and 3−5 million coelomocytes were plated onto each T25 flask in 5 mL UM containing 2X Anti-Anti. Tube feet were collected by placing the urchin in a shallow container filled with sterile ASW and using scissors and tweezers to remove about 50−100 tube feet. The urchin was then removed from ASW, and 30 spines were removed using scissors and divided among two 1.5 mL tubes. Muscle tissue from Aristotle’s lantern, ovary, and radial nerve cords were dissected from the urchin (~100−200 mg for each tissue type) and immediately placed in 2 mL tubes containing 1 mL sterile ASW with 10 mg/mL penicillin G, 10 mg/mL streptomycin sulfate, and 0.1 mg/mL metronidazole and incubated for two washes of 15 min each. Mechanical dissociation was conducted by mincing or squashing tissues using a cell strainer pestle (Cell Treat Cat no. 229480) in the wells of a 24-well tissue culture plate and immersed in UM with 5−15% FBS. Enzymatic dissociation was conducted in 1 mL of 2 mg/mL collagenase (Clostridium histolyticum, Sigma C0130) in Hanks Buffered Saline Solution or ASW for 15−30 min at room temp with intermittent mixing by gentle pipetting. Cells were counted on a hemocytometer and pelleted by centrifugation at 800 × g for 5 min. The cell pellets were resuspended in UM containing 5−15% FBS with 2X Anti-Anti, and 2–5 million cells were plated in T25 flasks. The cells were maintained at 22 °C and evaluated for proliferation and viability after 3 days in culture and then once per week thereafter as described above. If less than 3 million cells were present, all cells were reseeded into fresh media, otherwise 3 million cells were reseeded into fresh media. An additional media change was conducted 3–4 days after counting throughout the duration of the cultures and the amount of Anti-Anti was reduced to 1X after the first week in culture. Cells were imaged on the Olympus IX83 inverted microscope using the Olympus cellSens Dimension software v1.18.

Validating the identity of the cells in culture using 16S rDNA PCR and 18S rDNA targeted amplicon sequencing

Throughout the culture period, samples of cells (0.5–1 million cells) were collected and DNA was extracted using the Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol63. DNA purity and concentration were analyzed using the SpectraMax QuickDrop (Molecular Devices) and Qubit dsDNA broad range Assay Kit (ThermoScientific, Q32853). PCR was performed with OneTaq 2x master mix (NEB, M0482) and 100 ng of DNA with thermal cycling parameters of 94 °C for 3 min, 25 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 60 °C (or 64 °C for 16S) for 30 s, and 68 °C for 30 s, followed by 68 °C for 5 min. Amplification of 18S rDNA was conducted with the following primers: F:GCTTGTCTCAAAGATTAAGCC, R:GCCTGCTGCTGCCTTCCTTGGA64 and amplification of 16S rDNA was conducted using primers: F:GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA, R:GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT65. All 18S rDNA amplicons were purified using the DNA Clean and Concentrator kit (Zymo, D4033) before library preparation with the Nextera® XT Index Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Indexed amplicons were quantified with an Invitrogen™ Qubit™ fluorometer, pooled in equal concentrations, and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq using the MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 to generate 2 × 300 bp overlapping paired-end reads. The resulting sequences were filtered and trimmed with Trimmomatic v0.38 (SLIDINGWINDOW:4:25, CROP: 230, HEADCROP:9, MINLEN:45). Read pairs were joined (--fastq_mergepairs) and unique sequences collapsed (-id 0.9) using usearch v11.0.66. Sequences were QCed after each step using FastQC v0.12.1. Sequences were then analyzed using BLAST to identify their origin species.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single cell RNA sequencing was conducted on dissociated embryoid bodies/spheroids from cultures in UM with 3% FBS (at Day 278), and all cells in UM containing 5%, 10% and 15% FBS (at Day 169). For 5%, 10% and 15% FBS conditions, suspension cells and aggregates were removed before adding fresh media and scraping the adherent cells which were then combined with the suspension cells. The spheroids and embryoid bodies in UM with 3% FBS were collected by passing the culture through a 40 µm cell strainer and washed twice with 2 mL of UM with 3% FBS before collecting into fresh media. From this point forward, the cells from each of the conditions were processed in parallel following a modified protocol from Foster et al.34. The cells were washed with calcium- and magnesium-free seawater (CMFSW) comprised of 0.53 M NaCl, 0.01 M KCl, 2.4 mM NaHCO3, and 11 mM Na2SO4, pH 8.2, and centrifuged at 250 × g for 5 min. Cell dissociation was achieved by incubating in 400 µL of Hyaline Extraction Media (HEM)36 for 5 min at room temperature with periodic gentle pipetting. One milliliter of 0.5 M NaCl was added before centrifuging at 250 × g for 5 min. Cells were resuspended in 400 µL of 0.5 M NaCl and passed through a 40 μm cell strainer to remove any aggregates. Cell quantity and viability were assessed to ensure >80% viability by counting on a hemocytometer with trypan blue exclusion using a 1:1 dilution with 0.4% (w/v) trypan blue in sterile artificial sea water (ASW). Cells were diluted to a concentration of 700-1200 cells/µL with the goal of targeting 10,000 cells from each condition for the 10x Genomic Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ v3.1 protocol following the manufacturer’s instructions. Single-cell cDNA and libraries were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3 Chemistry. Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with the SP 200 cycle kit v1.5 to 30,000 read pairs (60,000 total reads) at the Center for Genome Innovation at the University of Connecticut (Storrs, CT).

For each condition (3%, 5%, 10% and 15% FBS) Illumina bcl files were demultiplexed with the 10x Genomics Cell Ranger v6.1.2 mkfastq pipeline (10.1038/ncomms14049). To create count matrices, demultiplexed files from each condition were aligned to the L. variegatus v3.0 genome reference33 using the Cell Ranger v6.1.2 count pipeline. The matrices were individually loaded into R studio to perform QC and differential expression following the standard pre-processing workflow with Seurat v4.3.066–70. A Seurat object was created with the “CreateSeuratObject” function specifying the removal of features detected in <3 cells (min.cells = 3) and removal of cells with <200 features (min.features = 200). Further filtering was completed based on feature counts (remove low-quality cells, empty droplets, cell doublets and multiplets) and percentage of mitochondrial genes (remove low-quality/dying cells). For 3, 5 and 15% FBS conditions: nFeature_RNA > 500 & nFeature_RNA < 4000 & percent.mt < 30. For 10% FBS condition: nFeature_RNA > 200 & nFeature_RNA < 4000 & percent.mt < 30. Next the data was normalized using a global-scaling normalization method (“NormalizeData” function), which normalizes feature expression for each cell by taking total expression and multiplying by a scale factor of 10,000 before log-transformation. Next, highly variable features were identified using the “FindVariableFeatures” function and standard options. All genes were then scaled using the “ScaleData” function before principal component analysis (“RunPCA” function on all variable features). The dimensionality of each data set was determined using the “elbowplot” function which ranks principal components based on the percentage of variance in each one. Based on the elbow plot, the appropriate number of dimensions (3%, 5% and 10%: 16 PC; 15%: 15 PC) were used to run the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) dimensional reduction technique with the “RunUMAP” function. The cluster cell type identities were assigned based on average expression of cell marker genes. The percentage of cells in each cluster expressing specific genes was calculated using an inhouse R script: (https://github.com/kcastellano13/Sea-urchin-cell-culture/tree/main/02_SingleCell_RNAseq). Average expression of all genes in each cluster was obtained using Seurat’s “averageexpression” function. Additional details can be found in the Github repository: https://github.com/kcastellano13/Sea-urchin-cell-culture/tree/main/02_SingleCell_RNAseq.

scRNA-seq data from 5, 10, and 15% FBS cultures were integrated with data from the L. variegatus single cell developmental atlas29. Reads were quantified with Cell Ranger v9.1.0 using the NCBI refseq genome assembly (GCF_018143015.1). Data integration and analysis were performed using Seurat v5.3.0. The standard SCTransform analysis workflow was employed for unintegrated analysis. Cultured cell projection onto atlas manifold was performed using FindTransferAnchors and MapQuery to transfer cluster annotations and UMAP embeddings. Clusters containing >5 cells from each source (atlas versus cultures) were considered shared between datasets. K-nearest-neighbor pseudotime for each cultured cell was computed as a distance-weighted mean developmental time from the nearest 20 neighboring atlas cells. Integration was performed using the PrepIntegrationAnchors, FindIntegrationAnchors, and IntegrateData functions using 3000 variable features obtained from SelectIntegrationFeatures. Integration used the conservative RPCA method. To capture diversity of cell types in the combined analysis, we included dimensions 1:50 in dimensionality reduction and anchor finding steps. Nebulosa71 was used for plotting gene expression density. An R notebook for the scRNAseq integration analysis with code sufficient to reproduce both the figures in this paper and detailed quality control measures is available at: https://github.com/UrchinOmics/urchinCultures.git.

RNA extraction, sequencing and analysis of whole cell cultures

Cells (0.5-1 million) were pelleted by centrifugation (800 × g for 5 min) periodically throughout the culture period and stored at −80 °C. Pellets from Day 20, 182, 313, 445 and 738 were chosen for RNA extraction and sequencing to span the first 2 years in culture. RNA was extracted from each pellet using the RNeasy® Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and quantity were analyzed using the Agilent DNF-472 HS RNA Kit on the Agilent 5200 Fragment Analyzer and the Qubit RNA high sensitivity kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Q32852). Samples were sent to the Center for Genome Innovation at the University of Connecticut (Storrs, CT) for library preparation (Truseq Stranded RNA sequencing kit) and sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Sequence quality was assessed using FastQC v0.12.1 and trimmed with Trimmomatic v0.39 (HEADCROP: 5, CROP: 94, SLIDINGWINDOW: 4:25, MINLEN: 45). The trimmed reads were mapped to the L. variegatus v3.0 genome using hisat2 v2.2.1. Gene count files were created using the ‘HTSeq’ (v.2.0.4) package72 with the alignment files described above and the L. variegatus v3.0 gene annotation file (.gtf) as input. Count files for each sample were then combined into one table using the base merge function in R v.2023.09.1 + 494 and genes with no counts in any sample were removed. Gene lengths were calculated with GTF tools v0.9.0 and the length of the merged exons was used for TPM analysis. TPM was then calculated using a python script (https://github.com/kcastellano13/Sea-urchin-cell-culture/blob/main/03_mRNAseq_timescale/08_convert_to_tpm.py) which normalizes for gene length before normalizing for sequencing depth.

Data processing was based on method of Chille et al.73. Briefly, the full TPM gene count matrix was pre-filtered to remove low coverage counts using the pOverA function of the ‘genefilter’ package v.1.86.074. For a gene to pass pOverA filtering, it must have had a count of at least 10 in at least one sample out of 15 total samples (pOverA = 1/15 = ~0.07; expression threshold = 10). This reduced the gene set from 9984 genes to 3,527 genes. Filtered counts were then transformed with the DESeq2 (v.1.44.0) variance stabilizing transformation (vst) function75 after confirming that all size factors were <4. To look at sample dissimilarity over time, an MDS plot was created using the plotMDS function from the edgeR package (v.4.2.2)76 using the matrix of filtered, VST-transformed TPM gene counts as input (Fig. 5A).

To visualize the bulk RNA sequencing data in a heatmap, the R package NbClust (v.3.0.1)77 was first used to calculate the optimal number of clusters within the gene matrix, considering a minimum of 2 and maximum of 10 clusters using 30 indices. K-means clustering was performed using the ‘kmeans’ function from the stats package in R (v.4.4.2). The filtered, VST-transformed TPM gene count matrix was centered by subtracting the mean expression value of each row from each element in that row before plotting as a heatmap (Fig. 5b). The heatmap was created using the ‘Heatmap’ function from the ComplexHeatmap package (v.2.20.0)78. Genes from Cluster 1 (increased expression over time) and Cluster 2 (decreased expression of over time) were subjected to GO and KEGG pathway analysis. A protein FASTA of Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 genes was created by matching gene IDs to the L. variegatus protein FASTA available through EnsemblGenomes (http://ftp.ensemblgenomes.org/pub/metazoa/release-60/fasta/lytechinus_variegatus_gca018143015v1/pep/Lytechinus_variegatus_gca018143015v1.Lvar_3.0.pep.all.fa.gz, last modified 2024-08-08, last accessed 2025-03-24). Protein FASTAs were then used as input for GO and KEGG enrichment analysis within the STRING database79 using S. purpuratus as the reference organism. STRING was run with a high (0.7) confidence setting and all interaction sources selected (textmining, experiments, databases, co-expression, neighborhood, gene fusion and co-occurrence).

Promoter assays and cloning

Promoter sequences for EF1α, Cyclin B, and Metallothionein A were extracted from the L. variegatus v3.0 genome annotations33. Promoter sequences consisted of 270 base pairs upstream of the putative transcriptional start site (TSS). Sequences were edited to remove start codons within 75 bp of the TSS. 15 bp cloning flanks homologous to pGEM-luc (Promega, Madison, WI) were appended to the promoter sequence and synthesized by Twist Biosciences (South San Francisco, CA). The synthesized promoters were cloned into pGEM-luc using New England Biolabs (Ipswich, MA) HIFI assembly master mix. Colonies were miniprepped and the reporter plasmids were sequenced by Plasmidsaurus (Eugene, OR). Reporter plasmids or empty vector were injected into L. variegatus zygotes at a concentration of 50 ng/µL. Injected embryos were reared at 18 °C for 24 h prior to collection. A luciferase assay was performed using the Luciferase Assay System (Promega E1500) according to manufacturer instructions. For the Metallothionein A promoter, we injected two plates of embryos (~200 embryos) with reporter plasmids and two plates with empty vector controls, and one plate of each was supplemented with 5 nM CuSO4 in ASW 1 h following injection.

For the dual reporter lentiviral vector, we concatenated open chromatin regions near the EF1α gene, promoter sequence immediately upstream of the EF1α TSS, a codon-optimized mNeonGreen coding sequence, P2A element, and luciferase sequence. This fragment was synthesized by Twist Bioscience (South San Francisco, CA) with homology arms for cloning into the backbone from lentiCRISPRv2, cloned by HIFI Assembly (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA), and sequenced by Plasmidsaurus (Eugene, OR). lentiCRISPRv2 was a gift from Feng Zhang (Addgene plasmid # 52961; http://n2t.net/addgene:52961; RRID:Addgene_52961) (Addgene, Watertown, MA)80.

Lentivirus production and transduction

Lentiviral vectors were produced in HEK-293T/17 cells obtained from ATCC (Gaithersburg, MD) via the PEI method using psPAX2, pMD2.G, and our dual reporter plasmid as described by Tang et al.81 with modifications. After 6 h, we replaced the transfection media with DMEM + 10% FBS supplemented with 50 units/mL DNase I (NEB M0303L) to eliminate plasmid DNA from the viral supernatant. After 72 h, viral supernatant was harvested, centrifuged briefly to remove cell debris, and concentrated 100-fold by ultracentrifugation at 125,000 × g at 4 °C in a SW 41 Ti rotor (Beckman 331362) for 90 min over a cushion of 20% sucrose in PBS. The viral pellet was resuspended in PBS and stored at -80 °C until use. An aliquot was used to titrate virus as described by Barczak et al.82, substituting primers directed against luciferase for primers against woodchuck hepatitis virus post-transcriptional regulatory element. psPAX2 was a gift from Didier Trono (Addgene plasmid # 12260; http://n2t.net/addgene:12260; RRID:Addgene_12260). pMD2.G was a gift from Didier Trono (Addgene plasmid # 12259; http://n2t.net/addgene:12259; RRID:Addgene_12259).

For transduction, two aliquots of virus were thawed, and one aliquot was heat-killed by incubating at 65 °C for 30 min. Urchin-derived cell lines were pelleted by centrifugation and 2.5 × 106 cells were resuspended in 50 µL of BUM and transferred to a 96-well plate. To the 50 µL cell suspensions, 1 × 108 live or heat-killed viral particles were added, and cells were incubated in virus mixture for 2 h. BUM was acidified to pH 4.2 with HCl and 100 µL of acidified BUM added to each well (final pH 5.5). After 5 min, cells were washed twice with BUM and resuspended in 100 µL of UM and incubated at room temperature in a humidified chamber for 2 days.

Cells were transferred to slides and imaged on a Zeiss Axio Observer Z1 using a 20x/0.80 Plan-Apochromat DICII objective (Zeiss 440640-9903) or pelleted and used for a luciferase assay. Fluorescence images were acquired using Zeiss Zen software (v3.8.99.010000) and quantified using ImageJ v1.54k83. Briefly, DIC channel images were inverted, a maximum filter was applied with a radius of 8 pixels to highlight cell boundaries, and auto thresholding with the Huang thresholding84 was used to generate a mask. A region of interest was built from the mask and used to measure area-averaged integrated fluorescence intensity in the mNeonGreen channel. A one-way t-test was used to determine statistical significance. For luciferase assays, luminescence was measured on a TD-20/20 Luminometer (Turner Designs), and assays were conducted using the Renilla Luciferase assay system (Promega E2810) according to manufacturer instructions.

Transfection of cultured sea urchin embryonic cells

Transfection of cultured sea urchin cells was conducted using mRNA encoding red fluorescence protein (RFP) produced from the plasmid (NPM1-RFP-pCS2 + ) (a gift from Clifford Brangwynne at Princeton University)40, a plasmid encoding cyan fluorescent protein (CFP) mCerulean behind the promoter of the L.variegatus polyubiquitin-C gene (LvPolyUb::H2B-CFP)41 (a gift from Amro Hamdoun at Scripps Institution of Oceanography, UCSD), and dextran tetramethylrhodamine (3000 MW, ThermoFisher Scientific) as reporters. To generate the RFP mRNA, the NPM1-RFP-pCS2+ plasmid was linearized using NotI and gel-purified using a QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, 28704). Linearized plasmids were column-purified under RNAse-free conditions using a Zymo DNA clean and concentrator kit (Zymo, D4034). Linearized plasmids were used as a DNA template for in vitro transcription using the HiScribe SP6 Kit (New England Biolabs, E2070S) and capped with a 3′-O-Me-m7G(5’)ppp(5’)G RNA cap structure analog (New England Biolabs, S1411S) following manufacturer’s instructions (New England Biolabs, RNA Synthesis Protocol, E2070). mRNA was column purified using the Zymo RNA clean and concentrator kit (Zymo, R1013) and quantified using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer and broad range RNA kit (Invitrogen, Q10211). mRNA was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C.

Lipid-mediated transfections were conducted with NPM1-RFP mRNA using Lipofectamine™ MessengerMAX™ (Invitrogen, LMRNA001) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Trials were conducted to increase the amount of mRNA added, to test different complex formation medias (basic urchin media vs. OptiMEM), the number of cells per transfection (200,000 vs. 400,000), the presence/absence of serum in the recovery medium, and the length of time plated cells were in media without serum as summarized in Supplementary Data 9.

Electroporation was performed with the BioRad Gene Pulser Xcell Electroporation System in Gene Pulser 0.2 cm gap cuvettes with 2 × 105–5 × 105 cells using the RFP mRNA, the LvPolyUb::H2B-CFP plasmid, and dextran tetramethylrhodamine, (3000 MW). Multiple media types were tested including basic urchin media (BUM) and a mannitol-based electroporation buffer (‘T + K’ buffer) used for the electroporation of sea urchin sperm and eggs85,86. A broad range of voltages, length of voltage (ms), intervals between pulses and number of pulses were tested (Supplementary Data 9). Nucleofection was performed using the Lonza 4D-Nucleofector X Unit Transfection System (Lonza Biosciences) using RFP mRNA, the LvPolyUb::H2B-CFP plasmid, and dextran tetramethylrhodamine, (3000 MW). A total of 2 × 105–2.5 × 105 cells were used per condition using different electroporation media (BUM, T + K, and PBS). Cell viability was assessed after electroporation by trypan blue exclusion. The electroporation and nucleofection conditions tested are summarized in Supplementary Data 9.

Attempts were made to transfect the L. variegatus cells with the LvPolyUb::H2B-CFP plasmid using linear Polyethylenimine (PEI, Polysciences Inc. cat #23966). Twenty-four hours prior to PEI transfection, 400,000 cells were seeded in UM with 10% FBS in 96 well tissue culture plates to achieve ~70% confluence. On the day of transfection, cells were boosted with the addition of 200 µL fresh UM with 10% FBS. Plasmids and PEI were diluted in BUM or OptiMEM. Diluted PEI concentrations were varied to test different mass ratios of PEI:DNA (from 1:1 to 5:1). Diluted plasmids were added to diluted PEI dropwise while gently vortexing. DNA-PEI mixes were incubated at room temperature for 15 min before adding dropwise to plated cells. Following transfections, cells were imaged at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-transfection on an Olympus IX83 inverted microscope using the Olympus cellSens Dimension software.

Statistics and reproducibility

Cell lines were derived from two independent mating pairs of L. variegatus to demonstrate reproducibility as well as one mating pair of S. purpuratus to demonstrate the approach worked with a different species. For each experiment where we have generated cell lines, we initiated 23 replicate flasks of 3−5 million cells per flask for each media condition. In some cases, we started with three replicate flasks but selected only one flask for long-term observations due to resource constraints (media costs and personnel time). For each independent replicate, we documented cell morphology by microscopy and growth characteristics with weekly manual cell counts and viability estimates (averaging values from the four large corner squares of a hemocytometer for each sample). All raw and processed cell count and viability data for each weekly timepoint for all replicate cultures are reported in the Supplementary Excel spreadsheets (Supplementary Data 1, 4, and 12). For L. variegatus adult tissue cell cultures, cells were isolated from replicate urchins for all tissues except spine and tube feet, and all raw and processed cell counts and viability values are recorded in Supplementary Data 11 for ovary (n = 7), Aristotle’s lantern muscle (n = 4), coelomocytes (n = 3), radial nerve (n = 2), tube feet (n = 1), and spines (n = 1). Spine and tube feet cultures were prone to contamination which limited successful replication. RNA sequencing was used to identify different cell types in the cultures; however, the high cost of single-cell and bulk RNA-seq limited our analysis to one replicate per media condition and timepoint. The limitations of lack of replication have been noted in the Results section. The lentivirus transduction experiments were repeated six times using cell lines generated from the two distinct matings of L. variegatus and n = 20 and n = 54 images were quantified for viral transduced and heat-killed controls, respectively. Statistical tests used to evaluate significance are detailed in the relevant sections of the Methods above.
