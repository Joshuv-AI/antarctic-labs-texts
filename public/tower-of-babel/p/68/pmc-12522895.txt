Parotid intranodal metastasis of Merkel cell carcinoma: a rare case report
Authors: Tong Gao, Dengshun Wang, Hongwei Yu, Yu’e Wang, Haibin Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMerkel cell carcinoma is a rare, highly aggressive neuroendocrine skin tumor with a low incidence rate, typically arising on sun-exposed areas. This case report aims to describe an unusual metastatic pattern involving the parotid gland lymph nodes and to discuss treatment strategies, thereby raising awareness of the disease and providing clinical insights.Case presentationA 63-year-old female presented with a mass in the left parotid gland region persisting for two months. Clinical examination revealed a firm, well-defined, mobile mass measuring approximately 2.0 × 2.0 cm at the lower posterior pole of the left parotid gland, located behind the left ear. The mass was non-tender, without spontaneous pain, ulceration, redness, or local temperature elevation. The patient had previously undergone excision of an earlobe mass at another hospital without pathological examination. CT imaging demonstrated a space-occupying lesion in the left parotid gland. Under general anesthesia, the superficial lobe of the left parotid gland and the mass were excised. Postoperative paraffin histopathology and immunohistochemistry confirmed Merkel cell carcinoma with lymph node metastasis in the left parotid gland. A PET-CT scan performed 10 days postoperatively revealed no definite primary lesion and no distant metastasis. At present, the patient’s systemic and local conditions remain stable.ConclusionThe diagnosis of Merkel cell carcinoma is challenging due to its rarity and early metastatic tendency, often resulting in misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis. Although imaging studies assist in detecting metastatic lesions, parotid gland lymph node metastases may present with nonspecific features, leading to diagnostic confusion with other parotid diseases. Histopathological examination remains essential, and the complex immunohistochemical profile necessitates the use of multiple markers for accurate diagnosis.

Introduction

Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC) is a rare, highly aggressive neuroendocrine carcinoma of the skin, typically arising in sun-exposed areas in elderly or immunocompromised individuals [1]. Men have a higher incidence than women, and MCC more frequently develops on the skin of the head and limbs [2]. Although its annual incidence is low (approximately 0.7 cases per 100,000 population), MCC has a marked propensity for local lymph node metastasis and distant dissemination, with over 26% of patients presenting with regional lymph node involvement at diagnosis [3].

The parotid gland, the largest salivary gland in the human body, contains both superficial and deep intraglandular lymph node groups. The superficial nodes primarily receive lymphatic drainage from the forehead, temples, eyelids, and the region above the auricle. Due to these unique anatomical features, the parotid region is a common site for regional metastases of head and neck skin malignancies [4]such as melanoma and squamous cell carcinoma [5–8]. However, metastasis of MCC to parotid lymph nodes is exceedingly rare [9].This unusual metastatic pattern poses diagnostic challenges. First, approximately 9% of MCC cases exhibit spontaneous regression of the primary tumor [10]making it difficult to identify the tumor’s origin. Second, when the primary cutaneous lesion is occult or located in an atypical anatomical site, isolated metastases in the parotid region may be mistaken for a primary parotid tumor, potentially leading to inappropriate treatment strategies.

Case report

Patient information

A 63-year-old female presented with a mass on the left side of her face, first noticed in February 2025. The lump, approximately the size of a walnut, had shown no significant change in size over two months. In April 2025, she visited a local hospital, where CT and ultrasound examinations revealed a space-occupying lesion in the left parotid gland. She was subsequently referred to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at Zhongshan Hospital, affiliated with Dalian University, for further evaluation and management. The patient had a history of earlobe mass excision at another hospital, without subsequent pathological examination. She denied any history of systemic diseases.

Clinical findings

On physical examination, the patient’s face appeared symmetrical, with slight prominence of the left parotid region posterior to the ear. Palpation revealed a firm, well-defined, mobile mass measuring approximately 2.0 × 2.0 cm at the lower posterior pole of the left parotid gland. The mass was non-tender, without spontaneous pain, ulceration, redness, or local temperature elevation. The overlying skin was intact. No significant lymphadenopathy was palpable in the cervical or submandibular regions. A head CT scan obtained upon admission demonstrated a space-occupying lesion in the left parotid gland (Fig. 1).

Therapeutic intervention and diagnostic assessment

Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) was considered preoperatively but ultimately not performed. The lesion appeared as a solitary, painless, well-defined mass in the superficial lobe of the left parotid gland, with imaging findings suggestive of a benign or low-grade tumor. Given the small size and superficial location of the lesion, together with the patient’s strong preference for definitive diagnosis and concurrent treatment, superficial parotidectomy was selected to achieve both diagnostic and therapeutic objectives.

Although FNAC can yield preoperative diagnostic information, its sensitivity for small intraparotid lymph node lesions is low, and results are frequently non-diagnostic. After being fully informed of the relevant considerations, the patient consented to proceed directly with surgical intervention. On April 10, 2025, under general anesthesia, she underwent left superficial parotidectomy with excision of the mass. Intraoperatively, the mass was located within the superficial lobe of the left parotid gland, measuring approximately 2.0 × 1.5 cm, with medium consistency and a brownish color.Postoperative paraffin histopathology revealed one lymph node in the left parotid gland containing clusters of small round cells (Fig. 2). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated: CK (perinuclear dot-like +), CK20 (perinuclear dot-like +), CgA (+), Syn (+), CD56 (+), INSM1 (+), P40 (−), P63 (−), CK5/6 (−), Vimentin (−), S-100 (−), Ki-67 (80%+), TTF-1 (−), CD117 (−) (Fig. 3). Based on morphological and immunophenotypic characteristics, the diagnosis was a neuroendocrine malignancy consistent with nodal metastatic Merkel cell carcinoma. Ten days postoperatively, a PET-CT scan showed no definite primary lesion and no evidence of distant metastasis.

Follow-up and outcomes

Following surgery, the patient received combined adjuvant chemotherapy and immunotherapy. During follow-up, she has remained clinically stable, with no evidence of tumor recurrence or metastasis. Concise review

A literature search of the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) and PubMed databases, from their inception to August 4, 2025, identified 17 articles on parotid Merkel cell carcinoma, with no domestically authored (Chinese) reports [11–27]. The retrieved studies involved a total of 52 patients, with detailed baseline data available for 33 cases. Among these 33 patients, 22 were men and 11 were women, with a mean age of 72.3 years. Of the 38 parotid MCC cases that specified the site of origin, 11 had an unknown primary, 15 were primary parotid MCC, and 12 represented metastases to the parotid. The metastatic primary sites included four eyebrow lesions, three ear lesions, two temporal-region lesions, and three lesions of the head and facial skin (Table 2).

Discussion

MCC is a rare but highly aggressive cutaneous neuroendocrine tumor that typically arises in sun-exposed areas of the head, neck, and extremities. Clinically, it usually presents as a painless, rapidly enlarging skin nodule and is often misdiagnosed as a benign lesion or another type of skin cancer. In recent years, the incidence of MCC has steadily increased. Studies suggest that this trend across many regions may be attributable to factors such as population aging, increased ultraviolet exposure, a rising number of immunocompromised individuals, and advances in diagnostic techniques, including CK20 immunohistochemical markers [28–30]. The rarity and nonspecific clinical presentation of MCC pose major challenges for early diagnosis, and failure to detect and treat it promptly is often associated with poor prognosis.

The most notable aspect of this case is that the patient’s initial symptom was an isolated, painless mass in the left parotid region. Preoperative imaging indicated a superficial lobe lesion of the parotid gland; however, the nature of the tumor could not be determined prior to surgery. Postoperative pathology revealed neuroendocrine carcinoma with lymph node metastasis, and the final diagnosis of MCC was confirmed by immunohistochemistry. The parotid region serves as a lymphatic drainage pathway for the skin and oral cavity of the head and neck, and intraparotid lymph nodes can function as a “relay station” for MCC metastasis. Presentation of MCC with intraparotid lymph node involvement as the initial and sole manifestation, without an identifiable primary cutaneous lesion, is extremely rare. Such cases are easily misdiagnosed as primary parotid tumors, lymphomas, or other small round blue cell tumors, thereby increasing the risk of diagnostic error.

Histological examination showed that the tumor was composed of small, round, densely packed basophilic cells with coarse chromatin and frequent mitotic figures, consistent with the morphology of neuroendocrine tumors [31]. Because its histological appearance overlaps with other “small round blue cell tumors” (e.g., small cell lung carcinoma, lymphoma, small cell adenocarcinoma), definitive diagnosis requires immunohistochemical confirmation [32].

MCC is linked to immunosuppression or infection with Merkel cell polyomavirus (MCPyV), which is detected in 60–80% of cases [33]. Virus-positive tumors typically arise in immunocompetent individuals, have a low mutational burden, and are characterized by MCPyV integration and expression of T antigen proteins, indicating a virus-driven oncogenic mechanism [34, 35]. In contrast, virus-negative tumors are more often associated with chronic ultraviolet (UV) exposure, exhibit a high mutational burden, and display a UV-induced mutation signature. These cases frequently harbor alterations in RB1, TP53, and NOTCH genes, along with activation of the PI3K–AKT–mTOR pathway, suggesting pathogenesis driven primarily by environmental factors [36, 37].MCC is highly immunogenic but can evade immune surveillance through mechanisms such as PD-L1 upregulation, providing a strong rationale for immunotherapy [37].

Although most MCC have a clear cutaneous primary site, some patients lack identifiable skin lesions on clinical or imaging evaluation and are classified as MCC of Unknown Primary (MCCUP) [38, 39. The reported incidence of MCCUP is approximately 19–25%[32, 40, 41], but it is likely underestimated in clinical practice. The pathogenesis of MCCUP remains unclear, yet its clinical behavior in some respects resembles that of MCC with known primary sites, warranting aggressive treatment and long-term follow-up to reduce recurrence and metastasis risks. This patient had previously undergone excision of a left earlobe mass at an outside hospital without pathological examination. During the current treatment, no suspicious primary lesion was detected intraoperatively or postoperatively, and PET-CT revealed no abnormalities. However, in the absence of prior histological documentation and pathological specimens—and with no recurrence or residual lesions on thorough clinical and radiological evaluation—this case does not strictly meet the diagnostic criteria for MCCUP.

According to the NCCN 2025 guidelines, first-line treatment for localized or regional MCC/MCCUP involves surgical resection combined with regional lymph node dissection—either sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) or regional lymph node dissection (RLND). The addition of adjuvant radiotherapy depends on pathological risk factors, such as lymph node positivity, vascular invasion, or tumor diameter exceeding 2 cm. In this case, the lesion was located in the parotid region; complete resection with intraparotid lymph node dissection was performed, achieving local control and aiding in staging to guide subsequent therapy, in line with current treatment strategies.

Literature evidence confirms that RLND significantly reduces local recurrence risk [42]. Patients undergoing regional lymph node dissection have a markedly lower local recurrence rate (hazard ratio = 0.26), and the 5-year disease-free survival rate for MCCUP patients can reach 59.3%. Therefore, for patients without an identifiable primary skin lesion but with localized regional disease, RLND remains a cornerstone of treatment.

In recent years, immunotherapy has made significant advances in the treatment of MCC. PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitors, including Avelumab and Pembrolizumab, are recommended by the NCCN as first-line therapies for advanced or recurrent MCC, especially in patients who are ineligible for surgery or resistant to chemotherapy [43, 44]. Studies have demonstrated that patients benefit from immunotherapy regardless of MCPyV status, although virus-positive MCC generally exhibits a higher immune response rate [45, 46]. Given the immunogenic nature of MCC, immunotherapy is increasingly becoming a core treatment modality for this malignancy.

Regarding prognosis, recurrence and survival rates closely correlate with disease stage. The 5-year survival rate for localized MCC is approximately 51%, whereas it drops to as low as 14% in cases with distant metastasis [47]. Notably, MCCUP patients have survival rates comparable to, or slightly better than, those with known primary lesions, possibly due to stronger viral associations and heightened immune activity [48]. Overall, MCC has a recurrence rate of about 40%, with 95% of recurrences occurring within the first three years [49]. Early diagnosis, accurate staging, and multidisciplinary management are critical to improving outcomes in MCCUP patients. Close postoperative surveillance and regular imaging are essential for preventing recurrence.

In conclusion, parotid intranodal metastasis as the initial presentation of MCC is exceedingly rare, and diagnosis depends on comprehensive histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation. When encountering parotid masses of unknown origin in the head and neck region—particularly those with neuroendocrine histological features—MCC should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Whole-body imaging is necessary to identify or exclude a cutaneous primary lesion, thus guiding appropriate treatment planning. Oral and maxillofacial surgeons play a pivotal role not only in precise surgical resection and pathological sampling of suspicious lesions but also in preoperative risk stratification and facilitating multidisciplinary collaboration. By working closely with pathology, radiation oncology, and medical oncology specialists to develop individualized treatment strategies, early intervention and long-term disease control can be achieved, ultimately improving patient outcomes and reducing the risk of recurrence and metastasis.

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare instance of MCC metastasis to intraparotid lymph nodes. It emphasizes the necessity of including MCC in the differential diagnosis of parotid masses, particularly when imaging reveals poorly defined margins and histology demonstrates small round blue cell morphology. Immunohistochemical evaluation—especially the positive expression of neuroendocrine markers such as CK20 and synaptophysin—is critical for confirming the diagnosis. Early consideration of MCC can improve diagnostic accuracy, encourage the use of FNAC and HC prior to surgery, and facilitate timely multidisciplinary treatment planning. Oral and maxillofacial surgeons and head and neck specialists should maintain a high index of suspicion for MCC, even in atypical clinical presentations, to reduce diagnostic delays and improve patient outcomes. Early recognition, precise diagnosis, and individualized treatment are essential to enhance the prognosis of this highly aggressive and rare malignancy.
