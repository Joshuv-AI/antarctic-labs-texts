Association of PM2.5 chemical components with metabolome and peak expiratory flow in patients with stable COPD: a panel study
Authors: Jiachen Li, Lirong Liang, Yutong Samuel Cai, Di Zhang, Yong Luo, Zhaohui Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe component‑specific toxicity of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and the underlying metabolic pathways driving airflow impairment in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) remain unclear. We evaluated how specific PM2.5 constituents alter circulating metabolites and mediate short‑term changes in peak expiratory flow (PEF) among COPD patients.MethodsA panel study was conducted in 32 patients with stable COPD in Beijing (2018–2019). Participants were followed-up across four seasons, yielding 3363 daily PEF measurements and 208 serum samples for untargeted metabolomics. PM2.5 components concentrations were obtained from a high-resolution (1 km) dataset integrating ground observations, satellite retrievals, and model simulations. Linear mixed-effects models were used to estimate the associations between PM2.5 components and PEF, and a meet-in-the-middle strategy was adopted to identify mediating metabolites and pathways.ResultsPEF declines were associated with interquartile range increase in several PM2.5 components (lag0-7 days moving average), most notably for black carbon [-5.88 (-8.76 ~ -3.01) L/min] and sulfate [-4.78 (-8.82 ~ -0.73) L/min]. Metabolomic analysis identified 12 metabolites significantly linked to PEF (q < 0.05). Pathway enrichment highlighted urea cycle/amino group metabolism (P = 0.041) and bile acid biosynthesis (P = 0.012) as key biological response routes. Five metabolites were identified as mediators of the associations between PM2.5 components and PEF, notably Nb-arachidoyltryptamine and 6-nitrochrysene.ConclusionThese findings highlight black carbon and sulfate as primary drivers of PM2.5-related lung function impairment in COPD patients, likely acting through systemic metabolic perturbations. Our study supports targeted emission controls and suggests metabolic profiling as a potential approach for targeted prevention in vulnerable populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03812-1.

Introduction

Ambient particulate matter (PM) pollution is the leading risk factor for global disease burden, contributing to 8% of total disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) in 2021, with substantial adverse effects on respiratory health [1]. Patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are more susceptible to harmful effects of PM pollution [2], as the pre-existing lung damage in COPD patients exacerbates the inflammatory response to air pollution [3]. COPD contributes significantly to the respiratory disease burden in China. It is crucial to clarify the health impacts and mechanisms of PM exposure in COPD patients.

Previous studies have identified heterogeneous effects of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) on health outcomes in patients with COPD across different regions, potentially due to variations in PM2.5 compositions [4, 5]. Chemical components such as sulfate (SO42−), nitrate (NO3−), ammonium (NH4+), chloride (Cl−), organic matter (OM), and black carbon (BC) may exert distinct physiological effects on lung function [6, 7]. Despite evidence linking PM2.5 exposure to lung function decline, the mechanisms by which its individual components contribute to respiratory health impairment remain poorly understood. Emerging evidence suggests that different chemical constituents of PM2.5 may trigger distinct biological responses via oxidative stress, immune activation, and disruption of microbial homeostasis [8]. These component-specific biological perturbations likely manifest as alterations in circulating metabolites.

A metabolomic approach could offer valuable insights into underlying pathways. Metabolomic profiling is a powerful tool for identifying the body’s dynamic response to environmental exposures, providing a deeper understanding of how pollutant exposure may lead to adverse health outcomes [9]. Recent studies have suggested that short-term exposure to PM2.5 could lead to metabolomic changes [10, 11]. However, the role of metabolites in the association between PM2.5 components and lung function among COPD patients has not been studied. Peak expiratory flow (PEF) measurement is a useful indicator to monitor airflow obstruction on a day-to-day basis and has been widely used in panel studies on short-term effects of air pollution among patients with COPD [12]. Identifying metabolomic signatures that mediate the effects of PM2.5 components on PEF could provide valuable information for understanding the mechanisms of air pollution-induced detrimental effects in COPD.

This panel study aims to address these gaps by examining the associations between PM2.5 components and PEF in stable COPD patients, and exploring the mediating role of untargeted metabolites. By elucidating the differential effects of PM2.5 components and identifying potential metabolic pathways, we aim to advance understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying air pollution-induced lung function decline in COPD.

Materials and methods

Participants and study design

In this longitudinal panel study, we recruited 32 stable COPD patients in Beijing during March 2018 to May 2019. The inclusion criteria were: (1) have a confirmed diagnosis of COPD based on pulmonary function test results; (2) age ≥ 40 years; (3) resident in Beijing for at least three years; (4) never smokers or ex-smokers who have quit for more than 1 year.

Baseline information including demographics, lifestyle, and medical history were collected by trained research staff using a questionnaire at recruitment. Each participant was followed-up for four one-month periods across different seasons. Blood, urine, and sputum samples were collected at the start and end of each one-month period (8 visits per person) and immediately stored at -80℃. PEF and respiratory symptoms were measured daily during each one-month period, resulting in a total of 3363 observations (Fig. 1). The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital (2017-ke-191), and signed informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Exposure assessment

Data on daily concentrations of PM2.5 and its inorganic chemical components, including SO42−, NO3−, NH4+, and Cl−, were obtained from the ChinaHighAirPollutants (CHAP) dataset (https://zenodo.org/communities/chap). Concentrations of PM2.5 and its components at 1 km spatial resolution were estimated using a four-dimensional spatiotemporal deep forest model integrating a high-density observation network, satellite retrievals, atmospheric reanalyses, and model simulations. Modelled PM₂.₅ concentrations from CHAP were in strong agreement with measurements from Beijing monitoring stations during 2018–2019 (Pearson r = 0.931, R² = 0.866). The coefficients of determination between estimated PM2.5 components and ground-based observations ranged from 0.66 to 0.75 [13]. OM and BC were obtained from the Tracking Air Pollution in China (TAP) database (http://tapdata.org.cn) with a spatial resolution of 10 km [14]. The resident addresses of participants were distributed across 9 different districts in Beijing (Figure S1). No participant changed residence during the study period. We assigned daily concentrations of PM2.5 and its components to each participant according to their resident address during the study period. Daily temperature and relative humidity data were collected from the China Meteorological Data Service Center (http://data.cma.cn/en).

Untargeted metabolomics

A total of 213 fasting serum blood samples contributed by 32 COPD patients at up to 8 time points were used for untargeted metabolomic analysis. Detailed metabolomics procedures are described in the Supplementary Material. Briefly, metabolomic profiling was performed by Beijing Protein Innovation Co., Ltd using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS). Pooled quality control (QC) samples were applied to condition the analytical platform and evaluate intra-study reproducibility [15] (Figure S2). Features with > 30% missing values or intra-sample coefficient of variation (CV) > 0.3 in QC samples were removed. Missing values were imputed using half the minimum detected value for each feature. Peak intensities were log-transformed to reduce skewness. Since all samples were processed in a single batch, no batch effects were present. MZmine [16] was used to process metabolomic data, including mass detection, chromatogram building, peak deconvolution, and peak alignment. Metabolites were identified by matching their mass-to-charge (m/z) ratio and retention time with HMDB database. According to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) guidelines, these annotations correspond to MSI Level 2. Metabolites were quantified using area-under-the-curve. After filtering out metabolites with an intra-sample coefficient of variation (CV) > 0.3 in the QC samples, a total of 1 482 metabolites were retained, including 886 in positive ion mode and 596 the negative ion mode. Of these, 702 features in positive mode and 525 features in negative mode were putatively annotated, with 184 and 71 features remained unidentified, respectively.

Pulmonary function

Forced spirometry measures, including forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), PEF, and forced expiratory flow (FEF), were performed in respiratory department of Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital at baseline and follow-up visits. At baseline, the research staff distributed a Mini-Wright standard peak flow meter to the participants and educated them on how to use it. Participants were required to carry out triplicate PEF measurements every morning before medication throughout the study period. The maximum value of 3 daily PEF measurements was used as the primary outcome. Participants were encouraged to contact the research team if necessary during the follow-up period.

Statistical analysis

Linear mixed model with a subject-specific random intercept was used to investigate the association between daily PM2.5 components and PEF. All 3363 daily observations with PEF measurements from 32 participants were included in this analysis. Covariates were selected based on prior knowledge, including age, sex, smoking pack-years, body mass index (BMI), GOLD stage, passive smoking, asthma, month, medication use (LABA, LAMA, and ICS), outdoor activity (Yes/No), temperature, and humidity. Given the high correlation between PM2.5 components (Table S1), we chose single-pollutant model to avoid multicollinearity. Different exposure lag times were modeled and compared, including single-day lags from 0 to 7 days and moving average lags of 0–3 days, 4–7 days, and 0–7 days. We estimated the changes in daily PEF associated with each interquartile range (IQR) increase in PM2.5 and its components. We reported β coefficients representing the main effects of the PM2.5 components on PEF in the linear mixed models, rather than interaction terms with time. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by stratifying the models according to daily outdoor activity. To better disentangle the independent effect of highly correlated PM2.5 components, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using Generalized Weighted Quantile Sum (gWQS) regression. The model included the six PM2.5 components as the mixture, with the same covariates as the primary models.

We adopted a meet-in-the-middle strategy to identify potential metabolites underlying the relationship between PM2.5 components and PEF. We examined the association of metabolomics with PM2.5 components and PEF separately, followed by identifying overlapping metabolites and performing mediation analysis. A total of 208 observations from 32 participants with complete daily PEF and metabolome data were included in the analysis. Exposure was defined as the 0–7 day moving average concentration prior to blood sampling, and serum metabolites and PEF were assessed on the same day to maintain temporal coherence in the mediation framework. With the current sample size, the study had 80% power to detect a 20% change in metabolite levels per IQR increase in PM2.5 concentration at a two-sided α of 0.05. First, we performed a metabolomic association analysis for PEF. We used a linear mixed model to analyze the association between each metabolite and PEF. P-values and false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted q-values were calculated to assess statistical significance. Pathway analysis was conducted for metabolites with P-values < 0.05 using Mummichog [17]. Pathways with an overlap size ≥ 3 and P < 0.05 were considered significant. Second, we constructed separate linear mixed models to identify metabolites associated with PM2.5 and its components. Metabolite levels served as the dependent variables, and a moving average exposure of 0–7 days were used in these models. We identified metabolic pathways associated with different pollutant components. Third, we identified metabolites that achieved nominal significance (P < 0.05) or FDR-adjusted significance (q < 0.05) in both PEF and PM2.5 components association analyses (Steps 1 and 2) as potential mediators. Then we performed a formal mediation analysis to test the mediation effects and estimate the effect sizes using the ml_mediation program in Stata, which could handle repeated measures nested within persons. Bootstrap methods were used to calculate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for mediated effects. All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.1.2, R core Team), Stata (version 15.1, StataCorp), and Python (version 3.12.5, Python Software Foundation).

Results

The mean age of participants was 64.8 (SD: 5.7) years. Most participants were men (90.6%) and ever-smokers (87.5%). The mean FEV1/FVC at baseline was 0.48 (SD: 0.12). The mean PEF at baseline was 227.3 (SD: 96.2) L/min (Table 1). 19 (59.3%) participants completed all 8 visits (Table S2). Baseline characteristics were generally comparable, except for a higher proportion of males in the complete follow-up group (Table S3). The distribution of daily PM2.5 and its components during the study period was shown in Table S4. The mean concentration of PM2.5 was 46.62 (SD: 31.49) µg/m3. OM had the highest daily mean concentration of 11.18 (SD: 7.27) µg/m3 among various PM2.5 components.

The associations between PM2.5 components at different lags and PEF are shown in Table S5. We found negative associations between 7-day moving average concentrations (lag0-7) of PM2.5 and daily PEF in COPD patients. An IQR increase in PM2.5 concentration was associated with a 4.64 (2.12 ~ 7.17) L/min decrease in PEF. PEF changes associated with each IQR increase in PM2.5 components were − 4.78 (-8.82 ~ -0.73) L/min for sulfate, -2.61 (-5.14 ~ -0.08) L/min for nitrate, -2.54 (-5.65 ~ 0.56) L/min for ammonium, 1.84 (-3.09 ~ 6.77) L/min for chloride, -3.82 (-6.79 ~ -0.85) L/min for organic matter, and − 5.88 (-8.76 ~ -3.01) L/min for black carbon, respectively (Table 2). Sensitivity analyses stratified by outdoor activity showed that the negative associations between PM2.5 components and PEF were stronger on days with outdoor activity (Table S6). In gWQS regression model, the overall mixture effect was significantly negatively associated with PEF (ψ = -6.70, SE = 2.89, p = 0.021). Black carbon contributed the largest weight (74.5%), followed by sulfate (23.6%) (Table S7), which is consistent with the single-pollutant results.

A total of 180 metabolite signals were associated with PEF at a nominal significant level (P < 0.05) (Table S8), mainly lipids and lipid-like molecules (19.4%), organoheterocyclic compounds (18.9%), organic acids and derivatives (13.9%), and benzenoids (13.3%). Twelve metabolites reached the FDR adjusted significance level (q < 0.05). Pathway enrichment analysis identified 2 pathways with an overlap size greater than or equal to 3 and P < 0.05 (Fig. 2): Urea cycle/amino group metabolism (P = 0.041, q = 0.095) and Bile acid biosynthesis (P = 0.012, q = 0.085). The urea cycle and amino acid metabolism pathways were primarily driven by Creatine, Phenylethanolamine, and L-Adrenaline, while bile acid biosynthesis was mainly contributed by Taurochenodeoxycholate, Calcidiol, and 3alpha,7alpha-Dihydroxy-5beta-cholestan-26-al (Table S9).

Metabolic signals associated with PM2.5 and its components were shown in Table S10 ~ S16. At a nominal significance level (P < 0.05), there were 63 metabolites associated with PM2.5, 54 metabolites associated with SO42−, 82 metabolites associated with NO3−, 63 metabolites associated with NH4+, 114 metabolites associated with Cl−, 70 metabolites associated with OM, and 84 metabolites associated with BC. Several associations remained statistically significant after FDR adjustment. Specifically, SO4²⁻ was associated with 2-Methyl-3-ketovaleric acid (q = 0.022), NO3⁻ was associated with 1-Pentanesulfenothioic acid (q = 0.008), and Cl⁻ was associated with 5-Iodo-2′-deoxyuridine triphosphate (q = 0.018) and PC(20:2(11Z,14Z)/22:6(5Z,7Z,10Z,13Z,16Z,19Z)-OH(4)) (q = 0.024). The different effects of PM2.5 and its components on metabolites were presented in Figure S3-S9. Linoleate metabolism and purine metabolism were metabolic pathways associated with multiple PM2.5 components. Valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation, glutathione metabolism, arachidonic acid metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, and aspartate and asparagine metabolism were exclusively associated with BC exposure. Ascorbate and aldarate metabolism was exclusively associated with NO3⁻ exposure (Table 3).

There were 13 metabolite signals associated with both PM2.5 exposure and daily PEF, and these metabolites were included in the mediation analysis. No metabolites met the dual-significance criterion when FDR-adjusted significance was applied (Table S17). Therefore, the 13 metabolites identified under the nominal significance threshold were included in the mediation analysis. Nb-arachidoyltryptamine (HMDB0040817) and 6-nitrochrysene (HMDB0247097) mediated the association between PM2.5 components and PEF, with mediation proportions ranging from 15% to 33%. Discussion

This panel study provides novel insights into the relationship between short-term exposure to PM2.5 and its components with daily PEF in COPD patients. We found that PM2.5 concentration was associated with decreased PEF, with SO42− and BC components having stronger adverse effects on PEF. By integrating untargeted metabolomics, we identified metabolites and pathways associated with both PM2.5 components exposure and PEF, shedding light on the mechanism by which PM2.5 components affect lung function in COPD patients.

PEF, a measure of airway obstruction, has been shown to be affected by short-term exposure to particulate matter pollution in healthy adults [18]. However, the association between particulate matter exposure and PEF in COPD patients was inconsistent. Two studies conducted in London, UK reported a null association between particulate matter and PEF [19, 20]. In contrast, panel studies conducted in other countries found that PM2.5 exposure was associated with reduced PEF in COPD patients [6, 21]. Our results also support the detrimental effects of PM2.5 on PEF in COPD patients. The discrepancy in these results may be attributed to the differences in the chemical composition of PM2.5 in different regions. The proportion of coal-related components such as SO42− in PM2.5 in the UK may be relatively lower than that in China. Therefore, we dissected the effects of individual PM2.5 components and observed a more pronounced effect of SO42− and BC on PEF as compared with other components. Similarly, SO42− in PM2.5 has also been found to be associated with reduced PEF among asthma patients [22]. These findings suggest that the toxicity of PM2.5 is influenced by its chemical composition. Special focus should be paid on specific harmful components of PM2.5 rather than total PM2.5 mass alone. Macro-scale pollutant control should focus air-quality management on the key sources, regions, and time windows for sulfate and black carbon. Priority should be given to high-emitting sources of sulfate and black carbon: cutting SO42− emission from coal-fired power plants and industrial boilers, curbing diesel-vehicle exhaust, and restricting high-sulfur fuels. Regionally coordinated, differentiated measures are essential - especially in sulfate-prone areas such as the North China Plain and in black-carbon hotspots like rural northern China during winter—while strengthening joint governance between the transport and energy sectors. Long-term technology pathways should accelerate the energy transition (renewables replacing coal) and transport electrification to eliminate these pollutants at the source.

We further identified metabolomic markers and pathways of PEF changes in COPD. There are 12 metabolites associated with PEF after FDR adjustment, mainly lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic acids and derivatives, and organoheterocyclic compounds.

Urea cycle/amino group metabolism and Bile acid biosynthesis pathways are associated with PEF changes. These pathways are involved in inflammation, oxidative stress, and energy metabolism, which are relevant to COPD pathophysiology [23]. Urea cycle/amino group metabolism pathway has been implicated as a mediator between air pollution and health markers among healthy volunteers in a previous study [24]. Perturbations in this metabolic pathways due to PM2.5 exposure may lead to lung function changes. Few studies have investigated the metabolomic profile of lung function in COPD patients. A large study based on European Prospective Investigation into Cancer-Norfolk (EPIC-Norfol) identified metabolites associated with FEV1 and FEV1/FVC, and these metabolites were enriched in amino acid metabolism pathways [25]. These findings indicate that amino acid metabolism plays an important role in affecting lung function. Functional studies such as in vitro experiments and animal model studies could help elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying the observed associations in the future.

Air pollution exposure could induce metabolome changes. Previous study identified untargeted metabolomic signatures and various metabolic pathways associated with short-term exposure to air pollution [10]. In addition, PM2.5 components had different effects on the metabolome in healthy men [26]. However, the differential effects of PM2.5 components on metabolome in COPD patients have not been studied before. Our study fills this gap and reveals distinct metabolic pathways associated with different PM2.5 components. Linoleate metabolism was exclusively associated with BC component, supporting the potential role of lipid peroxidation in BC-induced toxicity [27]. Ascorbate (Vitamin C) and aldarate metabolism and purine metabolism were specifically linked to NO3−, suggesting oxidative stress and DNA damage as potential mechanisms. These findings underscore the complexity of PM2.5-induced health effects and the importance of considering component-specific mechanisms.

More importantly, this study confirms the mediation role of metabolites between PM2.5 exposure and PEF decrease in COPD patients. Very few studies have examined the overlap between metabolites of air pollution and lung function within the same study [28]. We used the MITM approach to identify intermediate metabolic markers involved in health effects related to PM2.5 exposure. Nb‑arachidoyltryptamine and 6‑nitrochrysene were found to mediate the effects of multiple PM2.5 components. Nb‑arachidoyltryptamine is a tryptamine derivative that may reflect perturbations in tryptophan metabolism, which has been implicated in COPD exacerbation [23]. 6‑nitrochrysene is a nitro‑polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon metabolite associated with environmental combustion products and has been linked to reactive oxygen species formation and DNA damage, which are relevant to airway inflammation and pulmonary function decline. Additionally, we identified metabolites that showed a statistically significant mediation effect, including (+)-Epibatidine, anordrin, and androstane-3,17-diol 17-sulfate. Future research is needed to validate these metabolites as predictive markers for PM2.5-related respiratory effects.

The main strength of our study is the multiple repeated measurements of PEF and metabolome, which allowed us to identify intermediate metabolomic markers between exposure and lung function. However, our study also has limitations. The sample size is relatively small (208 metabolomic observations), limiting the statistical power to detect weak associations. Since few metabolites could reach the significance level after FDR adjustment, we used nominal significance level to select potential mediators. This approach, while necessary to maintain power, increases the risk of false discovery in high-dimensional metabolomics data. Therefore, the primary results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, and future independent validation in larger cohorts is needed. Daily PM2.5 composition data are based on address assignment rather than personal monitoring, but we use the CHAP database with high resolution and accuracy to minimize exposure misclassification. Finally, the identified metabolic markers and pathways require external validation in large studies and diverse populations.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrated that short-term exposure to PM2.5 and its components was associated with a reduction in PEF in stable COPD patients, with SO42− and BC showing most significant detrimental effects. Health-risk-oriented monitoring and early-warning systems should also be enhanced by adding real-time sulfate/black-carbon monitoring stations in densely populated areas, issuing risk maps integrated with health data, and dynamically adjusting control priorities. We identified specific metabolites and metabolic pathways associated with PEF and various PM2.5 components. These findings suggest that metabolites play crucial roles in the pathophysiology of PM2.5-related respiratory effects. Future research should focus on validating the identified metabolic biomarkers in larger cohorts and developing targeted prevention strategies to mitigate the adverse effect of specific PM2.5 components in COPD patients.
