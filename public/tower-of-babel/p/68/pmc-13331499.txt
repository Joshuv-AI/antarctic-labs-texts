Asymmetric calcification of the extracardiac Fontan conduit: Quantitative insights from long-term computed tomography imaging
Authors: Yuchen Cao, Masaaki Koide, Masafumi Yashima, Aya Miyazaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe long-term structural integrity of expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) conduits used for extracardiac total cavopulmonary connection (EC-TCPC) is not well established. Although conduit calcification has been reported, quantitative data remain limited. We retrospectively analyzed 20 EC-TCPC patients who underwent computed tomography more than 5 years after Fontan completion (median: 13.7 years). Cross-sectional area (CSA) was measured at five sites and expressed as a percentage of the original CSA (%CSA), and calcification volume was indexed to body surface area. Median values of mean and minimum %CSA were 74.0% and 65.5%, respectively. %CSA correlated inversely with calcification volume index (ρ = −0.47, P = 0.04). Calcification was greater along the lesser curvature than the greater curvature (0.66 vs. 0.20 mm3/m2, P < 0.001). These findings suggest curvature-specific vulnerability in ePTFE conduits and support the importance of long-term structural surveillance in Fontan patients.

INTRODUCTION

The extracardiac total cavopulmonary connection (EC-TCPC) using expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) conduits is the most commonly used Fontan modification for patients with single-ventricle physiology.[1] Although this technique provides favorable early outcomes and simplifies surgical access, its long-term structural durability remains debatable and warrants further research.[2] In particular, despite documented evidence of late complications such as conduit narrowing and calcification, quantitative data on their progression and anatomical distribution remain limited.[34]

Vascular calcification is influenced by prosthetic materials and local hemodynamics, particularly oscillatory shear stress.[5] Similar mechanisms may contribute to calcification in ePTFE conduits used for EC-TCPC, where complex flow patterns are inherent. Simulation studies have suggested that disturbed flow promotes focal mineralization[67]; however, clinical validation in this setting remains lacking.

This study aimed to evaluate the extent and spatial pattern of calcification in long-term EC-TCPC conduits using computed tomography (CT) imaging and explore potential hemodynamic influences through supplemental analysis in a representative case.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 20 patients who underwent EC-TCPC at our institution and had CT performed more than 5 years postoperatively (August 2015–April 2024). We analyzed only the most recent CT scan for each patient.

Contrast-enhanced CT was used to evaluate the cross-sectional area (CSA) of the conduit at five evenly spaced sites using Aquarius iNtuition (TeraRecon, the USA). The average CSA was expressed as a percentage of the theoretical original CSA derived from the conduit size:

%CSA = (Average CSA/Theoretical CSA) × 100.

The theoretical original CSA was calculated from the nominal conduit diameter assuming a circular cross-section: CSA = π × (d/2)2, where d represents the labeled inner diameter of the implanted conduit. The average CSA was measured by manual tracing of the actual conduit lumen on contrast-enhanced CT images and therefore did not presuppose a circular geometry. The %CSA thus represents the ratio of the measured luminal area to the theoretical circular area based on the original conduit size.

The calcification volume (Ca volume, mm3) was quantified using noncontrast CT and Ziostation2 VINCENT (Ziosoft, Japan) and indexed to body surface area. Volumes were computed separately for the lesser and greater curvatures. The lesser curvature was defined as the inner aspect of the conduit arc, independent of anatomical orientation. Conduit cross-sections were aligned perpendicular to the conduit axis according to its direction of curvature, and calcification volumes were measured for the lesser (inner) curvature segment and the total conduit; the greater (outer) curvature volume was calculated as the difference between total and lesser curvature volumes. In all patients, noncontrast CT for calcium volume quantification and contrast-enhanced CT for CSA measurement were performed during the same imaging session on the same day.

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analysis was performed in one representative case using the vessel geometry reconstructed from CT and meshed in ANSYS ICEM CFD 16.0. The inlet flows were derived from phase-contrast magnetic resonance imaging and estimated based on typical Fontan physiology, whereas the outlet boundary conditions mimicked pulmonary vascular resistance. Oscillatory shear index was computed using OpenFOAM. Blood was assumed to behave as an incompressible Newtonian fluid (density 1060 kg/m3, viscosity 0.004 Pa·s).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Jichi Medical University, Japan).[8] Continuous variables were expressed as medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]) and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Associations were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. A power regression curve was fitted for visualization of the trend. The Wilcoxon signed–rank test was used to compare calcification volume indices between the lesser and greater curvatures. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to estimate the 10- and 20-year freedom from %CSA falling to ≤70%. Observation time was defined as the interval from EC-TCPC completion to the date of the most recent CT scan. Patients with %CSA ≤70% at CT were defined as having reached the event at that time point; patients with %CSA >70% at CT were censored. Because only a single CT scan per patient was analyzed, this Kaplan–Meier analysis represents a cross-sectional approximation of event-free survival rather than a prospective longitudinal event capture. The threshold of %CSA ≤70% (representing a ≥30% reduction in conduit CSA relative to the original size) was selected as the event criterion for the Kaplan–Meier analysis, based on prior reports demonstrating that transcatheter intervention for restrictive extracardiac conduits was typically performed at a median CSA reduction of approximately 30% from the nominal conduit size.[910] In an exploratory analysis, patients were additionally stratified by the median total calcification volume index into high- and low-calcification groups, and Kaplan–Meier analysis with the log-rank test was used to compare event-free survival between groups.

RESULTS

Of the 20 patients, 16 (80%) were male; 8 (40%) had right atrial isomerism, while no cases of left atrial isomerism were observed. A total of 2 (10%) patients had other congenital anomaly syndromes. The median age and weight at the time of EC-TCPC were 3.7 (IQR: 2.3–8.7) years and 12.8 (IQR: 10.1–14.6) kg, respectively. The average diameter of the conduit was 18.4 ± 1.7 mm. The median interval from EC-TCPC to CT imaging was 13.7 (IQR: 11.7–16.6) years.

Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that the 10- and 20-year freedom from %CSA falling to ≤70% were 94.4% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 66.6%–99.2%) and 44.3% (95% CI: 16.8%–68.9%), respectively [Figure 1].

In an exploratory analysis, patients were stratified by the median total calcification volume index (0.85 mm3/m2) into high-calcification (n = 10) and low-calcification (n = 10) groups. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significantly lower freedom from mean %CSA ≤70% in the high-calcification group compared with the low-calcification group (log-rank P < 0.001). Six of 10 patients in the high-calcification group reached the event, versus 2 of 10 in the low-calcification group. The median time to reaching mean %CSA ≤70% was 13.7 years in the high-calcification group and 23.4 years in the low-calcification group [Supplementary Figure 1].

The %CSA on CT showed a significant negative correlation with the calcification volume index (ρ = −0.47, P = 0.04) [Figure 2]. Calcification was significantly more prominent on the lesser curvature than on the greater curvature, with median calcification volume indices of 0.66 (IQR: 0.33–1.23) versus 0.20 (IQR: 0.12–0.39) mm3/m2, respectively (P < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

Our study provides insight into the long-term structural changes of ePTFE conduits in the Fontan circulation. We observed a significant inverse correlation between %CSA and the calcification volume index, suggesting that greater conduit narrowing is associated with a higher calcification burden. Although progressive conduit stenosis in long-term Fontan survivors has been described clinically,[211] the present study is, to our knowledge, the first to quantitatively demonstrate an inverse correlation between conduit calcification burden and CSA using CT.

Calcification was more pronounced along the lesser curvature of the conduit. In a representative case, CFD analysis demonstrated higher oscillatory shear stress in this region. Although based on a single case, this observation is consistent with prior studies suggesting that disturbed or oscillatory flow contributes to endothelial injury and localized calcification.[567] These findings support a potential link between conduit geometry, flow disturbance, and asymmetric calcification, which warrants further investigation in larger cohorts. We note that the anatomical correspondence of the lesser curvature to specific anatomical landmarks may vary depending on conduit routing and surgical technique, which could influence calcification distribution patterns. This anatomical variability should be considered when interpreting our results.

Regarding material properties, ePTFE has traditionally been considered biologically inert; however, accumulating evidence suggests that prosthetic calcification may occur through passive mineral deposition or inflammatory mechanisms, particularly in the presence of compliance mismatch with native vessels.[4] Our findings raise the possibility that such processes may contribute to long-term structural changes in Fontan conduits.

Clinically, the 20-year event-free survival rate for maintaining %CSA ≤70% declined to 44%, indicating progressive structural deterioration over time. Given that conduit narrowing may remain clinically silent, periodic imaging assessment may be considered, particularly in long-term survivors. Although no consensus-based imaging interval has been established specifically for extracardiac conduit assessment, prior reports indicate that conduit stenosis is frequently identified during routine rather than symptom-driven surveillance.[10] Our finding that 20-year freedom from significant conduit narrowing declined to 44% – with substantial deterioration occurring between 10 and 20 years post-Fontan – supports the rationale for periodic cross-sectional imaging in long-term EC-TCPC survivors. The optimal imaging modality and interval warrant prospective evaluation. Further studies are needed to identify patients at higher risk based on anatomical or hemodynamic factors.

Furthermore, an exploratory analysis stratifying patients by total calcification volume index demonstrated significantly lower freedom from mean %CSA ≤70% in the high-calcification group (log-rank P < 0.001), further supporting a link between calcification burden and conduit narrowing. However, as both variables were assessed at the same time point, causal inference remains limited pending prospective longitudinal studies.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective and cross-sectional design precludes assessment of temporal progression and causality. Second, the small sample size limits statistical power and generalizability. Third, CFD analysis was performed in a single representative case and should be considered hypothesis-generating. Fourth, the Kaplan–Meier analysis was constructed using a cross-sectional design, in which patients with %CSA ≤70% at CT were classified as having experienced the event at the time of CT. This approach may underestimate or overestimate the actual event-free survival rate, as the precise timing of conduit narrowing relative to the CT date cannot be determined from a single scan. Fifth, our cohort included a notably high proportion of patients with right atrial isomerism (40%), which is associated with complex systemic venous anatomy and variable conduit geometry.[12] This patient composition may have influenced the distribution of conduit calcification, and future studies should account for cardiac morphology as a potential confounding factor. Finally, potential selection bias related to CT indication and measurement variability may have influenced the results.

Despite these limitations, our findings highlight the potential role of calcification in conduit narrowing and underscore the importance of long-term structural assessment in Fontan patients. Future studies integrating imaging, hemodynamic analysis, and material characterization are warranted.
