Training pediatric residents on navigating moral events in contemporary ethical practice: curriculum design and evaluation
Authors: Chinyere O’Connor, Hunter Hamilton, Amanda McCarthy, Margarita Ortiz, S. Shahrukh Hashmi, Sienna Wu, Shriya Sachdeva, Kaleigh Riggs- Harpur, Aymen Mirza, Michelle S. Barratt, Emma Omoruyi, Terri Major-Kincade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMorally challenging situations arise frequently in contemporary pediatric practice. Despite this, few pediatric studies have examined how to support residents navigating these experiences or measure growth in this domain. To address this gap, we developed and piloted a longitudinal, multimodal curriculum designed to prepare residents to recognize and respond to moral challenges.MethodsCurriculum development followed Kern's six-step model and was informed by a previously published needs assessment and review of existing literature. A mixed-methods design evaluated the intervention across Kirkpatrick's levels of evaluation. We designed two complementary tools: (a) an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) emphasizing applied ethical reasoning and professional accountability, and (b) the Cognitive Affective Reflection on Ethical Experiences: Moral Encounters Tool (CARE-MET), which provided preliminary validity evidence for assessing residents' perceived self-efficacy in navigating moral events. Thematic and content analyses were conducted on qualitative data. Paired analyses of matched participants at timepoint 1 (T1) and timepoint 2 (T2) were conducted using Wilcoxon Rank Sum test and Cohen's d effect sizes.ResultsOf 81 eligible residents, 65 completed the CARE-MET at T1 and 52 of 57 eligible residents completed it at T2. Most participants (64/65, 97%) rated the curriculum as valuable. Residents identified value in the use of real clinical cases, diverse perspectives, and protected space for emotional processing. Paired analyses revealed significant improvements across five of six CARE-MET domains with large Cohens d (> 0.7). Experiencing moral distress was unchanged pre- and post-curriculum. Two separate cohorts of PGY-3 residents completed the OSCE at T1 (n = 12) and T2 (n = 14). Residents in both cohorts scored similarly high in empathic practice. Communicating the possibility of death without treatment was one significant difference between cohorts. This shift occurred more frequently at T2 than T1 (36% vs 0%, p = 0.024).ConclusionsThis curriculum offers a promising framework for preparing pediatric residents to navigate care that might be morally distressing yet ethically permissible. Gaps persisted in applied decision-making, legal understanding, and professional accountability. These findings support the need for longitudinal instruction in the moral, ethical, and legal dimensions of pediatric practice and highlight the value of developing tools capable of measuring growth in these domains.

Introduction

The Accreditation Council of Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) requires that training programs enhance "residents' knowledge of ethical principles foundational to medical professionalism” [1]. Formal ethics curricula exist to address this mandate, but pediatric practice poses distinct challenges [2–4]. Residents engage with a vulnerable population (minors) and their legal guardians, each holding differing perspectives on what is “good,” “right,” or “desirable” [5]. Professional, ethical, and legal guidance frequently diverge, especially under the pressures of contemporary practice. Moral obligations, an added fourth dimension in care, have become more salient in a pluralistic society where beliefs surrounding best interests, beneficence, and non-maleficence vary among caregivers. Tensions from these competing obligations are further amplified for residents by their limited agency and frequent positioning as bystanders to the moral event.

Moral events are frequently conflated with ethical dilemmas. Ethical dilemmas are clinical situations in which competing ethical, professional, or legal obligations create uncertainty about the “right” course of action [6–8]. Moral events, on the other hand, encompass experiences of moral tension, conflict, uncertainty, dilemma, or constraint. They may arise even when an ethically permissible course of action exists and often produce moral distress, defined as the psychological discomfort that arises as a result of the moral event [9, 10]. If not addressed, moral distress can accumulate into moral residue and contribute to burnout [9, 10].

Decades ago, evidence linked inadequate ethics training to low confidence in end-of-life (EOL) decision-making [11, 12]. While EOL care represents a profound moral event for pediatric trainees, far less attention has been given to navigating the moral events that arise from the accelerated pace of medical and technological advancement [5, 13, 14]. Teaching through complex moral events, which may or may not involve an ethical dilemma, can also provide a concrete pathway for reinforcing professionalism [15]. Practicing within the ethical dimensions of pediatric care requires residents to integrate communication skills, moral and ethical reasoning, legal awareness, and accountability [16]. These same competencies underpin core professionalism expectations, and unsupported moral events may erode residents’ capacity for empathy and sensitivity. The limited impact of existing ethics and humanities curricula may in part reflect insufficient attention to moral distress [5]. Consequently, teaching residents to reason through ethical dimensions of care and reflect on their emotional responses offers a unified approach to advancing both ethical and professionalism development.

To ensure that such training achieves its intended outcomes, systematic evaluation warrants the same rigor as curriculum design. Traditional assessments of ethics and professionalism have relied largely on self‑evaluations and the “hidden curriculum,” which provide limited validity evidence and rarely translate into observable outcomes [13, 17–21]. Other fields have implemented innovative assessment modalities through high‑fidelity simulation, standardized patients, bereaved parents, and longitudinal mentorship curricula [22–30]. In pediatrics, however, few studies have examined innovative approaches such as Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs) to systematically teach and assess the interconnectedness of morality, the ethical dimensions of care, and professionalism [31].

To address the gaps in preparing residents to recognize and respond to ethics related moral events, we had two specific aims: (1) Implement Kern’s 6-step approach to design and create a needs driven curriculum focused on navigating moral events in pediatric practice, and (2) Demonstrate the effectiveness of the curriculum using all four Kirkpatrick's levels of evaluation [32, 33].

Methods

Design

This was a prospective mixed-methods study evaluating the implementation and impact of a pediatric curriculum focused on navigating ethics related moral events. Curriculum development followed Kern’s six-step model, and evaluation was guided by Kirkpatrick’s four levels of evaluation outcomes [32, 33].

Participants

Categorical pediatric residents at UTHealth Houston were eligible to participate in the curriculum. Residents across all PGY levels were invited to complete pre- and post-curriculum surveys. Two separate cohorts of PGY-3 residents completed the OSCE during protected educational time, one prior to curriculum implementation and one following it. Approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (HSC-MS-22–0891). The curriculum became a required component of residents’ education while assessment instruments remained voluntary. All participants who completed assessment instruments provided informed consent.

Curriculum development (Kern’s six steps)

Problem and needs assessment (step 1 and 2)

A 2020 retrospective chart review showed that while fewer than 1% of PGY-1 residents encountered a patient death, 96% of PGY-2 and 78% of PGY-3 residents did. These encounters were most often in intensive care and oncology settings [34]. With “curricular crowding” and time constraints common barriers to educational interventions, and even less time available for ethics related interventions, needs assessments can be useful for setting educational priorities. Thus, to better understand the ethical and emotional challenges residents face in these environments, an initial needs assessment consisting of monthly handwritten reflective exercises was conducted from January to June 2023 with rotating groups of pediatric residents in the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU). The handwritten format encouraged tactile engagement and authenticity, capturing in-the-moment responses. An interpretive and descriptive qualitative approach analyzed the data. Detailed results from our needs assessment are published elsewhere [35].

Goals and objectives (step 3)

Moral dimensions that emerged from the needs assessment included: balancing clinical care with quality of life, emotional burden communicating challenging news to families, tension between parental expectations and clinical realities, managing personal emotions with professional boundaries, and poor collaboration with other clinical teams. Residents described ethical conflict using terms such as ‘uncomfortable,’ ‘distress,’ ‘powerless,’ ‘frustrating,’ ‘learning opportunity’ ‘emotionally exhausting,’ and ‘uncertainty’ [35]. Our findings informed the development of targeted goals and objectives which aligned with Entrustable Professional Activity 2 and ACGME competencies in patient- and family-centered communication, but extended into the moral and ethical dimensions of care [36, 37]. The curriculum emphasized moral reasoning, values negotiation, emotional self-awareness and resiliency as core components of professional practice. Specifically, our learner-centered goals were: (a) ensure all graduating residents possess skills on exploring, navigating and distinguishing between moral events and real or perceived ethical dilemmas regardless of their future career path; (b) guide trainees in recognizing and critically analyzing ethical principles in medical practice that affect patient care and provide educational infrastructure that allows residents to voice and share in values clarification with their peers; and (c) emphasize empathy as a core dimension of care by identifying and examining personal assumptions, exploring value conflicts, addressing moral distress, and tolerance of opposing viewpoints.

Educational strategies and implementation (step 4 and 5)

The curriculum content was informed by both our needs assessment findings and a review of existing literature on ethics education, moral distress, and professionalism development in pediatric residency training [13–15, 34, 35, 38]. The curriculum was delivered over 12 months (August 2023 to August 2024) through two formats: a large group session series and a small group session series with at least one session offered monthly. Each session was self-contained, did not require prereading, and was incorporated into existing protected educational time. These didactic sessions implemented semi-structured modules built around familiar clinical cases designed to surface ethical tensions and raw emotional responses. Sessions followed adult learning theory moving from pedagogy to andragogy emphasizing self-reflection, relevance of content to learners, and addressing intrinsic motivators to learn [39]. We delivered the curriculum through a multimodal format that included interdisciplinary facilitation, role-play exercises focused on communication and delivering bad news, and narrative reflection. Core content areas included assent and shared decision making, the best interest standard and parental authority, brain death, perceived non-beneficial treatment, medical futility, breadth of parental hope, EOL care, delivering bad news, and moral distress. An OSCE centered on an ethical dilemma was implemented as both a teaching modality and assessment strategy. Facilitators represented a range of disciplines, including pediatrics, ethics, social work, palliative care, medical humanities, law, and psychology. Since participation was determined by rotation schedule and shift assignment, attendance was variable within both the large and small group sessions. Given the monthly frequency and overlapping content across sessions, residents who missed a session were not disadvantaged in their overall curriculum exposure.

Large group sessions

Six large group sessions were held over one year and open to all residents on day shifts from inpatient rotations or inpatient-based electives. Sessions were approximately 90 min long. Each session presented a real clinical case selected by faculty to cover the session content areas. Facilitated discussion provided residents the opportunity to reflect on the case, explore differing perspectives and examine the moral and ethical considerations arising from the case or from their own experiences. By directing the dialogue toward the tensions, the constraints to action, or the ambiguities of each scenario, the interdisciplinary team helped residents explore the complexities of each case and promoted ethical deliberation. PollEverywhere allowed anonymous real-time responses in all large group sessions.

Session 1 started with the painting “The Doctor” by Sir Luke Fildes as a narrative anchor to open discussion on the breadth of parental hope in EOL care [40]. Subsequent sessions addressed content areas on assent and parental authority, best interests standards, delivering bad news, non-beneficial treatment, and moral distress. Notably, these sessions had overlapping themes and content areas as they arose naturally. The non-beneficial treatment session incorporated excerpts from Ursula K. Le Guin’s “The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas” and structured written reflection to examine complicity, moral agency, and professional obligation [41]. The delivering bad news session used role play in which faculty and residents interchanged as physician and parent roles, practicing communication strategies and receiving peer and faculty feedback. A psychologist participated in multiple sessions including those on EOL care and moral distress. This was helpful in addressing grief, emotional regulation, and the psychological dimensions of caring for dying children. The final session consisted of the OSCE which provided PGY-3 residents with protected time to apply communication skills and ethical reasoning in a simulated encounter followed by a one-hour debrief.

Small group sessions

Nine small group sessions were held throughout the year except during transition periods in August 2023 and July/August 2024. These one-hour sessions were held in the PICU with rotating residents (PGY-2 and PGY-3) and PICU fellows. Each session had groups of approximately 8 to 10 learners, and sessions were facilitated by two to three members of the same interprofessional team. Session content areas centered on moral distress, EOL care, brain death, medical futility or non-beneficial treatments, and assent. Similar to the large group sessions, there were overlapping themes. Sessions were based on active cases in the PICU at the time of each session. These small-group discussions provided facilitated ethical inquiry to explore learners' real-time clinical experiences. We utilized Jonsen’s Four Boxes as a structured lens for inquiry into the underlying ethical and moral challenges inherent in the cases [42]. As a narrative reflective exercise, the EOL session incorporated “The Aquarium,” an essay by Aleksandar Hemon published in The New Yorker [43]. Hemon’s account of his daughter’s illness and death offered residents a parental lens through which to examine the moral and emotional dimensions of EOL care.

Evaluation and feedback (step 6)

Rigorous evaluation explored the curriculum’s impact, sustainability, and feasibility. Assessment tools

Cognitive affective reflection on ethical experiences: moral encounters tool (CARE-MET)

The CARE-MET was developed by the study investigators to measure residents’ reflection on their perceived preparedness on moral-ethical dimensions of care including their comfort with EOL care, confidence in communicating with families of medically complex patients, and their experiences of moral distress. Since no existing instrument adequately addressed our study objectives, we adapted items from previously published tools and developed new items specific to pediatric resident learning needs [20, 28, 44]. The survey included terms such as ‘life-limiting conditions’ (LLCs) and EOL care language to represent clinical settings where moral events are most often encountered. LLCs were defined as medical conditions associated with a shortened life expectancy [45].

Validity evidence and reliability

Messick’s validity framework guided collection with preliminary validity evidence established through three sources [46]. Content validity was addressed through expert review for item clarity, relevance, and domain coverage. Revisions were based on consensus. Response process evidence was gathered through two rounds of feedback with eight pediatric residents to verify that items were interpreted as intended. These residents did not participate in the final CARE-MET survey. Internal structure evidence was assessed through exploratory factor analysis. This provided a six-factor solution that explained 63% of total variance and aligned conceptually with our anticipated domains. Internal consistency was good to excellent across five domains (Cronbach's alpha 0.682 to 0.896), with the moral distress domain negatively correlating with the other five domains as anticipated and consistent with its distinct construct. The overall Cronbach's alpha excluding moral distress was 0.781. The final instrument six factors included: factor 1, ‘goals of care decision-making’ (α = 0.896); factor 2, ‘supportive care for families’ (α = 0.750); factor 3, ‘ethical knowledge’ (α = 0.791); factor 4, ‘navigating difficult conversations’ (α = 0.855); factor 5, ‘providing end-of-life care’ (α = 0.803); and factor 6, ‘experiencing moral distress’ (α = 0.682). Given the exploratory nature of this work and the sample size relative to the number of items, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Table 1 shows the CARE-MET.

Timeline of responses

Residents completed the CARE-MET before (T1) and after (T2) curriculum intervention. T1 responses were obtained from residents in August 2023. These residents consisted of PGY-1, PGY-2, and PGY-3 residents. T2 assessments were completed either following 9 months of curriculum intervention to accommodate residents who were graduating or shortly after transition to the subsequent PGY level. To maintain consistency in describing the cohort, their labels refer to their PGY status at the time of participation in the survey. Specifically, our T2 residents consisted of incoming PGY-2 residents (completed survey August-October 2024), graduating PGY-3 residents (completed survey April-June 2024), and incoming PGY-3 residents (completed survey August-October 2024). New incoming PGY-1 residents did not complete the CARE-MET at T2 as they had not yet participated in the curriculum. Since residents were assessed at variable timepoints between 9 and 12 months, paired analysis was selected to account for individual variation in baseline scores and curriculum exposure.

An ethically and morally challenging Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE)

OSCE creation

Operationalization of a moral encounter within the professionalism milestone requires assessment strategies capable of evaluating empathy and the broader responsibilities of the field including accountability toward patients, society, and the profession [47]. Faculty experts in pediatrics, ethics, humanities, and law developed the case, the assessment tool, and led the debriefing. The case was conducted at the Surgical and Clinical Skills Center at McGovern Medical School.

The case

A standardized patient (SP) portrayed a 17‑year‑old girl with newly diagnosed Hodgkin’s lymphoma who was accompanied by her mother (another SP). Four weeks after biopsy, they come to the pediatrician’s office seeking alternatives to chemotherapy. The patient refuses chemotherapy after witnessing her father’s suffering and death from glioblastoma treatment. Her mother supports this decision. The scenario ran for 15 min.

Door note

The case synopsis informed the residents that the patient’s cancer had an “80% cure rate,” and that they were the only physician left in the clinic that day. Considering its highly curable nature, respecting the decision of a 17-year-old and her mother conflicted with the trainees’ professional obligation to protect the patient from probable death. While the trainees were not expected to resolve this ethical dilemma or “force” chemotherapy, they were expected to identify the moral and ethical issues inherent to the case, articulate values, communicate concerns, and make permissible recommendations including accepting alternative therapies.

SP preparation

SPs were trained over a 2-h period which included learning and practicing appropriate responses based on the case objectives. They were instructed that “Mason Marriot” would never agree to chemotherapy no matter what was said. The SPs were also coached on the performance assessment checklist and were comfortable describing acceptable trainee actions that met the objective requirements.

Learner debriefing

The OSCE was video‑streamed for faculty observation which was followed by a one‑hour debrief led by experts. During the debrief, trainees shared impressions while faculty gave feedback on ethical and legal reasoning, addressed behaviors and trends, answered questions, and corrected misconceptions.

Evaluative tool

Objective measurement

We created a new performance assessment tool consisting of a 30-question checklist to evaluate residents’ demonstration of empathic practice and professional accountability within a morally complex encounter (Table 2). This tool was developed based on existing literature and milestones [30, 31, 47–50]. Items included a dichotomous rating of observed actions (done/not done) along with a section rating empathic behaviors using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree.” At the end of the encounter, SPs also provided verbal feedback to the residents. Piloting

The OSCE was piloted with 32 PGY-1 residents in April 2023 to evaluate the feasibility, clarity, and relevance of the case and assessment tool. Residents and SPs completed separate debriefs to confirm functionality. Pilot data were used solely for refinement purposes and pilot participants were excluded from all comparative analyses. Data analysis

OSCE analysis

Fisher’s exact test compared individual checklist items between cohorts. Items were also grouped a priori into two domains based on expert consensus: observed behaviors reflecting empathic practice, and action items reflecting professional accountability. Domain level comparisons used averaged Likert scores (1–5) for empathic practice behaviors and summed scores for binary accountability action items. We analyzed comparisons between cohorts in both domains using Mann–Whitney U tests. Box plots provided visual representation of distributions at T1 and T2.

Trainee reflective writing underwent content analysis conducted by three members of the research team who did not facilitate the OSCE (AM, MO, and AM). These researchers graded reflections blinded to T1 or T2 cohorts to minimize bias. All reviewers independently coded the data using an a priori theoretical framework of ethical reasoning [51, 52]. When participants did not demonstrate a response corresponding to a specific ethical reasoning component, that component was coded as 0. This value indicated the absence of that component rather than a lower level of reasoning. Discrepancies were resolved using a consensus approach, with agreement defined as concurrence by at least two of the three reviewers. Chi-square tests compared the overall distribution of ethical reasoning levels between T1 and T2 cohorts.

CARE-MET analysis

Mann–Whitney U tests compared ordinal variables between two groups whereas comparisons involving three or more groups used Kruskal–Wallis tests. When Kruskal–Wallis results were statistically significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Dwass–Steel–Crichtlow–Fligner (DSCF) method to identify specific group differences. Paired analyses of participants at T1 and T2 used Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test with effect sizes calculated using Cohen’s d.

Residents’ evaluative responses on the perceived value and key takeaways from the curriculum underwent thematic analysis by two independent coders (MO, AM) who reached consensus.

A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses used statistical software R 4.5.0.

Results

Kirkpatrick level 1 (reaction)

Of 81 eligible pediatric residents, 65 (80%) completed the CARE-MET at T1. Of 57 eligible residents at T2, 52 (91%) completed the survey. Of those 52, the majority reported attending 2–3 large group sessions (67%), 18% attended one large group session, and 15% attended four or more large group sessions. Attendance was highest among PGY-3 residents with 20 out of 27 (74%) PGY-3 respondents reporting attending 2–3 sessions and 7 out of 27 (26%) attending 4 or more sessions.

Overall, 97% (64/65) of residents found the curriculum valuable, and all PGY-3 residents (n = 12 at T1 and n = 14 at T2) found the OSCE valuable. Gender differences

T1 analysis by gender indicated that male residents reported greater confidence in factor 1 (goals of care decision-making), factor 4 (navigating difficult conversations) as well as in factor 5 (providing end-of-life care). At T2, no significant gender differences were observed across any of the six factor domains.

Qualitative responses further illustrated residents' learning at both affective and cognitive levels. Resident description of the curriculum clustered into three domains: cognitive (e.g., 'thought-provoking,' 'enlightening,' 'reflective'), affective (e.g., 'therapeutic,' 'heart-breaking,' 'uncomfortable'), and evaluative (e.g., 'invaluable,' 'insightful,' 'informative’).

Kirkpatrick level 3 (behavior)

Pre- and post-OSCE comparisons provided data to evaluate changes in behavior. There were 12 out of 18 (67%) and 14 out of 18 (77%) PGY-3 residents in the T1 and T2 cohort respectively.

Discussion

Few studies have examined how pediatric residents cognitively and emotionally experience moral encounters, how such experiences shape professional agency, or how to systematically measure growth in these domains [14]. Without a structured framework, residents may struggle to reconcile ethical reasoning and professional responsibility within clinical practice. Our pilot curriculum prioritized areas that most frequently generate uncertainty, moral distress, and tension in pediatric residents. We grounded design in Kern’s six-step framework and residents’ lived experience to ensure the curriculum was needs driven and structured [32].

Curriculum assessment tools

Despite their potential, simulated patient encounters emphasizing professionalism and ethical dilemmas are sparsely utilized in pediatrics [31]. Our OSCE emphasized applied ethical reasoning, highlighting the moral, legal and ethical dimensions of patient care. The CARE-MET assessed learners' self-efficacy in multiple domains related to moral encounters including goals of care decision-making, supportive care for families, ethical knowledge, navigating difficult conversations, providing end-of-life care and experiencing moral distress. The two tools provided complementary perspectives on learner growth and provided pertinent insights into observed performance and perceived self-efficacy.

Curriculum outcomes

The intervention’s educational value was reflected in the consistently positive responses reported by residents at every training level. Residents described the sessions using both distressing and affirming language. They characterized them as simultaneously “uncomfortable,” and “invaluable,” or “heart-breaking,” and “empowering.” The coexistence of negative and positive descriptors is reflective of the curriculum’s design to surface moral distress as a common feature of ethical practice.

The curriculum’s first goal was to provide residents with the skills to recognize and navigate moral encounters in clinical practice. Paired analysis of CARE-MET demonstrated significant improvements in all domains except experiencing moral distress. The lack of reduction in moral distress was an anticipated result of a curriculum that aimed to build recognition and normalization of distress rather than resolve it. Changes at T2 compared to T1 in the CARE-MET were characterized by a large Cohen’s d which suggests the intervention likely contributed to the magnitude of growth. Gender differences observed at baseline were also eliminated post-curriculum. This finding is of interest given prior literature documenting gender disparities in comfort with EOL discussions though the single-center design limits generalizability of this result [20]. Further highlighting achievement of our first goal was the significant three-fold increase in Level 3 reasoning in the OSCE reflective writing at T2. Residents moved from traditional rule-based ethical reasoning toward more pluralistic, reflective reasoning at T2. This shift toward richer and more nuanced reflections suggests that curricular exposure supported resident advancement in the cognitive dimensions required to navigate moral encounters.

The curriculum’s second goal was to guide trainees in recognizing and analyzing ethical principles while providing the educational infrastructure for values clarification. One specific goal included the ability to differentiate between concepts of moral distress and ethical dilemmas. Resident feedback provided insight into how the curriculum added value to their professional and emotional development, as well as their overall engagement with the material. The curriculum’s emphasis on psychological safety provided a necessary foundation for such a development [53, 54]. One participant identified an improved capacity to distinguish between moral distress and ethical dilemmas while another reported a subjective increase in confidence during EOL discussions stating, “I feel much more confident when talking to families about end of life care even if I may never fully feel comfortable in that situation.” Residents also explored the clarification of their values through interconnectedness with their peers. Residents provided feedback such as, “there is always more than one answer” and “being presented with different scenarios and viewpoints helped open up my mind to other perspectives so that I am not instantly close minded when hearing a different opinion.” These responses illustrate a growing capacity to frame emotional experiences as opportunities for professional learning.

The curriculum’s third goal was to emphasize moral sensitivity through recognition of empathy as a core dimension of care. In the post-curriculum evaluation, residents described the curriculum using cognitive and affective descriptors reflective of moral sensitivity. While the OSCE results complemented this finding, they also highlighted a less linear trajectory in observed behavior. Consistently high empathic practice scores at both timepoints suggest that relational and communicative behaviors were already relatively established among third-year residents. The most notable behavioral shift was that T2 residents more frequently communicated death as a potential consequence of treatment refusal, even when navigating a complex moral encounter. Physician trainees commonly lack the formal training and tools to disclose emotionally difficult truths, and EOL communication skills are frequently underdeveloped in graduate medical education [12, 13, 55, 56]. This shift suggests that curricular exposure may have supported residents in overcoming the tendency to use vague or euphemistic language when discussing critical outcomes. During the OSCE debrief, T2 resident reflections also suggested increased awareness of the physician’s duty to voice recommendations clearly, even if decisional authority rests with parents or caregivers.

Ongoing challenges

Beyond the primary curriculum outcomes, two recurring patterns emerged from the OSCE as secondary findings for future refinement: uncertainty about legal obligations in pediatric decision-making, and hesitancy in assuming professional accountability in morally distressing encounters. The OSCE revealed that trainees’ skills fell into two categories: learned behaviors and learned medical professionalism competencies. Learned behaviors are more intuitive, such as showing compassion during a difficult conversation, while learned medical professionalism competencies, such as structured ethical and moral reasoning, require formal instruction to develop [57]. Both cohorts demonstrated persistent gaps in knowledge of the professional, legal, and ethical obligations necessary in more complex situations. This hesitancy is not surprising as residents occupy a constrained role within the medical hierarchy and can frequently become bystanders to complex moral events.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center study, our findings may not be generalizable to other institutions or residency programs. Second, the OSCE component had a limited sample size composed of third-year residents. This limited sample size was primarily due to the associated costs of simulation encounters and challenges in scheduling additional simulation sessions during protected resident time. These challenges restricted the OSCE to a single encounter. End-of-year timing and conflicting commitments by soon-to-be graduating residents further limited participation. The OSCE was also designed primarily as a formative learning and evaluation tool. Although we did not perform inter-rater reliability testing, there were multiple measures to ensure integrity of our evaluation process. We had 2 SPs per trainee and required mutual agreement by both on resident actions and observed behaviors. The performance assessment tool primarily consisted of dichotomous ratings. Additionally, faculty members monitored video recordings and confirmed consistency.

The OSCE involved highly controversial content. This was by design and allowed us to (1) examine residents’ approach to the morally challenging encounter and (2) identify strengths and deficiencies of residents’ ethical reasoning. Given the multiple challenging aspects of the case, we were surprised that the age of maturity became a primary point of contention among residents. While many residents did not recognize the patient as a minor requiring parental consent, they clarified during the debrief session that their ethical dilemma would remain regardless of age, unless the patient was a very young child. One resident articulated a broader concern that contemporary medical practice now spans a spectrum from “paternalistic practice to concierge medicine,” making it difficult to discern the resident’s appropriate role.

While the SP encounter was brief, it was followed by a resident self-assessment questionnaire and up to an hour-long debriefing session. In the debrief, most residents admitted to being protective of the physician–patient relationship. Residents confirmed that they prioritized relational continuity over transparency regarding prognosis. These reflections revealed the complexity of prognostic discussions and the tension residents experience between building a longitudinal therapeutic relationship and providing transparent communication about prognosis.

Although the CARE-MET demonstrates internal consistency, it is a self-report measure and is therefore subject to response bias and social desirability effects. As the tool is at an early stage of validation, findings from this tool should be interpreted with caution. We acknowledge that maturation and time-in-training may have influenced residents’ development. However, the magnitude of effect size and patterns of the change observed are more consistent with the effects of a structured educational intervention than with typical progression through residency. Additionally, while the curriculum emphasized experiential and reflective learning, its implementation was limited to one institution which makes it difficult to determine long-term retention or transferability across residency stages. These limitations suggest that future studies should employ multi-center samples, incorporate formal inter-rater reliability analyses, and include longitudinal follow-up to assess the sustained impact of the intervention.

Conclusion

Our curriculum offers early evidence that structured, case-based education on moral encounters can support residents’ growth in perceived self-efficacy, and ability to engage honestly with complex ethical challenges. Pairing curriculum design with complementary assessment tools allowed us to examine learner growth across multiple levels. Our findings suggest that while residents may be well equipped to respond empathically, they often remain insufficiently prepared to assume agency and navigate the moral, ethical, and legal dimensions of complex pediatric care. Professional accountability within morally fraught encounters remains a developmental frontier for residents, and a one year educational intervention is unlikely to fully address this challenge. The identified gaps of ethical and professional knowledge among third-year pediatric residents are likely systemic across pediatric training programs. Literature consistently documents inadequate ethics preparation in graduate medical education [3, 4, 12–14, 58]. Incorporating practical education on moral encounters into residency curricula may better prepare residents for the complexities of contemporary pediatric practice. The development of tools such as the CARE-MET represents an early but important step toward measuring growth in the cognitive, and affective dimensions of how residents navigate moral events in clinical practice. Ongoing psychometric validation will further strengthen the ability to evaluate these essential outcomes in medical trainees.
