Severe Hyponatremia Presenting With Mild Symptoms and Multifactorial Etiology
Authors: Nathaniel Lin, Muhammad Cheema, Usman Fahim, Abdullah Ali, Andrew Weber
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHyponatremia is a common electrolyte disturbance; however, serum sodium level of 99 mEq/L without severe symptoms or known duration is rare and noteworthy. We report a case of an 87-year-old female who presented with fatigue, decreased appetite, and mild emesis for the past 2 days in which blood work revealed a serum sodium level 99 mEq/L. The etiology of hyponatremia in an elderly patient may be complex due to higher likelihood of co-morbid medical conditions. Additionally, syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) can pose a diagnostic challenge as fewer than 50 % of patients have typical lab results that confirm the diagnosis (Ewout, November 2009)1.Keywords: Hyponatremia, SIADH, Urinary retention: multifactorial, Ciprofloxacin, Bladder, Outlet, Obstruction, Euvolemia, Osmolality, Sodium

Introduction

Hyponatremia is not only a common electrolyte disorder with potentially serious complications, but is also significantly more prevalent in the elderly population due to factors such as increased medication use and higher likelihood of co-morbid medical conditions.2 One of the more frequent causes of hyponatremia in the elderly is syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone (SIADH), which can be diagnostically challenging since fewer than 50 % of patients have typical lab1 results that confirm the diagnosis. We present a case of an 87-year-old female who presented with a serum sodium level 99 mEq/L without severe symptoms or known duration. The etiology of her hyponatremia was multifactorial.

Case report

An 87-year-old female presented to our emergency department after a fall at home. She was brought in by her son who noted no loss of consciousness or seizure activity. The patient was not able to provide a history in the emergency department. Her son reports for the past two days, she had seemed more confused than usual with new complaints of worsening fatigue, decreased appetite, and mild emesis. Her medical history includes chronic urinary retention managed with an intermittent chronic indwelling catheter and antibiotic suppression therapy involving sequential courses of nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, and ciprofloxacin, one at a time. Her son also reports she consumed large amounts of water for months, as she believed this would lower her risk of urinary tract infections.

On physical exam, she was afebrile with normal heart rate, blood pressure, and respiratory rate. She was alert and followed commands, but confused about recent events. She was clinically euvolemic with moist oral mucosa, normal skin turgor, 2+ capillary pulses, and normal capillary refill. She had dried perioral blood from the fall without evident bony injuries, focal motor deficits in extremities, facial asymmetry, nor soiling from bowel or urinary incontinence. Her abdomen was distended without shifting dullness or lower leg edema. Her foley catheter was changed in the emergency department which revealed 600 mL of post-void residual urine.

Initial lab work (Table 2) revealed serum sodium of 99 mEq/L, serum chloride of 67 mEq/L, serum bicarbonate of 20 mEq/L, serum potassium of 3.1 mEq/L, and anion gap calculated of 15.1. There were no previous lab work or sodium levels available. Her blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, and liver function tests were normal. Her serum osmolality was 227 mOsm/kg, random urine sodium was 40 mEq/L, and random urine osmolality was 206 mOsm/kg. Her urine specific gravity was 1.008. Her Thyroid Stimulating Hormone (TSH) was 1.190 uIU/ml, free thyroxine was 1.21 ng/dL, and AM cortisol was 18.2 mcg/dL. She had normal lactate and creatinine kinase levels. Chest x-ray did not show any signs of infections or suspicious masses. Computed tomography scan of abdomen and pelvis also revealed moderate-severe bilateral hydroureteronephrosis without obstructing stone.

The etiology of her hyponatremia was deemed multifactorial. She was clinically euvolemic, which was supported by her physical exam, normal blood urea nitrogen, and normal serum creatinine levels. SIADH emerged as a significant contributor to her hyponatremia, supported by several key observations (Table 1): euvolemia, urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg in the absence of hypothyroidism and hypocortisolism, and identifiable causes for her SIADH. Our patient was managed with 1200 mL fluid restriction. Serial basic metabolic panels were trended every 2 h. Sodium was corrected by 4–6 mEq per day without needing 3 % hypertonic saline or NaCl tabs. The patient’s sodium rose by 4–6 mEq per day and throughout the hospital course her non-severe symptoms resolved.

Discussion

We present a case of an elderly woman who had no life-threatening symptoms despite a hypotonic serum sodium level of 99 mEq/L. Previous studies have shown that the prevalence of severe hyponatremia (serum sodium level <125 mEq/L) was 4.5 % in geriatric hospital wards, 0.8 % in nongeriatric wards, and 10.3 % in the intensive care unit.3 Additionally, the severity of hyponatremic symptoms generally correlates with the degree of hyponatremia. 4 There are only a few documented patient cases of serum sodium levels that fall below 100 mEq/L presenting with minimal symptoms.

The differential diagnosis of euvolemic hypotonic hyponatremia includes beer potomania, tea and toast diet, self-induced water intoxication (eg, psychogenic polydipsia or a marathon event), SIADH, hypothyroidism, and glucocorticoid deficiency. Our patient’s urine osmolality of >100 mOsm/kg indicated ADH (antidiuretic hormone) was not suppressed, 5,6 implying large fluid intake was not the sole culprit. Decreased endogenous glucocorticoids have also been shown to increase ADH6 due to decrease inhibition of glucocorticoids on ADH production. Hypothyroidism also causes elevated ADH attributed to hypothyroidism-induced decrease cardiac output.7 Our patient’s normal TSH and AM cortisol ruled out these causes for our patient’s urine osmolality of >100 mOsm/kg.

Previous case studies have shown a link between hyponatremia and urinary retention in the elderly, with bladder wall distention potentially triggering vasopressin release and resulting in a SIADH-like presentation.8,9 Our patient exhibited bilateral hydronephrosis on imaging, leading us to consider urinary retention as a contributing factor to hyponatremia. In addition, several case reports have shown that ciprofloxacin has been associated with SIADH.10,11 Together, these features make SIADH a significant cause of our patient’s hyponatremia.

On the contrary, urine osmolality is typically higher than serum osmolality in SIADH.12 Our patient’s relatively lower urine osmolality and low-normal urine specific gravity suggests that decreased free water excretion from bladder outlet obstruction may have also contributed to her severe hyponatremia.

Initial clinical signs of acute hyponatremia, such as nausea and malaise, are typically observed when serum sodium levels fall below 125 to 130 mEq/L (Table 3). More severe symptoms like obtundation, coma, and seizures can manifest when sodium levels drop below 115 to 120 mEq/L (Table 3). However, patients with chronic hyponatremia may exhibit cerebral adaptation and remain asymptomatic despite serum sodium levels below 120 mmol/L. Chronic hyponatremia can present with vague symptoms like nausea, vomiting, and cognitive impairment. In acute hyponatremia, the rate of correction should be no more than 6–8 mEq/L per day to prevent osmotic demyelination syndrome. In chronic hyponatremia, the rate of correction should be no more than 10–12 mEq/L per day to prevent osmotic demyelination syndrome.13 In the absence of knowing the duration of our patient’s low sodium, we surmised that her normal lactate and creatine kinase argued against her having an unwitnessed seizure with postictal confusion. Chronic hyponatremia was more likely given the paucity of her symptoms.

Conclusion

We present a case of an elderly woman who had a sodium level of 99 mEq/L with mild symptoms. Literature supports our suspicion of chronic rather than acute hyponatremia and management is dependent upon the clinical scenario and suspected duration of hyponatremia rather than merely aggressively treating a very low sodium.
