Improving phenolic antioxidants in Illicium griffithii fruit through optimizing subcritical water extraction method
Authors: Om Prakash Arya, Indra Dutt Bhatt, Kaustubha Mohanty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Illicium griffithii is a medicinal and aromatic plant species found in the temperate broadleaf forests of the Northeast region of India. The fruit of this species has been traditionally used in ethnomedicine and holds significant economic potential. This study aimed to determine the optimal conditions for better recovery of phenolic antioxidants from Illicium griffithii fruit using subcritical water extraction. Box-Behnken Design (BBD) was used to assess the impact of temperature (100–220 °C), pressure (30–90 bar), and time (10–50 min) on the extraction yield of phenolic antioxidants. Total phenolic content (TPC) varied from 15.60 to 30.91 mg GAE/g dw; whereas total flavonoid content (TFC) from 1.48 to 2.44 mg QE/g dw; while DPPH from 19.23 to 24.70 µg AAE/g dw; and ABTS from 16.36 to 17.92 µg AAE/g dw. Under the optimal conditions (161.32 °C, 45 bar, and 30 min), the phytochemical (TPC- 30.91 ± 0.07 mg GAE/g dw; TFC- 2.07 ± 0.02 mg QE/g dw) recovery echoed the scavenging activities (DPPH − 23.27 ± 0.13 µg AAE/g dw; ABTS- 17.82 ± 0.03 µg AAE/g dw). The phenolic profile revealed the improved concentration (1.60 to 9.77 m/g), with predominant phenolic compounds such as gallic acid (9.77 mg/g), catechin (8.84 mg/g) and p-hydroxybenzoic acid (4.06 mg/g). Among the different process parameters, temperature has significantly influenced the recovery of the phenolic antioxidants in Illicium griffithii fruit. ATR-FTIR spectra confirmed the occurrence of alcoholic, phenolic, and aromatic compounds in the SWE extract, with variations in temperature and pressure notably influencing the aromatic and phenolic functional groups. Introduction

Plants have always been a vital source of diverse chemical compounds, contributing a key role in promoting human health and enhancing the importance of life. Phenolic compounds stand out as the primary class of secondary metabolites in plants. Phenolic compounds sourced from nature find application in the fields of nutrition, medicine, and cosmetics and hold promise as natural substitutes for synthetic substances that pose risks to human well-being1,2. The health benefits associated with phenolic compounds derived from diverse fruits have prompted a growing number of research investigations to explore effective methods to extract these compounds3. Phenolic compounds derived from plants are extracted using various methods, including traditional and modern approaches4–6. The crucial stages in obtaining high-quality extracts containing valuable bioactive compounds during production involve choosing the appropriate extraction technology and configuring the necessary extraction parameters5,7–9. The traditional extraction process necessitates using organic solvents to attain a substantial extraction output. However, these organic solvents frequently possess harmful properties and demand meticulous procedures to eliminate them, especially when the extracted material is intended for consumption as food or medicine7,10. Moreover, these conventional techniques possess low extraction rates, generate waste, and pose environmental and safety hazards10. There is significant scientific eagerness for utilizing environmentally friendly technologies to harness the potential of plants. Adopting these technologies helps enhance plant extracts’ safety, quality, and usability, minimizing or eliminating the reliance on organic solvents6,9,11. In recent years, various novel techniques, including subcritical water extraction (SWE), have been employed to extract phenolics from botanicals9,12. With the emphasis on environmentally friendly practices, extraction methods like SWE are suggested as substitutes for organic solvent use9,13,14. SWE is a procedure that involves using subcritical water at temperatures exceeding its boiling point (100 °C) but staying below its critical point (374 °C). This process is performed under high pressure to retain the water in its liquid state during the extraction. When the water temperature surpasses 100 °C, its dielectric constant resembles that of organic solvents, resulting in a polarity reduction, which enhances the solubility of compounds with low polarity10. Highly polar organic compounds are selectively extracted at low temperatures, whereas lesser polar ones are preferentially extracted at high temperatures7. These specific conditions lead to variations in the physical as well as chemical attributes of water, impacting its ability to dissolve diverse substances15. Additionally, extracts acquired through subcritical water extraction (SWE) do not retain any harmful toxic remnants. Consequently, these extracts can be directly employed as intermediate or final products within the food and pharmaceutical sectors7,10. Optimization of SWE ensures higher yields of target compounds with reduced energy consumption and process waste1,7,16. Further, the precise optimization minimizes post-extraction purification steps, reducing costs, time, and resources14. This aligns SWE with the principles of green chemistry, enhancing its sustainability and scalability for industrial applications1,17.

Illicium griffithii Hook. f. & Thoms is a medicinal and aromatic tree variety found in North-eastern region in India18. The dried fruits or pods of this tree have significant economic value and show promising potential in both the spice and pharmaceutical markets. These fruits possess carminative, stomachic, and galactagogic properties, making them useful for treating conditions like regurgitation, dyspepsia, abdominal pain, and food poisoning19,20. Owing to its aromatic and medicinal significance, various researchers have conducted studies using traditional methods to explore aspects such as essential oil composition, antimicrobial effects, antioxidant properties, cytotoxicity, and genetic variability19–24. While previous studies have explored its antioxidant and antimicrobial activities along with qualitative phytochemical profiling, comprehensive research focusing on the extraction, quantification, and optimization of phenolic antioxidants from this species remained limited. Given the medicinal importance of the species, there is lack of studies applying the green extraction methods to study the phenolic and antioxidant compounds. This study addresses that gap by employing subcritical water extraction (SWE), an eco-friendly and sustainable extraction technique, for the efficient recovery of phenolic antioxidants from Illicium griffithii fruit.

This investigation aims to comprehend the subcritical water extraction (SWE) of Illicium griffithii fruit and optimize the process parameters using the response surface methodology for improved extractability of the phenolics and antioxidants through greener routes. The independent variables, which include temperature (T), pressure (P), and extraction time (t) were investigated. The effect of independent variables (T, P, t) was assessed for total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC) as well as on antioxidant scavenging activity (DPPH, ABTS). Further, SWE extracts were assessed using HPLC and ATR-FTIR to detect the phenolic compounds and compositional variations in the extracts under different SWE conditions.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and 2,2’-Azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) were procured from Merck & Co. (Darmstadt, Germany). The phenolic standards, Folin-Ciocalteu, and Folin-Denis reagents were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, Missouri, USA). The reagents and other chemicals utilized were of analytical and HPLC grade, and the deionized water (Rions India Lab Water System, India) was used for the preparation of solutions.

Plant material

The mature fruit samples of Illicium griffithii were collected from the West Kameng district of Arunachal Pradesh, India. The plant material was taxonomically identified through consultation with scientists and by referencing the herbarium at the Botanical Survey of India, Arunachal Pradesh Regional Centre (BSI-APRC), Itanagar. A voucher specimen (Specimen No. G.B.P. 7901) has been deposited in the herbarium of G.B. Pant National Institute of Himalayan Environment (GBPNIHE), Almora, Uttarakhand, India, for future reference. The cleaned fruit materials were dried at room temperature, powdered, and stored in an airtight container for further analysis.

Subcritical water extraction (SWE) of Illicium griffithii fruit

Subcritical water extraction (SWE) of I. griffithii fruit was executed using a high-pressure reactor (Parr Instruments Company, USA). The reactor’s pressure was maintained using pure nitrogen gas (99.99% purity, Apollo Engineering Kolkata, India). The experiments were conducted in a 100 mL reaction vessel with 3 g of I. griffithii fruit sample and 60 mL of deionized water. The outer jacket of the extraction vessel was electrically heated, and samples in the reaction vessel were stirred continuously using a magnetic stirrer. Throughout the experiment, the rotor speed remained constant at 200 rpm, and both temperature and rotor speed were monitored using a reactor controller (Model: 4848, Parr Instruments Company). The samples were cooled within the same reactor with the help of an ice bath on completion of the reaction. Subsequently, the cooled extracts were filtered via a 125 mm filter paper (Merck, Filter Paper No. 1) and stored at 4℃ for subsequent analysis.

Phytochemical analysis of SWE extracts

Total phenolic content (TPC)

TPC of extracts were estimated using the Folin-Ciocalteu method8. Quantification was performed using a gallic acid (GA) standard curve prepared in water and the results were denoted as mg GAE/g dw.

Total flavonoid content (TFC)

TFC was calculated using the aluminium chloride colorimetric method8. Quantification of TFC was done based on a quercetin standard prepared in water, and the results were articulated in mg QE/g dw.

Antioxidant activity of SWE extracts

Antioxidant activity of SWE extracts was estimated utilizing the standard procedures for DPPH (2, 2- diphenyl1-picryylhydrazyl) assay; and ABTS (2,2’-Azino-bis-3-ethyl benzothiazoline-6-sulphonic acid) assay25. A standard curve of ascorbic acid was developed, and the results were quantified as µg AAE/g dw.

Optimization of SWE

Single-factor experiments for SWE

Single factor experiments were executed in triplicate to examine the impact and robustness of certain independent factors on total phenolic content (TPC). These factors included extraction temperature (T), extraction pressure (P), and extraction time (t) and studied within a specified range with temperature from 100 °C to 220 °C, pressure from 30 to 90 bar, and extraction time 10 to 50 min as possible extraction ranges for optimization utilizing response surface methodology (RSM). The effect of each factor was investigated by varying it while keeping the other factors at a constant level.

Optimization of SWE and statistical analysis

The impact of individual factors on TPC yield led to the development of the Box-Behnken Design (BBD) test to explore the interaction between T, P, t and their combined effect on the extraction yield of TPC. The SWE of I. griffithii fruit was optimized to evaluate the optimal extraction conditions for the maximum TPC, TFC, DPPH, and ABTS yield. RSM was used to assess the impact of extraction parameters and to optimize the process parameters for multiple responses. The extraction variable includes temperature (A, 130, 160, 190 °C), pressure (B, 45, 60, 75 bar), and extraction time (C, 10, 20, 30 min) as independent variables. HPLC analysis of SWE extracts

The SWE extract at optimum conditions was analyzed for phenolic compounds in High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) instrument (Waters, USA, Alliance e2695). The filtered extract (10µL) was injected into the RP-C18 (250mmx4.6 mm id, x 5 μm) column in HPLC system. The mobile phase comprised of 40:60 (v/v) mixture of methanol (0.1% orthophosphoric acid v/v) and ultrapure water. The characterization and quantification of various phenolic compounds in SWE extract were done using photodiode array detector outfitted with HPLC system8. The detector wavelength was 190–400 nm with a 40 min run time, and keeping 0.8 mL min− 1 flow rate. The peak characterization was done on comparing the retention time of the corresponding phenolic standards. Deionized water was used to prepare the standard stock solution (20 to 100 ppm).

ATR-FTIR analysis of SWE extracts

The compositional variations in SWE extracts of fruit samples of Illicium griffithii, were evaluated by ATR -FTIR spectroscopy (Perkin Elmer). The miniscule quantity of SWE extract kept in the sampler holder was scanned in 400 cm− 1 – 4000 cm− 1 stretch with the standard method27.

Results and discussion

Single factor experimental analysis

The impact of different process parameters viz., extraction temperature (T), extraction pressure (P), and extraction time (t) on the TPC yield of Illicium griffithii fruit were evaluated. Figure 1 illustrates the effect of three variables on the extraction yield of TPC from Illicium griffithii fruit using SWE. Particularly, extraction temperature is vital in the SWE process, as highlighted by different studies12. The influence of temperature on TPC yield within a range of 100 to 220 °C was evaluated, holding the pressure and extraction time constant at 60 bar and 20 min, respectively. The results indicated that TPC yield increased as the temperature was raised from 100 to 160 °C. However, a significant decrease in yield was detected when the temperature was further increased (190–220 °C) as shown in Fig. 1a, which aligns with findings from previous studies involving different plant materials12. The influence of extraction pressure was investigated within the 30 to 90 bar range, with the temperature and time kept constant at 160 °C and 20 min. The results exhibited an upward trend in TPC yield with increasing pressure from 30 to 60 bar, whereas the further increase in pressure (60–90 bar) decreased the TPC yield (Fig. 1b). Although pressure’s influence on SWE is typically considered to have minimal significance, it has some notable effects. Elevated pressure serves a dual purpose by facilitating the effective filling of the matrix pores with the solvent and maintaining water in its liquid state. The application of high pressure disrupts the cell matrix, leading to improved mass transfer processes and the resolution of air bubble issues within the matrix. These conditions generally enhance the solubility of compounds and the rate of desorption. Limited studies explored the chemical composition of SWE extracts in accordant with pressure variations. Consequently, the comprehensive impact of pressure in this context remains largely unexplored12. The impact of extraction time on the TPC yield was examined over a range of 10 to 50 min, with the temperature and pressure held constant at 160 °C and 60 bar. It was detected that the TPC yield improved as the extraction time was extended from 10 to 20 min. However, beyond this point, with further prolongation of extraction time, there was a decline in the yield (Fig. 1c). This decrease could potentially be attributed to the oxidation and degradation of polyphenolics extricate through the extended extraction period, a phenomenon noted in prior studies12,28. Based on the single-factor experimental analysis, the extraction conditions, including the extraction temperature (130, 160, 190 °C), extraction pressure (45, 60, 75 bar), and extraction time (10, 20, 30 min), were chosen for optimizing the extraction conditions using RSM.

Optimization of the subcritical water extraction

BBD was utilized for the process optimization of TPC, TFC, DPPH, and ABTS. The impact of three operational factors (temperature, pressure, and extraction duration) on both the effectiveness of the SWE and the characteristics of the resultant extracts was studied. The objective components of interest during the SWE were phenolic compounds (TPC and TFC) and the antioxidative capacity (DPPH and ABTS). The design parameters of the SWE and the corresponding observed values for individual response under varied conditions are unveiled in Table 1. The TPC varied between 15.60 and 30.91 mg GAE/g dw. The maximum TPC was associated with the intermediate temperature (160 °C), lower pressure (45 bar), and a longer extraction period (30 min) among the investigated factors. On the contrary, the lowest values were linked to a lower temperature (130 °C), moderate pressure (60 bar), and brief extraction time (10 min). The TFC values varied from 1.48 to 2.44 mg QE/g dw. The maximum TFC value aligned with the minimum investigated temperature (130 °C), intermediate pressure (60 bar), and an extended extraction time (30 min). Conversely, the least TFC value corresponds with intermediate temperature (160 °C), elevated pressure (75 bar), and an extended extraction time (30 min). Further, the in-vitro antioxidant activities in DPPH and ABTS assay also varied with varying the investigated variables. The maximum antioxidant activity in DPPH and ABTS assays were recorded at mid-temperature (160 °C), intermediate pressure (60 bar), and moderate extraction time (20 min). However, the lowest antioxidant activity in the DPPH assay was found at a lower temperature (130 °C), lower pressure (45 bar), and mid-extraction time (20 min). Further, for the ABTS assay, the lowest activity was recorded at higher temperatures (190 °C), high & mid pressures (75 & 60 bar), and lower extraction time (10–20 min).

The results indicated a gradual increase in TPC when the extraction temperature and time were increased, which shows that solvent-to-solute interaction was enhanced with increased water temperature and required less energy for the breakdown of solute-matrix interaction16. At higher temperature, the increase in TPC also increases the amount of free phenolic compounds. However, extraction over 160 °C in SWE causes a slight decrease in TPC, suggesting the degradation of phytochemicals at elevated temperature15,29–31. Further, the increase in pressure has also enhanced the TPC slightly. This is in line with the alteration of water solubility and polarity (dielectric constant) with a small increase of dielectric constant on increasing the pressure6. It was also evident that the polyphenolic yield increased on elongating the extraction time, as evident by a previous study30.

The model equations effectively depict the experimental values, as indicated in the coefficient of determination (R2 for TPC, TFC, DPPH, and ABTS (0.9967, 0.9993, 0.9993, 0.9998, respectively). Additionally, all four responses exhibited statistically significant regression for the model, as specified by the p-value (< 0.0001) in Table 3. The R2 value nearing 1 of the current models suggests that nearly all the variability in polyphenolic yield can be described by the model. Additionally, the significant F-value (≥ 237.20) and a low p-value (< 0.0001) indicate that the impact on the corresponding response variable is statistically significant12. The model’s adequacy was further assessed by the lack of fit testing, which was significant lack of fit for TPC and antioxidant activities (DPPH, ABTS values), as denoted by a p-value (p < 0.0001) and non-significant lack of fit for TFC (Table 3). The lack of significance in the lack-of-fit (LOF) test indicates that the differences between the observed and predicted values were within the expected range of random error from the replicated design points. A significant LOF, on the other hand, could compromise the model’s predictive accuracy and would require further improvement. The experimental data obtained was utilized to generate three-dimensional response surface plots and regression calculations that predict response values within the experimental domain. ANOVA indicates that these equations can effectively describe the behaviour of phenolic and antioxidant values.

Influence of independent variables on investigated responses

The different parameters during the extraction viz. temperature, pressure, and reaction time, revealed varied impact on phytochemicals (TPC, TFC) and scavenging activities (DPPH, ABTS) of the extracts as depicted in the response surface plots (Fig. 2). The TPC value varies from 15.60 to 30.91 mg GAE/g dw (Table 1). Amongst the three parameters, temperature substantially impacts the TPC content during the extraction as also mentioned in different SWE studies7,33. The TPC increased significantly (50–90%) on elevating the temperature from 130 °C to 160 °C; however, further surge in temperature from 160 °C to 190 °C has decreased the TPC (22–28%). Unlike temperature, pressure has a lesser impact on TPC content (Fig. 2a). The increase in pressure from 45 bar to 60 bar at lower & moderate temperature has increased TPC (9–10%); however, at higher temperature (190 °C), there was around 5% decline in TPC. Whereas a further increase in pressure from 60 bar to 90 bar at lower, moderate, and higher temperature (130 °C, 160 °C & 190 °C) has resulted in a 10–15% decrease in TPC. Further, TPC content was low on lesser reaction time and increased (8–17%) on longer reaction time from 10 to 20 min at lower and moderate temperature (130 °C, 160 °C). In contrast, TPC yield declined (17%) while increasing the reaction time at high temperature (190 °C), congruently with previous study14. Similarly, at lower temperature (130 °C), the TPC yield increased (10%) on increasing the reaction time from 20 to 30 min, but at higher temperature (160 °C, 190 °C), the TPC yield decreased (11–20%). This shows the impact of the reaction time was lesser than the other two parameters.

Response surface plots depicting the impact of independent parameters on (a) TPC, (b) TFC, (c) DPPH and (d) ABTS radical scavenging activities of Illicium griffithii fruit.

The TFC varies from 1.48 to 2.44 mg QE/ g dw (Table 1). The temperature has impacted the TFC yield as visible in response surface plots (Fig. 2b). The TFC yield was highest at lower temperature (130 °C), moderate pressure (60 bar), and longer extraction time (30 min). In contrast, the lowest TFC was found at moderate temperature (160 °C), highest pressure (75 bar), and longer extraction time (30 min). At lower temperature, the TFC content was higher; however, as the temperature increased the TFC content also decreased. However, in some cases, there are variations depending on the extraction pressure and reaction time. On elevating the temperature from 130 °C to 160 °C at lower, moderate, and high-pressure conditions, TFC yield decreased of around 16–32%. Further upsurge in temperature from 160 °C to 190 °C increases the yield by 4–23%. This shows that some of the phytochemicals may be converting or forming into flavonoids at higher temperatures. Pressure has also impacted the TFC content. When the pressure increases, the TFC yield generally decreases. The TFC content was higher at lower pressure, whereas, at higher pressure, the TFC content decreased. On increasing the pressure from 45 bar to 60 bar, there was a slight decrease in TFC content (0.18%) at lower temperatures (130 °C); however, at moderate temperature (160 °C), the TFC content decreased significantly (20%), whereas at higher temperature (190 °C), the TFC content also decreases (9.12%). While increasing pressure from 60 bar to 75 bar, the TFC yield again showed a decrease (3–9%) at different temperature conditions (130, 160, and 190 °C). The reaction time has also impacted the TFC yield. Generally, on elongating the reaction time from 10 min to 20 min at lower to moderate temperature (130 & 160 °C), the TFC yield increased from 4 to 6%, but at higher temperature (190 °C), the TFC yield decreased severely (13%). When the reaction time increased from 20 min to 30 min at lower temperature (130 °C), there was an increase (4%) in TFC content. But at moderate and higher temperatures (160, 190 °C), TFC content declined (9.1 to 9.8%). A similar study also supported the present study where TFC values were higher at lower temperature, and at higher temperature conditions value decreases34. This shows that reaction time has slightly impacted the TFC yield.

The DPPH radical scavenging activities varied from 19.23 to 24.70 µg AAE/g dw. It has been reported that temperature has a significant influence on the antioxidative capacity7,30,33. The scavenging activity was low at lower temperature and increased (20–26%) on elevating the temperature from 130 to 160 °C at various pressures and reaction time (Fig. 2c). However, a further increase in temperature (190 °C) at different pressures and reaction time stimulated a decrease (11–17%) in the scavenging activity. The lower reaction pressure has a slightly lesser impact on the DPPH scavenging activity than temperature15. The scavenging activity was low at lower pressure and temperature. An increase in pressure also enhances the DPPH scavenging activity by (1.6 to 5.1%); however, increased pressure at higher temperature resulted in a reduction in the scavenging activity (0.9%). Further increase in pressure from 60 to 75 bar has decreased the scavenging activity (1.1 to 6.2%). Like pressure, the reaction time has merely greatly impacted the scavenging activity. Generally, there was a decrease in the scavenging activity, whereas the reaction time was elongated from 10 min to 20 min except at a moderate temperature 160 °C where the scavenging activity increased (5%). Whereas, further increase in reaction time from 20 min to 30 min at a lower temperature 130 °C, the scavenging activity slightly increased (1.6%). But increasing the reaction time (20 to 30 min) at moderate and higher temperature (160, 190 °C) has a negative impact on scavenging activity and decreases by (0.9 to 6.2%).

The ABTS scavenging activity varied from 16.36 to 17.92 µg AAE/g dw at different parameters. Among the different independent parameters, temperature has impacted the ABTS scavenging activity the most (Fig. 2d). At the same time, the pressure and reaction time have a lesser impact on ABTS radical scavenging activity. The temperature extensively influenced the extracts’ ABTS scavenging activity. While increasing the temperature from 130 °C to 160 °C, there was a significant increase in scavenging activity (5.3 to 7.4%). However, a further temperature increases from 160 °C to 190 °C has shown a significant reduction (6.2 to 7.5%) in the scavenging activity. Unlike temperature, pressure has a lesser impact on the scavenging activity, and an increase in pressure from 45 to 60 bar at lower and moderate temperature (130 °C to 160 °C) has shown an increase in scavenging activity (0.16 to 1.6%). However, at higher temperature (190 °C), activity slightly reduced (0.16%). Further increase in pressure from 60 to 75 bar at lower temperature showed a slight increase in activity (1.64%). However, there was not much impact at higher temperature (160 and 190 °C). Like pressure, reaction time did not have much impact on scavenging activity. The scavenging activity increased on elongating the reaction time from 10 to 20 min at lower and moderate temperature (130 °C and 160 °C). Similarly, on elongating the reaction time from 20 min to 30 min at a lower temperature (130 °C), there was an increase in the scavenging activity. But further increased reaction time at higher temperatures (160 °C and 190 °C), the scavenging activity decreases (0.15 to 0.16%).

It was shown that different temperature conditions in SWE significantly impacted the antioxidant activity and phytochemicals (TPC, TFC). High temperature states particularly enhanced the extraction efficiency of the bioactive constituents by increasing the antioxidant capacities in SWE3,7. The increased scavenging activity may cause an increase in the free phenolic compounds30. The phenolic content and antioxidant activities increased on a rise in the temperature from 60 °C to 160 °C. In a similar study of SWE, temperature strongly impacts the antioxidant activities of the SWE extracts. Extraction temperatures up to 150 °C exhibited higher activity and were found to be optimum for ABTS. This also indicated that the extraction efficacy of the antioxidant compounds was particularly favoured at temperatures between 140 and 160 °C3,33. In one study, the highest TPC was recorded at 160 °C, which highlighted that temperature over 180 °C led to pyrolysis. Further, rising temperature up to 200 °C instigated a minor decrease in antioxidant activity, signifying likely degradation of antioxidants from plant sample6 and well supported by the similar finding in SWE29. The high temperature in SWE can extensively damage the linkage between polyphenols and other substances in plant tissue16. Nevertheless, clear association between polyphenolic compounds and antioxidative capacity is inadequate, suggesting that non-phenolic co-extracted compounds also contribute to the extracts’ antioxidant activity. Moreover, antioxidant compounds could also be generated during the SWE process33. The pressure array among 30 and 60 bars generally exhibited better reduction capability, whereas the further increase of pressure also negatively impacted the antioxidant activity (p < 0.05). The higher pressure influences the phenolic molecules, which may be due to the interaction and aggregation of molecules. It is also feasible for molecules to undergo re-adsorption15. The earlier studies also suggest that a large variation in extraction pressure (from 10 to 80 bar) has slightly increased the TPC and insignificant difference between antioxidant activities at other pressures. This shows that efficient extraction of phenolic compounds doesn’t require high pressure. Further, the antioxidant activities also followed a similar trend as was the TPC6.This is in line with the recommended pressure of 30 and 45 bar rather than elevated pressure for safety purposes and trivial variances in the yield of phenolic compounds28. The increase in extraction time generally prolongs the contact time between solvent and solute, which helps in increasing the extraction yield. The tougher plant textures may require a longer extraction time to complete the extraction. However, increasing the reaction time decreased the DPPH free-radical-scavenging activity30. Prolonged exposure to high temperature could lead to the degradation of the compounds34. These results advocate that longer heat treatment may degrade the phenolic compounds30. The physicochemical properties of the compounds, such as their chemical structure and melting point, significantly influence their extraction, particularly at higher temperatures. As a result, some compounds may revert to their isomeric forms while keeping temperature and pressure high, and this transformation is reversible14. The results also complement better efficacy in SWE rather than the conventional method29.

Optimum conditions and validation

SWE process optimization is vital to enhance the separation of phenolic compounds and have extraction conditions in an optimal state to attain the optimal response goals. The optimal conditions were determined by Design Expert software (State-Ease, Inc. USA). Multiple response optimizations were executed to perceive the optimal points of extraction parameters to achieve the likely response goals. For the optimal response values, the optimum conditions were temperature 161.32 °C, pressure 45 bar, and time 30.0 min for the SWE extraction of Illicium griffithii fruit. The optimal conditions and predicted values were quantified using a desirability function (D = 0.804). Under optimal conditions and keeping a temperature 160 °C, a 3.0 g sample was extracted with 60 ml of deionized water in a high-pressure reactor using a 100 ml reaction vessel. The experimental values obtained under these conditions were found to be close to the model-predicted values (Supplementary file, table S2). The estimated and observed values exhibited no significant variance (p < 0.05), suggesting that the model aligns well with the experimental data. The present study is well supported for the optimum pressure, where the extract attained at 45 bar demonstrated better scavenging activity against ABTS and DPPH radicals15. In the earlier studies, the phenolic compounds and antioxidant activities were found to depend on the employed temperature in SWE6. The SWE tremendously increased antioxidant activities with extraction temperature and time, but temperatures higher than 160 °C were insignificant29. Further, the highest TPC was also obtained at 160 °C is SWE6. These results showed that temperature below 170 °C significantly impacted the water solvation supremacy. Further, maximum antioxidant activity was also attained at parameters revealing the highest TPC and TFC14. The results in the present study are compared with similar SWE studies, illustrating the significance of SWE process for the extraction of polyphenolics from different plant materials. The results show better efficacy of SWE process as compared to other processes in terms of higher yield, reduced time, higher bioactivity, etc., of phenolic antioxidants1,3,14,26,35–37. This approach to efficiency and waste reduction aligns SWE with green chemistry principles enhancing its sustainability and scalability for industrial applications6,17.

Phenolic profile identification and quantification by HPLC

The phenolic compounds contributing to the antioxidant activities were identified and quantified by HPLC system (Alliance e2695, Waters, Milford, USA). Total 7 phenolic compounds i.e. gallic acid, catechin, protocatechuic acid, chlorogenic acid, p-hydroxybenzoic acid, ferulic acid, and vanillic acid, were found in the subcritical water extracts of Illicium griffithii fruit (Fig. 3). This was evident in the previous study except protocatechuic acid24. The phenolic compound content was estimated through the calibration curves of each standard compound. The concentration of the phenolic compounds varied from 1.60 to 9.77 m/g, with gallic acid (9.77 mg/g) having the maximum concentration followed by catechin (8.84 mg/g). In contrast, chlorogenic was lowest (1.60 mg/g) (Supplementary file, table S1). In our previous study, gallic acid and catechin were high using the solvent extraction of Illicium griffithii fruit24. The present study is also well supported by the previous study on I. griffithii and I. verum20,24,38. This indicates that gallic acid and catechin are the predominant phenolic compounds in Illicium griffithii fruit. Notably, significant levels of gallic acid and catechin were observed in subcritical water extracts, highlighting the substantial variation in compound content within the identical medicinal species but from diverse origins1. Subcritical water extraction (SWE) recovered higher amounts of detected compounds compared to conventional solvent extraction, highlighting the effectiveness of SWE in recovering phenolic compounds1,3,14. Even against more advanced technologies, such as microwave-assisted extraction, SWE has produced much greater amounts of phenolic antioxidants37. The concentration of phenolic compounds significantly increased under subcritical conditions as compared to the previous study while using water as an extraction solvent in classical extraction24. These findings significantly advocated for the SWE as a green and efficient technique for separating phenolic compounds from I. griffithii fruit.

ATR-FTIR analysis

The extracts obtained from subcritical water extraction were analyzed using FTIR. The spectra were recorded using ATR-FTIR (Perkin Elmer) to classify the variation and presence of the different functional groups. The peaks observed between 3030 cm− 1 -3690 cm− 1 were ascribed to O-H stretching vibrations and hydrogen bonding within phenolic and aliphatic structures. The peak intensity at 1634 cm− 1 is ascribed to the aromatic stretching modes of C = C, which also signifies the presence of a benzene stretching ring showing the sign of extraction of phenolic compounds at all the temperature under subcritical conditions. The spectra between 1480 –1187 cm− 1 signify the C-H bending and C-O stretching vibration of aromatics and phenols interaction39. The FTIR spectra showed that variation in temperature and pressure also affected the aromatic and phenols interaction. The peak intensity was increased with an increase in temperature up to moderate condition (160 °C), thereafter a decrease at higher reaction temperature (190 °C) (Fig. 4a). However, at higher temperature (190 ˚C), the peak intensity decreased with increase in pressure (Fig. 4b). Further, on increasing the reaction time (10–30 min) at higher temperature (160 °C), the peak intensity also decreases (Fig. 4c). A similar trend was also found in phytochemical analysis for TPC content, which also shows that temperature has greatly impacted the phenolic content. In contrast, pressure and reaction time have a lesser impact on the phenolic content.

FTIR spectra of Illicium griffithii fruit extracts at different (a) temperature and (b) pressure and (c) time under SWE conditions.

The bands detected within 3600 –3200 cm⁻¹ resemble to O-H stretching, which is associated with hydroxyl deformation vibrations. Peaks within 1369 cm⁻¹ and 1374 cm⁻¹ designate the presence of C-H and aliphatic C-H₂ stretching in methyl and phenol groups27,40 confirming the presence of O-H structures. Two bands encompassing 1500 cm⁻¹, associated with phenolic fragments, were detected in all spectra. The band between 1150 and 1000 cm⁻¹ is linked to the occurrence of C-O-H group of alcohol, while the band at 1200 cm⁻¹ is ascribed to O-H deformation in polyphenols. Additionally, the band around 600 cm⁻¹, allied with the C-H group, designates the presence of aromatic carboxylic acids41. These bands were also observed in previous studies using solvent extraction of this species24 suggesting the occurrence of alcoholic, phenolic, and aromatic compounds in the SWE extract.

Conclusions

In this study, an eco-friendly subcritical water extraction process was employed to enhance the recovery of phenolic antioxidant from Illicium griffithii fruit, with process optimization perfomed using RSM. Compared to conventional solvent extraction, SWE yielded higher amounts of phenolic compounds and exhibited higher radical scavenging activity. Based on the single-factor analysis, the extraction conditions including the extraction temperature, extraction pressure, and extraction time were optimized using RSM. Among the different process parameters, temperature has pronounced effect on the recovery of the phenolic antioxidants in Illicium griffithii fruit. The optimum conditions were temperature 161.32 °C, pressure 45 bar, and time 30.0 min for the SWE extraction. Under these conditions, the estimated response values were 30.89 mg GAE / g dw, 2.07 mg QE /g dw, 23.22 µg AAE/g dw, and 17.79 µg AAE/g dw for TPC, TFC, DPPH, and ABTS respectively. The experimental values obtained under these conditions were found to be close to the model-predicted values and exhibited no significant variance (p < 0.05), suggesting that the model aligns well with the experimental data. The phenolic profile showed increased concentration of bioactive compounds with predominant phenolic compounds such as gallic acid, catechin, and p-hydroxybenzoic acid. ATR-FTIR analysis validated the occurrence of alcoholic, phenolic, and aromatic compounds in the SWE extract. Overall, SWE demonstrated superior phenolic compound recovery, particularly phenolic acids (gallic acid) and flavonoids (catechin), outperforming conventional methods in terms of extraction efficiency, reduced processing time, and enhanced bioactivity.
