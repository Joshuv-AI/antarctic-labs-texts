Macular and optic disc vascular densities measured by optical coherence tomography angiography in pediatric patients with different amblyopia types
Authors: Merve Simsek, Yesim Oral, Muberra Akdogan, Mehmet Cem Sabaner, Cem Simsek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe present study aimed to compare macular and peripapillary vascular density values measured by optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) in different types of amblyopia (ametropic amblyopia, anisometropic amblyopia, strabismic amblyopia, deprivation amblyopia) with age- and gender-matched healthy controls.Materials and methodsIn this prospective, cross-sectional study, between the ages of 8–18 years, 32 eyes of 32 patients with hypermetropic ametropic amblyopia and 21 amblyopic eyes of 21 patients with hypermetropic anisometropic amblyopia, 15 amblyopic eyes of 15 patients with strabismic amblyopia due to esotropia, 12 amblyopic eyes of 12 patients with deprivation amblyopia, and 30 eyes of 30 emmetropic individuals aged 8–18 years without any ocular pathology who were admitted to the same clinic were included in the control group. Refractive errors and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) with the Snellen chart were recorded as LogMAR. In all cases, superficial vascular plexus and deep vascular plexus whole image vessel density, foveal vessel density, parafoveal vessel density, perifoveal vessel density, and foveal avascular zone area (FAZ) values were measured and recorded for macular vascular density determination with OCTA. The whole image, intrapapillary, and peripapillary vessel density measurements were performed for peripapillary vessel density determination. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used for statistical analysis and the post hoc test was used for inter-group comparisons.ResultsThe mean BCVA (LogMAR) was found to be significantly lower in the anisometropic amblyopia, strabismic amblyopia, and deprivation amblyopia groups (unilateral amblyopia) compared to the ametropic amblyopia (bilateral amblyopia) and healthy group (p < 0.001). The whole image vessel density, foveal vessel density, and parafoveal vessel density of superficial and deep vascular plexus were found to be significantly lower in anisometropic amblyopia, strabismic amblyopia, and deprivation amblyopia compared to ametropic amblyopia and healthy volunteers (p < 0.001). In the correlation analysis, a negative correlation was found between the mean visual acuity in LogMAR and superficial vascular plexus foveal vessel density (p < 0.001, r = − 0.622), superficial vascular plexus parafoveal vessel density (p < 0. 001, r= -0.556), deep vascular plexus foveal vessel density (p < 0.001, r= -0.636), and deep vascular plexus parafoveal vessel density (p < 0.001 r=-0.596).ConclusionSuperficial and deep vascular plexus vessel densities in the macular region are affected in anisometropic amblyopia, strabismic amblyopia, and deprivation amblyopia, and OCTA may be useful in detecting these changes.Keywords: Amblyopia, Strabismus, Anisometropia, Vascular density

Introduction

Amblyopia is a clinical condition in which visual acuity is markedly reduced, which may occur in one or both eyes as a result of marked visual deprivation or abnormal binocular interaction during visual development [1]. Anisometropic amblyopia

Anisometropic amblyopia develops when the image in one retina is poorly focused compared to the other eye due to unequal refractive error [2]. While a difference of 1 diopter (D) between the eyes of patients with hyperopic anisometropia causes amblyopia, amblyopia may not develop until the difference between the two eyes reaches 3–4 D in patients with myopic anisometropia. For patients with astigmatism, the difference that carries a potential amblyopia risk is the difference of 1.50 D [3, 4].

Ametropic/isoametropic amblyopia

Bilateral refractive amblyopia (isoametropic or ametropic amblyopia) is an amblyopia that causes decreased vision secondary to high refractive errors in both eyes. Hyperopic values of more than 5D, myopia values of more than 8D, and astigmatism values of more than 2.5D in both eyes may lead to ametropic amblyopia as a result of a blurred image from both eyes to the retina [5].

Strabismic amblyopia

Confusion or diplopia develops as a result of different visual stimuli from both eyes due to the absence of bifoveal fixation caused by strabismus [6, 7]. Strabismic amblyopia occurs mostly in non-alternating esotropia and this is because the nasal retina is weaker than the temporal retina [8].

Deprivation amblyopia

In visual deprivation amblyopia, the distorted retinal image is caused by complete or partial occlusion of the visual axis [9]. The most common cause of deprivation amblyopia is a congenital or early onset cataract [10, 11].

Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) is a new non-colour imaging method, that can image the peripapillary and macular microvascular structure of the retina, vessel density, and part of the choriocapillaris as well as the great veins in the same area by performing multiple OCT scans, non-invasive, contrary to fundus fluorescein angiography and indocyanine green angiography [12].

The present study aimed to compare macular and peripapillary vascular density values measured by optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) in different types of amblyopia (ametropic amblyopia, anisometropic amblyopia, strabismic amblyopia, deprivation amblyopia) with age- and gender-matched healthy controls.

Materials and methods

All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Ethical approval was received from Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University Ethic Committe (date: November 06, 2020; number:2011-KAEK-2). Informed consent for participation has been obtained from the parent or legal guardian of any participant under the age of 16. After obtained ethics approval among patients aged 8–18 years who applied to Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University Ophthalmology Clinic Pediatric Ophthalmology Unit between December 2020 and March 2021, 32 right eyes of 32 patients with hypermetropic ametropic amblyopia, 21 amblyopic eyes of 21 patients with hypermetropic anisometropic amblyopia, 15 amblyopic eyes of 15 patients with strabismic amblyopia due to esotropia, 12 amblyopic eyes of 12 patients with deprivation amblyopia were included in the study group, and 30 eyes of 30 emmetropic individuals aged 8–18 years without any ocular pathology were included in the control group. All cases of deprivation amblyopia were patients with unilateral congenital cataracts who had previously undergone surgery. Age at surgery was median 8 weeks (IQR 7–10). Lens status was pseudophakia in 10 eyes and aphakia in 2. Posterior capsulotomy had been performed in 11 eyes, and the visual axis was clear in all included eyes. Amblyopia was defined based on the American Academy of Ophthalmology’s preferred practice pattern (PPP); unilateral amblyopia best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) ± 2 lines interocular difference and bilateral amblyopia BCVA worse than 20/30 in both eyes [13].

The presence or absence of strabismus was determined by performing open-close tests in all patients. In patients with strabismus, the amount of distance and near shift was measured with the prism occlusion test. Titmus test results were used for stereopsis examination. Worth-4-Dot test was performed to evaluate sensory fusion, and fusional vergence amplitudes performed to evaluate motor fusion. Cyclopentolate hydrochloride 1% Cyclopentolate 1.0% (Sikloplejin; Abdi İbrahim, İstanbul, Turkey) was instilled into the eyes of the patients twice at 5 minute intervals and after 45 minutes, refractive errors were determined with an auto-refractometer Canon RK-F1 autorefractometer (Canon RKF1). The detected refractive error was corrected with appropriate glasses, and best corrected visual acuities (BCVA) were obtained as decimal vision with Snellen’s chart, converted to LogMAR, and recorded. The axial length measurement was performed with the optical biometer (AL-Scan, Nidek CO., Gamagori, Japan).” For all patients, intraocular pressure measurements were done with the Goldmann applanation tonometry, and anterior segment and dilated fundus examinations were done with slit lamp biomicroscopy and recorded.

Patients who had previously received amblyopia treatment were excluded from the study. All patients included in our study were newly diagnosed amblyopia patients. Patients with nystagmus, opacities in the cornea and lens that could cause visual impairment and interfere with image acquisition, and anomalies in the optic nerve, macula, and vascular structures on ophthalmoscopic examination were excluded from the study. Findings of degenerative myopia in the fundus with anisometropic myopic amblyopia and ectatic findings in the corneal topography with anisometropia due to astigmatism were not included in the study.

Patients underwent 6 × 6 mm macula and 4.5 × 4.5 mm optic disc imaging in OCTA with RTVue XR Avanti AngioVue OCTA (version 2018.0.0.18, Optovue, Fremont, CA). Central macular scanning images (6 × 6 mm) were acquired using the AngioVue Imaging System Optovue RTVue XR 100 Avanti with software. Foveal vessel density was taken as % vessel density in a 1 mm diameter circle at the center of the fovea. Parafoveal vessel density was taken as % vessel density in the ring between 1 mm and 3 mm. Perifoveal vessel density was taken as % vessel density in the ring between 3 mm and 6 mm. Retinal whole image vessel density was taken as % vessel density in the 6 mm diameter circle from the center of the fovea (Figs. 1 and 2). In optic disc OCTA measurements, for radial peripapillary plexus vessel density determination, optic disc whole image, peripapillary and intra-disc vessel densities, superior-temporal-nasal-inferior optic disc small vessels density values were measured and recorded (Figs. 3).

The thickness of the 7 layers described in the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) macular retina ring sectors at 1, 3, and 6 mm was automatically analyzed with the SD-OCT device (Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) software (Segmentation Technology; Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany). These are the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), the ganglion cell layer (GCL), the inner plexiform layer (IPL), the inner nuclear layer (INL), the outer plexiform layer (OPL), the outer nuclear layer (ONL) and the photoreceptor-retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) complex. For correlation analyses between vessel density and retinal layer thickness, the retina was stratified as a superficial layer (RNFL + GCL + IPL) and a deep layer (INL + OPL) [14].

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS statistical package program, version 22.0 (IBM SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistical methods were used to evaluate the data. The normality distribution of all parameters was analyzed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. The difference in the parameters with abnormal distribution was evaluated between the five groups by the Kruskal-Wallis test. If a statistically significant difference was detected in this test, the post hoc test (Dunn) was used for pairwise comparison between groups. In addition, the Spearman correlation test was used to determine the correlation between parameters. The evaluations were performed at a 95% confidence interval and p-values less than 0.05 were accepted as a statistically significant difference.

Discussion

Amblyopia represents a reduced best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) during the years of visual development secondary to abnormal visual stimulation. With a prevalence of 0.8%−3.3% among children and the consequence of visual impairment if not treated in time, amblyopia has become an important public health problem worldwide [15]. Previous studies on amblyopia have revealed changes in the visual cortex and lateral geniculate nucleus [16]. However, it is still unclear whether the retina is involved in the development of amblyopia. After the introduction of OCTA into clinical use, to shed light on the pathogenesis of amblyopia and to detect microvascular changes in amblyopic eyes, an OCTA study with amblyopia was first performed in Los Angeles in 2017 by Lonngi et al. [17] in unilateral amblyopia patients and found that the superficial and deep capillary plexus vessel densities in the macula were significantly lower in patients with amblyopia compared to healthy eyes. In the same year in our country, Yılmaz et al. [18] found lower vascular densities in the superficial and deep vascular plexus with strabismic amblyopia compared to healthy eyes. This results is similar to the results of our study. In 2018, Sobral et al. [19] evaluated the optic disc vascular density in addition to the vascular densities and found that the vascular densities in the optic disc whole image vascular density were statistically significantly lower with anisometropic amblyopia and strabismic amblyopia compared to healthy volunteers. This result is in parallel with our study. Similar to our study, Dereli Can et al. [20] found that the vascular densities in the superficial vascular plexus and deep vascular plexus in the macular region were lower in amblyopic patients compared to healthy volunteers. Yilmaz Cinar et al. [21] and Chen et al. [22] found that the vascular densities in the superficial and deep capillary plexus in the macular region were lower in patients with amblyopia compared to healthy volunteers, and they also found a positive correlation between inner retinal thickness on OCT and vascular plexus vascular densities. This result is similar to our study. We also found a positive correlation between the foveal and parafoveal superficial retinal layers (RNFL + GCL + IPL) and the superficial vascular plexus vessel densities. İn addition we found a positive correlation between the foveal and parafoveal deep retinal layers (INL + OPL) and the deep vascular plexus vessel densities. We thought that the decrease in vascular density in the vascular plexuses as the thickness of the retinal layers decreases may be a result of impaired metabolism in the retinal layers with decreased visual stimulation in amblyopic eyes.

Doguizi et al. [23] performed a correlation analysis between OCTA parameters and visual acuity of hypermetropic anisometropic amblyopic patients and their results are in parallel with our study. In unilateral amblyopia types, we found a negative correlation between visual acuity in LogMAR and OCTA parameters. Amblyopic visual loss resulting from unilateral pathologies tends to be worse than bilateral cases of similar severity because interocular competition contributes to the direct amblyogenic effect of visual impairment [24–26] and we thinks this explains our results.

Conflicting studies conducted in OCTA are also present. In 2019, Pujari et al. [27], Araki et al. [28], and Demirayak et al. [29] could find no significant difference between the macular region superficial and deep capillary plexus vessel densities of patients with amblyopia and healthy eyes.

Most recently, in 2021, Gao et al. [30] conducted a meta-analysis including 12 studies and obtained a result parallel to our study. The results found in the amblyopia and OCTA studies conducted in 2022 are also similar to our study [31–33].

Despite the higher refractive error, patients with bilateral amblyopia (ametropic amblyopia) had statistically significantly better visual acuity than patients with unilateral amblyopia. Including ametropic amblyopia in their study, Karabulut et al. [34] although they found a decrease in superficial and deep vascular plexus vessel densities in ametropic amblyopia, we found no difference between healthy volunteers in ametropic amblyopia in our study. Data from both animal and human studies of amblyopia [35–39] suggest that monocular and binocular deprivation have different neural and behavioral consequences, with monocular amblyopia having a worse visual prognosis than binocular amblyopia. This helps to explain the results of our study.

We did not perform subjective refraction because studies and literature have shown that cycloplegic refraction is more sensitive than the subjective one to measure refractive error at all age groups, especially in children and young adults [40].

Our study has some limitations. First, since we conducted a study with the pediatric age group, we had patients who were excluded from the study due to the poor quality of the images because some children could not adapt to the OCTA device. Therefore, the number of patients included in the study decreased. One limitation of the present study is the unequal distribution of patients across amblyopia subgroups. In particular, the relatively small sample size of the deprivation amblyopia group (n = 12) compared with the hypermetropic ametropic amblyopia group (n = 32) may have reduced the statistical power, increasing the risk of Type II error. Therefore, the findings related to this subgroup should be interpreted with caution. However, we believe that reporting the results for this subgroup is still valuable, as it provides insights into a less common but clinically significant form of amblyopia. We did not divide our patients into subgroups such as receiving/not receiving treatment. In addition, we did not classify amblyopia according to clinical status such as mild/moderate/severe and there may be significant differences in refractive error (spherical equivalent) among the groups, and this parameter was not included in our analysis. We did not apply a formal magnification correction; while AL was comparable and covariate-adjusted models were consistent, this remains a limitation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, it may be stated that in anisometropic amblyopia, strabismic amblyopia, and deprivation amblyopia, superficial and deep vascular plexus vessel densities in the macular region and optic disc vessel densities are affected and OCTA can be useful in detecting these changes.
