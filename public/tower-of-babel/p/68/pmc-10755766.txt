A quantitative study of airway ultrasound in predicting difficult laryngoscopy: A prospective study
Authors: Lin Ning, Xing Zhu, Hong-Chao Li, Shi-Jie Zhou, Qi-Wei Zhang, Hong-Yu Zou, Qing-Xiang Mao, Hong Yan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
PurposeAs common clinical screening tests cannot effectively predict a difficult airway, and unanticipated difficult laryngoscopy remains a challenge for physicians. We herein used ultrasound to develop some point-of-care predictors for difficult laryngoscopy.MethodsThis prospective observational study included 502 patients who underwent laryngoscopy and a detailed sonographic assessment. Patients under 18 years old, or with maxillofacial deformities or fractures, limited mouth opening, limited neck movement or history of neck surgery were excluded from the study. Laryngoscopic views of all patients were scored and grouping using the modified Cormack-Lehane (CL) scoring system. The measurements acquired comprised tongue width, the longitudinal cross-sectional area of the tongue, tongue volume, the mandible-hyoid bone distance, the hyoid bone-glottis distance, the mandible-hyoid bone-glottis angle, the skin-thyrohyoid membrane distance, the glottis-superior edge of the thyroid cartilage distance (DGTC), the skin-hyoid bone distance, and the epiglottis midway-skin distance. ANOVA and Chi-square were used to compare differences between groups. Logistic regression was used to identify risk factors for difficult laryngoscopy and it was visualized by receiver operating characteristic curves and nomogram. R version 3.6.3 and SPSS version 26.0 were used for statistical analyses.ResultsDifficult laryngoscopy was indicated in 49 patients (CL grade Ⅲ – Ⅳ) and easy laryngoscopy in 453 patients (CL grade Ⅰ – Ⅱ). The ultrasound-measured mandible-hyoid bone-glottis angle and DGTC significantly differed between the 2 groups (p < 0.001). Difficult laryngoscopy was predicted by an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.930 with a threshold mandible-hyoid bone-glottis angle of 125.5° and by an AUC of 0.722 with a threshold DGTC of 1.22 cm. The longitudinal cross-sectional area of the tongue, tongue width, tongue volume, the mandible-hyoid distance, and the hyoid-glottis distance did not significantly differ between the groups.ConclusionDifficult laryngoscopy may be anticipated in patients in whom the mandible-hyoid bone-glottis angle is smaller than 125.5° or DGTC is larger than 1.22 cm.

Introduction

Airway management of trauma patients is a major challenge for anesthesiologists, emergency medicine physicians, and intensive care unit (ICU) physicians. Failure to recognize difficult laryngoscopy of trauma patients and improper airway management can have catastrophic consequences. The traditional bedside predictors of difficult laryngoscopy include the modified Mallampati classification, the thyromental distance, maximum mouth opening, and the neck range of motion classification. These traditional measures require the patient to be awake and cooperative with the physicians to complete the evaluation. However, trauma patients often have hypnosia, coma, and traumatic brain injury and cannot cooperate with physicians to complete airway assessments. Ultrasonography is a safe, quick, non-invasive, portable, and repeatable technique. It is recognized as a useful tool to evaluate upper airway anatomy and calculate distances between anatomical structures.1,2 Ultrasonography can allow quick and timely evaluation of the airway without causing secondary injury to trauma patients in the case of coma. When patients suffer from blunt neck trauma, ultrasonography allows for quickly viewing the deep anatomical profiles of the neck of patients, reducing the incidence of unforeseeable difficult laryngoscopy, and timely detection of neck profiles, bleeding, anatomical profile abnormalities, and other conditions.

Notably, these traditional predictors are relatively unreliable, and the sensitivity values are 61.5% for the modified Mallampati classification and 55% for thyromental distance.3,4 The sensitivity value for maximum mouth opening < 4 cm is even lower at 26.3%5; even so, the values are unavailable for unconscious or uncooperative patients. Therefore, the ability to more accurately predict difficult laryngoscopy would help physicians reduce airway-related adverse outcomes. And airway ultrasound could be used for pre-intubation assessment to identify the possibility of a difficult laryngoscopy.6 Falcetta et al.7 identified the distance between the skin and epiglottis as a highly sensitive predictor of difficult laryngoscopy. Wu et al.8 identified a distance > 1.28 cm between skin and hyoid bone as an independent risk factor of difficult laryngoscopy. However, most of these reported sonographic predictors are based on the superficial structure of the airway without elaborate exploration of the deeper anatomic profiles of difficult laryngoscopy.

In this prospective study, we performed upper airway ultrasound on patients, evaluated the effectiveness of previous reported sonography-based airway parameters, and explored some new sonographic predictors of difficult laryngoscopy.

Methods

The study protocol of this prospective observational study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Daping Hospital on May 25th, 2019 (Approval Number: 2019-027). Then, we used ultrasound to learn the anatomical structure of the patient's neck before patient enrollment. The trial was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry (ChiCTR2000034576, date of registration: July 10th, 2020). Patients underwent tracheal intubation for elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy at the Daping Hospital between July 2020 and January 2021 were included. The exclusion criteria were as follows: age < 18 years old; maxillofacial deformities or fractures; limited mouth opening; limited neck movement; and history of neck surgery. Each patient signed an informed consent form before diagnosis, wherein they consented to the use of their clinical records for further clinical research. All procedures were performed in accordance with Helsinki Declaration in 2013.

According to Cormack-Lehane (CL) classification, all patients were grouped into CL Ⅰ, CL Ⅱ, and CL Ⅲ – Ⅳ. CL grades Ⅲ – Ⅳ were considered to indicate difficult laryngoscopy. CL Ⅰ: can reveal most of the glottis; CL Ⅱ: can reveal part of the glottis; CL Ⅲ: can only reveal the epiglottis; CL Ⅳ: cannot reveal the epiglottis. A pilot study calculated the sample size by minimum sample size calculation formula.

The preoperative airway for all patients was evaluated by an attending anesthesiologist according to the modified Mallampati classification, maximum mouth opening, thyromental distance, and neck circumference. Another attending anesthesiologist with expertise in point-of-care ultrasound performed airway imaging and stored data. Patients were placed in the supine position with their head and neck in maximal extension, and they were asked to rest the tip of their tongue against their lower incisors during the airway sonography. The sonography used an M9 ultrasound machine (Mindray, Shenzhen, China) equipped with a high-resolution linear probe and low-frequency convex array probe. Then, general anesthesia was induced by intravenous midazolam (0.05 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.5 μg/kg), propofol (2 mg/kg), and cisatracurium besilate (0.15 mg/kg). Direct laryngoscopy was performed to assess the CL grade followed by tracheal intubation.

The following measurements were acquired during upper airway ultrasound observation: (1) the longitudinal cross-sectional area of the tongue, which was measured using median sagittal scans by tracking the borders of the tongue muscles; (2) tongue width, which was measured using transverse scans in the midsection of the tongue between the 2 widest points in the center of the tongue; (3) tongue volume, which was derived by multiplying the midsagittal cross-sectional area by the tongue width9; (4) the distance from the mandible to the hyoid bone, which was measured using median sagittal scans from the upper border of the hyoid bone to the lower border of the mentum of the mandible; (5) the distance from the hyoid bone to the glottis; (6) the mandible-hyoid bone-glottis angle (Fig. 1); (7) the distance from the glottis to the superior edge of the thyroid cartilage (DGTC, Fig. 2); (8) the distance from the skin to the hyoid bone (DSHB, Fig. 3); (9) the distance from the skin to epiglottis midway (DSEM, Fig. 3); and (10) the distance from the skin to the thyrohyoid membrane (DSTM).10Fig. 1Angle of mandible-hyoid bone-glottis. Statistical analysis

For statistical analyses, R (version 3.6.3, R Development Core Team) and SPSS (version 26.0, IBM, New York, US) were used. Normally distributed numerical variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For between-group comparisons of numerical variables, statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA with post-hoc tests. Categorical variables were expressed as absolute numbers and percentages, and between-group comparisons were performed using Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. The differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Statistically significant ultrasound variables were included in logistic regression analysis with backward stepwise method, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted. The best cut-off value for each ultrasonographic result was predicted using the Jorden index. A nomogram was constructed to predict difficult laryngoscopy based on the logistic regression analysis results, and the corresponding scorecard was output.

The ROC curve was used to calculate the diagnostic accuracy of the above significant variables in terms of their area under the curve (AUC). The mandible-hyoid bone-glottis angle was the best predictor of difficult laryngoscopy and had an AUC of 0.930 (95% CI: 0.885 – 0.975). Discussion

The neck circumference, the modified Mallampati classification, the thyromental distance and maximum mouth opening are widely used in preoperative airway assessment; however, these traditional bedside predictors have limited sensitivity and specificity for predicting difficult laryngoscopy. The modified Mallampati classification reflects the tongue size relative to size of the oral cavity.11,12 A meta-analysis reported that the modified Mallampati classification does not accurately predict difficult laryngoscopy or tracheal intubation as an independent factor, but likely to be a part of a multiparametric prediction model.13 The thyromental distance is still widely used as a classic traditional predictive factor. However, the measurement of the thyromental distance based on external landmarks reportedly has an error rate of up to 50% in female patients.14,15 In our study, the AUC of any bedside evaluation tool did not exceed 0.700, except for the AUC of neck circumference, which was 0.701. Therefore, there is a need to explore a better assessment tool to assess airway anatomy and predict difficult laryngoscopy.

Ultrasonography allowed for clear visualization of all major airway structures16, and many studies have aimed at identifying an effective indicator that is clearly visible on ultrasound to predict a difficult airway. Falcetta et al.7 and Wu et al.8 reported that the AUCs of DSEM, DSTM, and DSHB were 0.702, 0.605, and 0.590, respectively, and found these factors to be unsatisfactory. DSEM and DSHB depict the soft tissue thickness of the patient's neck and partially reflect the difficulty of laryngoscope alignment along the oral, pharyngeal, and laryngeal axes.17 In these studies by Falcetta et al.7 and Wu et al.8, the best cut-off values for DSEM and DSHB were 2.54 cm and 1.28 cm, respectively; conversely, in our study, the cut-off values were 2.20 cm and 0.68 cm, respectively. This inconsistency could be attributed to differences in the head position during ultrasonographic evaluation. In our study, the patients were positioned such that there was over-extension of the neck during measurement, which may reduce the measured thickness of anterior cervical soft tissues. According to Pawel et al.9, difficult laryngoscopy is associated with the total volume and the longitudinal cross-sectional area of the tongue. However, our results are not consistent with their findings. This could be due to the neck extension angle possibly affecting the consistency of the measure point on patients' tongue between different studies. These different findings require further exploration with a larger sample size.

In this study, we found 2 new assessment tools: an AUC of 0.930 for the mandible-hyoid bone-glottis angle and an AUC of 0.722 for DGTC. It is well-known that the oral, pharyngeal, and laryngeal axes should align to facilitate laryngeal visualization and intubation. Compared with distance variables, including distance from the mandible to the hyoid bone, DSHB, DSEM, and DSTM, the mandible-hyoid bone-glottis angle defined in this study better reflects the relative positions of the mandible, hyoid bone, and glottis, which is better correlated with the ease of exposing the oral, pharyngeal, and laryngeal axis alignment.18 According to the ROC curve analysis of the mandible-hyoid bone-glottis angle, the Youden index was 0.786, the optimal threshold was 125.5°, and the sensitivity was 0.837. Taken together, the mandible-hyoid bone-glottis angle can be an effective independent predictive factor of difficult laryngoscopy.

Notably, DGTC reflects the positional relationship between the glottis and thyroid cartilage. In this study, we found that patients with difficult laryngoscopy had a larger DGTC. The ultrasound measurement of the relative relationship between the glottis and thyroid cartilage had a Youden index of 0.395, corresponding to an optimal cut-off value of 1.22 cm and a sensitivity of 0.673. This finding can be possibly explained by the fact that the root of the epiglottis is attached to the thyroid cartilage and hyoid bone. When a Mac blade is used to lift the epiglottis, the movement of the epiglottis is limited by the thyroid cartilage. If the glottis is hidden deeper into the thyroid cartilage, the difficulty of exposing the pharyngeal and laryngeal axes will increase. We conclude that patients may have difficult laryngoscopy when the ultrasound-measured parallel distance between the glottis and thyroid cartilage under is greater than 1.22 cm.

At present, focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST) is widely used in clinics to quickly scan the key areas of the abdomen of patients to check for the presence of free fluid and extended-FAST (E-FAST) adds lung evaluation to this. However, neither offers upper airway assessment for trauma patients. Airway ultrasound, as a supplement to E-FAST, can aid timely observation of changes in the physiological structure of the neck of trauma patients, and ultrasound can quickly predict difficult laryngoscopy in trauma patients, improve the success rate of intubation, and reduce airway complications.

This study has some limitations. The sample size of difficult laryngoscopy group in this trial is small due to the low incidence of difficult laryngoscopy. Airway assessment and tracheal intubation were done by the same attending anesthesiologist to avoid selection bias; however, this also limits the number of cases with difficult laryngoscopy. Our study only enrolled patients in a single medical center, and a larger multi-center trial is needed to further develop the evaluation system.

In conclusion, the ultrasound measurement of the mandible-hyoid bone-glottis angle and the distance from the glottis to the superior border of the thyroid cartilage can help predict difficult laryngoscopy. Laryngoscopic exposure may be difficult to perform in patients in whom this angle is smaller than 125.5° or the distance from the glottis to the superior edge of the thyroid cartilage is greater than 1.22 cm.
