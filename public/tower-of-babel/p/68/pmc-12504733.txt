Metagenomic fingerprints in bronchoalveolar lavage differentiate pulmonary diseases
Authors: Dongsheng Han, Chang Liu, Bin Yang, Fei Yu, Huifang Liu, Bin Lou, Yifei Shen, Hui Tang, Hua Zhou, Shufa Zheng, Yu Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Recent advances in unbiased metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) enable simultaneous examination of microbial and host genetic material. We developed a multimodal machine learning-based diagnostic approach to differentiate lung cancer and pulmonary infections by analyzing 402 bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) mNGS datasets, including lung cancer (n = 123), bacterial infections (n = 114), fungal infections (n = 79), and pulmonary tuberculosis (n = 86). The training cohort revealed differences in microbial profiles, bacteriophage abundance, host gene and transposable element expression, immune cell composition, and tumor fraction derived from copy number variation (CNV). The integrated model (Model VI) achieved an AUC of 0.937 (95% CI, 0.910–0.964) in the training cohort and 0.847 (95% CI, 0.776–0.918) in the test cohort. A rule-in/rule-out strategy further improved accuracy in differentiating lung cancer from tuberculosis (accuracy = 0.896), fungal (accuracy = 0.915), and bacterial (accuracy = 0.907) infections. These findings highlight the potential of mNGS-based multimodal analysis as a cost-effective tool for early and accurate differential diagnosis.

Introduction

Lung cancer and pulmonary infections pose significant global health challenges with high incidence, mortality rates, and substantial socioeconomic burdens. In the absence of rapid and accurate histopathological or microbiological test results, clinicians often struggle to differentiate these conditions based solely on clinical and radiological features, leading to misdiagnoses and delays or inappropriate treatment1,2.

A variety of pathogens responsible for pulmonary infections, including bacteria (e.g., Pseudomonas, Streptococcus), mycobacteria (Mycobacterium tuberculosis, non-tuberculous mycobacteria), aerobic actinomycetes (Nocardia), and fungi (Aspergillus, Mucor, Cryptococcus), can closely mimic lung cancer. These infections often present with similar clinical symptoms such as dyspnea, fatigue, cough, and hemoptysis, as well as overlapping radiographic features including spiculated solid nodules or masses, cavities with nodular margins, and invasion of the chest wall or mediastinum2,3. Consequently, clinicians often rely on multiple diagnostic approaches to differentiate infections from malignancies4. A cost-effective method that requires fewer samples and supports timely and accurate clinical decision-making would significantly improve patient care and management.

Metagenomic Next-generation Sequencing (mNGS) is a sequencing technology capable of identifying pathogens in specimens within 24 hours or less5,6. In recent years, it has been widely employed in the diagnosis of various complex infectious diseases and has been confirmed to be a powerful tool with an excellent diagnostic accuracy in detecting pneumonia-related pathogens7,8. Excitingly, recent studies have confirmed that analyzing transcriptomic data derived from human sequences of mNGS testing can aid in distinguishing infectious diseases such as sepsis, acute respiratory infections, tuberculous meningitis, and non-infectious diseases9–11. Developing intelligent algorithms based on chromosomal instability and tumor-related copy number variations generated by mNGS data is useful to diagnose malignant tumors12–14. These studies prompt us to further contemplate whether it is possible to utilize mNGS data from respiratory tract samples to establish an integrative genomic diagnostic method that combines microbial and host response characteristics of the patients. Such a method is expected to distinguish pulmonary infections that may mimic lung cancer, without increasing diagnostic costs, requiring only minimal testing and samples, and delivering results within a relatively short timeframe.

Here, we conducted mNGS testing on bronchoalveolar lavage fluid samples (BALF-mNGS) from 402 clinical patients with lung cancer or pulmonary infections. Subsequently, we analyzed the microbial information and host response information derived from metagenomic sequencing data, and based on this, we established and validated an integrated host/microbe metagenomics-driven machine learning approach for the differential diagnosis of lung cancer and pulmonary infections.

Results

Clinical features of study cohort

Based on the established criteria (Methods, Fig. 1a), we enrolled a total of 402 patients, consisting of 123 lung cancer patients and 279 patients with pulmonary infections. According to etiological findings, the infection group was further subdivided into three subgroups: pulmonary tuberculous (n = 86), fungal infection (n = 79), and bacterial infection (n = 114). Most patients, regardless of their subgroup, exhibited similar clinical and imaging characteristics, such as race (all were Chinese), underlying medical conditions, white blood cells (WBC) count and inflammatory indicators such as Procalcitonin (PCT) and C-reactive protein (CRP), and results of chest computed tomography (CT) scan (e.g., patchy shadows and nodules, cavities, mediastinal lymphadenopathy) (Table 1). The median mNGS DNA data per patient was 21.9 million reads (IQR 18.0–27.6 M), with the vast majority of reads (>95%) being human. We compared and screened the differential features within the mNGS data of the lung cancer and pulmonary infection groups in the training cohort to establish a differential diagnosis approach for lung cancer and pulmonary infections. Subsequently, the lung cancer group was compared separately to the tuberculosis, fungal, and bacterial infection groups to develop a diagnostic method capable of rapidly distinguishing lung cancer from infections caused by different pathogens (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. S1).

Microbial community structure and specific taxon of different pulmonary diseases

Of these 284 patients, we analyzed the α-diversity, β-diversity, and specific taxon of each group in DNA and RNA sequencing data derived from BALF. β-diversity of DNA data revealed a significant distinction between lung cancer and pulmonary infection (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.0067, P = 0.002; Fig. 2a). Significant differences were also observed between lung cancer and specific infection subgroups, including bacterial infection (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.0193, P = 0.001), fungal infection (R2 = 0.0128, P = 0.002), and pulmonary tuberculosis (R2 = 0.0283, P = 0.001; Fig. 2b). However, no significant differences in α-diversity were detected between lung cancer and overall pulmonary infections across any ecological indices (Mann–Whitney-U test, P > 0.05; Supplementary Fig. S4a). The Richness index and Chao1 index showed significant differences specifically between lung cancer and bacterial infection (Mann–Whitney-U test, P < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. S4c). LEfSe analysis identified disease-discriminatory taxa. The oral/airway commensals S. oralis, P. micra, and P. gingivalis were significantly enriched in lung cancer samples compared to pulmonary infections (LDA score >2, adjusted P < 0.05; Fig. 2c). Conversely, pathogens including M. tuberculosis, P. aeruginosa, A. fumigatus, and C. neoformans were more prevalent in the pulmonary infection cohort. Analysis against infection subgroups indicated significant differences for all comparisons except between lung cancer and pulmonary tuberculosis (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.0085, P = 0.056, Fig. S5b). Differences in host immune response, transposable elements expression, and immune cell abundance of different pulmonary diseases

To minimize the impact of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) on sequencing data quality, we implemented an rRNA depletion step in our experimental protocol. Subsequent analysis revealed remarkably low residual rRNA levels, with eukaryotic rRNA constituting only 1.66% (IQR 1.01–2.62%) and total rRNA accounting for 2.3% (IQR 1.38–3.98%) of the sequencing reads (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. S6). The median number of detected genes per sample reached 17,827 (IQR 16,832.5–18,738.5), indicating high data utility. To delineate host immune responses distinguishing lung cancer from infection, we performed comprehensive host gene expression profiling of BALF. Volcano plot analysis demonstrated substantial transcriptional differences across comparison groups (Supplementary Fig. S7a–d). GSEA enrichment analysis identified significant enrichment of differential expression genes (DEGs) in innate immune pathways like T-cell receptor signaling and cytokine-cytokine receptor signaling (Fig. 3a). Employing PLIER on training datasets, we delineated host transcriptomic profiles across 545 canonical pathways, identifying multiple differentially expressed latent variables (LVs) with distinct biological functions across different groups (Fig. 3b, Mann–Whitney-U test, adjusted p-value < 0.05). Specifically, in the cancer group, lower airway transcriptomes exhibited upregulation of the cell cycle (LV102 and LV107), while LV165, annotated as cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathways, displayed upregulation, contrary to LV86 in the same pathways (Fig. 3b). To further investigate immune signatures, we identified differentially expressed immune-related genes (IRGs) from the ImmPort database and interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) from previous studies15,16. The tuberculosis group exhibited significantly elevated levels of TB-associated markers GBP1 and GBP5 (Supplementary Fig. S8a, adjusted p-value < 0.01). In contrast, the cancer group showed prominent upregulation of four chemokine genes: C-C motif chemokine ligand 7 (CCL7), CCL8, CCL13, and pro-platelet basic protein (PPBP/CXCL7) (Supplementary Fig. S8A, indicated by red triangle, adjusted p-value < 0.01). These findings carry particular biological relevance as CCL7, known to be highly expressed in tumor tissues, plays a dual role in recruiting cDC1 cells to enhance antitumor immunity while also being associated with tumor-associated macrophages (M2 polarization) along with CCL8 and CCL1317,18.

We identified 27 transposable elements among lung cancer and three infection groups (Supplementary Fig. S7e–h, Fig. S8b), notably finding significantly higher LTR-ERV (LTR6A and HUERS-P3-int) levels in lung cancer (Supplementary Fig. S8b, adjusted P = 0.019).

To investigate variations in immune cell abundance across different groups, we estimated cell-type levels in host transcriptomes using computational quantification methods, including a deconvolution approach implemented in CIBERSORTx. Macrophage M1 were significantly elevated in pulmonary tuberculosis (Fig. 4b, Mann–Whitney-U test, P < 0.05), whereas Mast cell activated levels were higher in bacterial infection comparing with lung cancer (Fig. 4c, Mann–Whitney-U test P < 0.01). Neutrophils were enriched in bacterial infection comparing with lung cancer (Fig. 4d, Mann–Whitney-U test, P < 0.01). Copy number variants and CNV-derived tumor fraction of different pulmonary diseases

To assess the adequacy of host-derived genetic material following DNA depletion during sample processing, we conducted a comprehensive quality control analysis across all 402 clinical specimens. The results demonstrated excellent retention of host sequences,with a median host read proportion of 98.22% (IQR 97.46–98.66) and a median sequencing depth of 19.23 million mapped reads per sample (IQR15.95–23.66 million). This data volume exceeds that reported in the previous study13, thereby ensuring the reliability of subsequent analyses.

To enhance CNV and tumor fraction estimations in BALF mNGS data, we used three distinct software tools. CNVkit revealed slight increases in CNV counts on chromosomes 11 (lung cancer) and 3 (pulmonary infection) (Supplementary Fig. S9, P < 0.05). Higher CNV percentages on chromosome 3 were noted in the infection group (Supplementary Fig. S10, P < 0.05). However, no significant CNV count or percentage differences emerged when comparing the cancer group with the three infection subgroups (Supplementary Figs. S11 and S12, P > 0.05).

Subsequently, ichorCNA estimated tumor fractions at 5.96% (lung cancer, 95% CI 4.15–7.77%) and 6.29% (pulmonary infection, 95% CI 0.54–12.04%) (Supplementary Fig. S13a). Notably, no significant differences in tumor fractions were observed between the lung cancer and the three infection subgroups (Supplementary Fig. S13b). Calculated scores (Stromal, Immune, ESTIMATE, Tumor Purity) using ‘estimate’ software showed no differences between lung cancer and all the pulmonary infection groups (Supplementary Fig. S13c, d). This suggests that, unlike Cancer-Negative (Benign) samples12, BALF samples from infection patients display comparable levels of CNVs seen in cancer patients.

Host/microbe metagenomics-based modeling for lung cancer and pulmonary infection diagnosis

To assess the discriminatory power of multimodal profiles for pulmonary diseases in this cohort, we extracted key features from each dataset and developed a series of ensemble models accordingly (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. S1). We developed six machine learning models (Models I-VI) using individual and ensemble approaches, evaluating their performance through mean AUC values on the test dataset (detailed modeling procedures in Methods). The optimal configurations emerged as: Model I (0.1LASSO + 0.9RF, mean AUC = 0.778), Model II (0.3RF + 0.7XGBoost, mean AUC = 0.691), Model III (0.3LASSO + 0.7RF, mean AUC = 0.867), Model IV (0.2LASSO + 0.8SVM, mean AUC = 0.604), Model V (0.9LASSO + 0.1XGBoost, mean AUC = 0.560), and Model VI (0.3LASSO + 0.7RF, mean AUC = 0.869), with detailed results shown in Fig. 5a and Supplementary Figs. S14–S18. Model VI consistently achieved the highest AUC values across all comparison groups (Fig. 5c–f). In subgroup analyses, Model VI exhibited consistently strong performance in differentiating lung cancer from bacterial infections (AUC = 0.847, sensitivity = 80.6%, specificity = 82.4%, precision = 0.829, recall = 0.806, F1-score = 0.817), fungal infections (AUC = 0.872, sensitivity = 94.4%, specificity = 69.6%,, precision = 0.829, recall = 0.944, F1-score = 0.883), and pulmonary tuberculosis (AUC = 0.909, sensitivity = 91.7%, specificity = 76.0%,, precision = 0.846, recall = 0.917, F1-score = 0.88) (Fig. 5d–f, Supplementary Data S3). In the comparison between lung cancer and all pulmonary infections, Model VI demonstrated a substantially higher recall on the test dataset than Model III (Model VI: recall = 0.944; Model III: recall = 0.667), although their F1-scores were relatively close (Model VI: F1-score = 0.667; Model III: F1-score = 0.696). Since metagenomic sequencing is not the gold standard for lung cancer diagnosis, the primary goal of our model is to maximize the identification of potential cases with lung cancer. A composite predictive model for Lung cancer and infection diagnosis

We observed that due to the heterogeneity of pulmonary infections, the precision, recall, and F1-score for distinguishing lung cancer from individual types of pulmonary infections were higher than those observed in the combined Lung Cancer vs. Pulmonary Infections comparison (Supplementary Data S3). To provide more accurate and actionable classification information for clinicians, we developed a composite predictive model based on a rule-in and rule-out strategy. This approach integrates the general Model VI with the corresponding subgroup-specific Model VI (Supplementary Fig. S1). Under this framework, a patient is assigned to the rule-in category (indicating a positive lung cancer diagnosis) if both the general and any subgroup Model VI identify the case as lung cancer. Conversely, a patient is assigned to the rule-out category (indicating a pulmonary infection) if both models classify the case as infection (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. S1). Patients who do not meet the criteria for either rule-in or rule-out are classified as indeterminate. This designation serves as a clinical alert, indicating lower confidence in classification and the need for further diagnostic evaluation.

Discussion

In the realm of diagnostics, BALF-based mNGS testing has emerged as a rapid and effective method for pathogen detection in pulmonary infections. Although human-derived sequences constitute over 90% of mNGS data and have traditionally been dismissed as background noise, emerging evidence suggests these host-originated reads may contain clinically significant biomarkers reflecting disease pathology11,19. Our study introduces an innovative diagnostic paradigm that comprehensively analyzes both host and microbial components in BALF mNGS data to simultaneously evaluate lung cancer and pulmonary infections. This integrated approach demonstrates outstanding accuracy in differentiating malignant pulmonary lesions from various infectious conditions including pulmonary tuberculosis, fungal infections and bacterial pneumonias. By extracting clinically meaningful information from both microbial pathogens and human genomic signatures, our method significantly expands the clinical utility of BALF mNGS testing, transforming previously overlooked host sequences into valuable diagnostic markers.

Overfitting represents a common challenge in machine learning, occurring when a model learns noise within the training data instead of the underlying patterns, consequently leading to diminished performance on unseen data20. Many studies may potentially overestimate their model’s performance metrics, such as the Area Under the Curve (AUC), which can subsequently compromise its clinical utility. Therefore, the dataset splitting strategy is critically important. Firstly, temporal sequence serves as a more objective and mandatory criterion. Given that our patient enrollment spanned two full calendar years and considering the minimal association between disease subgroups and temporal factors, partitioning the dataset into training and test sets based on chronological order approximates the establishment of a prospective validation cohort. This approach enhances the independence of the test set. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that models evaluated using time splitting often yield lower AUC values compared to those evaluated using random splitting21. This characteristic provides a more rigorous assessment of the model’s true performance, thereby mitigating the risk of overestimating its real-world efficacy.

Despite the inherent heterogeneity of BALF samples compared to blood or tissue specimens22,23, our integrated model (Model VI) demonstrated robust performance in distinguishing lung cancer from pulmonary infections. In the test cohort, the model achieved an AUC of 0.847 (95% CI: 0.776–0.918), effectively identifying pulmonary infections of various etiologies, including bacterial, fungal, and mycobacterial. This performance is comparable to other multi-omics models based on different sample types, such as the IMX-BVN model (AUC 0.85–0.86), whole-blood transcriptomic models for sepsis diagnosis (AUC 0.82)24, and plasma cell-free RNA transcriptomic models (AUC 0.77)10, highlighting the diagnostic potential of multi-omics approaches using BALF.

Unlike conventional mNGS, which is primarily used for pathogen detection, our model demonstrated high sensitivity in identifying both lung cancer and infection subtypes (e.g., pulmonary infections 94.4%, tuberculosis 91.7%), suggesting its utility as a complementary tool to mNGS in clinical settings. It may facilitate early diagnosis prior to the availability of pathological results and help reduce unnecessary biopsies and invasive procedures in patients with low tumor risk or high likelihood of infection. To enhance clinical applicability, we implemented a rule-in/rule-out strategy for risk stratification, which further improved diagnostic accuracy for distinguishing lung cancer from various infections (ACC 0.896–0.915), making it suitable for aiding diagnosis in complex cases. In real-world clinical practice, suppose a patient is admitted with a suspected diagnosis of either lung cancer or pulmonary infection and undergoes metagenomic sequencing of BALF. After pathogen detection is completed, the patient’s BALF metagenomic data are processed through our diagnostic model. If the clinician, based on the patient’s existing clinical presentation or diagnostic indicators, suspects a specific type of infection such as bacterial, fungal, or tuberculosis infection, we apply both the general diagnostic model that distinguishes lung cancer from pulmonary infections and additional models specifically designed to differentiate lung cancer from each infection type. A rule-in and rule-out integration strategy is then employed to generate a diagnostic assessment that supports the clinician in making a definitive diagnosis. If no clear clinical indicators or specific suspicions are present, only the general diagnostic model distinguishing lung cancer from pulmonary infections is applied. The corresponding assessment result is provided, and further confirmatory testing for lung cancer or pulmonary infection is recommended to ensure diagnostic reliability (Fig. 6d).

Importantly, we fully recognize that exogenous nucleic acid contamination is a critical concern in BALF mNGS, especially in low-biomass samples. To address this, our laboratory implemented a multi-layered contamination control protocol, including spatial separation of workflows, use of negative controls and internal spike-ins5,25. In bioinformatics processing, we systematically identified and removed potential contaminants by referencing microbial profiles from negative control and non-infectious BALF samples (Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3). While contamination can obscure low-abundance pathogens, our incorporation of RNA sequencing, host transcriptomic profiling, and immune cell composition enables a multi-dimensional modeling strategy that enhances diagnostic specificity and minimizes the impact of environmental or procedural contaminants.

Building upon this contamination-aware, host–microbe integrative framework, our model incorporates multiple layers of biological information derived from BALF mNGS data—microbial DNA/RNA abundance (including bacteriophages), host gene expression, transposable element expression, immune cell composition, and tumor fraction inferred from CNV. Compared with previous studies that primarily relied on single or limited features, our model provides a more comprehensive representation of the biological complexity underlying pulmonary diseases. Early 16S rRNA-based studies have suggested that the airway microbiota in lung cancer patients differs significantly from that of healthy individuals. Notably, genera such as Veillonella and Megasphaera demonstrated high diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.888), supporting their potential as microbial biomarkers for lung cancer26. Consistently, our data revealed compositional differences between lung cancer and infectious diseases, with Veillonella parvula showing significantly higher abundance in lung cancer compared to bacterial or fungal infections (Fig. 1c; LDA score >2, adjusted P < 0.05). However, in the test cohort, a model constructed using microbial features alone yielded an AUC of only 0.645, indicating limited diagnostic power in distinguishing lung cancer from pulmonary infections. These findings highlight the necessity of incorporating additional biological dimensions to improve diagnostic accuracy.

The development of pulmonary diseases is often accompanied by alterations in host immune status, which can disrupt the homeostasis of the respiratory microbiome27. In this study, we systematically evaluated the performance of various feature types for disease discrimination. Host immune response–related features (Model III) showed the strongest discriminative ability in distinguishing lung cancer from different types of pulmonary infections, significantly outperforming individual dimensions such as microbial composition, transposable element expression, and CNV-inferred tumor fraction (Fig. 5d–f; DeLong’s ROC test, P < 0.05).

Despite the limited cellular content in some BALF samples, we successfully obtained high-quality human gene expression data and identified multiple significantly different latent variables between lung cancer and pulmonary infections using the PLIER algorithm. These latent variables encompassed pathways such as cell cycle regulation, interferon signaling, and cytokine signaling. Further analysis revealed that key genes associated with these latent variables were not only involved in lung cancer-related signaling pathways (e.g., PI3K-Akt, p53) but also regulated by upstream factors such as EGFR28. In the tuberculosis group, interferon signaling was significantly activated (Supplementary Fig. S4a), and the active tuberculosis marker gene GPB5 was highly expressed29, confirming the biological relevance of the host response features captured by our model.

Using SHAP value analysis, we identified B3GAT1, ULBP1, and CCL13 as the most representative host genes distinguishing lung cancer from infections (Fig. S19). These genes have not previously been reported in transcriptomic studies of lung cancer based on body fluids, highlighting their novelty and clinical potential. B3GAT1 may influence tumor cell motility and neuro-associated pathways through its roles in integrin signaling and HNK-1 glycan biosynthesis30. ULBP1, a ligand for the NK cell receptor NKG2D, can enhance immune surveillance and has been shown to correlate with clinical outcomes in non-small cell lung cancer patients31,32. In contrast, CCL13, a ligand of CCR2, is implicated in various tumor-related processes, including metastasis and immune suppression. High CCR2 expression in M2-like macrophages promotes bidirectional signaling with tumor cells, thereby accelerating lung cancer progression33. Additionally, CCL13 secreted by M2 tumor-associated macrophages has been shown to promote oral cancer metastasis by inducing proinflammatory cytokine expression18.

This study is the first to evaluate the expression levels of transposable elements (TEs) in BALF samples from patients with pulmonary diseases. We found that HERVK11D expression was significantly elevated in lung cancer compared to fungal infections and tuberculosis, whereas ERVK-MER11B was more highly expressed in bacterial infections than in tuberculosis (Supplementary Fig. S4, adjusted P < 0.05). These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting a strong association between high HERV-K expression and the progression of basal-like and triple-negative breast cancers, suggesting that aberrant TE activation may promote tumorigenesis through the regulation of cancer-related gene expression. In addition, long non-coding RNAs derived from HERVs have also been implicated in cancer progression by driving extensive transcriptomic changes34.

We also observed that two ERV1 elements were significantly more highly expressed in lung cancer than in all categories of pulmonary infection (Supplementary Fig. S4, adjusted P < 0.05), further supporting the potential involvement of TEs in the development of lung cancer. Interestingly, certain TEs were expressed at higher levels in pulmonary infections than in lung cancer. For instance, GSAT, which is regulated by the transcription factor AP-1, has been shown to modulate host gene expression and activate inflammatory cells, suggesting a possible role in the immune response to pulmonary infections35,36.

Several studies have attempted to diagnose pulmonary malignancies by analyzing CNVs in body fluids12,14. These studies applied whole-genome analyses to metagenomic datasets and demonstrated that CNV profiling could significantly improve diagnostic accuracy, particularly in samples with negative results from conventional tests. However, our findings showed no significant difference in the inferred tumor fraction between the lung cancer and pulmonary infection groups based on CNV analysis of BALF samples. We also observed that acute infections can trigger apoptosis, potentially leading to chromosomal abnormalities that confound CNV results37. Furthermore, the performance of CNV detection tends to improve when the tumor cell burden in BALF is relatively high12. These observations further highlight that relying solely on single-dimensional information from conventional BALF mNGS may be inadequate for the accurate diagnosis of complex or multifactorial diseases.

Despite these important findings, this study has limitations. First, the cohort did not include patients with viral pneumonia or those with lung cancer complicated by infection. Viral pneumonia often presents with distinct clinical and radiological features compared to lung cancer, and therefore may not require similarly complex diagnostic approaches. Patients with concurrent lung cancer and infection represent an important yet often overlooked population. However, due to the complex interplay of features from both malignancy and infection, this group presents significant confounding factors, making it challenging to develop an accurate differential diagnostic model. In future research, it may be worthwhile to establish a dedicated diagnostic model specifically designed to distinguish lung cancer with coexisting infection. Second, our test cohort was derived from a single center, limiting the generalizability of our findings. In this study, we proposed and preliminarily validated a novel metagenomic data analysis strategy that expands the potential diagnostic applications of mNGS in pulmonary diseases. We hope future studies will build upon and refine our approach, ultimately advancing mNGS into a more precise and multidimensional diagnostic tool.

In summary, this study proposes an analytical model that integrates both host and microbial features to support the interpretation of mNGS results. While patients undergo BALF mNGS testing (from sample processing to result reporting within 24 hours), the model can provide a probability-based distinction between lung cancer and pulmonary infections. This may assist clinical decision-making during the waiting period for histopathological or other diagnostic results. In addition, the model shows potential in distinguishing infection subtypes, which could help guide antimicrobial treatment strategies. Since the model is built on standard mNGS data, it is compatible with existing workflows and shows promise for future integration and automation within information systems or analytical pipelines. This could facilitate the transformation of mNGS from a tool for pathogen detection into a precision diagnostic platform that incorporates both microbial and host information, thereby enhancing its practical value in real-world clinical settings. Overall, this model represents an initial exploration, and further validation with larger and more diverse clinical datasets is needed to determine its broader applicability and clinical utility.

Methods

Study design, patient collection and ethics statement

This observational study retrospectively analyzed adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) admitted to the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (FAHZU), who were clinically suspected of having lung cancer or pulmonary infections. Patient enrollment occurred between February 8, 2021, and May 27, 2023. Eligible cases were required to have undergone BALF sampling within 72 h of endotracheal intubation to assist in establishing an etiological diagnosis. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) a known history of hematological malignancies (e.g., leukemia), (2) no definitive diagnosis after extensive clinical follow-up, (3) absence of matched mNGS DNA and RNA data from BALF samples, (4) a confirmed diagnosis of both lung cancer and pulmonary infection, and (5) viral pneumonia (Fig. 1A). After applying these criteria, a total of 434 cases were included in the final analysis, comprising 123 patients with lung cancer, 279 with pulmonary infections (including tuberculosis, fungal, and bacterial infections), and 32 negative controls (e.g., immune-related pneumonitis, organizing pneumonia, and drug-induced pneumonitis).

Lung cancer diagnosis was based on clinical suspicion and supported by positive results from cytology, flow cytometry, and/or tissue biopsy. Histological classification followed the 2015 WHO Classification of Lung Tumors, based on surgically resected tissue sections38. Pulmonary infections were diagnosed through clinical evaluation and identification of plausible pathogens using standard microbiological methods, including cultures, antigen/antibody assays, PCR, and sequencing (detailed in Supplementary Data S1).

All data were derived from archived clinical material at FAHZU and analyzed under a no-patient-contact protocol approved by the FAHZU Institutional Review Board (approval number: IIT20220714A). Written informed consent was obtained before sampling procedures, which included permission for the use of residual clinical specimens for research purposes. We then divided the dataset into training and test cohorts based on the chronological order of sample collection. All lung cancer cases were sorted by collection date and split into the first 70% and the last 30% to form the training and test cohort, respectively (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Data S1). The training set was used for differential analysis, feature selection and ensemble model training. The test set was used to evaluate model performance and to implement rule-in/rule-out strategies based on the combined predictions.

DNA/RNA extraction, library construction and sequencing

For metagenomic sequencing (DNA sequencing), 1 mL of BALF sample was subjected to depletion of host nucleic acid using 1 U benzonase (Sigma) and 0.5% Tween 20 (Sigma) and incubation at 37 °C for 5 min. A total of 600 µL of the mixture was transferred to new tubes containing 500 µL of ceramic beads for bead beating using a Minilys Personal TGrinder H24 Homogenizer (catalogue number: OSE-TH-01, Tiangen, China). Then, the nucleic acid from 400 µL of the pretreated sample was extracted and eluted in 60 µL elution buffer using a QIAamp UCP Pathogen Mini Kit (catalogue number: 50214, Qiagen, Germany). The extracted DNA was quantified using a Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (catalogue number: Q32854, Invitrogen, USA). For metatranscriptome sequencing (RNA sequencing), 1 mL BALF sample was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. Then, 200 μL of the precipitate was lysed in TRIzol LS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA), followed by RNA extraction using a Direct-zol RNA Miniprep kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, 30 μL DNA was used to generate libraries with the Nextera DNA Flex kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and 10 μL of purified RNA was used for cDNA generation and library preparation with an Ovation Trio RNA-Seq Library Preparation Kit (NuGEN, CA, USA). A Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit was used to measure the library concentration. The library quality was assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and a High Sensitivity DNA kit. The library was sequenced using an Illumina NextSeq 550 sequencer.

Microbial annotation, community structure comparison and differential taxon analysis

As previous study described, we used a validated mNGS sequencing pipeline for microbial composition analysis39,40. In brief, Trimmomatic was used to remove low-quality and duplicate reads, as well as adapter contamination. Human sequences were excluded by mapping to the human reference genome(hg38) using SNAP v1.0beta. SortMERNA v4.3.7 was used for ribosomal RNA removement. Kraken2 v.2.0.7 and Bracken v.2.5 created taxonomic profiles using default settings and the default database (https://benlangmead.github.io/aws-indexes/k2). Sequencing reads for detected microbes were normalized as RPM (reads per million) to correct for various sequencing depths and used for decontamination. The BALF mNGS data from 32 non-infection and non-cancer cases were used as negative controls (NC, Supplementary Data S2). Further analysis was also done to identify possible contaminants in the mNGS DNA/RNA datasets. To this end, we compared the relative abundance of taxa between background bronchoscope control and BALF samples. Taxa with median relative abundance greater in background than in BALF/frequencies in NCs were higher than 50% and average relative abundances in NCs were higher than 0.1% were identified as probable contaminants and removed40,41. For bacteriophage annotation, the cleaned reads were aligned against a curated phage database (CPD) containing 26,159 phage representative genomes using BLAST (word size: 18, e-value: 0.0005, culling limit: (1)42. Microbial and phage counting in DNA (DNA microbial abundances, DMA)/RNA (RNA microbial abundances, RMA) mNGS data relied on relative abundances40,42.

The α-diversity of the microbial composition in mNGS DNA/RNA data, including the Shannon index, Simpson index, Chao1 index, and ACE index, were computed using the “vegan” package in R software after sequence processing. Permutational multivariate ANOVA (PERMANOVA) was conducted using the “vegan” package to determine the difference in sample β-diversity (measured by Bray‒Curtis distance). Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was used to identify differences of microbial community structure. LefSE assessed the difference between each group’s microbial taxon or bacteriophage43.

Gene expression (GE), transposable elements expression (TEE), cell-type composition analysis (CC)

For the analysis of host gene expression, high-quality data were aligned to the human genome hg38 using HISAT2 with default parameters. Gene-level quantification was performed using FeatureCounts44,45. The gene counts were aggregated using the featureCounts program from the Subread package release 2.0.0 (http://subread.sourceforge.net/)40,41. Additionally, trimmed clean reads were mapped using STAR with previously defined parameters. TEtranscripts software was utilized to estimate the abundances of Transposable Elements (TE) and to conduct differential expression analysis46. The GTF file containing transposable element annotations was obtained from https://hammelllab.labsites.cshl.edu/software/#TEtranscripts. All Genes and TE were normalized, corrected batch effect and calculated differential expression in each group using the DESeq2 package, applying criteria of FDR ≤ 0.05 and Fold-change ≥ 1.547. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) for DEGs was carried out using the REACTOME, KEGG, and GO databases48–50. Significantly enriched pathways or biological processes were determined based on Fisher’s exact test (p-value < 0.05), following Benjamin and Hochberg’s adjustment40,41. To estimate the relative proportions of invasive immune cell types and infer the proportions of immune cells, the CIBERSORT algorithm was applied with the original gene signature file LM22 and 1000 permutations51. Latent variables were calculated by PLIER R package52. Continuous nonparametric data of latent variables and cell proportions were compared using the Mann‒Whitney-U test. P values from multiple testing of latent variables were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment with a significance level of 0.05.

Copy number variant-derived (CNV) tumor fractions calling

The DNA metagenomic sequencing data were used in downstream analyses to identify CNVs through the ichorCNA53. CNVkit and the estimate software package to generate ctDNA tumor fractions as previously described and validated in tumor tissue and body fluid54,55. The ichorCNA ploidy parameter restart value was set to 2 and the maximum copy number to use was lowered to 3. The tumor fraction with the highest loglikelihood was retrieved and reported. Continuous nonparametric data were compared using the Mann‒Whitney-U test. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ensemble machine learning models for DMA, RMA, GE, TEE, CC and CNV

We evaluated the strength of the multimodal profiles to differentiate pulmonary diseases in this cohort. To this end, we identified features in each of these datasets and constructed a set of ensembled models (Supplementary Fig. S1):

Step 1: Differential analysis and feature identification

Differential analysis was performed across five data types: (1) Microbial/bacteriophage DNA/RNA relative abundances (DMA/RMA) (Model I and II); (2) Host gene expression (GE) in TPM (Model III); (3) Host cell relative abundances (CC) (Model III); (4) Transposable elements (TEE) in TPM (Model IV); (5) Tumor fraction/CNV scores (CNV) (Model V); (6) All differentiated features from those five data types combine candidate features of Model VI.

Using LEfSe (for DMA/RMA), DESeq2 (for GE/TEE), and Mann–Whitney-U tests with Benjamini–Hochberg multiple testing correction (for CNV/CC), features with adjusted p-value < 0.05 were selected as candidate biomarkers. Statistical adjustments included size factor estimation, batch normalization, and dispersion modeling.

Step 2: Feature selection and model training

Feature refinement

Candidate features underwent 1000 bootstrap iterations to calculate feature frequency, followed by sequential forward selection to identify optimal feature combinations per data type. To mitigate the impact of class imbalances on feature selection and model performance, we performed undersampling (The larger sample group was randomly down-sampled to match the size of the smaller group, ensuring balanced sample sizes between both groups in every iteration.) during each of the 1,000 bootstrap iterations.

Classifier training

Four classifiers including Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Random Forest(RF) were trained using 10-fold cross-validation stratified to maintain class balance. Hyperparameters were tuned: (1) LASSO (glmnet): λ via 10-fold CV, α ∈ [0,1] (0.1 intervals); (2) SVM: Censoring-adjusted regression; (3) XGBoost: Optimized via optimBoostPenalty; (4) RF: ntree (tree count) and mtry (variables per split) optimization.

Step 3: Model selection and ensemble integration

Six models (I-VI) were evaluated based on area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) performance in the test cohort, with statistical significance assessed via the DeLong test for ROC comparisons. For ensemble integration, cancer probability was computed as:

Pr(Cancer) = α·Pr(Classifier A) + β·Pr(Classifier B)

(α + β = 1; α/β weights tested in 0.1 increments).

The best-performing individual classifier or ensemble (prioritizing highest mean AUC) was selected for final deployment.

Step 4: Validation and clinical rule integration

The optimal model (Model VI) generated disease-specific predictions in three categories: rule-in (confirming lung cancer), rule-out (excluding lung cancer), and indeterminate. A patient is classified as rule-in if both the general Model VI and at least one subgroup-specific Model VI predict lung cancer. Conversely, a patient is classified as rule-out if both the general model and all subgroup-specific models consistently predict pulmonary infection. Patients who do not meet either criterion, due to inconsistent predictions across models, are classified as indeterminate, indicating lower classification confidence and the need for further clinical evaluation.

Model accuracy was quantified via AUC, sensitivity, and specificity, Precision, Recall and F1 score, with all analyses implemented in R 4.1.0 using mlr3, glmnet, and svm packages24,56.
