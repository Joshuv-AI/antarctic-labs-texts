The effect of research training on research skills, academic performance, and stress level among medical undergraduates in China: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Juan Jiang, Pengbo Deng, Ruijia Li, Qian Yang, Chengping Hu, Yuanyuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn increasing number of medical undergraduates are participating in research training. However, little is known about the benefits and challenges of undergraduate research training and how to optimize the training process. This study investigated the effects of medical undergraduates’ engagement in research training on their research skills, academic performance, and stress level.MethodsA single-center cross-sectional study was conducted at Central South University to collect data on research training from 123 medical undergraduates using structured questionnaires. The quantitative measures included assessments of research skills, medical English test scores, grade point averages and stress scores among undergraduates with or without research training. The effects of the weekly training duration and mentorship quality on these indicators were further explored.ResultsCompared with the untrained group, the medical undergraduates in the trained group had higher scores on scientific literature reading, research data analysis, scientific paper writing, and the medical English test scores, with no significant differences in grade point average or stress scores. Among the trained undergraduates, those with ≥ 10 training hours per week had higher scores on scientific literature reading, research data analysis and scientific paper writing. Weekly training hours were positively correlated with the scores on scientific literature reading, research data analysis, scientific paper writing and stress. Undergraduates rating mentorship quality ≥ 7 points presented better scores on scientific literature reading, research data analysis and scientific paper writing.ConclusionsThe participation of medical undergraduates in research training improves their research skills without adversely affecting their academic performance. A longer weekly training duration and better mentorship quality can further enhance the research skills of undergraduates, but their mental health, including their stress level, should be carefully monitored.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07809-0.Keywords: Medical undergraduate, Research training, Research skills, Stress, Mentorship

Introduction

Medical researchers play a crucial role in the advancement of scientific research within the field of medicine. As future clinical physicians and medical researchers, medical undergraduates are key reserve personnel. Engaging medical undergraduates in research activities is essential for fostering critical thinking, problem-solving skills, and a deeper understanding of medical concepts [1]. Therefore, it has long been proposed that research-based universities should prioritize research-based learning as an important educational criterion for undergraduates [2, 3]. Participation in scientific research not only enhances the academic experience of medical undergraduates but also prepares them for future careers as health care professionals who can contribute to evidence-based practice and innovative medical advancements.

Despite the benefits of involving medical undergraduates in research training, there are challenges and barriers that they may encounter [4]. These challenges include limited access to research opportunities, a lack of mentorship, time constraints, and competing academic demands, which inevitably lead to unrelenting stress. Additionally, students may face difficulties in balancing their research commitments with their coursework, clinical responsibilities, and personal obligations. Addressing these challenges is essential to ensure that medical undergraduates can fully benefit from research training and develop the necessary skills to thrive in the health care field.

This study aims to explore the effects of medical undergraduates’ participation in research training on their research skills, academic performance, and stress level. By investigating these key areas, we strive to provide valuable insights into the benefits and challenges of engaging medical undergraduates in research training, further enhancing the overall learning experience and preparing students for more successful careers in medicine.

Methods

Study design and participants

This survey was conducted at Xiangya Medical School of Central South University from June 1 to August 31, 2024. A total of 123 medical undergraduates were recruited as participants. The inclusion criteria for the participants were as follows: (1) were aged ≥ 18 years; (2) had completed the first two years of courses at Xiangya Medical School and entered clinical courses; (3) were informed of the purpose of this study; and (4) consented to participate. Undergraduates who had participated in any form of research training project for at least three months were assigned to the trained group, whereas other undergraduates were assigned to the untrained group. All mentors involved in undergraduate research training programs were certified professionals who completed standardized training programs at Xiangya Medical School of Central South University and passed institutional qualification assessments. Mentor–trainee matching occurred through mutual selection processes.

Data were collected using Wenjuanxing, an online questionnaire survey platform in China. The participants were asked to complete the questionnaire by using their smartphone, with each device limited to submitting the results once. The questionnaire did not contain any identifying information about the individual survey respondents. The participants had the right to quit the study at any time during the survey. All data were collected anonymously, with the promise that they would be used only in this study. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University.

Questionnaire items

A standardized and structured questionnaire was designed for this study (supplementary file). The questionnaire consisted of 35 items divided into seven aspects: (1) baseline characteristics, including age, gender and year in the program; (2) information on research training, including participation or nonparticipation in research training, the year in which research training was initiated, and the purpose, forms and weekly hours of research training; (3) evaluation of mentorship quality, which was calculated using a 10-point scale based on five aspects, including expertise and knowledge, guidance style, communication skills, support and encouragement, and accessibility; (4) the acquisition of research achievements or not, where the description of the research achievements, if any, had to be submitted; (5) self-evaluations of research skills in scientific literature reading, research data analysis and scientific paper writing (the full marks for each item are 10 points); (6) academic performance, as presented by the score on the standardized medical English test (on a scale of 100 points) and grade point average (GPA); and (7) stress level, measured by seven questions about stress (Items 1, 6, 8, 11, 12, 14 and 18) from the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale 21 (DASS-21) [5]. Each item of stress was scored on a scale from 0 (does not apply to me at all) to 3 (applies to me very much or most of the time); thus, the total score for each student can range from 0 to 21.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Continuous variables were tested for a normal distribution using the Lilliefors test before statistical expression and comparison. Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as the means ± standard deviations and were compared using Student’s t test, whereas nonnormally distributed continuous variables are expressed as medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as counts (percentages) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of medical undergraduates

As shown in Table 1, 123 medical undergraduates, including 42 males and 81 females, participated in this survey. The median age was 22 years. Among these undergraduates, 21.1% were juniors, 49.6% were seniors, and 37.4% were fifth-year students. These undergraduates were divided into trained and untrained groups on the basis of whether they had participated in research training. The trained group consisted of 95 individuals, whereas the untrained group comprised 28 individuals. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of gender composition, age, or program year distribution.

The effects of research training on research skills, academic performance and stress levels

We compared the research skills, academic performance and stress levels between the trained and untrained groups, and the results are shown in Fig. 1. The trained group demonstrated significant advantages in self-evaluation scores on scientific literature reading ability (6.0 [5.0–7.0] vs. 5.0 [4.0–6.0], P = 0.001), research data analysis ability (5.0 [4.0–7.0] vs. 5.0 [2.0–5.0], P = 0.014), and scientific paper writing ability (6.0 [5.0–7.0] vs. 3.0 [1.0–5.0], P < 0.001). Additionally, the undergraduates in the trained group had higher scores on the standardized medical English test (78.6 [69.7–83.2] vs. 71.2 [65.7–78.3], P = 0.023) and tended to have better GPAs (3.70 [3.48–3.80] vs. 3.30 [3.00-3.78], P = 0.073) than did those in the untrained group. There was no significant difference in the DASS-21 stress score between the trained and untrained groups (7.0 [2.0–8.0] vs. 7.0 [6.0–8.0], P = 0.283).

Summary of research training activities among medical undergraduates

We subsequently conducted further analysis of the characteristics of research training among trained medical undergraduates (Table 2). A total of 18.9%, 54.7%, and 24.2% of the undergraduates started participating in research training programs in their first, second, and third years of college, respectively. The common purposes of research training for these undergraduates included enhancing research skills (84.2%), learning laboratory skills (55.8%), obtaining research achievements (54.7%), and becoming interested in scientific research (51.6%). The majority of undergraduates participated in the National College Students’ Innovative Entrepreneurial Training Plan Program; 48.4% took part in research training programs provided by the university, and 42.1% conducted self-initiated research training under the guidance of supervisors. The median research training duration of these students was 7.0 h per week. They rated mentorship quality with a median score of 8.0 (IQR 5.0–9.0) points. Through research training, 60.0% of these medical undergraduates eventually obtained research achievements.

The effects of the weekly training duration on research skills, academic performance and stress levels

In the trained group, 95 medical undergraduates had varying weekly research training durations, represented by the hours spent on research training per week. We then compared the research skills, academic performance, and stress levels of the participants with different training durations (weekly training hours < 10: short-training group; weekly training hours ≥ 10: long-training group). As shown in Fig. 2, the long-training group demonstrated better performance in self-evaluation scores on scientific literature reading ability (7.0 [6.0–8.0] vs. 6.0 [5.0–7.0], P = 0.022), research data analysis ability (6.0 [5.8-7.0] vs. 5.0 [3.0–6.0], P = 0.012), and scientific paper writing ability (7.0 [6.0-8.3] vs. 5.0 [4.0–7.0], P = 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed in the medical English test score (76.6 [70.9–83.5] vs. 78.6 [69.1–83.1], P = 0.876) or GPA (3.7 [3.5–3.9] vs. 3.7 [3.5–3.8], P = 0.424) between these two groups. Interestingly, the long-training group tended to have higher stress scores (8.0 [5.75–9.75] vs. 6.0 [2.0–8.0], P = 0.139).

We further conducted a Pearson correlation analysis between weekly training hours and these indicators. The results (Table 3) indicated that weekly hours of research training were positively correlated with scientific literature reading ability (r = 0.243, P = 0.018), research data analysis ability (r = 0.235, P = 0.022), and scientific paper writing ability (r = 0.363, P < 0.001) but were not significantly correlated with GPA (r = 0.118, P = 0.308). Notably, weekly training hours were significantly positively correlated with stress scores (r = 0.248, P = 0.015).

The effects of mentorship quality on research skills, academic performance and stress levels

Research mentors play an important role in the research training of medical undergraduates. We subsequently divided the trained medical undergraduates into two groups on the basis of their ratings of mentorship quality (< 7 points and ≥ 7 points), and we compared their research skills, academic performance, and stress levels. According to the results (Fig. 3), medical undergraduates rating mentorship quality ≥ 7 points presented better self-evaluation scores on scientific literature reading ability (7.0 [5.0–8.0] vs. 6.0 [5.0–7.0], P = 0.041), research data analysis ability (6.0 [4.5-7.0] vs. 5.0 [3.8-6.0], P = 0.066), and scientific paper writing ability (6.0 [5.0-7.5] vs. 5.0 [4.0–7.0], P = 0.033). There was no significant difference in the medical English test score (73.1 [65.4–82.8] vs. 80.2 [73.1–83.3], P = 0.083), GPA (3.7 [3.5–3.9] vs. 3.7 [3.3–3.8], P = 0.188), or stress score (6.0 [2.0–8.0] vs. 7.0 [3.5-8.0], P = 0.372) between the two groups.

Discussion

The present study reveals that participation in research training significantly enhances the research skills and medical English proficiency of medical undergraduates without adversely affecting their academic performance. More importantly, our data show for the first time that the weekly training duration and mentorship quality are crucial factors in improving the research skills of medical undergraduates. However, longer training hours per week significantly increase the stress level of undergraduates, highlighting the need for focused attention to their mental health.

It has been well acknowledged that research training is conducive to undergraduate education and the career development of participants [6–8]. In the past, opportunities for medical undergraduates to comprehensively improve their research skills through early-stage research training were relatively limited [9]. In this study, nearly 80% of medical undergraduates actively participated in scientific research training in official or unofficial ways, suggesting that current undergraduates have a stronger motivation and more opportunities to engage in scientific research. However, this also presents challenges, including time management to balance academic and personal life, physical fatigue, and stress and anxiety derived from research work, which can harm the well-being of medical undergraduates [10]. Our research findings provide meaningful insights into the effects of medical undergraduates’ participation in research training on their research skills and academic performance. In contrast to concerns that engaging in research activities may lead to a decline in GPA, our data reveal that medical undergraduates who participated in research training demonstrate improvements in their research skills and medical English proficiency without compromising their overall academic performance. The positive association between research training and enhanced research skills aligns with the results of previous studies [2, 11, 12] highlighting the benefits of hands-on research experience in developing critical thinking, problem-solving, and analytical skills among medical students. By actively engaging in research projects, medical undergraduates have the opportunity to apply theoretical knowledge to practical scenarios, conduct literature reviews, design experiments, analyze data, and draw evidence-based conclusions. These experiences not only deepen their understanding of medical concepts but also prepare them for future research endeavors and clinical practice.

Notably, our research findings shed light on the relationship between the weekly duration of research training and its effect on undergraduates’ research skills, academic performance, and stress level. We found that a longer duration of engagement in research training is associated with stronger research skills without negatively affecting undergraduates’ GPA. This finding suggests that extended exposure to research activities allows students to further develop their critical thinking, problem-solving, and analytical skills, enhancing their overall research skills. However, our study also indicates a positive correlation between the duration of research training and students’ stress levels. While the data suggest a correlation between the research engagement duration and elevated stress levels, it must be emphasized that this finding may be influenced by confounding factors such as examination periods and clinical rotations. Compared with other student groups, medical students exhibit greater levels of stress and depression [13, 14]. It has been reported that 20% of medical students report problematic drinking due to stress and anxiety [15]. Elevated stress levels may stem from multifactorial interactions including concurrent stressors such as academic assessments and personal matters. The duration of research training is observed merely as a potential associated factor. The increased time commitment and responsibilities associated with scientific research training may contribute to heightened stress levels among students, especially when they must balance research duties with academic coursework, clinical rotations, and personal obligations. Mentors and medical school staff should focus on and regularly check the mental health of these medical undergraduates. More importantly, it is essential for educational institutions and research mentors to provide adequate support, resources, and mentorship to help students manage their workload effectively and cope with the challenges of research training while maintaining their well-being [16, 17].

The significance of mentorship for medical undergraduates’ research skills is highlighted in our study, as indicated by the positive correlation between higher mentorship ratings and stronger research skills among students. Mentorship plays a crucial role in guiding students through the research process, providing support, feedback, and expertise to help them develop essential research competencies and navigate challenges effectively [18]. The quality of mentorship can greatly influence students’ research experience and outcomes, underscoring the importance of effective mentorship in research training programs. To increase the effectiveness of mentorship for medical undergraduate students engaging in research training, several strategies can be implemented [19]. First, it is essential for mentors to establish clear expectations, goals, and timelines for research projects to ensure that students have a structured framework to follow. Regular communication and feedback sessions between mentors and students are vital for monitoring progress, addressing concerns, and providing guidance on research methodologies and data interpretation. Furthermore, mentors should create a supportive and inclusive learning environment that encourages open communication, collaboration, and knowledge sharing. By fostering a culture of intellectual curiosity and mutual respect, mentors can inspire students to explore diverse research topics, engage in critical discussions, and develop innovative solutions to meet research challenges. A recent randomized controlled trial reported that mentor training based on self-determination theory and peer group mentoring promoted the breadth of mentor–protégé dyad discussions and the quality of time together [20], offering new effective ways to improve the quality of mentorship and achieve a good mentoring relationship.

This research has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, our findings are based on data from a single institution in China and may not be generalizable to other medical education settings with different research training programs or student populations. Future research involving multiple institutions and diverse student cohorts could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of research training on medical undergraduates. Second, although our findings reveal several intriguing associations, this research cannot establish causality due to the inherent limitations of cross-sectional studies, such as the relationship between the research engagement duration and stress levels. Third, the retrospective nature of our data collection method may introduce bias and limit the depth of the information gathered. Longitudinal studies that track students’ research experiences over time could offer a more nuanced perspective on the long-term effects of research engagement. Finally, the subjective nature of mentor ratings and self-reported measures of research skills and stress levels may introduce potential biases in the data. Incorporating objective measures and multiple sources of feedback could increase the results of validity and reliability of future research on this topic.

Conclusions

In summary, our study highlights the positive effects of medical undergraduates’ participation in research training on their research skills and medical English proficiency without adversely affecting their academic performance. The weekly duration of research engagement was found to be positively correlated with enhanced research skills, although it was also positively related to students’ stress levels. Furthermore, the quality of mentorship provided to students significantly influenced their research skills, underscoring the importance of effective mentorship in research training programs. More studies focusing on research engagement among medical undergraduates are needed to optimize the research training experience and support students in achieving their academic and professional goals.
