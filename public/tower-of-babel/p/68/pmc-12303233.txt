On the Move in the community: Protocol for a hybrid 1 trial examining effectiveness and fidelity of a community-based group exercise program for older adults
Authors: Jennifer S. Brach, Jessie M. VanSwearingen, Janet Freburger, Bryan J. Weiner, John J. Zanardelli, Subashan Perera
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:On the Move (OTM), a group exercise program to improve mobility in older adults, is efficacious when delivered by research staff. The next step in the development of OTM as a fully implementable intervention is to conduct an effectiveness study in which the intervention is delivered in community settings by community providers.Methods:We describe the methods of a hybrid 1 cluster randomized, single-blind, intervention trial to compare the effectiveness of OTM to a delayed intervention control in 502 community-dwelling older adults across 44 sites. OTM classes are taught by certified instructors in the community twice a week for 12 weeks. Control centers receive no intervention for the first 12 weeks followed by 12-weeks of OTM classes. Participants are assessed at baseline, 12 and 24 weeks. The primary outcome is gait speed. Intervention fidelity, measured by adherence and competence in intervention delivery, is assessed by review of instructor intervention diaries and observation. Organizational, instructor, and participant-level factors which may impact fidelity are assessed using questionnaires, focus groups, and structured interviews.Conclusion:The findings of this trial will 1) establish the effectiveness of OTM on improvements in walking and post-intervention persistence of benefits, 2) assess intervention fidelity and identify the impact of organizational, instructor, and participant level factors on fidelity, and 3) determine the extent to which fidelity moderates the effectiveness of OTM. The information derived from this project will provide valuable insight into the real-world effectiveness of OTM as a health promotion program for improving mobility in older adults.

Background

On the Move (OTM), a group exercise program to improve walking in older adults, was shown through a cluster-randomized trial to be superior to a usual care exercise program for improving walking ability [1,2]. Though OTM demonstrated benefits of improving mobility through exercise [1,3,4], the program was developed and tested under research conditions [5]. In the original efficacy/effectiveness trial, the researchers began to address this evidence-practice gap by conducting OTM in community settings; however, the intervention was delivered by research personnel. The next step in the successful implementation of OTM is to conduct an effectiveness (Stage IV) study where the intervention is delivered in community settings by community providers [6].

Interventions tested in highly controlled trials often do not yield the same outcomes when applied in a real-life context. Poor intervention fidelity may factor in this dilution of effect and potentially lead to faulty conclusions about (lack of) effectiveness [7,8]. Intervention fidelity can be particularly important for exercise-based interventions for which sufficient dose, intensity, and duration are expected to impact the desired outcomes. It is imperative to monitor and measure intervention fidelity during translation into practice, to minimize type III error [9].

Intervention fidelity may be influenced by many factors. Hasson [10] proposes facilitation strategies, responsiveness to the intervention, intervention complexity, recruitment, and context as such factors (Table 1). These factors can be measured at the organizational, instructor, and participant level. Understanding factors that influence fidelity will help to inform future implementation efforts.

We describe a hybrid 1 trial, blending effectiveness and implementation aims to: 1) establish the effectiveness of OTM on improvements in walking ability and post-intervention persistence of benefits, 2) assess intervention fidelity (adherence and competence) and identify the impact of organizational, instructor, and participant level factors on fidelity, and 3) determine the extent to which fidelity, moderates the effectiveness. Establishing the effectiveness of OTM delivered by community instructors is necessary before wide-scale implementation. Understanding factors related to intervention fidelity and the impact of fidelity on outcomes is critical when transitioning from controlled trials to real-life contexts [10]. Unless fidelity is evaluated, it cannot be determined whether a lack of intervention effect is due to poor implementation or inadequacies inherent to the program itself (i.e., Type III error). Only by understanding and measuring whether OTM was implemented with fidelity can we gain a better understanding of how OTM works, and the extent to which outcomes can be improved.

Methods

Study overview

The study is a cluster randomized, single-blind, intervention trial to compare the effectiveness of OTM to a delayed intervention control in 502 community-dwelling older adults, across 44 community centers. OTM classes are taught by certified community OTM instructors twice a week for 12 weeks. Control arm centers receive no intervention for the first 12 weeks and then 12-weeks of OTM classes. Participants are assessed at baseline, 12 and 24 weeks by individuals masked to the treatment group (Fig. 1). Intervention fidelity is assessed via structured review of instructor intervention diaries and observation (see fidelity checklist in the supplemental material). Organizational, instructor and participant-level factors which may impact fidelity are assessed using mixed methods (questionnaires, focus groups, and structured interviews). The study is approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board and registered in clinicaltrails.gov (NCT05126355). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Study sites

Planned study sites include 44 community centers and fitness facilities in Western Pennsylvania designed to provide adults 60 years of age or older with a variety of social and educational experiences, including exercise, wellness, and physical fitness programs. We selected seven counties in Southwestern Pennsylvania, targeting centers in underserved communities that represent a diverse group of older adults.

Study participants

The target population is adults aged 60 or older, who attend community centers or fitness facilities, can ambulate household distances independently, and can participate in group exercise programs. To maximize external validity, exclusion criteria are minimal: 1) impaired cognition, defined as the inability to understand the informed consent process, 2) plans to leave the area for an extended period, and 3) any acute or unstable illness or medical condition.

On the Move (OTM) instructors

Individuals interested in being an OTM instructor are identified from the study sites or from the community. Ideally, they have a background in exercise/fitness, working with older adults or healthcare. All potential instructors must complete the 12-h training to become certified in OTM.

Intervention

The duration and frequency of OTM is 50-min sessions, twice weekly for 12 weeks. OTM is based on principles of motor learning that enhance smooth and automatic movement control [11,12]. The program contains a warm-up (5 min), stepping patterns (15 min), walking patterns (15 min), strengthening exercises (10 min), and cool-down exercises (5 min). The warm-up and cool down contain gentle range of motion exercises and stretches for the lower extremities and trunk. Considered essential components, the stepping and walking patterns are goal-oriented patterns that promote the timing and coordination of stepping integrated with the phases of the gait cycle [12–15]. Conceptually, the exercise is intended to achieve its effects by shifting the center of pressure posterolateral and then forward, encouraging hip extension prior to stepping, loading the trailing limb, coordinating activation of the abductors of the soon-to-be swing leg with adductors of the stance limb, and shifting the center of pressure in medial stance to unload the stepping limb and generate momentum [16–18]. The stepping and walking patterns are progressed time to increase motor skill in walking. Progression is based on separately increasing the speed, amplitude or accuracy of performance prior to a more complex motor skill task [12]. The strengthening exercises are conducted sitting and standing, targeting the lower extremity muscles.

Randomization and masking

To prevent cross-contamination and to preserve the group-based nature of intervention delivery randomization occurs at the facility level. To facilitate operational convenience and for stratification purposes, facilities are randomized in waves with facility location and/or size similar within a wave. For each wave, we randomize facilities to two generically labeled arms (e.g., A and B) in an approximately 1:1 ratio. Once a satisfactorily balanced assignment to A and B arms is observed, labels A and B are randomly assigned to immediate OTM and control (delayed OTM) intervention groups. In the facility consent form, the facility leadership will have agreed to keep randomized assignment confidential until after all baseline assessments have been completed at the facility by blinded study personnel.

Participant outcomes

Outcomes are assessed in both arms at baseline (week 0), week 12, and week 24 at the study sites by blinded staff.

Mobility and physical function

The primary outcome is gait speed, a reliable, valid, sensitive, and specific measure that correlates with functional ability and balance confidence [19–22]. Further, it is associated with future disability, falls, hospitalization, nursing home admission, and mortality [23–28]. Gait speed is a responsive measure sensitive to change over time [21,29]. An improvement in gait speed is likely a good indicator of the effectiveness of an exercise program [21,26,27]. Participants complete 2 passes on a 6-m course at their usual walking speed and is timed during the central 4 m. Gait speed is averaged across passes. The test-retest reliability is 0.98 [19]. A higher speed is better, and 0.05 m/s is considered a small but meaningful change [21].

The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Physical Function – Short Form 6b is a valid and psychometrically sound measure of function [30,31]. It has been studied extensively, is easy to administer and has a low participant burden. Higher scores indicate better physical function [32].

Satisfaction

Participant satisfaction is assessed at the conclusion of the exercise program using a survey with five items measured on a Likert scale and three yes/no questions. The Likert items assess the degree of satisfaction with various components of the exercise program (i.e., the exercises, instructor, music, space, and the overall program). The yes/no questions assess if the program met the participants’ expectations, if they feel they benefited from the program, and if they would recommend the program to others.

Safety.

Adverse events that occur during the exercise sessions are documented by the instructor. In addition, instructors record any complaints and other illnesses verbalized by the participants that occur outside class.

Fidelity measures

Fidelity is the degree to which an intervention is implemented as prescribed in the original protocol and as intended by the program developers [7,33]. Fidelity is assessed via adherence to content (inclusion of essential components of OTM), adherence to dosage (frequency and duration of exercise classes), and competence of delivery (the quality of program delivery by instructor).

Content

Inclusion of essential components of OTM is determined from class diaries. Instructors keep a diary of time spent on each component in each of the 24 classes. Study staff review the diaries to determine if the essential components (warm-up, walking, stepping patterns, strengthening exercises and cool-down) are included in each class. For each class, instructors are classified as being fully adherent, adherent to core components only, or non-adherent.

Dosage.

Dosage is quantified as the frequency, and duration of exercise classes and participant attendance. Each site documents when classes are offered, providing a measure of the dosage made available is recorded as number of classes (0–24), number of weeks (0− 12), and total minutes of exercise (0− 1200). Dosage is also measured at the participant level via class attendance logs.

Competence

Delivery quality is determined through review of class video recordings. Two of the 24 classes offered (one from the first 6 weeks and one from the second 6 weeks) are recorded. Recordings of the classes are used for two reasons: to likely decrease reactivity effects (i.e. instructors performing differently with a live observer) and permit the reviewers to watch the videos repeatedly if needed [33]. The content experts use a standardized checklist that assesses whether instructors appropriately demonstrated, modified, and progressed the exercises (see fidelity checklist in supplemental materials).

Adaptations

Adaptations are planned or unplanned modifications to the intervention during implementation. Some occur to retain fidelity while others occur in reaction to a circumstance. While some modifications may facilitate implementation and sustainability, they can also erode intervention integrity. Therefore, documenting adaptations is critical. Review of the exercise diaries, class videos, and self-report from the focused interviews with instructors and center management will capture modifications. Adaptations are recorded, defined, and classified using the Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications-Expanded (FRAME) [34].

Factors impacting fidelity

Factors impacting fidelity include facilitation strategies, responsiveness of participants, instructors and management, intervention complexity, recruitment, and context (Table 1) [35]. To capture information across organizational, instructor and participant levels, we utilize quantitative (e.g., established measures/surveys) and qualitative approaches (e.g., focus groups, semi-structured interviews), as appropriate. We also capture organizational, instructor and participant demographics.

Quantitative approach

Facilitation strategies

Instructors are encouraged to contact OTM support staff through the website, email, or phone if they have concerns. The support staff provide feedback and suggestions for class delivery and help instructors actively problem-solve through issues. Mostly, verbal consultation is adequate. Instructors can request an in-person visit/consultation for a persisting issue. The support system utilization is tracked.

Responsiveness

Responsiveness by those delivering and receiving the intervention can influence intervention fidelity. Factors may include a participant’s acceptance of the responsibilities, the management’s beliefs, and participants’ and instructors’ beliefs about the usefulness of OTM. We capture responsiveness using reliable and valid Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM) and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) [36].

AIM [36].

AIM is a measure of acceptability of OTM among participants, instructors and site managers. It contains 4 questions on if the intervention meets their approval, is appealing, and liked and welcomed. Scores range 4–20. Higher scores represent greater acceptability. AIM is administered before and after the intervention.

IAM [36].

IAM is a measure of the perceived fit, relevance and compatibility of the intervention for a given participant, instructor, and site manager. It contains 4 questions on if the intervention seems fitting, suitable, applicable and a good match. Scores range 4–20. Higher scores represent greater perceived fit. IAM is administered before and after the intervention.

FIM [36].

FIM is a measure of the extent to which OTM can be successfully used or carried out within a given agency or setting. FIM contains 4 questions scored on ability to implement, possibility, ability to do, and ease of use. Scores range 4–20. Higher scores represent greater feasibility. FIM is administered to site managers and instructors prior to OTM initiation.

Recruitment

We will obtain demographic characteristics such as gender and race of all members from the site managers to inform the generalizability of the findings of those who participate in On the Move.

Context

We used the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to guide our selection of items representing the inner setting (i.e. implementing organization) [37]. Items represent the constructs of structural characteristics, networks and communications, culture, implementation climate and readiness for implementation. Some items on the questionnaire include financial resources, prior experience offering evidence-based programs, compatibility of OTM with other organized programs and mission of the center, and amount and type of programming offered. The site manager completes the questionnaire prior to OTM initiation.

Qualitative approach

Qualitative methods, focus groups and semi-structured interviews, are used to better understand the factors and how or why they may impact fidelity and effectiveness. We delve into the intervention complexity from the instructor perspective; the acceptability of the intervention by the manager, instructor, and participant; the appropriateness of the intervention as viewed by managers, instructors, and participants; and the feasibility of the intervention as viewed by the managers and the instructors. The qualitative data builds on our quantitative data and is used to help explain the results of the quantitative findings [38].

Focus groups

Participant focus groups are conducted at each site after post-intervention data collection. Participants are compensated. Using the responses from the AIM and IAM questionnaires, we develop open-ended questions that expand on responses. For example, a participant selects “disagree” to “OTM is appealing to me” in AIM, we ask them why, delving into specific reasons.

Semi-structured interviews

We conduct 30-min interviews with all instructors and site managers in person or over the phone after quantitative data collection. Again, we use questionnaire responses to develop interview scripts, probing for clarity and elaboration. For example, if a manager states that OTM is not a good match for the participants (IAM), we ask them to explain why. Further, we ask the instructors about their feelings on intervention complexity, including questions on instructor support materials, such as the training manual.

Sample size justification and statistical power

We assumed a standard deviation of 0.13 m/s for gait speed change [1], an 80% retention rate, and an average cluster size of 13.14 (coefficient of variation 0.5529) and an intra-cluster correlation of 0.053 among senior centers [1]. The resulting design effect is 1.86 [39,40]. We will be able to detect a difference as small as 0.05 m/s in gait speed change between interventions with 80% power in a two-tailed test conducted at α = 0.05 with 251 + 251 = 502 enrolled participants or 22 + 22 = 44 sites including 4 contingency sites.

We will be able to estimate with 95% confidence rates of any participant-level dichotomized metric (such as percent with at least 80% class attendance) with a margin of error no greater than ±9.6% and ± 6.8%, respectively, in combined and separate arm analyses [41,42]. We will be able to detect within- and combined-arm correlations representing influencing factors-fidelity associations and fidelity-effectiveness associations as small as 0.27 and 0.19 [43–45].

Data analysis

Overview

We will perform all main analyses based on intention-to-treat. Participant flow will be summarized using a CONSORT extension diagram for cluster randomized studies [46]. Data will be summarized using appropriate descriptive statistics. Facility characteristics will be compared using Student’s t-, Wilcoxon rank sum, Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests. Baseline participant characteristics will be compared between the two arms using linear mixed and generalized estimating equations (GEE) models, as appropriate, considering site-clustering. We will control for any significant differences by including them as additional covariates in the sensitivity analyses. We will use multiple imputation to account for missing data where applicable/feasible [47–49].

Main effectiveness analyses

We will fit a series of linear mixed models [50], with baseline to follow-up changes in continuous outcomes as the dependent variable; intervention arm (immediate OTM/delayed OTM control), follow-up time point (12/24 weeks) and their interaction as the fixed effects; the baseline value of the continuous outcome as a covariate; a facility random effect to account for clustering; and an exchangeable working correlation structure to account for correlation between multiple post-intervention measurements. The magnitude and statistical significance at α = 0.05 of the active vs control difference at 12 weeks for gait speed will serve as the formal test of the primary hypothesis. If significant, we will compute percentage of responders (0.05+ m/s improvement) in both arms and number-needed-to-treat. We will employ the same strategy for PROMIS-mobility. While post-intervention assessments of the delayed intervention groups are not typically used in analyses, we will conduct a sensitivity analysis with that data via an additional means contrast which compares the average improvement of 0–12 weeks in immediate OTM and 12–24 weeks in delayed intervention group (both due to OTM) with the 0–12-week changes in the delayed intervention (due to control condition).

We will employ two strategies for evaluating the longer term 24-week changes due to intervention. There will be no 24-week control group for comparison, due to the delayed intervention delivery in the control group. As such, first, using the first 12 weeks of the control group as a conservative (because they are likely to have a greater decline over 24 weeks if not for the delayed intervention) reference, we will construct an additional means contrast to compare 24-week OTM changes to 12-week control group changes. Second, we will assess longitudinal changes (during 0–12-, 12–24-, and 0–24-week periods) in the immediate OTM arm. Means contrasts restricted to the immediate OTM arm will be constructed to estimate changes over 0–12 weeks (anticipated to be highly significant immediate benefit), 0–24 weeks (anticipated to be significant effect persistence), and 12–24 weeks (anticipated to be not significant attenuation).

Intervention fidelity and influencing factors

We will focus on estimation and testing exploratory hypotheses comparing intervention fidelity across factors of interest. We will compute appropriate descriptive statistics based on continuous/categorical nature of the metrics fidelity and construct 95% confidence intervals. We will consider dichotomizing metrics with skewed distributions (e.g., class attendance) or sparse categories. We will compare fidelity metrics across immediate/delayed nature of the delivery of intervention and organizational-, instructor- and participant-level factors of interest such as organization location, instructor experience, participant baseline characteristics and expectations. We will make comparisons using the same methods used for baseline comparisons.

To identify the impact of organizational, instructor and participant level factors on intervention fidelity we will focus on correlating factors potentially impacting fidelity (e.g., AIM, IAM and FIM at pre-, post-, and pre- to post-intervention change) to intervention fidelity metrics. We will combine data from both 0–12 weeks in the immediate and 12–24 weeks from delayed groups to make maximal use of the available data. We will use techniques that account for clustering of classes and participants by facility. Specifically, we will fit a series of linear mixed or GEE models, as appropriate, using intervention fidelity metrics as dependent variables; each of the factors potentially impacting fidelity as the independent variable of interest; and a facility random effect or an exchangeable correlation structure to account for clustering.

Intervention fidelity and effectiveness

We will employ a similar strategy to the preceding section but use pre- to post-intervention change in mobility measures as the dependent variable; intervention fidelity metrics as independent variables of interest; and pre-intervention value of outcome as a covariate. Fidelity metrics will be used in their native continuous (e.g., number of classes attended) or categorical (e.g., adherent vs nonadherent to content) scales in the above models to establish the associations with greatest statistical power. In addition, with alternative dichotomized operational definitions of continuous fidelity metrics using varying cut-points, we will explore whether there are any thresholds that elicit a large difference in mobility gains, which may indicate a level of fidelity above which effectiveness encounters diminishing returns (e.g., <80% vs ≥80% attendance).

Qualitative analysis of focus groups and interviews

Thematic analysis [51,52] will be used to develop a list of codes that identify the major emergent conceptual categories in the data. Initially, two coders will read through a subset of the focus group and interview transcripts to identify the major themes. The code lists will then be consolidated into a single codebook by the project manager, to then be approved by the principal investigator and research team. The two coders will then independently apply these codes to all of the focus group and interview transcripts using ATLAS.ti 8, a qualitative data analysis software program that enables multi-coder projects (Cleverbridge, Inc., Chicago, IL). Krippendorff’s Alpha will be used to measure inter-coder reliability, and disagreements in applications of the codes will be adjudicated jointly by the principal investigator and project manager. Based on the results of the adjudication, the project manager will remove invalid code applications from the ATLAS.ti data files and consolidate the two sets of codings into a single set of code applications for the focus group and interview transcripts. To understand the themes discussed in the focus groups and interviews, ATLAS.ti 8 will then be used to generate a list of quotations for each individual code.

Discussion

Walking is important for maintaining independence and is highly valued by older adults. Unfortunately, walking limitations affect up to 76% of older Americans [53], which accounts for $42 billion annually in healthcare costs [54]. Despite the growing evidence in well-controlled research trials (Stage III efficacy studies) that physical activity and exercise are beneficial for the health of older adults, few of these interventions have been adopted in clinical or community practice [5,55–57]. Most of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) compendium exercise interventions have only been studied in efficacy trials conducted under controlled conditions [5]. True effectiveness trials for exercise are rare but necessary to determine whether it makes sense to implement a program in real-world settings. To eliminate the evidence-practice gap there is a need to transition from efficacy research findings to effectiveness studies in community settings [5,55]. Clinical and research programs are available to a select group of individuals. Transitioning to a community-based program delivered by community instructors will greatly increase the reach of exercise programs. By meeting older adults where they live (urban, rural, high/low resource areas), community exercise programs may have a wider impact on walking and health.

At the conclusion of this proposed hybrid 1 trial, we will have important information to inform the wide-scale implementation of OTM in the future. Specifically, identifying organizational, instructor, and participant level factors that impact intervention fidelity is the first step in ensuring the OTM intervention is delivered as intended. Once influencing factors are identified, strategies can be developed to address these factors to improve fidelity of the intervention. For example, if we determine instructors with no prior experience delivering group exercise classes have low fidelity, we can either adjust our criteria for becoming a certified instructor (i.e., include experience in group exercise instruction as a requirement) or require additional training for potential instructors without this experience.

Understanding the extent to which intervention fidelity moderates the effectiveness of OTM is also critical. Intervention fidelity likely influences the effectiveness of OTM [10], but the level of fidelity required to achieve the desired outcome is unknown. Recognizing the consequences of differing levels of intervention fidelity on effectiveness will inform how strict we need to be in the delivery and “quality control” of the intervention. If a high level of fidelity is required to achieve the outcome of improved walking, then monitoring of fidelity throughout implementation and delivery will be more critical than if lower levels of fidelity are required to achieve the desired outcome.

Though this is a rigorously designed trial, important limitations should be considered. First, the assessment of delivery quality is determined through review of class video recordings which may influence the behavior of the instructors and participants. Individuals may behave differently if they know they are being recorded. However, recordings of the classes permit the reviewers to watch the videos repeatedly if needed to rate the delivery quality. Secondly, the dosage component of fidelity is quantified as the frequency and duration of exercise classes and is lacking an indicator of intensity. In this community-based effectiveness trial it is not feasible to obtain a measure of intensity. We can define dosage using two (frequency and duration) of the three components.

Conclusion

This hybrid 1 trial examining the effectiveness and fidelity of the OTM program delivered by real-world instructors is significant in that it addresses walking limitations, a common and costly problem for older adults and it transitions aspects of a clinical exercise program to the community, to enhance accessibility for a greater number of individuals. Examination of intervention fidelity and factors that may impact fidelity in community providers and the association between intervention fidelity and benefit will greatly inform future implementation efforts.
