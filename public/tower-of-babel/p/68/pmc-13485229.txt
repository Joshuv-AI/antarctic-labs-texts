Revisiting the life cycle of Margalefidinium polykrikoides Group III
Authors: Eduardo Pérez‐Vega, Kenneth N Mertens, Pjotr Meyvisch, Margaret R Mulholland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDinoflagellates produce cysts as a strategy to withstand environmental stressors, with nutrient depletion generally considered a key trigger for cyst production. Resting cysts are thick‐walled, typically composed of one to several layers, and characterized by a prolonged dormancy period. In contrast, pellicle cysts possess a thin, single wall and exhibit no dormancy or a markedly shorter dormancy than resting cysts of the same species. Margalefidinium polykrikoides produces pellicle and resting cysts, whereas its congener, M. fulvescens, has been shown to produce pumpkin‐like structures. Using phase‐contrast microscopy, time‐lapse microscopy, FlowCam, and attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared microspectroscopy (ATR μ‐FTIR), we investigated the environmental triggers of sac production, the role of the sacs in the life cycle of the M. polykrikoides American/Malaysian ribotype (Group III) strain, and the chemical composition of the sacs. The sacs observed here differed from pellicle cysts described in M. polykrikoides Group III NY and Group I Korea strains but were indistinguishable from the pumpkin‐like structures of M. fulvescens. Phosphate depletion of the culture medium was determined as the main trigger for sac production. Sacs were produced through both sexual and asexual processes. We report the presence of sac transporters—diploid cells that facilitate sac movement. The ATR μ‐FTIR data indicated that the sacs were primarily composed of sulfated polysaccharides, closely resembling the extracellular polymeric substances produced by M. polykrikoides (Japan strain) and by Gymnodinium sp. Based on their single thin wall and absence of dormancy, these sacs are considered pellicle cysts.Keywords: dinoflagellate, extracellular polymeric substance, harmful algae, Margalefidinium polykrikoides Group III, pellicle cyst, pumpkin‐like structure, sac, sulfated polysaccharide

INTRODUCTION

Dinoflagellates produce pellicle cysts (also known as temporary or ecdysal cysts) and resting cysts as a strategy to withstand unfavorable conditions for growth (Bravo & Figueroa, 2014; Figueroa & Bravo, 2005b). Dinoflagellate cysts can have organic, calcareous, and siliceous cyst walls (Chapman et al., 1982; Fensome et al., 1993; Gottschling et al., 2012). Pellicle cysts are of ecdysal origin, possess a thin, single‐layered wall, and do not undergo dormancy. In contrast, resting cysts are thick‐walled, possess one‐ to three‐layered walls, undergo dormancy, and can remain viable for long periods of time (Bravo & Figueroa, 2014; Fensome et al., 1993). Both pellicle and resting cysts can be formed asexually, when a haploid vegetative cell encysts (Figueroa & Bravo, 2005b; Kremp & Parrow, 2006), or sexually, following gamete fusion to form a diploid planozygote that subsequently encysts. Planozygotes may also divide and return to the vegetative phase (Figueroa & Bravo, 2005b).

It has long been assumed that unfavorable conditions or stressors, such as nutrient depletion or limitation, induce dinoflagellate encystment (Anderson et al., 1984; Barlow & Triemer, 1988; Blackburn et al., 1989; Ellegaard et al., 1998; Figueroa et al., 2008, 2011; Figueroa & Bravo, 2005b; Figueroa, Bravo, & Garcés, 2006; Figueroa, Bravo, Garcés, & Ramilo, 2006; Olli & Anderson, 2002; Sgrosso et al., 2001; von Stosch, 1973; Walker & Steidinger, 1979). This is supported by observations that dinoflagellates often encyst when inoculated into nutrient‐depleted media (Anderson et al., 1984; Blackburn et al., 1989; Ellegaard et al., 1998; Figueroa & Bravo, 2005b; Figueroa, Bravo, & Garcés, 2006; Walker & Steidinger, 1979). However, in Scrippsiella cf. lachrymosa, higher resting cyst encystment success and resting cyst yields were observed when cells were allowed to gradually deplete available nutrients, rather than being abruptly transferred into nutrient‐depleted conditions (Olli & Anderson, 2002). In Levanderina fissa, encystment occurs once intracellular nutrient pools are exhausted (Anderson et al., 1985).

Cyst germination is regulated by both endogenous (mandatory dormancy) and exogenous (secondary dormancy) controls (Andersen & Keafer, 1987). Mandatory dormancy can last up to 6 months (Anderson, 1980). Temperature, light, and oxygen concentration are the most common abiotic factors associated with inducing secondary dormancy (Andersen & Keafer, 1987; Anderson, 1980; Anderson et al., 1987). Planomeiocytes (diploid cells with two longitudinal flagella) most commonly germinate from resting cysts, although haploid cells have also been observed to germinate from such cysts (Figueroa et al., 2007; Li et al., 2015, 2020). Vegetative cells and planozygotes excyst from asexual and sexual pellicle cysts, respectively (Kim, Cho, et al., 2002; Kim, Oda, et al., 2002; Parrow & Burkholder, 2004; Shin et al., 2017).

Margalefidinium polykrikoides is a harmful algal bloom (HAB) species that blooms in coastal waters adjacent to East, West, and Southeast Asia and North, South, and Central America (Pérez‐Vega et al., 2025 and references therein). It is harmful to finfish, shellfish, and copepods (Gobler et al., 2008; Griffith & Gobler, 2016; Jiang et al., 2009; Mulholland et al., 2009). Margalefidinium polykrikoides is composed of four phylogenetic groups: Groups I, II, III, and IV (Reñé et al., 2013). Margalefidinium polykrikoides Group III blooms in the lower and middle Chesapeake Bay and in the coastal Atlantic Ocean during the summer and the fall (Pérez‐Vega et al., 2025).

The Margalefidinium polykrikoides Group III NY strain has been shown to produce both pellicle and resting cysts in culture, in both nutrient‐replete media as well as in media lacking added nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P; Tang & Gobler, 2012). Crossing experiments were performed between M. polykrikoides Group I strains from Korea, which were shown to be homothallic, but these cultures did not produce resting cysts (Li et al., 2020). However, these Korean strains did produce asexual pellicle cysts in response to darkness, which germinated upon subsequent exposure to light (Shin et al., 2017).

During preliminary experiments to determine triggers for resting cyst formation in cultures of the Margalefidinium polykrikoides Group III VA strain, colorless, sac‐shaped structures were observed. Similar structures were first reported in cultures during a morphological and phylogenetic study of M. fulvescens (Hu et al., 2018). However, the authors did not investigate the triggers of sac formation. Based on their observation that cells entered and later exited the structures, Hu et al. (2018) hypothesized that the sacs might protect the cells from grazing or other stressors.

The objectives of this study were to: (1) determine the role of sacs in the life cycle of Margalefidinium polykrikoides Group III, (2) assess the effects of nutrient conditions on sac formation in culture, and (3) determine the chemical composition of the sacs to better understand their function.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Culture methods

A non‐axenic culture of Margalefidinium polykrikoides Group III VA strain was established from a cell isolated from the Lafayette River (Pérez‐Vega et al., 2025). Cells were cultured in sterile 0.1 μm Lafayette River water, amended with L1 medium nutrient and trace metal and vitamin concentrations (Guillard & Hargraves, 1993), and maintained at a salinity of 20 under the following conditions: 28°C, 140 μmol photons · m−2 · s−1, and a 14:10 h light:dark cycle (L:D).

Sac formation nutrient experiment

To determine the effect of nutrient availability on sac formation, a parent culture was used to inoculate new cultures that were acclimated to 20°C for 24 days (~seven generations) by stepwise transfer (Yamaguchi & Honjo, 1989). This temperature corresponds to that at which encystment was previously observed in cultures (Tang & Gobler, 2012). Cells in exponential growth phase were then inoculated (~1 mL) into Costar® 12‐well clear tissue culture (TC)‐treated sterile well plates (Corning Inc., Corning, New York, United States) containing ~4 mL of different nutrient medium treatments. The treatments, applied in triplicate, included the following: no added N (‐N), no added P (‐P), no added N or P (‐N ‐P), and nutrient‐replete L1 medium (Appendix S1). The initial cell concentration in each well was ~1200 cells · mL−1. In addition, six wells per well plate were filled with Milli‐Q™ Reference ultrapure water (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, Massachusetts, United States) to saturate the air space with water vapor. Plates were sealed with laboratory labelling tape to prevent evaporation from the experimental wells. Sac abundance was quantified weekly using a phase‐contrast microscope (OLYMPUS CKX41 inverted microscope) at 200× magnification, as sacs were difficult to observe under standard brightfield illumination. The experiment lasted 7 weeks.

Time series of life‐cycle stages

To generate a time series of life‐cycle stages, the parent culture was subsampled, and then new cultures prepared using media with a salinity of 25 (adjusted using filtered and sterilized Atlantic Ocean water). Cultures were acclimated for 55 days (~33 generations) by stepwise transfer (Yamaguchi & Honjo, 1989). The cultures were also treated with 1% (v/v) antibiotics for 2 days and 17 h to prevent bacterial‐induced degradation of cysts, as observed by Tang and Gobler (2012). The antibiotic solution, adapted from Andersen (2005), contained a mixture of 10 mg · mL−1 Penicillin G potassium salt, 2.5 mg · mL−1 gentamicin sulfate, and 2.5 mg · mL−1 dihydrostreptomycin sulfate. After antibiotic treatment, cells from cultures in the exponential growth phase were inoculated (~2 mL) into 21 VWR® tissue culture flasks (25 mL, non‐treated, plug seal cap, sterilized, Avantor, Radnor, Pennsylvania, United States) containing ~8 mL of sterile L1 medium with no added P (three tissue culture flasks per timepoint; total of seven timepoints). The cultures were exposed to the following conditions in triplicate: 28°C, 140 μmol photons · m−2 · s−1, 14:10 h L:D, and a salinity of 25. Live samples (2 mL) were collected every 3 days to enumerate single vegetative cells and gametes (single cells with one longitudinal flagellum), two‐cell chains, four‐cell chains, planozygotes (bell‐shaped single cells with two longitudinal flagella), sacs (colorless and transparent structures with longitudinal striations; pumpkin, onion, or a butternut squash morphology; and a circular plug on one end), and sac transporters (square‐shaped single cells with one apical horn, two antapical horns, and two longitudinal flagella) automatically and manually using a FlowCam (Table S1). The length of the single cells with one longitudinal flagellum was measured to find the gamete formation signal. In parallel, a phase‐contrast microscope (OLYMPUS CKX41 inverted microscope) at 200× or 400× magnifications together with a Palmer‐Maloney counting chamber was used to quantify Lugol‐fixed sacs and cells. Afterward, FlowCam and microscope counts were compared. The experiment lasted 21 days, after which cultures were discarded.

Time lapses

Live cells and sacs of Margalefidinium polykrikoides Group III VA strain were photographed and recorded using an inverted microscope (OLYMPUS CKX41) and a camera (OLYMPUS DP72) to create time‐lapse reconstructions of the life cycle.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB R2023a to test for significant differences in sac yield across nutrient treatments. The normality of model residuals was assessed using the Lilliefors and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. As the residuals conformed to a normal distribution, a parametric one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. Post hoc pairwise comparisons among nutrient treatments were conducted using the Tukey–Kramer test to identify statistically significant differences.

Chemical composition of Margalefidinium polykrikoides sacs

Sacs were isolated from a lugol‐fixed subsample using a Primovert inverted microscope (Carl Zeiss™) and a glass Pasteur pipette. The isolated specimens were photographed at 400× using an AxioCam MRc5 (Carl Zeiss™) and AxioVision Rel. 4.9.1 software, then deposited onto a gold‐coated mirror (Thorlabs, model: PFSQ20‐03‐M03) using a Stemi DV4 stereomicroscope (Carl Zeiss™), following the procedure described by Meyvisch et al. (2022).

The mirror was transferred to the stage of a Hyperion 2000 IR microscope (Bruker) coupled to a Vertex 80v spectrometer (Bruker). Attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared microspectroscopy (ATR μ‐FTIR) data of individual sacs were acquired using a germanium ATR crystal, a liquid nitrogen‐cooled mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector and a KBr beamsplitter. The spectral range of 4500–600 · cm−1 was recorded under atmospheric conditions at 4 · cm−1 scanning resolution, averaging 256 scans per measurement. This measurement procedure is described in more detail in Meyvisch et al. (2022).

After data collection, atmospheric spectral contributions from water vapor and carbon dioxide were removed using OPUS 8.2.21 (Bruker). Further spectral processing was done in Quasar Version 1.7 (Toplak et al., 2021) and included smoothing (Savitzky–Golay, window size = 11, polynomial order = 2), truncation (keep: 3700–700 · cm−1), and rubberband baseline correction. The processed spectra were exported from Quasar, and final figure layouts were created in Inkscape v1.3.2 (Inkscape Project, 2020).

RESULTS

Morphological characterization of Margalefidinium polykrikoides sacs

The Margalefidinium polykrikoides sac structures were colorless and transparent and showed longitudinal striations several micrometers apart (Figure 1). The general morphology of the sacs resembled that of a pumpkin, onion, or a butternut squash and displayed a circular plug on one end. The sacs could be either completely closed or open at one end. The minimum, maximum, and average sac length were 52.93, 95.55, and 70.41 ± 9.75 μm, respectively (n = 23). The minimum, maximum, and average sac width were 16.66, 64.08, and 39.54 ± 10.73 μm, respectively (n = 23).

Time‐lapses

Gamete dancing—a characteristic swimming behavior in which groups of gametes swarm around one another in a coordinated rotational motion—was observed during the period when the mean length of single cells began to decrease (Video S1). Gamete fusion was observed at t (time) = 0, 1, and 2 days (Figure S2). The planozygote was observed at t = 3 days; it decreased in size over time, did not turn into a resting cyst, and swam away (Figure S2). In other cases, the planozygote size did not decrease over time, did not turn into a resting cyst, and swam away (Figure S3). These planozygotes exhibited two longitudinal flagella and a bell‐shaped morphology (Video S2).

The appearance of gametes and planozygotes was followed by the formation of sacs. The complete sac formation process was observed only once, around a single cell with abnormal morphology (Figure 4). This single cell initially displayed a nearly rectangular shape, had a navy blue amphiesma, contained granules, and had one longitudinal flagellum (not shown). At t = 5 h, the sac began to form around the cell and subsequently expanded. As the sac expanded, the cell gradually acquired a rounded shape. At t = 34, 55, and 74 h, the cingulum was discernible, the cell began escaping the sac through the side without the plug, and the sac was empty, respectively.

DISCUSSION

Comparison of Margalefidinium polykrikoides Group III VA strain sacs with Margalefidinium fulvescens pumpkin‐like structures

Sac structures have previously been described for Margalefidinium fulvescens (Hu et al., 2018). Our observations aligned with their findings in several ways: (i) the structures exhibited a sac‐ or pumpkin‐like morphology with visible striations and openings; (ii) single cells and two‐cell chains were observed both enclosed within and exiting the sacs; and (iii) cells were seen swimming inside the sacs. Notably, although Hu et al. (2018) reported four‐cell chains inside and escaping from sacs, such chains were not observed in this study. Sexual reproduction and sac transporters were not reported by Hu et al. (2018).

Comparison of Margalefidinium polykrikoides Group III VA strain sacs with Margalefidinium polykrikoides pellicle cysts

The sacs resembled Margalefidinium. polykrikoides pellicle cysts in that they encapsulated cells, the enclosed cells sometimes contained red bodies, and both the sac wall and hyaline membrane were colorless (Kim, Cho, et al., 2002; Kim, Oda, et al., 2002, Kim et al., 2007; Li et al., 2020; Shin et al., 2017; Tang & Gobler, 2012; Figures 4, 5, and Figure S4). However, notable differences included the presence of striations, plug‐like structures, single cells inside the sacs, circular cells without flagella inside the sacs, and diploid cells (sac transporters) within the sacs. Additional distinguishing features were the absence of four‐ and eight‐cell chains inside sacs, evidence of cell lysis within sacs, the presence of empty sacs, the ability of sacs to be transported, and a remarkable capacity of expansion—features not observed in pellicle cysts (Kim, Cho, et al., 2002; Kim, Oda, et al., 2002, Kim et al., 2007; Li et al., 2020; Shin et al., 2017; Tang & Gobler, 2012; Figures 4, 5, 6, and [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link]; Videos [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link]). Cells emerged from the sacs within a time frame similar to the excystment of M. polykrikoides Group III NY strain vegetative cells from pellicle cysts (Tang & Gobler, 2012; Figures 4 and 5).

Failure to produce resting cysts

Circular cells, characterized by granules, red bodies, and a navy blue amphiesma, were rarely observed, and they were always lysed (Figures S9 and S10). These cells shared features with the resting cysts reported by Li et al. (2015, 2020): the red body, circular shape, and presence of granules. However, they lacked brown coloration, an endospore, and processes. Therefore, we do not consider these cells true resting cysts. Tang and Gobler (2012) conducted crossing experiments to determine which Margalefidinium polykrikoides strains could form resting cysts and reported that multiple strains had this capability. However, two of their strains did not self‐cross and two others did not cross. Similar experiments were done by Li et al. (2020), but resting cysts were not formed. Therefore, the failure to produce resting cysts in this study could be strain dependent. Tang and Gobler (2012) observed no significant difference in resting cyst formation between the nutrient‐replete and ‐depleted treatments. Margalefidinium polykrikoides Group I Korea strains of Li et al. (2020) growing under nutrient‐replete conditions did not produce resting cysts. In this study, M. polykrikoides Group III VA strain did not produce resting cysts under any of the nutrient treatments. This suggests that nitrogen and phosphorus availability may not play a significant role in M. polykrikoides resting‐cyst formation. It must be noted that the temperature and irradiance used in this study were similar to those used by Li et al. (2020) and Tang and Gobler (2012). Conversely, salinity, the L:D cycle, and the nutrient medium used in the encystment experiments of Li et al. (2020) and this study differed from those used by Tang and Gobler (2012).

Sac transporters

This is a report of sac transporters for dinoflagellates (Figure 6; Videos [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link]). Since these cells have two, planozygote‐like longitudinal flagella, we assumed they were diploid (Videos S2 and S7). However, planozygotes have a rounded shape (Figures S2 and S3; Video S2), whereas sac transporters are square‐shaped (Figure 6; Videos [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link]). Also, sac transporters have one apical and two antapical horns, while planozygotes do not. Furthermore, planozygotes were observed before and during sac formation, whereas sac transporters were only observed during sac formation (Figure 3). We do not know the origin of sac transporters, but it is possible that they form via gamete fusion. Another possibility is that sac transporters are planomeiocytes, although resting cysts were not observed in this study. Planomeiocytes are known to germinate from resting cysts of certain dinoflagellates (Anderson et al., 1983; Blackburn et al., 1989; Figueroa et al., 2007, 2008). Since a sac transporter was observed inside a sac (Video S10), it is possible that the sacs encapsulate them in the same way they encapsulate cells with one longitudinal flagellum (Figure 4). Sac transporters used one longitudinal flagellum to attach to sacs and then transport them (Video S8), similar to Prorocentrum cf. balticum, which uses its longitudinal flagellum to haul mucous traps (Larsson et al., 2022).

Chemical composition of Margalefidinium polykrikoides sacs

The ATR μ‐FTIR data suggest that Margalefidinium polykrikoides sacs are predominantly composed of sulfated polysaccharides (Figure 7). The sac spectrum strongly resembled FTIR spectra of colorless, chemically homogeneous extracellular polymeric substances (EPSs) produced by M. polykrikoides Japan strain (Hasui, Matsuda, Okutani, & Shigeta, 1995). These EPSs, named A1 and A2, were found to consist of a sugar mixture of mannose, galactose, glucose, and uronic acid, together with sulfate groups (S = 7%–8% w/w). A similar mixture could be present in the colorless walls of the M. polykrikoides sacs. The A1 and A2 EPSs demonstrated antiviral activities (Hasui, Matsuda, Okutani, & Shigeta, 1995) and could, through other possible biochemical effects, contribute to the ichthyotoxicity of M. polykrikoides blooms (Kim, Cho, et al., 2002; Kim, Oda, et al., 2002).

Galactose‐based sulfated EPSs appear to be common among dinoflagellates, with demonstrated instances in Amphidinium carterae (Mandal et al., 2011), Gymnodinium sp. (Hasui, Matsuda, Yoshimatsu, & Okutani, 1995), G. impudicum (Yim et al., 2007), Heterocapsa sp. (Concórdio‐Reis et al., 2023), and Lepidodinium chlorophorum (Roux et al., 2021). The sulfated polysaccharides produced by Margalefidinium polykrikoides exhibited notable compositional similarities to those found in Gymnodinium sp. EPSs, although they contained fewer sulfated esters and different glycosidic linkages (Figure 7). Nonetheless, the resemblances might indicate some degree of overlap in the biosynthetic pathways of the respective compounds. In contrast, M. polykrikoides sacs appeared clearly chemically different from the EPSs produced by Heterocapsa sp., but it should be noted that the analyzed Heterocapsa sp. EPS residue was likely contaminated with salts (Concórdio‐Reis et al., 2023; Figure 7). The large absorption band centered ~1080 · cm−1 is likely evidence for inorganic contaminants, as it could indicate the presence of phosphate ions (perhaps from phosphate salts) and/or silicate ions (Coates, 2006; Figure 7). Additional FTIR analyses of dinoflagellate EPSs are needed to more accurately determine whether athecate and thecate forms produce distinct suites of sulfated polysaccharides.

In addition to dinoflagellates, sulfated galactans are also found in red and green algae, marine angiosperms, tunicates, and sea urchins (Pomin & Mourão, 2008). Sulfated fucans (fucoidans; i.e., fucose‐based) are another group of polysaccharides abundant in marine organisms, typically in brown algae and echinoderms (Pomin & Mourão, 2008). Although incomplete due to the removal of absorption bands from the polyethylene measurement substrate, the FTIR spectrum of fucoidan from Fucus vesiculosus showed considerable similarity to that of Marglaefidinium polykrikoides sacs (Figure 7). Although this resemblance may indicate the presence of fucoidan‐like compounds in the sacs, it appears unlikely given that fucose was not detected in the EPSs of M. polykrikoides (Hasui, Matsuda, Okutani, & Shigeta, 1995).

Ecological implications

The highest production of sacs occurred under P‐depleted conditions, similar to findings for Lingulaulax polyedra (formerly Lingulodinium) and Scrippsiella acuminata, for which P depletion was the main trigger of resting‐cyst production (Figueroa & Bravo, 2005b; Rengefors et al., 1996; Figure 2). Also, sexual reproduction was triggered, and gametes were observed dancing, as reported in multiple studies focused on the sexual reproduction of dinoflagellates (Blackburn et al., 1989; Kremp & Heiskanen, 1999; Pfiester, 1977; Salgado et al., 2017; Uchida et al., 1996; von Stosch, 1973; Video S1). Gametes are smaller than vegetative cells in most dinoflagellate species (Brosnahan et al., 2015, 2017; Pfiester, 1975, 1977; Salgado et al., 2017; Uchida et al., 1996; von Stosch, 1973; Walker & Steidinger, 1979; Zmerli Triki et al., 2015). Therefore, the decrease in mean length of single Margalefidinium polykrikoides cells observed in the time series from this study was interpreted as reflecting gamete formation (Figure 3a). Gametes successfully fused to form planozygotes with a bell shape and two longitudinal flagella (Figures S2 and S3; Video S2), similar to those reported in Tang and Gobler (2012). However, the cultures eventually escaped sexual reproduction. This was evidenced by the failure of planozygotes to turn into resting cysts (Figures S2 and S3). We hypothesize the planozygotes divided as reported for M. polykrikoides Group I Korea strain (Li et al., 2020), Levanderina fissa (Uchida et al., 1996), Gymnodinium nolleri (Figueroa & Bravo, 2005a), G. catenatum (Figueroa, Bravo, Garcés, & Ramilo, 2006), Protoceratium reticulatum (Salgado et al., 2017), and Alexandrium ostenfeldii (Figueroa et al., 2008; as A. peruvianum). We consider that the planozygotes divided because we observed a decrease of planozygote size over time, but we did not observe daughter cells (Figure S2). It has been hypothesized that planozygote meiosis could promote the emergence of genetic variability (Figueroa, Bravo, Garcés, & Ramilo, 2006). Another event that suggests that cultures escaped sexual reproduction was the increase in two‐cell chain concentrations at the end of the time series (Figure 3a). Margalefidinium polykrikoides two‐cell chains have one longitudinal flagellum and are considered vegetative cells (Li et al., 2020; Shin et al., 2017; Tomas & Smayda, 2008). Single vegetative cells of M. polykrikoides Group I Korea strains divide and form two‐cell chains (Li et al., 2020). The decrease in single vegetative cells and gametes concentration could be due to cell death (Figure 3a). Some single cells, surrounded and not surrounded by sacs, lysed in this study (Figures S4–, S10; Videos S5 and S6). Gametes from Biecheleria pseudopalustris die if fusion does not occur (von Stosch, 1973; as G. pseudopalustre). Pellicle cysts are defined as cysts with a thin wall of one layer and either no dormancy or a dormancy period significantly shorter than that of resting cysts from the same species (Bravo et al., 2010). Because the sacs possess a thin wall of one layer and lack dormancy, they are classified as pellicle cysts. Sac formation can occur via both asexual and sexual processes, as they were observed surrounding haploid (Videos S4 and S9) and diploid (Video S10) cells. Forming asexual pellicle cysts is less costly in time and energy than forming sexual pellicle and resting cysts, but it has the disadvantage that it does not involve sexual recombination (Figueroa et al., 2005; Figueroa, Bravo, & Garcés, 2006).

It was hypothesized that sacs may protect Margalefidinium fulvescens against grazing (Hu et al., 2018). The copepod Acartia tonsa ingested M. polykrikoides Group III NY strain cells at a significantly lower rate than non‐harmful prey (Jiang et al., 2009). Furthermore, Ac. tonsa ingested M. polykrikoides from the Chesapeake Bay at low concentrations but experienced very high mortality when exposed to M. polykrikoides at bloom concentrations (Corson & Millette, 2024). If M. polykrikoides concentrations are low when they form sacs in the Chesapeake Bay, then it is worth doing experiments to see if the sacs protect M. polykrikoides from grazing by Ac. tonsa. In contrast, the branchiopod Artemia salina ingested M. polykrikoides Group III NY strain cells at a higher rate than non‐harmful prey and significantly reduced M. polykrikoides Group III NY strain cell concentrations in bloom water relative to the initial and control treatments (Marcoval et al., 2013). Polykrikos hartmannii ingested up to 28% of a M. polykrikoides Group I Korea strain culture in 1 day (Lee et al., 2015; as Pheopolykrikos hartmannii). The ciliate Strombidinopsis sp. also ingested M. polykrikoides Group I Korea strain cells (Jeong et al., 1999). Grazing experiments should be done with branchiopods, heterotrophic dinoflagellates, mixotrophic dinoflagellates, and ciliates present in the Chesapeake Bay to determine if they ingest M. polykrikoides cells and if they play important roles in top‐down control or whether sacs protect M. polykrikoides cells from these predators.

Some Margalefidinium polykrikoides Group III VA strain cells inside and outside the sacs were not motile, accumulated at the bottom of the well, had a circular or subcircular shape, and lysed (Figures S4–, S10). Similarly, Heterocapsa circularisquama cells infected by a virus lost motility, settled to the bottom, became roundish, and lysed (Tarutani et al., 2001). Experiments should be done with M. polykrikoides Group III VA strain cells and Chesapeake Bay viruses to determine if viral infections are responsible for the formation and lysis of circular or subcircular shape non‐swimming cells. Since all the circular or subcircular shape non‐swimming cells lysed (Figures S4–, S10), we think they are not important for the completion of the life cycle. The ploidy of the circular or subcircular shape non‐swimming cells remains unknown due to the absence of flagella.

Cells inside the sacs cannot move the sacs while they are swimming inside the sacs (Videos S3, S4, [Link], [Link], [Link]). Therefore, they need the help of the sac transporters to move the sacs (Video S8). Margalefidinium polykrikoides cells vertically migrate in culture and in the field (Clayton et al., 2024; Lim et al., 2022). Sac transporters could help M. polykrikoides cells that are inside sacs migrate to a different layer of the water column. M. polykrikoides Group I Korea strain vertical migration is a strategy to avoid unfavorable water temperatures (Lim et al., 2022).

CONCLUSIONS

This study offers a report of sac‐like, thin‐walled pellicle cysts lacking dormancy in cultures of Margalefidinium polykrikoides Group III VA strain. These sac‐like pellicle cysts are morphologically different from the pellicle cysts produced by M. polykrikoides Group III NY strain (Figure 8) and are similar to the pumpkin‐like structures produced by M. fulvescens. Sac‐like pellicle cysts could form via either sexual or asexual processes (Figure 8). Phosphate depletion was identified as the main trigger for sac‐like pellicle cyst formation. Although gamete fusion and planozygote formation mirrored those processes of the M. polykrikoides Group III NY strain, the resulting planozygotes did not develop into resting cysts (Figure 8). We reported a diploid life‐cycle stage with novel morphology and behavior, termed the sac transporter (Figure 8). Sac transporters attach to sacs and drag them. The origin of the sac transporter is unknown but likely involves gamete fusion. If future studies confirm the formation of sac transporters via gamete fusion, they could be classified as planozygotes with morphology and behavior distinct from typical planozygotes. The ATR μ‐FTIR data of M. polykrikoides Group III VA strain sac‐like pellicle cysts indicate that they are primarily composed of sulfated polysaccharides, showing strong spectral similarity to the EPSs produced by the M. polykrikoides Japan strain and Gymnodinium sp.
