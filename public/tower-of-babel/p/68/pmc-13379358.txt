Guidewire entrapment in pulmonary veins during pulsed field ablation of atrial fibrillation: A case series
Authors: Mitsuhiko Shoda, Mitsuru Takami, Kimitake Imamura, Koji Fukuzawa
Published: 2026

Key Teaching Points

•Guidewire entrapment in the pulmonary veins is a rare but potentially serious complication during pulsed field ablation, particularly when the guidewire is advanced deeply into the inferior pulmonary veins. Therefore, to reduce this risk, excessive distal advancement and manipulation should be avoided.

Introduction

Pulsed field ablation (PFA) of atrial fibrillation (AF) is distinct from conventional thermal ablation in that it uses tissue-selective energy, thereby minimizing injury to surrounding extracardiac structures and resulting in fewer complications.1

Within the first year after the introduction of PFA at our institution, we performed 229 procedures and encountered 3 cases of guidewire entrapment in the pulmonary vein (PV) during the PFA procedure. In all cases, the entrapment occurred in the inferior PVs and was resolved without adverse sequelae by traction while maintaining coaxial alignment.

Guidewire entrapment in the PV may represent a previously unrecognized complication of PFA. Awareness of this phenomenon is important for operators performing PFA procedures.

Case 1

An 89-year-old woman presented with palpitations and was diagnosed as having paroxysmal AF at a previous hospital. Antiarrhythmic therapy with pilsicainide was administered; however, AF recurred, and the patient was referred to our institution for further treatment. She had a medical history of hypertension and dyslipidemia, without any history of cardiac interventions or structural heart disease.

Catheter ablation was performed using the FARAPULSE PFA system (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA). The FARAWAVE catheter was advanced through the FARADRIVE steerable sheath to deliver pulsed field energy. Computed tomography (CT) imaging before the procedure indicated left atrial enlargement and normal PV anatomy (Figure 1A and 1B). Deep sedation with propofol and noninvasive positive airway pressure was used during the PFA procedure, without administration of muscle relaxants.

For transseptal puncture, preprocedural CT images were reviewed to assess the branching direction and vertical height of each PV, and intracardiac echocardiography was used to guide the transseptal puncture during the procedure. During PFA applications, when advancing the guidewire into each PV, the direction of the target PV was confirmed using both intracardiac echocardiography and fluoroscopy.

After PFA applications were delivered to the right superior PV after ablation of the left PVs, the catheter was advanced into the right inferior PV over a 0.035-in J-tipped guidewire (Boston Scientific). After PFA of the right inferior PV, with 4 applications delivered in the basket configuration and 6 in the flower configuration, withdrawal of the guidewire from the PV was attempted but resistance was encountered, suggesting entrapment within the right inferior PV.

Initially, we attempted to pull back the guidewire with the catheter deployed in the flower configuration; however, the tip of the guidewire remained caught within the PV and could not be removed (Figure 1C and Supplemental Video 1). Subsequently, the catheter was folded back and retracted into the sheath, and the sheath was pulled downward. In this position, gentle traction on the guidewire allowed the guidewire to become free and be successfully withdrawn (Figure 1D and 1E and Supplemental Video 2).

Upon external inspection of the catheter system, a small fraction of tissue was found attached to the tip of the guidewire. The structures of both the catheter and the guidewire were intact, and insertion and withdrawal were feasible. Hemodynamic stability was maintained thereafter, with no signs of pericardial effusion. The patient was monitored during a 1-week hospitalization and was discharged home without any notable complications.

Case 2

A 60-year-old man with paroxysmal AF presenting with palpitations underwent PFA using the PulseSelect system (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN). The PulseSelect PFA loop catheter was advanced through the FlexCath Contour steerable sheath to deliver pulsed field energy. Preprocedural noncontrast CT revealed no anatomic abnormalities of the left atrium or PVs. Deep sedation with propofol and noninvasive positive airway pressure was used during the PFA procedure, without administration of muscle relaxants.

After PFA applications to the left superior PV, the 0.032-in J-tipped spring guidewire (Goodman Corporation, Nagoya, Japan) was advanced into the left inferior PV. After PFA applications to the left inferior PV, with 4 applications delivered within the PV and 7 at the PV antrum, the guidewire was found to be caught within the PV. Attempts to withdraw the catheter and guidewire were unsuccessful because the tip of the guidewire remained engaged and could not be removed (Figure 2A). To release the entrapment of the guidewire tip, the catheter was advanced together with the guidewire; however, the guidewire tip remained stuck, causing the catheter to bend (Figure 2B and Supplemental Video 3). The catheter was then retracted into the sheath, and gentle traction on the guidewire and the sheath together allowed the guidewire to disengage and be successfully withdrawn (Figure 2C and Supplemental Video 4).

This patient fortunately showed no evidence of pericardial effusion and was discharged without any complications.

Case 3

PFA using the PulseSelect system was performed for symptomatic paroxysmal AF in a 66-year-old man with a history of hypertension and chronic kidney disease under medical treatment. Preprocedural noncontrast CT revealed left atrial enlargement but no anatomic abnormalities of the PVs. Deep sedation with propofol and noninvasive positive airway pressure was used during the PFA procedure, without administration of muscle relaxants.

The ablation procedure was performed using the PulseSelect PFA loop catheter delivered through the FlexCath Contour steerable sheath. PFA applications were delivered to the left PVs and the right superior PV, achieving successful PV isolation. Subsequently, PFA was performed for the right inferior PV, with 5 applications delivered within the PV and 7 at the PV antrum. However, after completion of the application, resistance was encountered when attempting to withdraw the 0.032-in J-tipped spring guidewire (Goodman Corporation) from the right inferior PV, suggesting guidewire entrapment within the PV. The straightened J tip indicated that the tip of the wire was caught within the vein (Figure 3A and Supplemental Video 5).

B: The PulseSelect catheter was advanced toward the pulmonary vein along the guidewire. The red arrows indicate ventral sheath manipulation to release the entrapped catheter and guidewire.

To increase support and coaxial alignment, the PulseSelect catheter was advanced toward the vein (Figure 3B). As a result, even when the sheath was maneuvered ventrally within the left atrium in an attempt to disengage the catheter from the PV ostium, the catheter could not be detached, suggesting that the catheter itself had become fixed to the PV (Figure 3C and Supplemental Video 6). Therefore, the sheath was advanced as far distally as possible along the catheter, and sustained traction on the entire system allowed successful withdrawal (Figure 3D and Supplemental Video 7). Upon external inspection of the catheter system, some tissue was found to be trapped in the narrow gap between the catheter’s exit port and the guidewire (Figure 4).

Hemodynamic stability was maintained throughout the procedure, and no pericardial effusion or other complications were observed. The patient was discharged home in stable condition without adverse events.

Discussion

We encountered 3 cases of guidewire entrapment within the PVs during PFA of AF. At our institution, 229 PFA procedures were performed during the first year after its introduction, corresponding to an incidence of approximately 1.3% (3 of 229 procedures).

To date, only 2 reports have described guidewire or catheter entrapment in the PVs during PFA. 1 report described a case in which the guidewire may have dissected a small branch of the PV, causing the tip of the wire to catch and become entrapped in the PV.2 Another report described a case in which tissue fragments became trapped between the guidewire and the catheter tip, resulting in catheter immobility at the PV ostium.3

In our first case, the guidewire became entrapped in the distal portion of the right inferior PV. Attempts to withdraw the guidewire with the catheter deployed in the flower configuration were unsuccessful. After retracting the catheter into the sheath, gentle caudal traction with coaxial alignment between the sheath and the guidewire enabled successful withdrawal.

In the second case, the guidewire became trapped within the left inferior PV. Similar to the first case, successful withdrawal of the guidewire was achieved by retracting the catheter into the sheath and applying gentle traction while maintaining coaxial alignment between the guidewire and the sheath.

In the third case, the guidewire was initially trapped within the right inferior PV. Subsequently, advancement of the catheter resulted in soft tissue caught in the narrow gap between the catheter exit port and the guidewire, causing the catheter itself to become immobile. This tissue obstruction is considered to be caused by poor coaxial alignment between the guidewire and the PFA catheter.3 In this situation, careful advancement of the sheath along the guidewire and the catheter maintaining coaxial alignment, followed by gentle traction on the entire system, allowed successful retrieval without complications.

In all cases, there were no notable difficulties, resistance, or procedural complications during guidewire advancement into the PVs. However, in each case, the guidewire had been advanced relatively distally within the PV. We speculate that individual anatomic factors, including the branching location, take-off angle, and vessel diameter of the PVs in each patient, may have contributed to distal guidewire entrapment within the PV. Therefore, it may be important to confirm with fluoroscopy that the guidewire does not advance excessively distally or inadvertently enter small peripheral branches.

Although the precise mechanism underlying guidewire entrapment within the PVs remains unclear, it has been reported that deformation of the J-shaped tip under external force may create a structural vulnerability, predisposing the guidewire to entrapment. In addition, traction applied to an entrapped guidewire may alter the tight coupling between adjacent spiral coils, thereby increasing tissue clamping and further complicating guidewire retrieval.4 Therefore, guidewire manipulation in the distal portions of the PVs during PFA might be associated with an increased risk of guidewire entrapment. The inferior PVs have smaller diameters than the superior PVs, and the right inferior PV, in particular, has been reported to exhibit early branching near its ostium.5 Therefore, excessive manipulation of the guidewire in the distal portions of the PVs should be avoided, especially in the inferior PVs. Indeed, in our 3 cases and in previously reported cases, guidewire entrapment most commonly occurred in the inferior PVs, particularly in the right inferior PV.

Careful preprocedural evaluation of the PVs on CT imaging may be helpful to understand their branching angles and distal branching patterns. A thorough understanding of this anatomic configuration may help facilitate preferential advancement of the guidewire into the main venous trunk rather than unintended advancement into small distal branches. Furthermore, superimposing the preprocedural CT images during ablation may enhance anatomic recognition of the PVs and could represent a potential strategy to reduce the risk of guidewire entrapment.

In addition, when advancing the catheter in the setting of guidewire entrapment, inadequate coaxial alignment between the guidewire and the PFA catheter may allow tissue to enter the catheter tip and cause obstruction. Thus, maintaining proper coaxial alignment is essential during catheter manipulation when a guidewire becomes entrapped within the PV.

In all cases, the guidewire was safely retrieved without major complications. When guidewire entrapment occurs, maintaining proper coaxial alignment between the guidewire, catheter, and sheath is important to optimize the direction of traction applied to the guidewire (Figure 5). If withdrawal of the guidewire alone is unsuccessful, it is necessary to advance the catheter system to increase support and then gently pull down while maintaining proper coaxial alignment. This technique enables safe retrieval of entrapped guidewires and seems to minimize the risk of serious complications such as cardiac tamponade.

Conclusion

Operators should be aware that guidewire entrapment within the PVs can occur as a potential complication of PFA procedures. Careful manipulation of the guidewire in the inferior PVs is important, and appropriate release techniques should be well understood in case guidewire entrapment occurs.
