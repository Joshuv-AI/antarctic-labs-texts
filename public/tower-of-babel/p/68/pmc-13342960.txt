Mechanisms and advances of drug resistance in colorectal cancer: A systematic overview of multi-layered regulatory networks

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•CRC drug resistance arises from dynamic, multi-layered tumor–microenvironment interactions.•Tumor-intrinsic plasticity and cell death evasion drive resistance to chemo-, targeted, and immunotherapy.•Metabolic, epigenetic, and RNA-based reprogramming sustain drug-tolerant and stem-like states.•Tumor microenvironment and microbiota create protective niches that limit therapeutic efficacy.•Systems biology and evolutionary strategies inform rational combination and sequencing of therapies.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, imposing a substantial and steadily increasing global public health burden [1]. Over the past decade, advances in molecular stratification and precision oncology have profoundly reshaped the therapeutic landscape of CRC, shifting treatment paradigms from predominantly surgery- and chemotherapy-based approaches toward integrated multimodal strategies [2]. Contemporary management combines surgical resection with cytotoxic chemotherapy regimens such as FOLFOX or FOLFIRI, molecularly targeted therapies directed against the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) or vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and immune checkpoint inhibitors, which primarily benefit patients with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) or mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) tumors [3]. Paradoxically, however, these therapeutic advances have intensified selective pressures on tumor ecosystems, accelerating the emergence of drug resistance and severely limiting durable clinical benefit [4]. Indeed, resistance remains the principal determinant of treatment failure and disease progression in CRC.

Historically, investigations of drug resistance have relied largely on reductionist frameworks, focusing on discrete molecular alterations within tumor cells, including activating mutations in oncogenic drivers such as KRAS, NRAS, or BRAF, upregulation of drug targets such as thymidylate synthase (TS), or enhanced activity of drug efflux transporters including ABCB5 and MRP2 [5]. While these studies have identified critical resistance-associated nodes, they fall short of explaining the extensive heterogeneity, phenotypic plasticity, and dynamic clinical evolution observed in patients [4]. Even when specific oncogenic pathways are effectively inhibited, tumors frequently escape through compensatory bypass signaling, transitions between cellular states, and broader biological reprogramming [6]. These observations underscore that drug resistance in CRC cannot be adequately understood as the consequence of isolated molecular defects, but instead reflects a system-level adaptive process emerging from coordinated biological reorganization under sustained therapeutic stress [7].

At the level of tumor cell intrinsic regulation, this adaptive process is manifested through interconnected and mutually reinforcing programs, including dynamic rewiring and redundancy of oncogenic signaling networks, increased genomic instability accompanied by enhanced DNA damage response capacity, metabolic reprogramming that sustains survival under pharmacological stress, and epigenetically driven cellular plasticity that enables the emergence of drug-tolerant persister cells [5]. Importantly, resistance does not arise solely within malignant cells, but is deeply embedded within the tumor microenvironment, a complex and evolving ecosystem composed of cancer-associated fibroblasts, diverse immune cell populations, endothelial cells, extracellular matrix (ECM) components, and the gut microbiota [8]. Through bidirectional signaling, metabolic competition and cooperation, immune editing, and physical barrier formation, the tumor microenvironment (TME) establishes adaptive niches that actively promote tumor survival and therapeutic resistance [9].

Tumor cell intrinsic adaptive programs and microenvironmental factors are tightly coupled through continuous and dynamic interactions that collectively shape resistance evolution. Metabolic reprogramming within tumor cells can suppress antitumor immune responses, whereas inflammatory and stromal-derived signals can reinforce tumor stemness and epigenetic plasticity, further amplifying resistance [10]. Recent advances in single-cell multi-omics profiling, spatial transcriptomics, and liquid biopsy technologies have enabled high-resolution and longitudinal interrogation of these processes, revealing drug resistance as a continuous evolutionary trajectory driven by therapeutic selection pressures [11]. In this context, intertumoral and intratumoral heterogeneity is increasingly recognized not as a confounding variable, but as a defining feature of resistance that necessitates integrative, system-level analytical frameworks [12].

Because drug resistance arises through dynamic adaptation rather than fixed molecular lesions, effective strategies to overcome therapeutic failure in CRC require a conceptual shift from pathway-centered intervention toward system-level therapeutic design [13]. Viewing resistance as an adaptive and evolutionary process highlights the importance of identifying shared adaptive bottlenecks, disrupting feedback loops between tumors and their microenvironment, and exploiting temporal vulnerabilities through rational combination, sequencing, and adaptive treatment strategies. In this Review, we integrate current knowledge of CRC drug resistance within a unified, multilayered systems framework. Therapeutic landscape of colorectal cancer and resistance-driven clinical failure

Chemotherapy resistance

Chemotherapy remains a cornerstone of CRC treatment across both early and advanced disease stages; however, resistance to cytotoxic agents emerges in the majority of patients and represents a major limitation to durable disease control [14]. Standard regimens based on fluoropyrimidines, platinum compounds such as oxaliplatin, and topoisomerase I inhibitors, including irinotecan, frequently induce initial tumor regression or disease stabilization, yet sustained responses are uncommon [15]. In the metastatic setting, first-line chemotherapy typically achieves progression-free survival measured in months, whereas subsequent lines administered after resistance onset are associated with progressively shorter disease control intervals and diminishing clinical benefit [16].

Clinically, resistance to first-line fluoropyrimidine and oxaliplatin based chemotherapy is associated with a relatively gradual but irreversible decline in disease control, resulting in a stepwise shortening of progression-free survival across subsequent treatment lines [17]. Retrospective analyses and real-world cohorts consistently indicate that early disease progression, often operationally defined as progression occurring within the first 3 to 6 months of first-line chemotherapy or a markedly short progression-free survival, identifies a subgroup of patients with inferior long-term outcomes and limited benefit from subsequent treatment lines [18]. Once resistance develops, overall survival becomes increasingly constrained, particularly in patients who exhibit early resistance or limited responsiveness to initial therapy [19].

Beyond pharmacologic insensitivity, chemotherapy resistance is frequently accompanied by malignant phenotypic evolution. Resistant tumors often display enhanced invasive behavior, increased metastatic dissemination, and enrichment of tumor cell populations with stem-like or drug-tolerant characteristics [20]. These changes further reduce responsiveness to salvage therapies and contribute to accelerated disease progression, underscoring that chemotherapy resistance functions not only as a marker of treatment failure but also as an active driver of adverse clinical outcomes.

Resistance to targeted therapy

Molecularly targeted therapies have expanded treatment options for biologically selected subsets of CRC, yet resistance substantially constrains their impact on long term survival. Anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies such as cetuximab and panitumumab provide clinical benefit primarily in tumors lacking activating mutations in KRAS, NRAS, or BRAF [21]. Even in these selected populations, intrinsic resistance is frequently observed, and acquired resistance almost invariably develops during treatment. Longitudinal clinical monitoring, including circulating tumor DNA analyses, has demonstrated that resistance often emerges within months of therapy initiation and is associated with rapid disease progression [22].

The development of acquired resistance to anti-EGFR therapy marks a critical survival inflection point characterized by abrupt contraction of the therapeutic window. After progression on EGFR blockade, disease control achieved with subsequent systemic therapies is often short-lived, and overall survival benefit is substantially attenuated [23]. This survival impact is particularly pronounced in patients with aggressive molecular features or limited alternative treatment options. Similarly, anti-VEGF-based therapies confer modest survival gains but rarely induce durable tumor control. Once resistance to angiogenesis inhibition develops, patients typically derive limited benefit from further treatment lines, reflecting both biological adaptation and cumulative treatment-related toxicity [24].

Collectively, resistance to targeted therapy represents a decisive transition in disease course, during which therapeutic flexibility narrows, and survival trajectories become increasingly constrained.

Resistance to immunotherapy

Immune checkpoint inhibitors have transformed the treatment landscape for colorectal cancers characterized by microsatellite instability high or dMMR, enabling durable responses and prolonged survival in a subset of patients. Nevertheless, resistance remains a significant clinical challenge. A considerable proportion of eligible patients exhibit primary resistance, deriving minimal progression-free survival benefit despite favorable molecular features [25]. In addition, acquired resistance has been documented in patients who initially respond, leading to disease progression under continued immune checkpoint blockade [26].

In this setting, resistance to immunotherapy constitutes a particularly abrupt survival inflection. While initial responses may be long-lasting, the onset of acquired resistance is frequently followed by rapid disease progression and limited post progression survival. Importantly, effective salvage options after immunotherapy failure remain scarce, not only because of limited therapeutic alternatives but also partly due to the establishment of an immune-excluded or immune desert the TME [27]. Such microenvironmental states constrain the efficacy of subsequent immunotherapies and may also limit responsiveness to other treatment modalities, thereby compounding the adverse impact of resistance on long term survival.

These observations indicate that immunotherapy resistance reflects not only immune escape but also broader adaptive changes within tumor cells and the TME that directly shape survival outcomes.

Clinical consequences of resistance-driven evolution

From a clinical perspective, the cumulative emergence of drug resistance imposes a profound burden on patients and healthcare systems. Resistance is a principal determinant of shortened progression-free and overall survival and necessitates repeated transitions across multiple lines of therapy. With each successive resistance event, disease control intervals progressively shorten, while treatment-related toxicities accumulate and further limit therapeutic options [28]. Common adverse effects, including neurotoxicity, myelosuppression, and gastrointestinal toxicity, significantly impair quality of life and reduce treatment tolerability.

Importantly, resistance-driven clonal evolution alters tumor biology in ways that extend beyond drug insensitivity alone. Molecular profiles obtained from diagnostic biopsies frequently fail to reflect the dominant resistant clones present at later disease stages, complicating treatment selection and contributing to suboptimal outcomes [29]. Across therapeutic modalities, successive resistance events impose increasingly stringent biological and clinical constraints on disease control, resulting in cumulative compression of survival trajectories despite continued treatment.

Together, these observations indicate that drug resistance in CRC operates as a cumulative and directional process rather than as isolated treatment failures. This stepwise erosion of survival provides the clinical foundation for mechanistic analyses of tumor cell intrinsic adaptation, TME co-evolution, and temporal dynamics under therapeutic pressure, which are examined in the following sections.

Tumor cell-intrinsic adaptive programs driving drug resistance

Genetic alterations and oncogenic signaling rewiring

Intrinsic drug resistance in CRC does not arise from isolated genetic lesions or linear activation of single oncogenic pathways. Instead, it emerges as an adaptive phenotype rooted in a genetically conditioned capacity for dynamic rewiring of intracellular signaling networks under sustained therapeutic pressure. Oncogenic driver mutations, loss of tumor suppressors, and genomic instability not only initiate tumorigenesis but also establish a permissive molecular landscape that enables flexible reconfiguration of signaling dependencies, feedback circuits, and compensatory pathways (Table 1). Tumor cell-intrinsic mechanisms of drug resistance in colorectal cancer.

A first layer of this genetically conditioned adaptation involves reinforcement and stabilization of pre-existing pro-survival signaling programs rather than wholesale pathway replacement. Under chemotherapeutic stress, CRC cells frequently consolidate oncogenic outputs through feedback-controlled signaling loops. For example, ARL4C promotes resistance to oxaliplatin by sustaining PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling, thereby reinforcing epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), invasive behavior, and metabolic adaptability. This adaptive state is further stabilized by a positive feedback loop involving the deubiquitinase USP38, which preserves ARL4C protein abundance following chemotherapy exposure and effectively locks tumor cells into a pro-survival signaling dependency [30]. Such feedback-mediated stabilization exemplifies an early adaptive mode in which oncogenic signaling is buffered against pharmacological perturbation rather than bypassed by alternative pathways.

When reinforcement of canonical signaling alone is insufficient to ensure survival, CRC cells engage a second adaptive layer characterized by reprogramming of transcriptional and translational homeostasis, leading to altered cellular states. Beyond classical signaling cascades, genetic alterations can reshape intrinsic chemoresistance through modulation of RNA processing and structural dependencies. Downregulation of the ribosomal protein uL3 induces a non-mutational drug-tolerant state marked by increased reliance on G-quadruplex (G4) RNA secondary structures. In p53-deficient CRC cells, this translational vulnerability renders tumors selectively sensitive to G4-stabilizing ligands such as pyridostatin and RHPS4, particularly when combined with 5-fluorouracil [31]. These findings illustrate how genetically primed disruptions in translational control enable state transitions that support survival under therapy while simultaneously creating context-specific synthetic lethal opportunities.

As resistance evolves, adaptive rewiring can progress toward system-level stabilization through coordinated crosstalk among multiple oncogenic and stress-response pathways. In genetically defined CRC subtypes, such as KRAS-mutant tumors, concurrent TP53 loss and elevated WNT pathway activity cooperate to drive constitutive activation of canonical NF-κB signaling via Toll-like receptor–dependent mechanisms [32]. This transcriptional reprogramming enhances detoxification pathways, including glucuronidation, thereby diminishing sensitivity to Ras pathway inhibitors. Importantly, this resistance phenotype does not stem solely from the KRAS mutation itself but arises from functional coupling between multiple signaling axes, rendering it at least partially reversible. Consistent with this notion, combined inhibition of WNT and MEK signaling effectively restores drug sensitivity, underscoring the therapeutic relevance of disrupting compensatory network interactions that stabilize resistant states.

Once this adaptive capacity is established, therapeutic pressure further channels resistance evolution into treatment-specific dependency states shaped by the molecular targets and stress profiles of individual therapies. Resistance to 5-fluorouracil/leucovorin (5-FU/LV) is driven by treatment-induced upregulation of thymidylate synthase (TS), accompanied by increased expression of MYC and its downstream drug efflux transporter ABCB5 [33]. Notably, the TS inhibitor CF10 retains potent activity in 5-FU/LV-resistant settings by simultaneously suppressing MYC-ABCB5 signaling while maintaining effective TS inhibition, thereby exhibiting minimal cross-resistance. Similarly, irinotecan-resistant CRC cells acquire enhanced stemness and survival capacity through transcriptional addiction to the CDK7-MDK signaling axis, and genetic or pharmacological disruption of this dependency restores drug sensitivity and suppresses tumor growth [34].

In the context of targeted therapies, genetically conditioned signaling persistence and bypass activation represent central resistance mechanisms. Resistance to EGFR-directed antibodies frequently arises from failure to induce effective EGFR downregulation, allowing sustained EGFR/HER2 signaling even in tumors harboring KRAS, BRAF, or PIK3CA mutations [35]. Enforced degradation of EGFR and HER2 using the engineered enzyme PEPDG278D collapses this dependency and restores therapeutic efficacy. Additional adaptive mechanisms further reinforce EGFR pathway dependence, including loss of circSETD3, which activates an ErbB3–AKT positive feedback loop via the miR-4667–5p-RASA4 axis, thereby driving cetuximab resistance [36]. Likewise, downregulation of NDRG1 promotes STAT1 accumulation through impaired Smurf1-mediated ubiquitination, resulting in enhanced proliferation, EMT, and resistance to EGFR blockade [37]. Disruption of SNX3–retromer–mediated trafficking by honokiol further demonstrates the reversibility of such adaptive states by overcoming intrinsic EGFR-targeted therapy resistance in KRAS^G13D-mutant CRC through inhibition of autophagy, macropinocytosis, and RAS-GTP activation [38].

Resistance to BRAF inhibition in BRAF^V600E CRC exemplifies the complexity and robustness of adaptive signaling rewiring under targeted pressure. Multiple bypass mechanisms converge to sustain MAPK output and stabilize MYC-driven survival programs, including activation of SRC family kinases [36], sustained MKK3-p38 MAPK signaling that promotes c-MYC stability [39], and upregulation of MUC1-C. MUC1-C maintains proliferative and stemness programs through MYC and NOTCH1 while facilitating SHP2-mediated feedback reactivation of RTK-RAS-ERK signaling, thereby enabling escape from BRAF inhibition [40]. Together, these mechanisms form a highly resilient adaptive network that limits the durability of single-agent BRAF-targeted therapies.

Collectively, these observations demonstrate that genetic alterations in CRC do not merely activate discrete oncogenic pathways but instead condition tumors for hierarchical, multi-layered adaptive rewiring of signaling, transcriptional, and translational networks. Through sequential reinforcement of oncogenic programs, cell-state transitions, and system-level pathway coupling under therapy-specific selective pressure, CRC cells acquire intrinsic drug tolerance while retaining exploitable vulnerabilities. Systematic dissection of these genetically conditioned adaptive processes provides a conceptual foundation for rational combination strategies aimed at collapsing adaptive bottlenecks and preventing stabilization of drug-resistant phenotypes.

DNA damage response and genome maintenance

Beyond oncogenic signaling rewiring, intrinsic drug resistance in CRC is critically shaped by adaptive reprogramming of DNA damage response (DDR) pathways and genome maintenance mechanisms. Cytotoxic chemotherapy and radiotherapy impose substantial genotoxic stress, including replication fork collapse, DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), and oxidative DNA lesions. Rather than succumbing to this damage, CRC cells frequently acquire resistance by enhancing DNA repair capacity, tolerating replication stress, or dynamically redistributing repair pathway usage between homologous recombination (HR) and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) [[41], [42], [43]]. Importantly, these adaptations are often non-mutational and reversible, reflecting functional plasticity rather than fixed genetic defects.

A growing body of evidence indicates that altered HR competency represents a central axis of chemoresistance in CRC. In metastatic CRC resistant to FOLFIRI, tumors exhibit coordinated upregulation of innate immune signaling and mitochondrial metabolism alongside reduced expression of POLD1, whereas chemosensitive tumors display a functional, non-genetic “BRCAness” phenotype driven by low RAD51 abundance. Restoration of RAD51 attenuates HR-deficiency-associated genomic scars and reduces chemotoxicity, while paradoxically, pharmacological suppression of HR through ATM inhibition enhances FOLFIRI sensitivity, underscoring the therapeutic relevance of transient and context-dependent modulation of HR activity [44]. These findings collectively establish that HR proficiency in CRC is not a binary state but exists along a continuum that can be dynamically reshaped under therapeutic pressure.

Post-translational and epigenetic regulation further fine-tunes HR activity and stabilizes resistant phenotypes under sustained genotoxic stress. Hyper-O-GlcNAcylation driven by O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT) promotes intrinsic resistance to 5-fluorouracil by inducing ubiquitin-mediated degradation of Erbin, thereby relieving suppression of HR and enabling efficient DNA repair during ongoing chemotherapy exposure [45]. Similarly, NSUN5 overexpression enhances chemoresistance by upregulating and physically interacting with BRCA2 and BRIP1, reinforcing HR-mediated repair and protecting CRC cells from doxorubicin-induced cytotoxicity [46]. At the chromatin level, hepatoma-derived growth factor (HDGF) promotes drug resistance by recruiting CtIP to facilitate DNA end resection and HR, while acting as a putative H3K36me3 reader to preserve genomic integrity within transcriptionally active regions [47]. Together, these mechanisms illustrate how CRC cells consolidate HR proficiency through layered regulatory control rather than through permanent genetic alterations.

In parallel, activation of the NHEJ pathway provides an alternative and complementary route to genome maintenance, particularly in therapy-resistant contexts. Radiotherapy-resistant CRC cells exhibit elevated expression of CNOT7, which enhances NHEJ-driven DSB repair by stabilizing XRCC6 (Ku70) through suppression of TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination. Disruption of the CNOT7-XRCC6 axis markedly compromises DSB repair and restores radiosensitivity in preclinical models [48]. Chemotherapy-induced upregulation of USP10 similarly promotes resistance by preventing degradation of the Ku70/80 complex, thereby facilitating efficient NHEJ while simultaneously suppressing cell-death programs such as PANoptosis [49]. In addition, SUMOylation-dependent regulation of DDR components further modulates NHEJ efficiency: SUMOylation-induced liquid–liquid phase separation of RNF168 limits its recruitment to DNA damage sites, suppressing H2A ubiquitination and 53BP1 loading, whereas overexpression of the deSUMOylase SENP1 reverses this constraint, enhances DSB repair capacity, and promotes chemoresistance [50].

Adaptive tolerance to replication stress represents another critical dimension of genome maintenance–driven resistance. In KRAS-mutant CRC, stress-induced activation of KRAS signaling stabilizes nuclear CDC7 by suppressing HRD1-mediated ubiquitination, thereby licensing replication origin firing and enabling tolerance to replication stress induced by both 5-fluorouracil and oxaliplatin. Therapeutic targeting of CDC7 effectively re-sensitizes resistant tumors in vivo, highlighting replication stress adaptation as an actionable vulnerability [51]. Conversely, loss of ARID1A impairs DDR signaling through suppression of Chk2 phosphorylation, exacerbating floxuridine (FUDR)-induced DNA damage and apoptosis. Notably, ARID1A-deficient CRC cells exhibit preferential sensitivity to FUDR relative to 5-fluorouracil, suggesting that specific chemotherapeutic agents may be selectively effective in defined DDR-compromised genetic contexts [52].

Recent studies further reveal that DDR adaptation is dynamically regulated through RNA modification, alternative splicing, and post-translational modification pathways. Activation of METTL16 by SSB, coupled with reciprocal methylation of SSB by METTL16, establishes a positive feedback loop that elevates global m^6A levels and promotes chemoresistance in CRC [53]. SUMOylation of RAD51 at lysine 57 enhances Sp1 transcriptional activity, upregulating GOLPH3 while suppressing NDRG1 expression, thereby driving cisplatin resistance; disruption of RAD51 SUMOylation abrogates this resistance program [54]. Consistently, elevated expression of the SUMO E2 ligase UBC9 correlates with poor prognosis in chemotherapy-treated CRC patients and functionally promotes proliferation, invasion, and oxaliplatin resistance through coordinated regulation of MYC signaling, DNA repair capacity, and oxidative stress responses [55].

Importantly, DDR adaptation does not operate in isolation but intersects with mitochondrial and metabolic stress responses to shape resistance trajectories. Single-cell transcriptomic profiling combined with dynamic network biomarker analysis identifies early inhibition of mitochondrial complex I as a critical initiating event in acquired oxaliplatin resistance. MDM2-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of the complex I subunit NDUFB8 reduces reactive oxygen species (ROS) production under chemotherapeutic stress, thereby promoting survival, whereas extracellular vesicle–mediated delivery of siMDM2 restores oxaliplatin sensitivity by reactivating the MDM2-NDUFB8-ROS axis [56]. Complementarily, WFDC3 suppresses ATM/ATR signaling to maintain chemosensitivity, and its loss confers oxaliplatin resistance that can be reversed by pharmacological inhibition of ATM or ATR [57].

Additional adaptive layers further integrate genome maintenance with RNA processing and cellular stress responses. FAM135B promotes oxaliplatin resistance by facilitating SRSF1 nuclear translocation and SRSF1-mediated alternative splicing of DNA repair genes; specifically, FAM135B-driven exon IV inclusion of FAAP20 enhances Fanconi anemia core complex integrity and interstrand crosslink repair capacity [58]. Moreover, DNA-damaging chemotherapy induces Golgi dispersal, particularly in CRC stem cells, activating a PKCα-GSK3α-TFE3-mediated Golgi stress response that enhances protein trafficking and ABCG2-dependent drug efflux, thereby reinforcing resistance to genotoxic agents [59].

Collectively, these findings establish DDR and genome maintenance not as static resistance modules but as dynamically regulated adaptive systems that enable CRC cells to survive sustained genotoxic stress. Through coordinated modulation of HR and NHEJ balance, replication stress tolerance, RNA processing, post-translational modification, and metabolic crosstalk, tumors acquire intrinsic resistance while retaining context-dependent vulnerabilities. Understanding these adaptive DDR states provides a mechanistic foundation for rational therapeutic strategies aimed at transiently disrupting genome maintenance programs to re-sensitize resistant CRC.

Ubiquitin-mediated protein stability and chemoresistance in CRC

Ubiquitin-mediated protein turnover constitutes a highly adaptable regulatory system that enables CRC cells to survive chemotherapeutic stress by dynamically reshaping protein homeostasis across multiple cellular layers. At the earliest stage of drug adaptation, ubiquitin-dependent degradation primarily influences metabolic and mitochondrial fitness, thereby setting the threshold for acute chemotherapeutic tolerance. The E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF180, although frequently upregulated in CRC, exerts tumor-suppressive effects by targeting multiple oncogenic and metabolic regulators, including NOTCH1, TRIM24, FOXC1, and the lipogenic enzymes ACC1 and ACLY, for ubiquitin-dependent degradation. This coordinated suppression of Akt signaling and lipid droplet biogenesis constrains metastatic potential and sensitizes CRC cells to chemotherapy [60]. Similarly, HUWE1-mediated ubiquitination of the mitochondrial import protein TOMM20, through both direct targeting and PARKIN-dependent pathways, compromises mitochondrial function, elevates reactive oxygen species, and promotes apoptosis, thereby enhancing oxaliplatin-induced cytotoxicity [61].

However, metabolic and mitochondrial vulnerabilities predominantly affect short-term drug sensitivity and are insufficient to explain the persistence of chemoresistant clones under prolonged treatment. Surviving tumor cells must actively maintain proteostasis and safeguard genome integrity in the face of sustained genotoxic stress. This necessity shifts selective pressure toward ubiquitin-dependent stabilization mechanisms, particularly those mediated by deubiquitinases (DUBs). In this context, oxaliplatin-induced nuclear accumulation of USP10 leads to deubiquitination and stabilization of XAB2, which transcriptionally upregulates ANXA2 to enhance DNA damage repair and attenuate oxaliplatin-induced genotoxicity, thereby establishing a USP10-XAB2-ANXA2 axis that supports chemoresistant survival [62]. In parallel, sustained activity of the proteasomal DUBs USP14 and UCHL5 preserves proteasome function and NF-κB signaling, buffering proteotoxic stress and promoting acquired resistance to 5-fluorouracil. Pharmacological inhibition of USP14/UCHL5 disrupts proteostasis, elevates oxidative stress, and restores chemosensitivity in both sensitive and resistant CRC models [63]. Resistance to 5-fluorouracil is further reinforced by a self-amplifying EFTUD2-c-MYC feedback loop, in which EFTUD2 prevents ubiquitin-mediated degradation of c-MYC, while c-MYC transcriptionally upregulates EFTUD2, collectively sustaining oncogenic transcriptional programs under chemotherapeutic pressure [64].

Importantly, stabilization of survival and proteostasis networks ultimately converges on the regulation of DNA damage repair, which represents the decisive barrier to effective chemotherapy. BAZ1A overexpression enhances chromatin remodeling and DNA repair capacity, thereby supporting tumor growth and chemoresistance, whereas an alternatively spliced short BAZ1A isoform lacks DNA repair function and fails to restore drug resistance, underscoring splicing-dependent modulation of ubiquitin-regulated repair pathways as a critical determinant of DNA damage sensitivity [65]. Similarly, MGMT promotes cisplatin resistance by inducing a slow-cycling cellular state through ubiquitin-mediated degradation of CDK1 while simultaneously enhancing XRCC6-dependent nonhomologous end joining. MGMT expression is also associated with activation of Wnt signaling, collectively enabling CRC cells to evade cisplatin-induced cytotoxicity [66]. Disruption of the TNKS–USP25 protein–protein interaction by the neoantimycin analogue UAT-B promotes tankyrase degradation, suppresses Wnt/β-catenin signalling, and induces apoptosis in CRC cells with high TNKS expression. Targeting TNKS stability rather than enzymatic activity overcomes multidrug resistance and yields robust antitumour efficacy in cell-based, patient-derived, and genetic CRC models [67].

Collectively, these findings illustrate a hierarchical ubiquitin-dependent adaptive program in CRC, in which early metabolic and mitochondrial regulation transitions into sustained proteostasis control and ultimately culminates in reinforced DNA damage repair, thereby establishing a robust and therapeutically exploitable foundation for chemoresistance.

Metabolic reprogramming and redox adaptation

Metabolic remodeling has emerged as a central adaptive layer through which CRC cells coordinate survival, immune evasion, stemness maintenance, and resistance to diverse therapeutic modalities. Rather than representing isolated bioenergetic alterations, metabolic reprogramming functions as a cross-cutting regulatory system that integrates oncogenic signaling, epigenetic regulation, and tumor-immune interactions to sustain resistant phenotypes under sustained treatment pressure.

Within the tumor-immune ecosystem, metabolic adaptation directly undermines antitumor immunity and contributes to resistance to immune checkpoint blockade. ALDH2-driven transcriptional activation of ARG2 via PBX3 leads to L-arginine depletion within the TME, impairing CD8⁺ T-cell glycolysis and effector function and thereby promoting resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors [68]. Consistently, cetuximab-resistant CRC exhibits activation of the tryptophan-kynurenine pathway, with IFN-γ-driven upregulation of IDO1 and IDO2 suppressing immune surveillance; pharmacological inhibition of IDO restores sensitivity and limits tumor growth [69]. These findings underscore how tumor-intrinsic metabolic rewiring propagates immunosuppressive niches that blunt immunotherapeutic efficacy.

Metabolic plasticity also reinforces cancer stem cell (CSC) programs, which are closely linked to chemoresistance and tumor recurrence. PTBP1 promotes CSC maintenance and cisplatin resistance by destabilizing KLF9 mRNA, thereby derepressing FUBP1 transcription and sustaining stemness-associated transcriptional programs [70]. In TP53-mutant CRC, UCHL3 stabilizes ENO1 to drive heightened glycolysis, CSC expansion, and 5-fluorouracil resistance; pharmacological inhibition of the upstream JAK2-STAT3-UCHL3 axis suppresses glycolysis and restores drug sensitivity [71]. Similarly, loss of NAT1 expands LGR5⁺ stem-like populations and reprograms cellular metabolism toward glycolysis while simultaneously altering tumor–endothelial signaling through VEGFA upregulation, collectively promoting multidrug resistance and poor prognosis [72].

Enhanced glycolytic flux represents a recurrent metabolic strategy underpinning resistance to cytotoxic chemotherapy. Multiple regulatory axes converge to sustain glycolysis in resistant CRC, including ETV4-driven transcriptional upregulation of the amino acid transporter SLC38A5 [73], METTL14-YTHDF1-dependent induction of SAP30 [74], and METTL3-mediated stabilization of CPSF6 mRNA [75]. Resistance to oxaliplatin is further reinforced by disruption of NAD⁺ homeostasis, wherein PARP activation depletes NAD⁺ and suppresses SIRT1, thereby promoting glycolysis through PKM2 and LDHA upregulation. Restoration of SIRT1 or inhibition of glycolytic enzymes reverses this resistant phenotype [76,77].

Beyond glucose metabolism, amino acid and lipid metabolic rewiring contribute to therapy-specific resistance programs. Aberrant amino acid sensing through dysfunction of the GATOR complex sustains mTORC1 activation independently of growth factor signaling, driving chemoresistance that can be reversed by pharmacological intervention or dietary protein restriction [78]. In BRAFV600E-mutant CRC, acquired resistance to encorafenib and cetuximab is driven by MOGAT3-mediated diacylglycerol accumulation, which activates a PKCα-CRAF-MEK-ERK feedback loop; targeting DAG metabolism restores therapeutic sensitivity and identifies lipid metabolic remodeling as a druggable vulnerability [79]. In KRAS-mutant CRC, STAT1-dependent activation of sterol and lipid biosynthesis via SREBP1/2 is amplified by a YAP1-TEAD4 feedback loop, conferring resistance to both mevalonate pathway inhibitors and EGFR-targeted therapies [80].

Adaptive remodeling of redox balance and mitochondrial metabolism further stabilizes resistant states. KRAS-mutant CRC cells with acquired cetuximab resistance exhibit enhanced glycolysis coupled with MCT1-dependent lactate uptake and recycling; pharmacological inhibition of MCT1 disrupts metabolic symbiosis and suppresses tumor growth in vitro and in vivo [81]. In MRTX849-resistant CRC, aberrant activation of NRF2 and induction of GLS1 enhance antioxidant defense and metabolic flexibility, attenuating KRAS G12C inhibitor-induced stress and cytotoxicity [82,83]. Peroxisomal protection mediated by PMP70 further shields CRC cells from oxaliplatin-induced lipid peroxidation, whereas depletion of PMP70 disrupts lipid metabolism and restores chemosensitivity [84].

Mitochondrial reprogramming also plays a decisive role in the early establishment of chemoresistance. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses coupled with dynamic network biomarker modeling identify early inhibition of mitochondrial complex I as a critical initiating event in acquired oxaliplatin resistance. MDM2-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of the complex I subunit NDUFB8 reduces reactive oxygen species production under chemotherapeutic stress, promoting survival, while extracellular vesicle-mediated delivery of siMDM2 restores oxaliplatin sensitivity by reactivating the MDM2-NDUFB8-ROS axis [56]. Complementarily, DIRAS1 upregulation during oxaliplatin exposure stabilizes mitochondrial PHB1, sustaining mitochondrial integrity and directly driving chemoresistance [85].

Collectively, these studies establish metabolic reprogramming as an integrative and hierarchical adaptive system that links immune evasion, stemness maintenance, redox balance, and therapy-specific resistance in CRC. By dynamically reallocating metabolic resources across glucose, amino acid, lipid, and mitochondrial pathways, tumor cells acquire robust yet targetable resistant states. Systematic disruption of these metabolic dependencies offers a rational framework for overcoming resistance to chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy in CRC.

Adaptive signaling rewiring

Beyond long-term metabolic and proteostatic reprogramming, exposure to 5-FU elicits rapid adaptive rewiring of signaling and cell-cycle regulatory networks that enable CRC cells to transiently survive cytotoxic stress. These early compensatory responses, while initially reversible, play a critical role in limiting drug efficacy and seeding the emergence of stable chemoresistant clones.

Activation of pro-survival kinase signaling represents a prominent early adaptive response to 5-FU. AHNAK2 is markedly upregulated in 5-FU-resistant CRC and promotes resistance by activating the AKT-GSK-3β axis, thereby enhancing proliferation and survival under chemotherapeutic stress. Genetic silencing of AHNAK2 restores 5-FU sensitivity both in vitro and in vivo, underscoring its functional role in adaptive survival signaling [86]. Similarly, ZNF263 is aberrantly upregulated in CRC and drives resistance to chemoradiotherapy by transcriptionally activating and stabilizing STAT3 mRNA, thereby promoting proliferation, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and invasive behavior. Disruption of STAT3 signaling reverses ZNF263-driven malignant phenotypes, highlighting the ZNF263-STAT3 axis as a critical mediator of therapy-induced signaling adaptation [87].

Cytoskeletal and small GTPase signaling pathways further contribute to adaptive resistance under 5-FU pressure. Integrated in silico, in vitro, and in vivo analyses identify Rac1 signaling as a key driver of 5-FU resistance and tumor recurrence in CRC. Pharmacological inhibition of Rac1 using 1A-116 restores drug sensitivity, reverses resistance-associated morphological changes, and suppresses tumor growth and metastasis, indicating that Rac1-dependent cytoskeletal and migratory programs support adaptive survival during chemotherapy [88].

Adaptive modulation of cell-cycle control also limits the cytotoxic efficacy of 5-FU. Treatment with 5-FU induces compensatory upregulation of the mitotic kinase AURKB, which promotes tumor cell survival and attenuates chemotherapy-induced cell death. Pharmacological inhibition of AURKB disrupts this adaptive response and enhances 5-FU potency across in vitro systems and patient-derived tumor models, demonstrating that transient cell-cycle rewiring constitutes a druggable vulnerability in 5-FU-treated CRC [89,90].

Stress-response signaling pathways further shape early adaptation to therapeutic pressure. In KRAS-dependent CRC cells, KRAS silencing induces rapid activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) alongside WNT/β-catenin signaling. This adaptive state is characterized by reduced protein aggregation and decreased translational capacity, reflecting a protective response to oncogene withdrawal that buffers proteotoxic stress [91]. In parallel, TIMP-2 is upregulated in 5-FU–resistant CRC cells and in the serum of patients with refractory disease, where it promotes resistance through activation of JAK-STAT signaling. Genetic or pharmacological blockade of TIMP-2 or JAK-STAT signaling restores chemosensitivity, highlighting stress-induced cytokine signaling as an additional layer of adaptive resistance [92].

Collectively, these findings reveal that 5-FU treatment triggers a coordinated program of acute adaptive signaling rewiring encompassing pro-survival kinase activation, cytoskeletal remodeling, cell-cycle compensation, and stress-response signaling. While these responses are initially plastic and reversible, they substantially blunt chemotherapeutic efficacy and create a permissive landscape for the stabilization of long-term resistant phenotypes. Targeting these early adaptive signaling nodes represents a rational strategy to enhance 5-FU efficacy and prevent the emergence of durable chemoresistance in CRC.

Drug efflux and multidrug resistance (MDR)

In addition to intracellular signaling and metabolic rewiring, adaptive activation of drug efflux pathways represents a rapid and functionally reversible mechanism through which CRC cells attenuate intracellular drug accumulation and limit chemotherapeutic efficacy. This adaptive response is frequently coupled to inflammatory and stress-responsive signaling networks, enabling tumor cells to dynamically regulate transporter expression under therapeutic pressure.

Activation of the nuclear xenobiotic receptor pregnane X receptor (PXR) exemplifies this adaptive efflux-centered resistance program. In colon cancer, PXR activation promotes resistance to 5-FU by transcriptionally upregulating MDR1/P-glycoprotein (P-gp), thereby enhancing drug efflux despite transiently increasing short-term therapeutic responsiveness. Importantly, the traditional Chinese medicine formulation Fengliao Changweikang (FLCWK) effectively reverses PXR-mediated chemoresistance by suppressing P-gp expression and concurrently modulating IL-6-STAT3 signaling, restoring and enhancing the antitumor efficacy of 5-FU both in vitro and in vivo [93]. These findings highlight the dual and temporally dynamic role of xenobiotic-sensing pathways in shaping chemotherapy response.

Similarly, oxaliplatin resistance is reinforced by adaptive regulation of efflux machinery through vesicular trafficking components. VPS4A is markedly upregulated in oxaliplatin-resistant CRC cells, where it promotes resistance by sustaining expression of the multidrug resistance-associated protein MRP2 and facilitating oxaliplatin efflux. Inhibition of VPS4A reduces MRP2 abundance, prevents drug egress, and significantly enhances oxaliplatin sensitivity, identifying VPS4A as a druggable regulator of efflux-mediated chemoresistance [94].

Collectively, these studies define drug efflux activation as a rapid and plastic adaptive resistance mechanism in CRC, operating through coordinated regulation of transporter expression, vesicular trafficking, and inflammatory signaling. Although initially reversible, sustained engagement of efflux programs markedly limits intracellular drug exposure and contributes to treatment failure. Therapeutic strategies that disrupt efflux regulation or uncouple transporter activation from inflammatory signaling pathways may therefore provide an effective means to restore chemosensitivity and enhance the durability of chemotherapy responses in CRC.

Rewiring of cell death pathways as a core resistance axis

Apoptosis evasion

Evasion of apoptosis represents a central and terminal mechanism of chemoresistance in CRC, enabling tumor cells to survive cytotoxic and targeted therapies despite substantial upstream stress. Dysregulation of the mitochondrial apoptotic program, impairment of p53-dependent death signaling, and stabilization of anti-apoptotic proteins collectively allow CRC cells to bypass chemotherapy-induced apoptotic execution and maintain stable resistant phenotypes. Aberrant stabilization of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins is among the most prominent mechanisms of apoptosis evasion in resistant CRC. In particular, the anti-apoptotic protein MCL-1 is frequently upregulated in chemoresistant tumors, especially in the context of loss of the E3 ubiquitin ligase FBW7. Because FBW7 normally mediates ubiquitin-dependent degradation of MCL-1, its loss results in sustained MCL-1 accumulation, effectively blocking chemotherapy-induced mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and subsequent caspase cascade activation, thereby protecting CRC cells from apoptosis [95]. Targeting this dependency, pharmacological inhibition of MCL-1 with maritoclax restores apoptotic sensitivity in FBW7-deficient CRC models, indicating that MCL-1 addiction constitutes a therapeutically exploitable vulnerability. Similarly, acquired resistance to 5-FU is associated with elevated expression of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-XL, rendering resistant CRC cells selectively dependent on this survival factor (Fig. 2). The BH3 mimetic A-1331852 selectively induces apoptosis and suppresses tumor growth in 5-FU-resistant CRC both in vitro and in vivo [96]. High chromosomal instability (CIN) further amplifies this dependency, conferring pronounced sensitivity to BCL-XL inhibitors such as navitoclax or WEHI-539 [97]. Beyond mitochondrial apoptosis, functional suppression of p53-dependent death signaling also substantially contributes to chemoresistance in CRC. SIRT1 promotes tumor progression and 5-FU resistance by deacetylating and suppressing p53 activity, leading to downregulation of miR-101 and derepression of its downstream target KPNA3, thereby supporting tumor cell survival, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and drug resistance [99]. In contrast, restoration of p53 translational control enhances apoptotic competence. ANGEL2 increases TP53 translation by binding EIF4E and preventing its interaction with the translational repressor RBM38, thereby elevating p53 protein abundance and restoring apoptosis. Loss of ANGEL2 reduces p53 levels, promotes tumor growth, and drives chemoresistance [100].

Alterations in redox homeostasis further contribute to apoptotic evasion in resistant CRC. UGDH is upregulated in 5-FU-resistant CRC cells through ROS-induced activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway and promotes resistance by clearing intracellular ROS and suppressing mitochondrial apoptosis via downstream effectors EEF1A2 and PRDX1 [101]. By attenuating ROS-mediated mitochondrial damage, UGDH effectively raises the apoptotic threshold, allowing tumor cells to survive chemotherapy-induced oxidative stress. Restoration of oxidative stress reinstates apoptotic signaling and re-sensitizes CRC cells to chemotherapy.

Pharmacological reactivation of apoptotic programs represents an effective strategy to overcome established resistance. The PP2A activator PPA24 robustly induces apoptosis and oxidative stress while suppressing c-MYC expression, exhibiting potent antitumor activity against FOLFOX-resistant CRC without overt systemic toxicity and synergizing with gemcitabine or cisplatin [102]. The bifunctional amino-pyrimidine compound APD-94 triggers apoptosis by suppressing Bmi-1 expression, inducing G2/M cell-cycle arrest, and directly inhibiting tubulin polymerization, thereby effectively overcoming paclitaxel resistance in CRC [103]. In addition, the caspase-8-derived fragment p20BAP31 potently induces apoptosis through ROS accumulation, JNK activation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and translocation of apoptosis-inducing factor (AIF), providing a death-induction strategy resilient to classical resistance mechanisms [104].

Regulatory non-coding RNAs further fine-tune apoptotic competence in CRC. The lncRNA ASB16-AS1 promotes doxorubicin resistance by sponging miR-185-5p and activating TEAD1 signaling, thereby suppressing apoptosis, whereas nanoparticle-mediated delivery of sh-ASB16-AS1 restores apoptotic sensitivity and inhibits tumor growth in vivo [105]. Conversely, loss of the lncRNA AC092894.1 drives oxaliplatin resistance, while its restoration re-sensitizes tumors to chemotherapy by facilitating USP3-mediated deubiquitination and stabilization of the androgen receptor, enhancing RASGRP3 transcription, modulating MAPK signaling, and re-establishing apoptotic execution capacity [106].

Collectively, through stabilization of anti-apoptotic proteins, suppression of p53-dependent death signaling, attenuation of oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial apoptosis, and multilayered non-coding RNA regulation of apoptotic competence, CRC cells effectively evade therapy-induced cell death and establish stable chemoresistant states while retaining actionable apoptotic vulnerabilities. These findings highlight restoration of apoptotic execution as a promising therapeutic strategy for overcoming chemoresistance in CRC.

Ferroptosis resistance

Ferroptosis is a regulated form of cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and has emerged as a critical determinant of chemoresistance and immunotherapy resistance in CRC. Resistant CRC cells frequently evade ferroptotic death through coordinated regulation of antioxidant capacity, iron homeostasis, lipid metabolism, and stress-response signaling, thereby maintaining survival under therapeutic pressure. Among the most prominent mechanisms, upregulation of the cystine–glutamate antiporter subunit SLC7A11 plays a central role by sustaining glutathione biosynthesis and suppressing lipid peroxidation. Transcriptional and post-transcriptional control of SLC7A11 is therefore a recurrent feature of ferroptosis resistance. ARNTL2 has been identified as a key transcriptional regulator of SLC7A11 in CRC, reinforcing anti-ferroptotic programs and promoting chemoresistance [107]. Similarly, loss of ribosomal protein uL3 stabilizes SLC7A11 mRNA, elevates glutathione-dependent antioxidant capacity, suppresses ferroptosis, and drives both chemoresistance and metastatic potential [108].

Perturbation of iron metabolism represents another major axis governing ferroptotic vulnerability. Loss of FBXL5 restores oxaliplatin sensitivity by activating the IREB2-TFRC axis, increasing intracellular iron accumulation, and enhancing ferroptosis [109]. In oxaliplatin-resistant CRC, the stemness-associated transcription factor KLF5 activates the LIF-MTF1-FPN1 pathway to reduce intracellular iron levels and suppress ferroptosis; ketogenic diet intervention or KLF5 silencing disrupts this axis, induces iron overload, and restores drug sensitivity [110]. These findings highlight iron homeostasis as a metabolically and transcriptionally controlled vulnerability in resistant CRC.

Multiple signaling pathways converge to suppress lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic execution. CLK2 functions as an intrinsic regulator of ferroptosis through dual modulation of the KEAP1-NRF2-HMOX1 and p53-SLC7A11 pathways; CLK2 depletion unleashes HMOX1-driven lipid peroxidation, markedly enhancing ferroptotic sensitivity and synergizing with ferroptosis inducers to suppress tumor growth [111]. UBR5 overexpression confers oxaliplatin resistance by stabilizing Smad3 via Lys11-linked polyubiquitination, leading to repression of ATF3, upregulation of SLC7A11, and suppression of ferroptosis; combined targeting of UBR5 and ferroptotic pathways synergistically restores chemosensitivity [112]. Likewise, CDK1 overexpression suppresses ferroptosis through phosphorylation-dependent ubiquitination and degradation of ACSL4, whereas CDK1 inhibition restores lipid peroxidation and re-sensitizes CRC to oxaliplatin [113]. In BRAFV600E CRC, resistance to BRAF inhibition is driven by activation of the PLK1-CBX8-GPX4 axis, which suppresses ferroptosis; PLK1 inhibition reactivates ferroptotic cell death and overcomes therapeutic resistance [114].

Mitochondrial and metabolic adaptations further shape ferroptotic resistance. WBP1 maintains mitochondrial respiration and suppresses ferroptosis, thereby promoting resistance to both 5-FU and oxaliplatin; genetic or pharmacological inhibition of WBP1 impairs mitochondrial function, induces ferroptosis, and sensitizes resistant CRC cells to chemotherapy, with high WBP1 expression correlating with poor clinical outcomes [115]. Combination treatment with salinomycin and 5-FU enhances ferroptotic cell death by downregulating GPX4 and SLC7A11, effectively overcoming 5-FU resistance and suppressing proliferation, migration, and invasion [116]. Oxaliplatin-resistant CRC cells also exhibit aberrant activation of the nuclear export protein XPO1; inhibition of XPO1 with KPT-330 restores chemosensitivity by inducing both apoptosis and ferroptosis through p53 nuclear retention and downstream regulation of p21 and SLC7A11 [117].

Non-coding RNAs and ubiquitin-mediated mechanisms further fine-tune ferroptotic competence. LncRNA-HMG suppresses chemotherapy-induced ferroptosis by binding p53 and facilitating MDM2-mediated p53 degradation, leading to upregulation of SLC7A11 and VKORC1L1 and enhanced ROS detoxification; antisense oligonucleotide–mediated inhibition of LncRNA-HMG restores ferroptotic sensitivity in patient-derived xenograft models [118]. LncRNA SNHG4 promotes oxaliplatin resistance by binding PTEN mRNA and reducing its stability, thereby suppressing PTEN-dependent ferroptotic signaling [119]. CBX3 drives multidrug resistance by repressing CUL3 transcription, stabilizing NRF2, and upregulating GPX2, collectively inhibiting ferroptosis, whereas CBX3 knockdown reactivates ferroptotic cell death and restores drug sensitivity [120].

Ferroptosis resistance also intersects with tumor-immune interactions and systemic metabolic states. CYP1B1 suppresses ferroptosis through a 20-HETE-PKC-FBXO10 axis that promotes ubiquitin-mediated degradation of ACSL4, thereby conferring resistance to immune checkpoint blockade; inhibition of CYP1B1 restores ferroptotic vulnerability and enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy [121]. In contrast, APOL3 promotes ferroptosis and enhances CD8⁺ T-cell function by inducing ubiquitin-mediated degradation of LDHA, reducing lactate accumulation, and facilitating IFN-γ-dependent antitumor immunity [122]. Obesity-driven adipose-derived exosomes further contribute to ferroptosis escape by delivering MTTP, which forms an MTTP-PRAP1 complex to upregulate GPX4 and SLC7A11 while reducing lipid peroxidation; targeting exosomal MTTP restores ferroptotic sensitivity and re-sensitizes CRC to chemotherapy [123].

Collectively, these studies establish ferroptosis evasion as a multifaceted and therapeutically actionable mechanism of resistance in CRC. Through coordinated suppression of lipid peroxidation, remodeling of iron and redox metabolism, and integration of signaling, epigenetic, and microenvironmental cues, CRC cells acquire robust resistance while retaining context-dependent ferroptotic vulnerabilities. Targeting ferroptosis regulators, either alone or in combination with chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy, offers a promising strategy to overcome drug resistance and improve treatment durability in CRC.

Autophagy, senescence, and drug-tolerant persistence

CRC is one of the most common and lethal cancers worldwide, with chemotherapy being a cornerstone of treatment. However, chemotherapy resistance remains a major clinical challenge in CRC therapy. Recent studies have highlighted autophagy as a crucial cellular survival mechanism that enables CRC cells to maintain homeostasis under stressful conditions, including chemotherapy. In chemoresistant CRC cells, autophagy has been shown to support the survival of CRC stem cells by mitigating the toxic effects of chemotherapy. Specifically, autophagy helps reduce cellular damage caused by chemotherapy and supports the maintenance of stem-like properties, which are key contributors to tumor recurrence and chemoresistance. Inhibition of autophagy has been shown to reduce CRC cell survival and restore sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents, primarily by suppressing epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and promoting apoptosis, both of which enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy. For instance, autophagy inhibitors such as chloroquine have demonstrated promise in preclinical CRC models, indicating that autophagy inhibition could be a potential therapeutic strategy to overcome chemoresistance [124,125].

Beyond its conventional role in chemoresistance, autophagy is modulated by a variety of signaling pathways that can either promote or suppress its activity. For example, lamivudine (3TC), an antiviral drug, induces ROS accumulation in p53-mutant CRC cells, which activates autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. These processes may counteract the anticancer efficacy of 3TC by promoting cell survival under stress. However, the inhibition of both autophagy and ER stress has been shown to enhance 3TC’s therapeutic effects, suggesting that modulating these pathways could significantly improve treatment outcomes in CRC [126]. Furthermore, the downregulation of HMBOX1 enhances 5-FU resistance by promoting autophagy via reduced ubiquitination of ATG5, whereas HMBOX1 upregulation sensitizes CRC cells to 5-FU by inhibiting autophagy through transcriptional activation of HACE1 and subsequent ATG5 degradation [127]. Additionally, EZH2 upregulation in CRC promotes proliferation and irinotecan resistance by inhibiting autophagy through direct activation of NRP1. Silencing EZH2 or NRP1 restores autophagy and reduces chemoresistance [128]. TIPE3 has also been shown to promote drug resistance in CRC by upregulating USP19, which enhances autophagy through the USP19/Beclin1 pathway, while also stimulating M2 macrophage polarization, further contributing to chemoresistance [129].

In addition to these molecular mechanisms, the MYC upstream region (MYC UR) has been found to drive resistance to PI3K inhibitors by enhancing MYC transcription in a FOXO3a-dependent manner, leading to increased autophagic flux and drug resistance. However, inhibition of autophagy restores sensitivity to PI3K blockade in MYC-driven CRC and Burkitt lymphoma, suggesting that targeting autophagy could be an effective strategy for overcoming resistance [130]. The REV1 p.R704Q mutation serves as an independent biomarker for primary cetuximab resistance in RAS wild-type metastatic CRC, destabilizing REV1 protein and activating autophagy, thereby mediating resistance. This resistance can be reversed by autophagy inhibition, revealing a novel autophagy-dependent mechanism of primary resistance [131]. Similarly, circSEC24B promotes autophagy-dependent chemoresistance by enhancing SRPX2 protein stability through deubiquitination via OTUB1, driving CRC proliferation, invasion, and drug resistance [132]. USP14, which is highly expressed in MSI-H CRC, inhibits mitophagy by stabilizing BAG4, blocking Parkin recruitment to damaged mitochondria. USP14 knockdown restores mitophagy, suppresses tumor growth, and increases oxaliplatin sensitivity [133]. Moreover, loss of KLF4 promotes 5-FU resistance in colon cancer by derepressing RAB26-dependent autophagy, thereby sustaining tumor cell survival under chemotherapy. Restoring KLF4 suppresses autophagosome formation through transcriptional inhibition of RAB26, reduces autophagic flux, and re-sensitizes tumors to 5-FU [134].

Therapy-induced senescent CRC cells also rely on the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway and O-GlcNAcylation to maintain survival and evade apoptosis under low-dose chemotherapy. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of GFAT/OGT shifts the cellular response from senescence to apoptosis by enhancing DNA damage, suggesting that targeting O-GlcNAcylation can overcome senescence-associated persistence and improve the efficacy of pro-senescence therapies in CRC [135]. Thus, targeting autophagy, either alone or in combination with other therapeutic agents, presents a promising strategy to overcome chemoresistance in CRC and enhance the efficacy of existing treatments.

In summary, autophagy plays a crucial role in the development of chemoresistance in CRC. By understanding the underlying molecular mechanisms that regulate autophagy, such as signaling pathways involving HMBOX1, EZH2, and MYC, novel strategies can be developed to target autophagy and enhance the effectiveness of chemotherapy. Although preclinical studies have shown promising results with autophagy inhibitors, further clinical trials are necessary to assess their safety and efficacy in human CRC patients. Moreover, the multifaceted role of autophagy in CRC requires a careful and precise approach to modulate its activity to avoid potential side effects and maximize therapeutic benefit. Future research should focus on the development of targeted autophagy inhibitors and their combination with other therapeutic agents to overcome chemoresistance and improve patient outcomes.

Necroptosis and pyroptosis suppression

Necroptosis is a programmed necrotic cell death pathway that is tightly regulated by key signaling molecules such as RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL. In CRC, necroptosis is often suppressed through the aberrant activation of survival signaling pathways, contributing to chemotherapy resistance. One such pathway involves the Met receptor, which, when overactivated, impedes RIPK1-mediated necroptosis. This inhibition of necroptosis enhances CRC cell survival under chemotherapy stress, contributing to resistance. Interestingly, restoration of RIPK1 function using cabozantinib, a Met inhibitor, has been shown to reactivate necroptosis and enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy in CRC models, suggesting that targeting necroptosis-related pathways could represent a promising strategy for overcoming chemoresistance [136].

In addition to necroptosis, pyroptosis, a form of inflammatory programmed cell death, has also been implicated in CRC chemoresistance. Recent studies have demonstrated that circPDIA3 promotes oxaliplatin resistance in CRC by inhibiting GSDME-mediated pyroptosis. CircPDIA3 directly binds to the C domain of GSDME, preventing its palmitoylation by ZDHHC3/17, thereby enhancing the autoinhibition of GSDME and blocking pyroptotic cell death. Furthermore, circPDIA3 activates a positive feedback loop involving miR-449a and XBP1, which reinforces its effects on pyroptosis inhibition. This mechanism highlights circPDIA3 as a potential therapeutic target to overcome chemoresistance, particularly in CRC patients resistant to oxaliplatin [137].

The gut microbiota has also emerged as a critical modulator of chemoresistance in CRC. Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum), a bacterium commonly found in CRC, has been shown to promote chemotherapy resistance by suppressing chemotherapy-induced pyroptosis. Mechanistically, F. nucleatum activates the Hippo signaling pathway, leading to the upregulation of BCL2, which inhibits the caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptotic pathway. This results in the attenuation of pyroptosis and facilitates tumor recurrence after chemotherapy. These findings suggest that targeting microbial dysbiosis or manipulating the Hippo signaling pathway could provide novel therapeutic avenues for overcoming chemotherapy resistance in CRC [138].

Moreover, mitochondrial dysfunction plays a crucial role in chemoresistance. The mitochondrial chaperone TRAP1 has been identified as a key regulator in this context. Loss of TRAP1 forces the translocation of cyclophilin D, leading to the persistent opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP). This process enhances chemotherapy-induced necrosis and activates antitumor immune responses. Disruption of TRAP1 in CRC cells directly overcomes chemoresistance by promoting necrosis and reducing immunosuppression, thus enhancing the antitumor immune response and improving chemotherapy outcomes. These findings position TRAP1 as a potential therapeutic target for CRC chemoresistance [139].

In summary, both necroptosis and pyroptosis play significant roles in the development of chemoresistance in CRC. The suppression of these programmed cell death pathways through various mechanisms, such as Met receptor overactivation, circPDIA3-mediated inhibition of GSDME, microbial influences like F. nucleatum, and mitochondrial dysfunction via TRAP1, contributes to the resistance of CRC cells to chemotherapy. Targeting these pathways, either individually or in combination, holds great promise for overcoming chemoresistance and improving the efficacy of chemotherapy in CRC patients. Future research should focus on further elucidating the molecular mechanisms that govern necroptosis and pyroptosis in CRC, as well as developing therapeutic strategies that can specifically target these pathways to enhance clinical outcomes.

PANoptosis evasion as an integrative death-resistance node

Beyond classical apoptosis resistance, accumulating evidence indicates that CRC cells acquire chemoresistance by broadly suppressing multiple non-canonical programmed cell death pathways, including PANoptosis, paraptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and post-apoptotic survival programs. This coordinated shutdown of alternative lethal mechanisms enables tumor cells to survive cytotoxic stress, adapt to therapy, and ultimately drive relapse.

PANoptosis, an integrated cell death program that encompasses apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis, has recently emerged as a critical vulnerability in chemotherapy-treated CRC. WTAP-mediated m6A methylation attenuates oxaliplatin sensitivity by sustaining NRF2 expression, thereby reducing intracellular oxidative stress and suppressing PANoptotic cell death. Clinically, high WTAP expression correlates with poor prognosis and diminished benefit from oxaliplatin-based chemotherapy, highlighting the WTAP-NRF2-PANoptosis axis as a key determinant of therapeutic resistance and a potential target for intervention [140]. In parallel, chemotherapy-induced upregulation of USP10 reinforces intrinsic resistance by stabilizing Ku70/80, enhancing non-homologous end joining (NHEJ)-mediated DNA repair, and limiting persistent DNA damage signaling that would otherwise trigger PANoptosis. Loss of USP10 markedly increases chemosensitivity, underscoring its role as a central regulator linking DNA repair proficiency to suppression of cell death programs [49].

In addition to PANoptosis, suppression of alternative non-apoptotic death pathways further contributes to treatment failure. Cetuximab-resistant CRC cells exhibit profound inhibition of paraptosis, a caspase-independent form of programmed cell death characterized by cytoplasmic vacuolization. This resistance phenotype is driven by aberrant activation of focal adhesion kinase (FAK) signaling. Notably, targeted delivery of siFAK via engineered exosomes effectively reactivates paraptosis and reverses cetuximab resistance in metastatic CRC models, identifying paraptosis induction as a promising therapeutic strategy for tumors refractory to conventional apoptotic triggers [141].

CRC cells can also evade irreversible cell death through activation of anastasis, a survival program that allows recovery even after executioner caspase activation. Chemotherapy-treated CRC cells exploit a cIAP2/NF-κB-dependent anastatic response to survive transient apoptotic signaling. Importantly, anastatic cells acquire enhanced migratory, metastatic, and chemoresistant properties, thereby promoting tumor recurrence following chemotherapy and revealing an underappreciated mechanism of post-treatment disease progression [142].

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that chemoresistance in CRC is not solely a consequence of impaired apoptosis but instead reflects a broader rewiring of cell death networks. By simultaneously suppressing PANoptosis, paraptosis, and other non-canonical death programs while activating post-death survival pathways such as anastasis, CRC cells achieve remarkable plasticity under therapeutic pressure. Targeting these alternative cell death escape mechanisms, either alone or in rational combination with existing treatments, represents a promising strategy to overcome chemoresistance and improve durable responses in CRC.

Epigenetic, Transcriptional, and RNA-Based Regulation of Resistance

Chemoresistance in CRC cannot be fully explained by genetic alterations alone. Instead, mounting evidence indicates that CRC cells exploit multilayered regulatory mechanisms governing gene expression plasticity to survive therapeutic pressure. These mechanisms operate across interconnected levels, including chromatin remodeling and DNA methylation, RNA-based regulation through non-coding RNAs and RNA modifications, and downstream execution via alternative splicing and translational control. Together, these processes enable both the rapid acquisition and stable maintenance of drug-resistant phenotypes.

DNA methylation and chromatin remodeling

Chromatin-level reprogramming provides a stable epigenetic foundation that locks CRC cells into drug-tolerant states by durably altering transcriptional landscapes. Several studies highlight DNA methylation-dependent mechanisms that directly shape chemotherapy sensitivity. BDH2 enhances 5-FU sensitivity by promoting DNA methyltransferase-mediated hypermethylation of the GLIPR1 promoter, thereby suppressing its transcription. This BDH2-GLIPR1 epigenetic axis represents a reversible regulatory pathway governing 5-FU resistance [143]. Similarly, oxaliplatin resistance is driven by B-MYB-induced upregulation of the lncRNA CCAT1, which recruits DNMT1 to epigenetically silence SOCS3 through promoter methylation. Activation of this B-MYB/CCAT1/DNMT1/SOCS3 axis reinforces malignant phenotypes and confers resistance to oxaliplatin [144].

Histone modification-mediated chromatin remodeling further contributes to transcriptional plasticity underlying resistance. In BRAFV600E-mutated CRC, treatment with the DNA hypomethylating agent 5-azacitidine fails to derepress tumor-suppressor genes due to compensatory activation of Polycomb repressive complex (PRC)-mediated histone repression, characterized by increased H3K27me3 and H3K4me3. Combined inhibition of DNA methyltransferases and EZH2 effectively overcomes this epigenetic buffering, resulting in additive antitumor efficacy [145]. In APC-mutant CRC, high-dose tankyrase inhibition paradoxically activates BRD3/4-dependent E2F transcription and cell-cycle progression, generating intrinsic or acquired resistance. Co-inhibition of BET proteins abrogates this compensatory transcriptional response and restores drug sensitivity [146].

Chromatin remodeling also sustains chemoresistance through reinforcement of cancer stem-like properties. Transcriptional activation of ABCG2 via a TOX3-WDR5 axis promotes H3K4 trimethylation at the ABCG2 promoter, maintaining drug efflux capacity, stemness, and therapy resistance. Targeting WDR5 disrupts this epigenetic program and sensitizes CRC cells to chemotherapy [147]. Importantly, chromatin states are not static but are influenced by metabolic inputs. Oxaliplatin resistance is associated with reduced β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) levels and increased activating histone marks, including H3K79me, H3K27ac, and H3K14ac. Restoration of BHB reverses resistance by epigenetically reprogramming tumor cells through DOT1L-mediated H3K79 demethylation and H3K27 deacetylation, thereby suppressing proliferation, EMT, and metastasis [148].

While chromatin remodeling establishes long-term resistance memory, it cannot fully account for the speed, reversibility, and heterogeneity of therapeutic adaptation. These properties are instead largely governed by RNA-centered regulatory mechanisms.

Histone modifications and metabolic-epigenetic coupling

RNA-based regulation provides CRC cells with a highly dynamic and adaptable layer of control over gene expression, enabling rapid responses to chemotherapy without permanent genomic or chromatin alterations. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and circular RNAs (circRNAs) function as molecular scaffolds, decoys, or regulators of transcription and RNA processing. The lncRNA CACClnc enhances homologous recombination-mediated DNA repair by promoting YB1-U2AF65-dependent alternative splicing of RAD51, thereby driving chemoresistance. Elevated tumor and exosomal CACClnc levels predict poor prognosis and diminished chemotherapy response, identifying CACClnc as both a biomarker and therapeutic target [149]. In metastatic CRC, downregulation of the lncRNA-encoded protein PRDM16-DT promotes oxaliplatin resistance by enhancing HNRNPA2B1-mediated alternative splicing of CHEK2, impairing E-cadherin secretion, activating fibroblasts, and increasing MMP9-driven invasion. Notably, the natural compound cimicifugoside H-1 restores chemosensitivity by targeting FOXP3 to upregulate PRDM16-DT [150].

CircRNAs further modulate therapy response. Circ_0001766 is downregulated in CRC and suppresses tumor progression and rapamycin resistance by sponging miR-1203, thereby stabilizing PPP1R3C mRNA and inhibiting the mTOR/Myc pathway. Restoration of circ_0001766 resensitizes CRC cells to rapamycin [151]. Conversely, circ_0020095 functions as an oncogenic regulator of chemoresistance, as its silencing in patient-derived CRC organoids reverses resistance to both 5-FU and oxaliplatin [152].

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) act as both intracellular regulators and intercellular conveyors of resistance. MiR-196b is downregulated in cetuximab-resistant CRC, and its restoration enhances cetuximab sensitivity by suppressing NRAS and BRAF, thereby inhibiting ERK and AKT signaling [153]. Exosome-mediated transfer of miRNAs enables horizontal dissemination of resistance traits. Exosomes derived from 5-FU-resistant CRC cells promote malignant behavior and transmit resistance through downregulation of miR-224-5p and upregulation of its target S100A4. Restoring miR-224-5p suppresses tumor aggressiveness and resensitizes resistant cells to 5-FU [154]. Similarly, exosomes from GRP78-induced M2-like macrophages deliver miR-769-5p to CRC cells, inducing cell-cycle quiescence via RB1 and cyclin regulation and conferring 5-FU resistance [155].

Beyond non-coding RNAs, epitranscriptomic modifications critically shape chemoresistance. FTO promotes CRC resistance by demethylating NUPR1 mRNA at a critical m6A site, preventing YTHDF2-mediated degradation and sustaining iron homeostasis gene expression [156]. IGF2BP3, in cooperation with METTL14, stabilizes and enhances translation of EGFR mRNA in an m6A-dependent manner, sustaining EGFR signaling and driving cetuximab resistance [157]. Additionally, the METTL1-mediated m7G-modified tsRNA-GlyGCC activates JAK1-STAT6 signaling by targeting SPIB, promoting 5-FU resistance; nanoparticle-assisted inhibition of tsRNA-GlyGCC restores drug sensitivity [158]. Intriguingly, HDAC1 K412 lactylation activates FTO and ALKBH5 through H3K27ac, reduces global m6A levels, destabilizes FSP1 mRNA, and enhances ferroptotic vulnerability, revealing a complex interplay between chromatin modification and RNA methylation [159].

Although RNA-based mechanisms enable rapid and flexible adaptation, durable chemoresistance ultimately requires reinforcement at the protein level.

Alternative splicing and translational control

Alternative splicing and translational control represent the final execution layer that translates upstream epigenetic and RNA-based alterations into stable proteomic states supporting chemoresistance. Sustained unfolded protein response (UPR) signaling exemplifies this process. The adaptor protein 14-3-3σ promotes CRC progression and chemoresistance by sequestering the transcription factor YY1 in the cytoplasm, thereby relieving YY1-mediated repression of UPR genes. Loss of 14-3-3σ or pharmacological inhibition of the UPR sensitizes CRC cells to chemotherapy [160].

Transcriptional and translational upregulation of specific effector proteins further consolidates resistance. ANXA9 is markedly upregulated in chemoresistant CRC, where ZMYM2 directly activates its transcription. This resistance-driving ZMYM2-ANXA9 axis can be suppressed by the FLT3 inhibitor G749, demonstrating the therapeutic potential of targeting downstream execution pathways [161].

Collectively, these findings reveal a hierarchical yet highly interconnected regulatory network in which chromatin remodeling establishes permissive transcriptional states, RNA-based mechanisms enable rapid and reversible adaptation, and alternative splicing and translational control execute and stabilize chemoresistant phenotypes. Targeting multiple layers of this gene expression plasticity, rather than single pathways, may therefore be required to achieve durable therapeutic responses in CRC.

Cancer Stemness and Drug-Tolerant Persister Cells

Increasing evidence indicates that chemoresistance in CRC is tightly linked to cellular plasticity rather than fixed genetic alterations. CSCs and drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells represent dynamic subpopulations that survive cytotoxic stress, seed tumor relapse, and fuel metastatic progression. These states are sustained by transcriptional, epigenetic, metabolic, and stress-adaptive programs that confer stemness, slow-cycling behavior, and reversible drug tolerance.

CSC-associated resistance programs

Transcriptional and signaling networks that reinforce stemness play a central role in therapy resistance. The transcription factor HOXC8 promotes CRC proliferation and CSC traits by transcriptionally activating TRIM22, which stimulates NF-κB signaling via IκBα degradation, thereby conferring chemoresistance [162]. Similarly, overexpression of the BMP antagonist CHRDL2 suppresses BMP signaling while enhancing WNT-driven stemness programs. CHRDL2 expands the CSC compartment, elevates stem-cell marker expression, suppresses differentiation, and enhances survival under chemotherapy and radiotherapy through activation of DNA damage response pathways, positioning CHRDL2 as both a biomarker and driver of treatment resistance [163].

Dysregulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling further reinforces CSC-associated resistance. HUWE1 functions as a context-dependent ubiquitin ligase that restrains β-catenin signaling; loss of HUWE1 stabilizes β-catenin, enhances EMT and CSC traits, and promotes drug resistance through excessive mitochondrial biogenesis that fuels ATP-dependent ABC transporter activity [164]. Aberrant transcriptional regulators also modulate stemness. ARID3A is upregulated in CRC and correlates with CSC marker expression and chemoresistance, while perturbation of ARID3A expression alters stemness programs and paclitaxel responsiveness [165].

Post-translational regulation of pluripotency factors further sustains CSC phenotypes. SENP1 promotes cisplatin resistance by deSUMOylating OCT4, thereby enhancing stem-cell marker expression and drug tolerance [166]. In parallel, metabolic–epigenetic coupling reinforces CSC survival: histone lactylation enhances chemoresistance in CRC stem cells by suppressing ferroptosis. Specifically, p300-catalyzed lactylation of histone H4 at lysine 12 (H4K12la), antagonized by HDAC1, upregulates GCLC, thereby maintaining redox homeostasis and resistance to chemotherapy. Inhibition of p300, LDHA, or GCLC restores chemosensitivity, revealing a metabolic-epigenetic axis that sustains CSC-associated resistance [167].

CSC reprogramming also underlies resistance to targeted therapies. In RAS/RAF wild-type CRC, primary resistance to cetuximab is driven by AURKA-mediated phosphorylation of YAP1 at Ser397, which induces stem-like transcriptional programs. Pharmacological inhibition of AURKA or disruption of YAP1 Ser397 phosphorylation restores cetuximab sensitivity and suppresses CSC traits [168].

EMT-stemness plasticity

CSC properties are closely intertwined with EMT, enabling tumor cells to dynamically shift between epithelial, mesenchymal, and stem-like states. PLK1 overexpression promotes 5-FU resistance by activating the CRAF-MEK-ERK pathway, driving EMT and stemness programs. Inhibition of PLK1 or downstream ERK signaling reverses chemoresistance and suppresses CSC-like traits, identifying PLK1 as an actionable node linking EMT and stemness [169].

Chemotherapy itself can paradoxically induce stemness through stress-adaptive signaling. In colon cancer, 5-FU triggers autolysosome-dependent degradation of TMPO, leading to ERK-mediated phosphorylation of c-Fos. Activated c-Fos transcriptionally upregulates NANOG, promoting CSC reprogramming and acquired drug resistance [170]. Cell-surface signaling hubs further stabilize EMT–stemness coupling. Claudin-1 directly binds and stabilizes the receptor tyrosine kinase EPHA2 via its PDZ-binding motif, enhancing AKT signaling and CD44 expression. This CLDN1-EPHA2 axis sustains tumor-initiating capacity and chemoresistance [171].

Hypoxia-responsive pathways integrate EMT and stemness with metabolic adaptation. USP51 drives CRC stemness and drug resistance by deubiquitinating and stabilizing HIF1α within the VHL-CUL2 E3 ligase complex. Hypoxia-induced HIF1α, in turn, transcriptionally upregulates USP51, forming a positive feedback loop reinforced by SENP1-mediated deSUMOylation of ELOC that sustains aggressive, therapy-resistant phenotypes [172].

Slow-cycling and persister states

Beyond classical CSCs, a subset of CRC cells adopts a slow-cycling, drug-tolerant persister (DTP) state that enables survival under chemotherapy. Tumor relapse has been linked to a DPPA3-HIF1α positive feedback loop that enforces cellular quiescence by epigenetically repressing FOXM1 and delaying cell-cycle progression. Disruption of this axis restores proliferative capacity and chemosensitivity, identifying DPPA3 as both a regulator and biomarker of tumor persistence [173].

Stress-adaptive pathways further regulate DTP survival. The ERO1α/miR-451a/ARF1 axis modulates endoplasmic reticulum stress and 5-FU responsiveness; inhibition of ERO1α activates ER stress signaling and reverses 5-FU resistance [174]. Metabolic rewiring also supports persister states. CDH17 maintains CRC stemness by sustaining LGR5 expression and Wnt/MYC signaling while promoting chemoresistance through upregulation of the glutamine transporter SLC38A5. Targeting CDH17 or SLC38A5 disrupts nutrient support for CSCs and markedly enhances sensitivity to 5-FU and irinotecan [175].

Collectively, these findings highlight CSCs and DTP cells as dynamic, therapy-evasive states driven by non-genetic plasticity rather than fixed mutations. Transcriptional rewiring, EMT-stemness interconversion, metabolic–epigenetic coupling, and stress-adaptive quiescence jointly enable CRC cells to withstand cytotoxic and targeted therapies. Importantly, these states are reversible, suggesting that therapeutic strategies targeting CSC regulators, EMT-stemness circuits, metabolic dependencies, or persister-specific vulnerabilities-particularly in rational combinations, may prevent relapse and achieve more durable responses in CRC.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), immunosuppressive myeloid and lymphoid populations, endothelial and perivascular niches, and microbiota collectively establish protective circuits that blunt drug efficacy, promote tumor cell plasticity, and enable relapse (Table 2). Tumor microenvironment-mediated mechanisms of drug resistance in colorectal cancer.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts and stromal protection

CAFs constitute a major stromal component that supports CRC survival through ECM remodeling, metabolic support, and secretion of resistance-promoting factors. CAF-driven CRC-CAF crosstalk reinforces ECM accumulation and metabolic reprogramming to promote chemoresistance, while hepatic stellate cell (HSC)-derived CAFs can further shape a pro-metastatic niche in the liver. Notably, nanoparticles co-delivering oxaliplatin and EGCG disrupt this crosstalk and reprogram the metastatic microenvironment, simultaneously enhancing chemosensitivity and suppressing liver metastasis [176].

CAF-derived soluble mediators can directly establish drug-tolerant niches. Chemotherapy-activated CAFs secrete high levels of DKK1, which drives immunosuppressive remodeling by promoting MDSC infiltration and suppressing NK-cell activity, thereby mediating acquired resistance to 5-FU and other agents. DKK1 blockade restores chemosensitivity in vivo, highlighting the CAF-immune axis as a key stromal resistance program [177]. Spatially resolved analyses further refine CAF heterogeneity in resistance: single-cell and spatial transcriptomic profiling identified THBS2⁺ CAFs as key drivers of oxaliplatin resistance, where THBS2⁺ CAF–secreted COL8A1 activates ITGB1-PI3K-AKT signaling in malignant cells to promote EMT and drug resistance; this phenotype is reversible by ITGB1 knockdown or AKT inhibition [178].

A prominent stromal mechanism involves metabolic coupling. CAFs with activated glycolysis secrete lactate, which induces histone lactylation and stabilizes ANTXR1 in CRC cells, thereby activating RhoC/ROCK1/SMAD5 signaling, enhancing stemness, and promoting oxaliplatin resistance. Inhibition of lactate shuttling or ANTXR1 signaling improves oxaliplatin sensitivity [179]. Beyond chemotherapy resistance, CAF-driven inflammatory remodeling can also underlie anti-angiogenic therapy failure: CCL24 is highly upregulated in CRC liver metastases and bevacizumab-resistant tumors, where it promotes inflammatory fibroblast formation and resistance; silencing CCL24 restores bevacizumab sensitivity [180]. In parallel, tumor-intrinsic factors can directly blunt anti-VEGF efficacy, as SNCG forms a ternary complex with VEGF and VEGFR2 to enhance VEGFR2 phosphorylation and angiogenesis, reducing bevacizumab sensitivity; SNCG knockout or anti-SNCG antibody restores therapeutic efficacy [181].

Although these CAF-centered programs are often framed as paracrine resistance, they frequently converge on chromatin regulation and transcriptional reprogramming within tumor cells. For example, histone H3K23 succinylation is enriched in 5-FU-resistant cells and cooperates with FOSL2 and KLF6 to recruit PCAF-p300/CBP, driving SEMA3C expression and activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling to sustain 5-FU resistance [182]. Thus, stromal protection not only shields tumor cells but also helps “write” durable resistant transcriptional states.

Immune suppression and immune escape

Immune suppression is a major determinant of resistance to both chemotherapy and immunotherapy, particularly in microsatellite-stable (MSS) CRC. Specific myeloid and lymphoid populations can directly restrict effector T-cell recruitment and function. CCL7⁺ tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) represent an immunosuppressive population linked to poor ICI response; CCL7 reprograms macrophage metabolism toward fatty-acid oxidation (FAO) and suppresses CXCL10 production, thereby limiting CD8⁺ T-cell recruitment and reducing PD-L1 therapy efficacy [183]. More broadly, resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) can be reinforced by Treg- and MDSC-mediated immune suppression. ATAPL1, a PD-L1 × TNFR2 bispecific antibody, reduces immunosuppressive myeloid and regulatory T-cell populations while enhancing cytotoxic T-cell function, markedly improving therapeutic efficacy [184].

Although MSI-H/dMMR CRC demonstrates robust and durable responses to immune checkpoint blockade, approximately 80–85% of CRC cases are microsatellite stable and exhibit primary resistance to immunotherapy [26]. MSS CRC therefore represents the predominant clinical scenario and the central translational challenge in CRC immunotherapy. Unlike MSI-H tumors, which are characterized by high tumor mutational burden (TMB), abundant neoantigen generation, and pre-existing T-cell infiltration, MSS tumors typically display an immune-excluded or immune-desert phenotype with low baseline immunogenicity [185].

Several intrinsic and microenvironmental features collectively shape this resistant landscape. First, the relatively low TMB and limited neoantigen repertoire in MSS CRC reduce spontaneous priming of tumor-specific T cells [186]. Second, impaired activation of innate immune sensing pathways, including attenuated cGAS–STING signaling and diminished type I interferon responses, restrict dendritic cell recruitment and antigen presentation [187]. Aberrant oncogenic pathways such as WNT/β-catenin signaling further suppress dendritic cell trafficking into the TME, preventing effective T-cell priming and infiltration. In addition to insufficient immune activation, active immune exclusion mechanisms are prominent in MSS CRC. TGF-β–driven stromal remodeling promotes ECM deposition and forms a physical and biochemical barrier that impedes cytotoxic T-cell penetration. CAFs reinforce this exclusionary niche by secreting immunosuppressive cytokines and sustaining EMT-associated programs linked to immune resistance. Concurrent enrichment of MDSCs, M2-like TAMs, and Tregs further establishes a profoundly immunosuppressive milieu. Metabolic competition mediated by enhanced tumor glycolysis, lactate accumulation, arginine depletion, and altered lipid metabolism exacerbates CD8⁺ T-cell dysfunction and promotes exhaustion [188].

KAT6A represses cGAS-STING-mediated interferon signaling via a c-MYC/DNMT1-dependent mechanism, limiting CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and contributing to poor immunotherapy responsiveness; targeting KAT6A may synergize with ICIs [189]. PIN1 promotes Treg recruitment and CAF activation via an NF-κB-CCL3-CCR5 axis, restricting CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and driving resistance to PD-1 blockade in MSS CRC [190]. BRD4 transcriptionally activates Serpine1/PAI-1 to promote M2-like TAM polarization and oxaliplatin resistance, and disruption of the BRD4/PAI-1 axis improves chemosensitivity and antitumor immunity [191]. In radiotherapy contexts, ATM limits ROS-mediated mitochondrial damage and suppresses STING-dependent antitumor immunity; ATM inhibition enhances radiosensitivity and synergizes with anti-PD-L1 therapy [192].

Given this multilayered immune dysfunction, checkpoint inhibition alone is often insufficient in MSS CRC [193]. Accordingly, multiple combinatorial strategies are being explored to convert “cold” MSS tumors into immunologically active states. Combination regimens incorporating ICIs with cytotoxic chemotherapy or radiotherapy aim to induce immunogenic cell death and enhance antigen release. Anti-angiogenic agents may normalize tumor vasculature and improve immune cell infiltration, providing a rationale for PD-1/PD-L1 plus anti-VEGF combinations [194]. Dual blockade of PD-1 and TGF-β signaling seeks to simultaneously relieve immune suppression and disrupt stromal exclusion. Additional approaches under investigation include MEK inhibition to enhance antigen presentation, STING agonists or oncolytic viruses to stimulate innate immune activation, bispecific antibodies to bypass defective endogenous priming, and personalized neoantigen vaccines [195].

CRC-specific senescence signature (CSS) impairs MHC-I antigen presentation and CD8⁺ T-cell function, predicting poor prognosis and resistance to immunotherapy; senolytic targeting exemplified by afatinib reduces tumor aggressiveness in CSS-high CRC [196]. Tumor-intrinsic metabolic and nucleotide pathways can similarly suppress immune activity: high cytidine deaminase (CDA) expression promotes UDP release that activates P2Y6 signaling in TAMs, establishing an immunosuppressive milieu and impairing anti-PD-1 responses [197]. Despite these emerging strategies, substantial translational challenges remain. MSS CRC exhibits pronounced intertumoral and intratumoral heterogeneity, complicating biomarker development and patient stratification. Many combination regimens increase toxicity without guaranteeing durable immune conversion, and the optimal timing and sequencing of immune activation relative to chemotherapy or targeted therapy remain unresolved [57,198]. Therefore, overcoming immune resistance in MSS CRC will likely require biomarker-guided, rationally sequenced, and ecosystem-oriented therapeutic designs that integrate tumor-intrinsic targeting with microenvironmental remodeling. Conversely, some chemotherapy-induced immune programs can enhance treatment response: tumor-derived IL-33 activates ST2-annexin A1 signaling to orchestrate robust CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T-cell responses, synergizing with chemotherapy to induce tumor apoptosis [199]. Oxaliplatin-induced TXNIP can also restrict Treg differentiation by negatively regulating GDF15, promoting CD8⁺ T-cell activity; loss of this TXNIP-GDF15 responsiveness correlates with advanced disease and chemoresistance [200].

At the level of immune response biomarkers, scRNA-seq analyses indicate that responders and non-responders exhibit distinct immune landscapes. C1QC⁺ resident tissue macrophages are enriched in ICI responders across MSI and MSS metastatic CRC, and their MHC-II-dependent interaction with CD4+ T cells distinguishes responders; in non-responders, CAFs disrupt this macrophage-T-cell interaction [201]. Additional single-cell analyses of treated CRC further show that non-responders are characterized by TMEs enriched with pericyte-like fibroblasts and exhausted CD8 T cells, whereas responders show dendritic cell-dominant landscapes, emphasizing TME composition as a determinant of therapy response [202]. Mechanistically, primary ICI resistance in MSI-H/dMMR metastatic CRC can be driven by IL-1β-dependent accumulation of immunosuppressive MDSCs, suggesting IL-1β-targeted strategies to overcome resistance [203].

Immune escape also interacts with other therapeutic modalities. Chemoresistant CRC cells exhibit reduced sensitivity to oncolytic viruses due to TBK1-driven type I interferon activation that impairs viral replication; TBK1 inhibition enhances OV efficacy by reprogramming the immune microenvironment and activating NK cells via ICAM-1 upregulation [204]. In adoptive cell therapy contexts, LPS-induced resistance to CAR-T therapy is mediated by TLR4/MYD88-driven TGF-β secretion that enhances stemness; targeting TGF-β and MYD88 may improve CAR-T efficacy [205].

Exosome-mediated and cytokine-driven resistance circuits

Beyond cell composition, the TME enables resistance through intercellular communication mediated by exosomes and cytokine feedback loops. In chemoresistant CRC cells, RAB22A upregulation increases exosome biogenesis by preventing PKM2 ubiquitination and promoting SNAP-23 phosphorylation, enabling exosome-mediated transfer of resistance traits to neighboring tumor cells [206]. Under nutrient stress, CRC cells secrete Rab11a-positive exosomes carrying amphiregulin (AREG); exosome-bound AREG competes effectively with cetuximab for EGFR binding and sustains EGFR-KRAS signaling, inducing non-genetic cetuximab resistance in KRAS-wild-type CRC [207]. Tumor-derived circRNAs can also remodel immune landscapes, as circPHLPP2 promotes immune evasion by inducing IL36γ transcription via ILF3 nuclear accumulation, reducing NK-cell infiltration and effector function in anti-PD-1-resistant CRC [208].

Cytokine and metabolite feedback circuits can stabilize resistant niches. Chemotherapy-induced macrophage-tumor crosstalk upregulates CXCL7 in TAMs, driving CXCR2-STAT1 activation and PHGDH-dependent serine/SAM metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells; tumor-secreted SAM then promotes M2 polarization, forming a CXCL7-SAM positive feedback loop that sustains the chemoresistant niche [209]. In another macrophage-driven mechanism, TAMs promote 5-FU resistance by enhancing MRP1-dependent drug efflux through a CCL17/CCL22-CCR4-PI3K-AKT cascade. AKT-mediated ER calcium remodeling activates ATF6-GRP78, driving MRP1 membrane translocation [210]. Hypoxia is a major upstream regulator of immune exclusion: HIF-1α represses the CXCL9/10/11-CXCR3 axis, thereby limiting CD8⁺ T-cell trafficking. In contrast, therapeutic inhibition of HIF-1α restores chemokine signaling, enhances CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, and sensitizes tumors to chemotherapy [211]. Metabolic lactate signaling provides another axis of autocrine-paracrine reinforcement: ALDOB rewires bioenergetics through PDK1-dependent lactagenesis, with lactate promoting LDHB expression and CEACAM6 stabilization via lysine lactylation, thereby driving proliferation and chemotherapy resistance [212].

Microbiota-driven therapeutic resistance

The gut microbiota and intratumoral bacteria can act as external drivers of both chemoresistance and immunotherapy failure by modulating tumor signaling and immune composition. Bacteroides fragilis promotes chemotherapy resistance by activating Notch1 signaling via its surface protein SusD/RagB, inducing EMT and stemness; phage targeting of B. fragilis restores chemosensitivity, supporting microbiota manipulation as a therapeutic strategy [213]. Fusobacterium nucleatum–associated CRC displays chemoresistance and an immunosuppressive microenvironment that limits oxaliplatin efficacy. A multifunctional nanodrug integrating metformin, oxaliplatin, and lauric acid eradicates intra- and extracellular F. nucleatum, restores chemosensitivity, and remodels the TME by inducing immunogenic cell death and promoting M1 polarization [214]. Mechanistically, F. nucleatum can also promote oxaliplatin resistance through TLR4/NF-κB-dependent induction of the lncRNA PVT1, which stabilizes ATAD3A and suppresses ER stress-mediated cell death [215].

Microbiota can also confer resistance to targeted therapies by activating bypass signaling. Peptostreptococcus stomatis adheres to CRC cells via an FBA-integrin α6/β4 interaction and activates ERBB2-MEK-ERK signaling, blunting responses to EGFR inhibitors in KRAS-wild-type CRC and BRAF inhibitors in BRAFV600E-mutant CRC [216]. Similarly, Peptostreptococcus anaerobius induces anti-PD-1 resistance by promoting CXCR2⁺ MDSC accumulation and function through integrin α2β1-NF-κB-CXCL1 signaling and direct enhancement of MDSC immunosuppression via lytC(22)-Slamf4 signaling [217].

Together, these findings show that TME-mediated resistance arises through layered protection mechanisms: CAF-driven stromal shielding and metabolic coupling, immune suppression and immune exclusion driven by myeloid/Treg programs, exosome- and cytokine-based feedback circuits, and microbiota-driven rewiring of tumor signaling and immune composition. These processes are often reversible and thus represent actionable vulnerabilities. Rational strategies may include stromal disruption, immune reprogramming, disruption of exosome-dependent resistance transfer, and microbiota-targeted interventions. Ultimately, integrating tumor-intrinsic targeting with ecosystem-level remodeling may be required to overcome non-genetic resistance and achieve durable responses in CRC.

Evolutionary Dynamics of Drug Resistance

Accumulating evidence indicates that therapeutic resistance in CRC is a dynamic and evolutionary process shaped by treatment-induced selective pressures rather than fixed genetic alterations. Serial molecular monitoring has provided critical insights into how resistant clones emerge, expand, and, in some cases, can be therapeutically eliminated.

In BRAF V600E-mutant colon cancer, serial circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) profiling revealed rapid clonal evolution during combined BRAF/MEK/EGFR inhibition, with MET amplification emerging as a dominant resistance mechanism. Notably, sequential administration of chemotherapy selectively eradicated the MET-amplified resistant clone and restored sensitivity to BRAF/EGFR inhibition, demonstrating a lack of cross-resistance between these therapeutic modalities. These findings underscore the clinical utility of cfDNA-guided treatment adaptation and support rational sequential combination strategies informed by real-time clonal dynamics [218].

The impact of oncogenic context on resistance evolution is further illustrated in dMMR/MSI metastatic CRC. In this setting, the presence of BRAF V600E does not appear to significantly affect overall progression-free survival (PFS) or overall survival (OS) in patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) across all treatment lines. However, when ICIs are administered as first-line therapy, BRAF V600E is associated with an increased incidence of secondary resistance, resulting in shorter PFS and OS beyond six months of treatment. These findings suggest that BRAF V600E influences the durability, rather than the initial efficacy, of immune checkpoint blockade in MSI-high CRC [219].

Adaptive mutational escape has also been documented under direct RAS pathway inhibition. KRAS-mutant CRC cells initially respond to the pan-RAS(ON) inhibitor RMC-7977 but subsequently acquire resistance through specific adaptive mutations, including KRAS Y71H and RAF1 S257L. These alterations directly restore ERK signaling and abrogate drug sensitivity, highlighting the capacity of MAPK pathway components to evolve under targeted pressure [220].

Similarly, anti-EGFR therapy exerts strong selective pressure on CRC tumors, leading to the emergence of concurrent resistance mutations in EGFR and MAPK pathway genes. These mutations occur preferentially in tumors with high baseline EGFR ligand expression and frequently involve the extracellular domain of EGFR. Importantly, these findings reveal a ligand-dependent reliance on EGFR/MAPK signaling and link chromosome 8q/MYC amplification to the development of anti-EGFR resistance [221].

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that resistance in CRC is governed by adaptive clonal evolution driven by therapy-specific selective pressures. Integrating longitudinal molecular monitoring, particularly cfDNA-based approaches, with rational sequencing or a combination of therapies offers a promising strategy to anticipate resistance, exploit non-cross-resistant vulnerabilities, and prolong treatment efficacy.

Therapeutic Implications and Strategies to Overcome Resistance

The diverse resistance mechanisms described above, ranging from epigenetic and transcriptional plasticity to cancer stemness, clonal evolution, and TME remodeling, underscore the need for therapeutic strategies that move beyond single-agent targeting [222]. Rather than fixed genetic determinants, resistance in CRC frequently arises from adaptive, reversible, and context-dependent processes. This insight has profound implications for treatment design, favoring rational combination, sequential therapy, adaptive targeting of signaling bottlenecks, and ecosystem-level intervention.

Rational combination and sequential therapies

One promising strategy to overcome adaptive resistance is the rational sequencing or combination of therapies that exploit treatment-induced vulnerabilities. Chemotherapy itself can generate such vulnerabilities. For example, cytotoxic treatment induces upregulation of CLDN1 in CRC cells via activation of the p38–GSK3β-Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, conferring apoptosis resistance and driving acquired chemoresistance. Importantly, sequential targeting of this chemotherapy-induced dependency using an anti-CLDN1 antibody–drug conjugate effectively overcomes resistance and enhances therapeutic efficacy, illustrating how therapy-induced adaptations can be therapeutically exploited rather than avoided [223].

These findings support a broader paradigm in which resistance is not viewed as a terminal state but as a transient, targetable condition. Sequential strategies, such as cytotoxic therapy followed by targeted elimination of drug-tolerant states, or targeted therapy followed by chemotherapy to eradicate resistant clones, may outperform upfront combinations by minimizing selective pressure and exploiting non–cross-resistant vulnerabilities.

Targeting adaptive bottlenecks

Adaptive rewiring of signaling networks under drug pressure represents a central mechanism of resistance in CRC. While tumors can bypass individual pathway inhibitors, they often converge on a limited set of adaptive bottlenecks required for survival. Identifying these bottlenecks requires dynamic, systems-level approaches rather than static genomic profiling.

Network-based phosphoproteomic frameworks such as VESPA enable time-resolved inference of kinase and phosphatase activity during drug exposure, capturing transient adaptive responses that precede overt resistance. By revealing how signaling networks reorganize in response to therapy, such approaches provide a rational basis for selecting synergistic drug combinations or optimal treatment sequences that preempt adaptive escape [224]. Importantly, this strategy shifts therapeutic design from pathway-centric inhibition to network-level constraint, which may be particularly effective against non-genetic resistance.

Pharmacological inhibition of drug efflux transporters

Drug efflux transporters constitute a classical but still clinically relevant resistance mechanism, particularly in tumors with stem-like or metabolically adapted phenotypes. ABCB1 (P-glycoprotein) overexpression limits intracellular drug accumulation and contributes to multidrug resistance in CRC. ML210 selectively inhibits ABCB1 transport activity by directly binding its substrate pocket, restoring intracellular levels of chemotherapeutic agents without affecting ABCB1 expression or interfering with related transporters such as ABCG2. This specificity enables effective reversal of multidrug resistance while minimizing off-target effects [225].

Targeting such cellular defense mechanisms is especially relevant when combined with therapies that induce stemness or stress-adaptive states, as these programs frequently rely on enhanced efflux capacity for survival.

Biomarkers for resistance prediction

Given the dynamic nature of resistance, predictive and real-time biomarkers are essential for guiding therapeutic decisions. Longitudinal monitoring approaches, such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), exosomal RNA, and immune or stromal signatures, offer opportunities to detect emerging resistance before clinical progression [226]. Although biomarker development is still evolving, integrating molecular monitoring with adaptive treatment strategies holds promise for identifying optimal treatment windows, selecting appropriate combinations, and avoiding ineffective therapies.

Importantly, biomarkers should not only predict resistance but also inform actionable interventions, linking detection of adaptive states to specific therapeutic responses.

Advanced drug delivery and precision therapeutics

Innovative drug delivery systems can overcome pharmacokinetic limitations, reduce systemic toxicity, and selectively target resistant tumor compartments. A dual-responsive KGM-PTX/CSM oral delivery system exemplifies this approach by enabling colon-targeted, ROS-triggered release of paclitaxel-based micelles. This platform enhances cytotoxicity, particularly in drug-resistant CRC cells, while reducing systemic toxicity. In vivo, it synergistically suppresses tumor growth by combining chemotherapy with inflammation reduction, reversal of drug resistance, immune activation, and restoration of gut microbiota homeostasis [227].

Similarly, conjugation of gemcitabine to an ENO1-targeting peptide improves tumor selectivity and efficacy in colon cancer. The resulting conjugate demonstrates enhanced cytotoxicity toward cancer cells, minimal effects on normal colorectal tissue, and acid-responsive drug release in vivo, highlighting the potential of precision conjugation strategies to expand the therapeutic index of conventional chemotherapeutics [228].

Remodeling the tumor ecosystem

A unifying theme emerging from resistance research is that durable therapeutic responses cannot be achieved by targeting tumor cells in isolation. Instead, resistance is often sustained by a permissive tumor ecosystem comprising CAFs, immunosuppressive myeloid populations, metabolic coupling, cytokine feedback loops, and microbiota-derived signals [229].

Therapeutic strategies that remodel this ecosystem, by disrupting stromal protection, reprogramming immune suppression, normalizing metabolic crosstalk, or targeting microbiota-driven resistance, represent a critical frontier in CRC treatment. Rather than directly killing tumor cells, these approaches aim to collapse the supportive niches that enable drug tolerance and relapse, thereby restoring sensitivity to existing therapies [230,231].

From this perspective, effective resistance management will likely require multi-layered interventions: targeting tumor-intrinsic adaptive programs, constraining signaling bottlenecks, and simultaneously reshaping the surrounding microenvironment. Such ecosystem-aware strategies may transform resistance from an inevitable outcome into a controllable, and potentially reversible, therapeutic challenge.

Future perspectives: Overcoming therapeutic resistance in colorectal cancer

Overview and challenges

Therapeutic resistance remains the central challenge in achieving durable remission and curative outcomes for CRC, particularly in metastatic settings where it accounts for nearly 90% of treatment failures [42,232]. This resistance is driven by a complex interplay of genetic and epigenetic alterations within the tumor cells and the dynamic remodeling of the TME under therapeutic pressure. CRC evolves in response to treatment, and the emergence of resistant subclones or de novo mutations often compromises initial therapeutic responses [233]. Current "one-size-fits-all" static treatment strategies often fail to accommodate the dynamic evolution of resistance mechanisms.

The key to overcoming CRC resistance lies in developing adaptive therapies that can respond to the tumor’s dynamic evolution. This requires a shift away from one-size-fits-all approaches towards more personalized, real-time treatment regimens guided by continuous monitoring of tumor biology and the TME. Achieving durable therapeutic responses will require integrated strategies that address both intrinsic tumor resistance mechanisms and the tumor-supportive functions of the TME.

Overcoming the core challenges

To address these multifaceted challenges, a synergistic approach integrating cutting-edge technologies and targeted strategies is required. Below is a detailed discussion of the core solutions and innovative directions:

One of the most promising innovations in this area is the integration of AI-driven predictive models with multi-omics data [234]. By incorporating ctDNA sequencing and other real-time monitoring technologies, these models can track tumor evolution and anticipate resistance patterns. This will enable clinicians to adapt treatment strategies earlier, reducing the risk of relapse and improving patient survival rates. For example, the phase II DYNAMIC trial demonstrated that ctDNA-guided adaptive therapy significantly improved progression-free survival in metastatic CRC patients, and AI models can further optimize such therapeutic adjustments by integrating multi-omics data to predict clonal evolution [235,236]. Beyond predicting resistance, AI-driven models also facilitate the optimization of combination therapies, laying the foundation for personalized treatment regimens.

Another major challenge in CRC treatment is the persistence and phenotypic plasticity of CSCs, which are key drivers of therapeutic resistance and tumor recurrence. These cells exhibit remarkable plasticity and can switch between stem-like and differentiated states in response to therapeutic pressure, making them difficult to target. Epigenetic modulation of the pathways that sustain CSC stemness, such as Wnt, Notch, and Hedgehog signaling, represents an important strategy to prevent or overcome resistance [237]. By targeting these signaling networks and using epigenetic inhibitors, we can reduce the self-renewal capacity of CSCs and make them more susceptible to conventional therapies, improving long-term patient outcomes.

Moreover, the TME plays a crucial role in CRC resistance by providing a supportive niche for tumor cells, fostering drug resistance through immune suppression and ECM remodeling. CAFs, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs) contribute to this resistance by promoting immune evasion, increasing drug efflux, and inducing EMT in tumor cells [237]. Strategies aimed at reprogramming the TME to enhance anti-tumor immunity and improve drug delivery will be essential. Combining immune ICIs with agents that target CAFs, TAMs, or Tregs could significantly enhance therapeutic responses, particularly in cases where conventional treatments have failed.

The metabolic plasticity of CRC cells, characterized by their reliance on aerobic glycolysis and glutaminolysis, is another key contributor to resistance. Tumor cells can adapt their metabolism to survive under therapeutic stress, bypassing the effects of therapies that target a single metabolic pathway. To overcome this, combination therapies that target multiple metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis and glutaminolysis, could prevent the compensatory rewiring of metabolic networks and enhance the effectiveness of existing therapies. For instance, combining HK2 inhibitors with GLS1 inhibitors has shown synergistic efficacy in preclinical CRC models by blocking compensatory metabolic rewiring [238]. Targeting specific metabolic enzymes, such as HK2 or GLS1, holds great promise in sensitizing CRC cells to chemotherapy.

In addition, overcoming immune resistance in CRC, particularly in microsatellite-stable (MSS) tumors, remains one of the most significant challenges. Despite the success of ICIs in other cancer types, MSS CRC tumors generally exhibit poor responses. New approaches to overcoming this resistance could include targeting TAMs to reprogram the immune microenvironment, as well as introducing novel immune agonists like STING or OX40 agonists to stimulate T-cell responses [239]. Ongoing clinical trials evaluating the combination of anti-PD-L1 antibodies and TGF-β inhibitors have shown promising response rates in MSS CRC, providing new hope for overcoming immune resistance. Personalized cancer vaccines targeting tumor-specific neoantigens are also gaining attention as a potential method for overcoming immune resistance and improving the efficacy of ICIs.

The gut microbiome, which plays a significant role in CRC progression and treatment response, is another area of increasing interest. It has been shown to influence the immune system and modulate the effectiveness of chemotherapy and immunotherapy [240]. Future therapies may involve modulating the microbiome through the use of probiotics, prebiotics, or even fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) to enhance treatment responses and reduce toxicity. By incorporating microbiome profiling into personalized treatment strategies, we can better predict individual responses to therapy and potentially overcome some forms of resistance.

In addition to intrinsic tumor characteristics and microenvironmental factors, the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of CRC further complicates therapeutic efficacy, representing another critical barrier to overcoming resistance. Tumor heterogeneity exists not only between different clones but also across different regions of the tumor and over time. Multi-region sequencing and longitudinal monitoring are essential for understanding the dynamic evolution of CRC and identifying potential resistance mechanisms. The concept of "adaptive evolutionary therapy," in which treatment regimens are dynamically adjusted to maintain tumors at a clinically manageable size while preventing the emergence of resistant clones, may offer a new way forward [241]. By using low-dose or alternating therapies, we can control sensitive clones and suppress the growth of resistant populations, treating the tumor as a chronic disease rather than aiming for complete eradication.

Conclusion

In conclusion, overcoming therapeutic resistance in CRC will require a comprehensive, integrated approach that combines real-time monitoring, molecular profiling, immune modulation, and targeted therapies. By utilizing AI to predict tumor evolution, advancing our understanding of CSCs and the TME, exploiting metabolic vulnerabilities, and modulating the microbiome, we can develop more personalized and effective treatments. The ultimate goal is to shift from a reactive approach to one that anticipates, prevents, and manages resistance proactively, offering hope for long-term, manageable treatment outcomes and improving patient survival.
