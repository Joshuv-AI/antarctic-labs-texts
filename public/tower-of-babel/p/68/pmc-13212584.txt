Inhibitory effect of epigallocatechin-3-gallate as a potent anti-amyloidogenic agent against the G138E mutant of SOD1
Authors: Zahra Sheikhpour, Bagher Seyedalipour, Farangis Ataei, Payam Baziyar, Mona Akhlaghi, Saman Hosseinkhani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SOD1 misfolding leads to protein aggregation, which is a common feature of neurodegenerative diseases such as ALS. The effect of epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) as a potent anti-amyloidogenic polyphenol on the G138E-SOD1 mutant was investigated using computational/experimental approaches. MD simulation results (RMSD, RMSF, Rg, SASA, PCA, and FEL) showed that EGCG binding stabilizes the mutant in a structure closer to the native structure, which was consistent with the FTIR and DSSP results. Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy calculated Ksv and Kq values as 1.8 × 104 M−1 and 5.8 × 1012 M−1s−1, respectively, indicating the participation of a static quenching mechanism. TEM images provide compelling evidence for the potential inhibitory effect of EGCG on protein aggregates in the G138E mutant, confirming the results of the ThT assay. DLS results showed a reduction in the size of aggregated particles in the presence of 80 μM EGCG, confirming the inhibition of amyloid aggregation by this compound. Finally, MTT assay on SH-SY5Y cells showed that cell survival in the presence of SOD1-G138E aggregates was approximately 40%, which increased to 60% with the addition of 80 μM EGCG. Taken together, this study suggests that EGCG may inhibit amyloid aggregation and reduce cytotoxicity by affecting nucleation and structural stabilization, making it a promising compound for ALS therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

A neurological condition known as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) gradually damages motor neurons, reducing movement. Both the lower motor neurons in the spinal cord’s ventral horn and the upper motor neurons of the motor cortex and brainstem sustain this damage1. Although the precise cause of ALS is unclear, a combination of lifestyle, environmental, and genetic factors are believed to be responsible. The majority of ALS patients succumb to respiratory failure within 3 to 5 years post-onset due to the destruction of the neurons regulating the respiratory muscles2. 90% of cases of ALS develop randomly (sALS), and 10% develop familially (fALS), with point mutations in the gene encoding the antioxidant enzyme Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) primarily responsible for the remaining 10%3. Only 20% of cases are linked to familial ALS (fALS), despite the fact that more than 230 mutations in the SOD1 gene have been identified in fALS patients to far4. The 32-kDa metalloenzyme known as human SOD1 is found in homodimers. The structure has four highly conserved cysteine residues and an eight-stranded Greek-key β-barrel shape5. Loop IV consists of a highly stable intramolecular disulfide linkage between Cys57 in loop IV and Cys146 in a β-strand within the core β-barrel of SOD16, which facilitates the coordination of copper and zinc ions at the active site7. Pathogenic mutations associated with ALS cause a significant reduction in protein stability by impairing folding8, leading to metal ion loss, net charge change9,10, cleavage of the disulfide bond, and dissociation of dimers—processes that promote aggregation and filament formation in disease states11–14. Currently, no effective treatment is available to halt or reverse ALS progression, primarily due to the unclear mechanisms underlying its pathogenesis15,16. While numerous clinical trials have aimed at developing therapeutic options for ALS, only four drugs have received FDA approval: Riluzole, Edaravone, Relyvrio17 and Tofersen18. However, Relyvrio was voluntarily withdrawn after failing to demonstrate effectiveness in a Phase 3 trial17. The authorized medications and current therapies for ALS primarily address symptom management and alleviation; they do not reduce the disease’s development or cure it19. Since ALS is a complex neurological condition, there is a significant unmet medical need for novel, efficient therapies. Research into pharmacological combinations or medications that target several therapeutic targets may be fruitful. A vast number of studies provide unquestionable support for the idea that one effective therapeutic strategy against SOD1-associated ALS is to prevent SOD1 misfolding or aggregation. These strategies include preventing SOD1 demetallation, stabilizing the native dimer of SOD1, inhibiting the prion-like propagation of SOD1, and promoting the formation of SOD1 intra-subunit disulphide bonds.

Natural plant compounds are currently opening up new avenues for the development of drugs to treat neurodegenerative illnesses. The therapeutic potential of natural compounds made from plants in the treatment of neurodegenerative illnesses, such as Huntington’s disease (HD), Parkinson’s disease (PD)20, and Alzheimer’s disease (AD)21,22, has been thoroughly investigate23,24. Licochalcone-A, Hesperetin and Curcumin, as a neuroprotective drug, have shown beneficial effects on a number of neurodegenerative diseases, including AD25–27. These processes include anti-inflammatory effects, oxidative stress reduction28, protein aggregation inhibition, and other neuroprotective techniques29. By modifying protein misfolding and aggregation, small compounds are thought to expand novel prospects in the field of therapeutic development in neurodegenerative disorders30,31. There are several examples in the literature of small compounds that are said to prevent SOD1 from misfolding and aggregating32–35. Stabilization of the SOD1 dimer interface is proposed as the mechanism of inhibition for some small compounds36,37. To combat protein aggregation, a variety of treatment strategies have been created and put into practice38. The use of small compounds to target the several intermediates that form throughout the microscopic stages of the aggregation process is becoming more and more popular, with an emphasis on developing potent inhibitors33. Low molecular weight substances with several important biological characteristics, such as polyphenols, flavonoids, etc., have become potential anti-aggregating and disaggregating agents in recent years32. The hydrophobic and aromatic residues of amyloidogenic proteins may interfere with the phenolic hydroxyl groups and aromatic rings of flavonoids or polyphenols, respectively, to stop proteins from self-association39. Numerous biochemical targets linked to neurodegeneration can be modulated by some naturally occurring plant compounds, especially those in the polyphenol class. Among these phytochemicals is Epigallocatechin Gallate (EGCG), which interacts directly with misfolded proteins to prevent amyloid formation40,41. In addition to its anti-bacterial, anti-cancer, anti-thrombotic, cardioprotective, and neuroprotective properties, EGCG is a naturally occurring flavonoid glycoside42,43. Its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties are principally responsible for these biological processes. Green tea contains six primary Catechin compounds: Gallocatechin, Catechin, Epicatechin (EC), epicatechin gallate (ECG), epigallocatechin (EGC), and EGCG, the most active component. Gallic acid (3,4,5-trihydroxybenzoic acid) and epicatechin gallate (EC) are two well studied derivatives42. The four rings that make up EGCG, an ortho-benzoyl benzopyran byproduct, are designated A, B, C, and D (Fig. 1). With a gallate group at C3 and a phenyl group at C2, the benzopyran ring is represented by A and C. The D ring galloyl moiety is set up as an ester at C3, while the B ring contains positional 3,4,5-trihydroxyl groups. SOD1 mutations have different effects on ALS depending on their location. Among them, we focused on the c.416G > A p.G138E mutation located in the electrostatic loop (loop VII). The median survival time of ALS patients carrying this mutation is 18 months45. The idea that negative charge alterations and other destabilizing variables contribute to increased protein aggregation by decreasing repulsive negative charges was supported by the results of earlier research46. Several mutations, such as L126S, G138E, N139D, N139H, G141A, and G141E, have been reported to cause destabilizing and deleterious effects on the SOD1 protein47. Therefore, targeting the G138E mutant could be a good choice for understanding the mechanism of SOD1 pathology in ALS. Subsequently, EGCG was tested as a potent anti-amyloidogenic polyphenol to analyze its effect on G138E mutant amyloid fibrils. These studies were conducted to answer the question of how EGCG inhibits G138E mutant amyloid aggregation and how different concentrations of EGCG alter the aggregation and inhibition mechanisms. Answering these questions using combined (computational/experimental) approaches could be essential to designing a strategy regarding the potential effect of this phytochemical to achieve the goals. This includes: (I) elucidating the molecular mechanism by which EGCG inhibits ALS-associated G138E mutant SOD1 amyloid aggregation, and (II) evaluating the mechanism of modulation of aggregation and inhibition pathways by different concentrations of EGCG.

Methods

Computational section

Toxicity prediction

Evaluating the toxicity of a compound is an important part of the drug design process. Predicting the toxicity of compounds is essential for optimizing lead compounds and reducing the risk of failure in drug development. To evaluate the hazardous properties, the pkCSM server (http://structure.bioc.cam.ac.uk/pkcsm) was used. The pkCSM server is a tool for rapidly screening toxicity properties48.

Ligand preparation and structural optimization

The initial 3D structural coordinates of the ligand (PubChem CID: 65064-EGCG) were obtained in SDF (Structure Data File) format from the PubChem compound database49. PubChem serves as a comprehensive chemical repository maintained by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), providing experimentally determined and computationally generated molecular structures with associated physicochemical properties. Prior to computational analysis, the obtained SDF file was converted to PDB (Protein Data Bank) format using Open Babel 3.1.149, an open-source chemical toolbox designed for structural format interconversion. This conversion was performed, preserving the original atomic coordinates and bond connectivity while ensuring compatibility with downstream molecular modeling software packages50. The PDB format was specifically chosen for its widespread acceptance in molecular docking and dynamics simulation platforms. To achieve a more physiologically relevant conformation and improve computational accuracy, the ligand structure underwent quantum mechanical geometry optimization. This was performed using Gaussian 09 software51, with the hybrid B3LYP (Becke, 3-parameter, Lee–Yang–Parr) density functional theory (DFT) method and the 6-31G** basis set. The optimization protocol consisted of initial geometry cleaning to remove any steric clashes, full structural relaxation through energy minimization, and frequency calculations to confirm the absence of imaginary frequencies. The B3LYP functional was selected for its well-established performance in reproducing molecular geometries and electronic properties of organic molecules, while the 6-31G** basis set provided an optimal balance between computational efficiency and accuracy for systems of this size. This rigorous preparation protocol yielded a structurally refined ligand model with enhanced electronic and steric properties, critical for subsequent molecular docking studies and protein–ligand interaction analyses.

Molecular docking methods

Our comprehension of the ligands’ structure–activity connections was improved by the docking experiments, which offered insightful information about the ligands’ binding mechanisms inside the target protein. In this investigation, we looked at the atomic resolution structure of Cu–Zn Human Superoxide Dismutase (PDB code: 2C9V) using the RCSB PDB database52. Molecular docking was performed using the AutoDock 4.2 software package53. Initially, the AutoDock builder module was used to optimize the protein model by adding hydrogen atoms and including Gasteiger charges. The macromolecule was maintained in a rigid state, allowing all torsion angles in the small molecules to rotate freely. In order to allow for unhindered ligand rotation within the protein’s spatial domain, the grid box dimensions were chosen to 126 × 80 × 80 Å3. Using 200 separate docking simulations with a maximum of 25,000,000 energy assessments, 27,000 generations, a mutation rate of 0.02; a crossover rate of 0.80; a cluster tolerance of 2 Å; and a population size of 150, the Lamarckian genetic algorithm (LGA) was used. The ideal configuration was determined to be the docking position from the most populated cluster with the lowest free energy of binding.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were evaluated using the hSOD1 dimer (chains A and F). The systems studied included a WT-SOD1 protein, a G138E mutant without ligand, and a G138E mutant with ligand (EGCG), all of which were contained within a triclinic water box with dimensions of 7.06400 nm × 8.87600 nm × 7.22600 nm. These systems were simulated using the GROMACS 2022.6 software package, employing the CHARMM36-JUL2022 force field54. The optimal docking poses of the ligands served as the initial configuration for the MD simulations, and the ligand force field parameters were generated using the SwissParam server55. The TIP3P model was used to depict water molecules. Some solvent molecules were replaced with sodium (Na⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻) ions to guarantee the systems’ overall charge neutrality and to reach a healthy ionic strength of 0.15 M. There were four phases to the simulations. Energy minimization was used in the first stage to relax the systems and remove the extra forces operating on the atoms. Two 1 ns (ns) simulations were run in the canonical (NVT) and isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensembles with position restraints imposed to the heavy atoms in order to maintain the temperature and pressure at 300 K and 1 bar, respectively, in the second and third stages. In the last phase, the position restrictions of all atoms were removed, and a simulation with a time step of 2 fs and a duration of 300 ns was executed. This made it possible to record trajectories and extract various physical characteristics. The metal ions of zinc (Zn) and copper (Cu) were restricted to their specific binding sites. In the first phase, the steepest descent method was employed to save energy56. To ensure constant temperature and pressure during the second and third stages, the Nose–Hoover thermostat57 and Parrinello-Rahman barostat58 were applied. In addition, the Lennard–Jones potential, the particle mesh Ewald (PME) technique59, and the LINCS algorithm60 were used to compute van der Waals forces, long-range electrostatic interactions, and covalent bond constraints, respectively. A variety of metrics were assessed for a more thorough examination of both the free protein and protein–ligand complexes, including radius of gyration (Rg), solvent accessible surface area (SASA), principal component analysis (PCA), free energy landscape (FEL), hydrogen bond interactions, root mean square deviation (RMSD), and root mean square fluctuation (RMSF). Additionally, the secondary structure of the proteins was analyzed using the DSSP method, as reported in other studies61–63.

Experimental section

Materials

Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), Thioflavin T (ThT), 8-anilinonaphthalene-1-sulfonic acid (ANS), Phenylmethylsulfonyl Fluoride (PMSF), Kanamycin, Coomassie Brilliant blue (G-250), Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA), Dialysis tubing cellulose membrane (12 KDa cutoff) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Dithiothreitol (DTT) and Isopropyl-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) were acquired from Molekula. Yeast Extract and Tryptone were purchased from IBRasco. The Ni–NTA agarose resins were purchased from Agarose Bead Technologies (ABT).

Expression and purification of mutant SOD1

The Quick-Change PCR site-directed mutagenesis experiments using HL Taq DNA polymerase were performed with pET-28a( +)-hSOD1 as a template, resulting in the pET-28a( +)-G138E mutant64. pET-28a ( +) plasmid encoding G138EhSOD1 mutant cDNA fused with a His tag was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells for protein production by heat shock at 42 °C for 90 s. The bacterial culture was induced by adding 0.8 mM IPTG and 7 mM lactose at 22 °C for 18 h and then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 25 min at 4 °C. Protein was purified by using Ni–NTA affinity column chromatography. After purification, the protein was charged with ZnSO4 (60 μM) and CuSO4 (200 μM) by dialyzing two or three times against a Tris–HCl buffer (25 mM, pH 8.0) at 4 °C for 12 h. Protein was re-dialyzed for 12 h in phosphate buffer (25 mM, pH 7.4) to remove the unbound metals.

Preparation of SOD1 aggregate samples

SOD1 aggregates were obtained under amyloid-inducing conditions by adding 0.2 M Potassium thiocyanate (KSCN), 50 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 50 mM Tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane hydrochloride (Tris–HCl) incubation at 37 °C, and 190 rpm for a time interval of (0–72 h). Protein concentration was 20–30 μM in 25 mM phosphate buffer pH 7.4. An EGCG stock solution (1 mM) was prepared in 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and subsequently added to protein samples at final concentrations of 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 µM.

Fluorescence spectroscopy

The interaction between EGCG and the native protein was investigated using fluorescence spectroscopy. To assess the fluorescence of the protein’s intrinsic tryptophan, a 30 μM SOD1 sample in phosphate buffer at pH 7.4 was mixed with varying doses of EGCG (10, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 µM). A FP-8300 spectrofluorometer-JASCO was used to measure the fluorescence in a quartz cuvette with a 10 mm path length. Emission spectra were collected between 310 and 450 nm with the slit width maintained at 5 nm, while the excitation wavelength was held at 295 nm. ANS fluorescence was utilized to assess the hydrophobicity of SOD1 aggregates formed in the presence of EGCG. The fluorescence of ANS was measured under amyloid-promoting conditions (50 mM DTT, 50 mM Tris–HCl, 0.2 M KSCN, pH 7.4, with agitation at 190 rpm) at 37 °C for time periods ranging from 0 to 72 h. Fluorescence spectra were recorded between 400 and 650 nm, with an excitation wavelength of 350 nm and slits set to 5 nm for excitation and 10 nm for emission.

FTIR spectroscopic

The protein’s secondary structure was examined using KBr tablets and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (BRUKER TENSOR 27, Germany). The infrared spectrum was set at a wave number of 400–4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1. Protein samples, both with and without the ligand EGCG (80 μM), were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h in amyloidogenic conditions, with a final protein concentration of 30 μM. The amide I region (1600–1700 cm−1) spectra were processed using OriginPro 2021 software. Curve fitting of the FTIR spectra in the amide I region is commonly used to analyze protein secondary structures. To resolve overlapping peaks, second derivative spectra and Fourier Self-Deconvolution (FSD) techniques were applied to the amide I band, providing insights into the components of the protein’s secondary structure.

Dynamic light scattering

The size of the formed aggregates was examined using the DLS technique. Samples of aggregated SOD1 in the presence and absence of EGCG were collected and their sizes were examined after the aggregation kinetics were completed. G138E protein samples were collected at a concentration of 30 μM in the presence and absence of 80 μM EGCG after 24 h of incubation under amyloid formation conditions. DLS studies were performed in a polystyrene cuvette at 25 °C with a Malvern Zetasizer ZS90 equipped with a 633 nm “red” laser. The data analysis was performed by the built-in Zetasizer 6.01 software.

Thioflavin T (ThT) fluorescence assay

As stated above, amyloid aggregating formation under inducing conditions was evaluated by the ThT fluorescence assay. SOD1 concentration was 30 μM and ThT with a concentration of 20 μM was added to the incubated samples at different time intervals. The amyloid aggregate formation was monitored with excitation at 444 nm and through the increase in ThT fluorescence at 460–515 nm using a BioTek Synergy H4 Microplate Reader. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

SOD1 fibrillation samples obtained after the fibrillation reaction were used for TEM analysis. Briefly, 5 μL of the collected samples were extracted at different time (0 and 24 h) intervals and subsequently adsorbed onto 400-mesh carbon-coated copper grids for 5 min. Negative staining was then performed using 2% uranyl acetate and air-dried for 20 s. The samples were subsequently analyzed using a transmission electron microscope (Philips, EM 208S, Netherlands) with an accelerating voltage of 100 kV.

Cytotoxicity assay: MTT assay

The study of the mechanisms underlying the toxicity of chemical substances on neuronal cells is made possible by the human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y, which can either be undifferentiated or developed into neuron-like cells67. According to earlier reports, the 3-(4, 5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay was utilized to evaluate cell viability68,69. Mitochondrial succinate dehydrogenase can only transform MTT into formazan crystals in living cells70. Here, human SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells (ATCC) were cultivated at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO2 in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco). In 96-well plates, roughly 5 × 105 viable cells were planted, and they were grown for 24 h. Then, the media was replaced with a fresh medium (200 μL) containing 3 and 6 μM concentrations of mutant SOD1 aggregates in the presence and absence of EGCG at concentrations of 40 and 80 μM and the untreated cells served as the control. Before treatment, extensive dialysis of all the aggregated samples was done against sodium phosphate buffer (25 mM, pH 7.4) to remove the different additives like DTT, EDTA, and flavonoid. After 24 h of incubation, 10 μL of MTT solution at a concentration of 5 mg/mL was added to each well and the cells were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C to perform the MTT reduction reaction to formazan crystals. Finally, the culture medium was removed and 100 μL of DMSO was added to each well. The plate was shaken for 10 min to completely dissolve the formed formazan crystals and the absorbance of each well measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Cytation TM5 Multimode Reader). Each experiment was performed in triplicate to ensure the accuracy of the results. The percentage of viability was calculated from the resulting absorbance values with respect to untreated control cells. Differences between sampling groups were examined for statistical significance using analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s test.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 22.0 is used to analyze the data, which are presented as mean ± SD. A post hoc test (Tukey) was performed to look for differences between the groups after a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to compare the groups. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and NS: not significant.

Results and discussion

Small-molecules therapeutic potential

Protein misfolding and aggregation is a phenomenon of general protein propensity, which is considered as the dark side of the protein world, and its precise mechanism has not yet been deciphered. Understanding the complexity of protein aggregation is currently a major concern and challenge in biology and medicine due to its association with various human debilitating proteinopathies and neurodegenerative diseases. The mechanism of protein aggregation, the associated diseases, and the development of efficient therapeutic strategies against these diseases are very challenging71–73. A comprehensive understanding of the various aspects of aggregation, the molecular steps governing the various features of protein quality control, and fundamental questions about the modulation of these processes and their interactions with other systems in cellular protein quality control can be useful for understanding the mechanism, designing effective approaches to prevent protein aggregation, elucidating the etiology, and developing new strategies against the treatment and management of proteinopathies. Small molecules such as polyphenols, osmolytes, cellular metabolites, nanoconjugates, and peptides are increasingly emerging as crucial therapeutic agents for neurodegenerative disease74,75. Among small-molecules, phytochemicals have outstanding properties. Additionally, compounds have been suggested as potential treatments for serious diseases, including Alzheimer’s76, Parkinson’s77,78. EGCG is one of the small molecules that demonstrates potent anti-neurodegenerative properties by directly inhibiting and disassembling toxic protein aggregates. It targets the misfolding and accumulation of specific proteins involved in various neurodegenerative diseases79,80. In this context, the anti-amyloidogenic effect of EGCG on the G138E-SOD1 mutant was investigated using computational and experimental approaches.

Toxicity assessment

The evaluation of the potential harmful effects of chemicals, drugs, or other agents on living organisms is known as toxicity testing or toxicity analysis. The main objective of toxicological analysis is to evaluate the safety of various compounds and identify potential risks to human health and the environment. Molecules developed as potential drugs can sometimes be toxic, negatively affect other organs, and lead to immunotoxicity, cytotoxicity, mutagenicity, and organ toxicity in humans or animals. In this study, we evaluated the toxicity of the proposed compound (EGCG) using the pkCSM web tool. The toxicity characteristics of the ligand under consideration are presented in Table 1. EGCG was predicted to be non-mutagenic in the AMES test, indicating no genotoxic risk. It also showed no hepatotoxicity or skin sensitization, supporting its safety for systemic and dermal exposure. The maximum tolerated dose in humans (0.441 log mg/kg/day) and chronic LOAEL (3.065 log mg/kg/day) indicate a favorable safety margin for repeated doses. Although EGCG was not predicted to inhibit the hERG I channel (which is associated with QT prolongation), it did show potential inhibition of hERG II. While hERG II inhibition is less clinically relevant, this finding requires further experimental validation to rule out any cardiac risk. The acute oral toxicity (LD50) was calculated to be 2.522 mol/kg. This very high LD50 value classifies EGCG as practically non-toxic in acute exposure scenarios. Furthermore, the predicted low toxicity against Tetrahymena pyriformis (0.285 log μg/L) and fathead minnow (7.713 log mM) indicates minimal biotoxicological concern. Overall, these in silico predictions suggest that EGCG has a favorable safety profile that supports its potential as a lead compound for further development in neurodegenerative diseases. Investigating these interactions is essential for evaluating the ligand’s potential as an effective lead compound. MD simulation

Despite the valuable information obtained from molecular docking on protein–ligand interactions, it has limited application in drug development due to the predicted static positions. Protein–ligand interactions are dynamic by nature; hence MD simulations are frequently employed in computational biology to understand how ligands from various biomolecules bind and unbind. MD simulations aid in assessing the therapeutic progress of molecules in drug development as well as confirming the molecular binding created by protein–ligand interactions. Thus, using MD simulations for 300 ns at 310 K, the structural dynamics of the WT-SOD1 protein, the G138E mutant, and the mutant complexed with EGCG compound were examined.

The first important analysis of MD simulation is the RMSD, which shows the stability and structural changes throughout the course of the simulation. The RMSD value illustrates how the particle location deviates from the original structure at any given time85. The RMSD value for the Cα backbone was tracked by looking at the MD trajectories every 20 ns in order to assess the stability of the protein–ligand complexes. The RMSD fluctuation data for the WT-SOD1, mutant, and mutant-ligand complex are displayed in Fig. 4a. WT and G138E had respective RMSD values of 0.27 and 0.21 nm. The findings demonstrated that a mutation would produce a unique protein shape, which subtly suggests that the stability of the mutant differs from that of the WT. The RMSD values rose to 0.24 nm when EGCG interacted with the G138E mutant. The findings showed promise for the partial restoration of the dynamic behavior of the mutant by binding the polyphenolic phytochemical substance (EGCG), which has been shown to improve the structural similarity between WT and G138E31,41,57. Overall, the protein–ligand complex’s RMSD data point to EGCG’s possible function in regulating structural flexibility, which will help with further analysis.Fig. 4Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation results of WT-SOD1, G138E mutant and G138E-EGCG. Structural change analysis: (a) RMSD, (b) RMSF, (c) Rg, (d) SASA.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation results of WT-SOD1, G138E mutant and G138E-EGCG. Structural change analysis: (a) RMSD, (b) RMSF, (c) Rg, (d) SASA.

Since RMSD is an unsuitable metric to depict the mobility of structural components, the flexibility of the structure was investigated using the root-mean-square fluctuations (RMSF) for the Cα carbon atoms of the WT, mutant, and protein–ligand complexes. RMSF is an important metric that calculates the deviation of a group of atoms in a structural system using MD simulation86. In order to look at the structural stability factor, we examined the flexibility values for every residue region. The G138E mutant and WT-SOD1 dimer had flexibility values of 0.149 and 0.165 nm, respectively (Fig. 4b). Residues producing functionally significant loops, such as residues 49–83 (zinc loop/loop IV) and 121–142 (electrostatic loop/loop VII), showed more flexibility, but residues building β-sheets showed less flexibility. Throughout the simulation, the loops often displayed more variations, suggesting a significant distortion or disorder. The (G138E) mutation might be the cause of this higher loop fluctuation. Interestingly, following interaction with EGCG, the average residual flexibility returned to 0.16 nm, which is comparable to the WT. The evidence is consistent with the hypothesis that EGCG binding promotes a more dynamic but non-aggregating structural assembly by increasing local flexibility, especially in critical loops. Increasing flexibility in aggregation-prone regions may thus prevent the rigid stacking of β-sheets that is essential for fibril formation, thereby modulating the aggregation pathway. Hence, the increase in fluctuations supports the hypothesis that EGCG exerts its anti-amyloidogenic effects by altering the local dynamics of structure-sensitive regions.

Since the stability of the protein is dependent on the total of the intramolecular interactions within the protein structure, the radius of gyration (Rg) represents the relative compactness of the protein structure87. The Rg, which determines the root-mean-square distance of an atom’s motion from its center of mass, was used to study G138E and protein–ligand complexes. The average Rg values for unbound G138E and WT-SOD1 during the simulation were 2.06 and 2.02 nm, respectively (Fig. 4c). These values increased to 2.05 nm due to the interaction between EGCG and G138E. EGCG binding reduced the compactness of the G138E mutant and made it similar to WT-hSOD1, indicating that the chemicals had a structural effect on the G138E mutant. The average Rg values of the protein–ligand complexes and the G138E mutant do not differ much, but it is important to note that it is clear from looking at their trajectories that none of them are coordinated with one another. Initial analysis of MD simulation trajectories revealed that the mutation’s structural perturbations result in a pathologically unique dynamic location of the G138E mutant that is structurally changed upon interaction with the flavonoid EGCG. This change in compactness implies that EGCG returns the mutant to a conformation that is similar to the WT, perhaps by inhibiting connections that are prone to aggregation and cause abnormally compact states. According to this Rg-intermediate interaction, EGCG may lessen the mutation-induced misfolding and guide the G138E conformation toward a non-aggregating assembly. These findings corroborate the previously described anti-amyloidogenic mechanism of G138E30,31,88,89.

The regions of protein structures that are exposed to and accessible by solvent molecules are known as solvent accessible surface area (SASA), and they are a crucial component when examining a protein’s stability, folding, characteristics, and activities. The degree of ligand binding to the receptor and the structural alterations that the protein experiences as a result of contact with the ligand can both be evaluated using SASA. WT-SOD1 dimer, G138E mutant, and G138E-EGCG had mean SASA values of 156, 152, and 155 nm2, respectively (Fig. 4d).

The G138E mutant showed fewer fluctuations, which might have caused structural alterations and a more compact conformation of SOD1. By partially restoring the decreased surface area, EGCG binding to the G138E mutant may stabilize a more stable molecule that resembles the WT. Since EGCG reduces compaction, it may lessen the chance of hydrophobic collapse, which would prevent amyloidogenic aggregation. The solubility environment surrounding the protein may alter as a result of the presence of chemical substances. Proteins frequently undergo structural alterations as a result of misfolding, which could reveal “deeply buried hydrophobic residues from the protein core to the solvent”90. A reduction in SASA is indicative of protein misfolding, which causes hydrophobic residues to become less exposed and hidden in the protein core. Therefore, the partial recovery in SASA upon EGCG binding suggests a protective effect against misfolding and subsequent aggregation. These results were confirmed by the fluctuations in Rg values observed in the simulations, which were consistent with the findings of previous research30,31,88.

It is well known that the first bonds to respond to structural changes brought on by different protein mutations are hydrogen bonds. One indication of bond strength and stability in any chemical structure is the creation of hydrogen bonds, which are crucial for the stability of protein structures91,92. Additionally, a study found that the stability of SOD1 is considerably reduced and aggregation results from the loss of negatively charged gatekeeper residues and H-bonds on the protein surface93. Therefore, we conducted H-bonding studies for protein–ligand complexes with WT-SOD1 and G138E. WT and G138E mutant proteins had an average of 204 and 207 hydrogen bond alterations, respectively (Fig. 5a). The findings demonstrated that when Gly was substituted for Glu, there were more H-bonds and more intramolecular interactions. In contrast to the WT, the G138E mutant’s enhanced protein density and mutation led to an increase in the intermolecular hydrogen bonds that were created. These outcomes aligned with the Rg and SASA parameter results. SOD1 mutations may enhance the protein’s tendency to aggregate into oligomers and amyloid fibrils by promoting abnormal hydrogen bond formation and hydrophobic interactions94, as previously reported95. Even if these alterations are small and local, they could have a big impact on the folding process and structural integrity. However, for the protein–ligand complex (EGCG), 205 hydrogen bonds were found for protein–protein interactions. The findings demonstrated that the chosen flavonoid molecule, which resembles the pattern observed in WT-SOD1, decreases the quantity of abnormal hydrogen bonds in the mutant-ligand complexes. As a result, EGCG is among the best options for reestablishing the hydrogen bonding pattern.Fig. 5(a) Analysis of hydrogen bond changes between protein–protein; WT-SOD1, G138E mutant and G138E-EGCG (b) Protein–ligand hydrogen bond profiling of the G138E mutant following interaction with EGCG.

To further understand how the ligand affects the mutant structure, we also computed protein–ligand hydrogen bonding for the SOD1 dimer. The intensity and direction of hydrogen bonding have a major impact on the formation and stability of inhibitor-enzyme complexes. According to the molecular docking data, the average number of hydrogen bonds in protein–ligand complex interactions with EGCG was five (Fig. 5b). This suggests that in addition to hydrogen bonding, EGCG also employs hydrophobic contacts to prevent aggregation. The study allowed for the development of hypotheses on the relationship between the length of patient survival and the stabilizing and destabilizing effects of mutations in different structural and functional regions of SOD1. More research using powerful molecular modeling techniques is certainly needed to better understand the processes of the hydrogen bonding influence on the development of pathogenic characteristics of SOD1 mutations96,97. The binding of EGCG, which may change the immediate environment surrounding the G138E mutation site, is what causes these outcomes. Since phenolic groups maintain the immediate structure around the mutation site, they may enhance the chains’ capacity to break abnormal hydrogen bonds. Hydrogen bonds are therefore essential for preserving the three-dimensional structure of proteins, and lowering these abnormal interactions may make the protein more likely to fold.

Principal component analysis

In particular, each macromolecule has functional motions. They emerge from complex correlations between its atomic motions. These internal movements are required for its proper functioning, e.g. structural changes in different biological environments, etc. Protein molecules have many internal motions that are difficult to interpret. Principal component analysis (PCA) or essential dynamics (ED) is a tool to reduce the huge dimensions of data sets to principal components and reveal the major changes that represent the global protein movement with essential information. The PCs collected during MD simulations are essentially eigenvector values collected from the covariance matrix, each corresponding to a change in the protein trajectory. The eigenvector values collected from the simulation covariance matrix were determined first. In the following, the motions of the dominant protein are determined after filtering different trajectories110. To compare the dynamics of protein/ligand systems and comprehend how ligand binding affects protein movements, a two-dimensional (2D) PCA was carried out. For the first 25 kinetic states, the eigenvalues for the SOD1 mutant and mutant-ligand systems were plotted against the appropriate eigenvector index (Fig. 7a). The image demonstrates that only a few numbers of eigenvectors have greater eigenvalues, which significantly influenced the proteins’ overall motion. The eigenvalues showed variations of the eigenvector in the hyperspace. PC analysis was carried out on MD trajectories during the final 50 ns of the simulation for each system in order to illustrate changes in motion brought about by interactions with natural molecules in SOD1 aggregates. Plots of the eigenvalues against eigenvector indexes, which are derived from the diagonalization of the covariance matrix, are shown in Fig. 7a. The eigenvector reveals the directions of the movements, while the eigenvalues show the strength of the movements. In the overall system, the other eigenvectors were discovered to have very low eigenvalues, but the top three eigenvectors were found to have highly dominant movements with a higher eigenvalue. The amplitude of the first few eigenvalues that correspond to coordinated motions rapidly drops to a more limited and restricted fluctuation. Free energy landscape analysis

Protein aggregation can be accurately assessed using the various conformational states that are occupied in the free energy landscape (FEL)111. One important biomolecular cause for amyloid development that eventually results in amyloid fibrils is the misfolding of protopathic proteins112. As a result, we assessed the FEL for the ligand–protein complexes, G138E mutant, and WT-SOD1 independently. Likewise, to examine the pathophysiology of the G138E mutant during the simulation period, a FEL was created to count the incidence of global energy minima113. The two coordinates utilized for FEL analysis to demonstrate the protein’s compaction and structural stability during the molecular dynamics phase were RMSD and Rg. Figure 8 illustrates that the produced FEL of WT-SOD1 and the G138E mutant had Gibbs free energies ranging from 0 to 10 kcal/mol. The free energy varies from purple to yellow, with the yellow region representing metastable states and the purple region representing the minimal global energy composition. We came to the conclusion that the SOD1 substitution mutation had a substantial impact on the folding pattern, potentially resulting in higher RMSD values in the direction of the multiple energy minimum that the mutant conformers were able to reach. A natural protein typically has a minimum global energy number of one. A distinct advantageous area can be seen in the free energy landscape for the WT-SOD1 structure (Fig. 8a), which is situated between the Rg and RMSD values of around 2.08 and 0.37 nm, respectively. However, the G138E mutant protein’s studies showed that it might undergo global energy minima in a variety of thermodynamically plausible conformational states, any of which could result in misfolding and, eventually, amyloid aggregation. A more compact basin of favorable free energy was formed as a result of substitution mutation’s impact on this process (Fig. 8b), which was ~ 2.04 and ~ 0.27 nm for Rg and RMSD, respectively. As the free energy diagram showed, the presence of G138 in WT played a prominent role in the existence of a restricted free energy basin along RMSD and Rg, which by substituting Glu, led to change of the SOD1 protein and reached a compact basin with a global free energy minimum. Furthermore, the free energy range achieved by WT-SOD1 was larger than that of the G138E mutant, indicating that more favorable conformations were adopted in WT than in the mutant. Overall, the results support the idea that an increased percentage of compact structures with lower free energy in the mutant compared to the WT are directed towards the formation of predominantly misfolding states. In indirect confirmation of these results, comparison with the results of previous studies shows that aggregated proteins achieve multiple minimum energy conformations, indicating the formation of toxic aggregates in mutant SOD1114,115. Subsequently, we also calculated the FEL for the mutant-ligand complex conformational preferences along the coordination RMSD and Rg. Since EGCG affects mutant SOD1 and changes the compaction and conformational stability of the complexes, the G138E mutant exhibits a global energy minimum after binding with EGCG that is located at Rg ~ 2.06 nm and RMSD ~ 0.29 nm (Fig. 8c). The assertion that EGCG’s dynamic interaction with mutant SOD1 improves conformational stability and decreases compaction is supported by the appearance of a minimal global peak. Consequently, it structurally and thermodynamically limits the mutagenic state from its unique SOD1 route. Last but not least, the findings from the examination of molecular binding, conformational stability, and secondary structure orientation as well as the free energy viewpoint generally demonstrated that EGCG, in contrast to other flavonoid compounds, inhibited mutant SOD1 more than WT-SOD1, as was previously documented 31,97,116,117.Fig. 8Free energy landscape (FEL) Plots. ANS fluorescence analysis

Aggregation-prone species have more hydrophobic patches on the surface to facilitate protein–protein interactions to form aggregates. The results showed that at different times, the fluorescence intensity was reduced for the mutant compared to the WT. The mutation in SOD1 alters the protein structure and leads to changes in the binding of ANS to the hydrophobic pockets of the WT protein compared to the mutant (Fig. 10a). Thus, ANS fluorescence revealed the hydrophobicity of the produced fibrils. The G138E mutant displayed a decrease in ANS fluorescence intensity across the (0–72 h) period after incubation, indicating a decrease in the availability of hydrophobic areas. Protein aggregation or structural changes that reveal fewer hydrophobic sites to the ANS probe may be the source of this. Fluorescence intensity decreased with increasing incubation period under amyloid-inducing conditions, as fewer hydrophobic regions were exposed. This was probably caused by the G138E mutant misfolding and aggregating. As seen in Fig. 10b, these alterations were noted without any blue shift. Over the course of 0–72 h, variations in fluorescence intensity were noted when EGCG was given to the mutant sample at a concentration of 80 μM under amyloid-inducing conditions. The EGCG effect combined on the aggregation behavior of mutant SOD1 may be the reason for the observed smaller increases in ANS fluorescence intensity compared to the G138E mutant alone (Fig. 10c). These results support the theory that G138E-EGCG is more likely to have hydrophobic interactions with other molecules due to its exposure to more hydrophobic regions. Certain SOD1 mutations are more likely to aggregate when hydrophobic areas change93. The available hydrophobic surface becomes blocked as a result of the alignment of β-sheets and the growth of prefibrillar species. Likewise, prior research has demonstrated that SOD1 mutations can lessen the amount of exposed hydrophobic areas in amyloid-inducing environments11–14,46. A naturally occurring compound called EGCG reduces the formation of compact aggregates, increases the exposure of hydrophobic patches in G138E mutant aggregates, and inhibits the development of β-sheets. Thioflavin T (ThT) binding assay

Amyloid development is frequently detected with ThT fluorescence. When the fluorescence intensity of amyloids increases significantly, the ThT molecule attaches to them specifically. The ThT fluorescence test was used to assess the kinetics of amyloid fibril production caused by the G138E mutant under physiological conditions of induction of the previously stated amyloid aggregates, both with and without the addition of varying doses of EGCG. Representative fluorescence time courses for the mutant and mutant-ligand are shown in Fig. 11, and a complete set of kinetic data is given in Table 4. The increase in ThT fluorescence observed in the G138E mutant sample after incubation provides compelling evidence of the development of β-sheet-rich aggregates. A lag period was observed just before the ThT fluorescence intensity increases in mutant fibrillation. The lag phase time was different for WT-SOD1, mutant and the G138E-EGCG. By comparing the lag phase, it was found that the mutant tended to have shorter lag phases than the WT. On the other hand, Comparing the lag phase revealed that the mutant tends to shorten the lag phase and accelerate fibrillation in the absence of ligand (EGCG). Variations in the intensity of ThT fluorescence are caused by (i) changes in the quantum yield (fluorescence intensity) of fibril-bound ThT and (ii) the number of linked ThT molecules119. Variations in the morphologies, dynamics, and structures of fibrils may result in variations in the quantum yield and binding affinity. For instance, “fibril matting” refers to interactions between fibrils120, may result in disturbances of secondary structure or decreased solvent accessibility, which would reduce ThT binding. This shift in lag phase time is in line with other findings that have been examined in various contexts121,122. It should be mentioned, nevertheless, that fALS-causing mutations might not necessarily lead to increased SOD1 aggregation propensity and/or decreased latency. According to the kinetics of aggregation production in amyloid fibrils, there is a lag phase followed by an initial spike in ThT fluorescence intensity (which was larger in the mutant alone than in the ligand-mutant) and a subsequent decrease in ThT intensity. Plateau phase for the mutant and WT occurred at 24 and 60 h after incubation, respectively, which was observed for the EGCG-bound mutant at 24 h, but was accompanied by a decrease in fluorescence intensity. A decrease in fluorescence intensity was observed with increasing ligand concentration. This may be because some ThT binding sites are inaccessible or the mat-like fibril aggregates are destroyed. Ultimately, the plateau phase starts when the intensity of ThT fluorescence peaks. This time frame is brief, though, since amyloid fibrils frequently create network topologies that resemble webs. In this regard, there are multiple aggregation pathways, some of which lead to amorphous aggregates, while others lead to amyloid aggregates. Furthermore, the results show that hSOD1 forms amyloid aggregates under different conditions, including agitation, ionic strength, temperature, and physiological pH. All of these results suggest that the kinetic differences between protein aggregates may be influenced by hydrophobic interactions. By promoting primary nucleation events (by raising the local concentration of SOD1 at the surface and/or changing the shape of SOD1 or its oligomers in ways that accelerate nucleation), these hydrophobic contacts may speed up fibrillization. The results also showed that a concentration of 80 µM EGCG was able to shorten the lag phase and reduce the fluorescence intensity of the mutant, bringing it closer to the WT state. These results are consistent with other studies that demonstrate the role of SOD1 fibrillation or oligomerization in the pathophysiology of ALS12,121–123. FTIR spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy has become an effective method for analyzing protein secondary structures and structural changes due to aggregation and misfolding due to its greater sensitivity to β- structures. FTIR spectra of the amide I regions were used to identify various amyloidogenic conformers and β-structures. The stretching vibrations of the C=O peptide bond, which has the highest vibrational absorption, show a significant and sensitive vibrational band in the region of 1700–1600 cm−1, which is characteristic of proteins. WT-SOD1 samples were incubated for 60 h for secondary structure analysis. For the G138E mutant, pre-incubation and post-incubation for 24 h (200 rpm, 37 °C) with and without EGCG (80 μM) were performed. Protein aggregation causes fibrils and β-oligomers with an intermolecular β-sheet or β-cross structure to form, as indicated by the peak at 1610–1630 cm−1. This disordered conformation (commonly referred to as random coil) is usually associated with the IR band from 1640 to 1648 cm−1. The β-sheet is represented by the bands between 1630 and 1640 cm−1. Bands between 1662–1678 cm−1 and 1689–1699 cm−1 are ascribed to loops and turns, whereas the area between 1647 and 1662 cm−1 exhibits α-helices33,124. For the WT, a peak at 1638 cm−1 was observed after 60 h of incubation. For G138E mutant, a peak at 1629 cm−1 was observed before incubation (0 h time). After 24 h of incubation without ligand, a peak at 1626 cm−1 was observed for the G138E mutant. After 24 h of incubation with 80 µM EGCG, a peak at 1635 cm−1 was then found (Fig. 12a). The β-sheet structures are associated with these peaks. After 24 h of incubation, the number of amyloid fibrils increased, according to FTIR examination of β-sheet structures (Fig. 12b). However, the lowering effect of disordered structures is further improved when EGCG is present at a concentration of 80 µM (Fig. 11b). Furthermore, second derivative or Fourier self-deconvolution (FSD) analysis of the FTIR spectrum of amide I was used to increase the spectral resolution and accurately identify overlapping absorption bands. This method, by reducing the bandwidth of the bands, allows for more accurate identification of hidden secondary structure components in the amide I region. The positions of the bands obtained from the FSD analysis were used to determine the number of bands and select the initial values in the curve fitting process. The application of this method increased the accuracy of secondary structure determination and improved the reliability of the spectral analysis. The results of the FSD analysis on amide I for SOD1 variants (WT, G138E mutant and G138E-EGCG) are shown in Fig. 12c. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis

Dynamic light scattering spectroscopy (DLS) is used to monitor protein aggregation in liquids. The easiest approach to tell if a protein is in an aggregating state is to measure its molecular weight or particle size. DLS spectroscopy is a practical and noninvasive biophysical technique for assessing the size distribution and aggregation behavior of proteins in solution. This method is widely used to monitor amyloid fibrils and amorphous aggregates, which are distinct structural results of protein misfolding. In the context of neurological disorders and biopharmaceutical stability investigations, DLS allows for the rapid detection of early oligomer formation, hydrodynamic diameter changes, and multiple dispersions in a sample. Amyloid fibrils typically have a uniform, high molecular weight distribution due to their organized, β-sheet-rich structures, whereas amorphous aggregates have more varied profiles due to nonspecific protein–protein interactions. By analyzing the intensity-weighted size distributions and scattering patterns using DLS, researchers can distinguish between these types of aggregates and monitor their changes over time under varied physicochemical circumstances. As a result, it is a crucial instrument for understanding the mechanisms behind protein aggregation and assessing how well aggregation inhibitors work125–127. In this study, DLS was used to investigate the particle size in the soluble fraction of WT-SOD1 samples G138E mutant under amyloid induction conditions with and without ligand in the presence of 80 μM EGCG. Size distribution plots of SOD1 samples at different incubation times (24 and 60 h) were plotted. DTT-treated WT-SOD1 samples showed small species with sizes between (90–140) nm. The larger aggregates observed in the G138E mutant (range: 160–460 nm) indicate the early stages of fibril formation. EGCG may stabilize the protein and stop further aggregation, as seen by the smaller aggregates that the G138E-EGCG complex generated (range: 100–220 nm) (Fig. 13). One measure of the size distribution of a sample is the polydispersity index (PDI). Less than 0.2 PDI: This denotes a high level of homogeneity, or “monodispersity”. The particles in the solution are all very close to the same size. For a protein sample, this suggests that it is properly folded and stable, with minimal aggregation. Moderate PDI (0.2 to 0.5): This range suggests some variability in particle size. For a protein sample, this could indicate that a portion of the protein is starting to aggregate or that there are multiple populations of particles of different sizes. High PDI (greater than 0.5): This indicates a very broad, or “polydisperse”, sample with a wide range of particle sizes. In a protein sample, this is a strong indicator of significant aggregation or denaturation, which can compromise the protein’s function and stability128. The PDI for WT and G41D mutant samples was 0.2 and 1, respectively, indicating a highly dispersed sample and significant aggregation for the mutant, while the lower PDI in the G138E-EGCG complex (0.258) indicates a relatively homogeneous solution with minimal aggregation. By interacting with amyloid fibrils, EGCG may either stabilize or disrupt aggregation, producing a range of aggregate sizes. This variation implies that EGCG may play a part in controlling the G138E mutant’s aggregation, potentially stabilizing some conformations while destabilizing others. Zeta potential sheds light on how proteins aggregate and how stable colloidal dispersions are129. WT-SOD1 showed a zeta potential of − 18.9 mV, consistent with electrostatic stabilization and a moderate negative surface charge. Under amyloid-inducing conditions, the G138E mutant’s zeta potential of − 11.6 mV shows protein aggregation, indicating that hydrophobic interactions or other causes override electrostatic repulsion and cause the mutant to assemble. The G138E-EGCG complex’s zeta potential of − 13.2 mV denotes decreased aggregation and enhanced electrostatic repulsion. The partial recovery of zeta potential upon EGCG binding correlates well with the particle size reduction and lower dispersion observed by DLS, supporting the stabilizing effect of the ligand against mutation-induced aggregation. Cytotoxicity assay (MTT) of protein aggregates

EGCG and SHSY5Y neuronal cell lines were used to assess the toxicity of aggregates. Before and after incubation (protein sample concentrations 3 and 6 μM) and after treatment with the flavonoid EGCG (40 and 80 μM), the MTT test was used to quantitatively evaluate the toxicity of untreated SOD1 fibrils (Fig. 15). To get rid of additives like EDTA, DTT, and flavonoids from the sample solution, all of the combined samples were dialyzed against phosphate buffer. Prior to incubation alone, the percentage of MTT (cell viability) was around 85% when SOD1 protein aggregates were present at concentrations of 3 and 6 μM. However, during a 24-h incubation period at 37 °C, SOD1 aggregates at concentrations of 3 and 6 μM decreased cell viability to approximately 40% (p < 0.01). The formed aggregates/fibrils increased cell viability by 50% after treatment with 40 μM EGCG (p < 0.05). However, cell viability rose to almost 60% (p < 0.05) when produced fibrils were treated with 80 μM EGCG. The MTT assay results demonstrated that EGCG decreased SOD1 fibril neurotoxicity. Recent research has demonstrated that the primary hazardous species are prefibrillar species produced in the SOD1 amyloidogenic pathway131. Amyloid aggregates’ cytotoxicity has been linked to their interaction with the membrane132. These findings align with our FTIR results on decreased β-cross and β-sheets in EGCG-containing samples. Conclusion

This study aimed to identify an inhibitor of ALS-related protein aggregation in mutant SOD1 through experimental and computational approaches to better understand the physicochemical properties of the anti-aggregation effects of the active substance on the mutant. Docking and molecular dynamics simulation results showed that EGCG binding restored structural features similar to WT-hSOD1, in particular by stabilizing key loops and reducing structural fluctuations, which were significantly reflected in the results of RMSD, Rg, SASA, H-bond and RSMF parameters. These changes seem to prevent the formation of higher-order aggregates by stabilizing aggregation-prone species. As shown by ThT and TEM analyses, the G138E mutant showed a lower density of fibrillar aggregates and formed fewer fibrils in EGCG-treated samples. Furthermore, ThT aggregation kinetics indicate that EGCG suppresses or inhibits the aggregation of the G138E mutant in a dose-dependent manner. Secondary structure analysis using FTIR and DSSP revealed a decrease in beta-sheet content in EGCG-treated samples, consistent with inhibition of amyloidogenic conformational transitions. ANS fluorescence also confirmed a slight increase in hydrophobic surface exposure in EGCG-treated samples, which may indicate local surface rearrangements that contribute to the inhibition of amyloid aggregation and the maintenance of a quasi-native topology. These multilevel findings provide coherent insights into the mechanism of how EGCG interferes with amyloid formation by targeting structural flexibility and aggregation-prone regions. Consequently, we proposed EGCG as a potential candidate for inhibiting ALS-associated SOD1 aggregation. It is well known that aggregation-prone stretches in proteins actually play a role in the formation of critical nuclei that initiate fibrillation. Therefore, we speculate that EGCG molecules interfere with early nucleation events by directly interacting with amyloidogenic stretches in non-aggregating SOD1. Furthermore, EGCG molecules strongly bind to these aggregation-prone regions of intermediates in the aggregation pathway and block the obvious aggregation site on these species, hence, preventing the formation of toxic species in the aggregation pathway. We envision that small molecule such as flavonoids, etc., could act as promising anti-amyloidogenic and degradative agents to combat neurodegenerative diseases. Advances in the identification of phytochemical compounds for use in protein systems, particularly their abilities to inhibit target proteins, offer significant potential for further advances in pharmaceutical science and protein engineering, as our research supports the potential of EGCG as an anti-amyloidogenic compound for therapeutic development in ALS.
