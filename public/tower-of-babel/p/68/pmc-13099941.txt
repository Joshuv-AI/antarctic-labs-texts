Wildlife surveys detect Trypanosoma brucei gambiense in duikers and mangabeys in Gabonese historical and active foci of human African trypanosomiasis
Authors: Larson Boundenga, Sarah Parfaite Ambourouet, Franck Mounioko, Neil Michel Longo-Pendy, Monique Nzale, Félicien Bangueboussa, Ben Saïd Mourad, Patrice Makouloutou-Nzassi, Barthélemy Ngoubangoye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human African trypanosomiasis (HAT) continues to represent a public health concern in Central Africa, particularly in Gabon, where sporadic cases persist in historical foci despite ongoing elimination efforts. The existence of an autonomous sylvatic cycle involving non-human animal reservoirs has been hypothesized but remains insufficiently documented. The objective of this study was to characterise the diversity of trypanosomes present in wild animals from a number of historically and currently endemic provinces of Gabon. Between 2019 and 2022, a total of 240 blood samples were collected from wild non-human primates (n = 172) and ungulates (n = 68). The molecular identification of trypanosomes was performed using nested PCR targeting the 18S rRNA gene, followed by sequencing and phylogenetic analysis. The overall infection rate was 12.1%. A higher prevalence was observed in great apes (gorillas: 36.4%) than in ungulates. Six trypanosome species and subspecies were identified, belonging to different subgenera: T. brucei gambiense and T. brucei brucei; T. congolense; T. vivax; as well as T. simiae (subgenus Nannomonas) and T. theileri (subgenus Megatrypanum). The most prevalent species detected were T. brucei brucei and T. congolense. Trypanosoma brucei gambiense was identified in mangabeys and duikers from the Ogooué-Maritime and Haut-Ogooué provinces. Although no statistically significant associations were found between infection status and factors such as sex, age, or season, ecological and spatial trends were observed. The analysis of host-parasite interactions demonstrated that hosts, including duikers and monkeys, can harbor multiple Trypanosoma species, indicating their role as reservoirs for diverse parasites. These findings provide further evidence supporting the hypothesis of the circulation of T. brucei gambiense in Gabonese wildlife, and underscore the potential for wild animals to serve as cryptic reservoirs. Integration of eco-epidemiological surveillance within a One Health framework is imperative for strengthening elimination strategies and reducing the risk of HAT resurgence or zoonotic emergence.

Introduction

Human African trypanosomiasis (HAT), also known as sleeping sickness, is a neglected tropical disease caused by the subspecies of Trypanosoma brucei, primarily Trypanosoma brucei gambiense in Central and West Africa (Kennedy, 2019). This chronic form of HAT, transmitted by tsetse flies (Glossina spp.) (Krafsur and Maudlin, 2018), accounts for over 97% of reported HAT cases (Franco et al., 2024).

Historically, humans were considered the exclusive reservoir of T. b. gambiense, leading to control strategies focused on case detection and treatment (Cordon-Obras et al., 2015; WHO, 2019). While this approach substantially reduced transmission (Franco et al., 2014, 2024; Cordon-Obras et al., 2015; Franco et al., 2024), persistent HAT resurgence in historical and active foci suggests unresolved challenges, including asymptomatic carriers (Courtin et al., 2010; Jamonneau et al., 2012), surveillance gaps, and potential animal reservoirs (Courtin et al., 2008, 2010; Büscher et al., 2018).

In parallel, African animal trypanosomiasis (AAT), caused by diverse trypanosome species (e.g. T. congolense, T. vivax, T. simiae, T. simiae Tsavo, and T. brucei brucei) affects wildlife and domestic animals (WOAH, 2024; FAO, 2025). Notably, T. brucei gambiense has been detected in animals; however, their role as a wildlife reservoir remains debated (Njiokou et al., 2010; Maganga et al., 2017; Umeakuana et al., 2019; Squarre et al., 2020; Vourchakbé et al., 2020). Reports of atypical human infections with animal-derived trypanosomes further underscore zoonotic risks (Deborggraeve et al., 2008; Truc et al., 2013, 2014). However, the dynamics of sylvatic transmission cycles remain poorly understood (Funk et al., 2013).

In Gabon, HAT persists in historical and active foci like Estuaire and Ogooué-Maritime (Simon et al., 2012; Iroungou et al., 2020), with 52 deaths reported in 2018 (WHO, 2019). However, persistent cases in peri-urban and coastal settings highlight the limitations of existing intervention strategies (Simon et al., 2012; Kohagne Tongué et al., 2013; Iroungou et al., 2020). Additionally, the lack of data on trypanosome diversity in Gabonese wildlife (Maganga et al., 2020) limits the assessment of zoonotic spillover potential. We hypothesize that wild animals act as cryptic reservoirs, maintaining T. b. gambiense transmission independently of human cases. To investigate this, we characterized trypanosome diversity in wildlife from historical and active HAT foci in Gabon and assessed their role in pathogen circulation. Our findings may enhance One Health strategies for HAT elimination by integrating wildlife, vector, and human surveillance.

Materials and methods

Study area and sample collection

Between 2019 and 2022, 240 biological samples were collected from wild animals in six historically endemic provinces of Gabon for HAT: Estuaire (ES); Haut-Ogooué (HO); Ogooué-Ivindo (OI); Ogooué-Lolo (OL); Ogooué-Maritime (OM); and Moyen-Ogooué (MO) (Fig. 1). The sampled fauna included non-human primates and ungulates. For live animals, authorized veterinarians collected blood samples following strict ethical and animal welfare guidelines. The animals were temporarily immobilized using species-appropriate sedation, and blood was drawn via venipuncture, typically from the femoral or saphenous vein, depending on body size and accessibility. For bushmeat samples, blood was collected post-mortem from freshly killed animals, either directly from major blood vessels during field dressing or from pooled blood in body cavities. These samples were obtained in collaboration with provincial officers from the Gabonese Ministry of Water and Forests, following confiscations linked to illegal hunting activities. Additional samples were collected from bushmeat vendors during authorized hunting seasons. To ensure data reliability, all bushmeat samples were processed in a clean area with sterile instruments. Approximately 2–7 ml of whole blood was collected per animal into EDTA tubes, with volume adjusted based on body weight. Samples were immediately stored in portable coolers and transported to the laboratories of the Centre Interdisciplinaire de Recherches Médicales de Franceville (CIRMF), where they were stored at −20 °C until molecular analysis. Whenever possible, metadata including sex, age class (juvenile or adult), and season of collection (dry or rainy) were recorded for each animal. A detailed list of samples with geographical localization of sampling is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The sampling protocol, which was opportunistic in nature, was dictated by the availability of animals and governed by the legal and ethical authorizations in force. DNA extraction and Trypanosoma spp. characterization

Total DNA was extracted from 200 μl of whole blood from each sample using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Courtaboeuf, France) according to the manufacturer’s procedures. All samples were screened using both nested PCR assays targeting the 18S rRNA and ITS regions (Auty et al., 2012; Votýpka et al., 2015). The primers used in these assays are listed in Table 1. The protocol of Votýpka et al. (2015) was modified by using Qiagen Multiplex PCR Master Mix for the first round and Platinum Taq DNA Polymerase (Invitrogen, California, USA) for the second round. Amplified products were electrophoresed on 1.5% agarose gels in Tris-acetate-EDTA (TAE) buffer and visualized under UV light. PCR products were sequenced using the Sanger method for species confirmation. Molecular identification of Trypanosoma brucei gambiense

To specifically identify T. b. gambiense among samples previously classified under the subgenus Trypanozoon (based on an initial ∼400-bp amplicon), a nested PCR assay has been performed following the protocol described by Cordon-Obras et al. (2010). The first amplification round targeted the T. b. gambiense-specific glycoprotein gene (TgSGP) using the primers TgSGP-1 (5′-GCT GCT GTG TTC GGA GAG C-3′) and TgSGP-2 (5′-GCC ATC GTG CTT GCC GCT C-3′) (Radwanska et al., 2002). Each 25 μl reaction mixture contained 1× PCR buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2), 15 pmol of each primer, 100 μM of each dNTP, 1 U of Taq DNA polymerase, 5 μl of DNA template, and sterile nuclease-free water to adjust the volume. The thermal cycling profile included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 63 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min. For the second round, a nested primer set, TgsGP-s (5′-TCA GAC AGG GCT GTA ATA GCA AGC-3′) and TgsGP-as (5′-GGG CTC CTG CCT CAA TTG CTG CA-3′), was used as described by Morrison et al. (2008). The reaction mixture (25 μl) contained the same components as PCR1 but used 2 μl of the first-round product as template and included only 25 amplification cycles. PCR2 products (10 μl per sample) were electrophoresed on a 1.5% agarose gel in TAE buffer and visualized under UV light. Samples showing a distinct ∼270-bp band were considered positive for T. b. gambiense.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analyses were performed using a fragment of 950 bp of the 18S rRNA gene. To examine the relationship between the newly generated sequences and the various known trypanosomes, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using a set of reference sequences belonging to different trypanosome species from GenBank (Supplementary Table S2). The multiple alignment of all sequences was done using ClustalW v.1.8.1 in BioEdit v.7.0.9.0 software (Hall (1999). The Maximum Likelihood (ML) method was used for phylogenetic tree construction. The best-fitting ML model under the Akaike Information Criterion was GTR (general time reversible) assessed in ModelTest (Posada and Crandall, 1998; Posada, 2006). The highest-likelihood DNA tree and corresponding bootstrap support values were obtained with PhyML (freely available on the ATGC Bioinformatics Platform) (Guindon et al., 2010) using NNI (nearest neighbour interchange) + SPR (sub-tree pruning regrafting) branch swapping and 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software. Pearson’s Chi-square (χ2) test was employed to compare the proportions of animals infected with Trypanosoma spp. across sex, age, species, and season, with a critical P-value set at < 0.05. To prevent false-positive interpretations, P-values were assessed with caution, and no correction for multiple testing was applied due to the limited sample size. We used a Bayesian generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a binomial distribution and logit link to estimate the probability of Trypanosoma infection in wildlife. The model accounted for the fixed effects of species, sex, age, and season, and incorporated a random intercept for sampling site to control for potential confounding.

Results

Prevalence of Trypanosoma spp. in wild animals

A total of 240 wild animals were analyzed, comprising non-human primates (n = 172) and ungulates (n = 68) (Table 3). PCR screening revealed that 12.1% of the tested individuals were positive for a trypanosome infection. Specifically, 22 non-human primates (12.8%) and 7 ungulates (10.3%) tested positive (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S3). Among non-human primates, gorillas exhibited the highest infection rate at 36.4%, followed by chimpanzees at 21.7%, while other non-human primate species demonstrated a prevalence of less than 9.0% (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S3). Among ungulates, bay duikers showed a greater prevalence (14.3%), compared with blue duikers, which had an infection rate of 7.5%. Among the 29 trypanosome-positive samples, 3 were confirmed as T. b. gambiense by nested PCR targeting the TgSGP gene, representing 10.3% (3/29) of infected individuals. Trypanosome diversity in wild animals

Sequencing of the 29 positive amplicons revealed a significant diversity of trypanosomes, leading to the identification of six species and subspecies across different subgenera: Trypanozoon (T. b. gambiense and T. b. brucei), Nannomonas (T. congolense), and Duttonella (T. vivax), along with T. simiae and T. theileri. The distribution of Trypanosoma species among different hosts illustrated in Fig. 3C, revealed significant heterogeneity. Trypanosoma b. brucei was detected in multiple hosts, with a higher prevalence in chimpanzees. In contrast, Trypanosoma congolense and Trypanosoma simiae were predominantly associated with gorillas. Trypanosoma vivax displayed a uniform but low prevalence across several hosts. Trypanosoma b. gambiense was mainly identified in mangabeys and blue duikers, whereas Trypanosoma theileri was only detected at low frequency in chimpanzees.

Additionally, the distribution of trypanosomes among different host species showed variable prevalence, reflecting differences in sensitivity or levels of ecological exposure. For instance, the prevalence of T. b. brucei in chimpanzees was 8.7%, followed by T. simiae at 6.5% and T. congolense at 4.3%. In contrast, infection rates for T. vivax, T. congolense and T. b. brucei were lower in bay duikers, at 2.5%. The infection rate was 3.6% for T. b. brucei and T. b. gambiense, and 7.1% for T. congolense in blue duikers (Fig. 3C).

Geographical distribution of infections

The analysis of the geographical distribution of positive cases revealed considerable variability among provinces. The highest positivity rates were observed in Ogooué-Lolo (22.2%), followed by Estuaire (21.7%) and Ogooué-Maritime (21.4%). The spatial distribution of Trypanosoma spp. illustrated in the map in Fig. 4B, emphasizes the significant geographical and ecological heterogeneity in the distribution of these parasites. Trypanosoma b. brucei was the most widely distributed species, having been identified in six provinces, with particularly high representation in Haut-Ogooué (n = 7) and Ogooué-Maritime (n = 5) (Fig. 4B). Trypanosoma vivax and T. congolense exhibited a relatively widespread distribution, though to a lesser extent. Two trypanosome species, T. b. gambiense and T. simiae, were host-specific and geographically restricted (Fig. 4B).

Factors influencing the trypanosome infection rate and distribution

The statistical analysis did not reveal any significant associations between the presence of trypanosomes and individual factors, such as sex and age, or abiotic factors, including season and geographical location. However, specific trends have emerged. The overall prevalence of infection was higher in females (17.3%) compared to males (8.5%), although this difference was not statistically significant (χ2 = 3.52, P = 0.060). Regarding age, adults exhibited a marginally higher prevalence (13.9%) than juveniles (10.9%), with no statistically significant difference observed between these groups (χ2 = 0.26, P = 0.608). Ecologically, a heightened prevalence was documented during the rainy season (15.63%) compared to the dry season (8.04%), though this variation was also deemed non-significant (χ2 = 2.56, P = 0.109). Furthermore, a comparison of the sampling provinces revealed no statistically significant variation in the distribution of infections (P = 0.224).

The Bayesian generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) revealed no significant effects of sex, age, season, or species on the probability of Trypanosoma infection (Table 4). Discussion

The objective of this study was to assess the potential role of wildlife as reservoirs for the transmission of T. b. gambiense in historical and active foci of HAT, and to determine whether these animals pose a risk to human populations in these areas.

With a prevalence of trypanosome infection of 12.1% in wild animals, our findings are consistent with previous studies in Central Africa that have reported infection rates ranging from 5% to 14% (Herder et al., 2002; Njiokou et al., 2006; Anderson et al., 2011). However, this prevalence is lower than the 18–39% observed in Cameroon and Equatorial Guinea (Njiokou et al., 2004; Cordon-Obras et al., 2015), likely due to ecological differences such as vector density, host species distribution, or methodological factors related to sampling effort and detection sensitivity. Animals inhabiting areas in close proximity to water bodies, such as gallery forests, mangroves, and riverbanks, where tsetse flies are known to be abundant, are more exposed to the infection (Ford, 1963; Smith et al., 2005; Wamwiri and Changasi, 2016). The findings of the present study demonstrated that non-human primates exhibited a higher infection rate in comparison to ungulates, with a particularly elevated prevalence observed in gorillas and chimpanzees. Other primates demonstrated infection rates that remained below 9%. This discrepancy can be attributed to their contrasting ecological behaviors: great apes are more terrestrial, thereby increasing their exposure to vectors, whereas most cercopithecines are arboreal and less exposed to ground-level vector activity (Manaster, 1979; MacDonald, 1994; Remis, 1997). Among ungulates, red-backed duikers exhibited a higher prevalence compared to blue duikers, which aligns with the findings of a previous study by Njiokou et al. (2004). Taken together, these results, in conjunction with earlier studies, suggest that trypanosome infection prevalence varies significantly by host species and ecological context, and is likely influenced by environmental factors such as habitat type, vector distribution, and seasonal dynamics (Simo et al., 2008; Massussi et al., 2009, 2010). Therefore, the observed significance in gorillas should be interpreted as a preliminary indication of species-specific differences in ecological exposure to tsetse flies, rather than as definitive evidence of host susceptibility.

A total of six species of trypanosomes were identified in wild animals through molecular analysis: T. b. brucei, T. b. gambiense, T. congolense, T. simiae (subgenus Nannomonas), T. vivax, and T. theileri (subgenus Megatrypanum). These species have been extensively documented in the extant literature on the fauna of Central Africa (Herder et al., 2002; Njiokou et al., 2006; Anderson et al., 2011; Auty et al., 2012; Cordon-Obras et al., 2015; Jirků et al., 2015). The identification of T. b. gambiense, the causative agent of human African trypanosomiasis, in mangabeys (Cercocebus torquatus) and duikers (Cephalophus monticola) suggests their potential role as reservoirs (Herder et al., 2002; Njiokou et al., 2004; Cordon-Obras et al., 2015). The variation in trypanosome species across host taxa suggests complex host-parasite dynamics. In gorillas, T. congolense and T. simiae were found in equal proportions, while chimpanzees were mainly infected by T. b. brucei and T. simiae (Capewell et al., 2015; Jirků et al., 2015). In mangabeys, the detection of T. b. gambiense is consistent with earlier findings by Herder et al. (2002) and Njiokou et al. (2004, 2006). The detection of trypanosomes in mandrills represents a novel observation, raising questions regarding the previously assumed resistance of this species (Thomson et al., 2009; Uzureau et al., 2013; Capewell et al., 2015; Cooper et al., 2016). Among ungulates, the trypanosomes detected included T. vivax, T. congolense, T. b. brucei, and T. b. gambiense, with infection rates ranging from 2% to 8%, which were lower than the rates reported in Equatorial Guinea (15–40%) (Cordon-Obras et al., 2015b), but are comparable to data from Cameroon and Zambia (Herder et al., 2002; Njiokou et al., 2004; Anderson et al., 2011). The presence of trypanosomes in antelopes can be explained by their frequent occupation of swampy habitats and riverine ecosystems, which are also utilized by humans and tsetse flies. Collectively, these results, when considered alongside prior studies, suggest that ungulates could act as potential reservoirs for human-infective trypanosomes and may play a significant role in the maintenance of the sylvatic transmission cycle of T. b. gambiense. The localized and host-specific occurrence of species such as T. b. gambiense and T. simiae also indicates focal transmission patterns, which are likely dependent on ecological constraints or vector availability.

Despite the absence of statistically significant associations between trypanosome infection and host- or environment-related variables, several noteworthy trends have emerged. The higher infection rate observed in females compared to males was not statistically significant. However, it may be suggested that this higher rate in females is indicative of a biological predisposition, potentially linked to physiological stressors such as pregnancy or lactation, as previously reported in camels and bovines (Bhutto et al., 2010). In addition, the marginally elevated prevalence observed in adults in comparison to juveniles may be indicative of cumulative exposure over time. However, it is noteworthy that this discrepancy did not attain statistical significance. Despite the fact that the associations examined did not attain statistical significance, the trends observed have the potential to reflect authentic ecological patterns that merit further exploration through longitudinal studies incorporating more extensive sampling and multivariate statistical models.

From an ecological standpoint, a higher prevalence was recorded during the rainy season than during the dry season, which is consistent with studies showing increased tsetse fly activity and transmission risk during wetter months (Ohaeri, 2010; Shields, 2017). Although seasonal and geographical variations did not attain statistical significance, elevated infection rates in provinces such as Estuaire and Ogooué-Maritime suggest the presence of local ecological or anthropogenic drivers, including vegetation density, water availability, and human-wildlife interactions, as previously documented in Cameroon and Zambia (Anderson et al., 2011). This finding is consistent with research indicating that periods of increased rainfall are associated with higher levels of tsetse fly activity and a correspondingly greater risk of transmission (Patz et al., 2000; Alderton et al., 2018).

Of particular significance is the host-parasite interaction network (Fig. 5), which demonstrates that certain host species, such as Cercopithecus cephus and Cephalophus monticola, are linked to multiple Trypanosoma species. This finding suggests that these species may harbor multiple species of Trypanosoma, indicating their role as reservoirs for diverse parasites. Conversely, other parasites such as T. theileri and T. simiae appeared to be host-specific, suggesting more restricted transmission cycles that may be influenced by physiological or ecological factors. These patterns underscore the necessity of incorporating considerations pertaining to the ecological functions of the host and the intricate interconnectivity within the ecosystem when assessing the circulation of trypanosomes within wildlife populations. The detection of multiple Trypanosoma species, including T. b. gambiense, in wild animals such as mangabeys and duikers gives rise to significant public health concerns. The presence of zoonotic trypanosomes in wildlife provides support for the hypothesis that sylvatic transmission cycles may persist independently of human cases and act as cryptic reservoirs, as previously suggested in studies conducted in Equatorial Guinea, Cameroon, and Chad (Njiokou et al., 2006; Cordon-Obras et al., 2015; Vourchakbé et al., 2020). While the detection of T. b. gambiense in mangabeys and duikers aligns with the hypothesis of a wild reservoir, its epidemiological role remains to be substantiated. Indeed, in the absence of evidence from the analysis of tsetse fly blood meals, it is impossible to demonstrate cyclical transmission between wildlife and humans. The distribution of T. b. gambiense in wildlife showed significant geographical concordance with historical and active foci of HAT in Gabon (Iroungou et al., 2020). Notably, positive samples were identified in the Estuaire and Ogooué-Maritime provinces, which correspond to regions with documented sporadic human cases in recent decades (Simon et al., 2012; Kohagne Tongué et al., 2013; Iroungou et al., 2020). This spatial overlap provides further evidence of persistent sylvatic transmission cycles that are ecologically connected to areas of human disease risk. While our study does not provide direct evidence of human cases, it highlights the importance of wildlife surveillance as an early warning system for residual transmission. Therefore, our findings support the implementation of integrated, cross-sectoral surveillance strategies based on the One Health model. This would enable more effective monitoring and mitigation of transmission risks at the interface between humans, animals and the environment. Nevertheless, the data presented herein support the need to develop integrated surveillance systems based on the One Health concept, with a view to better understanding the dynamics of transmission.

The presence of zoonotic species in wildlife supports the hypothesis that sylvatic transmission cycles may persist independently of human cases and serve as cryptic reservoirs (Deborggraeve et al., 2008; Truc et al., 2013, 2014). The confirmed absence of T. brucei rhodesiense supports previous reports that this subspecies is geographically restricted to East and Southern Africa. This finding emphasizes the significance of integrating wildlife into surveillance programmes and public health strategies through a One Health framework (Boundenga et al., 2023). Furthermore, the co-circulation of multiple Trypanosoma species within the same geographical areas and host groups suggests that ecological and behavioral interfaces between wildlife, humans, and vectors play a critical role in sustaining transmission dynamics (Boundenga et al., 2022). The observed clustering of infections in specific provinces, notably Estuaire and Ogooué-Maritime, highlights zones of heightened zoonotic potential that should be prioritized for control and monitoring interventions.

Future research should concentrate on three fundamental aspects: (i) quantifying the epidemiological significance of key wildlife hosts, particularly mangabeys and duikers, in sustaining local T. b. gambiense transmission cycles; (ii) elucidating transmission pathways by using molecular analysis of tsetse blood meals to investigate the connectivity between infected wildlife and vector populations; and (iii) rigorously evaluating the spatial overlap between wildlife infection hotspots and historical or active HAT foci to refine risk mapping and target surveillance. These priorities emerge directly from our results and will critically advance the understanding of sylvatic transmission in Gabon. Integrative approaches that combine molecular diagnostics, ecological monitoring, and spatial epidemiology remain essential for developing evidence-based control strategies within a one health framework.

Conclusions

This study provides robust molecular evidence of the diversity and circulation of Trypanosoma spp. in wild animal populations from historical and active HAT foci in Gabon. The detection of T. b. gambiense in both primates and ungulates supports the hypothesis that wildlife may act as cryptic reservoirs and contribute to the persistence of sylvatic transmission cycles. The observed diversity of trypanosome species, coupled with their variable host and geographical distribution, highlights the complexity of parasite ecology in tropical forest ecosystems. These findings offer a potential explanation for the maintenance and sporadic resurgence of HAT in regions considered under control and underscore the limitations of human-centric surveillance strategies. The study further demonstrates the relevance of integrated, One Health-based approaches that consider wildlife, vector dynamics, and ecological interfaces in disease surveillance. In order to enhance early detection and targeted interventions, future research should prioritize longitudinal monitoring, expanded host and vector sampling, and spatial modeling of transmission risk. Implementing an ecologically informed and cross-sectoral surveillance system will be critical for advancing HAT elimination goals and mitigating the threat of zoonotic emergence in Central Africa. Future studies integrating vector blood-meal analyses and longitudinal sampling will be critical to validate the observed host-parasite associations.
