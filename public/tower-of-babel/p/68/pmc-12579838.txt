Organizational Resilience as a Mediator of the Impact of Digital Transformation on Job Satisfaction in Primary Healthcare During Crisis: Evidence From Poland and the Netherlands
Authors: Magdalena Kludacz-Alessandri, Liliana Hawrysz, Wioletta Pomaranik, Katarzyna Żak, Wei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePrimary-care organizations must be resilient to keep essential services running during crises. The recent crisis in healthcare systems caused by the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic has triggered a global increase in interest in digital transformation and the concept of resilience in the healthcare sector. In this context, this study examines how two dimensions of digital transformation —Transformation Management Intensity (TMI) and Digital Intensity (DI)—affect job satisfaction (JS) in primary healthcare centres (PHCs) through the mediating role of organisational resilience (OR).MethodsA survey was conducted among 400 managers of PHCs in Poland and the Netherlands using the CAWI method. Validated scales from the corporate sector were adapted. Reliability and convergent validity were confirmed (Cronbach’s α > 0.80; AVE > 0.50). Structural equation modelling (SEM) was applied using SPSS v29 and AMOS v29 to test relationships between two DT dimensions (TMI, DI), OR and JS.ResultsTMI had a significant indirect effect on JS through OR (β = 0.562, p < 0.001), whereas DI had no significant indirect effect (p = 0.684). The direct effect of TMI on OR (β = 0.728, p < 0.001) and the effect of OR on JS (β = 0.772, p < 0.001) were both strong and statistically significant. In contrast, the direct effect of DI on OR was not statistically significant (p = 0.512).ConclusionThis study advances previous research by empirically testing a mediation model in the underexplored context of PHCs during crisis. It offers novel evidence that TMI rather than DI alone enhances OR and supports JS. The results provide actionable insights for healthcare leaders seeking to strengthen workforce stability and crisis readiness through effective DT.Keywords: digital transformation, organizational resilience, job satisfaction, primary healthcare facilities, leadership

Introduction

In today’s rapidly evolving healthcare environment, digital transformation (DT) has become a cornerstone of organizational adaptation, enabling institutions to respond proactively to shifting demands, technological advancements, and crises. Research suggests multiple reasons why key healthcare entities have been slow to adopt health informatics and seize the opportunities offered by DT.1 Through the integration of digital tools, platforms, and strategic management practices, DT empowers primary healthcare organizations (PHCs) to redesign their service delivery models, improve efficiency, and remain agile in uncertain contexts. The urgency and relevance of DT became particularly evident during the COVID‑19 pandemic, which not only exposed systemic vulnerabilities but also accelerated the adoption of digital initiatives across healthcare systems. Research suggests that organizations investing strategically in DT—especially in leadership and change management—are better positioned to sense environmental shifts, seize innovation opportunities, and reconfigure service offerings effectively.2–4

As digital transformation advances, organizational resilience (OR) emerges as a key complementary capability. Organizational resilience is widely acknowledged as an important capability that enables organizations, particularly service organizations, to absorb shocks and adapt to external disruptions.5 In healthcare, where personal interactions are central and services cannot be stockpiled, disruptions can lead to significant operational and financial challenge.2 The COVID‑19 pandemic starkly highlighted these vulnerabilities, causing unprecedented disruptions in healthcare delivery worldwide. Therefore, building resilience has become a necessity for healthcare organizations in today’s increasingly unpredictable environment.6

In this context, the resilience of primary healthcare organizations is critical. The pandemic further underscored the urgency for PHCs to enhance this capability to ensure continuity of care. It is argued that building the resilience of primary healthcare organizations will reduce their vulnerability to crises, ensuring better preparedness, effective responses, and continuity or minimal disruption of essential healthcare.6

While the concept of OR is relatively new in healthcare, it has been long applied in other sectors.6 Originating in the physical sciences as the ability of systems to rebound from disturbances (“engineering resilience”),7 it has evolved in the social sciences to include adaptive and transformative capacities8 This contemporary view emphasizes the importance of structural and cultural change, including the development of supportive policies, processes, and leadership approaches that sustain performance under pressure.

As healthcare organizations strive to digitally transform and become more resilient, the implications for human capital—particularly job satisfaction (JS)—are increasingly important. Job satisfaction is a pivotal outcome within the healthcare sector as it directly impacts staff retention, engagement, and quality of patient care. Increased levels of job satisfaction are associated with reduced burnout, which was already high before COVID-19 but has been overlooked.9,10 JS also depends on improved teamwork and increased organizational commitment—factors that are particularly relevant during periods of systemic stress or crisis. Understanding the roles of digital transformation and organizational resilience in fostering job satisfaction is essential for developing sustainable workforce strategies and ensuring the continuity of essential healthcare services.

A number of recent studies suggests that digital transformation (DT) can enhance organizational resilience (OR).11 It is increasingly recognized that DT is not just about new technology but involves people, processes, and strategic leadership.12,13 In a complex system such as an organization, DT must include not only the technical dimension (eg, technology) but also the social dimension (eg, leadership, culture).14 However, many studies still equate DT with the implementation of new digital tools, overlooking the broader social aspects that make transformation effective.11

One such aspect is leadership.13 Leaders must act as catalysts for change, effectively communicating the rationale behind digital initiatives, addressing resistance, allocating resources, and fostering a culture of continuous learning.15 In practice, effective DT strategies should integrate participatory decision-making, interprofessional collaboration, and supportive work climates so that digital innovations stay aligned with organizational goals and patient care priorities.13

Despite this recognition, empirical studies rarely examine non-technical elements such as leadership alongside technological elements such as technology.16 In DT research, the technological dimension is still prioritized, while the leadership dimension remains underexplored—particularly in healthcare.

The vast majority of the resilience literature focuses on definitions and principles.2,7 Existing studies conducted at the local healthcare system level emphasize that resilience research should be conducted in the more complex context of primary care.17 The literature also highlights the crucial role of executive support in enhancing organizational resilience (OR), which in turn facilitates effective adaptation to digital transformation processes18 Recent research on digital transformation stresses the importance of organizational support mechanisms to enable effective change, demonstrating how these technologies contribute to the resilience of the healthcare workforce.19 Additionally, the impact of digital transformation on organizational resilience has been examined through deeper cultural and educational components within organizations.20

Together, these contemporary studies underscore the need to examine resilience from multiple perspectives—executive support, digital transformation, and leadership—providing a more nuanced understanding of how these factors interrelate to influence organizational resilience and job satisfaction in primary care settings. It is essential to address gaps in the literature concerning the differential effects of digitalization intensity (DI) and transformation management intensity (TMI) on outcomes such as organizational resilience and job satisfaction to advance research in this critical area. Furthermore, almost no research has investigated how these two dimensions of digital transformation—DI and TMI—differently affect OR and, consequently, job satisfaction (JS) among primary care staff.

The urgency of this gap was heightened by the COVID‑19 pandemic, which simultaneously overwhelmed digital infrastructures, tested leadership agility, and increased risks to workforce well-being. Despite this, empirical evidence explaining how the technical (DI) and managerial (TMI) facets of digital transformation interact to foster OR and, in turn, protect JS in primary healthcare remains scarce. While prior research has often treated digital transformation as solely a technological endeavor, our study adopts a novel dual-dimensional approach that integrates both technological and leadership aspects.The aim of this study is to investigate whether digital intensity and transformation management intensity influence job satisfaction through the mediating role of organizational resilience in primary healthcare organizations. To address this objective, we develop and empirically test a novel cross‑national structural model that positions OR as a mediator between DI, TMI, and JS in primary care facilities. Our research seeks to address the following question: How does organizational resilience mediate the relationships between digital intensity, transformation management intensity, and job satisfaction in primary healthcare during times of crisis?

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to simultaneously investigate the dual nature of digital transformation and its indirect effects on job satisfaction via organizational resilience in the underexplored context of primary healthcare. Moreover, the cross-national design—including data from Poland and the Netherlands—enables us to provide innovative comparative insights with practical relevance for healthcare systems operating under diverse institutional conditions.

This study endeavours to fill the existing gaps by empirically testing a structural model that integrates the key dimensions of digital transformation, organizational resilience, and job satisfaction across two national contexts, Poland and the Netherlands. In so doing, it offers novel comparative insights and practical implications for enhancing resilience and job satisfaction in primary care facilities. We intend to show that digital transformation can improve organizational resilience at both the organizational and individual levels while simultaneously contributing to increased job satisfaction. Our findings are expected to demonstrate that well‑orchestrated digital transformation enhances resilience at both organizational and individual levels, subsequently uplifting job satisfaction.

The remainder of the article is organized as follows: Section 2 synthesizes the literature and develops hypotheses; Section 3 details the methodology; Section 4 reports the empirical results; and Section 5 discusses implications and concludes.

Literature Review and Hypotheses Development

DT have become essential resources in primary care and their utilization has expanded. However, when the Declaration of Alma-Ata was established, the integration of these technologies into health services was complex, costly, and limited.21 Today DT constitutes a strategic and comprehensive organizational shift that transcends the mere adoption of digital tools. While digital technologies pertain to specific solutions, such as electronic health records, telemedicine platforms, and mobile health applications, DT involves a thorough reconfiguration of organizational processes, culture, and mindset. Successful digital transformation in primary healthcare facilities (PCFs) requires the integration of digital tools into routine clinical and administrative workflows, cultivation of digital competencies among staff, and capacity to swiftly adapt to evolving circumstances, particularly during crises. DT plays a essential role in enhancing OR in healthcare. OR in primary healthcare refers to the ability to effectively absorb disruptions, develop situation-specific responses, and ultimately engage in transformative actions to leverage disruptive surprises that could potentially threaten an organization’s operations.17 DT can improve organizational performance, streamline decision-making, and enhance interactions among employees, managers, and patients. Most importantly, it is essential to maintain the continuity of operations in the healthcare sector.22 Westerman et al13 emphasize that DT is a dual challenge involving both technology implementation and people management. Researchers agree that DT may contribute to organizational resilience to external risks.2 This contribution pertains to the technology implementation aspect, which we will refer to as Digital Intensity (DI), and the leadership capabilities necessary to drive digital transformation in the organization, which we will refer to as Transformation management intensity (TMI).

Through investments in DT, organizations can quickly integrate internal and external sources of information, expand the scope and depth of data, accelerate decision-making processes, and significantly reduce response times.23 Investments in DT enable PCFs to manage disruptions caused by uncertain environments better. As demonstrated during the COVID-19 pandemic, these technologies are essential for maintaining operational continuity in the healthcare sector.22 The implementation of DT has allowed PCFs to offer, among other services, remote consultations, monitoring, and self-management alongside digital systems for appointment scheduling, prescription ordering, and access to electronic medical records.24 Research shows that DT helps organizations create networks that enable connections with other companies (eg, supply chain partners), providing useful information and resources to ensure business continuity and build resilience.2 Based on this argument, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1. DI positively affects OR.

Leadership capabilities necessary to drive DT in the organization after Westerman et al13 called transformation management intensity address both everyday challenges and significant disruptions by fostering trust, empowerment, motivation, and engagement among employees and other stakeholders. The key strategic competencies of such leaders include developing a transformative vision, leading individuals to adopt new mindsets, and building a digital culture.15,25,26 Developing a transformative vision involves leaders leveraging DT to transform processes and drive innovation within an organization.26 This requires leaders to look beyond existing strategies and past procedures, envision the digital future of their organizations,25 and align their actions with this vision while making it comprehensible to their subordinates.26 Leading individuals to adopt a transformative vision means coordinating employee behaviour with clear and understandable goals. Researchers have noted that OR is enhanced when leaders practice complex leadership. Rather than controlling and directing, they act as mediators and facilitators of organizational activities and influence the conditions to guide interactions.17 Coordinating employee actions around the transformative vision and involving them in the DT process form the foundation for building a digital culture. Such a culture focuses on empowering employees, motivating them to develop their skills, and creating innovative solutions to handle crises.2 In summary, we propose the following hypotheses:

H2. TMI positively affects OR

JS depends not only on personal but also on environmental ones.27 It is increasingly believed that organizational factors play a key role in shaping the attitudes and behaviors of individuals at work28,29 and are responsible for more variations JS than personal characteristics.27

According to the literature, resilient organizations thrive even in uncertain, unstable, adverse, and surprising conditions.30–32 This fosters a collective attitude among employees and encourages them to make decisions despite uncertainty. In order to cope with crises, it is necessary to build interpersonal bonds between employees and the organization, and such interactions contribute to a pleasant working atmosphere.33,34 In this way, OR allows people can effectively cope with challenges while achieving their goals, which translates into JS.35,36

The literature also emphasizes that a resilient organization enables the acquisition of new skills and encourages learning and innovation.37,38 Such advantages of a resilient organization contribute to the fact that employees perceive the workplace as an environment conducive to using and improving their skills and acquiring new competencies, which leads to achieving a higher level of JS. OR allows people to cope better with different situations, increasing optimism and making them more engaged and satisfied in the workplace.39 Therefore, the following research hypothesis was proposed:

H3. OR positively affects JS.

In healthcare, DT improves the quality of medical services,40 and supports research and administrative activities.41 In this way, DT can be associated with reducing the effort that employees put into solving professional problems, which has a positive effect on their motivation and JS.42,43 There are many studies that examine the relationship between DT and JS and confirm the finding of a positive relationship between the two phenomena with empirical data.44–47 However, such studies have not been conducted in PCFs. The new technologies available to organizations in today’s world offer a huge opportunity to achieve better outcomes in terms of employee well-being. Technology can be used to design support networks to provide better management, as well as to introduce a sense of community into work environments, thus increasing positive flexibility in the workplace.48

OR is a key factor that can impact JS. However, organizations with a high level of OR are more likely to support the DT process and work effectively with new technologies, which leads to positive behavioral reactions among employees. On the other hand, low OR during DT can result in significant employee resistance to change, including that caused by DT.2 OR can, therefore, play a significant role in the impact of DT on JS.49,50 The ability of organizations to withstand major disruptions and crises during DT can, therefore, influence employees’ participation in or withdrawal from this transformation and, consequently, JS.51 For this reason, the following hypotheses were put forward:

H4a: OR mediates the relationship between DI and JS

H4b: OR mediates the relationship between TMI and JS

Materials and Methods

This study examines the impact of digital transformation on OR and, consequently on JS among managers of PCFs. DT includes two dimensions in this study: TMI and DI. The conceptual research model and suggested relationships between the study variables are presented in Figure 1.

Sampling

We present a matched sample of 400 primary care managers from primary healthcare facilities in Poland (200) and the Netherlands (200). The surveys were conducted in the first half of 2024 using two methods: computer-assisted web-based interviews (CAWI) and computer-assisted telephone interviews (CATI). Respondents were recruited through professional online research panels managed by established survey agencies in both countries. These panels consist of pre-verified individuals who agree to participate in research studies. Only panel members holding a formal managerial position in a primary care facility and actively involved in organizational or digital decision-making were eligible to participate in the study. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. In the CAWI mode, consent was obtained electronically through the survey platform after participants had reviewed an information sheet describing the purpose of the study, their rights as respondents, and data protection measures. In the CATI mode, informed consent was obtained verbally at the beginning of the call by trained interviewers. Participation was entirely voluntary, and respondents could withdraw at any time. Stratified sampling techniques were applied to ensure balanced representation across regions and facility types. Recruitment continued until 200 complete and valid responses were obtained in each country, which corresponds to the minimum total sample size required for structural equation modeling (SEM).52 This sample size is sufficient for SEM according to the rules of (a) a minimum sample size of 100 or 200, (b) five or ten observations for each estimated parameter, and (c) ten cases per variable.53 Anonymity was guaranteed throughout the study. Strict eligibility criteria and quality control procedures were implemented to ensure the reliability and validity of the final sample. Quality control measures applied by the panel providers included identity verification, eligibility screening, response time monitoring, and consistency checks. Only responses meeting predefined data quality standards were retained.

The majority of surveyed respondents were women (67.5%), aged 36 to 45 (30.5%), with a medical background (eg doctors, nurses, midwives) (64%) and having worked as managers for 1 to 5 years (35.25%).

Measurement Scales

The questionnaire consisted of two main parts. Part I collected demographic and occupational data, including age, gender, education, and years of managerial experience. Part II measured four constructs: digital intensity (DI), transformation management intensity (TMI), organizational resilience (OR), and job satisfaction (JS), using a total of 26 items. To mitigate potential common method bias, we employed several procedural remedies, including interspersing items from different constructs within the survey instrument and using reverse-coded questions. Furthermore, we assessed the presence of common method bias by conducting Harman’s one-factor test. The results indicated that the first factor accounted for 44.55% of the total variance. Therefore, common method bias does not pose a significant concern in this study.

Items for each construct with their sources can be found in Appendix 1. All measurement scales that were derived from previous studies were adapted to the specifics of the healthcare sector: TMI;2,13 OR,38,54–56 (DI),2,13,57 JS.58,59

Digital transformation is measured using a two-dimensional scale that is applicable to a wide range of contexts. The scale consists of two dimensions: digital intensity (DI) with 6 items and transformation management intensity (TMI) with 9 items.2 DI refers to investments in technology-based initiatives to change the way an organization serves patients and internal operations.13 Thus, increasing DI motivates organizations to explore digital opportunities in an effort to engage patients and conduct business through digital technologies.57 TMI refers to the leadership capabilities needed to drive digital transformation in a healthcare organization. High TMI organizations often include a transformational vision, governance, and culture that aim to coordinate digital initiatives to improve healthcare delivery.13

The OR dimension was developed by integrating two perspectives: adaptive capacity (AC) and systematic control (SC) — with a total of 8 items; It is based on the assumption that OR can be developed through a combination of AC, which creates and develops individual competencies among key employees, and SC, which facilitates the exchange of information and resources both within and outside the organization. SC refers to providing information and resources by organizational systems and subsystems (eg, teams and task forces) to develop organizational capabilities that may be relevant in a crisis situation.38,54,55 AC refers to the competence of individual employees to apply knowledge and skills to obtain the necessary information and resources to understand the environment and develop innovative and rapid solutions in response to external risks and crisis situations.38,56 The Overall Job Satisfaction Scale consists of 3 items to measure overall satisfaction with their current job.58,59

The research utilized a methodology wherein data pertaining to both individual and organizational constructs were gathered at the individual level, specifically from managers operating in primary care settings. JS was characterized as an individual-level construct that directly reflected the personal experiences and perceptions of the participants. Conversely, DT and OR were regarded as organizational-level constructs, yet they were evaluated based on managers’ subjective perspectives of their organizations’ operations. This key informants approach,60 wherein respondents provide insights into both their personal attitudes and organizational characteristics, aligns with established practices in organizational research, particularly in the healthcare context. It facilitates the collection of informed judgments from individuals who are well-positioned to observe and assess organizational phenomena. The integration of variables from different levels of analysis, such as individual and organizational constructs, is a well-recognized methodology in organizational research. This approach is especially beneficial when the objective is to capture the complexity of phenomena that occur concurrently at both individual and organizational levels.

The variables used were measured based on previously tested scales, on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree with a neutral midpoint of 4 – “Neither agree nor disagree”.

Descriptive statistics, internal consistency analyses, and correlation matrices were computed using IBM SPSS (version 29). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and structural equation modeling (SEM) were conducted using IBM SPSS AMOS (version 29). Model fit was assessed using commonly accepted indices: comparative fit index (CFI ≥ 0.90), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA ≤ 0.08), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR ≤ 0.08), and χ²/df < 3. Construct reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha (α ≥ 0.70) and composite reliability (CR ≥ 0.70). Convergent validity was assessed using average variance extracted (AVE ≥ 0.50), and discriminant validity was verified using the Fornell–Larcker criterion. All factor loadings exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.50 and were statistically significant.

Results

Path analysis was performed as the next step in the research using IBM SPSS AMOS v. 29 to verify the theoretical model and hypotheses. The model was constructed with digital intensity (DI) and transformation management intensity (TMI) as the independent variables, organizational resilience (OR) as the intermediate variable, and job satisfaction (JS) as the dependent variable. The final model consists of twenty-six observable variables and four latent variables. Various models were calculated during the research process. The statistically significant model (χ2 = 599.941; df = 282; p = 0.000) was obtained after introducing the correlation of measurement errors and exhibited a good fit. The measurement model fit is good, with incremental fit indices such as CFI = 0.953, NFI = 0.915, and TLI = 0.946, all above 0.9. The obtained results allow for the formulation of conclusions constituting the basis for verifying the proposed set of hypotheses and the theoretical model. The results of the path analysis performed on the given sample provide support for the proposed hypotheses. The second hypothesis, which validates the relationship between transformation management intensity (TMI) and organizational resilience (OR), is supported. The results indicate that TMI has a statistically significant, positive and strong impact on OR (path coefficient = 0.728, p < 0.001), validating hypothesis H2. A higher level of TMI is associated with greater OR in the organization. Digital intensity (DI) was not found to be a statistically significant predictor of organizational resilience (OR) (path coefficient =0.05, p =0.512), so hypothesis H1 is not supported. The analysis also showed that OR has a positive, significant and strong impact on job satisfaction (JS) (path coefficient = 0.772, p < 0.001), so hypothesis H3 is supported.

Discussion

This study focuses on the role of OR in the impact of DT on JS. DI and TMI defined digital transformation in this study. The results revealed no statistically significant relationship between DI and OR (H1 was not confirmed). This means that investment in digital technologies may not be useful in strengthening the organizational capabilities of the organization so that the organization can better maintain its operations in a crisis situation.38,54,55 DI may also not be sufficient in strengthening the competence of individual employees in applying knowledge and skills to understand the environment and develop innovative solutions in response to external risks and crisis situations38,56 This suggests that without proper integration into existing workflows and without supporting employee readiness and engagement, digital technologies remain an untapped resource. Effective use of digital tools depends on supporting employee engagement, and without proper training and integration of digital technologies, organizations may not realize their full potential.64 Engaged employees, supported by appropriate training, are more likely to effectively use digital innovations.65 Our findings emphasize that “more technology” does not automatically translate into “more resilience.” Instead, effective digital transformation requires aligning technology with organizational processes, culture, and human factors, such as training, leadership support, and adaptability.66 Organizations must develop resilience during crises through strategic digitalization and adaptability, rather than relying solely on digital tools.67 In other words, the mere presence of digital tools is not enough; their effective use depends on how well they are embedded in everyday practices and how empowered employees feel to use them creatively and confidently in the face of challenges. This underscores the importance of a holistic approach to digital transformation, where technology and human capabilities evolve hand in hand to support true organizational resilience65 In line with H2, there is a positive relationship between TMI and OR (β = 0.728, p<0.001). TMI equips the organization with a transformational vision, governance, and digital culture that helps employees develop their ability to respond quickly to crises. In addition, leaders with transformational vision and governance can actively develop relationships and work closely with individuals both inside and outside the organization to ensure that digital efforts are moving in the right direction, thereby increasing OR.2,13

H3 confirms the positive relationship between OR and JS (β=0.772; p<0.001). In our study, organizational resilience includes, among others, the ability to accurately understand the external crisis, which means that these PCFs, in conditions of crisis and turbulence, are able to offer appropriate opportunities for development and social integration to their employees.37 Resilient organizations are characterized by workplaces that encourage seeking information, admitting mistakes, experimenting and providing critical feedback.37 This may be the basis for a positive evaluation of work experiences by employees, which will translate into greater satisfaction. Previous research conducted in companies indicates that organizational resilience particularly affects the personal aspect of job satisfaction, which includes the enjoyment of performed tasks, satisfaction with working conditions, offered opportunities, interpersonal relations, and the financial aspect.32,36

By examining the mediating role of OR in the relationship between digital transformation and JS, this study thoroughly examines how DI and TMI activities affect OR and how this factor, in turn, affects JS. The impact of digital intensity (DI) on job satisfaction (JS) through OR was not confirmed in the case of H4a. Increasing DI itself causes a change in work processes, which may not be well received by employees.55 Such transformations often require employees to acquire new skills and adapt to unfamiliar technologies, which can increase stress and reduce job satisfaction if not managed appropriately. The literature emphasizes the importance of continuous professional development that is aligned with organizational strategies to help employees develop the necessary skills in the digital era.68 This argument supports the position that without proper preparation, digital tools may distract from employee satisfaction rather than enhance it.69 Furthermore, without appropriate support structures, including effective training and leadership, the introduction of digital tools may be perceived as disruptive rather than empowering. The implementation of digital tools, if not planned well, can inadvertently complicate work processes and lead to increased workload for employees, thus affecting job satisfaction.70 This indicates that simply increasing digital intensity does not automatically improve employee experience or satisfaction.

Instead, the success of digital transformation in improving job satisfaction depends largely on how well the changes are integrated into the organizational culture and how effectively employees are supported during the transition. This means that job satisfaction depends mainly on the leadership skills necessary to implement digital transformation in the organization. For example, Topçuoğlu et al argue that digital leadership plays a key role in guiding employees through technological change, as effective leaders help alleviate employees’ concerns about the challenges of substitution and adaptation at work.71 Research indicates that when digital transformations are poorly managed, they can lead to increased employee stress and job role dissatisfaction.72 The empirical results confirms that OR plays a significant mediating role in the relationship between TMI and JS in the ever-changing digital environment (H4b). The leadership capabilities required to implement digital transformation enable managing employee behaviors in line with the digital vision, which positively impacts their satisfaction.13 Additionally, OR ensures that digital activities move in the right direction during turbulent times. This motivates employees to develop their skills and develop creative solutions to cope with crises and enhances job satisfaction.2,73

From a leadership perspective, developing transformational leadership competencies is crucial for effectively communicating the vision of digital transformation, motivating teams, and managing change processes. It is therefore important to recognize the potential challenges that primary healthcare managers may face when implementing these digital transformation initiatives. Among the inherent challenges are technological barriers such as limited infrastructure, lack of interoperability, and fragmented implementation, all of which significantly hinder the adoption and integration of digital health solutions.74 Personal barriers also play a significant role, with employee resistance to change often stemming from fear of new technologies or technostress. Such resistance can lead to low engagement, seriously impacting the success of digital health programs. Studies have shown that high implementation costs and insufficient training contribute to staff anxiety and reluctance during digital health transitions75 Additionally, data security concerns pose significant obstacles, as rapid digitization of medical records increases vulnerability to breaches. Addressing privacy issues is essential to building trust in these new systems.75,76 In this context, organizational resilience plays a critical role in enabling healthcare organizations to cope with these challenges. By fostering the ability to withstand adversity, adapt to change, and thrive during crises, resilience acts as a key enabler for successful digital transformation.7

This study makes significant contributions to both theory and practice by deepening the understanding of how digital transformation impacts organizational resilience (OR) and job satisfaction within primary healthcare settings. From a theoretical perspective, the research disentangles digital transformation into two distinct but interrelated facets—technological (DI) and managerial (TMI)—and empirically demonstrates their differential effects on OR.

This nuanced approach advances existing digital transformation theories by highlighting that technology alone is insufficient; managerial capabilities and leadership development are equally critical in shaping resilient healthcare organizations. Moreover, by empirically validating the mediating role of OR between digital transformation and job satisfaction, the study enriches multilevel organizational theory. It bridges the gap between organizational capabilities and individual-level outcomes, showing how investments in digital and managerial resources translate into improved workforce well-being. This extends prior resilience research, which has predominantly focused on business or hospital settings, by providing evidence from primary care—an underexplored context.

Practically, the findings emphasize that successful digital transformation requires balanced attention to both technological upgrades and human factors such as transformational leadership, employee engagement, and psychological safety. These insights support the design of interventions that foster a culture of adaptability and learning, essential for sustaining resilience in fast-evolving healthcare environments. Embedding resilience metrics into performance management further operationalizes the link between digital transformation and job satisfaction, offering healthcare managers tangible tools to monitor and enhance organizational health.From a managerial standpoint, the findings highlight that leadership development is as crucial as technology acquisition. Healthcare organizations should invest in programmes that build transformational leadership competencies, foster employee engagement in co‑designing digital workflows, and create safe environments for experimentation. Concrete actions include establishing digital champions, offering tiered training to reduce technostress, and embedding resilience metrics (eg, service‑recovery speed, staff morale indicators) in performance dashboards. Policymakers, in turn, should complement funding for digital infrastructure with incentives for leadership capability‑building to ensure that technology deployments translate into resilient, satisfied workforces.

This study is the first to demonstrate the importance of organizational resilience in the relationship between digital transformation and job satisfaction in primary healthcare facilities. The model can also be used for future research in a variety of contexts in identifying, explaining, and predicting the intention of managers of primary healthcare facilities. Therefore, it is highly recommended to replicate this study in different environments to generalize the results across domains. This study contributes to the field of organizational resilience research because it changes the approach to measuring OR in healthcare organizations previously tested in business entities and empirically tests how dimensions of digital transformation impact organizational resilience and how organizational resilience impacts job satisfaction in PFCs.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling evidence that organizational resilience (OR) plays a crucial mediating role in the relationship between transformation management intensity (TMI) and job satisfaction (JS) among primary healthcare managers during systemic disruptions. While digital intensity (DI) alone does not significantly impact OR or JS, this appears to stem from barriers such as insufficient digital skills among staff, lack of integration of digital tools into daily workflows, and resistance to change. These factors may limit the effective use of technology, making it insufficient to enhance resilience or satisfaction without complementary management efforts. Effective leadership and strategic management of digital transformation (DT) are therefore essential to unlock these benefits. These findings deepen our understanding of how specific dimensions of DT contribute to workforce well-being and organizational robustness in primary healthcare facilities (PCFs).

This study contributes to the understanding of digital transformation (DT), organizational resilience (OR), and job satisfaction (JS) in primary healthcare facilities (PCFs) during crises. It specifically addresses the research problem of how different dimensions of DT influence OR and, in turn, affect JS among primary healthcare managers during periods of systemic disruption. The aim of this study was to empirically verify whether OR mediates the relationship between two distinct dimensions of DT—digital intensity (DI) and transformation management intensity (TMI)—and JS in PCFs. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to explore this mediation mechanism in the primary healthcare context, providing new evidence from both Poland and the Netherlands. The findings highlight the critical role of OR in mediating the relationship between TMI and JS, emphasizing the importance of leadership capabilities in successfully managing DT. However, the study also found that digital intensity (DI) alone does not significantly influence OR or JS, suggesting that technology implementation must be accompanied by strong leadership and strategic vision.

The findings have several practical implications for healthcare organizations and policymakers. First, they highlight the necessity of strong leadership capabilities in driving DT and fostering OR. Healthcare leaders should prioritize developing TMI by cultivating a digital culture, engaging employees, and aligning digital initiatives with organizational goals—thus overcoming barriers that limit the impact of DI alone. Workforce adaptation must be supported through comprehensive training programs that enhance digital competencies and facilitate employee engagement and acceptance, thereby boosting job satisfaction and reducing resistance. These efforts should be integrated into leadership development strategies to ensure leaders are equipped not only with technical skills but also with people management capabilities critical for transformation success.

Second, while DT is essential for maintaining operational continuity, organizations should prioritize employee adaptation and support mechanisms to minimize resistance and enhance JS. Training programs that equip healthcare professionals with digital competencies and change management strategies should be implemented. Finally, policymakers should develop frameworks that encourage both technological investment and leadership development to ensure a resilient healthcare workforce capable of navigating future crises. By addressing these considerations, healthcare organizations can leverage DT to enhance organizational resilience, improve JS, and ultimately strengthen the overall effectiveness of PCFs.

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, it is based on a cross-sectional research design, which limits the ability to establish causality between the examined variables. Future longitudinal studies could provide deeper insights into how these relationships evolve over time. Second, the study includes a short data collection period does not analyse potential cultural differences in healthcare systems. It focuses exclusively on PCFs in Poland and the Netherlands, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare systems with different digital maturity levels and regulatory environments. Future research should include a broader range of countries, particularly those with varying healthcare structures and levels of digital transformation. Such broader cross-country research would allow for explicit examination of cross-national differences and a better understanding of the contextual determinants of the phenomena under investigation. Third, a limitation of this study is the narrow operationalization of the job satisfaction construct, which does not cover several commonly examined dimensions, such as salary, promotion opportunities, supervision, or coworker relationships. Employing a more comprehensive, multidimensional measurement of job satisfaction would enable richer analysis in future studies. Fourth, relying solely on manager-reported data may introduce response bias and might not capture the full organizational perspective. We suggest triangulating our findings with employee-level data and incorporating qualitative methods, such as interviews or focus groups with staff members, to gain a more comprehensive understanding of how digital transformation is experienced across multiple organizational levels. Additionally, investigating the role of other moderating variables, such as organizational culture, workforce digital readiness, institutional differences, workforce diversity and patient engagement, could provide a fuller picture of how digital transformation influences organizational resilience and job satisfaction. Furthermore, qualitative studies involving employee perspectives could offer deeper insights into how digital transformation is experienced at different organizational levels.
