Intrinsic immune properties of carrier MSC impact on the clinical outcome of children with solid tumors receiving oncolytic virotherapy
Authors: Alvaro Morales-Molina, Lidia Franco-Luzón, Patricia Garcia-Rodriguez, Africa González-Murillo, Javier García-Castro, Manuel Ramírez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur previous clinical data showed that autologous mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) can be used as carriers for the systemic delivery of oncolytic adenoviruses in children with advanced cancers, a therapy known as Celyvir. Despite achieving clinical remissions, treatment responses have been heterogeneous. Here, we sought to determine whether intrinsic biological characteristics of carrier MSCs influence therapeutic outcomes. Transcriptomic profiling was performed on MSCs from responder and non-responder patients to identify pathways differentially regulated according to clinical response. Expression of MAVS (mitochondrial antiviral signaling), NF-κB activation, and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines were analyzed in MSCs and Celyvir products both at baseline and after adenoviral infection. Among the pathways differentially regulated in MSCs according to treatment response, MAVS was identified as a relevant modulator, showing significantly lower expression in Celyvir products from responder patients. Consistently, MSCs and Celyvir products from responders exhibited reduced NF-κB activation and secreted significantly lower levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines at baseline and post-infection. To validate these findings, immunocompetent mice were treated with murine Celyvir prepared using MAVS-deficient (MAVS−/−) MSCs as a model of “silent cells”. Accordingly, murine Celyvir treatment using MAVS−/− MSCs significantly enhanced antitumor efficacy compared to wild-type MSCs. Tumors from animals treated with murine Celyvir MAVS−/− displayed increased infiltration of T cells and NK cells. In summary, carrier MSCs with a diminished antiviral innate immune response—characterized by low MAVS expression and attenuated pro-inflammatory signaling—provide a therapeutic advantage for systemic delivery of oncolytic viruses. To the editor

The Advanced Therapy Medicinal Product named Celyvir employs autologous bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) as cellular vehicles to deliver the oncolytic adenovirus ICOVIR-5 (Fig. 1A). This strategy has been implemented through a clinical trial and compassionate use programs in pediatric and adult patients with solid tumors, showing promising responses, including complete remissions [1–3]. We hypothesized that intrinsic MSC variability may influence treatment efficacy. In this work, we performed transcriptomic analyses to identify genes or proteins associated with the differential clinical responses observed among Celyvir-treated patients. Our results highlight key gene and protein candidates related to MSC behavior and viral carrier efficacy, providing insights that may support optimization of MSC selection and enhance Celyvir therapeutic potential.

Firstly, we obtained transcriptomic profiles from Celyvir products administered to pediatric responder and non-responder patients from the investigation cohort (n = 3 responders and n = 4 non-responders; all neuroblastoma) (Fig. 1B). A total of 113 genes showed significant uncorrected p-values in at least one of three statistical tests when comparing Celyvir products from responders and non-responder patients (Fig. 1C, D and Table S1). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of infected MSCs revealed differential enrichment of pathways related to “viral infection”, “nucleic acid metabolism”, “immune response”, “regulatory signaling of MSCs”, and “cell–cell adhesion” (Fig. 1E). Genes involved in viral replication and antiviral defense pathways were further analyzed using an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) analysis encompassing multiple cellular and molecular subprocesses. Transcriptomic analysis identified five key genes associated with the “defense response to virus” process that were downregulated in responders (TRAF3, MAVS, PCBP2, FASN, and NDRG1) (Tables S2 and S3). Some of these genes, together with additional ones, also showed differential expression in the viral replication process (CLEC5, CHMP2A, RAB29, MAVS, XBP1, TRAF3, NDRG1 and BMPR1B).

An independent cohort of patients (n = 5 responders and n = 10 non-responders), including multiple pediatric solid tumor entities (see “Methods” in Supplementary Material), was used to validate these findings by RT-qPCR. Among the bioinformatically inferred candidates, MAVS—implicated in both viral replication and antiviral defense—showed significant expression differences between groups, while NDRG1, MEMO1, and BMPR1B exhibited non-significant trends in the same direction (Fig. 1F).

Although mitochondrial antiviral signaling (MAVS) expression has been described in T cells, monocytes, epithelial cells, and hepatocytes, this is, to our knowledge, the first report of its differential expression in MSCs. MAVS functions as a central hub in RIG-I-like receptor (RLR) signaling, promoting activation of transcription factors such as NF-κB [4]. Consistent with this role, non-infected MSCs and Celyvir products from responders showed lower NF-κB activation than those from non-responders (Fig. 1G). Responders’ cells also secreted lower levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines at baseline and within the Celyvir product (Fig. 1H, I). This inherently silent profile was also observed in canine MSCs used for Celyvir treatment compared with human MSCs [5], correlating with greater antitumor efficacy in clinical application in dogs with spontaneous tumors [6].

Following in vitro validation of MAVS as a candidate for improving Celyvir efficacy, we next assessed its validation in vivo using C57BL/6 mice bearing CMT64 adenocarcinoma tumors, an immunocompetent mouse model previously developed by our group [7, 8]. This murine cell line is semi-permissive to human adenovirus replication, allowing partial assessment of the oncolytic effect, as adenoviral replication is species-specific. Murine Celyvir (mCelyvir) treatment was generated using wild-type (WT) or MAVS−/− murine MSCs infected with the human oncolytic adenovirus ICOVIR-5. MAVS knockout showed reduced basal activation of pro-inflammatory pathways (pAKT and pJun) (Fig. S1). Mice were systemically treated with single or multiple doses of mCelyvir. Across all treatment regimens, mCelyvir using MAVS−/− MSCs showed higher antitumor efficacy than mCelyvir using WT cells (Fig. 2A–C).

To further characterize the immune response, tumor-infiltrating leukocytes from treated mice were analyzed. While total leukocyte density was similar between treated and untreated tumors, mice receiving mCelyvir MAVS−/− showed a trend toward increased infiltration of NK cells, T cells, and CD8+ T cells compared with WT mCelyvir (Fig. 2D–F). Both treatments similarly reduced the CD4+ /CD8+ ratio (Fig. 2E). Based on our previous clinical observation of increased tumor B-cell infiltration in patients with stable disease [9], we also assessed B cells in this model. Tumors treated with mCelyvir MAVS−/− exhibited a non-significant trend toward higher B-cell infiltration relative to mCelyvir WT (Fig. 2G-I). Together, these results demonstrate that mCelyvir using MAVS−/− MSCs enhances the antitumor efficacy of the treatment in vivo and may potentiate immune-mediated antitumor effects, leading to increased tumor immune infiltration.

This finding is particularly intriguing given that RLRs primarily recognize viral RNA [4, 10, 11], whereas ICOVIR-5 is a DNA virus. However, adenoviruses have been described to express small non-coding RNAs known as virus-associated (VA) RNAs I and II, which allow them to be recognized through the RLR-MAVS signaling pathway [12, 13]. These VA RNAs are also immunostimulatory, inducing IFN-β expression [13]. However, while VA RNAs are present in human adenovirus serotype 5, Kremer et al. have been unable to identify a genomic region encoding VA RNAs in canine adenovirus (CAV), nor have they detected purified VA RNA bands in CAV-infected cells (personal communication, Dr. Eric J. Kremer). This difference may explain the greater antitumor effect observed in our veterinary clinical experience, where dog patients treated with a canine version of Celyvir—comprising canine MSCs and the canine oncolytic adenovirus ICOCAV-17 (derived from CAV-2)—showed superior outcomes [6]. Given that CAV seems to lack VA RNAs, the MAVS pathway may remain inactive, leading to a lower pro-inflammatory state and potentially enhanced antitumor efficacy in canine patients.

In summary, this work highlights that MSCs actively shape the clinical response to Celyvir therapy beyond their function as viral carriers. These findings support strategies to optimize Celyvir by selecting or generating MSCs with a low pro-inflammatory (“silent”) profile [14]. The feasibility of using allogeneic MSCs from donors—demonstrating antitumor activity and mechanisms comparable to autologous MSCs [6, 8]—further reinforces this approach. Extensive preclinical studies have enabled the initiation of three clinical trials evaluating AloCelyvir (Celyvir using allogeneic MSCs) in extracranial pediatric tumors (EudraCT 2019-001154-26), brain tumors including diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma and medulloblastoma (EudraCT 2020-004838-37), and in adult patients with metastatic uveal melanoma (EudraCT 2020-005207-39). The results of this study provide a strong rationale for further evaluation in these ongoing trials and in others using MSCs carrying oncolytic adenoviruses.
