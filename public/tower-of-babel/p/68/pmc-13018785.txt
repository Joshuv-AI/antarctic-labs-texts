Impact of online patient access to clinical notes on quality of care: a systematic review
Authors: Yoko Yoshimura, Geva Greenfield, Elena Lammila-Escalera, Brian Mcmillan, Benedict Hayhoe, Azeem Majeed, Ana Luisa Neves
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccess to electronic health records (EHRs) has the potential to improve the quality of care. Clinical notes, free-text entries documenting clinicians’ observations and decisions, are central to EHRs. Sharing these notes may reduce information asymmetry, enhance transparency and empower patients. However, their impact on care quality remains unclear.AimTo assess the impact of sharing clinical notes online with patients on the domains of quality as defined by the Institute of Medicine (ie, patient-centredness, effectiveness, efficiency, safety, timeliness and equity).MethodologyA systematic review was conducted with no time limit, using CINAHL, Cochrane, OVID Embase, HMIC, Medline/PubMed and PsycINFO. A narrative synthesis method was employed to extract the study characteristics, and reported outcomes were organised using the six IOM quality domains. The risk of bias of included studies was assessed using the Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool.ResultsNineteen studies involving 203 152 participants met inclusion criteria. Outcomes included patient-centredness (n=16), patient safety (n=14), equity (n=6), efficiency (n=4), timeliness (n=0) and effectiveness (n=0). Patient-centredness studies reported high satisfaction (n=6), increased engagement (n=11) and stronger patient–provider trust (n=7). Patient safety studies noted improvements in medication adherence (n=4) and note accuracy (n=5), alongside privacy concerns (n=5). Equity studies found benefits for minority (n=3) and less-educated patients (n=2), with one reporting equitable outcomes (n=1). No significant changes in efficiency were observed (n=4).DiscussionOnline sharing of clinical notes with patients positively impacted self-reported patient-centredness and patient safety, particularly benefiting underserved populations. However, privacy concerns must be effectively addressed, and robust safeguarding is essential to mitigate confidentiality issues. Further research is needed to evaluate the long-term impact on timeliness, effectiveness and efficiency of care.Keywords: Healthcare quality improvement, Patient-centred care, Patient Safety, Patient satisfaction

Introduction

Achieving high-quality care is a paramount objective across healthcare services globally. Quality of care is a complex and multifaceted concept, with the Institute of Medicine (IOM) defining it as ‘the degree to which health services for individuals and populations increase the likelihood of desired health outcomes and are consistent with current professional knowledge’.1 2 This widely recognised and adaptable definition accommodates the evolving challenges of modern healthcare, including the rise in populations living with chronic conditions and the complexity of healthcare systems.

Providing patients access to their electronic health records (EHRs) can enhance the quality of care. In 2009, the USA introduced an incentivisation programme of ‘Meaningful Use’ and the development of ‘Health Information Exchange (HIE)’ to encourage the widespread adoption and effective use of EHRs.3 4 The US 21st Century Cures Act and the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) have further reinforced patients’ rights to access their medical records, positioning EHRs as powerful tools for patients and healthcare professionals (HCPs).5 6 However, interpreting these effects is complex due to the heterogeneity of the interventions considered, arising from a diverse range of functions and types of information included in EHRs. Therefore, further research is needed to identify which specific features of EHRs have a meaningful impact.

Clinical notes (ie, free-text narratives that allow clinicians to document their observations, conditions, interventions and the reasoning behind care decisions in their own words) have garnered interest as a crucial component of EHRs.7 8 Unlike structured data, these notes provide rich, contextual insights that effectively ‘tell the story’ of an individual’s care.8 9 Consequently, sharing these clinical notes with patients may reduce information asymmetry, enhance transparency and empower patients to actively manage their health and disease.10 11 This form of information sharing has also been shown to facilitate shared decision-making by enabling more informed and collaborative interactions between patients and clinicians.12,14 Several initiatives have trialled clinical notes in this context, such as the ‘Open Notes’ initiative, which aims to increase transparency in healthcare by making information more accessible to patients.1115,17

Additionally, sharing clinical notes may introduce risks affecting patients and providers. Complex medical language or releasing sensitive test results without appropriate context can cause patient confusion or anxiety. 1115,17 Furthermore, concerns about the inappropriate disclosure of sensitive information and potential declines in documentation quality have been noted. These factors may increase the volume of patient queries, placing additional strain on already overstretched healthcare resources. Together, such risks contribute to the reluctance among healthcare providers to fully implement or promote clinical note-sharing, despite the growing availability of patient portals. Although initiatives to enhance transparency are expanding, variability in information-sharing practices persists.

Despite these challenges, preliminary research shows that most patients believe the benefits of accessing clinical notes outweigh their drawbacks.18Recent discussions surrounding patients’ rights and data ownership have helped drive initiatives to provide access, culminating in policies such as NHS Digital’s mandate for GP practices to offer patients prospective online access to their medical records and aligning with the NHS’s broader digital health transformation strategy.19 20 Despite progress and growing consensus on shared EHR access, the impact of sharing narrative clinical notes on care quality remains poorly understood. Therefore, this work aims to comprehensively capture and synthesise evidence on the impacts of sharing online clinical notes (ie, free text notes written by HCPs) on the six domains of quality of care, as described by the IOM.

Methods

The systematic review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.21 The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024576820).22

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across CINAHL, Cochrane, Embase, HMIC, Medline/PubMed and PsycINFO on 13 June 2024. The search terms combined free text and Medical Subject Headings terms, covering the following concepts: (1) clinical notes, (2) patient information access and (3) the IOM’s six domains of care. Search terms for the IOM’s domains of care were adapted from a previously published review.23 The search strategy was developed with a medical librarian, and the included studies were limited to those published until June 2024. Terms related to online medical information sharing (eg, patient portals, EHRs) were included in the search strategy. No lower time limit or language restrictions were imposed to ensure the inclusion of all studies on online medical information sharing. The full search strategy can be found in online supplemental appendix 1.

Study selection criteria

This systematic review used the ‘Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, Study design (PICOS)’ framework for structuring eligibility criteria (table 1). It focused on studies involving adults (aged ≥18 years), with any health condition accessing outpatient care services.24 The review targeted studies that evaluated the impact of sharing clinical notes online with patients as a single intervention, excluding studies that focused on non-narrative clinical information or interactive messaging that did not specifically involve clinical notes. Primary outcomes were categorised according to the IOM’s six domains of quality of care: patient-centredness, effectiveness, efficiency, timeliness, safety and equity (table 2). These domains, outlined in the IOM’s ‘Crossing the Quality Chasm’ report, were chosen for this review as they provide a comprehensive framework for evaluating healthcare performance.1

The review included randomised controlled trials (RCTs), cluster randomised trials, quasi-experimental studies, case–control studies, cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, longitudinal studies and cost-effectiveness studies; mixed-method studies were considered only if they included a quantitative component.

Screening and data extraction

Duplicates were automatically removed using three tools (i.e., Embase, Covidence and Zotero). Two reviewers (YY and ELE) screened titles and abstracts independently, followed by full-text screening to determine eligibility. Intercoder agreement was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa, and disagreements were resolved through discussion or consulting a third reviewer. Data from eligible studies were extracted using a standardised data extraction form (online supplemental appendix 2). Outcome results included quantitative evaluations such as effect size, the mean (SD) or percentage of each group, differences between groups, statistical significance and overall quality score.

Risk of bias

Given the predominance of non-randomised observational studies, the Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was used for quality assessment.25 Two independent reviewers (YY and ELE) assessed the bias of each study, and any disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer.

Data synthesis

Due to clinical heterogeneity among the studies, a meta-analysis was not deemed feasible.26 Instead, a descriptive narrative synthesis was employed to analyse the impact of the interventions, with findings mapped to the IOM’s quality of care domains (i.e., patient-centredness, effectiveness, efficiency, timeliness, safety and equity).27 The overall characteristics of each study, risk of bias and relevant outcome data were compiled into the summary table to facilitate the comparison of outcomes across studies.

Results

The searches retrieved 6867 studies (figure 1). After removal of duplicates (n=213), 6645 studies underwent title and abstract screening, of which 6560 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria (Kappa value: 0.956).28 Eighty-three studies underwent full-text screening, with 64 excluded during this phase (Kappa value: 0.808).28 Nineteen studies were included in this systematic review.29,47

Characteristics of included studies

All 19 studies were conducted between 2009 and 2023. Study durations ranged from 4 weeks 39 to 3 years 29 (online supplemental appendix 2), as sample sizes ranged from 935 to 44 951 participants.47 Studies included eight cross-sectional surveys,3133 39 40 42,44 46 seven observational studies,2934,38 45 two quasi-experimental studies 30 32 and two cohort studies.41 47 While all studies involved sharing clinical notes with patients, the comparators varied, including no note-sharing or before-and-after comparisons of note-sharing introduction.

Risk of bias assessment

Except for one study rated as ‘High Risk of Bias’,37 the remaining 18 were rated as ‘Moderate’ risk (online supplemental appendix 3). All studies clearly defined and appropriately categorised the interventions, resulting in a low risk of bias related to misclassification of interventions. All studies involved the same intervention (providing access to patient notes), without considerable variations or heterogeneity, and the risk of bias related to deviations from intended interventions was evaluated as low. In many observational studies,2931 33,36 38 survey respondents were used as the intervention group, minimising the impact of missing data. On the other hand, biases related to patient selection29,3840 and confounding factors2930 33,39 41 were present in almost all trials (16 out of 19 in both biases).

Outcomes

Of the 19 studies eligible for inclusion in this review, 16 examined patient-centred outcomes,29,3436 38 14 evaluated patient safety outcomes,29,3638 39 41 6 investigated equity outcomes31 32 34 35 39 41 and 4 analysed efficiency outcomes30 34 37 47 (table 3). None of the included studies assessed outcomes related to timeliness or clinical effectiveness.

Discussion

Main findings and comparison with existing literature

Sharing clinical notes online can support patient-centred and safe care, with particular benefits observed among historically underserved populations (non-white, older and non-English-speaking patients). However, this systematic review found limited evidence of consistent improvements in efficiency following the implementation of shared access. Concerns around privacy and data security also emerged as significant issues for patients.

Notably, the included studies did not examine the quality domains of effectiveness or timeliness, limiting the ability to draw firm conclusions in these areas. Nonetheless, previous research suggests that sharing clinical notes may support better health outcomes48,50 and facilitate timely access to vital health information.51,54 For instance, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that providing patients access to EHRs had a positive impact on clinical effectiveness.55

Patient-centredness

All 16 studies examining patient-centredness reported positive outcomes.29,3436 38 Specifically, shared access to clinical notes is associated with increased patient satisfaction, deeper engagement in therapy, improved understanding of health conditions, enhanced communication and greater trust in healthcare providers. These findings align with a systematic review suggesting a positive association between EHR access and healthcare engagement.14 Furthermore, it is consistent with other prior research demonstrating that note sharing promotes patient-centred care by improving satisfaction, fostering trust and empowering individuals to manage their health.1630 50 56,60

Safety

Evidence from eight studies included in this review suggests that granting patients access to clinical notes may enhance medication adherence and help uncover errors in their medical records.30 32 33 35 36 38 43 46 These safety-related benefits align with previous studies that emphasise the role of note sharing in enhancing patients’ understanding of their care, enabling the detection of inaccuracies and helping to prevent misdiagnosis or inappropriate treatments.32 57 61 62 Improved adherence to treatment plans, prescription management and error detection benefits patients and may also support caregivers in preventing adverse events.32 57 61 63 64 While some studies have raised concerns about the potential for confusion, misinterpretation or anxiety (particularly among patients with complex conditions),15 60 such adverse impacts were minor in this review, with seven studies reporting no increase in anxiety or confusion.29,3133 34 39 42 These findings suggest that the benefits patients gain through a better understanding of their health conditions may outweigh the potential drawbacks identified by providers, despite privacy emerging as a key patient worry.

Equity

One study found no significant differences in how patients perceived the accuracy and clarity of clinical notes, suggesting an equitable benefit of sharing these notes with patients.32 However, three others demonstrated that access to clinical notes significantly benefited specific patient groups.31 34 39 For example, individuals who are non-white, older, non-English-speaking, and those with lower education levels showed improved recall of care plans for medication adherence and appointment scheduling. These findings suggest that narrative elements in notes can enhance understanding and help reduce health disparities, particularly for patients facing verbal communication challenges.36 Previous research also indicates improved engagement with care plans among marginalised populations when clinical notes are accessible.48 49

Efficiency

The impact of sharing free-text clinical notes on healthcare efficiency appears largely neutral, with no change in utilisation. This contrasts with earlier studies suggesting it could reduce consultations.21 23 While one study did report a rise in patient queries, potentially adding strain to HCPs, this may be influenced by other features of patient portals, such as reminder notifications.30 Prior research found no changes in note length or time spent in the EHR after policy implementation, suggesting that patient access does not increase documentation burden.65 66

Strengths and limitations

This review comprehensively evaluates the impact of sharing clinical notes using the IOM framework, focusing on multiple healthcare quality domains. However, despite a comprehensive search of multiple databases, this review did not capture evidence regarding the timeliness and effectiveness of sharing clinical notes. Due to the challenges of randomising participants in countries where note-sharing is mandatory, this review included both randomised and observational studies, allowing the capture of diverse participants from various demographic groups in real clinical settings.

However, other limitations should be acknowledged. Except for one study,64 all included studies were conducted in the USA, despite no geographical restrictions being applied during the search. This lack of diversity is reflected in the limited evidence collated for the equity domain, as many studies had limited representation of racially, ethnically or linguistically diverse populations or lacked detailed reporting of these sociodemographic factors. Additionally, most study participants were likely more digitally engaged due to reliance on patient portal access. As a result, the findings may not fully reflect the experiences of patients in more diverse or underserved settings or those with limited English proficiency or lower digital literacy. These factors may limit the generalisability of the review to different cultural, economic and regulatory contexts.

Furthermore, a meta-analysis was not possible due to heterogeneity. Instead, a descriptive narrative synthesis was performed using the IOM framework to organise and compile outcomes into a summary table, successfully facilitating cross-study outcome comparisons. While this approach allowed for easier comparison across studies, statistical consistency and quantitative effect estimates were not provided. Thus, the findings must be interpreted within the context of each study. Additionally, outcome assessments rely heavily on self-reported data rather than objective measurements and are subject to reporting and information biases. Although RCTs are recommended as the optimal design for high-quality systematic reviews to reduce bias and examine causality, no RCTs were identified in searches.67

Implications for research

Future research should use objective measures and leverage real-world data to evaluate the effects of online access to clinical notes on both timeliness and clinical effectiveness (which remain currently underexplored) and efficiency (where evidence is neutral). Considering the heterogeneity of the extracted studies, standardising outcomes, measures and tools across studies could enhance evidence quality through pooled analyses and meta-analytic approaches. Additionally, future research should incorporate mixed methods to evaluate interest, uptake and use of interventions that share clinical notes with patients and their caregivers.

Evaluating these interventions in countries without access to clinical notes through phased rollouts also provides an opportunity to assess long-term impacts. Since most of the studies reviewed were US-based, further research from other countries is essential to enhance the global applicability of findings. Researchers must keep pace with the rapid digitalisation of our healthcare services and consider exploring where artificial intelligence (AI) may support this practice when integrated with EHRs. For example, innovations like ambient AI scribes must be evaluated for their impact on clinician efficiency and patient safety to build a robust evidence base for their use in practice. While these tools offer the potential to alleviate documentation burdens, they also raise significant privacy concerns, particularly due to third-party companies recording clinical encounters. To fully understand their implications, longitudinal studies are required to assess their effects on provider workload, patient trust and the adequacy of existing organisational safeguards.

Implications for policy and practice

These findings will have system-level impacts, providing valuable evidence to support the broader implementation of sharing clinical notes with patients. Additionally, although healthcare providers often express concerns about sharing clinical notes, five out of eight studies found minimal or no evidence supporting these concerns. Despite fears that shared access could potentially widen health disparities, this review suggests that marginalised populations can benefit from access if provided with adequate support.

It is also important to consider barriers that might limit information sharing, including patient literacy, physician engagement and the nature of clinical notes, which often contain medical jargon and are not necessarily written in a patient-friendly manner. Therefore, the successful implementation of clinical note sharing will require healthcare organisations to adopt frameworks for securely scaling this practice, including robust training for clinicians and education for patients to optimise its benefits. For example, HCPs must use clear, jargon-free language in clinical notes to encourage active patient engagement and support shared decision-making.68 However, evidence shows that only 26% of primary care physicians began to adopt more patient-centred language in anticipation of patients accessing their notes, highlighting an urgent need for awareness and skill development.56 While these adjustments aim to benefit patients, they also raise concerns about whether this practice reduces note quality, leading other HCPs to misinterpret the notes, potentially compromising care unexpectedly.

Addressing patients' privacy concerns is crucial as platforms for accessing medical records expand.69 A multifaceted approach is essential to prevent unauthorised access, which includes developing robust security systems, establishing legal protections and implementing data privacy policies. Demonstrating these efforts can alleviate patients' concerns and foster trust in sharing clinical notes.70 71

Finally, local initiatives would benefit from national and institutional policies that support establishing the sharing of clinical notes as a standard of care across systems. These policies should also ensure clear communication about both the benefits and challenges of this practice.

Conclusion

This review highlights the significant benefits of online sharing of clinical notes for patients regarding the quality of care, particularly in terms of patient-centredness and safety. Future research should evaluate the impacts on timeliness and effectiveness of care, where evidence remains scarce. The broader implementation of sharing clinical notes with patients should be accompanied by robust training for both patients and providers to optimise language use and interpretation, reliable infrastructure and high-level policies that ensure the sustainability and scalability of these efforts.
