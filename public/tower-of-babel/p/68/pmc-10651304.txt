Impact of immunosuppression level on liver allograft fibrosis after pediatric liver transplantation: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yi-Zhou Jiang, Xin-Yan Zhao, Guang-Peng Zhou, Lin Wei, Wei Qu, Zhi-Gui Zeng, Shan-Shan Wu, Hai-Ming Zhang, Ying Liu, Yu-Le Tan, Jun Wang, Zhi-Jun Zhu, Li-Ying Sun
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Liver allograft fibrosis (LAF) is prevalent among children with long-term survival after liver transplantation (LT). The authors aimed to identify clinical risk factors, with a focus on the impact of immunosuppression (IS) level in the early post-transplant period on LAF.Methods:A retrospective study was conducted on pediatric LT recipients with at least 1-year of follow-up. Cox regression models were used to analyze risk factors associated with LAF, and landmark analysis was used to evaluate the impact of IS level on LAF. Longitudinal analysis was also conducted in patients with paired biopsies.Results:A total of 139 patients involving 174 liver biopsies were included. With 2.3 to 5.9 years of follow-up, LAF was detected in 91.4% of patients (7.9% were significant), up to 88.2% of whom showed normal liver function. Episodes of acute rejection, biliary complications, cytomegalovirus infection, and prolonged cold ischemia time were independent risk factors. Besides, the risk of LAF in patients with relatively low IS levels at postoperative 1–3, 3–6, 6–12, and 12–36 months was higher than the counterparts. Especially, in patients with relatively high IS levels (mean tacrolimus trough concentration ≥5.1 ng/ml) during postoperative 12–36 months, the risk of LAF was 67% lower in the short future (P=0.006). In paired analysis, patients with increased IS levels were more likely to achieve fibrosis-reduction (HR=7.53, P=0.025).Conclusions:Mild to moderate LAF is common among pediatric LT recipients and can appear early and silently. Maintaining adequate levels of IS during 1–3 years after LT seems crucial to ensure protection against LAF.Keywords: immunosuppression level, liver allograft fibrosis, liver biopsy, liver transplantation, risk factors

Pediatric liver transplantation (LT) is now considered a curative treatment for children with chronic end-stage liver diseases and specific metabolic defects, attaining a 20-year survival rate of more than 80%1–3. However, concerns have been raised regarding an increase in abnormal histological findings in children with more than 5 years of follow-up after LT. Liver allograft fibrosis (LAF) is one of the major factors affecting the quality of life of recipients and the long-term survival of grafts. The prevalence of LAF increases from 31 to 52% at 1-year to more than 65% at 5 years. LAF can develop into bridging fibrosis or cirrhosis, and eventually lead to graft loss4–6.

Pediatric recipients require much longer survival than adults for many decades. And due to the relatively low risk of recurrence of primary disease after LT, they represent an ideal model to identify LAF. This type of histological abnormality can be observed in patients with normal graft function or liver biochemistry7–9. To date, the etiology and pathogenesis of LAF remain unknown, and serum-based fibrosis markers have unsatisfactory diagnostic accuracy5,10–12. The potential risk factors for LAF remain controversial in previous studies with small sample sizes6,13–15. LT-related clinical risk factors need to be further explored.

Additionally, as an indispensable part of transplantation, optimal lifelong immunosuppression (IS) management is also crucial for achieving better outcomes in LT recipients. Tacrolimus (TAC) is currently the most widely used IS after LT, and the trough concentration is a protocol item during follow-up16. Recently, high intrapatient variability of TAC level has been reported to be a predictor of inferior outcomes in solid organ transplantation17,18. And most studies on the influence of IS have only focused on the most recent data, ignoring the status and fluctuation of IS levels in the long-term, particularly in the early postoperative period. However, LAF appeared as early as 6 months after LT, indicating that this histopathological change might be the result of ongoing allograft injury. The role of IS level early after LT in LAF needs to be clarified. Therefore, by analyzing the histology of liver biopsies taken at least 1-year postoperatively in a cohort of LT recipients treated mainly with TAC, our study aimed to identify clinical risk factors associated with biopsy-proved LAF, with a particular focus on the impact of IS level in the early post-transplant period on LAF evolution.

Methods

Study population

All consecutive pediatric liver recipients who underwent LT at age 18 years or younger at our center with at least 1-year of follow-up after LT and received a liver biopsy (LB) between June 2013 and August 2019 in our hospital were recruited. Patients with (1) ineligible LB specimens; (2) unavailable detailed clinical information; (3) positive serologic markers for hepatitis B or C virus before LT; (4) etiology of primary biliary cirrhosis, autoimmune hepatitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, congenital hepatic fibrosis, or malignant tumor were excluded. No organs were obtained from executed prisoners. This study was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry. This work has been reported in line with the Strengthening the reporting of cohort studies in surgery (STROCSS) criteria19 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A879).

The baseline LT data comprised age at LT, donor age, donor type, cold ischemic time (CIT), warm ischemic time, and operation time. A history of LT-related complications including acute rejection (AR) episodes, vascular (portal vein stenosis, hepatic artery thrombosis, etc.) and biliary complications (leakage, stenosis, etc.) after LT were obtained. Simultaneously, liver function tests (LFTs), and serological testing for cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), hepatitis B, and TAC trough level, carried out as per routine follow-up protocol, were also assessed.

The diagnosis of AR was defined as patients who exhibited increased liver enzyme levels, had histologic features according to the Banff classification20, and had undergone treatment with intravenous methylprednisolone boluses. De novo HBV infection was considered in patients whose serum HBV-DNA turned positive after LT. EBV and CMV infection was diagnosed when EBV-DNA and CMV-DNA detected by polymerase chain reaction exceeded the upper limit in our hospital (≥1E+3 copies/ml and ≥400 copies/ml, respectively). Data were retrospectively collected from the liver transplant recipient database and hospital electronic medical records.

A cohort study was conducted in all patients; for those with multiple biopsies, only the biopsy in which fibrosis was first diagnosed counted. To assess the evolution of LAF in individuals, the recipients who had more than two biopsies were further enrolled in a longitudinal analysis.

Immunosuppressive regimen

In our center, patients after LT were mainly on a dual immunosuppressive regimen consisting of a calcineurin inhibitor and a steroid. TAC is generally preferred over cyclosporine A (CsA) as the mainstay of CNI therapy, as CsA has increased nephrotoxicity and can cause hirsutism16. The steroid should be gradually tapered and weaned within the first 12 months if appropriate to reach maintenance TAC monotherapy. The target TAC trough level was 8–10 ng/ml for the first 6 months after LT, 6–8 ng/ml until 1-year after transplantation, and around 5 ng/ml thereafter. Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) was added in cases of recurrent AR, chronic rejection, and renal dysfunction to avoid CNI toxicity. And TAC was sometimes switched to CsA when neurotoxicity or post-LT diabetes mellitus was observed. This immunosuppressive protocol was in line with the standard protocols in our center as we previously published21,22. Types and blood trough levels of IS at regular intervals after the postoperative 1 month were recorded.

Most patients in this study were with TAC-based IS. Median TAC levels for the period from 1 to 3 months, 3 to 6 months, 6 to 12 months, 1 to 3 years, and 3 to 5 years after LT were calculated for each individual using Microsoft Excel, respectively, if available. At least two values for each period were considered valid. And the above median values for the different period after LT were used as a cut-off to divide patients into high-concentration and low-concentration group during analysis.

In the longitudinal analysis, two liver biopsies with the longest interval in one patient were selected for comparison. To evaluate the impact of IS level changes on LAF evolution, we defined ʻTAC variation (ΔTAC)ʼ as the mean TAC level during the period of two biopsies minus the mean TAC level during the year before the first biopsy.

Liver biopsy and histology assessment

Percutaneous ultrasound-guided LB was performed when clinically-indicated if the patient had an abnormal liver function, or routine protocol biopsies (generally, protocol liver biopsies were carried out annually after LT). Consent for all the procedures was obtained at the time of the biopsy. LB specimens with Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining were reviewed by an experienced pathologist blinded to the clinical status of the patients. The quality of the sample (length, number of portal tracts) was also systematically analyzed in each slide.

An adequate LB was considered to have a minimum length of 6 mm and contain at least five complete portal tracts. Fibrosis was staged by the semi-quantified liver allograft fibrosis score (LAFSc)23, and scored separately in three main areas: portal (0–3), sinusoidal (0–3), and centrolobular areas (0–3), with a total score of 9 (0=absent, 1=mild, 2=moderate and 3=severe fibrosis). LAFSc 1–2 was defined as mild fibrosis, LAFSc 3–4 was defined as moderate fibrosis, and LAFSc greater than or equal to 5 was defined as significant fibrosis. After reviewing the clinical data of all patients, children who had a score above 3 were included in the ʻliver fibrosisʼ group, while the others were classified into the ʻnonfibrosisʼ group during analysis. Patients who underwent repeat liver biopsies were divided into the fibrosis-reduction (decrease in LAFSc) and fibrosis-progression/stable (increase or same in LAFSc) groups.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean± SD, or as the median (interquartile range) for highly skewed variables. Categorical variables were described by frequency and percentage (%). Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons of continuous variables between groups; the χ 2-test was used for categorical data. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify variables that were associated with LAF. Variables with a P-value <0.1 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate analysis. The interactions between variables were considered, and the results were further suitably adjusted by clinical confounders. The cumulative event probability was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method as previously reported24,25. Because the intervals of TAC measurements varied in each patient, ʻfollow-up timeʼ in Kaplan–Meier method was defined as ʻtime since the last TAC measurementʼ, which estimates the probability of event over time (from the last clinical visit). As LAF is a time-to-event outcome, the underlying risk changes between different time periods of follow-up, it needs to be analyzed separately by time period. The landmark analysis, performed according to a landmark point, with the hazard ratio (HR) calculated separately for events that occurred up to those days and events that occurred between the day and the end of the follow-up period26, was consequently used to evaluate the impact of IS levels on LAF. The landmark points were chosen based on clinical significance and the number of patients at risk in both groups before and after the landmark points to make the distribution more balanced27. Only a two-tailed value of P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. IBM SPSS Statistics 20.0 and R 3.6.0 were used for statistical analysis.

Results

A total of 139 eligible patients with 174 LBs were included in the study. Of all these enrolled children, 109 were with one LB, and the other 30 patients underwent repeat liver biopsies (Fig. 1).

Incidence and severity of LAF after LT

Nearly half of the patients performed clinically-indicated biopsies (n=68, 48.9%). Based on LAFSc, graft fibrosis (with LAFSc >0) was found in 91.4% of the 139 patients included. The majority of patients had mild (LAFSc 1–2) or moderate (LAFSc 3–4) fibrosis; significant fibrosis (LAFSc ≥5) was diagnosed only in 7.9% of patients (Fig. S1a, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A880). According to the acinar distribution of the LAFSc, the incidence of the portal, sinusoidal and centrilobular fibrosis were 74.8, 23.0 and 69.8%, respectively. When evaluating the overall severity of LAF at different post-LT periods, the mean LAFSc in post-transplant years 1–2 was 2.22; and for patients with more than 4 years follow-up, the mean LAFSc was 2.29, with no significant difference (P=0.86). However, there was an increasing trend in LAF severity over time (see trend lines for LAF0 and LAF5–6 in Fig. S1b, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A880). In addition, up to 88.2% of patients developed LAF with normal LFTs (data not shown).

Clinical risk factors for LAF

Table S1 (Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A880) summarizes the univariate analysis of potential clinical risk factors for LAF. The patients who underwent cadaveric LT had a CIT of more than 6 h or warm ischemic time of more than 10 min, episodes of AR, biliary complications, and positive CMV-DNA had an increased risk of LAFSc greater than or equal to 3. Multivariate Cox regression analysis of the variables with a P-value <0.1 in univariate analysis was performed. Only prolonged CIT (HR=3.681, 95% CI: 1.025–13.228, P=0.046), occurrence of AR after LT (HR=5.168, 95% CI: 2.192–12.187, P=0.000), biliary complications (HR=1.954, 95% CI: 1.034–3.692, P=0.039) and positive CMV-DNA (HR=2.488, 95% CI: 1.265–4.892, P=0.008) were independent risk factors for LAF after pediatric LT (Fig. 2).

Impact of immunosuppression level on LAF and its dynamic evolution

A cohort study of patients with nonrepetitive liver biopsies (n=139)

Concerning immunosuppressive regimen at biopsy, 94.2% of the study patients were on a TAC-based regimen (74.8% were on TAC monotherapy, 19.4% were on TAC+MMF, and/or steroid), whereas only 1.4% received CsA as the CNI. There were still 23 patients receiving steroid therapy due to complications such as postoperative infection or rejection (Table 1). The median TAC trough level in the patients corresponding to the sections at post-transplant months 1–3, 3–6, 6–12, 12–36, and 36–60 were 7.7, 7.2, 6.6, 5.1, and 3.3 ng/ml, respectively. To further analyze the impact of IS level on graft fibrosis, patients were divided into the high-concentration and low-concentration group according to the median value in each period as the Method section mentioned. No statistically significant differences were observed regarding baseline and follow-up data between groups. The Kaplan–Meier estimates of cumulative event probability curves were then determined for the two groups at post-transplant month 1–3 (log-rank test, P=0.87, Fig. S2a, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A880), 3–6 (log-rank test, P=0.69, Fig. S2b, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A880), 6–12 (log-rank test, P=0.71, Fig. S2c, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A880) and 12–36 (log-rank test, P=0.69, Fig. S2d, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A880). The risk of LAF in the high IS concentration group was lowered by 5, 12, 11, and 12% (HR=0.95, 0.88, 0.89, 0.88), respectively, although with no statistically significant differences.

As the underlying fibrosis risk changes with different time periods after LT, landmark analysis was used for comparing time-to-event outcome between groups (Table 2). It was found that for post-transplant months 1–3, 3–6, and 6–12, there was no significant difference between groups in the LAF probability before or after the landmark point of 24 months; the results were the same after adjusting for the use of a dual immunosuppressive regimen and the above-mentioned clinical risk factors. For post-transplant months 12–36, the high-concentration group (≥5.1 ng/ml) was shown to have a reduced risk of LAF in the following 8 months (HR=0.33, 95% CI: 0.15–0.72, P=0.006; Fig. 3). A statistically significant difference was still seen after adjustment (Table 2). In further stratified analyses, the impact of low TAC level on LAF was extremely strong in recipients with elevated transaminase, deceased donor liver, biliary complications/AR, or prolonged CIT (all with P<0.05, Fig. 4).

Longitudinal analysis of patients with repeated liver biopsies (n=26)

After excluding four patients who discontinued TAC due to post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease, a total of 26 patients were eligible for the paired longitudinal study. The distribution of fibrosis stage for the two times of LBs is shown in Figure 5A. In portal and centrolobular areas, a conversion from the moderate/severe fibrosis stages to none/mild stages was observed. The numbers of patients with moderate fibrosis (LAFSc ≥3) and significant fibrosis (LAFSc ≥5) in the second biopsies were 5 and 2 less, respectively. The dynamic change between two biopsies for each patient is displayed in Figure 5B. It was found that 16 patients had progressed/stable LAF (increase or same in LAFSc, shown in red or white); 10 had ameliorated LAF (decrease in LAFSc, shown in green).

With regard to the immunosuppressive regimen after the first-time biopsy, ten patients were administered with additional IS, 10 with an increased dosage of IS, and others remained the same (Fig. 5C). Among the four patients who had a significant fibrosis (LAFSc 5–6) in the first biopsy, all of them had a regressed fibrosis under dual therapy with a combination of steroid/MMF or increased levels of IS (with an improved LAFSc by 1–4, respectively). Another one patient who progressed to significant LAF (LAFSc=5) with additional IS showed biliary complications, and need a surgical treatment. To further investigate the impact of TAC level on LAF evolution, we compared the fibrosis-progression/stable and fibrosis-reduction groups. There were no statistically significant differences in demographic and clinical data, including the time interval of the two biopsies between the two groups (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A880). The Cox regression model showed that the group with an increased level of TAC after the first LB (ΔTAC ≥−0.41 ng/ml) was significantly associated with a higher fibrosis-reduction probability (HR=5.39, P=0.04, Fig. S3, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A880). After adjustment for using additional IS after the first biopsy, there was still a statistically significant difference (HR=7.53, P=0.025).

Discussion

In the present study, we found that the histologically-proved mild to moderate LAF was extremely common even in children with normal liver function, and the severity tends to increase with time. Except for clinical status including AR, biliary complications, CMV infection, and prolonged CIT, a relatively low IS level during the early post-transplant period (1–3 years after LT) seems also a chronic risk factor. Furthermore, the degree of LAF may be improved by administering adequate levels of IS.

LAF is one of the major factors affecting long-term graft survival in children. We confirmed that LAF is common in patients with at least one year of follow-up after transplantation, but compared with previous studies, the proportion was relatively high5,6,9,13,28,29. One reason for this could be the semiquantitative fibrosis scoring system LAFSc we used, which was not confined to the portal area, avoiding underestimation as with the METAVIR and Ishak scores. By assessing three different areas specifically adapted to liver allografts, LAFSc has become a more accurate tool for staging LAF in pediatric LT recipients. In addition, 51% of patients had abnormal liver enzymes when biopsies were obtained instead of protocol biopsies in stable patients; thus, the results in this study may be overestimated. However, most of the patients had mild or moderate LAF, and only 7.9% showed a LAFSc of more than 5, which was similar to the data from other centers10,30. This was also consistent with other reports that the majority of LAF after transplantation was mild to moderate30. But long-term evolution and impact still need further investigation.

In our study, up to 88% of patients developed LAF despite normal liver enzymes, and LFTs were not significantly correlated with fibrosis severity. In previous studies, serum markers including hyaluronic acid (HA), tissue inhibitor of matrix metalloproteinase 1 (TIMP1), Fibrotest, APRI, and the Fibrosis-4 Index (FIB-4) had unsatisfactory diagnostic accuracy after LT; hepatic imaging and transient elastography were of limited use in detecting LAF10,11,31,32. Currently, LB remains the gold standard for detecting LAF, especially in a significant proportion of long-term asymptomatic survivors. Considering the invasiveness of LB, determining whether there are clinical factors related to the development of LAF is crucial. Previously, associations were found between CIT, biliary complications, graft type, young recipient age, AR episodes, post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease, multiple elevations of transaminase, interface inflammation, subclinical rejection, and LAF8,15,29,33,34, which varied among studies. We also analyzed donor/recipient characteristics and transplant-related factors. To ensure the idiopathy of LAF, patients with positive serologic markers for HBV/HCV before LT were excluded. It was demonstrated that episodes of AR, biliary complications, CMV infection, and prolonged CIT were independent risk factors for LAF. Other related factors previously reported such as donor-specific antibodies were not included in this study due to a large number of missing values. In addition, given the centralization of fibrosis scores, the analysis was not stratified by areas, which need to be studied in the future. Although the pathogenesis of LAF still needs further investigation, we have explored the clinical factors that can potentially lead to graft fibrosis, and when patients have any of these factors, additional timely vigilance is necessary.

The immunosuppressive regimen after LT should be adjusted over time depending on the clinical status of the patients. TAC is currently the most widely used immunosuppressive drug after LT, and trough concentration measurement is standard routine during follow-up. This value reflects the individual’s immune status to a certain extent, thus contributing to the optimization of immunosuppressive therapy. To date, the causal link between IS and graft outcome remains uncertain35. Some claimed that patients who achieved the TAC < 5 ng/ml state in less than 4 years after LT were relatively safe36. Other researchers have found that recipients with a TAC trough level no lower than about 4 ng/ml as maintenance IS are recommended for better long-term graft survival37,38. It was also observed in children that relatively high TAC levels contributed to fewer histological findings in protocol biopsies39, whereas reduced IS caused a significant increase in fibrosis40. The adjustment of IS seems to be a major factor for the long-term outcome. What is noteworthy about the above-mentioned studies is that they are all based on several most recent IS trough concentrations before the LB for each patient. However, it is known that multiple factors including administration dose, medication formulation, and nonadherence can cause erratic IS level fluctuations16,41. Furthermore, new evidence suggests that the development of LAF is an early rather than a late event42. Hence, a detailed investigation of IS level in the early post-transplant period is necessary for LAF.

Our study demonstrated that patients with lower TAC levels in different periods during the first 3 years after LT tended to have a higher risk of LAF. Moreover, a TAC trough level below 5.1 ng/ml during post-transplant years 1–3 was a definite adverse factor for LAF in the short future, with three times the risk. The results were still robust after adjusting for other clinical factors and combination therapy with MMF/steroid. Therefore, we speculated that low IS levels in the early post-transplant period might be a potential primitive trigger for future LAF. Besides, another study recently observed a significant progression of LAF between 2 and 5 years from LT, but not between 5 and 10 years of follow-up. In addition, LAF seems to remain stable in the 7–10 post-transplant years, without continuous progression30,35. All these reports further support our notion that LAF appears early and silently in pediatric recipients, and the chronic injury due to under-IS during the first couple of years after LT leads to persistent fibrosis or even progression. Maintaining adequate levels of IS in the first three years seems crucial to protect from LAF. Considering that medical nonadherence is a key issue in adolescents, especially during transition periods from pediatric to adult clinical care35, support and cooperation from family, physicians, and social service professionals are helpful43–45. Our study also provided a reference threshold of TAC level for close monitoring.

LAF is a dynamic process that experiences progression or regression29. ʻLAFScʼ is just suitable to describe the evolution of fibrosis, allowing us to assess the effect of therapeutic intervention. By analyzing patients who underwent repeat liver biopsies, we further explored the impact of IS level on the evolution of LAF. More than one-third of them had LAF regression after adjustment of immunosuppressive protocols; patients with increased IS levels after the first biopsy were more likely to achieve reduced fibrosis. Although the number of cases in this longitudinal analysis was relatively small, it is reasonable to infer that the severity of fibrosis may be alleviated by administering adequate levels of immunosuppressive therapy. This agrees with the growing evidence that innate immunity may play an important role in LAF progression. However, further clarification is needed46.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study from a single center including liver biopsies with different intervals, which makes the level of evidence not strong enough. Second, we failed to describe risk factors such as donor-specific antibody and autoantibody positivity, which have been indicated to play a role in the development of graft injury13,30 due to the lack of data. Moreover, longer-term histological follow-up including the evolution of LAF is still needed in the future.

Nevertheless, our findings are of great significance as this is the first study quantitatively analyzing the impact of IS levels at different periods during the early post-transplant period on LAF, taking into account extreme values and fluctuations under special circumstances. It may allow early detection of patients with a potentially high risk of LAF according to IS level and other risk factors in the future; help clinicians to alter the IS regimen in time and eventually prolong the life of liver graft, which is the main value of this study. This is also a wake-up call for those with problems of medication nonadherence and IS withdrawal without histological monitoring.

In conclusion, mild or moderate LAF is common among pediatric LT recipients with more than 1-year of follow-up. Apart from episodes of AR, CMV infection, biliary complications, and prolonged CIT, low IS level in the early post-transplant period (1–3 years after LT) is an important risk factor for LAF. Therefore, we postulated that LAF can appear early and silently, with chronic injury due to under-IS as a potential trigger. Maintaining adequate IS levels during the first 3 years after LT seems crucial to ensure protection against LAF.
