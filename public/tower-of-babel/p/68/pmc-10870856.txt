Sustained Improvement of Cystic Fibrosis Lung Disease following Initiation of Elexacaftor/Tezacaftor/Ivacaftor Therapy: Lessons from Real-World Studies
Authors: Eva Steinke, Mirjam Stahl
Published: 2023

The past four decades have seen significant advances in therapy for cystic fibrosis (CF), with the recent advent of CFTR (CF transmembrane conductance regulator) modulators as probably the most important step. The currently most effective therapy, approved for the majority of people with CF (pwCF), is the triple combination of the two CFTR correctors elexacaftor (ELX) and tezacaftor (TEZ) and the potentiator ivacaftor (IVA) (1–4). PwCF with at least one F508del allele (European and Australian approval; pwCF ⩾6 years of age) or at least one F508del allele or a CFTR mutation proven to respond to this treatment in preclinical studies (U.S. approval; pwCF ⩾2 years of age) can be treated with ELX/TEZ/IVA. Clinical trials in adults and adolescents (2–4), as well as school-age (5, 6) and preschool (7) children, clearly demonstrated alleviation of CF lung disease by means of percentage predicted FEV1, lung clearance index (LCI), and a reduced pulmonary exacerbation rate but also of extrapulmonary CF manifestations with reduced body mass index and self-reported quality of life.

Real-world studies were able to reproduce these findings and even contribute further to our understanding by, for example, investigation of CF lung disease by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (8, 9) and nitrogen (N2) multiple-breath washout (MBW), not only in patients with preserved lung function but in those with a wide range of baseline FEV1 values. Furthermore, longitudinal investigation of inflammatory markers in the airways of pwCF revealed both a substantial improvement after treatment initiation with ELX/TEZ/IVA to follow-up over one year and a substantial residual degree of neutrophilic inflammation (10).

With an overall diminished degree of CF lung disease, it is likely that a substantial proportion of pwCF will not only reach adulthood with normal FEV1 but hopefully remain at that status for decades. However, we have learned that there can be quite tremendous airway abnormalities despite normal FEV1 (11). These can be detected using both more sensitive lung function assessments, such as the MBW, and cross-sectional imaging of the lung using computed tomography (CT) and MRI (11, 12).

In this issue of the Journal, McNally and colleagues (pp. 917–929) offer interesting additional information on the growing body of evidence supporting an effect of ELX/TEZ/IVA treatment not only in clinical trials but also in real-world settings in pwCF (13). They followed a cohort of 117 pwCF aged 12 years and older for 1 year after the initiation of ELX/TEZ/IVA treatment as part of the RECOVER (Real World Clinical Outcomes with Novel Modulator Therapy Combinations in People with CF) study. Improvements in FEV1 were in the range reported in clinical trials. The decline in LCI was sustained compared with another short-term real-world study in the same age group (Figure 1) (8). However, a real comparison is hampered by the fact that different MBW software versions from the same provider were used to determine the LCI in these studies. Recently, a cross-talk error in the oxygen and carbon dioxide sensor of the MBW device Exhalyzer D (Eco Medics) was described, leading to overestimation of N2 and, therefore, LCI (14). Correction of this error is possible by using the most recent versions of Spiroware (3.3.1 and above) (Eco Medics). Several CF centers using MBW in their clinical routines have switched to this newer software version, which was been used in a study by Graeber and colleagues (8). Absolute LCI values and absolute changes in LCI are substantially lower, but still significant, using the corrected software version (15). In the meantime, until the new software version has been implemented for clinical trials and routine monitoring, it would be worth reporting both the original values determined using one of the older software versions and the LCI values and changes determined by reanalysis using the new version to enable real comparability with other studies and assessments in real life (Figure 1). In addition, it is encouraging that structural CF lung disease not only decreases in pwCF aged 12 years and older after approximately 3 months of ELX/TEZ/IVA treatment (8) but remains improved after 12 months using the CT-based Perth-Rotterdam Annotated Grid Morphometric Analysis for CF score (Figure 1) (13). Interestingly, the same findings showed improvement on both MRI and CT, especially for mucus plugging (8, 13).

Overall, real-world studies such as RECOVER add to our understanding of the course of lung disease in CF under CFTR modulator treatment. The differences in LCI between this study and another real-world study using the updated MBW software version highlight that N2 LCI values derived using different software versions are not interchangeable. To enable a better comparison with data acquired in routine monitoring, LCI values derived with both commonly used software versions should be reported if the measurements were not performed using the newest version available. It is encouraging to see which improvements can be achieved and sustained for at least 12 months after the initiation of the highly effective CFTR modulator therapy with ELX/TEZ/IVA in adolescents and adults with CF. Given the results of clinical trials on ELX/TEZ/IVA in younger pwCF, it will be most interesting to see the potential of this therapy in stages of milder CF lung disease, maybe enabling real prevention of this CF manifestation for the first time.
