Treatment of extra-articular scapula fracture malunion using pre-operative three-dimentional printing: a case report and overview of the literature
Authors: Dena Akhoundzadeh, Stefan F van Wonderen, Bouke W Hepkema, Johannes GG Dobbe, Charlotte M Lameijer, Leo MG Geeraedts
Published: 2026

Introduction

Scapular fractures are rare, comprising less than 1% of all fractures and 3%-5% of shoulder girdle fractures.5,7 The average patient age is 41 years, with 78.4% of cases occurring in males.4 Over 50% of scapular fractures result from high-energy trauma, particularly motor vehicle accidents, and 90% of patients experience associated injuries.32,40 Most scapular body fractures heal well with nonoperative management due to the scapula's rich blood supply and muscular coverage, yielding high patient satisfaction.16 However, intra-articular glenoid fractures often require surgical intervention to restore alignment and shoulder function, as early surgery has been linked to better outcomes and fewer complications.24,28 For extra-articular fractures, Cole et al6 identified key surgical indications, with union rates ranging from 94.7% to 100%.5 Malunion, though rare, can impair shoulder function due to chronic pain, scapulothoracic dyskinesis, or nonunion.29,41 In cases of malunion, such as the one presented in this review, pre-operative planning using three-dimensional (3D) printing technology plays a critical role. Beyond serving as a visual aid to better understand complex deformities, 3D-printed models have been used in prior studies to develop patient-specific surgical guides, optimize osteotomy orientation, and even design customized implants. These applications enable precise anatomical restoration and enhance surgical accuracy, ultimately leading to better functional outcomes.

Methods

We present the case of a female patient who sustained a comminuted scapula fracture following a motorcycle accident, which resulted in a symptomatic malunion. Surgical treatment was performed, and follow-up was conducted up to 1 year post-operatively. To evaluate the outcomes, 2 patient-reported outcome measures were used: the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (DASH) questionnaire14 and the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Upper Extremity v2.0 (PROMIS-UE v2.0).37 Both questionnaires are validated tools frequently used in the assessment of upper extremity function. The DASH is widely validated and has demonstrated high reliability and validity in measuring functional outcomes in patients with upper extremity injuries.38 A score of 0 on the DASH indicates no disability, whereas a score of 100 reflects severe disability. The minimal important change (MIC) for the DASH has been established as approximately 10 points,8,31 meaning that a change of 10 points or more is considered clinically significant.30 The PROMIS-UE v2.0 is a more recent tool for evaluating upper-extremity function and has shown excellent structural validity in the Dutch population.14,36 PROMIS measures are scored on a T-score metric, where 50 represents the mean of the reference population, and each 10-point difference corresponds to one standard deviation. A change of 3 to 4 points in PROMIS-UE scores is considered the MIC, indicating a clinically relevant change in function.3,35 During the follow-up period, active and passive range of motion (ROM) were measured to objectively track the patient's functional recovery. ROM assessments were supplemented by reviewing relevant studies identified through a structured search in PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar using search terms such as "scapula fracture" and "malunion."

Reference lists of relevant articles were screened to ensure comprehensive coverage. The DASH assesses upper extremity disability and symptoms, while the PROMIS-UE v2.0 evaluates physical function of the upper extremity on a T-score scale, where 50 reflects the reference population mean.15,42 Both questionnaires are validated for use in patients with shoulder and arm conditions. Written consent was obtained from the patient for participation and publication of this case study.20,37

Case presentation

A 51-year-old female, a high-demand acrobat and actress, presented to the emergency department following a motor vehicle accident with multiple injuries. These included a comminuted open transverse midshaft femoral fracture on the right with severe displacement (Gustilo-Anderson type IIIA), a comminuted open supracondylar distal femur fracture on the right with associated soft tissue injury (Gustilo-Anderson type IIIA)13 a radial head fracture (left), a comminuted fracture of the second metacarpal bone on the right, and a comminuted extra-articular scapular body fracture on the right with displacement. The patient was hospitalized for 29 days. Treatment for the femoral fracture involved external fixation, the insertion of a retrograde femoral nail (T2 Femoral Nailing System), multiple surgical débridements, vacuum-assisted closure therapy, and split-thickness skin grafting. The fractures of the scapula, second metacarpal, and radial head were initially managed nonoperatively.

Five months post-trauma, the patient presented with persistent pain and restricted movement of the left scapula, confirmed by computed tomography (CT) imaging, which revealed a malunion of the left scapula blade with inferolateral displacement (Fig. 1A). CT of the scapula showed a severely displaced comminuted scapular body fracture with consolidation in bayonet position, including medial displacement of the margo medialis by 4 cm (Fig. 1B), medial varus angulation of 30° (Fig. 1C), and lateral varus angulation of 45° (Fig. 1D). In addition, an ultrasound examination of the shoulder was performed to exclude rotator cuff injury.

Pre-operative 3D CT model of scapular malunion (left) (A). A printed 3D model showed dislocation of margo medialis left by 4 cm (B), medial varus angulation left measured at 30° (C), lateral varus angulation left measured at 45° (D). CT, computed tomography; 3D, three-dimensional.

Prior to surgery, the patient underwent conservative management consisting of initial immobilization followed by a structured course of physical therapy aimed at restoring shoulder mobility and strength. Despite these measures, she continued to experience persistent pain, functional limitation, and visible scapular deformity, which motivated the decision for corrective osteotomy.

Clinical examination also demonstrated a marked asymmetry of the shoulder girdle, with visible deformities in the left scapular region (Fig. 2, A-D). Shoulder ROM was systematically assessed using a goniometer. To differentiate between glenohumeral (GH) and scapulothoracic contributions, ROM was measured both with and without manual stabilization of the scapula. On the affected side, active shoulder elevation was limited to 100°, compared to 105° on the contralateral side, while passive GH elevation with scapular stabilization reached 105° bilaterally. Active abduction was 70° on the affected side compared to 85° on the contralateral side, with scapular stabilization revealing restriction of isolated GH abduction. Internal rotation reached only to the level of L4, and external rotation was 45° on the affected side compared to 60° on the contralateral side. These findings confirmed impaired GH motion in addition to scapulothoracic dyskinesis, supporting the indication for surgical correction.

Patient with reduced ROM pre-operative active elevation (A), active abduction (B), passive external rotation (C), active internal rotation (D). ROM, range of motion

Active shoulder abduction was 70° on the affected side compared to 85° on the contralateral side. The passive ROM for shoulder abduction was 105° on the affected side, as measured with both GH and scapulothoracic movement. However, when the scapula was manually stabilized to isolate GH motion, passive GH abduction was limited to 4°, indicating that almost all of the abduction movement was compensatory from the scapulothoracic joint rather than true GH function. The mild limitation observed on the unaffected side was attributed to generalized discomfort and protective posture during early recovery rather than structural pathology. These findings suggest that while the GH joint itself likely retains good passive function, the limitations are primarily due to impaired scapulothoracic mobility resulting from the malunion.

The patient demonstrated internal rotation to the level of L4, and external rotation was 45° on the affected side compared to 60° on the contralateral side. Scapulothoracic motion was assessed by observing scapular kinematics during active elevation and abduction, with and without manual scapular stabilization. Both medial and lateral rotation of the scapula were notably limited (Table I), accompanied by visible scapular winging during elevation. Specific clinical tests for muscular imbalance revealed preserved function of the trapezius, rhomboids, and serratus anterior, and no clinical signs of long thoracic or spinal accessory nerve palsy were present. In addition, no crepitation, snapping, or catching was noted during shoulder motion, supporting the conclusion that the functional impairment was secondary to the bony malunion rather than neuromuscular pathology.

Given the patient's persistent poor shoulder function in the context of a significant scapular malunion, and despite a course of rehabilitation, the decision was made to proceed with corrective osteotomy. While other factors such as prolonged immobilization, polytrauma-related deconditioning, and possible post-traumatic capsulitis may have contributed to the functional limitation, the degree of bony deformity was considered a major factor impeding recovery. A 3D model was produced and printed to assist in preoperative planning, providing detailed insight into the malunion (Fig. 1, A–D).

A correction osteotomy and open reduction and fixation were performed six months after initial injury by 2 senior consultant trauma surgeons (LG, CL). The patient was placed in a lateral decubitus position using a carbon table. A modified Judet approach was performed.11 Muscles were detached from the medial to the lateral scapular border while gradually protecting the neurovascular structures. A series of osteotomies were performed along the fracture pattern. Medial remodeling was performed using a sagittal saw, and lateral reposition was performed using an osteotome, resulting in correction of the invalidating severe valgus deformation. Fixation was achieved medially by applying a curved 9-hole Acumed dynamic compression plate. Lateral fixation was achieved by application of a straight 10-hole Acumed dynamic compression plate. Ultimately, the surgical site was closed over a suction drain.

Post-operatively, the patient was placed in a shoulder vest for 1 week and subsequently in a sling for an additional five weeks, with a restriction of maximum 40° abduction and anteflexion. Supervised physiotherapy was initiated immediately after sling removal, while pendulum exercises and passive-assisted mobilization were already started during the immobilization period under guidance of a physical therapist to prevent capsular stiffness. The rehabilitation protocol included progressive active-assisted ROM exercises from week 6 onward, advancing to active strengthening of the rotator cuff and periscapular muscles after 10 weeks. Specific attention was given to scapular stabilization training and gradual return to functional tasks. The patient was closely monitored to balance protection of the osteotomy site with early restoration of mobility and strength. Two days after surgery, the patient was discharged pain-free without post-operative complications and referred to a primary care physical therapist to continue this structured rehabilitation program.

At the 12-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated significant improvements in active ROM, indicating a successful post-operative outcome. Notably, post-operative active ROM values on the operated side exceeded the pre-operative ROM of the contralateral shoulder. This was explained by the fact that the contralateral shoulder had also been mildly limited pre-operatively due to generalized discomfort and deconditioning after polytrauma. With ongoing rehabilitation, both shoulders eventually reached full, symmetrical ROM.

As detailed in Table I, both elevation and abduction reached full motion at 180°, while internal rotation improved to the Th8 level (Fig. 3A), and external rotation reached 80° (Fig. 3B). These values represent marked enhancements from pre-operative conditions, reflecting substantial gains in shoulder movement and function. This progressive improvement in active range of motion during follow-up is shown in Fig. 4. Patient-reported outcomes also showed significant progress, as indicated by the DASH and PROMIS scores (Table II). The DASH score showed an improvement from 62.5 pre-operatively to 58.3 at 6 months post-operatively. The work/sport component of the DASH decreased from 100 to 25, indicating that although the patient had been severely limited in daily and professional activities before surgery despite conservative treatment, substantial functional gains were achieved afterward. PROMIS T-scores increased from 27.0 pre-operatively to 40.9 at 6 months.

A patient-specific 3D-printed model of the scapular malunion was created as part of the pre-operative planning process. This model allowed the surgical team to appreciate the complex 3D deformity more accurately than on CT renderings alone, to simulate the osteotomy cuts pre-operatively, and to select and contour the fixation plates before surgery. These advantages improved surgical accuracy, shortened intraoperative decision-making, and facilitated anatomical restoration.

At 6 months, the patient showed meaningful improvements in upper extremity function and overall quality of life. These changes exceeded the MIC thresholds reported in the literature, suggesting that the improvements were likely clinically relevant. While causality cannot be established in a single case, the findings are consistent with clinically meaningful benefits and indicate that corrective osteotomy contributed to enhanced functional abilities in this patient.

In this study, anteroposterior and posteroanterior radiographs were obtained at 2 weeks and 1, 3, and 6 months post-operatively. Both anteroposterior and posteroanterior radiographic imaging of the patient demonstrate a well-consolidated fracture of the scapula with healing in an anatomical alignment and no evidence of hardware loosening (Fig. 5, A and B).

Fracture union was defined as consolidation of the osteotomy sites on CT scan, which was performed at 12 months post-operatively prior to removal of the hardware. In addition to axial, coronal, and sagittal images, 3D reconstructions were obtained to allow direct comparison with the pre-operative deformity and to confirm restoration of scapular anatomy. Discussion

This study highlights the beneficial radiographic and functional outcomes of open reduction and fixation in the case of a patient with extra-articular comminuted scapular fracture malunion. As illustrated in Table II, the overall DASH score shows a gradual decline from 62.50 to a final score of 1.67 and a PROMIS T-score of 47.7 (95% confidence interval [40.08, 55.32]) at 12 months post-operatively. In addition, at the time of the latest follow-up, the mean ROM outcomes of the shoulder were found to have shown a marked improvement.

The management of extra-articular scapular malunions has received growing attention, emphasizing the importance of timely surgical intervention to improve functional outcomes. To contextualize our findings, we summarized the main characteristics and outcomes of published reports on surgical management of scapular malunions (Supplementary Table A). Although based on small case series and individual reports, these studies consistently describe high union rates, pain relief, and meaningful functional improvement after corrective osteotomy. Cole et al6 demonstrated significant functional improvements following the reconstruction of extra-articular malunions.5 Mishra et al26 highlighted that surgical indications for such cases are relatively rare, underscoring the challenge of patient selection for operative treatment.27 The complexity of scapular malunions, particularly when combined with chest deformities, was reported by Malovrh et al21 Similarly, Miller and Ivey et al25 emphasized the potential complications resulting from inadequate healing of comminuted scapular fractures, which can necessitate surgical correction.25 Sorenson and Armstrong34 presented a case where surgical intervention led to significant symptom resolution in a patient experiencing persistent upper-extremity pain years after the initial injury.

Earlier studies by Wilkes and Halawa and Martin and Weiland were among the first to document anatomical and functional improvements following surgical treatment of scapular malunions, with complete symptom resolution observed within 6-15 months.18,22 Collectively, these findings support the importance of prompt diagnosis and intervention to optimize functional outcomes in symptomatic extra-articular scapular malunions.

In this study, the surgical technique employed to correct scapular deformities followed the method described by Cole et al.5 In their study, all 5 patients with extra-articular scapular malunions achieved successful radiographic union, healed without complications, and experienced pain-free outcomes with high satisfaction regarding shoulder function. Comparable favorable results were reported by Mishra et al,26 Malovrh et al,21 and Miller and Ivey et al25 using similar surgical techniques.21,25,26 While nonoperative treatment is generally recommended for minimally displaced extra-articular scapular fractures, the decision for surgery in extra-articular fractures remains debated due to the lack of randomized controlled trials.

Cole et al6 defined clear surgical indications for both intra-articular and extra-articular fractures. For intra-articular fractures, surgery is recommended in cases with more than 4 mm of articular step-off or involvement of over 20% of the glenoid surface. For extra-articular fractures, relative indications include angular deformity greater than 45°, lateral border offset exceeding 15-20 mm (especially when combined with angular deformity >35°), a glenopolar angle less than 22°, or displaced double lesions of the superior shoulder suspensory complex. These parameters provide a framework for identifying patients at risk of persistent functional impairment if managed nonoperatively.

Furthermore, GH joint instability resulting from glenoid fractures is another significant indication for surgery.5 In contrast to the relatively scarce literature on extra-articular fractures, intra-articular scapular fractures have been more extensively studied due to their complexity and the higher risk of complications, particularly with respect to joint instability, functional impairment, and the development of post-traumatic arthritis.5,12

The management of extra-articular fractures, which often yields positive outcomes with conservative treatment like immobilization and physiotherapy, has been less emphasized.19 This imbalance in the literature likely reflects the less complicated nature of extra-articular fractures.9,12,22,19,33,43

In our case, the patient fulfilled multiple surgical indications as described by Cole et al. The scapula demonstrated a lateral border offset of approximately 40 mm, clearly exceeding the 20 mm threshold. In addition, medial varus angulation measured 30° and lateral varus angulation 45°, thereby meeting the criterion of angular deformity >45°. The glenopolar angle was reduced to below 22°, further supporting surgical indication. These radiological parameters, obtained on CT and confirmed using 3D reconstructions, justified surgical correction in this patient. An important consideration is whether this fracture might have been more appropriately managed surgically at the index event. Given the degree of comminution, displacement, and angular deformity seen on initial imaging, surgical fixation could arguably have prevented the development of symptomatic malunion. However, in the acute polytrauma setting, treatment priority was given to life-threatening and limb-threatening injuries, and the scapular fracture was initially managed conservatively. Whether earlier operative fixation of the scapula would have led to a faster or superior functional recovery compared to delayed corrective osteotomy remains uncertain. Our case demonstrates that delayed reconstruction can still achieve favorable outcomes, but it does not allow conclusions about the relative benefit of acute vs. late surgical management. Additional indications include complete acromioclavicular displacement or scapular fractures displaced by more than 10 mm.5,39

A key strength of this study is the integration of 3D printing technology into pre-operative planning. In both extra-articular and intra-articular fractures, 3D models provide a comprehensive understanding of fracture geometry, supporting anatomical realignment and reducing the risk of malunion and joint instability. Beyond serving as visual aids, 3D printing also enables the production of patient-specific instrumentation guides, which have been described in recent literature to assist with osteotomy cuts and implant placement. Although patient-specific instrumentation-guides were not applied in this case, their use represents a valuable extension of 3D-assisted surgery that may further increase precision and reduce operative time in similar complex deformities.

This is particularly important because malunited fractures can lead to pain, functional limitations, and long-term complications if not corrected accurately. Similar observations have been reported for scapular malunions, where patients frequently present with chronic pain, scapulothoracic dyskinesis, and impaired shoulder function if left untreated.6,21,26

The precise assessment and correction of deformities, especially angular displacements and lateral border shifts, have been shown to lead to improved functional outcomes and patient satisfaction.1,23

By facilitating individualized pre-operative planning, 3D printing allows for greater accuracy during corrective osteotomies, offering tailored treatment approaches for fractures with complex anatomy.2 The technology is especially useful in cases where traditional 2D imaging may fall short, as it enables surgeons to fully appreciate the spatial configuration of the deformity and plan precise surgical interventions.2,10

Clinical relevance is associated with a clinically meaningful improvement in function, as defined by the MIC.9 MIC values indicate the smallest change in a patient-reported outcome that is perceived as beneficial by the patient, thus guiding the evaluation of treatment effectiveness.19,35 For instance, MICs for PROMIS-UE scores range from 2.1 to 8.0, and visual analog scale pain scores have an MIC between 1.6 and 1.9.17,31

In this case, the observed improvements exceeded reported MIC thresholds, which indicates that they were likely meaningful from the patient's perspective. While it is not possible to demonstrate statistical significance in a single case, these findings suggest that 3D-assisted planning may have supported accurate correction and functional recovery. However, without a comparison to surgery performed without 3D models, the specific contribution of the printed model cannot be determined.

With evolving surgical techniques and a growing emphasis on optimizing patient-reported outcomes, the integration of 3D printing into pre-operative planning is becoming increasingly important for both intra- and extra-articular fracture management.

This individualized approach contributes to better restoration of function and higher patient satisfaction, as demonstrated in several studies on corrective osteotomies, particularly of the distal radius. While these results may not be directly transferable to scapular pathology, recent case reports and small series have suggested similar benefits for 3D-assisted scapular surgery. As research continues, establishing MICs for both clinician- and patient-reported outcomes will further clarify the potential advantages of 3D-printed guides in complex scapular reconstructions.6,21,26 A review of the literature and summary of comparable case reports and outcomes from the existing literature is provided in Supplementary Table B.

Conclusion

This report highlights that surgical intervention can be a valuable option for patients with symptomatic extra-articular scapular malunion, in line with recent literature supporting operative management in selected cases. At 12-month follow-up, our patient showed consolidation of the scapula, restoration of normal anatomy, and substantial improvements in shoulder motion and patient-reported outcomes. While most scapular fractures heal satisfactorily with conservative treatment, malunions can occasionally result in disabling symptoms. In such cases, corrective osteotomy with 3D-assisted planning may support anatomical realignment and functional recovery. The optimal timing of surgery remains uncertain, and further studies are needed to determine whether earlier intervention yields superior outcomes compared to delayed correction.
