Computational investigation of mechanical mechanisms underlying diverse cerebellar lobular morphologies
Authors: Yoshitaka Kameo, Ikkei Hanatani, Haruki Nakamura, Nozomi Shimbata, Taiji Adachi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The mammalian cerebellum is a densely folded structure composed of lobules separated by deep fissures, while individual lobules display striking diversity in shape and size. Although multicellular processes such as granule cell proliferation and migration drive cerebellar morphogenesis, mechanical mechanisms that generate diverse lobular morphologies after initial folding remain poorly understood. Spatially heterogeneous cortical growth arising from multicellular dynamics is considered critical for the formation of characteristic lobular morphologies. In this study, we employed mathematical modeling and computer simulations to investigate how heterogeneous cortical growth influences cerebellar lobular morphology. We developed a mathematical model of cerebellar cortical growth based on continuum mechanics and simulated lobular deformation under spatially heterogeneous cortical growth using the finite element method. Our simulations indicated that heterogeneous cortical growth modulates the rates of increase in lobular height and width; however, under most conditions, lobules elongate during cortical growth, forming columnar morphologies because of the strong constraints imposed by anchoring centers, i.e., the bases of the initial fissures. In contrast, fan-shaped lobules emerged only when relatively large cortical growth occurred in a flat cortical region at the lobular apex, resulting in expansion along the anterior–posterior axis. These results confirm that the interplay between spatially heterogeneous cortical growth and initial lobular morphology is a key mechanical requirement for generating diverse cerebellar lobular morphologies, highlighting the utility of computational approaches for dissecting complex morphogenetic processes.

Introduction

The mammalian cerebellum is a densely folded structure with lobules separated by deep fissures running along the medial–lateral (ML) axis (Sillitoe and Joyner 2007). The basic parallel folding pattern is conserved within and across species, creating ten major s (Haldipur et al. 2019; Yaguchi et al. 2009). Individual s exhibit diverse shapes and sizes, and their branching structures are further divided into subs. For example, in sagittal section of the mouse medial cerebellum (vermis), columnar (e.g., s IV/V) and fan-shaped (e.g., s VI/VII/VIII) s expand along the anterior–posterior (AP) axis (Fig. 1) (Sudarov and Joyner 2007; Yaguchi et al. 2009). Malformation of these characteristic cerebellar morphologies can cause motor and cognitive deficits (Leto et al. 2016; Sillitoe and Joyner 2007); therefore, clarifying the mechanisms underlying physiological cerebellar morphogenesis is important for understanding the relationship between cerebellar structure and function.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the midsagittal section of a mouse cerebellum during morphogenesis from E17.5 to P3. Cerebellar s are formed through the multicellular dynamics of granule cells (GCs), including proliferation and migration (Fig. 1) (Consalez et al. 2021). The initial folding of the embryonic cerebellum, marked by the emergence of fissures, is driven by the proliferation of granule cell progenitors (GCPs) within the external granule layer (EGL). This process is characterized as a buckling event triggered by differential growth between the EGL and the underlying core (Lawton et al. 2019). Subsequently, the GCs in the EGL migrate inward along Bergmann glial (BG) fibers through the molecular layer (MOL) and Purkinje cell layer (PCL), forming the internal granule layer (IGL) (Rahimi-Balaei et al. 2018; Yacubova and Komuro 2003). Owing to this cellular behavior, each bounded by two fissures elongates outward while maintaining the relative positions of the bases of the initial fissures, referred to as anchoring centers (Sudarov and Joyner 2007). Although the multicellular dynamics contributing to cerebellar lobular formation have been identified, the mechanism underlying the generation of diverse cerebellar lobular morphologies remains unclear.

The multicellular dynamics involved in cerebellar lobular formation are expected to induce spatial heterogeneity in cortical growth. GCPs in the EGL, which proliferate through oriented cell division along the AP axis (Cook et al. 2024; Legué et al. 2015), have been shown to exhibit fluid-like behavior driven by cell rearrangement (Lawton et al. 2019). This rearrangement is influenced by mechanical forces from neighboring cells and can therefore result in a spatially heterogeneous cell distribution based on the local mechanical environment. Subsequent GC migration may further contribute to the heterogeneous cortical growth because the orientation of BG fibers that guide GC migration, is rearranged in response to deformation of the cerebellar cortex, leading to heterogeneous GC accumulation in the IGL (Sudarov and Joyner 2007). In addition, because GCP proliferation is stimulated by sonic hedgehog (SHH) secreted by PCs (Corrales et al. 2006, 2004), the density and distribution of PCs within each may represent additional factors driving heterogeneous cortical growth. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that spatially heterogeneous cortical growth is an essential factor in generating diverse morphologies among individual cerebellar s.

Cerebellar lobular formation is a complex process regulated by coupled mechanical and biochemical factors making it difficult to experimentally quantify spatial heterogeneity in cortical growth unless individual cellular behaviors can be tracked during development. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to test our hypothesis using mathematical modeling and computer simulations. Previous studies have shown that such an in silico approach is a powerful tool for understanding the mechanisms of cerebellar morphogenesis from a mechanical perspective (Lawton et al. 2019; Lejeune et al. 2019, 2016). We developed a mathematical model of cerebellar cortical growth based on continuum mechanics and computationally investigated the mechanical role of heterogeneous cortical growth in the formation of characteristic lobular morphologies.

Finite element models of cerebellar lobules

Each cerebellar lobule resulting from the initial formation of fissures exhibits a distinct shape and size. Consequently, we developed finite element models of two types of cerebellar lobules by idealizing the morphology of the mouse cerebellum, as shown in Fig. 3. Given that in the cerebellar vermis, lobules are aligned along the ML axis (Sillitoe and Joyner 2007), two-dimensional sagittal section models were constructed under the plane strain assumption. Model A represents a lobule with a semicircular cortex (Fig. 3a), whereas Model B represents a wider lobule characterized by a semicircular cortex and a flat apical region (Fig. 3b). For simplicity, the underlying white matter, which is composed of axons, was not included in either model. As boundary conditions, the bottom surface Y = 0 was fully fixed to represent anchoring centers. Considering the symmetry of the problem, displacement in the X-direction on the surface X = 0 was constrained. Because the present formulation is based on a quasi-static growth analysis, the predicted deformation depends on the accumulated growth stretch rather than on the absolute growth rate. Thus, τ serves primarily as a scaling parameter for growth progression. In the reference case (f* = 1), τ corresponds to the time required for the growth stretch to increase by approximately a factor of e, which is estimated to be on the order of 10 days in the developing mouse cerebellum. Both models were discretized using first-order hexahedral finite elements. Model A and Model B consisted of 1,326 and 1,547 elements, respectively. Numerical integration was performed using Gauss quadrature, and the resulting nonlinear system of equations was solved using the Newton–Raphson method in conjunction with the PARDISO sparse direct solver. Mesh and time-step sensitivity analyses were performed to verify numerical convergence. For mesh convergence, Model A was discretized into 480, 1,326, and 1,880 elements, whereas Model B was discretized into 560, 1,547, and 2,180 elements. For time-step convergence, simulations were performed using a range of initial time increments from Δt0/τ = 1 × 10−3 to 5 × 10−5. Because the growth stretch θ increases approximately exponentially with time according to Eq. (7), a variable time-stepping scheme was adopted such that Δt/τ = Δt0/(τ + n f*Δt0), where n denotes the time-step number. Negligible differences were observed between the medium and fine meshes and among the tested time increments, confirming that the reported results were sufficiently converged with respect to both spatial and temporal discretization. Based on these results, the simulations were performed using the medium mesh and initial time increments of Δt0/τ ≤ 1 × 10−3. All finite element analyses were performed using an in-house finite element code implemented in Fortran 90.

Results

Effect of stress-dependent cortical growth

The cerebellar cortex is not a purely elastic solid; it also exhibits fluid-like properties because of the stress-dependent rearrangement of GCPs (Lawton et al. 2019). Based on these cellular dynamics, we investigated the effect of stress-dependent cortical growth on lobular morphology and the internal mechanical states by simulating Model A (Fig. 3a) under three conditions. The effect was evaluated by varying the ratio of the stress-dependent growth rate (k*) to the biomechanical growth rate (f*), setting k*/f* = 0.1, 1, and 10 while keeping f* = 1 (constant).

Figure 4 shows the changes in the cerebellar lobular morphology and the corresponding residual stress trσ from the initial state until time t = 1.8τ. Regardless of the k*/f* ratio, lobular height increased over time under all conditions (Figs. 4a–c), successfully reproducing the columnar lobule morphology observed in the actual cerebellum. When the effect of stress-dependent growth was relatively small (k*/f* = 0.1), the cortex exhibited compressive stress in the outer layer and tensile stress in the inner layer (Figs. 4a, d). As k*/f* increased, the residual stress in the cortex decreased (Figs. 4b–d), indicating significant stress relaxation caused by cell rearrangement. Tissue-cutting experiments in the mouse cerebellum during initial folding revealed circumferential tension in the cortex (Lawton et al. 2019). This differs from the stress pattern obtained in k*/f* = 0.1 simulation, suggesting the effect of stress relaxation caused by cell rearrangement in the actual cerebellar cortex. Effect of spatially heterogeneous cortical growth

Spatially heterogeneous cortical growth is caused not only by stress-dependent cell rearrangement but also by spatial variations of cell proliferation and subsequent cell migration, both of which are regulated by signaling molecules. We investigated the effects of spatially heterogeneous cortical growth driven by biochemically regulated cellular dynamics on lobular morphology using two lobular models with different initial morphologies.

Model A: A lobule with a semicircular cortex

We simulated cerebellar lobular deformation caused by tangentially heterogeneous cortical growth (Eq. (12)) and radially heterogeneous cortical growth (Eq. (13)) using Model A (Fig. 3a). In this simulation, the intensity of spatial heterogeneity, ζ, was varied from − 0.4 to 0.4, whereas k* was kept constant at 1.0. In the case of tangentially heterogeneous cortical growth (Figs. 5a, b), regardless of the value of ζ, the lobular aspect ratio, h/w (height/width), increased with cortical growth; however, the rate of increase gradually decreased over time. A relatively high growth rate around the base of the lobule (ζ = 0.4) promoted lobular elongation, whereas a relatively high growth rate around the apex (ζ = − 0.4) increased lobular width. Similar behaviors were observed for radially heterogeneous cortical growth (Figs. 5c, d). A relatively high growth rate around the inner layer (ζ = 0.4) contributed to an increase in lobular height, whereas that around the outer layer (ζ = − 0.4) contributed to an increase in lobular width. Model B: A lobule with a semicircular and a flat cortex

Given the slight difference in the initial lobular morphology, we investigated the effects of tangential and radial heterogeneity of cortical growth on the resulting lobular morphologies using Model B (Fig. 3b). The same simulation conditions as those used for Model A were employed. For tangentially heterogeneous cortical growth (Fig. 6a), a relatively high growth rate around the base of the lobule (ζ = 0.4) promoted lobular elongation, similar to that observed in Model A. In contrast, a relatively high growth rate around the upper flat region (ζ = − 0.4) markedly increased lobular width, resulting in a fan-shaped lobule expanded along the AP axis. Subsequently, the original lobule was divided into two sublobules through the gradual concave penetration of the flat region. For the radially heterogeneous cortical growth (Fig. 6b), there was a slight difference in the lobular morphologies between the conditions of relatively high growth rate around the inner layer (ζ = 0.4) and that around the outer layer (ζ = − 0.4). Discussion

We simulated cerebellar lobular deformation driven by heterogeneous cortical growth along the tangential and radial directions of the cerebellar cortex using two models with different initial morphologies. Consequently, we identified cortical growth conditions that increase lobular height, leading to columnar lobules, as well as those that increase lobular width, resulting in fan-shaped lobules. These results indicate that the combination of spatial heterogeneity in cerebellar cortical growth and initial lobular morphology is a primary determinant of the generation of diverse cerebellar lobular morphologies.

Mathematical modeling and computer simulations have significantly contributed to clarifying the mechanisms underlying the formation of characteristic cerebellar morphology. A tri-layer cerebellar model consisting of EGL and IGL with comparable stiffness and a softer intermediate PCL successfully reproduced surface folding, providing insights into the initiation and positioning of anchoring centers (Lejeune et al. 2016). In addition, a bi-layer tissue model coupled with an agent-based cell model demonstrated that anisotropic tissue growth driven by oriented cell division generates the parallel folding patterns typically observed in the cerebellum (Lejeune et al. 2019). Despite their utility, purely elastic multilayer models fail to capture certain essential features of actual cerebellar folding. To address this limitation, cerebellar models incorporating the fluid-like behavior of GCPs have been proposed (Engstrom et al. 2018; Gandikota and Schwarz 2021; Lawton et al. 2019). However, these studies focused on the initial cerebellar folding arising from mechanical instabilities induced by differential growth among cerebellar layers and did not address subsequent lobular deformation. Our previous work revealed that heterogeneous cortical growth resulting from fiber-guided GC migration plays a crucial role in lobular elongation (Takeda et al. 2021a, 2021b). Building on these findings, the present study demonstrated that heterogeneous cortical growth can account for the morphological diversity observed among cerebellar lobules.

The shapes and sizes of early cerebellar lobules established during the initial folding of the cerebellar cortex are critical determinants of subsequent lobular morphology because anchoring centers maintain relatively fixed positions throughout morphogenesis (Sudarov and Joyner 2007). In addition, clarifying the mechanisms underlying the characteristic morphology of individual lobules requires investigation of the multicellular dynamics within each lobule and the resulting spatial heterogeneity of cortical growth because granule cells cannot migrate across anchoring centers into adjacent lobules (Joyner and Bayin 2022; Legué et al. 2015). Our simulations demonstrated that heterogeneous cortical growth in the tangential and radial directions can modulate the rates of increase in lobular height and width. However, under most growth conditions, the lobular aspect ratio increased with cortical growth because of the deformation constraints imposed by anchoring centers, resulting in the formation of columnar lobules (e.g., lobules IV/V). In contrast, fan-shaped lobules emerged only when a lobule possessed a relatively flat cortical surface at its apex and exhibited relatively greater growth in this region (e.g., lobules VI/VII/VIII). These findings confirm that lobules intrinsically tend to elongate during cortical growth and that heterogeneous cortical growth along the tangential direction is critical for the formation of lobules expanded along the AP axis.

To assess whether the conclusions drawn from the present simulations depend on the choice of material properties, we further examined the sensitivity of the predicted lobular morphologies to tissue stiffness. Additional simulations performed over a four-fold range of Young’s modulus (0.27–1.08 kPa), centered on the value adopted in this study, revealed only minor differences in the resulting lobular morphologies when tissue stiffness was spatially uniform (data not shown). This finding suggests that the characteristic lobular morphologies predicted by the present model are governed primarily by the pattern of cortical growth rather than the absolute magnitude of tissue stiffness. Nevertheless, spatial heterogeneity in tissue stiffness, which may arise during cerebellar development, could generate heterogeneous mechanical states within the cortex and thereby influence lobular morphogenesis. Further studies incorporating spatially heterogeneous material properties are therefore warranted.

One limitation of the present study is that we neglected the effect of the underlying white matter on cortical deformation. Axons constituting the white matter increase their natural length when subjected to chronic overstretching (Bray 1984; Dennerll et al. 1989; Holland et al. 2015). Based on this observation, we assumed that the volume of the white matter increases passively in response to cortical growth and therefore did not explicitly incorporate it into our model. Although differential growth between the cortex and white matter has been suggested to play an important role in cerebellar cortical folding, the extent to which the white matter acts as a mechanical constraint on the cortex during subsequent lobular morphogenesis remains unclear. As shown in Fig. 4, we demonstrated that heterogeneous cortical growth induced by stress-dependent cell rearrangements has a negligible impact on lobular morphology; however, this result may be influenced by the omission of white-matter effects. If the white matter constrains increases in cortical height or width, spatial heterogeneity in the mechanical state along the tangential direction may arise within the cortex and thereby exert a pronounced influence on lobular morphology. Another limitation of the present study is that we did not consider interactions with neighboring lobules, despite cerebellar lobules being densely packed in vivo. The presence of adjacent lobules is expected to restrict the space available for lobular growth and alter the mechanical state within a lobule through contact forces, thereby substantially affecting the resulting lobular morphology. This consideration further suggests that differences in growth rates between neighboring lobules, arising from differences in the rate or timing of granule cell proliferation (Joyner and Bayin 2022; Legué et al. 2016), may modify contact interactions among lobules and contribute to the emergence of characteristic cerebellar lobular morphologies.

These limitations also restrict the possibility of quantitatively validating the present model against actual cerebellar morphologies. Quantitative morphometric measures, such as lobular aspect ratios and curvature profiles, are expected to be strongly influenced by both the mechanical effects of the white matter and contact interactions among neighboring lobules. In particular, interactions with adjacent lobules may significantly affect lobular aspect ratios by constraining deformation through contact forces. Therefore, quantitative comparison with histological observations would require a more comprehensive model that explicitly incorporates these factors. Nevertheless, we believe that the principal conclusion of this study remains valid despite the lack of quantitative morphological validation. Although the underlying white matter and neighboring lobules may influence the quantitative morphological features of cerebellar lobules, we do not expect these factors to alter the qualitative relationship identified in this study between tangentially heterogeneous cortical growth and the formation of fan-shaped lobules. Therefore, our finding that tangentially heterogeneous cortical growth is a key mechanical requirement for fan-shaped lobule formation is expected to remain robust even when these additional factors are considered.

Based on mathematical modeling and computer simulations, this study provides a powerful framework for clarifying the mechanically necessary conditions through which cerebellar lobules acquire characteristic morphologies throughout morphogenesis. Overcoming the limitations discussed above and reproducing the morphogenetic process of the entire cerebellum in silico are expected to deepen our understanding of the mechanical mechanisms that enable the cerebellum to robustly form its complex architecture. Such a constructive, simulation-based approach is expected not only to contribute to cerebellar developmental biology but also to clarify the fundamental principles underlying complex morphogenesis in living tissues.
