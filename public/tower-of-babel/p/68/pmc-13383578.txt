Vertebral artery dissection can lead to pseudoaneurysm formation and subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), accounting for approximately 3% of all SAH cases, of which 12%–30% are bilateral.1,2 Ruptured bilateral vertebral artery dissecting aneurysms (VADAs) are rare conditions in which identification of the rupture site remains challenging, making treatment decisions difficult.1–4 Furthermore, bilateral VADAs involving the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) represent a particularly complex subset, as preservation of perfusion to the PICA territory is required, further complicating their management. In the present case, we treated ruptured bilateral VADAs involving both PICA origins using a bilateral condylar fossa approach. This strategy was intended to 1) directly visualize both vertebral arteries to identify the rupture site, 2) achieve single-stage revascularization of both PICA territories, and 3) reconstruct the vascular anatomy to allow future endovascular treatment while preserving the parent artery in cases of enlargement of the contralateral lesion. We report this case along with a review of the relevant literature.

Illustrative Case

A previously healthy man in his 50s presented with an altered mental status following a 3-day history of posterior neck pain and was transported to our hospital. On admission, his Glasgow Coma Scale score was 14 (E3V5M6) with no apparent motor or sensory deficits. Noncontrast head CT revealed an SAH (Fisher group III), and the patient was classified as Hunt and Kosnik grade II and World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies grade II. CT angiography revealed pearl-and-string signs in both vertebral arteries, leading to the diagnosis of SAH due to bilateral vertebral artery dissection. Preoperative digital subtraction angiography (DSA) confirmed that both PICAs originated from the dissected segments. Based on the aneurysm morphology and hematoma distribution, the right side was presumed to be the rupture side and surgery was performed (Fig. 1).

Surgical Findings

The patient was placed in the prone position with the head fixed in the neutral midline position without rotation. The upper body was slightly elevated, and the vertex was tilted downward by neck flexion to obtain a caudocranial operative trajectory. A midline skin incision was made from the inion to the C2 spinous process and extended laterally toward the mastoid process to harvest the occipital artery (OA), creating an L-shaped incision. Suboccipital craniotomy with bilateral condylar fossae removal was performed. After opening the dura, proximal control of both vertebral arteries was obtained at the dural penetration points, followed by direct inspection of both vertebral arteries (Fig. 2). Contrary to preoperative expectations, the right vertebral artery showed no discoloration of the vessel wall, and the surrounding hematoma was easily removed. In contrast, the left vertebral artery appeared dark red, and a thrombosed dissecting aneurysm, which had not been clearly identified on DSA, was observed. Therefore, the left vertebral artery was identified as the rupture site (Fig. 3). A PICA-PICA bypass was first performed, followed by OA–left PICA bypass to revascularize the bilateral PICA territories. The left vertebral artery was then surgically occluded at the dural penetration point and just distal to the origin of the left PICA, resulting in exclusion of the aneurysm in a blind-ended configuration. Occlusion of the right PICA was deferred pending confirmation of OA-PICA bypass patency and was therefore not performed intraoperatively. The procedure was completed without any intraoperative complications.

Postoperatively, the patient exhibited no apparent neurological deficits. However, gradual enlargement of the unruptured right vertebral artery aneurysm was observed. After initiation of antiplatelet therapy, stent-assisted coil embolization was performed on postoperative day 21 (Fig. 4). The origin of the right PICA was preserved at that time, but further enlargement was noted at 5 months; therefore, additional coil embolization including the PICA origin was performed. No further recurrence has been observed.

Discussion

Observations

In bilateral vertebral artery dissection, accurate identification of the rupture site is crucial for secure trapping of the ruptured lesion and preservation of the contralateral vertebral artery. Imaging findings such as pearl-and-string signs and aneurysm dilatation may be present on both the ruptured and unruptured sides, making definitive identification based solely on imaging difficult.5 Additional clues such as hematoma distribution, headache localization, and contrast pooling within the false lumen may be helpful but are not definitive. Recent advances such as vessel wall imaging and computational fluid dynamics have been reported as adjunct tools.6,7 Particularly, vessel wall imaging has been shown to be useful in identifying ruptured aneurysms in patients with multiple intracranial aneurysms. However, in bilateral vertebral artery dissection, inflammatory changes may be present on both sides, resulting in bilateral wall enhancement.8 Furthermore, the requirement for high-resolution MRI and the difficulty in imaging unstable patients limit its clinical utility. Therefore, direct intraoperative observation remains one of the most reliable methods for identifying rupture sites. In the present case, although the right side was initially suspected, the left side was confirmed as the rupture site under direct visualization, highlighting the importance of intraoperative assessment.

The lateral suboccipital approach performed in the lateral position is commonly used for vertebral artery aneurysms. However, in the present case, we selected a bilateral condylar fossa approach that allowed access to both lesions. This approach, described as the mid-lateral suboccipital approach by Sugiyama et al., offers relatively simple positioning and craniotomy and enables simultaneous observation and management of both vertebral arteries within a single surgical field.9 In addition, it provides a wide and shallow operative field dorsal to the medulla, facilitating manipulation of the caudal loop of the PICA and enabling both OA-PICA and PICA-PICA bypass procedures. Furthermore, because surgical manipulation is performed caudally to the lower cranial nerves, the incidence of postoperative lower cranial nerve deficits is reportedly lower than that in the lateral approach.10

Another important consideration in bilateral vertebral artery dissection is the increased hemodynamic stress on the contralateral side after treatment of the ruptured lesion, with the risk of delayed rupture occurring days to several months later.11,12 Therefore, therapeutic management for the unruptured side should be considered in the initial stage. Because bilateral vertebral artery occlusion may lead to poor outcomes, preservation of the parent artery using stent-assisted coil embolization is generally preferred for the unruptured side. In the present case, because both PICAs were involved, we performed single-stage revascularization of both PICA territories using OA-PICA and PICA-PICA bypasses. This strategy allowed subsequent endovascular treatment of the contralateral lesion while preserving the parent artery. Taken together, in ruptured bilateral vertebral artery dissection involving both PICAs, a strategy that combines direct identification of the rupture site, revascularization of critical territories, and anticipation of future endovascular treatments may provide a rational and effective treatment paradigm.

Lessons

In ruptured bilateral VADAs involving the PICAs, direct intraoperative identification of the rupture site combined with revascularization of the bilateral PICA territories using the bilateral condylar fossa approach enables effective treatment while preserving critical circulation and facilitating subsequent endovascular management.
