Videoconferencing for Physical Function Assessment in Patients with CKD
Authors: Jack H. Markillie, Sanya Tinaikar, Sarah Cohen, Sarah J. Schrauben
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Introduction

CKD is highly prevalent, affecting more than one in seven adults in the United States.1 Among these patients, a decline in functional status is common and seen across all stages of the disease.2–5 This impaired functional status is strongly associated with adverse outcomes, including frailty, sarcopenia, and higher mortality.6–8 Furthermore, functional status is an important factor for transplant eligibility in people with CKD.

Several validated objective measures are available to assess physical function, an important component of functional status, including handgrip strength, timed up and go, and gait speed.9,10 Despite clinical practice guidelines emphasizing the importance of monitoring functional status, these tests are currently not routinely used in renal clinics.5,11–13 Barriers expressed by CKD care stakeholders include limited time and space in the clinic.14 Incorporating telemedicine into renal clinics offers a promising strategy to address these barriers by expanding access to physical function assessment without increasing in-clinic burden.

The feasibility and acceptability of telemedicine in CKD populations are increasingly evident. During the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic, 98% of patients receiving care at an academic nephrology clinic reported access to videoconferencing for telemedicine visits.15 Moreover, individuals with advanced CKD (stages 4–5) and their care providers have reported that telemedicine visits are more convenient, less costly, and more time-efficient than traditional in-person encounters.16 Beyond nephrology, a recent systematic review on videoconferencing as a tool to assess functional capacity across chronic conditions, including SLE, Parkinson's disease, heart failure, cerebral palsy, and type 2 diabetes, demonstrated high patient satisfaction and usability.17 In addition, simple physical function tests such as timed up and go and gait speed require minimal time and equipment and have shown strong reliability when administered remotely.17–20

Despite these encouraging findings, important gaps remain in the virtual assessment of physical function. Few studies have directly compared remote and in-person assessments to establish criterion validity and even fewer have conducted concurrent remote and in-person evaluations.17 Furthermore, to our knowledge, videoconference-based assessment of physical function has not been formally evaluated in individuals with CKD.

Accordingly, this study aimed to examine the validity, reliability, and usability of Zoom videoconferencing for assessing physical function in adults with CKD. We sought to determine whether remote assessments conducted in a controlled research setting yield results comparable with in-person gold standard measurements while maintaining high interrater, test–retest reliability, and intermethod reliability. By evaluating this telemedicine approach, our study aims to establish a proof-of-concept that addresses a critical gap in access to routine functional monitoring in nephrology care. In doing so, we seek to inform future studies developing scalable, patient-centered strategies for more functional status assessment in more accessible settings.

Methods

Study Population

This was a prospective, single-center validation study. Adults living with CKD who were evaluated at nephrology clinics at the University of Pennsylvania were recruited. Eligible participants had a CKD diagnosis of any stage, BP ≤180/100 mm Hg, and the ability to speak and comprehend English. Participants were excluded if they were unable to walk, lived in a long-term care institution, had cognitive impairment, experienced a cardiovascular event in the last 3 months, or had a fall in the last 6 months. Potentially eligible patients were contacted and recruited via electronic health record portal invitations and phone calls. At the baseline visit, participants had to meet further eligibility criteria: ability to walk without the assistance of another person, BP <180/110 mm Hg, and absence of the following current symptoms: chest pain, shortness of breath, lightheadedness, and dizziness (Supplemental Table 1). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Pennsylvania, all researchers abided by the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent. The study was conducted between September 2023 and April 2025.

Physical Function Assessments

Physical function was evaluated using two performance-based measures: the timed up and go test and 4-m usual gait speed and a strength measure (handgrip strength) at two study visits conducted approximately 4–6 weeks apart. Instructions for the tests were provided by videos and the remote assessor guided the participant through the testing procedures. Handgrip strength, timed up and go, and gait speed were assessed concurrently by two trained in-person research staff in a clinical research environment with adequate testing space, while a third trained researcher conducted the assessments remotely using a commercially available videoconferencing platform (Zoom Video Communications, San Jose, CA) from a separate location. The camera was positioned in a standard position to visualize each test. Handgrip strength (kilograms) was first assessed in triplicate for both hands using a manual Jamar hydraulic hand dynamometer (Sammons Preston, Bolingbrook, IL), the reference standard for clinical and research measurement of grip strength. This was followed by duplicate assessments of timed up and go (seconds) and 4-m usual gait speed (seconds). Handgrip strength was then reassessed in triplicate using a low-cost, widely available digital hand dynamometer (EH-108 model, Hichor, China). All testing procedures followed standardized protocols.18,19,21–24 Please see Supplemental Methods for further details on testing procedures. Two additional trained researchers who were not involved in the original assessments independently reviewed recorded remote sessions and rescored all physical function measures to evaluate interrater reliability.

Composite scores for each physical function outcome were created using a two-step aggregation process (Supplemental Figure 1). First, the duplicate (timed up and go and gait speed) or triplicate (handgrip strength) measurements made by each individual assessor were averaged to generate a single score for each participant. Second, the mean scores for each physical function measure from step one were then averaged across assessor type: in-person score (averaging the two in-person assessors) and a secondary remote score (averaging the two independent remote assessors for inter-rater reliability). This process was completed at baseline and for the follow-up visit. A primary remote composite score was not necessary as only one remote assessor conducted the videoconference-based assessments.

Patient-reported physical function was collected on a study tablet after the objective physical function tests were completed with the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) 20a and the Kidney Disease Quality of Life Short Form (KDQOL-SF) Version 1.3 at baseline and follow-up visits.25,26 The short form-12 health survey (SF-12) physical component summary (PCS) and PROMIS-20a T-scores were calculated using the KDQOL-SF version 1.3 Scoring Program and the 2024 Health Measures PROMIS manual.27,28 Scores that reached the ceiling of the PROMIS-20a questionnaire (100/100) were assigned a T-score equating to a raw score of 99/100, corresponding to the highest available T score as the instrument cannot differentiate the level of physical function beyond this point.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome was the criterion validity of remotely assessed physical function measures, the extent to which measurements obtained using a new method agree with those obtained from an established reference standard, as determined by the absolute agreement with corresponding in-person assessments. Absolute agreement was quantified with intraclass correlation coefficients and visualized with Bland–Altman Plots. Concordance correlation coefficients and concordance plots supplemented the evaluation of agreement.

Secondary outcomes included interrater reliability, test–retest reliability, consistency agreement of remote assessments, criterion validity and consistency agreement of the digital dynamometer compared with the Jamar reference standard, construct validity of remote measures with patient-reported physical function, and usability of videoconference-based assessments. Interrater reliability of remote assessments was evaluated by comparing scores from the initial remote assessor with those independently obtained by two additional remote assessors who reviewed recorded sessions. Test–retest reliability was assessed by examining agreement between the primary remote assessor scores obtained at the two study visits. Consistency agreement, a form of intermethod reliability, evaluated whether remote and in-person assessments, or digital versus Jamar dynamometers, produced measurements that changed in parallel across individuals after accounting for systematic differences between methods. Criterion validity of the digital dynamometer was evaluated by assessing the absolute agreement with the reference standard Jamar dynamometer. Construct validity was assessed by examining correlations between remotely assessed physical function measures and patient-reported physical function using PROMIS-20a T-scores and the SF-12 PCS. Usability of videoconference-based assessments was evaluated using the System Usability Scale (SUS)29 and responses to the open-ended survey question: “Please provide any feedback you would like to share, and please include anything you think could improve the video-based functional status testing.”

Other Variables

At the baseline visit, participant characteristics were collected via a self-report questionnaire, including age, sex, race, ethnicity, marital status, educational attainment, household income, and digital health literacy as measured by the eHealth literacy scale.30 CKD stage was classified according to eGFR, using the value obtained from the electronic medical record closest in time to the baseline study visit.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study population, with continuous variables reported as means and SDs or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and proportions. Gait speed was converted to meters/second (m/s).

Agreement and reliability were quantified using intraclass correlation coefficients estimated from linear mixed-effect models. Criterion validity, interrater reliability, test–retest reliability, and consistency agreement (intermethod reliability) models included participant as a random intercept to account for clustering of repeated measurements across visits and testing settings. Criterion validity, interrater reliability, and consistency agreement models included visit number as a fixed effect to adjust for any systematic difference in measurements across the two visits. Consistency agreement models additionally included rater setting (remote versus in-person) or device type (digital versus Jamar) as a fixed effect to adjust for any systematic differences between the testing setting or dynamometer device. Intraclass correlation coefficients were calculated from each model as the ratio of between-subject variance (from the participant random intercept) to total variance (between-subject plus any additional unexplained variance stemming from random noise or lack of agreement/reliability not explained by model components).

Bland–Altman plots were generated to visually assess agreement between remote and in-person assessments and between device types, including evaluation of systematic bias and limits of agreement. These plots were derived from linear mixed-effect models. Model outcomes were the (1) difference between remote and in-person objective measures (handgrip strength measured via Jamar, timed up and go, gait speed) and (2) the difference between digital and Jamar dynamometer measurements obtained during in-person evaluations. Each model included participant as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements and visit number to adjust for any bias over the baseline and follow-up visits. For visualization, scatterplots were constructed, plotting the difference between paired measurements against their mean. The average difference was represented by a horizontal line, and the limits of agreement were calculated and displayed as the mean difference ±1.96 times the residual SD from the mixed-effect models.

To assess the robustness of intraclass correlation coefficient estimates for the primary outcome, sensitivity analyses were performed in which intraclass correlation coefficients were recalculated using two-way ANOVA models stratified by visit number. This approach evaluated whether intraclass correlation coefficient estimates were sensitive to model specification or to pooling measurements across visits. No violations of model assumptions were observed for the mixed-effect analyses. For an additional agreement assessment between remote and in-person measurements, concordance correlation coefficients and concordance plots were generated.

Construct validity was evaluated by examining correlations between patient-reported physical function and remotely assessed physical function measures. PROMIS-20a T-scores, SF-12 PCS, and remote objective measures were standardized to z scores. Associations were estimated using linear regression models with cluster-robust SEMs at the participant level to account for repeated baseline and follow-up measurements. Standardized β coefficients, equivalent to Pearson correlation coefficients, quantified the strength of associations, and corresponding P values were derived from regression test statistics. Relationships between patient-reported physical function and remote objective measures were visualized using scatterplots with fitted regression lines and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model diagnostics indicated no major violations of regression assumptions, aside from minor non-normality of residuals. A sensitivity analysis stratified by visit number was performed, which estimated Spearman correlation coefficients between patient-reported physical function and remotely assessed physical function measures, to assess the robustness of the original analysis pooling across visits. Participants with missing items on the PROMIS-20a and KDQOL-SF questionnaires were excluded from construct validity analyses. A total SUS score was calculated for each participant using published scoring guidelines to assess usability of the videoconference-based physical function assessment.31 Participants with missing SUS items were excluded from these calculations. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA v.18.0, with a two-sided significance level of α=0.05.

Qualitative data from the open-ended survey responses were analyzed using an inductive thematic approach to provide more context around usability and acceptance. Two investigators independently reviewed all responses and generated initial codes without a predetermined coding framework, managed using Microsoft Word for Mac version 19.63.32,33 Each investigator developed a separate codebook, and discrepancies were resolved through iterative discussion with a third investigator to achieve a consensus.33 Codes were then grouped into overarching themes.

Results

Nineteen adults with CKD completed the baseline visit, and 18 completed the follow-up visit. The median age was 68 years (interquartile range, 62–76), with a balanced sex distribution. Mean (SD) eGFR was 47±22 ml/min per 1.73 m2, with most participants categorized as having stage 3 CKD. The majority identified as Black or African American, and approximately half demonstrated adequate digital health literacy based on the eHealth Literacy Scale. For criterion validity, left and right handgrip strength and timed up and go demonstrated high levels of absolute agreement (intraclass correlation coefficients of 0.86–0.88 and 0.95, respectively), whereas absolute agreement for gait speed was more moderate (intraclass correlation coefficient=0.69; 95% CI, 0.48 to 0.84; Table 2). Sensitivity analyses using two-way ANOVA models stratified by visit number yielded similar results (Supplemental Table 2). Agreement between remote and in-person assessments was further supported by concordance analysis, where concordance correlation coefficients yielded similar results to the intraclass correlation coefficients, and most data points laid close to the 45° line on the concordance plots (Supplemental Figure 2 and Supplemental Table 3). Bland–Altman plots showed no systematic mean differences for handgrip strength measurements by remote or in-person assessment (Figure 1A and Supplemental Table 4). Timed up and go times demonstrated small mean differences, and gait speed exhibited moderate mean differences between remote and in-person assessments. Left and right handgrip strength measured using the digital dynamometer demonstrated moderate-to-high absolute agreement with the Jamar dynamometer (intraclass correlation coefficients of 0.80 and 0.86, respectively; Table 2). In Bland–Altman plots, there was a modest systematic bias between digital and Jamar hydraulic dynamometer measurements (Figure 1B and Supplemental Table 5).

Usability of videoconference-based assessments was high. Across both visits, 72%–83% of participants agreed they would use video-based physical function testing if available, 94%–100% agreed that the videoconferencing platform was easy to use, and 89%–94% felt confident using videoconferencing for assessment. SUS scores exceeded the threshold for adequate usability (≥68) at both study visits, with mean (SD) scores of 84.5 (11.0) at visit 1 and 82.1 (15.6) at visit 2 (Table 4).

Qualitative inductive thematic analysis provided more information regarding acceptability and usability. Many participants reported that videoconferencing enabled successful completion of physical function assessments, citing clear verbal instructions and the convenience of remote testing. However, some participants noted technical challenges, such as unclear audio, expressed discomfort with the absence of an in-person clinician providing instructions for the testing, or questioned the need for remote assessment (Table 4).

Discussion

In this prospective, proof-of-concept study conducted in a controlled research environment, videoconference-based physical function assessment in adults with CKD performed comparably with in-person testing. Remote measures of gait speed, handgrip strength, and the timed up and go test demonstrated moderate-to-high agreement and minimal systematic biases, highlighting the potential of videoconferencing as an alternative approach to functional assessment. Participants reported excellent usability of videoconferencing for remote physical function assessment, with higher perceived usability than over 90% of other systems evaluated using the SUS scale. Collectively, these findings indicate that videoconferencing platforms such as Zoom represent a feasible and scalable option for assessing physical function in adults with CKD in both research and clinical settings.

This work builds on previous studies evaluating remote assessments of physical function across a range of chronic disease populations. In individuals with cerebral palsy, chronic heart failure, and stroke, remotely assessed handgrip strength and timed up and go in controlled research settings have demonstrated high interrater and test–retest reliability, as well as strong agreement with in-person assessments conducted on the same day but during separate, nonsimultaneous tests.34–36 In contrast, remote assessment of gait speed has been less extensively validated. An at-home study in cancer survivors reported only moderate criterion validity for remote gait speed compared with in-person assessment (intraclass correlation coefficient=0.65; 95% CI, 0.49 to 0.76), along with low test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient=0.47; 95% CI, 0.27 to 0.63).37 Within the CKD population, remote assessment of objective physical function has not previously been tested. Among kidney transplant candidates, Watford et al. demonstrated that remotely collected patient-reported physical function scores (short form-36 health survey) were correlated with in-person objective performance measures, including the 6-minute walk test and sit-to-stand performance.38 Our study extends this work by directly comparing simultaneously assessed in-person and remote objective physical function measures. We also address an important gap in the literature, as remote physical function assessments have not been well validated against in-person objective measures in CKD or, more broadly, in populations with chronic disease.

Despite overall validity of the videoconferencing-based assessments, we observed systematic differences in certain measures. In our cohort, remote gait speed was, on average, 0.11 m/s slower than in-person measurement, a difference that may be clinically meaningful given prior associations between a 0.1 m/s decrement in gait speed and increased mortality risk.39 Notably, in this study, remote gait speed showed the strongest correlation with patient-reported physical function among the objective performance-based measures. These findings underscore not only the importance of using gait speed in CKD care but also the sensitivity of remote assessments to variations in video capture. Accurate measurement of gait speed depends on precise identification of when a participant crosses the finish line, which may be inconsistently visualized due to subtle changes in camera positioning, framing, or autofocus during videoconferencing. This likely contributed to the greater residual variability observed for remote gait speed compared with in-person assessment, indicating reduced precision and increased measurement error. In contrast, timed up and go performance was anchored to discrete, sustained visual events (standing and sitting) that are easier to identify and remain visible even with minor variations in camera positioning, likely contributing to its higher agreement. Together, these findings highlight the need for optimized remote measurement protocols for gait speed, such as slow-motion video review, to improve accuracy and reliability. Such refinements are likely necessary before remote and in-person gait speed assessments can be considered interchangeable.

In addition, there was moderate-to-high absolute agreement observed between the digital dynamometer, a lower-cost, more accessible option for clinical and research use, and the more costly and bulky, Jamar dynamometer, suggesting that these devices may not be completely interchangeable. However, higher consistency versus absolute agreement indicates that the digital dynamometer may be suitable for reliably tracking within-person changes in handgrip strength over time. From a clinical perspective, this device could potentially be used consistently for longitudinal monitoring of strength or applied with an appropriate calibration or correction factor relative to the Jamar. Further studies in larger and more diverse CKD populations are needed to confirm these findings and define optimal implementation strategies.

Qualitative feedback from the open-ended survey question provided additional nuance to the feasibility and acceptability of remote assessments. Although most patients viewed the Zoom-based assessments favorably, technical and interpersonal challenges potentially limit the format's perceived utility. For example, participants noted issues with audio clarity and expressed discomfort with the absence of an in-person clinician providing instructions. These mixed experiences highlight that, although videoconferencing could be a useful tool for remote physical function assessment in this population, successful implementation may require careful attention to technology optimization and strategies to increase patient comfort.

Despite this study's promising findings, several limitations merit consideration. Although comparable to prior studies of similar methodological rigor, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the precision of the validity and reliability estimates. In addition, participants were recruited from a single academic center and were predominantly individuals with stage 3 CKD, potentially limiting the generalizability to those with more advanced disease. Generalizability is further limited by the exclusion of individuals with recent cardiovascular events, recent falls, inability to walk alone or without assistance of another person, or cognitive impairment. As a result, the study population likely represents a more functionally stable group of individuals with CKD, and findings may not fully extend to more medically and functionally vulnerable patients who may also benefit from remote assessment. However, this study also has several important strengths. The cohort included racial diversity and a balanced distribution of sex, with many participants demonstrating low digital health literacy, supporting the feasibility of videoconference-based assessment in populations that may face barriers to digital health engagement. The predominance of stage 3 CKD reflects the patient population commonly seen in nephrology clinics, enhancing clinical relevance. Furthermore, the use of a widely available, commercially accessible videoconferencing platform in Zoom enhances generalizability and scalability. Additional strengths include concurrent in-person and remote assessments, rigorous evaluation of validity and reliability across multiple physical function measures, and incorporation of both quantitative usability metrics and qualitative participant feedback. Together, this study provides an important foundation and serves as a proof-of-concept for larger, multicenter studies evaluating the use of telemedicine-based physical function assessment across diverse CKD populations.

Future studies of remote functional status assessment should include larger, multicenter cohorts with representation across the full spectrum of CKD stages. In addition, extending assessments to the home setting represents an important next step. Home-based approaches have already been successfully implemented for other components of CKD care, including urine albumin testing.40 Remote assessment of physical function could be adapted for home-based use, leveraging accessible tools such as the digital dynamometers evaluated in this study. Incorporation of widely available videoconferencing platforms, such as Zoom, into home-based assessments may help address the persistent underutilization of functional status assessment in CKD care.

In summary, videoconference-based assessments conducted in a controlled research environment demonstrated acceptable validity, reliability, and usability for evaluating physical function in adults with CKD. Although larger, multicenter centers are needed to confirm these findings, this work extends previous research on remote functional assessment in other chronic disease populations. The findings support the feasibility of videoconferencing platforms such as Zoom for assessing physical function in CKD and provide a foundation for future studies evaluating the scalability and clinical integration of remote physical function assessments in the home and across the CKD spectrum.
