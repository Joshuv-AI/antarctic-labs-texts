Parental occupational exposure to pesticides at birth and risk of adult testicular germ cell tumours in offspring: a French nationwide case-control study
Authors: Adèle Paul, Aurélie Danjou, Astrid Coste, Hans Kromhout, Marie Lefevre, Johan Spinosi, Brigitte Dananché, Ann Olsson, Céline Lamouroux, Margot Guth, Rémi Béranger, Olivia Pérol, Helen Boyle, Christel Hersant, Vanessa Loup-Cabaniols, Segolene Veau, Jeanne Perrin, Joachim Schüz, Barbara Charbotel, Béatrice Fervers, Aude Fléchon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the association between parental occupational pesticide exposures at birth and adult testicular germ cell tumour (TGCT) by histological subtype and the agreement between two pesticide job-exposure matrices (JEM).MethodsTGCT cases (n=454) and matched controls (n=670) aged 18–45 years were recruited from 20 French university hospitals into the TESTIS national case-control study. Paternal and maternal jobs at birth were obtained from interviews of participants and their mothers/relatives and coded into official nomenclatures (International Standard Classification of Occupations, French nomenclature of activity-1999). Two complementary JEMs, ALOHA+ and FRIJEM, assessed occupational exposure to all pesticides, herbicides, insecticides, fungicides with ALOHA+ for each parent by linking job titles to JEM-derived probability and intensity estimates (no/low/high). Cohen’s Kappa coefficient (κ) assessed their agreement. ORs and 95% CIs comparing exposure and levels of exposure with no exposure were estimated using conditional logistic regression adjusted for literature-based covariates statistically associated with TGCT and stratified by histological subtypes.ResultsAgreement between ALOHA+and FRIJEM was moderate to substantial (κ 0.52–0.80). A statistically non-significant higher TGCT risk was observed for high paternal occupational pesticide exposure with ALOHA+ (OR 1.95, CI 0.98 to 3.84) and FRIJEM (OR 1.70, CI 0.88 to 3.28). With ALOHA+, statistically significant positive associations were seen for high paternal occupational exposure to herbicides (overall: OR 4.23, CI 1.75 to 10.22; seminomas: OR 4.78, CI 1.79 to 12.77; non-seminomas: OR 3.53, CI 1.21 to 10.3) and fungicides (overall: OR 2.09, CI 1.05 to 4.18; seminomas: OR 2.31, CI 1.02 to 5.23). No statistically significant associations were observed for other levels of paternal exposure or pesticide groups nor for maternal exposure.ConclusionOnly high paternal occupational exposure to pesticides at birth, especially herbicides and fungicides, was positively associated with TGCT.

Introduction

Over the past 40 years, incidence rates of testicular germ cell tumours (TGCT) have increased and are expected to further rise in many countries.1 TGCT is the most frequent cancer in men aged 15–45 years, representing 1–2% of male cancers.2 Established risk factors include familial history of TGCT and some genetic polymorphisms.3

Seminomas and non-seminomas derive from preinvasive germ cell neoplasia in situ (GCNIS), originating from abnormal fetal gonocytes and can be associated with altered spermatogenesis and specific congenital malformations (cryptorchidism and hypospadias), grouped together as Testicular Dysgenesis Syndrome (TDS).4 Thus, the impact of prenatal exposures on TGCT development is of particular interest. Exposure to agents with endocrine disrupting properties, including some pesticides,5 may contribute to TGCT pathogenesis.6

The number of pesticides and the quantity used have grown rapidly since the 1940s and declined only very recently in France.7 They include a broad category of substances including herbicides, insecticides and fungicides. Each category has different modes of action, targets and thus possible toxicity in humans.8 Several studies suggest that prenatal paternal or maternal occupational pesticide exposure could be involved in TGCT in the offspring but studies suffer from lack of statistical power or difficulty to assess exposure in the relevant window of exposure of fetal life.9–11 Differential associations according to pesticide group exposure have been observed, with a higher risk of TGCT for maternal domestic exposure to fungicides during pregnancy, while not to insecticides or herbicides.12

Job-exposure matrices (JEMs) are an established method for exposure assessment in occupational epidemiology designed to translate job histories into estimates of probability and intensity of occupational exposures. Because no gold standard exists, formal validation of JEMs is difficult and represents a common limitation. Their strengths include independent expert-based development, consensus procedures and the systematic assignment of occupational exposures, which helps avoid differential misclassification between cases and controls. However, JEM assessments may diverge for several reasons, including population-specific data sources, differences in job classification granularity, variations in expert judgement differences and temporal changes in exposure.13 Their agreement on occupational exposure to pesticides has rarely been assessed.13–15

This work aimed to estimate the association between paternal and maternal occupational exposure to pesticides at birth as a proxy of prenatal exposures and the risk of developing TGCT in adulthood, overall and by histological subtype, using two different JEMs. A secondary aim was to assess agreement between the two JEMs.

Methods

Study design and recruitment

This study is part of the TESTIS project, a multicentre prospective case-control study conducted between January 2015 and April 2018 in 20 university hospital centres in Metropolitan France, aiming to investigate environmental, domestic and occupational risk factors of TGCT.12 The detailed study protocol has been published previously.12 16 Briefly, patients diagnosed with primary TGCT, aged 18–45 years, referred for semen preservation to the Centres for the Study and Storage of Human Sperm and Eggs (CECOS) were included as cases. A TGCT expert (HB) confirmed TGCT cases from pathology reports (92.2%) and serum tumour markers (84%). Only cases with GCNIS-related tumour, classified as seminoma or non-seminoma, were included.17 In case of missing pathology report (7.8%, n=43), patients were included as cases, since the proportion of false-positive TGCT among those ascertained by pathological report was low (5.1%). Two groups of controls were recruited: group A were sperm donors recruited in the CECOS or partners with normal sperm production of women consulting in assisted reproduction treatment centres; group B were partners of women in high-risk pregnancy services of the hospital centre. Cases and controls were frequency matched on hospital centre administrative region and year of birth (+/– 5 years). Participants not being born in Metropolitan France and controls with a personal history of testicular cancer or cryptorchidism were not eligible. All participants provided written consent and were asked for permission to contact their biological mother or, if not available, the closest relative alive who were then invited to participate in the study. Trained investigators (IPSOS company) conducted separate interviews with participants and mothers/relatives, blinded to the case-control status16 to collect information on socioeconomic status, birth characteristics, medical history, lifestyle factors and parental jobs at birth. Information on the jobs held at birth by the biological mother and father was collected from the mother or another relative. All were given a document to prepare for the interview, especially regarding their job history. They were asked to have their health record at hand during the interview to increase the reliability of responses. A total of 1124 participants were included: 454 TGCT cases, 384 group A controls and 286 group B controls (figure 1).

The two-stage recruitment process of participants’ mothers/relatives resulted in a 50% participation rate of mothers/relatives. Given the good agreement between the employment data reported by the mothers and by the sons,18 the analyses were carried out on the whole study population using the data collected from the sons.

Exposure assessment

Paternal and maternal occupational pesticide exposures at birth were assessed using two complementary JEMs (ALOHA+and FRIJEM). For each parent, pesticide exposure was defined by linking their job titles to the probability and intensity estimates provided by each JEM. Parental occupations and industries at birth were encoded by an industrial hygienist (BD) blinded to the participants’ case-control status, using the International Standard Classification of Occupations 1968 (ISCO-68)19 and the French nomenclature of activity 1999 (NAF-1999).20 The coding method has been described in detail elsewhere.18

ALOHA+ is an expert-based JEM applied to populations from different countries, including the French population,21 as well as birth cohorts from five countries (Australia, Denmark, Israel, Norway and the UK)22 to assess parental occupational exposures during fetal development. Pesticide exposures in ALOHA+ are estimated by assigning to each ISCO-88 code probability and intensity levels of exposure to overall pesticides, herbicides, insecticides and fungicides. The transcoding from ISCO-68 to ISCO-88 was based on transcoding data from the International Labour Office.23 For ISCO-68 codes with multiple matches in ISCO-88, experts in occupational exposure research (AP, BC, BD, JSp) assigned the most relevant code. Using a semiquantitative approach, exposure levels were classified as high, low or not exposed (2, 1 or 0) depending on the intensity and the probability of exposure. In order to favour specificity over sensitivity, only jobs with a high intensity and a high probability of exposure were assigned a ‘high’ exposure; jobs with a low probability of exposure irrespective of the intensity were assigned ‘low’ exposure, as were jobs with low exposure probability and low intensity.

A French Job Exposure Matrix (FRIJEM) was developed by French occupational experts (BC, BD, JSp) specifically for the French TESTIS population. The matrix assigns to each ISCO-68/NAF-1999 pair a level of intensity and a level of probability of pesticide exposure overall, according to the experts’ judgement and further categorises exposure as high, medium, low or not exposed (3, 2, 1 or 0). FRIJEM does not differentiate groups of pesticides.

Insufficient or missing job data made it impossible to assign a complete ISCO code and apply JEMs to 85 participants (48 fathers and 37 mothers) for ALOHA+ and 83 participants (47 fathers and 36 mothers) for FRIJEM and were excluded in paternal or maternal analyses, respectively. Agreement between the two JEMs (exposed/non-exposed status and level of exposure) was assessed in fathers and in mothers using Cohen’s Kappa and weighted Kappa coefficients.24 To enable direct comparison with the ALOHA+ exposure categories, FRIJEM exposure intensity and probability ratings were recoded into three aggregated categories:

‘no exposure’: intensity and probability ratings both equal to 0.

‘no exposure’: intensity and probability ratings both equal to 0.

‘low exposure’: any combination of intensity and probability ratings not meeting the criteria for high exposure.

‘low exposure’: any combination of intensity and probability ratings not meeting the criteria for high exposure.

‘high exposure’: intensity and probability ratings both equal to levels 2 or 3.

‘high exposure’: intensity and probability ratings both equal to levels 2 or 3.

For example, working as ‘primary education teaching professionals’, ‘veterinary assistants’ or ‘agricultural, fishery and related labourers’ were considered to involve no exposure, low exposure or high exposure to pesticides, respectively.

Statistical analyses

OR and 95% CIs for adult TGCT comparing exposure and levels of exposure to no exposure were estimated for maternal and paternal occupational pesticide exposure at birth using conditional logistic regression models. A two-sided Cochrane-Armitage trend test was used to assess exposure-response relationship (p value trend). All models were conditioned on matching factors (hospital’s administrative region and birth year grouped in five year categories).

Covariates considered for adjustment included literature-based perinatal characteristics: gestational age, birth weight, sibship size, birth order and being born from a multiple pregnancy; family-related factors (regarding father, brothers, uncles except step-uncles): family history of testicular cancer, family history of cryptorchidism; personal risk factors: tobacco smoking, cannabis use, cannabis frequency use in adolescence or during young adulthood, personal history of testicular trauma requiring consultation or hospitalisation; personal protective factor: age at voice change as a proxy for age of puberty.25 Univariate analysis was performed to assess associations of TGCT with each factor. Those with p value of the Wald test <0.20 were included in a joint model and a manual backward stepwise selection was performed. The final model included the following covariates statistically significantly associated with the outcome (p<0.05): born from multiple pregnancy, sibship size, personal history of testicular trauma, family history of cryptorchidism and family history of testicular cancer.

Analyses were further stratified by histological TGCT subtype, seminoma and non-seminoma, with the same set of covariates. The likelihood ratio test of polytomous logistic regression for matched case-control studies was used to test heterogeneity of associations.26

In sensitivity analyses, TGCT cases with a personal history of cryptorchidism (n=40), TGCT cases not confirmed by pathology reports (n=43) and participants with family history of TGCT (n=49) were respectively excluded. To avoid residual confounding due to the broad 5-year age-matching strata, additional analyses adjusted for age as a continuous variable were conducted (matching solely based on region).

ORs were considered statistically significant when the CIs did not include the value of 1.00 (p<5%).

Spearman’s rank coefficient assessed the correlation between the three pesticide exposure groups (herbicides, insecticides, fungicides) appraised by ALOHA+.

Data analysis was performed using SAS statistical software V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Correlations between exposures to pesticide groups (ALOHA+)

Spearman correlation coefficients among pesticide group exposures assessed with ALOHA+ ranged from 0.79 (herbicides and fungicides) to 0.85 (insecticides and fungicides) for fathers and from 0.57 (insecticides and herbicides) to 0.84 (fungicides and herbicides) for mothers with available exposure data (n=1076). Given the small number of parents exposed to pesticides in this population, especially mothers, Spearman coefficients were further calculated in the population exposed to at least one pesticide and ranged from 0.71 (herbicides and fungicides) to 0.85 (insecticides and fungicides) for fathers (n=92) and from 0.75 (insecticides and fungicides) to 0.90 (insecticides and herbicides) for mothers (n=28) (online supplemental table 3).

Agreement between ALOHA+ and FRIJEM

Agreement between ALOHA+and FRIJEM was substantial for parental occupations at birth assessed as exposed/non-exposed to pesticides (κ=0.66, CI 0.58 to 0.75 for fathers; κ=0.80, CI 0.69 to 0.92 for mothers). Agreement for exposure level was moderate to substantial (weighted κ=0.52, CI 0.44–0.60 to 0.67, CI 0.60 to 0.74 for fathers; weighted κ=0.71, CI 0.59–0.84 to 0.78, CI 0.68 to 0.88 for mothers)24 (table 4).

Parental pesticide occupational exposure at birth and TGCT risk

With both JEMs, high exposure to overall pesticides in fathers (adjusted OR (ORadj) 1.95, CI 0.98 to 3.84 (ALOHA+); ORadj 1.70, CI 0.88 to 3.28; (FRIJEM)) showed a higher risk although statistically non-significant of TGCT overall (table 2). No statistically significant association was observed neither with ALOHA+nor FRIJEM for maternal occupational pesticide exposure (table 3, online supplemental table 5). Crude ORs are presented in supplemental materials (online supplemental tables 5, 6).

With ALOHA+, we observed positive associations between high paternal occupational exposure to insecticides (statistically non-significant ORadj 1.97, CI 1.00 to 3.89), fungicides (statistically significant ORadj 2.09, CI 1.05 to 4.18; p value trend=0.08) and herbicides (statistically significant ORadj 4.23, CI 1.75 to 10.22 and exposure-response relationship (no/low/high-exposure; p value trend=0.05)) and TGCT overall. Statistically significant positive associations were observed by histological subtype for high paternal occupational exposure to herbicides (ORadj 4.78, CI 1.79 to 12.77 for seminomas; ORadj 3.53, CI 1.21 to 10.30 for non-seminomas; p-het 0.60) and fungicides (ORadj=2.31, CI 1.02 to 5.23; p value heterogeneity=0.89 for seminomas) (table 2, online supplemental table 6).

In sensitivity analyses, with ALOHA+, the positive associations between high paternal exposures to pesticides overall or insecticides and TGCT overall became statistically significant after excluding cases not confirmed by pathology report (ORadj 2.14, CI 1.08 to 4.21 and ORadj 2.16, CI 1.09 to 4.26, respectively) or with personal history of cryptorchidism (ORadj 2.11, CI 1.05 to 4.21 and ORadj 2.13, CI 1.07 to 4.26, respectively). Positive associations remained unchanged for high occupational exposure to herbicides and fungicides (online supplemental table 7). With FRIJEM, regarding exposure to all pesticides, adjustment for age resulted in a borderline statistically significant positive association between high paternal exposure and TGCT overall (ORadj 1.93, CI 1.00 to 3.72). Sensitivity analyses showed no statistically significant association for maternal exposures (online supplemental table 8).

Discussion

In this case-control study, high paternal occupational exposures at birth to herbicides or fungicides were associated with increased odds of TGCT in adulthood (all histological subtypes for herbicides; TGCT overall and seminomas for fungicides), with a positive exposure-response relationship for herbicides (ALOHA+). The observed positive associations of TGCT with high paternal exposures to overall pesticides and insecticides became statistically significant after excluding TGCT cases without pathology confirmation and cases with a personal history of cryptorchidism. There was overall a strong correlation between the different groups of pesticides, leading to a cautious interpretation of the potential primary effect of a single class of pesticide. While there was a moderate to substantial agreement (κ) between the two JEMs, no statistically significant association was observed with FRIJEM except for high pesticide exposure among fathers after adjustment on age of participants. No statistically significant associations were found with maternal pesticide exposures, yet the number of exposed women was small.

The observed disparities between the two JEMs could be explained by the consideration of the sector of activity in addition to occupation (ISCO-68/NAF-1999 pair) in FRIJEM, unlike ALOHA+ (ISCO-88)27 as well as by variations linked to the transcoding of ISCO-68 (FRIJEM) to the more detailed ISCO-88 (ALOHA+): one ISCO-68 code might match with several ISCO-88 codes with different exposures in ALOHA+, leading to a possible misclassification bias.28 Moreover, differences in pesticide exposures between France and the foreign population on which ALOHA+was developed may have contributed (variations in agricultural practices and use patterns of pesticides,13 differences in job tasks).

To our knowledge, this is the first study comparing two complementary JEMs to assess the robustness of the results, one constructed for the general population, although not French, and the other, constructed specifically for our French TESTIS population. One study compared two general JEMs containing an item on insecticides, one Finnish (FINJEM) and the other Canadian, but the number of workers exposed was too small to assess their agreement.15

The association between prenatal parental pesticide exposure and the risk of TGCT in adulthood has been poorly investigated in previous studies with divergent results.9–11 29 30 A systematic review of the literature identified five studies that investigated agriculture-related exposures and TGCT risk in the offspring, with small populations and low exposure prevalence, resulting in limited statistical power. Only one study reported a higher risk of TGCT overall and non-seminomas for sons of Norwegian agricultural workers using nitrate fertilisers compared with the total rural population, even if fertiliser chemicals do not fit the strict definition of pesticides.10 A large registry-based case-control study (9569 cases and 32 028 controls) found no statistically significant association between paternal or maternal occupational pesticide exposure before birth (FINJEM) and the risk of TGCT.9 However, the prevalence of pesticide exposure was low and limitations to assess prenatal parental exposure based on occupational information on job codes derived from censuses as well as lag time between census and child’s birth (0–15 years) might have contributed to null results. Yet, TGCT risk was statistically significantly associated with parental pesticide exposure for the 1960–1965 birth cohort and for paternal occupational pesticide exposure before birth in Denmark (1457 cases and 5764 controls). Methodological limitations to assess exposure to singular active ingredients might explain the heterogeneity of observations. The TESTIS study has previously shown a significant positive association between maternal domestic fungicides use from 1 year before start of pregnancy to 1 year after birth and the risk of TGCT overall and non-seminomas. No statistically significant association was observed for insecticides nor herbicides.12 Moreover, prenatal environmental exposure to endocrine disrupting pesticides has been associated with significantly higher risk of TGCT.31 Furthermore, parental occupational32 and domestic33 exposures to pesticides during pregnancy have been associated with significantly higher risk of developing hypospadias proposed to be part of the TDS. Very few studies have used maternal serum concentrations of pesticides with endocrine disrupting properties to assess the risk of TGCT in sons.11 29 34 35 A nested case-control study with a very small sample size (15 TGCT cases and 45 controls) found a significantly higher ratio of p,p′-DDT to p,p′-DDE in cases’ mothers and an inverse association between maternal DDE and TGCT,34 while a nested case-control study of 332 mother-son pairs (65 TGCT cases and 267 controls) found no association between maternal serum concentrations of organochlorine compounds and risk of TGCT.35 A case-control study (45 case mothers and 44 control mothers) reported positive associations with the risk of TGCT in adult offspring and exposures to hexachlorobenzene, trans-nonachlordane and cis-nonachlordane. However, concentrations in maternal blood samples collected at time of the adult sons’ inclusion may not be representative of prenatal exposures.11 29

Strengths of our study are the relatively large sample size, the distinction of histological subtypes of TGCT and the collection of detailed occupational histories from participants. Since every JEM might have specific limitations related to their development, the application of two complementary JEMs increases the robustness of our findings. Compared with self-reported exposures, JEM-based exposure characterisation is less subject to recall bias and over-reporting. Also, the use of JEMs helps prevent confirmation bias since they are developed objectively and blinded to the case-control status. Besides, cases and controls were frequency matched on hospital centre administrative region and year of birth (+/– 5 years) to avoid overmatching and to prevent loss of efficiency that would result from discarding cases when suitable individually matched controls could not be identified.

Limitations include the recruitment of cases from the CECOS, possibly leaving out TGCT cases that did not have sperm cryopreservation. Prevalence of parental exposure to pesticide at birth was low, particularly among mothers, resulting in limited statistical power. Moreover, the two JEMs did not differentiate exposure periods while birth years of the TESTIS population cover a broad period (1969–1999). Changes in agricultural practices and pesticide use did not occur according to clear-cut periods and made it difficult to differentiate exposure periods in FRIJEM. Of note, only two of the 40 JEMs assessing pesticide exposure reviewed by Carles et al13 distinguished exposure periods. Finally, pesticide exposure was estimated using maternal and paternal jobs as a proxy, which does not capture inter-individual variations in exposures, unlike biomonitoring. Also, the use of employment data at birth as a proxy for prenatal paternal and maternal occupational pesticide exposures may have introduced bias as changes in employment and activities might have occurred around this period. It may induce a recall bias as well, although birth is a significant event and may help to remember the job held at the time.

Conclusion

Our study suggests an association of TGCT in adulthood with high paternal occupational exposure at birth to herbicides (for TGCT, seminomas, non-seminomas) and fungicides (for TGCT, seminomas), supporting involvement of prenatal exposures in the development of GCNIS related TGCT. No significant associations were observed for other paternal exposure levels or pesticide groups, nor for maternal exposure. Future studies should consider the combined role of periconceptional, prenatal and later-life exposures and take into consideration genetic polymorphisms.
