ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe AV node is a complex structure with multiple outputs into the atria and ventricles, which allows for arrhythmias with multiple patterns of atrial and ventricular relationships.Case SummaryA patient with supraventricular tachycardias received ablation for both typical AVNRT through slow pathway modification and orthodromic reciprocating tachycardia through left posteroseptal accessory pathway ablation. Despite this, she continued to have episodes of tachycardia that were symptomatic. Repeat 12-lead ECG demonstrated a regularly irregular tachycardia with both narrow and wide complexes. An electrophysiology study was performed and showed sinus rhythm with dual AV nodal physiology with conduction down both the fast and slow pathways, resulting in double ventricular responses to a single atrial impulse. Additional slow pathway modification was performed, and no further arrhythmias were noted.DiscussionDual AV nodal non-reentrant tachycardia is an uncommon arrhythmia that can occur in patients with dual AV nodal physiology, particularly after slow pathway modification for AVNRT.Take-Home MessageCareful evaluation of P wave to QRS relationships on ECG can help elucidate complex patterns of arrhythmia Dual AV nodal non-reentrant tachycardia is an uncommon arrhythmia that can occur in patients with dual AV nodal physiology, particularly after slow pathway modification for AV nodal reentrant tachycardia.

Case

A 52-year-old woman presents with recurrent supraventricular tachycardia episodes, status post slow pathway modification for typical atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia (AVNRT) and ablation of left posterior accessory pathway for orthodromic reciprocating tachycardia. She noted episodes of shortness of breath and tachycardia on her smart watch, despite flecainide and beta-blocker therapy. Discussion

The underlying mechanism of the arrhythmia is D. The arrhythmia shown is a case of dual atrioventricular (AV) nodal nonreentrant tachycardia, also known as the double fire phenomenon.1 The ECG (Figure 1) demonstrates a predominantly narrow complex tachycardia with a ventricular rate of approximately 130 beats/min, which is regularly irregular. Also noted is that the QRS complex morphology has regular aberrancy. The underlying sinus rate however is closer to 70 beats/min, as seen in Supplemental Figure 1. The differential diagnosis for this ECG includes frequent atrial ectopy, atrial tachycardia with variable AV block, and junctional ectopy in pattern of bigeminy.

The patient had previously undergone ablation for typical AVNRT. During ablation, the slow pathway region in the floor of the right atrium is ablated, usually with radiofrequency or cryoablation energy. Because the slow pathway is a diffuse region, only certain parts of the region are likely modified during ablation, leaving the potential of residual slow pathway conduction that is no longer robust enough to sustain AVNRT, but can still participate in arrhythmias. Atrial signals are normally able to conduct down both the fast pathway and the slow pathway; however, the delayed input from the slow pathway often arrives on refractory tissue in the lower portion of the AV node (lower common pathway) that has already been depolarized by the fast pathway, resulting in a single ventricular activation. In the ECG scenario, antegrade pathway conduction was so slow that enough time had passed to allow the lower common pathway to recover, leading to a second ventricular systole. We can see how this occurs in the intracardiac tracings (Supplemental Figure 2 and Supplemental Figure 3), where a single atrial stimulus results in 2 ventricular responses, seen with commitment of His bundle potentials. A critical clue is that the interval from the single P-wave to the first QRS interval remains constant (reflecting fast pathway conduction), as does the interval to the second QRS interval (reflecting slow pathway conduction) provided the “grouped” beating characteristic on the ECG (Supplemental Figure 4). Additionally, the difference in coupling intervals with wide beats is prolonged in comparison with narrow beats, suggesting potentially an alternative slow pathway or phase IV aberration.

After additional ablation of the slow pathway with radiofrequency energy, no further double fire events were noted. Her beta blockade and flecainide therapy were discontinued, and she had no recurrence of paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia subsequently.
