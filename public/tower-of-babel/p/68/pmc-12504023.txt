Interventional Radiology for Primary Postpartum Hemorrhage: Optimal Strategy Based on the Pathogenesis of Bleeding
Authors: Reiko Woodhams, Kaoru Fujii, Yutaro Kurihara, Daigo Ochiai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe need for interventional radiology in hemostatic strategies for postpartum hemorrhage has been growing as a reliable and hopeful treatment method because of its non-invasive characteristics, high success rate of hemostatic outcomes, and potential to preserve fertility. Moreover, with the global trend of increasing postpartum hemorrhage, interventional radiology is expected to play an important role. An accurate pre-procedural diagnosis of the underlying cause of bleeding, including tone, trauma, tissue, or thrombin, utilizing contrast-enhanced computed tomography and clinical information, is essential for planning the appropriate technical approach to interventional radiology. This study outlines the fundamental aspects of primary postpartum hemorrhage, compares the computed tomography imaging characteristics associated with various causes, and discusses the appropriate procedural choices of interventional radiology based on the identified etiology.Keywords: post-partum hemorrhage, interventional radiology, arterial embolization, gelatin sponge, NBCA

Introduction

Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) is a primary contributor to maternal mortality during the perinatal period. The global increase in PPH can be attributed to several factors, including the increase in the average age of mothers, a greater incidence of cesarean sections, and a notable increase in obesity rates among pregnant women [1, 2]. The prompt and appropriate initial management, which includes recognizing abnormal bleeding, swiftly engaging a multidisciplinary team of obstetricians, midwives, anesthesiologists, emergency physicians, and interventional radiologists, and rapidly initiating treatment including timely introduction of drug therapy, blood transfusion, and intrauterine balloon tamponade, is crucial for restoration and maintenance of hemodynamic stability [3, 4].

Previously, PPH refractory to all other treatment measures was managed by hysterectomy. However, along with the loss of fertility, this resulted in high morbidity and mortality, in which the enlarged uterus, previous cesarean section, coagulation failure, and the mother's deteriorating condition all played a role [5, 6]. Interventional radiology (IR) offers a nonsurgical approach to the treatment of PPH that has the potential to preserve fertility. It is generally offered to patients who do not respond to conservative methods and can be a crucial, ultimate solution before opting for a hysterectomy [7]. Previous studies have demonstrated the high technical and clinical success rate (>90%) of IR for PPH [8]. To ensure the high quality of IR and thus efficient hemostasis in PPH patients, the technique of IR needs to be meticulously adapted according to the bleeding etiology. This review article examines tailored IR techniques designed to manage various pathological conditions effectively in the treatment of primary PPH.

Comprehensive consent was secured from the patients whose data contributed to this review article.

Bleeding Etiology of PPH

PPH is classified as primary PPH, defined as occurring within 24 hours after birth, and secondary PPH, occurring >24 hours after birth. The focus of this paper is primary PPH, because it includes conditions that are more critical than those in secondary PPH, and IR significantly enhances patient survival and outcomes.

The etiology of primary PPH is divided into four categories: tone, trauma, tissue, and thrombin [9], although multiple factors may coexist in bleeding. The most frequent pathology for primary PPH is tone, which is represented by atonic bleeding. The failure of the uterine myometrium to contract leads to a lack of mechanical hemostasis at the placental separation site and thus to bleeding. Most atonic bleeding is unpredictable and occurs suddenly. The risk factors include multiple pregnancies, a very large fetus, prolonged delivery, overuse of uterine contraceptives, intrauterine hematoma, and general anesthesia. Amniotic fluid embolization (AFE) can be a hidden factor in patients with atonic bleeding [10].

The second most frequent bleeding etiology is trauma, including cervical and vaginal laceration, puerperal genital hematoma (PGHA), and uterine rupture. Trauma is highly associated with instrumented vaginal delivery. It also includes iatrogenic vessel trauma during cesarean operation and hysterectomy.

Tissue refers to placenta accreta spectrum (PAS), including placenta accreta, increta and percreta, and retained placenta. The increases in retained placenta and PAS have been attributed to increased rates of cesarean operation and the use of assisted reproduction technologies [9, 11].

Thrombin, that is, coagulopathy, is a critical condition in PPH. The representative causes of primary coagulopathy are placental abruption, HELLP (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets) syndrome, pre-eclampsia, intrauterine fetal death, and AFE [12, 13].

Utility of Contrast-Enhanced CT in PPH

The diagnosis and treatment of PPH have been greatly improved by the use of contrast-enhanced CT (CE-CT), as demonstrated in previous studies [14-17]. The scan protocol is designed to include pre-contrast, early phase, and delayed phases, with images taken from the upper abdomen to below the pubic bone.

CE-CT is useful for decision making regarding the introduction of IR according to the presence of extravasation [18]. The sensitivity of CE-CT for active bleeding is higher than that of angiography [19]. In the absence of extravasation on CE-CT, the decision to perform embolization is made by comprehensive evaluation of clinical course, clinical findings, and laboratory data, although conservative management may be a reasonable option [16].

One major benefit of CE-CT is its ability to provide wide-ranging insights into vascular anatomy, possibly decreasing the need for conventional aortography and pelvic angiography. CE-CT can be used to identify the access route to the bleeding point, to highlight anatomic variations, and to depict the collateral circulation (Figure 1). The uterine artery and the internal iliac artery (IIA) may be empirically embolized during arterial embolization for PPH, as the catheterization of these arteries is relatively simple. Meanwhile, extrapelvic collateral vessels, typically the ovarian artery and the artery of the round ligament of the uterus (RLA), also known as Sampson's artery, are not routinely embolized [20-22]. Catheterization of the ovarian artery can be challenging due to its varying branching level, angle, and origin, as well as the possibility of a narrowed origin. Moreover, visualization of the ovarian artery using aortography is reported to be possible in only 20% of patients [23]. However, CE-CT facilitates precise catheter engagement in such cases by providing specific anatomic information. Recognizing the likelihood of a particular artery's contribution to bleeding in advance improves the execution and effectiveness of catheterization and subsequent procedures. Preoperative evaluation of CE-CT may also prevent overlooking bleeding vessels, resulting in secure hemostasis [22, 24]. The involved ovarian artery and RLA usually dilate and anastomose with the uterine artery at the ovarian bursa. Nevertheless, in cases of coagulopathy, even minor arteries that do not seem to interact with the uterine artery may be susceptible to bleeding.

The location of a hematoma in a CT image can offer insights into the cause of the bleeding. Intraperitoneal hematoma is a sign of uterine rupture, which may lead to the choice of laparotomy as the primary treatment approach. Coagulopathy can result in the development of hematomas in the retroperitoneal and intraperitoneal cavities due to diffuse bleeding from the incision scar of the uterus and the abdominal wall (Figure 2). CT also has a great advantage in demonstrating both the extent of PGHA, especially supralevator hematoma, which can be clinically and sonographically occult [25] (Figure 3). Atonic bleeding, AFE, and PAS are responsible for the development of intrauterine hematomas. CE-CT is highly recommended for patients in whom intraperitoneal bleeding is suspected, with a history of ligation and embolization, who experience rebleeding after embolization and ligation and/or bleeding after perinatal surgery, and for those for whom the clinical diagnosis is unclear or treatment has been ineffective, resulting in further deterioration of condition [15, 26, 27]. In these situations, unexpected bleeding sources and etiology may be involved. However, the use of CE-CT must be weighed against the radiation exposure, the risk of an abrupt decline in the patient's condition during imaging, and the potential delay of therapeutic intervention [28, 29]. Safely conducting a CT scan for PPH patients requires hemodynamic stabilization through the administration of red blood cells and fresh frozen plasma or fibrinogen preparations, along with continuous monitoring of hemodynamic status throughout the scan. An emergency care room equipped with a CT table (hybrid emergency room) offers the ideal setting for performing CT in patients with PPH [16].

IR Indications

When standard treatments, including drug therapy, uterine massage, balloon tamponade, and suture of the tear fail, IR will be an option. The application of IR in managing PPH is usually reserved for individuals with stable circulatory status. Nevertheless, in situations where there is circulatory instability, the decision to implement IR relies on various factors, including the facility's emergency response capability and the expertise and experience of the medical personnel involved [9].

Atonic bleeding, genital tract injury, PGHA, and PAS are good indications for IR. Meanwhile, surgical repair is preferred for uterine rupture and uterine inversion. The utility of IR in disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is controversial, as it is one of the possible causes of embolization failure in PPH [8, 30, 31]. Nevertheless, a high success rate in achieving hemostasis in patients with DIC has been reported [32]. The outcome of arterial embolization for DIC is thought to be influenced by factors such as the embolization technique, the quality of the clinical management, and the patient's condition.

Preparation for IR Procedures

To understand the etiology of PPH and to plan the IR treatment strategy, detailed information on the clinical course, clinical diagnosis, hemodynamics, and blood biochemistry (including coagulation factors) should be obtained, together with evaluation of CE-CT and ultrasound images prior to the IR procedure [33]. A priority of PPH treatment is hemodynamic stabilization. If necessary, the emergency physician or anesthesiologist should be consulted for systemic management. Transfusions, especially coagulation factor replacement, should be administered promptly in patients with coagulopathy.

3. To confirm obstruction of the embolized artery, it is important to take sufficient time during angiography to allow the contrast material to fully reach the distal end of the vessels and thus demonstrate collateral communications.

4. Angiographic visualization of the peripheral segment of the uterine artery or other original bleeding artery through collateral communications is an indication of the culprit artery. Extravasation is not always observed even with active clinical bleeding. Vaginal inspection is essential to confirm hemostasis. The cessation of extravasation or the absence of targeted vessel visualization in angiography does not necessarily indicate the achievement of clinical hemostasis. In patients with PGHA without vaginal bleeding, stable hemodynamics indicate hemostasis.

5. In patients with ongoing vaginal bleeding or no signs of improvement in hemodynamic stability, one should check for recanalization of the embolized artery and perform aortography at the level of the renal artery or catheterize the extrapelvic collateral arteries such as the ovarian artery and RLA, when present.

6. Carbon dioxide angiography is sensitive in the detection of minor and peripheral active bleeding, due to its low viscosity and vasodilatory effect [34, 35]. Critical Hemodynamics

In patients with PPH, hemodynamics can rapidly deteriorate. Once a patient experiences cardiopulmonary arrest, resuscitation becomes significantly challenging. Improving and maintaining hemodynamics are therefore of paramount importance in the management of PPH. The interventional radiologist must pay close attention to the patient's hemodynamics during the embolization procedure and modify it accordingly.

Resuscitative balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA), originally utilized in patients in hemorrhagic shock due to trauma [36], is a rapid and powerful hemostatic method that should be prioritized in hemodynamically critical patients and those with impending or actual cardiac arrest. When REBOA is performed for PPH, the balloon position is usually at the infrarenal level (zone III) to occlude the ovarian artery, but the level of balloon occlusion should be modified depending on the branching level of the ovarian artery and the patient's hemodynamics [37, 38]. Although there is no consensus regarding the duration and interval of REBOA occlusion, a duration of <30 minutes and an interval of 1-5 minutes are common choices [39, 40]. Heparinization is controversial, especially for patients with exsanguination and a complex coagulopathic status. Low-dose heparin may be a compromise to prevent thrombosis [41]. Complications of REBOA in PPH patients include thromboembolism, limb ischemia, reperfusion injury, puncture site pseudoaneurysm, and aortic rupture and dissection. [36, 40, 42]. While REBOA is a simple procedure, it should be performed with extreme caution.

Nonselective embolization from the IIA or the anterior division using a gelatin sponge may be an emergency salvage procedure, as either can temporarily block blood flow into the pelvis and stabilize hemodynamics. Once the sheath is placed, the procedure time for bilateral IIA embolization is not as significant as in REBOA. The improvement in hemodynamics after this procedure may be temporary, as recanalization can occur following the relief of vasospasm, or in patients with coagulopathy, or due to the opening of a collateral channel. During the maintenance of hemodynamics, selective catheterization and embolization should be attempted (Figure 7).

Bleeding Causes and Embolization Techniques

Atonic Bleeding

Atonic bleeding, defined as bleeding from the placental detachment site due to the failure of uterine myometrial contraction, is the most common cause of PPH. A dilated and tortuous uterine artery is a typical finding of atonic bleeding on angiography (Figure 8).

Atonic bleeding after vaginal delivery. CE-CT demonstrated an enlarged uterus with prominent myometrial vascularization in the anterior wall of the uterine corpus and a dilated vascular structure or extravasation protruding into the uterine cavity (a, arrows). An angiogram from the right uterine artery showed uterine artery dilation during the arterial phase, and contrast agent at the capillary phase (b), but no apparent contrast extravasation. Hemostasis was achieved by gelatin sponge embolization of the bilateral uterine arteries.

CE-CT: contrast-enhanced CT

However, the extravasation of contrast medium is not an essential finding in the decision to perform embolization, as previous reports have demonstrated that not all atonic bleeding shows extravasation in angiography [21, 43]. Embolization from the bilateral uterine artery using a gelatin sponge, prepared using the cutting method, is the basic technique to manage atonic bleeding. The size of the gelatin sponge may be modified according to the size of the uterine artery and coagulation status. The endpoint of embolization is stasis of the contrast material in the main trunk of the uterine artery during angiographic assessment accompanied by hemostasis via vaginal inspection. If bleeding is detected in vaginal inspection after embolization, aortography, pelvic angiography, or selective angiography from the ovarian artery, RLA, and other candidate arteries should be performed to evaluate collateral communication (Figure 9).

Embolization of the bilateral uterine artery with a gelatin sponge failed to stop the bleeding. Angiography of the bilateral ovarian arteries (c) and the RLAs (d) revealed their communication with the uterine arteries (c, d, arrows). Embolization of these arteries with a gelatin sponge terminated the bleeding.

In patients who receive recanalization after embolization using a gelatin sponge, a more robust embolization may be expected using n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA), as it reliably obstructs vessels and provides prompt hemostasis. On the other hand, ongoing bleeding could persist after NBCA embolization because of the steady blood supply from collateral vessels that connect to distal uterine arteries not reached by NBCA and remain patent due to coagulopathy. In such instances, the antegrade administration of additional embolic agents may be complicated by the presence of NBCA located in the uterine artery trunk, which must then be accessed via collateral circulation. Catheterization and embolization from collateral pathways tend to be more challenging, as collateral arteries are generally tortuous, smaller, obscure, and multiple, in addition to being located farther from the bleeding point, such that the probability of the embolic material reaching the bleeding site is reduced. Therefore, in these situations of recanalization after embolization with a gelatin sponge, the focus is on restoring normal blood clotting. In these cases, additional rounds of embolization using gelatin sponges until coagulopathy is resolved and bleeding is under control can be considered.

When local extensive bleeding from the placental detachment site is not resolved using a gelatin sponge, the preferred approach is NBCA embolization. To administer NBCA, a microcatheter needs to be advanced as close as possible to the bleeding point (Figure 10).

Lower uterine segment atony (LUSA) is a distinct entity in which the upper segment of the uterus is well contracted, but the lower segment, a part of the cervix, is distended (Figure 7). Conservative therapy for LUSA is usually difficult, as the lower uterus consists of fewer myoﬁbrils than the upper uterus [44]. CE-CT demonstrates a hematoma-filled distended cervix with extravasation, although the uterine corpus will demonstrate contraction without hematoma. The arterial supply of the cervix derives from the lower segment of the uterine artery or its vaginal branch. Thus, during arterial embolization for LUSA, it is necessary to ensure that the embolic material primarily targets the blood vessels supplying the cervix. At the same time, caution should be taken not to overlook collateral communication from the contralateral cervical artery and vaginal artery, which usually branches off the internal pudendal artery.

Trauma (Genital Tract Laceration, PGHA, Iatrogenic Vessel Injury)

Genital tract laceration frequently occurs in childbirth, and most cases can be treated by suturing. However, when the injury extends into the upper vaginal canal and cervix, arterial embolization may well be indicated, because suturing is hindered by the poor visibility [45]. PGHA includes vulvar, paravaginal, and supravaginal hematoma [46]. Vulvar hematoma is clinically apparent because it is palpable and causes severe pain whereas paravaginal, supravaginal, and supralevator (retroperitoneal, broad ligament) hematomas can be clinically occult because they do not necessarily present as vaginal bleeding or pain. Indeed, in supralevator hematoma, there is discordance between the amount of visible bleeding and the patient's poor general condition, including unstable hemodynamics and progressive anemia [45]. Delayed diagnosis may result in further deterioration. The vaginal artery, the cervicovaginal branch of the uterine artery, the internal pudendal artery, and the external pudendal artery in rare instances are the main bleeding arteries in genital tract laceration and PGHA (Figure 11) [27]. In the case of broad ligament hematoma, the main trunk or the first branch of the uterine artery can be the cause of bleeding (Figure 3). The superior rectal artery may also be a source of bleeding in patients with vaginal laceration and vulvovaginal hematoma (Figure 6) [27, 34, 47]. In such cases, CE-CT may reveal rectal displacement by the neighboring hematoma with extravasation [48]. The mechanism of injury to the superior rectal artery in genital tract laceration and hematoma is assumed to be either direct injury due to a laceration that extends to the abutting vessels or a secondary injury due to a distended genital tract associated with hematoma and edema. A pre-existing anatomic anomaly of communication between the uterine artery and the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) is another possible cause of bleeding [49].

Intrapelvic bleeding after hysterectomy and ligation is associated with the opening of collateral channels, improper ligation of the vessels, clot dislodgement, unexpected vessel injury due to poor surgical visibility, unusual collateralization following previous ligation and embolization, and coagulation disorders. Previous studies have demonstrated that intrapelvic non-uterine arteries, including the cervicovaginal artery, internal pudendal artery, lateral sacral artery, iliolumbar artery, extrapelvic artery, including the ovarian artery, RLA, and IMA, are associated with bleeding after hysterectomy and ligation [50-53] (Figure 12).

When extravasation is clearly demonstrated on angiography in a patient with genital tract laceration and PGHA, it is advisable to perform superselective catheterization, placing the catheter tip at the site of hemorrhage or within the region of extravasation. When angiography reveals the outflow segment of the damaged artery, isolation technique may be attempted. However, due to spasm or temporal thrombosis induced by tissue damage, there may be undetected damaged vessels communicating with the extravasation space and have the potential to be recanalized. Therefore, coil embolization for visible blood vessels poses a risk of rebleeding because of occult damaged vessels. When bleeding is fast and the amount is massive, or the patient's circulatory condition is poor, NBCA embolization is recommended to achieve prompt and reliable hemostasis. Furthermore, NBCA embolization from the rupture point or in the extravasation space may be a reasonable choice for genital tract laceration and PGHA because it can fill the space of injured tissue, thus preventing retrograde blood flow from collateral channels into the space (Figure 11). In this situation, one should perform contralateral angiography, pelvic angiography, and aortography at the level of the ovarian artery to evaluate retrograde communicating arterial blood flow into the injured site. Gelatin sponge is used when the bleeding speed and amount are moderate and there is no evidence of a coagulation disorder. Special attention should be paid to ensuring the recanalization of embolized vessels after gelatin sponge embolization. Repeating angiography after gelatin sponge embolization is mandatory. Proximal embolization carries a risk of continuous bleeding from a collateral pathway because of the rich arterial communication in the pelvis.

Retained Placenta and PAS

Retained placenta, defined as a the persistence of placental tissue in the uterine cavity after vaginal delivery or cesarean section, has been attributed to PAS, atonic uterus, prolonged oxytocin use, and placental under-perfusion [54]. Up to 50% of PAS cases remain undiagnosed until delivery, which may result in retained placenta [55]. CE-CT shows a mass lesion with strong contrast enhancement protruding into the uterine cavity [46, 56]. The management for retained placenta includes surgery, such as Dilatation and Curettage (D&C) and transcervical resection, as well as manual removal of the placenta, but conservative management is also an option [11]. Arterial embolization may be performed prophylactically before surgical or manual removal of the placenta, or on an emergency basis for uncontrolled bleeding due to incomplete separation of the placenta, a forced attempt at placental removal, or coexisting atonic bleeding [57, 58]. Several case series have demonstrated the utility of arterial embolization in the conservative management of a retained placenta, as it resulted in the disappearance of blood flow or the placenta [57]. However, there is a lack of evidence supporting conservative management after arterial embolization for retained placenta. There is also a risk of infection, endometritis, bleeding, and the recurrence of abnormal placentation in a future pregnancy [54, 59].

In the management of antenatally diagnosed PAS, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists and the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics guidelines recommend hysterectomy, performed at a center of excellence, as the standard of care [60, 61]. Otherwise, in patients with PAS and possible bladder involvement, a cesarean section should be scheduled. In these situations, the support of a multidisciplinary team, including an anesthesiologist, a urologist (for placing a ureter stent and separation of the bladder), interventional radiologist (to perform prophylactic arterial balloon occlusion [PABO] and arterial embolization), a neonatologist, obstetric nurses, and staff from the blood transfusion department capable of guiding a massive transfusion protocol is required. PABO mitigates perioperative blood loss by reducing blood flow into the pelvis via arterial occlusion after delivery. In its evaluation in many retrospective studies, inconsistent and conflicting results have been obtained regarding its efficacy [62, 63]. This can be explained by the differences between each patient group, namely the degree of placental invasion and the differences in the location, timing, and duration of PABO. PABO is ideally performed in a hybrid operation suite to prevent dislodgement of the balloon catheter during transfer of the patient from the angiographic lab to the operating room, as dislocation of the balloon catheter can be a factor of failure [21]. Although the most common site of PABO is the IIA, its effects at the CIA and the abdominal aorta are theoretically more promising, because proximal occlusion can block collateral blood flow [64]. However, complications such as vessel injury, clot formation, ischemia, and reperfusion injury following occlusion of the CIA and abdominal aorta may result in a worse critical outcome than that of PABO of the IIA. Moreover, the introduction of larger catheters to occlude the CIA and abdominal aorta carries a higher risk of puncture site complications. Patients who are candidates for PABO, and especially PABO of the abdominal aorta or CIA, should be carefully selected, and the final decision to perform occlusion should be made during laparotomy, based on evidence of parametrial invasion or severe vesicouterine adhesion [65]. In general, the greatest and most definitive benefit of PABO is the availability of additional IR strategies, including REBOA and arterial embolization, in case of sudden bleeding during the operation, as sheath introducers are already placed.

Angiography of a retained placenta and PAS reveals a tortuous uterine artery dilated in varying degrees, with hypervascular staining or the pooling of contrast material indicating residual intervillous space [57]. Extravasation may or may not be observed [57, 66]. The standard procedure of arterial embolization in patients with retained placenta and PAS-related bleeding involves the use of a gelatin sponge in the bilateral uterine arteries. In many countries, particles are also widely utilized in combination with a gelatin sponge, but they have yet to be officially approved in Japan [67, 68]. A large number of gelatin sponge sheets may be needed to stop the blood flow during embolization. Localized embolization targeting the placental attachment site or only the extravasation site may allow bleeding recurrence due to the development of collateral communication within or from outside the uterus, when the placenta is left in situ after embolization. When massive extravasation from the PAS detachment site occurs, which is attributed to rupture of the dilated spiral vessels and sinuses in the myometrium, tissue injury, or extensive neovascularization [68], NBCA embolization may be an alternative following failure of gelatin sponge embolization (Figure 13). The advancement of the microcatheter as close as possible to the extravasation site is needed to prevent proximal embolization during NBCA embolization.

Disseminated Intravascular Coagulopathy

Coagulopathy in PPH often progresses as a combination of consumptive and dilutional coagulopathy. Dilutional coagulopathy refers to a condition where there is a depletion of coagulation factors, resulting from the extensive administration of fluids and red blood cells aimed at compensating for blood loss. In consumption coagulopathy, activated coagulation triggered by the migration of fetal tissue into the maternal blood consumes fibrinogen. AFE is a representative etiology of consumption coagulopathy [69]. The major clinical presentations of AFE include sudden cardiovascular collapse, respiratory distress, and DIC [70]. Atonic bleeding due to AFE typically occurs immediately after delivery in patients with an extremely low fibrinogen level. Uterotonic agents are usually ineffective; instead, the fundamental treatment is hysterectomy to remove the cause of the anaphylactoid reaction [10, 71]. Angiography for AFE may not show extravasation (Figure 4). During arterial embolization for AFE, prompt embolization of the bilateral uterine artery using a gelatin sponge is first attempted. However, clot development may not solely result from the embolization of the uterine artery, as the embolic agents typically fail to reach the distal regions of the uterine artery, where retrograde blood flow from the ovarian artery and the RLA can occur, thereby inhibiting clot formation. The achievement of hemostasis in this situation may require embolization of not only the bilateral uterine arteries but also the ovarian arteries and RLA. The use of NBCA as a primary embolic material is discouraged because of the same issues observed in cases of atonic bleeding.

Rebleeding after Embolization

The causes of rebleeding include recanalization of the embolized arteries due to DIC or following the relief of arterial spasm, a missed bleeding artery, or the development of a collateral artery [30, 32, 72]. The hemodynamic changes after embolization may open the collateral circulation such that it becomes a source of bleeding [73]. In the case of genital tract hematoma, there may be hemodynamic instability and worsening anemia rather than vaginal bleeding. Clinical assessments in these cases often face obstacles due to swelling and pronounced pain, potentially preventing the confirmation of hemostatic status. In patients with suspected rebleeding, CE-CT is recommended to detect the sources of rebleeding.

Complications

Complications are rare. A common minor complication after arterial embolization is post-embolization syndrome, consisting of nausea, transient pain, and fever, but it is usually self-limiting. Major short-term vascular complications are arterial dissection, puncture site aneurysm, hematoma, and arterial or deep venous thrombosis [74]. Delayed complications include uterine and other-organ ischemia [8, 75, 76]. Previous studies raised concerns regarding the small size of embolic material, such as PVA (≤200 μm) and gelatin sponge slurry or gelatin sponge powder as potential contributors to necrosis in the uterus [77-79]. Moreover, the introduction of embolic agents under high pressure rather than free flow injection may cause too far distal embolization, amplifying the risk of ischemia by limiting blood supply from collateral vessels. The combinations of different types of embolic material or different hemostatic methods, such as embolization, uterine compression suture, and ligation, the number of embolized arteries, infection, cocaine abuse, which is vasoconstrictive, older age, and prolonged hypovolemic shock may also contribute to uterine necrosis [75, 80-83]. Accordingly, ischemic complication will be multifactorial, as will vesicle, vaginal, and buttocks necrosis caused by untargeted embolization from the IIA [84].

Intrauterine synechia (Asherman syndrome) is a long-term complication that causes menstrual disorders and infertility [85] (Figure 14). Its occurrence correlates with membrane inflammation resulting from ischemia. Earlier studies showed a higher rate of intrauterine synechia after embolization with PVA and gelatin sponge slurry than with gelatin sponge pledgets [86]. The presence of small particles can obstruct distal vessels, which may restrict retrograde blood flow through collateral channels, resulting in ischemia. Excessive over-embolization should thus be avoided.

Asherman syndrome after arterial embolization for amniotic fluid embolization is associated with dead fetus syndrome. The bilateral uterine arteries, the right ovarian artery, and the RLA were embolized with ten sheets of gelatin sponge. After the procedure, the patient developed amenorrhea. A hysteroscopy revealed adhesions around the cervical region and extensive defects in the mucosal lining. Sagittal T2-weighted image showed a small uterine body, an extensive defect of the mucosal layer, and obliteration of the lumen just above the cervix (arrow). The uterine cavity was dilated due to hematometra (arrowhead). After adhesiolysis under hysteroscopy, the patient’s normal menstrual cycle resumed.

Obstetric and Fertility Outcomes

Studies examining the impact of arterial embolization for PPH on subsequent fertility have shown that arterial embolization does not influence fertility outcomes [84]. The impact of a patient's background and the technical characteristics of arterial embolization on fertility remains poorly understood due to a lack of sufficient data, warranting further examination of the accumulated information [81, 82]. A comprehensive review found a potential increase in the risk of placental abnormalities in subsequent pregnancy [85]. The impact of ovarian artery embolization on ovarian function and fertility is unclear, as it has been examined in only a few studies [20, 87]. Although ovarian artery embolization may lead to a decrease in ovarian function, the absolute priority during embolization for PPH is saving lives and avoiding hysterectomy.

Conclusions

The judicious application of IR for primary PPH represents an effective, reliable, and less invasive method for achieving hemostasis. The incorporation of IR into the management protocol for primary PPH will greatly benefit maternal prognosis. Its effective use requires a thorough understanding of the underlying pathology and appropriate techniques.
