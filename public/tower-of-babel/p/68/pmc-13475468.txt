Prevalence of Cerebrovascular Disease in Patients with Coronary Artery Disease After Pharmacological Stress Test
Authors: Eman M. Abdelfattah, Muhammad S. Muhammad Hagrass, Khalid A. Elkhashab, Sameh E. Sadek, Hassan M. Ebeid, Lamya O.A. Salih, Najah M. Al-Amri, Mostafa S. Mostafa, Haytham S. Ghareeb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesCoronary artery disease (CAD) and cerebrovascular disease (CVD) share common atherosclerotic pathways. However, the clinical significance of headache absence during stress testing remains unexplored. We investigated whether the absence of headache during pharmacological stress testing reflects the presence of subtle cerebrovascular atherosclerosis detectable by transcranial Doppler ultrasonography (TCD).MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled 225 patients with CAD undergoing pharmacological stress testing with TCD and myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) at centers in Egypt (Cardiology Department, Fayoum University). Patients were stratified by headache status during stress testing. Multivariate logistic regression and receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) analysis identified independent predictors of abnormal TCD findings.ResultsThe cohort comprised 123 patients without headache (mean age 59.0 ± 6.8 years) and 102 with headache (mean age 56.5 ± 4.5 years). TCD abnormalities were significantly more prevalent in patients without headache (81.3% vs 27.5%, p < 0.0001). On univariate analysis, age (p < 0.001), hypertension (p = 0.014), and positive MPI (trend, p = 0.191) were associated with abnormal TCD. After adjustment for between-cohort heterogeneity, multivariate analysis confirmed the absence of headache as the strongest independent predictor of TCD abnormality (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 0.09, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.04–0.19; 91% risk reduction; p < 0.001). Age was the only cardiovascular risk factor that retained independent significance (aOR = 1.09 per year; 95% CI: 1.03–1.16; p = 0.005). The full predictive model achieved excellent discrimination (area under the ROC curve (AUC) = 0.833; 95% CI: 0.780–0.884), with sensitivity of 68.0%, specificity of 86.6%, and positive predictive value (PPV) of 87.0%.ConclusionsThis is the first evidence of a paradoxical association in pharmacological stress testing: absence of Headache is strongly associated with advanced subclinical cerebrovascular disease. This finding challenges the conventional interpretation of stress-test symptomatology and suggests new strategies for identifying high-risk CAD patients with silent cerebrovascular atherosclerosis who warrant aggressive preventive therapy. The utility of stress-induced symptom (Headache) profiles for risk stratification warrants prospective validation.

1. Introduction

Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular atherosclerosis constitute a major global health burden, collectively accounting for the majority of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide [1,2]. Coronary artery disease (CAD) and cerebrovascular disease (CVD) are closely intertwined, sharing underlying mechanisms such as atherosclerosis, endothelial dysfunction, and systemic vascular inflammation [3,4]. Epidemiological data demonstrate substantial overlap between coronary and cerebral arterial disease, with patients carrying established CAD at substantially elevated risk of stroke and silent cerebrovascular involvement [5–7]. Pharmacological stress testing with vasodilators is widely used to stratify myocardial risk in CAD [8,9]. Headache is the most commonly reported symptom, attributed to vasodilation of intracranial vessels [10]. However, the clinical significance of headache absence during stress testing remains unexplored. TCD ultrasonography non-invasively assesses cerebral hemodynamics and can detect intracranial atherosclerosis via flow velocity patterns and pulsatility indices [11]. Cerebrovascular reactivity is impaired in atherosclerotic vessels [12], and endothelial dysfunction predicts future cardiovascular events [13,14]. We hypothesized that the absence of headache during pharmacological stress testing (a common side effect) reflects reduced cerebrovascular reactivity attributable to underlying atherosclerotic changes detectable on TCD. This study investigated the prevalence of subclinical CVD in CAD patients using TCD, stratified by headache response during pharmacological stress testing, and identified independent predictors of abnormal TCD findings.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and population

This prospective cross-sectional study enrolled 225 consecutive patients with documented coronary artery disease from the cardiology department who underwent pharmacological stress myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) at nuclear medicine and radiology centers in Egypt (Fayoum University) between March 2023 and September 2024. Patients were enrolled prospectively at the time of pharmacological stress MPI, and TCD was performed as part of the same study protocol. CAD was defined as the presence of at least one of the following: (1) a documented prior diagnosis of coronary artery disease (previous myocardial infarction or prior coronary revascularization by percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass grafting); or (2) objective evidence of stress-induced myocardial ischemia on myocardial perfusion imaging (reversible perfusion defect on stress-rest comparison). Following approval by the Faculty of Medicine, Fayoum University Ethical Committee, the study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Inclusion criteria were: (1) Male patients >40 years and female patients >50 years; (2) confirmed stable CAD diagnosis by coronary angiography or prior myocardial infarction; (3) Patients who are not eligible for treadmill stress test: as chronic pulmonary disease, peripheral vascular disease, musculoskeletal, or mental condition; (4) Baseline ECG abnormalities: LBBB, ventricular pre-excitation (WPW syndrome) and permanent ventricular pacing; (5) All enrolled patients were free of headache. All patients were required to meet criteria 1 and 2; pharmacological (rather than exercise) stress testing was indicated when a patient additionally met criterion 3 or criterion 4. These baseline ECG abnormalities (LBBB, ventricular pre-excitation, permanent ventricular pacing) render the exercise stress ECG uninterpretable — notably, exercise induces false-positive septal perfusion defects in LBBB — and therefore constitute established indications for vasodilator pharmacological stress [15].

Exclusion criteria were: (1) History of cerebrovascular stroke (Stroke or TIA); (2) Previous history of dipyridamole, adenosine, and regadenoson allergy or tracer allergy; (3) Asthmatic patients with ongoing wheezing should not undergo the dipyridamole stress test. However, it has been reported that patients with controlled asthma can undergo the test and can have pre-treatment with two puffs of albuterol or a comparable inhaler; (4) Second- and third-degree AV block without a pacemaker or sick sinus syndrome; (5) Systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg, (6) Acute coronary syndrome (7) History of chronic headache or migraine.

2.2. Data collection and clinical variables

Demographic characteristics, cardiovascular risk factors, and detailed clinical history were systematically collected. Risk factor definitions were as follows: hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive medication); known history of diabetes mellitus, or use of antidiabetic medication; dyslipidemia (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol >3.4 mmol/L or statin therapy); obesity (body mass index ≥30 kg/m2); smoking (current or cessation <1 year) [16]. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was assessed by echocardiography or gated MPI. CAD severity was determined by angiographic extent: single-vessel disease (SVD), two-vessel disease (2VD), or three-vessel disease/left main disease (3VD/LM).

Headache status during or post-pharmacological stress testing was recorded. Patients were questioned at 5-min intervals during stress testing and at 30 min after stress testing regarding the presence of headache. For analysis, patients were dichotomized as follows: (1) without headache (no symptoms reported), and (2) with headache (any headache of any severity).

2.3. Pharmacological stress testing

Stress MPI was performed in accordance with standard institutional protocols [15]. Dipyridamole (0.56–0.84 mg/kg IV over 4 min), adenosine (0.14 mg/kg/min for 6 min), or regadenoson (0.4 mg IV bolus) was administered, followed by radionuclide tracer injection (Tc-99 m sestamibi). Gated SPECT imaging was performed at rest and following stress, per American Society of Nuclear Cardiology guidelines [15]. MPI abnormalities were defined as reversible perfusion defects consistent with inducible ischemia.

2.4. Transcranial Doppler ultrasonography

TCD examination was performed by certified sonographers using a 2 MHz pulsed-wave Doppler (GE Logiq) [17,18]. TCD examinations were performed and interpreted by certified sonographers who were blinded to the patient’s headache status during stress testing. Bilateral middle cerebral arteries (MCA) and anterior cerebral arteries (ACA) were insonated via transtemporal acoustic windows. Peak systolic velocity (PSV), end-diastolic velocity (EDV), and mean flow velocity (MFV) were measured. The pulsatility index (PI) was calculated as (PSV–EDV)/MFV. Abnormal TCD findings were defined as: (1) MFV >150 cm/s (suggestive of stenosis); (2) PI > 1.0 (elevated, suggesting distal resistance); or (3) asymmetry >30% between sides [19]. Bilateral and unilateral lesion patterns were documented.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD); between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test and reported as medians (ranges). Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariate associations between cardiovascular risk factors and abnormal TCD findings were assessed using crude odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Given significant between-cohort heterogeneity in baseline characteristics (headache vs no-headache groups), the headache group was included as a covariate in all multivariate logistic regression models to control for confounding. A full multivariable model including all cardiovascular risk factors was constructed. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to evaluate the discriminatory ability of multiple predictive models; the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals was estimated using 2000-iteration bootstrap resampling. Optimal operating thresholds were determined by Youden’s J statistic (maximum sensitivity + specificity – 1). Pairwise AUC comparisons between nested models were performed using bootstrap tests. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p < 0.05 throughout. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY) for data management and descriptive statistics, and Python 3.12 (scipy 1.x, statsmodels 0.14, scikit-learn 1.x) for inferential analyses, multivariate modeling, and ROC analysis.

3. Results

3.1. Patient characteristics

Of the 225 CAD patients, 123 (54.7%) reported no headache during stress testing, whereas 102 (45.3%) experienced headache symptoms. Baseline characteristics by group are presented in Table 1. Patients without headache were slightly older (59.0 ± 6.8 vs 56.5 ± 4.5 years; p = 0.003) and more frequently male (66.7% vs 51.0%; p = 0.017). The no-headache group had a higher prevalence of hypertension (76.4% vs 55.9%; p = 0.001) and diabetes mellitus (62.6% vs 50.0%; p = 0.057). Notably, the with headache group had a significantly higher prevalence of obesity (48.0% vs 17.9%; p < 0.0001) but the no headache group has better ejection fraction (61.6 ±11.2% vs 58.5 ± 10.3%; p = 0.035).

3.2. MPI and TCD findings

MPI abnormalities were more prevalent in the no-headache group (65.0% vs 45.1%; p = 0.0042). Notably, abnormal TCD findings were significantly more prevalent in patients without headache compared to those with headache (81.3% vs 27.5%; p < 0.0001; Table 2). Among patients with abnormal TCD findings (n = 128), bilateral lesions predominated in the no headache group (69.0% vs 50.0%), whereas unilateral abnormalities were relatively more common in the with-headache group (31.0% vs 50.0%).

3.3. Univariate associations with abnormal TCD findings

Age was significantly associated with TCD abnormality: patients with abnormal TCD were older than those with normal TCD (59.27 ± 6.60 vs 55.97 ± 4.49 years; p < 0.001). A clear gradient was observed (Table 3): abnormal TCD was present in 52.9% of patients ≤50 years, 45.3% aged 51–60 years, 74.7% aged 61–70 years, and 100% of those >70 years (χ2 = 20.556; p < 0.001).

Hypertensive patients had a significantly higher prevalence of abnormal TCD findings compared to non-hypertensive patients (62.9% vs 44.6%; χ2 = 6.069, p = 0.014; Table 4). The crude odds ratio for abnormal TCD in hypertensive patients was 2.11 (95% CI: 1.21–3.68).

Table 5 presents the univariate associations between cardiovascular risk factors and abnormal TCD findings. On unadjusted analysis, family history showed a paradoxical inverse association with abnormal TCD (OR 0.30, p < 0.001). This finding is attributable to confounding by headache group membership and is corrected in the multivariate models below. Hypertension was significantly associated with abnormal TCD (OR 2.11, p = 0.014). Dyslipidemia (p = 0.093), obesity (p = 0.088), and smoking (p = 0.055) showed trends that did not reach statistical significance.

Positive MPI showed a non-significant trend toward higher TCD abnormality (61.1% vs 51.5%; OR = 1.48, 95% CI: 0.87–2.52; p = 0.191).

3.4. Multivariate predictors of abnormal TCD

Multivariate binary logistic regression was performed with abnormal TCD as the dependent variable. Given significant between-cohort heterogeneity in baseline characteristics (headache vs no-headache groups), headache group membership was included as a covariate in all multivariate models to control for confounding. A full model including all cardiovascular risk factors and MPI status was constructed (Table 6).

In the full model (Table 6; n = 225, Pseudo-R2 = 0.265, LLR p < 0.001, AIC = 246.1), headache absence was confirmed as the strongest independent predictor of abnormal TCD (aOR = 0.09, 91% risk reduction; p < 0.001). Age was the only cardiovascular risk factor with independent predictive value (aOR = 1.09 per year, 95% CI: 1.03–1.16; p = 0.005). Hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, obesity, smoking, and positive MPI were not independently associated with abnormal TCD after adjustment.

A sensitivity analysis restricted to variables selected on univariate screening (p < 0.2) plus the headache covariate yielded concordant results. Notably, after adjustment for headache group, the paradoxical univariate association observed for family history (crude OR 0.30 →aOR 1.14, p = 0.763) was resolved, confirming that the univariate inverse association was an artifact of cohort-level confounding rather than a true protective effect.

Receiver-operating characteristic analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminatory ability of multiple predictive models (Table 7; Fig. 1). Headache status alone demonstrated good discrimination (AUC = 0.772; 95% CI: 0.714–0.830), outperforming age alone (AUC = 0.668) and the combination of age plus hypertension (AUC = 0.685). The addition of headache to the age-plus-hypertension model substantially improved discrimination (AUC = 0.830; 95% CI: 0.775–0.880). The full model incorporating all cardiovascular risk factors, MPI status, and headache group achieved the highest AUC of 0.833 (95% CI: 0.780–0.884). However, the full model was not statistically superior to the parsimonious age-plus-hypertension-plus-headache model (ΔAUC = 0.004, p = 0.584), suggesting that headache status, age, and hypertension capture the essential predictive information.

At the optimal threshold determined by Youden’s J statistic, the full model achieved sensitivity of 68.0%, specificity of 86.6%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 87.0%, negative predictive value (NPV) of 67.2%, and overall diagnostic accuracy of 76.0% (Table 7). The high specificity and PPV indicate that a positive prediction from this model reliably identifies patients with true TCD abnormalities, supporting its utility as a screening tool for cerebrovascular disease in CAD patients.

3.5. TCD lesion pattern distribution

Of the 128 patients with abnormal TCD, bilateral lesions predominated in the no-headache group (69.0% vs 50.0%), while unilateral abnormalities were more common in the headache group (50.0% vs 31.0%); this difference did not reach statistical significance (χ2 = 3.46, p = 0.063).

4. Discussion

This is the first report of a paradoxical association between the absence of headache during pharmacological stress testing and subclinical cerebrovascular disease: patients without headache had a markedly higher prevalence of TCD abnormalities, indicating intracranial atherosclerosis. This counterintuitive finding has important implications for patient risk stratification.

4.1. Mechanistic explanation: impaired cerebrovascular reactivity

In healthy individuals, vasodilatory stress agents produce rapid cerebral vasodilatation that mechanically stimulates perivascular pain receptors, causing headache [20]. Headache absence despite pharmacological challenge suggests impaired vasoreactivity in atherosclerotic vessels. This interpretation is consistent with epidemiological links between migraine, a disorder of altered cerebrovascular reactivity, and cardiovascular disease [21].

The aOR of 0.09 (91% risk reduction with headache presence) far exceeds the predictive strength of all established cardiovascular risk factors, suggesting that the response to pharmacological challenges reflects fundamental cerebrovascular pathophysiology rather than being merely a secondary effect.

4.2. Age and hypertension as contributing factors

Age showed a strong, independent association with TCD abnormality (aOR = 1.09 per year; p = 0.005), with a clear gradient from 52.9% in patients ≤50 years to 100% in those >70 years (Table 3) [22–24]. This pattern is consistent with the established decline in cerebrovascular reactivity with advancing age [25]. Despite being slightly older, the no-headache group’s higher TCD burden was not explained by age alone, as multivariate adjustment had minimal impact on the headache effect. Hypertension showed a significant univariate association (OR = 2.11; p = 0.014) but lost independent significance after adjustment (aOR = 1.28; p = 0.490) [], though hypertension-induced vascular remodeling likely contributes to the overall atherosclerotic burden.

4.3. Clinical implications for risk stratification

These findings suggest new strategies for identifying high-risk CAD patients [26,27]. A parsimonious model using headache status, age, and hypertension alone achieved AUC = 0.830, equivalent to the full model (p = 0.584), indicating that three readily available clinical variables capture the essential predictive information. CAD patients without stress-test headache, particularly if older or hypertensive, merit enhanced cerebrovascular evaluation and intensified preventive therapy.

The paradox also highlights a hidden high-risk population: patients who appear asymptomatic yet harbor significant cerebrovascular atherosclerosis [28]. The low prevalence of low TCD abnormality among headache-positive patients (27.5%) supports the interpretation that headache reflects preserved cerebrovascular reactivity.

4.4. Limitations

Several limitations of this study merit acknowledgment. First, as an inherent constraint of the cross-sectional design, causal directionality cannot be established; longitudinal studies with hard cerebrovascular endpoints are required to confirm that headache absence during stress testing independently predicts future cerebrovascular events [29]. Second, TCD has recognized limitations in sensitivity and specificity for stenosis detection and cannot visualize extracranial disease; advanced imaging (CT angiography, MR angiography, carotid ultrasound) would enhance the assessment [30]. Third, the study was conducted at a specialized cardiac center; generalizability to primary care or diverse populations may be limited. Fourth, referrals of cardiac patients to the nuclear medicine department decreased (physicians preferred coronary CT angiography or even direct coronary angiography). Fifth, the availability of the TCD operator is limited (mainly a radiologist). Finally, the TCD study is operator-dependent and requires continuous training. Finally, medication use (antiplatelet agents, statins) was not systematically analyzed and may influence headache perception; prospective studies with systematic symptom phenotyping are warranted.

5. Conclusions and future directions

We describe a novel paradoxical association in pharmacological stress testing: absence of headache is strongly associated with advanced subclinical cerebrovascular disease detected by TCD [15]. This finding challenges the conventional interpretation of stress-test symptomatology. Prospective validation in large cohorts with long-term event follow-up is required. If confirmed, headache status during and after stress testing could serve as a rapid, zero-cost screening tool to identify CAD patients who warrant intensive cerebrovascular evaluation and prevention.
