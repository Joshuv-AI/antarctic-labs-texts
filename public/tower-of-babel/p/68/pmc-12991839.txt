Self-management of reflux symptoms after esophagectomy in China: A qualitative study
Authors: Wei Zhang, Jing Wang, Huixia Li, Yuqing Zhao, Xiaotong Yang, Quanbo Huo, Yaxin Fu, Ling Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis qualitative study aimed to explore the self-management experiences, expectations, and needs of Chinese patients experiencing reflux symptoms after esophagectomy, guided by the Integrated Theory of Health Behavior Change (ITHBC).MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with 18 patients who had undergone esophagectomy, selected via maximum variation sampling. Data were analyzed using directed content analysis based on the ITHBC framework, focusing on three domains: knowledge and beliefs, self-regulation skills, and social facilitation.ResultsOur analysis identified three core themes reflecting significant gaps in current supportive care: the need for actionable knowledge, as patients struggled with complex medical information and desired simplified explanations, standardized symptom assessment, and long-term risk management guidance; the need for personalized self-regulation skills, with difficulties reported in implementing dietary adjustments, posture management, and self-monitoring due to a lack of tailored guidance; and the need for continuous and collaborative support, as existing support from health care providers, family, and peers was often fragmented or insufficient. These findings underscore a systemic misalignment between patient needs and clinical support, highlighting an urgent demand for more integrated, practical, and accessible self-management resources.ConclusionsSignificant gaps exist between patient needs and current supportive care practices. There is an urgent need for tailored interventions, including metaphor-based education, structured skill training, enhanced social support, and digitally accessible micro-learning resources, to improve self-management outcomes in this population.Keywords: Esophageal cancer, Post-esophagectomy, Gastroesophageal reflux, Self-management, Qualitative research, Patient expectations

Introduction

Esophageal cancer poses a significant challenge to global health, accounting for the seventh highest rate of cancer-associated deaths worldwide.1 As a primary curative treatment, surgical resection necessitates digestive tract reconstruction, which often leads to long-term postoperative complications.2 Among these, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is particularly prevalent, affecting up to 80% of esophagectomy patients and significantly impairing their health-related quality of life for years.3,4 Although this is a global issue, the disease burden is particularly high in Asia. China alone accounts for nearly half of the global incidence and mortality annually,5necessitating focused research on this key population.

The effectiveness of reflux management following esophagectomy hinges on the patient’s self-management capabilities, encompassing symptom recognition, lifestyle modifications, and adherence to behavioral recommendations.6,7 The chronic nature of GERD also imposes a substantial and ongoing economic burden on patients and health care systems, including costs related to long-term medication, repeated consultations, and productivity losses, which underscores the urgency of developing effective and sustainable management strategies.8 Evidence suggests that enhanced self-management is a cornerstone of successful interventions for chronic diseases,8 current supportive care strategies in China predominantly rely on standardized patient education delivered during hospitalization. This one-size-fits-all approach often fails to address individualized needs, personal preferences, and the complex challenges of the post-discharge environment, as highlighted in prior studies on post-surgical patient education.9,10 Although quantitative surveys indicate suboptimal self-management levels in this population,11 they fall short of elucidating the underlying reasons—the nuanced cognitive perceptions, practical difficulties, and contextual barriers that patients face daily. This gap underscores an urgent need for an in-depth, qualitative exploration of the patient experience to inform truly effective support strategies.

A theory-guided approach is valuable for systematically understanding the complexities of health behavior change. The Integrated Theory of Health Behavior Change (ITHBC) provides a robust, patient-centered framework for this purpose.12 The ITHBC integrates concepts from self-regulation and social cognitive theories, positing that three interdependent components—knowledge and beliefs, self-regulation skills and abilities, and social facilitation—are fundamental for successful behavior change.13 This framework has been successfully applied in chronic conditions like COPD and cardiac rehabilitation,14,15 However, its application to understanding the self-management journey of esophagectomy patients remains virtually unexplored. Employing the ITHBC offers a unique lens to move beyond mere description of problems; it allows for a structured analysis of how patients’ understanding (knowledge), their day-to-day competencies (self-regulation), and their support systems (social facilitation) interact to either facilitate or hinder effective reflux symptom management.

Therefore, guided by the ITHBC,12 this qualitative study aims to systematically investigate the self-management experiences, expectations, and needs of esophagectomy patients in China concerning reflux symptoms. Specifically, the study is designed to explore:

(1)Knowledge and Beliefs: Patients’ understanding of the mechanisms of reflux, their perceptions of its long-term impact, and any misconceptions causing anxiety.

Knowledge and Beliefs: Patients’ understanding of the mechanisms of reflux, their perceptions of its long-term impact, and any misconceptions causing anxiety.

(2)Self-regulation Skills and Abilities: The specific skills patients employ (or lack) in daily symptom management, such as diet adjustment, posture management, and self-monitoring, as well as the challenges they face in implementing these skills.

(3)Social and Environmental Facilitation: The perceived support or barriers from health care providers, family, peers, and the broader health care environment that influence their self-management practices.

The findings are expected to generate a theoretically grounded evidence base that is essential for developing tailored, context-sensitive interventions to improve self-management support and clinical outcomes for this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a theory-informed qualitative descriptive design to explore patients’ self-management experiences, expectations, and needs.16,17 While the interview guide was structured around the core constructs of the ITHBC, the primary aim was descriptive and exploratory rather than theoretical refinement or development. The ITHBC served as a sensitizing framework to organize data collection and analysis, ensuring a structured yet open exploration of the cognitive, behavioral, and social dimensions of self-management.12 This approach allowed us to remain grounded in participants’ narratives while systematically mapping their experiences onto a relevant theoretical model.

An exploratory study using a qualitative descriptive design, informed by the ITHBC, was employed. This design facilitated an in-depth exploration of patients’ lived experiences while providing a structured, theory-based lens to examine the cognitive, behavioral, and social-contextual dimensions of self-management.

This study employed a purposive sampling strategy guided by the principle of maximum variation to ensure the inclusion of a diverse range of experiences related to reflux self-management.18 To operationalize this, we first identified four key characteristics anticipated to influence the experience of self-management based on the study aims and existing literature: age, gender, educational level, and duration of reflux symptoms. A recruitment matrix was established to track these dimensions. From the pool of patients who voluntarily responded to the study advertisement and met the basic inclusion criteria, we iteratively selected participants. Selection was actively guided by the need to fill gaps in this matrix, thereby prioritizing individuals whose characteristics would broaden the sample’s diversity across the predefined dimensions (e.g., seeking a younger female participant with lower education or a participant with long symptom duration). The iterative process of recruitment and parallel data analysis continues until the subject data is saturated. This approach aimed not for statistical representativeness but to capture a wide spectrum of perspectives pertinent to the phenomenon under study. Patients who underwent esophagectomy in the Department of Esophageal Oncology, Tianjin Medical University Cancer Hospital from March 2025 to May 2025 were identified as potential participants.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathologically confirmed esophageal carcinoma treated with curative-intent esophagectomy; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) self-reported experience of gastroesophageal reflux symptoms at the time of the study, regardless of whether the onset pre-dated or post-dated the esophagectomy; (4) ability to read, write, and complete questionnaires independently; and (5) provision of informed consent to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were: (1) history of other advanced cancers or digestive system tumors; (2) diagnosis of severe cognitive impairment or psychiatric disorders; and (3) use of anti-anxiety or antidepressant medications within the past six months. This study adheres to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines.13

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face, semi-structured interviews, which were designed to create a supportive environment for participants to openly share their perspectives. The semi-structured interview guide was systematically developed based on the three core constructs of the ITHBC: knowledge and beliefs, self-regulation skills and abilities, and social facilitation. To ensure theoretical consistency, each question was mapped to one or more ITHBC constructs, facilitating a focused exploration of the mechanisms underlying self-management behavior. This theoretical foundation guided the formulation of interview questions to ensure alignment with the study’s objectives. The guide was further refined through a review of relevant literature and research team discussions. Its purpose was to explore patient experiences across three key dimensions: knowledge and beliefs, self-regulation skills, and social and environmental facilitators. The guide was pilot-tested with two participants and subsequently refined to enhance the clarity and flow of the questions. The final interview guide, revised based on expert consultations and results from these pilot interviews (which were not included in the formal analysis), is summarized in Table 1.

The interviews were conducted by a researcher with over a decade of experience in qualitative interviewing. Prior approval was obtained from the nursing manager of the participating hospital. A study advertisement, displaying the researcher’s name and contact information, was posted in the Esophageal Tumor Ward. Potentially eligible patients who expressed interest were instructed to contact the researcher voluntarily via telephone or WeChat.

Those who met the preliminary inclusion criteria were provided with a detailed information sheet outlining the study’s purpose and procedures. The consent process was conducted in two stages. First, verbal consent was obtained after the initial contact and discussion of the study. Then, prior to the commencement of each interview, each participant provided written informed consent in a private room. The sheet emphasized that participation was entirely voluntary and that participants could withdraw at any time without providing a reason. Anonymity and the confidentiality of personal information were assured. Prior to the interview, each participant provided written informed consent.

Interviews were conducted in private rooms chosen by the participants and were audio-recorded in Chinese. During the interviews, participants were encouraged to share their personal experiences and provide additional comments on the topic. The interviews had an average duration of 45 minutes (range: 30–70 minutes). Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved, following the iterative process described in the Data analysis section.

Data analysis

The determination of data saturation was integral to the analysis process and guided recruitment. Data saturation was defined as the point at which additional interviews yielded no new thematic insights or information relevant to the research questions. The analytical process, which began concurrently with data collection, adhered to several key steps. Following data collection, the interviews were transcribed for analysis. Initially, two researchers (W. Zhang and J. Wang) immersed themselves in the data by listening to the audio recordings to gain a preliminary understanding of the context. The interview transcripts were then thoroughly and repeatedly read, followed by a process of line-by-line coding. The analysis was conducted by two researchers (W. Zhang and J. Wang) using a directed content analysis approach,18 which is particularly suitable for theory-based qualitative research. To ensure methodological coherence with the ITHBC framework, data were analyzed using directed content analysis.19 The coding and categorization were explicitly and deductively guided by the three core domains of the ITHBC:12 knowledge and beliefs, self-regulation skills and abilities, and social facilitation, this approach ensures that the analysis is still based on the theoretical structure of the survey.

A rigorous procedure was implemented to ensure the equivalency and integrity of meaning between the original Chinese data and the English-language manuscript. The analysis, including initial coding and theme development, was conducted on the original Chinese transcripts to preserve the nuanced meanings and cultural context of participants’ narratives. When selecting quotations for the manuscript, the original Chinese excerpts were first carefully chosen for their representativeness and relevance to the themes. These quotations were then translated into English by a bilingual member of the research team with high proficiency in both languages. To ensure accuracy and conceptual equivalence, the translated quotes were subsequently reviewed and verified by another bilingual researcher. This process aimed to faithfully convey the participants’ intended meanings, emotional tone, and contextual expressions while making them accessible to an international readership.

The analytical process adhered to several key steps: (1) repeated and immersive reading of all transcriptions to achieve familiarity with the data; (2) utilization of the interview textual data as the primary unit of analysis; (3) development of a preliminary thematic framework based on the ITHBC theory12 and relevant literature, which structured the analysis around the dimensions of knowledge and beliefs, self-regulation skills, social support, and pathways to enhancing self-management of reflux symptoms; (4) meticulous analysis of the text to sort relevant data into the predefined themes, which, through an iterative process of review, were subsequently elaborated into specific sub-themes; and (5) establishment of coherent connections between the textual materials, themes, and sub-themes.

The research team convened regular meetings to critically review and refine the emerging thematic structure. The categories and sub-themes were precisely defined and iteratively polished to ensure they accurately captured the essence of each interview and represented specific aspects of the collective data. To enhance the trustworthiness and validity of the findings, a member-checking procedure was implemented. A summary of the identified themes and sub-themes relevant to their experiences, accompanied by direct quotations from their own interviews, was returned to each participant. This allowed them to verify and comment on whether the analysis accurately reflected their personal experiences and perspectives. The entire process of data collation and directed content analysis was conducted under the supervision of a professor (L. Yang) with substantial expertise in qualitative research methodologies.

Methodological rigor

To ensure methodological rigor in this study, we adhered to the following criteria established for qualitative research: credibility, dependability, and transferability.20,21 To address credibility, member checking was employed by providing participants with the opportunity to review and validate the identified themes and sub-themes. Any ambiguities or discrepancies concerning these themes were thoroughly discussed and resolved through collaborative efforts within the research team. Furthermore, participants were recruited from multiple clinical departments to ensure a diversity of experiences.21

Dependability was ensured through several strategies. The researchers engaged in regular peer debriefing sessions to discuss and scrutinize their insights, interviewing techniques, and analytical approaches throughout the research process. Additionally, all researchers involved in data collection and analysis had received rigorous training in qualitative methodologies, equipping them with the fundamental skills and knowledge to conduct the study reliably and ensure the validity of the findings.21 To enhance transferability, a thick description of the study context, participant selection criteria, participant characteristics, data collection and analysis procedures, and the findings is provided. These comprehensive details allow readers to thoroughly evaluate the applicability and relevance of the study’s results to their own specific contexts.21

Results

Participant and interview characteristics

A total of 42 patients expressed interest in the study and were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 24 were excluded for the following reasons: 15 did not meet the inclusion criteria (8 due to the absence of significant reflux symptoms, 5 due to a history of other malignancies, and 2 due to cognitive impairment), and 9 declined to participate, primarily citing personal time constraints or a lack of interest after reviewing the study details. Therefore, 18 participants were recruited and interviewed, and the specific recruitment process is shown in Fig. 1.

Recruitment process flow chart.

Data saturation was reached after 16 patient interviews. Two additional participants were subsequently interviewed to confirm that no new themes emerged, at which point recruitment concluded. Consequently, a total of 18 patients participated in this study. The cohort included 12 males and 6 females, with ages ranging from 48 to 77 years (mean = 63 years). The duration of reflux symptoms varied from 1 to 24 months, with a mean duration of 7 months. This variation indicates that some symptoms have preceded surgery. During the interviews, the focus was explicitly placed on their experiences, self-management strategies, and support needs in the post-surgical period. The analysis confirmed that the themes related to knowledge, self-regulation, and social support were pertinent to their current lived experience of managing reflux after esophagectomy, irrespective of the symptom’s original onset. The ITHBC framework guided us to explore how patients understand, regulate, and seek support for symptoms within their altered post-surgical reality, making the findings directly relevant to clinical support for this population. Regarding educational background, 33% of the participants had received a primary school education. In addition, 83% were married, and 56.2% resided in rural areas. Theme 1: need for accessible knowledge about reflux

Struggles with complex medical terminology

Patients who have undergone esophagectomy generally possess a limited understanding of the mechanisms underlying reflux, primarily due to the reliance of existing nursing manuals on anatomical diagrams and specialized terminology. These materials often lack intuitiveness and fail to translate complex medical concepts into relatable, everyday language. Consequently, patients struggle to connect abstract medical information with their specific symptoms, leading to confusion and misinterpretation. There is a clear preference among patients for straightforward, simple explanations.

“The nurse gave me a handbook on reflux care. It had anatomical drawings of the esophagus and stomach, with labels like impaired anti-reflux barrier. After reading it, I still didn’t understand.” (P1)

“I had hoped to learn about self-managing reflux from the booklet, but in reality, the content was too general.” (P6)

Beyond the need for visually intuitive representations of the mechanisms, patients also expressed a practical need for simplified explanations to alleviate cognitive anxiety. Some patients, unable to comprehend medical terms such as “functional reflux,” tend to mistakenly associate postoperative reflux with negative perceptions like “poor surgical recovery” or “disease progression”. There is a critical need for health care providers to use real-life analogies to dispel these misconceptions and reduce psychological distress.

“Being told about an ‘impaired anti-reflux barrier’ at discharge just made me more anxious—was it a faulty valve? Did the surgery not hold? I even ate less because of it. A simple comparison, like calling it a ‘leaky lid,’ would have calmed my fears about the surgery failing.” (P13)

Desire for visual and interactive symptom guides

Patients demonstrated considerable subjectivity and ambiguity in identifying and assessing their reflux symptoms, a process often hindered by fragmented information and the lack of intuitive, practical aids. This gap was not merely about information scarcity, but about the format and applicability of knowledge in real-time, daily decision-making. The core need here was for immediate, actionable tools that could transform abstract descriptions into clear, personal judgments.

“I used to mistake the heartburn from reflux for a regular stomachache … If we had standardized guidance—for example, teaching us how to identify reflux based on the location of the symptom, how it feels, and its patterns—we could make a preliminary judgment ourselves and avoid delays.” (P1)

This need for clarity extended to the preferred medium of learning. Patients explicitly favored visual, interactive, and easily retrievable formats over static text or verbal instructions, highlighting a desire for knowledge that is embedded in engaging and memorable experiences.

“I used to mistake heartburn for a stomachache and took wrong medicine. A video or short course on symptom recognition—maybe with a quick self-test—would have helped a lot.” (P8)

Furthermore, the desire was not only for educational content but for tools that seamlessly integrated into daily routines as practical references, reducing cognitive load and reliance on memory.

“I often struggle with questions like when to reintroduce certain foods or schedule follow-ups. Short videos with printable checklists would help—I could post them on my fridge instead of relying on memory.” (P12)

Uncertainty regarding long-term recovery and risk

Distinct from the immediate need for symptom identification tools, patients expressed a profound and forward-looking need for structured, timeline-based guidance to navigate the uncertainties of long-term recovery. This theme centers on the future-oriented knowledge gap—the lack of a clear roadmap outlining how reflux risks, dietary freedoms, and self-care priorities evolve across different post-surgical stages. The anxiety here stemmed not from identifying present symptoms, but from not knowing what to expect and how to plan for the months and years ahead.

A significant source of uncertainty involved the sustainability of restrictions and the prevention of future complications. Patients sought proactive, phase-specific information that would empower them to look forward with confidence rather than fear.

“I’m afraid to eat many foods now. I need to know: can my diet be eased in a year or two? And how do I prevent long-term damage—are there specific check-ups or habits? A clear plan would give me hope.” (P11)

Beyond practical management, the absence of a clear long-term prognosis and recovery trajectory significantly impacted psychological well-being and adherence. The need was for evidence-based narratives and examples that could provide hope and a framework for sustained self-management.

“I lie awake worrying that this reflux is forever. It would make all the difference if the doctor could give me a timeline—when does it usually get better? And hearing about patients who managed it well long-term would give me the confidence to keep going.” (P13)

Theme 2: need for practical self-management skills

Lack of personalized dietary protocols

Although informed of basic principles like “eating small, frequent meals”, patients reported a lack of the specific skills needed for personalized implementation. Due to the absence of individualized criteria for assessing food tolerance and clear quantitative guidance, patients often faced a “trial-and-error dilemma”. Furthermore, the lack of convenient digital tools for dietary planning resulted in insufficient precision. The core need was for personalized skill-building in food selection and meal planning.

“I eat small meals as advised, but still get reflux—like with a roll but not a steamed egg. I can’t figure out why, and wish the doctor would just tell me what to eat and avoid.” (P5)

Beyond immediate confusion regarding food selection, patients also exhibit a more pronounced need for phased and detailed dietary management when balancing postoperative nutritional supplementation with reflux prevention. Existing guidance does not cover dietary adaptation standards for different recovery stages or details of food preparation (such as cutting specifications and cooking duration), making it difficult for patients to ensure safety while attempting to supplement nutrition.22

“I’ve lost too much weight and need to eat well, but I’m scared of causing reflux. I don’t just need general advice; I need a specific week-by-week meal plan that tells me exactly what to eat and how to prepare it safely, so I can regain strength without fear.” (P17)

Vague instructions for posture and emotional control

A prominent need among most patients post-esophagectomy involves the standardization of physical posture management. Taking sleep posture adjustment as an example, current guidance predominantly relies on vague instructions such as “elevate the head of the bed” lacking concrete, actionable execution standards. Patients have no reference for practically measuring height values and possess insufficient understanding of suitable pillow types, stacking methods, or angle control (e.g., the angle between the upper body and the bed surface). This often leads to discomfort or ineffective reflux prevention due to improper adjustment in practice.

“The nurse said to elevate the head of the bed, but one pillow isn’t enough and two hurts my neck. How high is correct? Others say 15 or 20 cm, but I can’t measure it myself.” (P2)

Beyond physical posture, patients also lack specific standardized guidance for behavioral interventions related to emotion-induced reflux. Existing advice mostly remains at the abstract level of “managing emotions”, without clear definitions for immediate coping behaviors during emotional arousal (such as deep breathing, posture changes) and their operational details, or for daily behavioral norms to prevent emotion-related reflux (such as duration of relaxation activities, limits on social interaction). This makes it difficult for patients to translate abstract advice into concrete action.

“After a family quarrel left me with reflux for two days, I realized how much my emotions trigger it. Telling me to ‘control my emotions’ is pointless. I need concrete behavioral steps—like ‘listen to music for 20 minutes when upset’—that I can actually follow.” (P16)

Tools for symptom tracking and professional feedback

This sub-theme captures the need for tools that close the loop between daily symptom tracking and actionable professional insight. The challenge was not merely recording symptoms but transforming isolated data points into a coherent narrative understandable to both the patient and the clinician. Existing methods were often flawed, leading to inaccurate or disorganized data that hampered effective self-assessment and subsequent clinical consultation. The expressed need centered on tools that are user-adaptive, enabling consistent and accurate data capture, especially for those with physical or technological constraints.

“I use a calendar to track my reflux—I draw a circle every time I have heartburn. But after a while, I can’t tell if it was acid reflux in the morning or heartburn at night … I’d really like a tool with large buttons designed for seniors, something I can see clearly despite my poor eyesight.” (P4)

Beyond basic recording needs, patients further expressed a desire for seamless data integration and remote care coordination. Patients envisioned tools that could bridge the geographical and temporal gap between home and clinic, enabling asynchronous review of their condition by health care providers. This desire highlights a need for a collaborative care model where self-monitoring directly informs timely, personalized professional guidance without the necessity of frequent in-person visits.

“Living far from the hospital, I cannot discuss my daily symptom logs with my doctor in a timely manner. I wish for a tool that could sync my records directly to the doctor’s system and allow for weekly feedback—enabling professional guidance without frequent hospital visits.” (P15)

Seeking authoritative digital skill demonstrations

Distinct from the need for monitoring tools, this sub-theme addresses the foundational acquisition and application of knowledge required for effective self-management. Patients faced a dual challenge: generic advice was insufficient for daily implementation, while publicly available digital information was often overwhelming and of questionable credibility. The core need here is for trusted, professional-led educational content that provides concrete, visually clear, and context-specific instructions on how to perform self-management skills, thereby building competence and confidence.

“I truly hope for videos or courses created by healthcare professionals to understand reflux symptoms, learn what to eat after surgery, and how to prevent it. The internet is flooded with information, but it’s hard to know what’s accurate.” (P7)

This expressed preference underscores a critical gap in accessible, standardized skill transmission. Patients sought not just information, but demonstration and guided learning from authoritative sources. The desired resources—such as instructional videos—would serve to translate abstract principles into tangible actions, effectively equipping patients with the practical “how-to” knowledge essential for daily symptom control and long-term adaptation.

Theme 3: need for continuous and collaborative support

Discontinuity of professional support after discharge

For most patients who have undergone esophagectomy, the effectiveness of health guidance provided at discharge is often diminished due to limitations in immediate information retention (e.g., memory fading, content forgetting). Furthermore, the lack of a designated, personalized point of contact after discharge leaves patients struggling to access targeted guidance when encountering reflux-related issues during home recovery. This creates a support gap immediately after discharge, highlighting a pronounced need for regular, dedicated follow-up guidance.

“I quickly forgot the post-discharge guidelines and was left guessing when issues arose. It shouldn’t be like this. A weekly check-in call from a nurse who knows my case and gives specific guidance would make all the difference.” (P6)

Beyond the need for follow-up during the initial post-discharge period, patients also exhibit significant needs for professional support during the medium to long-term recovery phase (e.g., between scheduled follow-up visits).23 The current follow-up model primarily relies on patients proactively returning for appointments and lacks proactive check-in reminders from doctors or nurses, as well as accessible channels for seeking help. Consequently, when new reflux symptoms arise between follow-up visits, patients often face confusion with nowhere to turn.

“After my 3-month check-up, I was told to return in six months. But by the fifth month, new reflux symptoms appeared. I had no way to ask if this was normal—the clinic phone was always busy, and there was nowhere to leave a message.” (P18)

Family care based on tradition versus medical advice

Most family caregivers of patients post-esophagectomy rely primarily on personal experience rather than professional guidance when providing care. Often lacking scientific understanding of dietary principles for reflux prevention—such as appropriate food selection and cooking methods—caregivers tend to adopt traditional, experience-based care models focused on “boosting nutrition”. These approaches, however, frequently conflict with patients’ actual needs for reflux control, potentially aggravating reflux symptoms and even leading to conflicts between patients and family members regarding care strategies.

“My husband insists on traditional ‘nutrition-boosting’ foods that trigger my reflux, citing his own recovery experience. This conflict highlights the need for structured family education on evidence-based dietary management post-surgery.” (P8)

Peer support groups lacking professional oversight

Clinical observations indicate that while post-esophagectomy patients have formed peer-initiated online support groups for basic experience sharing and emotional connection, the content shared within these groups often consists of non-standardized individual experiences. Without screening, verification, or guidance from health care professionals, such information may be biased or lack practical applicability, thus failing to fully meet patients’ core need for peer support that combines emotional resonance with professional reliability.

“I wish healthcare providers could organize regular online discussion sessions for us. We do have a patient chat group, but everyone is just sharing based on their own personal experiences. Alternatively, it would be helpful if some kind of course could be developed that we could watch repeatedly.” (P5)

Balancing work, finances, and symptom management

The challenges of managing reflux symptoms profoundly impacted patients’ professional lives, financial stability, and overall quality of life, highlighting the need for integrated socioeconomic support. Many patients expressed a strong desire to return to work, believing that maintaining a relatively normal life rhythm could aid disease management. However, reflux symptoms and their management requirements often conflicted with this goal.

“I’m not even 50 yet, I still need to earn money. Everything in the family costs money, and furthermore, having this illness itself is expensive. I want to know to what extent I can recover in the future? How long until I can start working again?” (P10)

“Regarding work, I’m only in my 40s. Treating this illness has cost so much, and I need to support my family. Furthermore, the nature of my job involves drinking alcohol. How am I supposed to manage my reflux under these circumstances?” (P2)

Concurrently, patients desired support tools that could coordinate disease management with daily life, including work. They hoped not only to conveniently record symptoms but also to receive timely professional feedback through digital platforms, thereby balancing health needs with work and life more effectively.

“I hope for a platform where I can log my daily reflux symptoms and have a dedicated nurse provide me with professional responses after reviewing them.” (P3) This expectation for integrated, interactive support tools reflects patients’ urgent need for efficient and accessible professional guidance when navigating the multiple pressures of health, work, and finances.

These complex interactions among symptom management, occupational function, economic burden, and support systems collectively constitute key environmental factors influencing patients’ self-management efficacy and quality of life.

Discussion

Main findings

Grounded in the ITHBC,12 this qualitative study elucidates the interconnected struggles faced by Chinese patients managing reflux symptoms after esophagectomy. Our findings reveal that deficits in actionable knowledge hinder the development of self-regulation skills, a cycle that is further exacerbated by fragmented social support. This dynamic, influenced by individual factors such as educational level and rural residence, creates a persistent barrier to effective self-management.

A core finding is the critical gap between the complex medical information provided to patients and the practical knowledge they need for daily decision-making. This aligns with global evidence on health literacy barriers among cancer survivors.20 Patients across all educational levels struggled to translate abstract concepts—such as an “impaired anti-reflux barrier”—into their daily experience.24 This lack of conceptual understanding undermines the formation of accurate illness beliefs and self-efficacy, which are core elements of the ITHBC,12,25 thereby sustaining a well-documented knowledge-behavior gap.26,27

This gap is particularly pronounced in diverse populations. Contrasting with studies from Japan28 and South Korea29 that reported benefits from standardized education in more homogeneous, urban populations, our findings underscore its limitations in a heterogeneous Chinese cohort. Over half of our participants were rural residents, one-third had only primary education, and many had mid-to-late stage disease. For them, standardized, hospital-delivered education failed to accommodate varying cognitive levels, the realities of family care, and the need for long-term guidance. This highlights that the effectiveness of any intervention is highly dependent on the population’s socioeconomic and clinical profile.

The acquisition of self-regulation skills is essential for effective reflux management,30 yet our participants reported profound difficulties in implementing generic advice. Within the ITHBC, self-regulation is the process through which patients apply learned skills to daily life.31 Our findings critically extend this concept by showing that self-regulation capacity is heavily constrained by personal and environmental context. Patients found advice like “elevate the head of the bed” unworkable without concrete, measurable standards—especially those with limited resources or education to experiment safely. Context significantly influenced skill development: rural residents and physical laborers faced greater challenges in standardizing posture, while those with lower health literacy needed more personalized dietary guidance. A key barrier was the bidirectional relationship between symptom burden and psychological distress,32 creating a vicious cycle that depletes the cognitive and emotional resources necessary for self-regulation.33 These interconnected gaps highlight the need for integrated, patient-centered interventions that address deficits in knowledge, self-regulation skills, and social support simultaneously.34,35

Social support is a critical determinant of self-management, but its value lies in quality, scientific validity, and continuity.36 Our study identified significant shortcomings in this domain: professional follow-up was fragmented; family caregiving was often rooted in traditional “replenishing nutrition” beliefs rather than evidence-based guidance; and peer support groups lacked professional moderation. These fragmented inputs fail to create a facilitative environment for sustained behavior change. Chinese patients’ support needs exhibit distinct regional characteristics compared to other high-incidence Asian countries.28,29 The high proportion of rural residents (56.2%) highlights disparities in health care access. Family caregivers typically lack systematic training, and cultural anxiety about cancer recurrence is pronounced, with many patients mistakenly linking reflux to disease progression. Unlike settings with established psychological support or caregiver training programs,37, 38, 39 Chinese patients’ needs are often overshadowed by these pragmatic and socio-cultural dilemmas.

To systematically address these gaps, we propose developing structured micro-learning interventions grounded in the ITHBC.40,41 Current resources like short videos are often fragmented and lack authority.37 Culturally adapted micro-learning courses should be designed for diverse educational backgrounds,41,42 delivering metaphor-based knowledge, demonstrating self-regulation skills via video, and facilitating access to continuous, moderated support—thereby addressing all three ITHBC constructs simultaneously. For Chinese patients, interventions require regional adaptations: (1) Stratified tools for urban-rural differences (printed manuals + community follow-up vs. digital apps + online consultation);39,42 (2) Integration of family caregivers via “caregiver workshops”;36,43 (3) Phased risk communication plans to address recurrence anxiety, aligning expectations with clinical evidence on reflux natural history.25,41 Ultimately, applying the ITHBC framework reveals that post-esophagectomy self-management challenges stem from interconnected systemic failures in knowledge, skill, and support. Effective intervention must be equally integrated, providing actionable knowledge, personalized skill training, and continuous, quality-controlled support that extends into patients’ socio-economic environments.

Patients reported three overarching unmet needs. First, they lacked accessible knowledge: they struggled to comprehend complex medical terminology, desired visual and interactive symptom guides, and faced uncertainty about long-term recovery trajectories. Second, they lacked practical self-regulation skills: generic advice on diet, posture, and emotional control was difficult to implement without personalized, measurable instructions, and they had no tools for systematic symptom tracking or access to credible skill demonstrations. Third, they lacked continuous and collaborative support: professional follow-up was fragmented, family caregiving often conflicted with medical advice, peer support groups operated without professional oversight, and socioeconomic pressures related to work and finances compounded self-management challenges.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a qualitative study relying on semi-structured interviews, data are subject to recall and social desirability biases. Individual differences in cognitive ability and expressive skills may also have influenced the depth and accuracy of the information shared. Second, the varied duration of symptoms (1–24 months) indicates that some participants experienced reflux prior to surgery, while others developed it de novo postoperatively. Although our analysis confirmed that the identified themes were consistently raised across participants regardless of symptom duration, future longitudinal studies that precisely track symptom onset from the time of surgery are needed to distinguish any potential differences in experience between patients with pre-existing versus new-onset postoperative reflux. Third, participants were recruited via purposive sampling from a single tertiary hospital in Northern China. Although maximum variation sampling was employed, regional, educational, and health care contexts may limit transferability to other settings or cultural backgrounds. Multi-center studies involving more diverse populations are recommended to enhance generalizability.

Conclusions

This qualitative study, grounded in the ITHBC, systematically uncovered the self-management challenges faced by Chinese patients with reflux symptoms after esophagectomy. Patients expressed substantial unmet needs for accessible knowledge, practical self-regulation skills, and continuous collaborative support. These findings highlight the inadequacy of standardized, hospital-based education and the urgent need for patient-centered, context-sensitive interventions. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating culturally adapted, theory-driven interventions that can be scaled across diverse health care settings.
