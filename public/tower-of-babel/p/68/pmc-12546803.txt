Optimization of flavonoids from Astragalus membranaceus stem and leaf waste using nonionic surfactant-integrated ultrasound-assisted extraction☆
Authors: Xinyu Yang, Yue Xu, Huimin Huo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Astragalus membranaceus is a well-known medicinal herb widely used in traditional Chinese medicine, primarily for its bioactive roots [1]. The extensive cultivation of A. membranaceus in China results in an impressive annual production volume, exceeding 20,000 tons. However, the large-scale cultivation of this plant generates substantial stem and leaf residues, which are often discarded despite containing appreciable amounts of flavonoids and other bioactive compounds [2,3]. The underutilization of this biomass not only represents a loss of potential resources but also poses environmental concerns. This necessitates the exploration and implementation of ecologically sound and environmentally friendly technologies to enhance the utilization of all parts of the plant [4].

Currently, the extraction of flavonoids from the stems and leaves of A. membranaceus typically relies on traditional extraction techniques that utilize conventional solvents such as water, ethanol, and methanol. Common methods include cooling, heating, or reflux extraction [5]. However, these traditional approaches are not without their drawbacks. They often involve the use of hazardous and toxic solvents, which can pose health and environmental risks. Additionally, these methods tend to be time-consuming, exhibit lower efficiency, and often require complex operational procedures. These limitations underline the pressing need for innovative extraction strategies that prioritize both efficiency and ecological sustainability, thereby addressing the ongoing challenge of maximizing the potential of A. membranaceus biomass while minimizing environmental impact. UAE has recently emerged as a promising green alternative, as acoustic cavitation improves mass transfer and significantly enhances extraction efficiency [5,6]. Incorporating nonionic surfactants into UAE may further improve flavonoid recovery by enhancing solubility while maintaining low toxicity, thus aligning with the principles of sustainable processing. [7,8]. Consequently, this leads to higher yields of flavonoids when compared to more traditional extraction methods, underscoring the potential advantages of utilizing surfactants in the extraction process [9,10].

Numerous studies have underscored the significance of various extraction parameters, including the solvent-to-solid ratio, type of solvent, duration of extraction, and temperature, in determining the recovery efficiency of bioactive compounds [11]. Optimizing these factors is essential for enhancing extraction efficiency, particularly concerning flavonoids obtained from a wide array of plant sources. In this study, we aimed to develop an efficient and eco-friendly approach for the extraction of flavonoids from A. membranaceus stem and leaf waste. Specifically, we investigated the performance of nonionic surfactant-integrated UAE in comparison with conventional techniques, optimized the extraction parameters using RSM, and assessed the antioxidant activity and environmental sustainability of the obtained extracts. The findings are expected to provide a scientific basis for the valorization of AMW as a renewable source of natural antioxidants.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

A. membranaceus specimen (voucher number 20241012) was delivered to Jilin Agricultural University College of Chinese Medicinal Materials. We purchased nonionic surfactants from Shanghai Biyuntian Biological Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), which included Genapol-X080, Tween 20, and Tween 80. ITS DNA molecular sequencing was used to identify A. membranaceus stems and leaves that were gathered from the Naiman Banner Medicinal Materials Research and Development Center in Inner Mongolia, China (ITS sequence supplied in Fig. S1).

Extraction of flavonoids

Preparation of samples

After being allowed to naturally dry, the stems and leaves of AMW were finely ground and passed through a 40-mesh sieve. The extraction was carried out using a KS-700XDS ultrasonic cleaner. Following the ultrasonic extraction, the resulting extract was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to obtain the final solution.

Nonionic surfactants screening

Surfactant-assisted ultrasonic extraction was used to maximize total flavonoid extraction, with the flavonoid yield acting as the main assessment parameter. The impact of several surfactants and their concentrations (1.0 %, 1.5 %, 2.0 %, 2.5 %, and 3.0 %) on the extraction efficiency of flavonoids was methodically evaluated in this work. For comparison, a blank control group that had no surfactant applied was included. Using a KS-700XDS ultrasonic apparatus (Kunshan Ultrasonic Instruments Co., Ltd., China), extraction was carried out under carefully monitored conditions: 70 % ethanol concentration, 30 mL/g liquid–solid ratio, 50 °C, and 150 W for 90 min. After that, the extracts were separated by centrifuging them for ten minutes at 11,000 rpm.

Comparison of several extraction methods

After the best solvent was found, the UAE method was contrasted with three traditional extraction methods: UAE, heating extraction (HRE), and stirring-assisted extraction (SAE). Consistent parameters were used for all extraction techniques: a 1:30 AMW powder-to-solvent ratio, a 70 % ethanol concentration, a 90-minute extraction period, and Genapol-X080 surfactant. The UAE approach was carried out in accordance with Section 2.2.2. While SAE was conducted using a DF-101S heated magnetic stirrer (Henan Aibot Technology Development Co., Ltd., China) at 100 rpm and 50 °C, HRE was conducted in an HH-6 thermostatic water bath (Nanjing Ronghua Scientific Equipment Co., Ltd., China) at 50 °C. Following extraction, the samples were centrifuged for ten minutes at 11,000 rpm.

Assessment of environmental impact

The Analytical GREEnness measure (AGREE) was used to assess the extraction technologies' environmental effect. The environmental impact of each approach was evaluated using the AGREE program (version 0.5), which was created by the University of Vigo and Gdansk University of Technology. The AGREE metric is an open-source tool that may be found at https://mostwiedzy.pl/AGREE. It assigns scores based on the twelve principles of the Green Analytical Chemistry Index (GAC) on a range of 0 to 1 [12]. According to these guidelines, a green analytical approach is considered successful if its total score is higher than 0.6. In order to clearly represent the performance levels across each of the twelve GAC principles, the program uses a color-coded scheme that goes from red to yellow to green. This helps to emphasize the analytical method's strengths and limitations. Each GAC principle's corresponding segment width provides a visual depiction of how it affects the method's total score, including critical factors such as sampling, transportation, storage, preparation of samples, and the reagents and solvents utilized in obtaining the final analytical results.

Determination of total flavonoids content (TFC)

A revised version of the procedure outlined in earlier research was used to calculate the total flavonoid content [13]. The regression equation was found to be y = 0.1033x + 0.0463 (R2 = 0.9994). In short, 300 μL of 5 % NaNO2 aqueous solution, 400 μL of water, and 100 μL of the extract were mixed together. The mixture was vortexed and then allowed to sit at room temperature for five minutes. The mixture was then incubated for five more minutes after 100 μL of a 10 % AlCl3 solution based on ethanol was added. A reference blank sample was used to measure absorbance at 510 nm following the addition of 1200 μL of 1 M NaOH.

Single-factor experiment

The effects of four important factors on the TFC were assessed in order to identify the ideal conditions for flavonoid extraction: ethanol concentration (50, 60, 70, 80, and 90 %); ultrasonic power (50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 W); ultrasonic time (30, 60, 90, 120, and 150 min); and ultrasonic temperature (30, 40, 50, 60, and 70 °C).

Anti-oxidant activity

A modified approach from Zhang et al. [14] was used to evaluate the AMW extract. To put it briefly, 100 μL of AMW extract and 100 μL of a 50 μmol/L DPPH solution were mixed together and left to incubate for 30 min in the dark. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm. The ABTS+ scavenging ability was evaluated using a total antioxidant capacity test kit, per the manufacturer's instructions. A 10 μL sample was mixed with 170 μL of ABTS working solution and 20 μL of application solution. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm following a 6-minute reaction at room temperature. The FRAP test was performed according to the guidelines in the kit. A 5 μL sample of AMW extract was mixed with 180 μL of FRAP working solution, and the combination was incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm, and the calibration curve was created using FeSO4 as the reference. The hydroxyl radical scavenging activity was tested by adding 5 μL of AMW extract, 0.05 mL of 6 mmol/L FeSO4, 0.05 mL of 6 mmol/L salicylic acid in ethanol, and 0.05 mL of 6 mmol/L H2O2 in that order. The absorbance of the reaction mixture at 510 nm was measured following 30 min of incubation at 37 °C.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis and data visualization were carried out using Origin 2021 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA).

3.Results and discussion

Selecting the optimal nonionic surfactants

The effect of several nonionic surfactants and their concentrations on the effectiveness of flavonoid extraction from AMW was assessed through a methodical analysis. Comparing the findings to the blank control group, it was shown that the use of nonionic surfactants significantly increased the flavonoid yields. However, the particular surfactant used determined how much of an improvement there was. Most likely because of its ideal hydrophilic-lipophilic balance (HLB) and molecular structure, which are especially favorable for the solubilization of flavonoids, Genapol-X080 had the best extraction efficiency among the studied surfactants. Within the surfactant concentration range of 1.0 % to 2.0 %, additional optimization studies showed a dose-dependent improvement in extraction efficiency (Fig. 1A). This improvement is explained by the surfactants' capacity to raise cell wall permeability and lower solvent surface tension, which promotes flavonoid release [15]. However, extraction efficiency decreased at concentrations higher than 2.0 %. Selecting the optimal extraction methods

A comparative study was conducted to evaluate the effects of different extraction methods on the yield of natural flavonoids. The methods examined included nonionic surfactant-integrated HRE, SAE, and UAE. As shown in Fig. 1B, nonionic surfactant-integrated ultrasonication extraction yielded significantly higher flavonoid levels compared to the other two methods: HRE and SAE. The distinct processes of ultrasonication are responsible for the significant variation in flavonoid yields. In particular, the mechanical forces produced by ultrasonic cavitation—such as shock waves and microjet formation—effectively break down the raw materials' cell walls and exterior structures. Flavonoids are then released from the plant matrix as a result of this disruption, which also makes solvent penetration easier [16]. Furthermore, the extraction efficiency is further increased by the localized high pressures created during cavitation, which improve flavonoid solubilization. Thus, an effective method for optimizing the yield of natural flavonoids is nonionic surfactant-integrated ultrasonication extraction [17].

On the other hand, the extraction process of nonionic surfactant-integrated HRE mostly depends on solvent diffusion and heat energy. However, this approach is less successful in breaking down hard cellular structures, which may result in the heat destruction of sensitive flavonoid molecules. These limitations underscore the need for alternative methods that can better preserve the integrity of bioactive compounds during extraction [18]. Similarly, SAE involves mechanical agitation without the aid of cavitation-induced disruption. While SAE is a relatively simple and widely used technique, its lack of ultrasonic shock waves limits its ability to break down cell walls efficiently. This can result in incomplete flavonoid release from the lignocellulosic matrix, compounded by poor solvent penetration and limited interfacial contact between the solid and liquid phases, all of which hinder mass transfer kinetics crucial for efficient extraction.

Environmental impact evaluation

The growing focus on environmentally sustainable practices, particularly within chemical processes, has spurred the development of innovative methods that align with sustainability principles [19]. This shift underscores the significance of employing greener reagents and strategies that reduce both energy consumption and waste generation. In line with these sustainability objectives, the researchers conducted a comprehensive assessment of various solvents, utilizing AGREE software version 0.5 and referencing established classifications from the literature. The results of this evaluation, presented in Fig. 2, highlight the performance ratings for each extraction technique, considering key factors such as energy consumption, sample throughput (processed in triplicate), and waste production during the extraction process. The score of 0.78 for UAE, a score of 0.60 for HRE, and a score of 0.62 for SAE, as illustrated in Fig. 2, display the performance scores of each extraction technique. Notably, the combination of a nonionic surfactant with UAE outperformed both stirring-assisted and heating methods. Compared with HRE and SAE, UAE exhibited distinct operational advantages in terms of automation, analytical throughput, number of analytes detectable within a single run, and energy efficiency. Specifically, the UAE system, operating in a fully automated mode, enabled the simultaneous extraction of 12 samples per run, with detection of a single analyte and an energy consumption of 0.225 kWh. In contrast, the HRE method, which was semi-automated, processed only one sample per run, detected one analyte, and required 2.25 kWh of energy. Similarly, the SAE method, also semi-automated and limited to one sample per run, permitted detection of one analyte with an energy consumption of 1.2 kWh. This superior performance is primarily attributed to the smaller operational scale and significantly lower energy requirements of the nonionic surfactant-integrated UAE. The observed differences among these methods can be largely explained by the variations in experimental scale and the associated energy usage. Additionally, the potential for automation plays a crucial role in enhancing process efficiency. The nonionic surfactant-integrated UAE technique, with its suitability for automation and precise control, stands out as an optimal choice for both pilot studies and large-scale industrial applications.Fig. 2Green evaluation of different methods of ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE), stirring-assisted extraction (SAE), and heating-assisted extraction (HRE) by AGREE.

Single-factor experiment

Effect of liquid–solid ratio

We carried out a thorough comparison analysis to evaluate the effects of many parameters—liquid–solid ratio, ultrasonic duration, ultrasonic temperature, ethanol concentration, and ultrasonic power—on the TFC recovered from waste agricultural products. By adjusting the mass transfer driving forces involved, the liquid–solid ratio is one of these crucial parameters that affects extraction efficiency [20]. As the liquid–solid ratio rose from 10 to 30 mL/g, a significant rise in TFC was noted, as illustrated in Fig. 3A. Nevertheless, there was a discernible drop in extraction efficiency at this level. The combined impacts of solvent volume on mass transfer kinetics are responsible for this tendency. By increasing the contact surface area, raising the osmotic pressure, and decreasing viscosity, increasing the solvent volume improves mass transfer rates. These changes also enhance the ultrasonic cavitation effect, improving extraction efficiency. After optimizing these competing factors, a liquid–solid ratio of 30 mL/g was determined to be the optimal condition for maximizing flavonoid extraction in this study.Fig. 3Effects of various extraction parameters on flavonoids yield. Effect of ultrasonic time

The extraction rate exhibited a significant improvement within the ultrasonic extraction time range of 30 to 150 min, with the TFC reaching its peak at 60 min, as shown in Fig. 3B. During this period, the prolonged application of ultrasonic waves intensified cavitation effects, enhancing cellular disruption. This increased cavitation contributed to higher extraction efficiency by promoting more complete breakdown of cell walls, thereby facilitating the release of the targeted compounds. Extending the extraction time generally aids in maximizing flavonoid yield by ensuring thorough extraction and recovery [21]. However, this trend has its limitations. Beyond 60 min, the extraction rate begins to decline, likely due to the rise in solution temperature from prolonged ultrasonic exposure. Elevated temperatures can lead to oxidative polymerization of flavonoids, compromising their structural integrity and reducing the concentration of active compounds recovered [22]. Therefore, based on these observations, a 60-minute extraction time was selected as the optimal duration for further optimization studies.

Effect of ultrasonic temperature

The extraction of bioactive compounds is significantly influenced by ultrasonic temperature, which plays a crucial role in modulating the forces that drive mass transfer [23]. As shown in Fig. 3C, flavonoid yield increases notably as the ultrasonic temperature rises from 30 to 70 °C. However, after reaching this threshold, the yield begins to decline sharply, indicating that while higher temperatures can enhance extraction efficiency, they also pose a risk to the integrity of the flavonoids. Ultrasonic temperature serves a dual role in this process: it acts as a catalyst to accelerate extraction but can also jeopardize the preservation of valuable flavonoids. When the temperature is raised to an optimal level, it softens the plant’s cellular structure, which facilitates the more efficient release of flavonoids normally confined within the plant matrix [24]. In addition to optimizing temperature, prolonging the extraction duration under ideal conditions can further enhance extraction efficiency. As the extraction time increases, the solvent temperature also rises, leading to a reduction in the viscosity and surface tension of the solvent. These physical changes improve the dynamics of the extraction process, promoting higher yields of flavonoids. The resulting improvements in transfer and diffusion rates further enhance overall extraction efficiency [25].

A critical factor in the enhanced extraction efficiency, especially when using ultrasonic systems to obtain bioactive compounds from plants, is the phenomenon of cavitation. Ultrasonic waves generate rapid fluctuations in pressure, leading to the formation and collapse of microbubbles within the extraction medium. This cavitation generates substantial shear forces that disrupt plant cell walls, allowing the solvent to penetrate more effectively into the cells and facilitating the release of intracellular substances into the extraction medium [26]. Following a thorough evaluation of various factors such as temperature, viscosity, and extraction time, a temperature of 50 °C was determined to be optimal for this study. This temperature was selected not only to maximize flavonoid extraction efficiency but also to balance the other critical parameters influencing the process. The careful consideration of these factors highlights the importance of a methodical approach in achieving optimal extraction outcomes in plant-based extractions.

Effect of ethanol concentration

Flavonoids, important bioactive compounds in plants, can be efficiently extracted using ethanol as a solvent. The volume fraction of ethanol plays a critical role in the extraction process, directly influencing flavonoid yield. In this study, we investigated the effect of varying ethanol concentrations, ranging from 50 % to 90 %, on flavonoid production. Our results indicated a significant increase in flavonoid yield as the ethanol concentration increased from 50 % to 70 %, with the highest yield observed at 70 %. However, further increases in ethanol concentration from 70 % to 90 % led to a decline in flavonoid yield, as shown in Fig. 3D. This tendency can be linked to two primary factors. First, ethanol creates a concentration gradient between plant cells' internal and external environments, allowing flavonoids to diffuse into the solvent. Second, ethanol disturbs the associations between flavonoids and the plant matrix, which increases extraction efficiency. However, when ethanol concentration exceeds an optimal threshold, it may hinder the dissolution of polar flavonoids, as higher ethanol concentrations can alter the polarity of the solvent [27]. Based on these observations, a 70 % ethanol concentration was identified as the optimal condition for extracting flavonoids from plant materials.

Effect of ultrasonic power

As ultrasonic power increases, the TFC first climbs, but then declines as the power approaches 100 W, as seen in Fig. 3E. The initial rise in TFC is due to the greater cavitation effect caused by higher power levels, which amplifies cellular damage. This increased fragmentation accelerates the extraction of flavonoids from the plant matrix. Within an optimal range, higher ultrasonic power effectively promotes cell disruption, leading to greater flavonoid release into the extraction medium [28]. However, excessively high ultrasonic power can have detrimental effects. For example, excessive bubble formation during ultrasonic treatment can impair shock wave propagation. The merging of bubbles into larger structures weakens their implosive effects, which are essential for efficient extraction. Additionally, vigorous cavitation at high power levels can cause chemical bond cleavage, molecular degradation, and the formation of free radicals in specific flavonoids, particularly those extracted during AMW extraction. These changes may weaken flavonoids' structural integrity and hence decrease their biological activity. These results led to the conclusion that 100 W was the ideal ultrasonic power to maximize extraction efficiency.

Effect of extraction parameters on TFC

The 3D response surface and 2D contour plots were plotted according to equation. As shown in Fig. 5A-F. Notably, increases in both ultrasonic time and temperature led to a significant boost in TFC extraction. This enhancement is attributed to elevated temperatures and prolonged ultrasonic exposure, which contribute to the softening and disruption of cell walls, intensifying cavitation and ultimately improving flavonoid yield [29]. Furthermore, TFC yield was first raised by the interplay of ultrasonic duration and ethanol concentration. However, a decrease in yield was noted over a certain ethanol percentage. This reduction is likely due to excessive cell wall disruption, with ethanol acting as a swelling agent that interferes with the solvent-solid matrix contact area and alters the dielectric constant of the solvent. TFC yield first increased dramatically with increasing ultrasonic temperature and ethanol concentration. Under ideal extraction conditions, this effect is ascribed to the improved mass transfer efficiency and increased solubility of flavonoids. While moderate ethanol concentrations enhanced solvent polarity and improved the extraction process, higher temperatures promoted flavonoid release by facilitating cell wall disintegration [30]. However, the TFC yield decreased when specific criteria were exceeded. The denaturation of heat-sensitive chemicals and the thermal degradation of flavonoids at unreasonably high temperatures can be attributed to this reduction. To evaluate the reliability and practicality of the experimental method, slight adjustments were made to these parameters. Specifically, the ultrasonic extraction was carried out under the following optimized conditions: ultrasonic time was set to 55 min, temperature was reduced to 50 °C, ethanol concentration was adjusted to 70 %, and ultrasonic power was decreased to 100 W. A series of validation experiments were performed under these conditions, each repeated three times to ensure consistency and reliability. The experimental results revealed an actual total flavonoid content of 14.77 ± 0.05 mg/g, which closely aligned with the predicted yield obtained from the RSM analysis. This close agreement between experimental and predicted values demonstrates the robustness of the method and confirms the effectiveness of the proposed extraction conditions.

Impact of optimisation on UAE

Before and after the optimization procedure, the UAE method's energy usage and flavonoid extraction efficiency were carefully assessed. After optimisation, the findings demonstrated notable increases in extraction yield and energy efficiency. In particular, the flavonoid extraction rate increased significantly from 11.76 mg/g to 14.77 mg/g using the optimized ultrasound-assisted extraction (OUAE) approach, indicating a significant 25.60 % improvement in extraction efficiency. This result demonstrates how well the optimizations worked, since they improved the cavitation effect by fine-tuning operating parameters including ultrasonic frequency, power, and extraction time. The total extraction yield was raised as a result of the enhanced cavitation, which made it easier for flavonoids to be released from the plant matrix. Additionally, the OUAE technique demonstrated significant energy savings. The energy consumption of the extraction process dropped from 810,000 J to 330,000 J, representing a 59.26 % decrease (Table 7). This decrease illustrates the sustainability of the enhanced UAE process as it not only increases extraction efficiency but also reduces the environmental impact often linked to high energy usage. In addition, compared with Cui et al. (2022), our nonionic surfactant–integrated UAE achieved a 34.1 % higher flavonoid yield under milder conditions. Our method also reduced energy consumption by 59.3 % and achieved a higher greenness score (0.78 vs. 0.56) [3]. Evaluation of antioxidant capacity

The DPPH radical scavenging activity was used to evaluate the antioxidant capacity of AMW extracts, where antioxidants donate electrons to the DPPH radical, resulting in a color shift from purple to yellow. Spectrophotometric measurement of this color shift provides a trustworthy quantitative evaluation that is directly correlated with the antioxidant chemicals' electron transfer efficiency [32]. The DPPH radical scavenging activity showed a clear dose-dependent relationship with the addition of nonionic surfactants to AMW extracts during UAE. At concentrations ranging from 0.2 to 1.4 mg/mL, the extracts displayed a linear relationship between concentration and DPPH radical scavenging activity, suggesting a stoichiometric interaction between the extracts' phytochemicals and the free radicals (Fig. 6A). Like the popular DPPH test, the ABTS radical cation assay is a crucial technique for assessing the antioxidative qualities of different substances [33]. Within the concentration range of 0.2–1.4 mg/mL, a positive correlation was observed, suggesting that the extract's ability to neutralize ABTS radicals improved with increasing extract concentration. A thorough evaluation of the AMW extracts' antioxidant capacities was given by the highest concentration tested, 1.4 mg/mL, highlighting their noteworthy antioxidative potential (Fig. 6B). Because antioxidants may contribute electrons and reduce ferric ions to their ferrous form, the intensity of this blue chromophore is a direct measure of the sample's reducing capability [34]. The antioxidant qualities of the extracts also became more noticeable as the concentration rose; the highest association was seen between 0.2 and 1.4 mg/mL. The antioxidant capabilities of the AMW extracts were comprehensively assessed at the highest concentration tested, 1.4 mg/mL, offering important information about their potential as powerful antioxidants (Fig. 6C). It is commonly acknowledged that hydroxyl radicals, which are produced as byproducts of several metabolic activities, are among the most hazardous and reactive free radicals [35]. The findings showed that the concentration of AMW extraction solutions and their capacity to scavenge hydroxyl radicals were significantly positively correlated. Within the concentration range of 0.2 to 1.4 mg/mL, this link was consistently seen for both OUAE and UAE that had been combined with nonionic surfactants. Notably, the antioxidant capabilities of the AMW extracts were comprehensively assessed against stable hydroxyl radicals at the maximum tested dose of 1.4 mg/mL (Fig. 6D). Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the integration of nonionic surfactant-assisted UAE significantly improves the recovery of flavonoids from AMW. Genapol X-080 was identified as the most effective surfactant, enabling optimized extraction conditions that enhanced flavonoid yield while reducing energy consumption. The resulting extracts exhibited strong antioxidant capacity across multiple assays, confirming the value of AMW as a promising source of bioactive compounds. Beyond methodological optimization, the findings highlight the broader potential of combining surfactant-assisted strategies with ultrasonic techniques to achieve both efficiency and environmental sustainability in natural product recovery. This approach provides a scientific basis for the valorization of plant-derived biomass, with implications for functional food development, pharmaceutical applications, and green extraction technologies. Nonetheless, the study was performed under controlled laboratory conditions, which may not comprehensively capture the complexities encountered at an industrial scale, including raw material heterogeneity and process robustness. Future studies should focus on mechanistic investigations of surfactant–flavonoid interactions under ultrasonic conditions, as well as scale-up trials to assess process robustness in industrial settings. Expanding this strategy to other agricultural and medicinal residues could further establish surfactant-assisted UAE as a versatile platform for the sustainable extraction of high-value natural products.
