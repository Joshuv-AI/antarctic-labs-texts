Structure basis for C-C chemokine receptor 4 modulation by orthosteric and allosteric antagonists
Authors: Ning Zhou, Rong Wang, Baozhi Chen, Xiaofeng Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceC-C chemokine receptor 4 (CCR4) guides immune cells to sites of inflammation and tumors and plays a central role in certain leukemias and lymphomas. It has emerged as a promising target for cancer immunotherapy and the treatment of immune disorders. This study used cryoelectron microscopy to determine high-resolution structures of CCR4 in its unbound state and bound to various drugs. The findings reveal that some drugs block the chemokine binding site, while others bind allosterically to inhibit signaling. Moreover, the therapeutic antibody mogamulizumab binds to the CCR4 N terminus without interfering with natural chemokines, acting instead through immune cell–mediated killing. These insights provide a structural framework for designing next-generation CCR4-targeted therapies.

Chemokines are a family of small cytokines (8 to 10 kDa) that regulate leukocyte migration (1). Approximately 50 chemokines have been identified in humans, all sharing a conserved structural feature consisting of an N-terminal cysteine motif, a three-stranded β-sheet core, and a C-terminal α-helix (1). Based on the number and position of cysteine residue(s) in the N-terminal cysteine motif, chemokines are classified into four groups: C (containing one cysteine), C-C (two adjacent cysteines), C-X-C (two cysteines separated by one residue), and C-X3-C (two cysteines separated by three residues) (1, 2). Classical chemokine receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) and include ten C-C chemokine receptors (CCR1–10), six C-X-C chemokine receptors (CXCR1–6), one C chemokine receptor (XCR1), and one C-X3-C chemokine receptor (CX3CR1) identified in humans (2, 3). Upon stimulation by chemokines, these receptors couple to the Gi class of G proteins, activating second messenger pathways that trigger polarized actin polymerization, leading to integrin-mediated adhesion or amoeboid cell migration. In addition to classical receptors, four atypical chemokine receptors (ACKR1–4) have been identified in humans (4). Although they share the seven-transmembrane (7-TMs) structure characteristic of GPCRs, they do not couple to G proteins or induce chemotaxis. Instead, they regulate chemokine availability by mediating storage, transcellular transport, or degradation (4).

Individual chemokines can bind to multiple receptors, and conversely, a single receptor can interact with multiple chemokines (3, 5). This redundancy and cooperativity form a complex regulatory network that plays a central role in leukocyte trafficking, encompassing both homeostatic migration under physiological conditions and targeted recruitment during infection, inflammation, and cancer (6). Recent studies increasingly highlight the critical roles of the chemokine system in recruiting immune cells to the tumor microenvironment (TME) and modulating the immune landscape within tumors (7). These effects include either suppressing the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells, such as regulatory T cells (Tregs), or enhancing the infiltration of immune effector cells, such as cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and natural killer cells, by targeting chemokine signaling pathways (5, 7). Consequently, therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating chemokine signaling, either by inhibiting pathways that attract suppressive cell populations or by enhancing those that promote effector cell recruitment, are under active investigation (6). Numerous clinical trials are currently evaluating approaches that target chemokines alone or in combination with other immunotherapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors or chimeric antigen receptor T cells (5, 8).

CCR4, the receptor for CCL17 and CCL22, is expressed on T helper 2 (Th2) cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), skin-homing T cells, and certain subtypes of T cell lymphomas (9, 10). CCR4 plays an essential role in both T cell homeostasis and recruitment to the TME, highlighting its therapeutic potential in both CCR4+ T cell leukemia and solid tumors (11, 12). In the context of lymphoma treatment, mogamulizumab, a humanized anti-CCR4 monoclonal antibody, has been approved for the treatment of adult T cell leukemia/lymphoma, a rare and aggressive malignancy associated with human T-lymphotropic virus type 1 (HTLV-1) in which CCR4 serves as a marker, through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) (13–15). Mogamulizumab is also used for relapsed or refractory CCR4+ cutaneous T cell lymphoma, including the most common subtypes, mycosis fungoides and Sézary syndrome (14, 16). Treg cells, well known for their immunosuppressive function, are frequently recruited to the TME in solid tumors via the CCL17/CCL22–CCR4 signaling axis, thereby limiting the efficacy of cancer immunotherapies (12). Small-molecule CCR4 antagonists, such as Tivumecirnon (FLX475), have shown promise in blocking Treg migration and enhancing antitumor immunity across multiple cancer models (17). These findings underscore the potential of CCR4 inhibition as a strategy to improve immunotherapy outcomes.

Although numerous studies and clinical trials have targeted chemokine receptors, CCR4 remains the only receptor for which an approved cancer therapy exists, positioning it as a particularly attractive target for further therapeutic development. However, the structural basis and mechanisms of CCR4 inhibition have remained poorly defined, limiting rational drug design. Here, we report the cryo-EM structures of the human CCR4 complex in multiple functional states. Structural analysis and comparison reveal the inhibitory mechanisms of CCR4 antagonists through both orthosteric and allosteric regulation.

Results

Structure Determination of CCR4.

As a typical class A GPCR, CCR4 comprises 7-TMs, preceded by an N-terminal loop of approximately 30 residues and followed by a ~50-residue C-terminal tail. The relatively small size of CCR4 (~40 kDa) poses challenges for cryo-EM studies, particularly when focusing on its inactive state. To increase the molecular weight and introduce a distinct feature for particle alignment, residues between Cys232 and Lys236 in intracellular loop 3 (ICL3) were replaced with a thermostabilized apocytochrome b562 (BRIL). AlphaFold predictions (18) indicated that the inclusion of two short linker sequences, derived from the A2A adenosine receptor, helped maintain continuous α-helical conformations at the junctions between CCR4-TM5 and the N terminus of BRIL, as well as between CCR4-TM6 and the C terminus of BRIL (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A).

The CCR4-BRIL chimeric construct was expressed and purified and followed by assembly with an anti-BRIL Fab and an anti-Fab nanobody (Nb) (19) and gel filtration to isolate a stable CCR4-BRIL–Fab–Nb complex suitable for structural studies (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). Cryo-EM structure of human CCR4 in the apo state revealed well-defined density maps, enabling unambiguous modeling of BRIL, Fab, Nb, and the 7-TM domain of CCR4 (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D–G and Table S1). Therefore, this complex, termed CCR4Cryo, was used for the structure determination of inactive CCR4 in complex with different antagonists.

Zelnecirnon and Tivumecirnon Bind to the Orthosteric Site of CCR4.

The CCR4 antagonists Zelnecirnon (RPT193) and Tivumecirnon (FLX475), which share similar chemical scaffolds characterized by a 2,4-dichlorobenzyl group and pyrrolidinyl-piperidine rings (Fig. 2A), have been evaluated in clinical trials for immune disorders and various types of cancer (17, 20). Their therapeutic potential stems from their ability to inhibit the migration of Th2 and Treg cells that express CCR4 on the cell surface (17, 20). To elucidate the binding modes of RPT193 and FLX475, each compound was individually added to the CCR4Cryo sample for structural analysis by cryo-EM. The resulting structures of CCR4Cryo–RPT193 and CCR4Cryo–FLX475 were determined at overall resolutions of 3.40 Å and 3.48 Å, respectively (Fig. 1 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–H and Table S1).

Both RPT193 and FLX475 bind to the same orthosteric site of CCR4 and engage TMs 2, 3, 5, 6, and 7 (Figs. 1 B and C and 2 B and C). The interaction surfaces between CCR4 and RPT193 or FLX475 are approximately 590 Å2 and 540 Å2, respectively. In the case of RPT193, the 2,4-dichlorobenzyl group inserts vertically into a pocket formed by TMs 3, 5, and 6, where it forms a π–π interaction with Phe121 on TM3 (Fig. 2B). Additional interactions to the 2,4-dichlorobenzyl group involve residues Trp255, Tyr258, and Leu262 on TM6 of CCR4 (Fig. 2B). The remaining portion of RPT193 extends horizontally into the extracellular pocket of CCR4 and is further stabilized by nearby residues including Trp95, Tyr98, Tyr117, Leu118, and Ile206. FLX475 adopts an overall conformation and binding mode highly similar to that of RPT193 (Fig. 2C), suggesting a shared mechanism of antagonism. Notably, the trifluoromethyl group of FLX475 forms a hydrogen bond with Asn207 (Fig. 2C).

Structural comparisons between CCR4Cryo–RPT193 and CCR4Cryo–FLX475 revealed minimal differences, with a RMSD of 0.507 Å, consistent with their highly similar binding modes. However, compared to the apo CCR4Cryo structure, an outward shift of the extracellular end and an inward shift of the intracellular end of TM5 were observed, likely contributing to the formation of a more accessible binding pocket for the antagonists (Fig. 2D).

Structures of other chemokine receptors in complex with antagonists that bind to the extracellular pocket have been reported previously, including CCR2 with BMS-681 and MK-0812 (21, 22), CCR5 with maraviroc (23), CCR6 with OXM1 and OXM2 (24), and CXCR4 with 1T1t (25) plerixafor (AMD3100), mavorixafor (AMD070), and HF51116 (26) (Fig. 2 E–I and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–I). Among these compounds, RPT193 and FLX475 exhibit a unique binding mode in which the head groups insert vertically into the deep extracellular pocket, while the tail groups extend horizontally. Notably, RPT193 and FLX475 penetrate more deeply into the 7-TM bundle of CCR4 than any previously reported chemokine receptor antagonists (Fig. 2 E–I). These features distinguish RPT193 and FLX475 as a structurally unique class of chemokine receptor antagonists and may inform the rational structure-based design of RPT193/FLX475-like inhibitors for other chemokine receptors of therapeutic interest.

AZD2098 and GSK2239633A Bind to an Allosteric Site of CCR4.

AZD2098 and GSK2239633A are potent CCR4 antagonists that are structurally distinct from RPT193 and FLX475, each featuring a central sulfonamide motif (Fig. 3A) (27, 28). To elucidate their binding modes, each compound was individually added to the CCR4Cryo sample and analyzed by cryo-EM. The resulting structures of CCR4Cryo–AZD2098 and CCR4Cryo–GSK2239633A were determined at overall resolutions of 3.54 Å and 3.88 Å, respectively (Fig. 1 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–H and Table S1).

Both AZD2098 and GSK2239633A bind to the same intracellular pocket of CCR4, formed by TMs 1, 6, 7, and Helix 8 (H8) (Figs. 1 D and E and 3 B and C). The interaction surfaces between CCR4 and AZD2098 or GSK2239633A are approximately 330 Å2 and 450 Å2, respectively. As for AZD2098, the central sulfonyl group forms three hydrogen bonds with the main chain amide groups of Glu309, Lys310, and Phe311 in the turn between TM7 and H8, located at the interface between the inner leaflet of the cell membrane and the cytosol (Fig. 3B). The 2,3-dichlorophenyl group inserts into the transmembrane domain and binds a hydrophobic pocket formed by Leu78, Met243, Tyr304, and Phe311 (Fig. 3B). Similarly, the sulfonyl group of GSK2239633A also interacts with Glu309, Lys310, and Phe311 through hydrogen bonding (Fig. 3C), and its 5-chlorothiophen-2-yl group occupies the same hydrophobic pocket as the 2,3-dichlorophenyl group of AZD2098 (Fig. 3C). Structural comparisons revealed minimal differences between CCR4Cryo–AZD2098 and CCR4Cryo–GSK2239633A, with a RMSD of 0.384 Å, likely reflecting their similar binding modes. However, a slight outward displacement of the TM7 C-terminal region was observed compared to the apo CCR4Cryo structure, creating additional space to accommodate antagonist binding (Fig. 3D).

To validate the structural observations, we mutated Leu78 to Tyr to introduce steric hindrance and Met243 to Lys to alter the electrostatic environment of the AZD2098/GSK2239633A binding site in CCR4. We then measured AZD2098 binding by ITC. The results showed that AZD2098 binds to WT CCR4 with a Kd of 30 nM, while binding was abolished in both the L78Y and M243K mutants (Fig. 3E and SI Appendix, Fig. S1B).

Interestingly, another chemokine receptor antagonist, Vercirnon (GSK1605786A), which targets CCR9, also contains a central sulfonamide motif that interacts with Glu322, Arg323, and Phe324 (corresponding to Glu309, Lys310, and Phe311 in CCR4), via its sulfonyl group (Fig. 3F and SI Appendix, Fig. S5A) (29). This observation suggests a shared antagonistic mechanism among sulfonamide-containing molecules targeting chemokine receptors. Sequence alignment further reveals that Gly308, located at the beginning of the TM7–H8 turn, and Phe311 are highly conserved across chemokine receptors (with the exception of CXCR6) (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Based on this conservation, we define this structural feature as the G-X-X-F motif, which appears to serve as a common recognition site for sulfonyl or related polar groups. Notably, the second residue following the Gly is typically a positively charged residue (Arg or Lys) (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). As this residue is located on the inner leaflet of the membrane, it may contribute to membrane protein topology in accordance with the “positive-inside” rule (30).

In addition to AZD2098, GSK2239633A, and Vercirnon, several previously structurally characterized intracellular antagonists of chemokine receptors also bind to this G-X-X-F motif. These include CCR2-RA-(R) (targeting CCR2) (22), squaramide 1 (SQA1 or 00767013) (targeting both CCR6 and CXCR2) (24, 31), and Cmp2105 (targeting CCR7) (32) (Fig. 3 G–J and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B–D). Despite substantial chemical diversity, these antagonists all feature a central hydrogen bond receptor group, such as a hydroxy-pyrrolinone in CCR2-RA-(R), a cyclobutene dione in SQA1, or a thiadiazole dioxide in Cmp2105, that interacts directly with the G-X-X-F motif (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B–D). Together, our CCR4Cryo–AZD2098 and CCR4Cryo–GSK2239633A structures, along with prior structural studies, strongly suggest that the G-X-X-F motif at the TM7–H8 interface represents a conserved allosteric binding “hot spot” for chemokine receptor antagonists. These findings imply that successful allosteric antagonists should include a hydrogen bond receptor group capable of engaging this conserved motif.

Mechanisms of CCR4 Inhibition.

Multiple structures of chemokine receptors in complex with both chemokines and G proteins have been reported, including C-C chemokine receptors (CCR1, CCR2, CCR5, CCR6, and CCR8) (33–39), C-X-C chemokine receptors (CXCR1–4) (31, 40–46), the X chemokine receptor XCR1 (47), and the C-X3-C chemokine receptor CX3CR1 (48) (Fig. 4 A–E and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A–F). These structures consistently support the classical “two-site” binding model for chemokine–receptor interaction (49, 50). In this model, chemokine recognition site 1 (CRS1) comprises the N-terminal loop of the receptor (preceding TM1), which interacts with a groove on the globular domain of the chemokine (Fig. 4 A–E). Chemokine recognition site 2 (CRS2) involves the extracellular pocket formed by the receptor’s 7-TMs, which accommodates the N terminus of the chemokine (Fig. 4 A–E). CRS1 primarily mediates chemokine recruitment, while CRS2 is responsible for receptor activation and subsequent G-protein coupling (51, 52).

Structural comparison of CCR4Cryo–RPT193/FLX475 with representative active-state C-C chemokine-receptor complexes revealed that RPT193 and FLX475 occupy the CRS2, thereby interfering with chemokine binding at this site and supporting its role as an allosteric antagonist (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, an AlphaFold3-predicted model of the CCL17–CCR4–Gi complex reinforces this interpretation. The predicted CRS1 and CRS2 interfaces closely resemble those observed in experimentally determined active chemokine–receptor complexes (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). Notably, residues 1 to 5 of the CCL17 N terminus are positioned within the same pocket that accommodates RPT193 and FLX475, indicating direct competition at the CRS2 site (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B).

Chemokine receptors primarily couple to the Gi/Go protein family. Structural studies of Gi/Go-bound chemokine receptors from the C, C-C, and C-X-C subfamilies reveal a conserved mode of G-protein engagement. Upon activation, the cytoplasmic end of TM6 undergoes a pronounced outward displacement relative to the inactive state, creating a binding pocket formed by the intracellular ends of TMs 2, 3, 4, 5, and 7. The α5-helix of Gαi or Gαo inserts into this pocket to facilitate coupling (47). An exception is CX3CR1, which exhibits only minor TM6 displacement and a correspondingly narrow cytoplasmic pocket for Gαi engagement (48). AZD2098 and GSK2239633A occupy a portion of the cytoplasmic pocket near the TM7–H8 turn of CCR4, thereby obstructing G-protein coupling and downstream signaling (Fig. 4F). This is further supported by the AlphaFold3-predicted model of the CCL17–CCR4–Gi complex, which reveals that the binding site of AZD2098/GSK2239633A overlaps with the Gαi α5-helix, consistent with its role as intracellular allosteric antagonists (SI Appendix, Fig. S8C).

In addition to small-molecule antagonists, the monoclonal antibody mogamulizumab, which targets CCR4, has been clinically approved for the treatment of multiple cancer types. To elucidate its binding mechanism, we purified the Fab fragment of mogamulizumab (designated MFab) and assembled it with WT human CCR4 for cryo-EM analysis. 2D classification revealed that the Fab region exhibited relative flexibility in its association with the 7-TMs of CCR4 (Fig. 4G), suggesting that MFab binds to a flexible region of the receptor. To further define the epitope, we truncated the N terminus of CCR4 and performed pull-down assays. The results identified the MFab epitope as residing between residues 10 to 21, corresponding to a flexible loop preceding TM1, consistent with our cryo-EM analysis (Fig. 4H). Notably, this region does not overlap with CRS1, suggesting that MFab may not compete with chemokine binding (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). To test this hypothesis, we performed competition assays, which showed that MFab does not compete with CCL17 for CCR4 binding (Fig. 4I). These findings indicate that mogamulizumab antagonizes CCR4 function exclusively through ADCC, rather than by blocking ligand binding at the CRS1 site.

Discussion

As a central system governing immune cell trafficking, the chemokine system represents a critical therapeutic target for both immune disorders and cancer immunotherapy. CCR4, in particular, is clinically validated, with mogamulizumab approved for the treatment of multiple T cell lymphomas. Additional clinical trials are ongoing to evaluate CCR4-targeting agents for conditions such as atopic dermatitis and advanced cancers. Our structural analysis of CCR4 in its inactive state lays the foundation for understanding the mechanisms of existing CCR4 antagonists and will facilitate structure-based drug discovery for novel CCR4-targeting therapeutics.

The structures of CCR4Cryo–RPT193 and CCR4Cryo–FLX475 revealed that both compounds act as orthosteric antagonists, engaging a unique binding mode not observed in previously reported chemokine receptor structures. A hallmark of this mode is the deep insertion of the 2,4-dichlorobenzyl group into the orthosteric pocket. Notably, the residues that form this binding pocket, Phe121, Gly210, Trp255, Tyr258, and Asn259, are highly conserved among chemokine receptors (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), suggesting that molecules sharing similar structural features with RPT193 and FLX475 may serve as potential antagonists for other chemokine receptors.

The structures of CCR4Cryo–AZD2098 and CCR4Cryo–GSK2239633A identified an allosteric binding site located at the TM7–H8 turn, a region that emerges as a conserved hot spot for antagonist binding. Our structural data, together with previous studies, indicate that effective antagonists targeting this site share a key pharmacophore: a central hydrogen bond donor capable of forming interactions with the main-chain amide groups of the conserved G-X-X-F motif. This insight provides a valuable framework for the future design of allosteric modulators across chemokine receptors.

Materials and Methods

Construct Design.

The chimeric CCR4–BRIL expression constructs were generated by referencing the BRIL-fused Frizzled-5 structure (PDB ID: 6WW2), wherein cytochrome b562 RIL (BRIL) was inserted into the third intracellular loop (ICL3) without introducing any thermostabilizing mutations, as previously described (19). WT CCR4 sequence comprising residues 1 to 231 and 237 to 360 was fused to BRIL via two short helical linkers: ARRQL linking residue 231 to the N terminus of BRIL, and ERARSTLNE connecting the C terminus of BRIL to residue 237.

Protein Purification.

The complementary DNA encoding the human CCR4–BRIL fusion protein, appended with a C-terminal FLAG epitope, was subcloned into the pEZT-BM plasmid. Site-directed mutations were introduced via a two-step overlapping PCR method. Expression of CCR4–BRIL was carried out in HEK293S GnTI− cells (ATCC) through baculovirus infection. Recombinant baculoviruses were produced by transfecting Sf9 insect cells with bacmids encoding the constructs using Cellfectin II (Gibco). Suspension cultures of HEK293S GnTI− cells (2 to 3 × 106 cells/mL, total volume 0.8 L) were transduced with 50 mL of P2 baculovirus encoding CCR4–BRIL construct. Sodium butyrate was added to a final concentration of 10 mM at 8 h postinfection, and cells were collected 48 h later for downstream purification.

The collected cell pellet was resuspended in buffer A (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl) supplemented with 10 μg/mL leupeptin and 1 mM PMSF, then lysed via sonication. The resulting lysate was incubated with 1% (w/v) lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG; Anatrace) at 4 °C for 1 h. Following high-speed centrifugation (20,000×g, 4 °C, 30 min), the supernatant was incubated with anti-FLAG M2 affinity resin (Sigma) for 1 h at 4 °C. The resin was transferred to a gravity-flow column, washed with 20 column volumes of buffer B (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 0.01% LMNG, pH 7.5), and the target protein was eluted using buffer B supplemented with 0.1 mg/mL 3× FLAG peptide. Further purification was performed via size-exclusion chromatography using a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated with buffer C (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.06% digitonin) for subsequent experiments.

The synthetic anti-BRIL Fab fragment was produced in Escherichia coli and purified following the protocol described before (19). A DNA sequence encoding the anti-Fab nanobody (Nb) was chemically synthesized and inserted into the pET21b vector, containing an N-terminal pelB leader sequence, a 6× His affinity tag, and a TEV protease cleavage site. Protein purification followed established procedures (19).

For complex formation, purified CCR4–BRIL protein was incubated on ice with 1.2-fold and 1.5-fold molar ratios of anti-BRIL Fab and anti-Fab Nb, respectively, for 2 h. The assembled complex was concentrated and further subjected to size-exclusion chromatography on a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) using buffer C. Fractions corresponding to the peak were pooled and concentrated to 8 to 10 mg/mL using centrifugal filters with a 100-kDa molecular weight cutoff (MilliporeSigma) for cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) analysis. To prepare cryo-EM samples of the CCR4–BRIL–Fab–Nb complex (termed CCR4Cryo) bound to inhibitors (RPT193, FLX475, AZD2098, or GSK2239633A), each compound was added to a final concentration of 250 μM in 1% DMSO.

The complementary DNA encoding human CCL17 with a C-terminal 6× His tag was subcloned into the pFastBac1 plasmid. Recombinant baculoviruses were generated in Sf9 insect cells as described above. Suspension cultures of Sf9 cells (2 to 3 × 106 cells/mL) were infected with P2 baculovirus, and the cultures were harvested 48 h postinfection. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and passed through Ni-NTA resin twice. The resin was washed with buffer A supplemented with 20 mM imidazole, and the protein was eluted with 250 mM imidazole in buffer A and concentrated for further experiments.

To express the Fab region of mogamulizumab, the variable regions of the light and heavy chains were codon-optimized and cloned into separate shuttle vectors—one for the light chain and the other for the Fab region of the heavy chain which included a C-terminal 6× His tag (53). The resulting constructs were cotransfected into HEK 293S GnTI− cells (2 to 3 × 106 cells/mL) using PEI MAX transfection reagent (Polysciences) and cultured at 37 °C. 48 h posttransfection, the culture medium was collected, and the Fab protein was purified following the same protocol used for CCL17.

Cryo-EM Sample Preparation and Data Collection.

A 3 μL aliquot of each sample was deposited onto glow-discharged Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 400 mesh gold grids coated with holey carbon film (Quantifoil). After blotting, the grids were vitrified by rapid plunging into liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI). The frozen grids were subsequently transferred to a 300 kV Titan Krios transmission electron microscope (FEI) equipped with a Falcon 4i direct electron detector and an energy filter set to a 10-eV slit width for data acquisition.

Image stacks were recorded at a calibrated pixel size of 0.735 Å for apo CCR4Cryo and 0.738 Å for CCR4Cryo in complex with antagonists with a nominal defocus range spanning from 0.8 to 2.2 μm. The electron dose rate was maintained at approximately 8.5 electrons per pixel per second. Each exposure lasted 4 s, resulting in a cumulative electron dose of approximately 60 electrons per Å2 per movie.

Cryo-EM Data Processing.

For all cryo-EM datasets, motion correction and contrast transfer function estimation were performed using cryoSPARC Live with default settings (54). Low-quality micrographs with ice contamination were discarded, and the remaining micrographs were exported for further processing. Particle picking was conducted using either crYOLO or cryoSPARC (54, 55). All subsequent procedures were carried out in cryoSPARC. For apo CCR4Cryo, particles from 2D classification were used to generate three bad models and one good model, which were subjected to three rounds of heterogeneous refinement. The best 3D class was selected for nonuniform refinement. The particles were then subjected to a secondary 2D classification to further remove low-quality particles. The remaining particles were used to generate a new 3D model, which were subsequently refined by homogeneous refinement, nonuniform refinement, and local refinement using a local mask covering the transmembrane helices of CCR4, BRIL, and half of the anti-BRIL Fab. The final map was sharpened using DeepEMhancer (56). For CCR4Cryo–RPT193, two bad models and one good model were generated and subjected to nine rounds of heterogeneous refinement. The best 3D class was selected for nonuniform refinement. Similar to apo CCR4Cryo, further processing included secondary 2D classification, 3D model generation, homogeneous refinement, nonuniform refinement, local refinement with a local mask, and DeepEMhancer sharpening to obtain the final map. For CCR4Cryo–FLX475, two bad models and one good model generated from a preliminary cryo-EM study were subjected to four rounds of heterogeneous refinement. The best 3D class was selected for nonuniform refinement. Subsequent processing followed the same workflow as for apo CCR4Cryo, including secondary 2D classification, homogeneous refinement, nonuniform refinement, local refinement with a local mask, and DeepEMhancer sharpening. For CCR4Cryo–AZD2098, two bad models and one good model from the preliminary cryo-EM study were subjected to two rounds of heterogeneous refinement, followed by nonuniform refinement using the best 3D class. Further processing steps mirrored those used for apo CCR4Cryo, including secondary 2D classification, 3D model generation, homogeneous refinement, nonuniform refinement, local refinement with a local mask, and DeepEMhancer sharpening. For CCR4Cryo–GSK2239633A, two bad models and one good model were subjected to three rounds of heterogeneous refinement. The best 3D class was refined by nonuniform refinement, followed by the same downstream processing steps as for apo CCR4Cryo, including secondary 2D classification, homogeneous refinement, nonuniform refinement, local refinement with a local mask, and DeepEMhancer sharpening to generate the final map.

Model Building and Refinement.

The AlphaFold predicted structure of CCR4–BRIL and structural models of the Fab and Nb (from PDB: 6WW2) were docked into the cryo-EM map as initial models (18, 19). The coordinates and restraints for RPT193, FLX475, AZD2098, and GSK2239633A were generated in PHENIX using SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) strings from the PubChem database. Manual model building and adjustments were performed in Coot (57). Final models underwent real-space refinement in PHENIX with default settings, including restraints for secondary structure elements, Ramachandran plots, and rotamers (58). Due to low local resolution, residues 1 to 34 and 326 to 360 of CCR4, and residues 45 to 54 of BRIL, were not built in any of the structural models. Additionally, residues 319 to 325 of CCR4 were not built in the structures of CCR4Cryo–AZD2098 and CCR4Cryo–GSK2239633A. The final models were validated using PHENIX, and structural figures were generated with PyMOL (https://www.pymol.org) and ChimeraX (59).

ITC Analysis.

ITC measurements were performed at 20 °C using a MicroCal ITC200 instrument (Malvern) in buffer C supplemented with 5% DMSO. The titration involved injecting a 200 µM inhibitor solution from the syringe into a cell containing 20 µM of CCR4–BRIL protein (either WT or variant). A total of 20 injections were carried out, each delivering 2 μL of ligand solution, with a 120-s interval allowed for thermal equilibration after each addition.

The thermal response resulting from each injection was integrated, and the corresponding background signal was subtracted to obtain net binding enthalpies. The processed data were fitted using the Wiseman isotherm model, implemented through Origin software designed for ITC data analysis. Each titration experiment was independently repeated at least twice, and consistent results were obtained across replicates.

Pull-Down Assay.

The WT CCR4 protein and its truncated variants were purified following the same protocol employed for cryo-EM sample preparation. These purified proteins were then incubated with MFab fragments and anti-Flag M2 affinity resin (Sigma) at 4 °C for 2 h to facilitate affinity-based binding. Subsequently, the resin was washed three times using buffer B, and the bound complexes were eluted with 30 μL of buffer B supplemented with 0.1 mg/mL 3× Flag peptide. To each 10 μL of the eluate, protein loading buffer was added. After a brief centrifugation, the samples were analyzed by western blotting using anti-FLAG (MBL Life Science, M185-3L) and anti-His antibodies (Millipore Corp, 05-949). This experiment was conducted in triplicate, yielding consistent results across replicates. To assess the competition between CCL17 and MFab, a pull-down assay was performed in the presence of MFab at a molar ratio of CCR4:CCL17:MFab = 1:5:5. The assay was conducted as described above, and the resulting gel was stained with Coomassie Blue.
