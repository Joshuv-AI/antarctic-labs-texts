Comparison of high-intensity interval training protocol designs on accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O₂max: a network meta-analysis
Authors: Steffen Held, Ludwig Rappelt, Florian Micke, Tim Wiedenmann, Lars Donath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccumulated time ≥ 90% of maximal oxygen uptake (V̇O2max) during high-intensity interval training (HIIT) represents a key stimulus for aerobic adaptations. Thus, this network meta-analysis (NMA) aimed to compare different HIIT protocol structures with respect to accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O2max using direct and indirect evidence.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, and SPORTDiscus up to January 31st, 2026 for controlled HIIT studies reporting accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O₂max in healthy athletes. HIIT protocols were classified using predefined operational rules based on interval duration, work-to-rest structure, intensity pattern, and within-session progression. We conducted a frequentist NMA using standardized mean differences (SMD) within a random effects model, with even long intervals as the reference condition, and added local inconsistency and contribution diagnostics.ResultsNineteen publications contributed 20 study units, including 240 athletes (25.5 ± 5.3 years; V̇O₂max 60.5 ± 8.1 ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹). The network demonstrated substantial heterogeneity and significant inconsistency (I² = 69.2%, 95% CI 49.9% to 81.0%; total Q = 55.12, df = 17, p < 0.0001; between-design Q = 20.78, df = 6, p = 0.002). None of the HIIT approaches differed significantly from even long intervals in accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O₂max. Point estimates were highest for decreased-duration (SMD = 0.87, 95% CI -0.30 to 2.04) and varied-intensity protocols (SMD = 0.54, 95% CI -0.42 to 1.49), whereas 1:1 blocks showed the lowest point estimate (SMD = -0.68, 95% CI -1.40 to 0.04). P-score ranking favored decreased-duration (89.6%), followed by varied-intensity (76.7%) and decreased-intensity protocols (68.4%), while 1:1 blocks ranked lowest (2.8%).ConclusionNo HIIT protocol structure showed clear superiority for accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O₂max. Decreased-duration and varied-intensity protocols ranked highest, but these rankings were exploratory because all reference comparisons were statistically non-significant and the network showed substantial heterogeneity, significant inconsistency, and sparse evidence for several nodes. Protocol selection should therefore prioritize feasibility, athlete characteristics, and workload-matching considerations rather than ranking alone.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-026-01891-7.Keywords: HIT, Aerobic capacity, Performance

Introduction

Elite endurance athletes typically perform substantial volumes of endurance training, with most sessions performed at low intensity [1]. Nevertheless, athletes and coaches should include high-intensity interval training (HIIT) to induce the metabolic adaptations that support increases in maximal oxygen uptake (V̇O2max) [2]. HIIT consists of repeated bouts of high intensity exercise separated by recovery periods [2, 3]. The primary goal of this training methodology is to improve V̇O2max [3, 4], a crucial determinant of endurance performance and the upper limit of aerobic metabolism [5]. To increase V̇O2max in highly trained endurance athletes, it is important to accumulate prolonged time at intensities corresponding to a high percentage of maximal oxygen uptake during training sessions. [4, 6]. As a result, the efficacy of a HIIT session may be assessed by average oxygen uptake (V̇O2mean) or cumulative training time ≥ 90% V̇O2max [4, 7].

Recent longitudinal evidence supports the practical relevance of these V̇O2 based within session indices, showing that a higher fraction of V̇O2max achieved during interval training is associated with greater improvements in performance related outcomes and V̇O2max [8]. Moreover, V̇O2 based measures were related to more outcome variables and showed better session to session repeatability than heart rate based surrogates, suggesting that they may capture the adaptive stimulus more consistently [8]. The time ≥ 90% V̇O2max metric captures how much time athletes sustain near maximal oxygen uptake within a session, which is considered a key stimulus for central and peripheral adaptations that underpin improvements in V̇O2max. The potential for these adaptations is attributed to the substantial metabolic stimulus triggering both myocardial morphological changes that augment maximum cardiac stroke volume, and peripheral adaptations in skeletal muscle [4].

So far multiple HIIT approaches have been used to increase the cumulative training time ≥ 90% V̇O2max: (i) repeated short intervals interspaced with short rest periods were grouped together to several longer interval blocks (short interval blocks) [9–12]; (ii) short intervals with even work to rest ratio (1:1 blocks) [10, 13]; (iii) using decreasing intensity intervals by starting each interval with higher intensity and finish each interval with a slightly lower intensity [14, 15]; (iv) using intervals with varied intensity during each interval, i.e. using start and mid sprints [16]; (v) using intervals with decreasing durations towards the end of the HIIT session; i.e. the first intervals are characterized by longer durations compared to the last intervals [11, 12]; or traditional even paced (vi) short [10, 12] and (vii) long intervals [15, 17].

Although several pairwise meta-analytical comparisons revealed positive effects of HIIT on endurance related surrogate parameters [18, 19], no study has yet compared these approaches within a single overarching meta-analytical framework. Furthermore, because high-intensity interval training protocols vary across multiple dimensions, evidence from pairwise comparisons alone does not provide a sufficiently comprehensive basis for informed decisions by coaches, athletes, and practitioners. A network meta-analysis can address this limitation by integrating both direct and indirect evidence across multiple interventions [20]. Since a network meta-analysis enables simultaneous comparison of multiple HIIT protocol structures, we can evaluate the above-mentioned HIIT specific approaches within one coherent analytical framework. Additionally, a NMA approach enables a treatment ranking based on relative effectiveness [21].

Against this background, the present systematic review and NMA aimed to examine and compare the effects of different HIIT approaches on cumulative time ≥ 90% V̇O2max by integrating direct and indirect evidence within a single network framework. We hypothesized that varied intensity intervals and short interval blocks would increase cumulative time ≥ 90% V̇O2max compared with traditional even paced high-intensity interval training. The findings may help athletes and coaches select evidence-based strategies to optimize high-intensity interval training sessions by maximizing time spent ≥ 90% V̇O2max.

Methods

Search and screening procedures

This network-analytical review was carried out in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Network Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-NMA) guidelines [22] and was registered under PROSPERO CRD42023391814. Two researchers (SH and FM) independently conducted literature search and study selection. PubMed, Web of Science, and SPORTDiscus were searched up to January 31st, 2026. Key search terms were combined with Boolean operators across three search levels (Table 1). A database-specific search log including search dates, search strings, and records retrieved from each database is provided as Supplementary Table S1. Additionally, cited articles were traced backwards and pertinent primary articles and reviews were searched manually.

Search strategy

Duplicate entries were discarded, and the residual studies were subjected to manual evaluation. The selection process was implemented progressively via screening (1) titles (2), abstracts, and (3) full texts of potentially relevant articles. The selection was guided by the PICOS framework [population (P), intervention (I), comparators (C), main outcome (O), and study design (S)]. Full-text article published in English in a peer-reviewed journal; Participants were healthy athletes ≥ 18 years (P), without any cognitive, neurological, orthopedic and/or cardiac conditions that could affect physical testing and training; acute endurance based (cycling, running, swimming, rowing or canoe/kayak) HIIT interventions (I); active control condition(s) that receive different HIIT intervention served as a comparator (C); accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O2max served as outcome (O); Prospective two- or multi-armed controlled testing (S); The exclusion criteria were: No adequate control conditions, which made integration into the network impossible.

Assessment of methodological quality of the studies

The methodological quality (including risk of bias) of the included studies was rated via the risk of bias, of the incorporated studies using the PEDro (Physiotherapy Evidence Database) scale [23]. The PEDro scale is comprised of 11 binary (yes or no) items, with items 2–9 assessing randomization and internal validity and items 10–11 determining the presence of statistically reproducible results. Item 1 pertains to external validity but is not included in the calculation of the total PEDro score. Studies achieving a PEDro score of 6 or above on a scale of 0 to 10 [23] are considered high quality.

Data extraction

Necessary data for computing effect sizes were extracted using a standardized extraction spreadsheet adapted from the Cochrane Collaboration [24]. The extracted data included means, standard deviations of test scores, and the number of participants evaluated in condition. If these specific and variability measures were not included in the full-text article, either the means and combined within-group standard deviations of change scores were inputted into an electronic spreadsheet, or the authors were contacted to request the missing values up to three times. If studies only presented means and standard deviations graphically, the WebPlotDigitizer Version 4 (Free Software Foundation, Boston, MA, USA) was utilized to extract the means and standard deviations [25]. In addition to these measures, pertinent study details such as authorship, publication year, intervention data (interval type, duration, rest period, set count), control condition, and PEDro scale scores were also documented. Protocol classification used mutually exclusive operational categories consistent with Table 2. The categories were defined so that each interval structure could be allocated to only one node. Protocols with systematic within-session reductions in interval duration were classified as decreased-duration; protocols with systematic within-interval reductions in intensity were classified as decreased-intensity; protocols with repeated within-interval intensity fluctuations were classified as varied-intensity; protocols with equal work and recovery durations of 30 to 120 s were classified as 1:1 blocks; repeated bouts of ≤ 1 min with recovery shorter than work and organized into longer blocks were classified as short interval blocks; remaining even-paced intervals were classified by work interval duration as even short (1 to < 3 min) or even long (≥ 3 min). This rule-based structure prevented the same interval type from being assigned to more than one category while preserving the clinically relevant differences between protocol designs.

Statistical analysis

The standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals was computed for all interventions. SMDs were calculated by dividing differences in means by the pooled standard deviations (trivial: SMD < 0.2, small: 0.2 ≤ SMD < 0.5, moderate: 0.5 ≤ SMD < 0.8, large: SMD ≥ 0.8) [37]. We fitted a frequentist random effects network meta-analysis [20, 38], using even long intervals as the reference condition because this approach was the most frequently used traditional HIIT comparator [15, 17]. Interventions were ranked using P-scores, which are analogous to SUCRA values in Bayesian NMA [20, 21]. We decomposed Q-statistics into within-design heterogeneity and between-design inconsistency and quantified heterogeneity using I² [39]. In response to the observed global inconsistency, we additionally generated a complete league table, local inconsistency diagnostics using node-splitting, direct and indirect estimates, and a contribution matrix. Certainty of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach, which provides a structured framework for rating confidence in effect estimates by considering methodological limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias [40]. For the network meta-analysis, GRADE was applied to comparisons against even long intervals using the GRADE Working Group approach for treatment effect estimates from network meta-analysis [41]. Small-study effects were explored using a comparison-adjusted funnel plot and an exploratory Egger regression test for funnel plot asymmetry [42]. For the Egger regression, protocols were ordered a priori from 1:1 blocks, even short intervals, short interval blocks, even long intervals, decreased-intensity, varied-intensity, to decreased-duration. Given the small number of studies and substantial heterogeneity, funnel plot asymmetry and Egger regression were interpreted cautiously. All computations and illustrative figures were generated using R software and the netmeta package [43].

Results

Study characteristics

After screening and study selection 19 studies were included in this network meta-analytical approach (Fig. 1). The full list of selected studies, with corresponding study details, are displayed in Table 3. Overall, 240 healthy athletes were examined, consisting of 42 females and 198 males. Included trials enrolled on average 12 ± 3 participants per study (range from 8 to 20) with an average age of 25.5 ± 5.3 years (range from 19.0 to 41.0 years). Mean VO2max was 60.5 ± 8.1 ml/min/kg (range from 44.0 to 73.9 ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹). The average study quality was high as indicated by a PEDro score of 7.1 ± 0.4 (range from 6 to 8; Table 3). PEDro item-level ratings are reported in Supplementary Table S2 in addition to total scores. Apart from 3 three-armed study designs [11, 12], all other studies employed a two-armed study design [9, 13, 14, 16, 17, 26–36], if only the relevant conditions for this network meta-analytical approach are considered. All included studies used a cross-over design in which participants completed all relevant conditions. For the network model, Beltrami et al. [14], (Table 3) contributed separate cycling and running strata because these strata differed by exercise modality and participant group. Accordingly, the review included 19 publications, whereas the NMA model included 20 study units.

Intervention outcomes

The network meta-analysis included 19 studies contributing 26 pairwise effect sizes across seven training modalities (Fig. 2). The most frequent direct comparison was even long vs. decreased-intensity (k = 4) and short interval blocks vs. 1:1 blocks (k = 4).

Network plot

The NMA model revealed substantial heterogeneity and significant network inconsistency. The estimated between-study variance was τ² = 0.356 (τ = 0.596), and overall heterogeneity was high (I² = 69.2%, 95% CI 49.9% to 81.0%). The global Q test indicated significant heterogeneity within the network (Q = 55.12, df = 17, p < 0.0001). Decomposition of heterogeneity demonstrated significant within-design heterogeneity (Q = 34.34, df = 11, p = 0.0003) and significant between-design inconsistency (Q = 20.78, df = 6, p = 0.002), indicating that effect estimates varied across study designs beyond random variation. Node-splitting indicated significant local direct-indirect inconsistency for 1:1 blocks versus even long intervals (p = 0.0147) and short interval blocks versus even long intervals (p = 0.0132). The direct and indirect estimates are summarized in Supplementary Table S4, the complete league table in Supplementary Table S6, and the contribution matrix in Supplementary Table S7.

Transitivity was interpreted cautiously. The included nodes differed in modality, training status, sex distribution, baseline V̇O₂max, recovery prescription, intensity anchoring, and whether protocols were time- or workload-matched. These factors may modify accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O₂max and are plausible contributors to the observed heterogeneity and inconsistency. A structured effect-modifier summary is provided in Supplementary Table S3. Direct and indirect evidence is summarized in Supplementary Table S4, and GRADE-NMA certainty ratings are summarized in Supplementary Table S5.

Using even long intervals as the reference condition, none of the alternative interval configurations showed statistically significant differences in acute performance outcomes. Point estimates indicated a lower effect for 1:1 blocks (SMD = − 0.68, 95% CI − 1.40 to 0.04, p = 0.063), while decreased duration, decreased intensity, even short intervals, short interval blocks, and varied intensity protocols all demonstrated wide confidence intervals crossing zero, reflecting substantial uncertainty in comparative effectiveness. Thus, the forest plot (Fig. 3) illustrates wide confidence intervals across all comparisons, indicating substantial uncertainty around point estimates and no clear superiority of any protocol over even long intervals.

Forest plots

Ranking based on P scores (Fig. 4) suggested that decreased-duration protocols ranked highest (P score = 89.6%), followed by varied-intensity (76.7%) and decreased-intensity protocols (68.4%). Even long intervals ranked in the mid range (45.2%), followed by short interval blocks (35.1%) and even short intervals (32.2%), whereas 1:1 blocks ranked lowest (2.8%). Because no reference comparison reached statistical significance and the network showed significant inconsistency, these rankings should be interpreted as exploratory indicators rather than evidence of superiority.

P-score rankings

Visual inspection of the network funnel plot (Fig. 5) did not indicate a clear pattern of asymmetry. However, the exploratory Egger regression test for comparison-adjusted funnel plot asymmetry was statistically significant (bias estimate = 1.73, SE = 0.83, t = 2.09, df = 24, p = 0.0469), indicating that small-study effects could not be ruled out. Because the network included few studies, sparse comparisons, and substantial heterogeneity, we interpret this test as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Funnel plot

GRADE-NMA certainty was rated as very low for all comparisons against even long intervals (Supplementary Table S5). Downgrading was driven by significant global inconsistency, significant local direct-indirect inconsistency for central comparisons, sparse direct evidence for several nodes, wide confidence intervals, and the inability to rule out small-study effects.

Discussion

This review provides the first network meta-analysis that compares different HIIT protocols with respect to accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O2max using direct and indirect evidence. Overall, the network did not identify statistically significant differences from the even-long reference condition. Some non-reference network contrasts excluded zero, but these estimates were interpreted cautiously because of significant inconsistency and very low certainty. Point estimates suggested potentially higher accumulated time for decreased duration and for varied intensity protocols, whereas 1:1 blocks tended to show lower accumulated time. However, confidence intervals were wide and overlapped across all comparisons, indicating substantial uncertainty around these estimates. In addition, the network showed considerable heterogeneity and statistically significant inconsistency, which further limits confidence in protocol specific effect estimates and rankings. Therefore, we interpret the apparent hierarchy from point estimates and P score ranking as hypothesis generating rather than conclusive evidence.

In order to maximize exercise time ≥ 90% V̇O2max [4, 7], a speed of 90 to 100% of maximal aerobic speed was previously recommended [44]. However, continuous work at this maximal aerobic speed can only be sustained for about six minutes in well-trained endurance athletes [45]. Hence, there is an ongoing search for methods to design HIIT sessions that maximize the accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O2max. This can be achieved by carefully balancing work and recovery durations and intensities [3]. Thereby, our network meta-analysis did not identify statistically significant differences from the even-long reference condition, and the corresponding effect estimates were characterized by wide, overlapping confidence intervals and substantial heterogeneity and inconsistency. Accordingly, our results revealed point estimates consistent with broadly similar effects for the even long [9, 13–17], even short [10–12] and short interval block [9–12] approach. In contrast, the decreased- [14, 15, 17] and varied-intensity [15, 16] approach revealed point estimates suggesting small positive effects regarding the accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O2max. These intensity depending approaches are primary intended to target the priming effect [14, 16, 46]. Thereby, a fast-start strategy might speed up the V̇O2 kinetics [46, 47]. Furthermore, Jones and co-authors [48] found that a quicker rise in V̇O2 is associated with a fast start compared to a slower one, indicating a larger V̇O2 response in the decreased- and varied-intensity approach. This concept aligns with the proposition that the absolute rate at which V̇O2 rises at the onset of exercise is positively correlated with the difference between the current and required steady-state V̇O2 in the working muscle [49]. Therefore, the larger discrepancy between the current and required V̇O2 in the working muscles during the start of work intervals in the decreased- and varied-intensity approaches are likely to expedite the V̇O2 response. Furthermore, additional factors may contribute to the higher mean V̇O2 in the decreased- and varied-intensity approach compared to the traditional even paced approaches in later intervals: This includes slower V̇O2 kinetics of the initially recruited type II fibers, reduced contractile efficiency, and a gradual increase in O2 demand from the recovery processes in fatigued fibers [49]. These findings suggest that starting intervals with higher intensity could be an effective strategy for maximizing V̇O2 responses and, in turn, enhancing the benefits of HIIT sessions [14, 15, 17], although the present network meta-analytical evidence remains inconclusive given the observed uncertainty, heterogeneity, and inconsistency.

Based on our findings, the decreased duration approach [11, 12] showed the highest point estimate and the highest P score ranking, but the network meta-analysis did not identify statistically significant differences and confidence intervals overlapped across protocols. Therefore, the decreased duration approach [11, 12] should be considered a promising configuration that warrants further testing, rather than a clearly superior option compared with decreased intensity or varied intensity approaches. Apart from the above mentioned aspects, the observed beneficial effects of the decreased-duration approach [11, 12] might be linked to the following mechanisms: High-intensity exercising results in reduced muscle phosphocreatine concentration [50, 51], making it challenging to sustain exercise over a prolonged duration [52, 53]. Since muscle oxygen consumption is linked to the reduction in muscle phosphocreatine concentration [51, 54], the faster the phosphocreatine concentration depletes, the quicker the V̇O2 rises. Given this correlation, a HIIT protocol aiming to stimulate V̇O2max could initiate with longer work intervals (2–4 min) and gradually transition to shorter intervals as the session progresses [11]. This decreased-duration approach rapidly elevates V̇O2, kick-starting the system. As the participant nears exhaustion, the transition to shorter intervals can help to prolong the exercise duration. In such a scenario, the recovery ratio is swift, necessitating only a few seconds to ensure sufficient recovery while simultaneously preventing V̇O2 from dropping too significantly [11]. However, it should be noted that, in contrast to all other comparisons [9, 10, 13–17], the decreased-duration approach was not time-matched [11, 12]. Instead, the duration of each decreased-duration HIIT session was significantly longer than the corresponding comparison [11, 12]. However, the resulting additional accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O2max for the decreased-duration approach was significantly higher than the additional total duration of these decreased-duration HIIT session [11, 12]. Therefore, it can be hypothesized that the observed positive effects would persist in a time-matched comparison. Nevertheless, future research should aim for a time-matched, and if possible, a work-matched (time * load) comparison.

The point estimates suggesting negative effects observed with the 1:1 block approach could potentially be explained by the structure of the rest-to-work ratio in this approach. In a 1:1 work-to-rest ratio, the recovery periods are equal in length to the periods of high-intensity activity [10, 13]. Consequently, the relatively long resting periods may allow for a more substantial decrease in oxygen consumption (V̇O2) between intervals. When the next high-intensity interval commences, it could take a significantly longer time for V̇O2 to rise back to 90% V̇O2max. This delay in response might lead to less accumulated time at or above 90% V̇O2max throughout the session. Therefore, the 1:1 blocks approach [10, 13] could potentially undermine the desired HIIT objective of maximizing the accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O2max [4, 7], although our network meta-analysis did not identify statistically significant differences between protocols and the confidence intervals were wide and overlapping.

This study has several important limitations. First, conclusions for some interventions rely on limited direct evidence, particularly varied-intensity and decreased-duration protocols, which were each represented by only two studies. Second, the network showed substantial heterogeneity and statistically significant inconsistency, limiting confidence in indirect comparisons, treatment rankings, and protocol-specific estimates. Potential sources include differences in training status, exercise modality, sex distribution, baseline V̇O₂max, intensity prescription, recovery type, workload or time matching, protocol duration, and methods used to quantify accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O₂max. Third, PEDro total scores provide only a summary of methodological quality; item-level risk-of-bias information and certainty of evidence should be considered when interpreting the findings. Fourth, GRADE-NMA certainty was rated as very low for all reference comparisons because the network showed global and local inconsistency, sparse direct evidence for several nodes, wide confidence intervals, and potential small-study effects based on the exploratory Egger regression. Overall, the network provides a structured quantitative synthesis, but the certainty of protocol rankings remains limited.

Practical applications should be framed cautiously. The present findings do not support a definitive recommendation that one HIIT protocol structure is superior for maximizing accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O₂max. Coaches and athletes may consider decreased-duration or varied-intensity structures as promising options for further testing, but these choices should account for feasibility, athlete preference, modality, recovery demands, and whether the session is time- or workload-matched. The lower ranking of 1:1 blocks may reflect longer recovery periods that allow V̇O₂ to fall between work bouts, but this interpretation remains uncertain because confidence intervals were wide and the network showed significant inconsistency. Therefore, the present results are best used to guide hypothesis generation and protocol selection for future controlled studies rather than to prescribe a single optimal HIIT format.

In summary, the current network meta-analysis did not identify statistically significant differences from the even-long reference condition for accumulated time ≥ 90% V̇O₂max. Decreased-duration, varied-intensity, and decreased-intensity protocols ranked highest, whereas 1:1 blocks ranked lowest, but these patterns should be interpreted as hypothesis generating because confidence intervals were wide, several nodes were sparsely informed, and the network showed substantial heterogeneity and statistically significant inconsistency.
