Navigating challenges in healthcare education: perspectives from ethnic minority students
Authors: Mei Fung Florence Wong, Jasvir Kaur Jessie Dhaliwal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe enrollment of ethnic minority students in healthcare professional programmes has been increasing. However, current curricula are primarily designed for students from the majority culture, potentially overlooking the unique learning challenges faced by ethnic minority students. This study aims to explore these challenges to better understand their learning experience.MethodsThis phenomenological qualitative study utilized face-to-face focus group interviews with 16 ethnic minority students enrolled in healthcare programmes at tertiary professional training institutions.ResultsFour key themes emerged (1) Language barriers resulting in heavy workload and stress, (2) Restriction in social network and interactions, (3) Stereotypes towards ethnic minorities, and (4) Obstacles related to maintaining religious faith. These themes highlight significant barriers that hinder learning engagement and social integration, adversely impacting academic and clinical performance.ConclusionThe findings highlight the imperative for institutional reforms to create an equitable learning environment. Four strategies include implementing targeted language support programmes to alleviate academic pressures, fostering peer mentorship initiatives to enhance social integration, incorporating anti-bias training to address stereotypes, and developing faith-sensitive policies and facilities. These evidence-based interventions not only support ethnic minority students’ success but also cultivate cultural competence across healthcare education, ultimately strengthening the inclusivity of the profession.

Introduction

The global landscape is witnessing a remarkable demographic transformation as ethnic minority populations burgeon in multicultural centers worldwide. In Hong Kong, ethnic minorities, including Filipinos, Indonesians, Indians, and Pakistanis [1], account for 8.4% of the population, mirroring global trends observed in metropolitan hubs like London (40%) [2] and New York (37%) [3], Toronto (47%) [4], and Sydney (45%) [5]. This pattern aligns with United Nations-reported urbanization trends [6], revealing how migration reshapes educational demographics globally. This demographic shift coincides with growing ethnic minority enrollment in healthcare fields, a trend attributed to both the profession’s social mobility potential and their alignment with multilingual students’ cultural capital [7–11]. Over the past decade, ethnic minority participation in Hong Kong’s post-secondary education surged from 21.0 to 36.3% [7, 8], with nursing and allied health programmes emerging as pathways for upward mobility despite systemic barriers.

Ethnic minority students encounter multifaceted challenges, as evidenced by Hong Kong studies documenting how language barriers (such as limited Cantonese proficiency) exacerbate clinical assessment anxiety and cultural isolation [8, 9]. Structural factors like concentrated poverty, underfunded schools in minority neighborhoods, and clinically untested language supports compound these issues [9, 12–14]. Moreover, the language barrier, particularly the limited proficiency in Chinese, poses a significant impediment to accessing educational and career pathways, exacerbating disparities in opportunities and outcomes [14–17].

A recent study focusing on profound challenges faced by ethnic minority nursing students, with 68% experienced anxiety during clinical assessments due to language barriers and 52% experiencing cultural isolation from peers and instructors [15]. These findings not only reveal the limitations of current support systems but also demonstrate an urgent need for culturally responsive interventions. Complementary research on ethnic minority undergraduate nursing students further elucidates the complex interplay of cultural, emotional, and social factors that hinder academic and professional development, reinforcing the call for targeted support services [17]. Despite existing language enhancement initiatives, significant gaps remain in addressing the unique needs of these students at tertiary level, exacerbating academic stress and creating additional barriers to professional success [18–20].

The challenges confronting ethnic minority students in health and social care programmes are multifaceted and profoundly impactful, affecting both academic trajectories and career prospects [21–23]. To promote student well-being and educational attainment, institutions must develop innovative strategies, comprehensive support measures that empower these individuals to throughout their educational endeavors.

Guided by Pickford’s framework [24] which posits that equitable outcomes require policy, pedagogy, and community adaptations for achieving equitable outcomes. Our qualitative approach uniquely identifies practical institutional strategies, including simulated clinical learning environments and peer mentoring programmes, designed to mitigate existing challenges.

By systematically addressing cultural, linguistic, and socioeconomic disparities, educational institutions and policymakers can foster truly inclusive environments where ethnic minority students can thrive academically. This study employs qualitative methods to examine these challenges and identify actionable strategies for creating a more equitable healthcare education system. Centering the lived experiences of minority students, we move beyond generic support mechanisms to develop and test scalable interventions tailored to Hong Kong’s multilingual healthcare context. Our findings will provide evidence-based policy recommendations aligned with Pickford’s theoretical framework [24], offering actionable insights into systemic improvement.

This phenomenological study explores the lived experiences of ethnic minority students in their learning journey, focusing on their perceived challenges, emotional and academic dimensions, and interpretations of support structures within their educational environment.

Conceptual framework

The study employed Pickford’s conceptual framework [24] as an analytical tool to systematically examine the lived educational experiences of ethnic minority students. The three interdependent dimensions: academic engagement (classroom learning), emotional engagement (personal connections), and transactional engagement (institutional interactions) interact with six key conditions: development (skill-building opportunities), challenge (academic rigor), inclusion (equitable participation), support (mentorship systems), organization (resource distribution), and responsiveness (feedback mechanisms). Together, these elements provide a structured yet flexible approach to understanding how systemic factors shape student experiences.

Pickford’s conceptual framework offers particular value for capturing the multifaceted nature of educational challenges faced by ethnic minority populations. The academic dimension helps analyze discipline-specific barriers, while the emotional dimension illuminates issues of belonging and self-efficacy. Transactional engagement reveals how daily institutional interactions may facilitate or hinder progress. These dimensions are further contextualized through the six conditions, which serve as analytical touchpoints for identifying both challenges and potential areas for institutional growth.

This study adapts Pickford’s framework as a phenomenological tool to uncover how students themselves perceive and interpret their educational journeys. For instance, narratives about clinical placement difficulties might reveal gaps in both transactional engagement (supervisor communication) and organizational conditions (resource allocation for language support). Similarly, discussions of identity and values will be examined through the lens of emotional engagement and inclusion conditions.

The framework’s strength lies in its ability to connect individual experiences to broader institutional patterns without imposing predetermined categories. By maintaining this analytical flexibility, the study remains grounded in student perspectives while still providing structured insights that can inform future policy and practice discussions. This approach ensures findings emerge organically from participant narratives while being theoretically informed by Pickford’s comprehensive model of student engagement.

Method

Study design

This qualitative phenomenological study utilized focus group interviews to explore the lived educational experiences of ethnic minority students enrolled in healthcare professional programmes in Hong Kong. The phenomenological approach was particularly suited to investigating the essence of participants’ experiences and the meanings they attribute to their challenges within healthcare education. By focusing on the students’ subjective perspectives, this design allowed for an in-depth understanding of how they navigate their academic environments.

Participants and setting

Participants were recruited through purposive sampling from a major healthcare training institution offering diverse programmes including nursing, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and medical health sciences. The sample of 16 participants represented approximately 12% of the total ethnic minority student population across these programmes, exceeding the citywide demographic representation of ethnic minorities at 8.4% [1]. Careful consideration was given to ensuring variation across programme types, years of study, and ethnic backgrounds to capture a broad range of perspectives while maintaining the depth characteristic of phenomenological research. This sampling strategy was aligned with established qualitative methodology standards that emphasize information-rich cases over statistical representation [25, 26].

Data collection

Data collection followed rigorous protocols approved by the institutional ethics review board (CRG2022/10). After obtaining informed consent, participants completed brief demographic surveys before engaging in semi-structured focus group interviews. Six focus groups of 5–6 participants each were conducted by a bilingual moderator fluent in both Cantonese and English. Groups were homogenous by academic level but heterogeneous in ethnic/religious backgrounds to balance shared context with diverse perspectives [26]. Sessions lasted 45–60 min and were held in private settings to ensure confidentiality. Before the interview, participants completed a demographic survey and engaged in informal conversation to establish rapport. The interviews followed an interview guide. Interviews began with an open-ended prompt asking participants to describe challenges in their healthcare education, followed by guided questions from a structured protocol. All sessions were audio-recorded and supplemented with detailed field notes documenting nonverbal cues and contextual observations.

Several measures were implemented to ensure methodological rigor and trustworthiness. The principal investigator (PI), drawing on over ten years of clinical and educational experience, employed bracketing techniques through reflective journaling to identify and set aside potential preconceptions. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved, determined through ongoing analysis where no new themes emerged from subsequent interviews. Member checking procedures allowed participants to verify the accuracy of transcript interpretations, while peer debriefing with research colleagues provided additional validation of emerging findings. An audit trail documented all analytical decisions to enhance transparency and reproducibility.

Data analysis

The analysis process began with verbatim transcription of all interview recordings by a research assistant (RA), followed by careful review by the PI to ensure accuracy. We employed Colaizzi’s phenomenological method [27] to systematically analyze the data, as this approach provides a rigorous framework for uncovering the essential structure of lived experiences. The method’s seven-step process guided our team in moving from raw data to comprehensive understanding: we first immersed ourselves in each transcript to identify significant statements, extracted and interpreted these statements to derive meanings, then organized these meanings into thematic clusters that captured the core of participants’ experiences. Through iterative analysis, we developed a synthesized description of the fundamental phenomenon in which the challenges ethnic minority students face in healthcare education that remained firmly grounded in the participants’ own words and perspectives [28].

To enhance the reliability of our findings, two independent reviewers (PI and RA) conducted the initial coding and theme identification separately. The team then held three consensus meetings to compare interpretations, resolve discrepancies through discussion, and finalize the thematic structure. This collaborative approach not only minimized individual bias but also enriched our understanding through multiple perspectives on the data. The final step involved returning to participants for validation, ensuring our interpretations accurately reflected their lived experiences.

Rigor and trustworthiness

The study employed multiple strategies to establish and maintain trustworthiness throughout the research process. Purposive sampling ensured we captured diverse perspectives across different healthcare programmes, academic levels, and ethnic backgrounds, providing a rich foundation for understanding the varied experiences of ethnic minority students. We implemented triangulation method by combining data from focus group transcripts, field notes, and member checking sessions, allowing for comprehensive cross-verification of emerging findings.

The research team maintained rigorous reflexivity through bracketing practices, with the PI documenting and setting aside preconceptions prior to and during data collection and analysis. Member checking served as a crucial validation step, as participants reviewed and confirmed our interpretations of their experiences. The audit trail documenting all analytical decisions, along with peer debriefing sessions with colleagues not involved in the study, provided additional safeguards against researcher bias while enhancing the credibility of our findings.

These methodological choices collectively ensured that our phenomenological analysis remained both systematic and true to participants’ lived experiences, while the transparent documentation of our processes allows for evaluation of the study’s trustworthiness [29]. The resulting understanding of ethnic minority students’ educational challenges emerges from this careful balance of rigorous methodology and authentic engagement with participant perspectives.

Results

A total of six focus group interviews with 16 student participants was conducted between January to August 2024, including two males and 14 females. The participants comprised 12% of the ethnic minority student cohort (n = 135) in the institution’s healthcare programmes, reflecting the broader demographic trends in Hong Kong. They were from Years one to four from various healthcare professional programmes, including nursing (62.5%), medical health sciences (31.25%), and physiotherapy (6.25%). Introduction of the participants

The study identified four main themes related to learning challenges faced by the student participants in their healthcare professional studies, including: (1) Language barriers resulting in heavy workload and stress, (2) Restriction in social network and interactions, (3) Stereotypes towards ethnic minorities, and (4) Obstacles related to maintaining religious faith.

Theme 1. Language barriers resulting in heavy workload and stress

Ethnic minority students in healthcare professional studies face significant challenges due to the language barrier, which hampers their academic performance. They struggle to be engaged in learning, devoting extra time to match the course materials. Student #7 said, “…sometimes you get lost and cannot catch up.” Similarly, student #9 expressed, “…my first year was quite bad…and I had a hard time learning in Chinese… the language barrier really scares me.” Student #6 highlighted another aspect of the language barrier, which is the need to communicate with patients in Chinese. She said, “I am currently not as fluent in Chinese, and it’s obviously expected that we speak to our patients in Chinese……just adds another level of difficulty.”

Recognizing the importance of mastering proficient in Chinese language, students face a dual challenge: the pressure to excel academically combined with the additional burden caused by language barriers, which indirectly increases their workload. For instance, encounters with faculty who dismissed English preferences (e.g., insisting on Chinese despite students’ struggles) not only induced stress but also created additional labor that students reported spending extra time deciphering lectures or redoing assignments due to misunderstandings. Student #9 emphasized her efforts to master Chinese for practicums but described falling behind academically because language gaps doubled her study time. Similarly, Student #11 recounted an incident where a teacher’s response to her English-speaking preference resulted in an uncomfortable situation, with the teacher displaying annoyance and insisting on English for the lesson. Student #13 described a comparable encounter “A teacher asked the same thing in the lecture. When I told her that I mainly spoke English, she continued to speak Chinese in the lecture.” indicating their English-speaking preference, the teacher continued delivering the lecture in Chinese. Together, these experiences illustrate how language barriers extended workload pressures, as students compensated for linguistic disconnects through unpaid emotional and logistical labor.

As a result, most ethnic minority students resort to self-study and find lectures conducted in Chinese to be a waste of time. A participant from the Medical Health Sciences programme preferred self-study over lectures where the lecturer only read every word on each slide. They would read textbooks and surf the internet for related information. Student #7 acknowledged the importance of learning Chinese to prepare for her future work but hoped to learn professional terms in Chinese that would help her in her future career. Student #8 echoed this sentiment, saying, “Yes, we also need to learn more healthcare-related terminologies.”

Theme 2. Restriction in social networks and interactions

Ethnic minority students in healthcare professional studies often feel isolated or alienated due to racial and cultural differences from local students, especially during their first impressions. Language difference is the primary reason they find it challenging to approach other local students.

One student participant (#3) shared her experience, saying, “I was sitting in the lecture hall, and all students were looking at me. I was very embarrassed and just kept my face down.” Another student participant (#4) echoed this sentiment, saying, “Yes, after I entered the hall, I looked for another student like me. I found you (another student participant sitting beside her) so I was very happy.”

Some ethnic minority students in their programmes are required to speak Chinese as they need to provide care and communicate with people in Chinese while working in clinical settings. Group projects are commonly used to develop students’ collaborative skills and abilities to prepare their clinical practice. However, this format is usually challenging for ethnic minority students. Student #1 expressed feeling singled out, saying, “I feel so singled out…everyone is just so scared to talk to me because they (local students) will have to speak English…” Student #8 recalled that most classmates were reluctant to approach her when students were asked to form groups by themselves.

Language difficulties can make it intimidating to participate in class discussions or group projects. Student #9 faced this problem in group discussions, and classmates were not willing to help her translate to English as their English proficiency may not be good. Student #3 explained, “They (local students) will form a WhatsApp group and discuss the project. They will be aware of using English. However, they will change back to Chinese…I cannot understand all the Chinese because they may speak some slangs..” Moreover, most students find challenging to engage in activities in Chinese communities. Student #1 expressed, “Not all activities are inclusive for these specific students. Even if I join, I will be left out or do nothing there.”

Theme 3. Stereotypes towards ethnic minorities

Several ethnic minority students reported encountering stereotypes associated their backgrounds with assumptions about academic capability and professional suitability. Participants’ perceptions reflected broader societal biases rather than individual circumstances. For example, Student #2 shared, “The teacher assumed all mistakes were because I am an ethnic minority student, even when others made the same errors.” Student #7 described being reprimanded for speaking English to a simulation patient, with the instructor implying this compromised clinical competence. These narratives reveal how societal prejudices, particularly those conflating language proficiency or cultural background with ability, can permeate educational environments. Importantly, participants emphasized these experiences stemmed from others’ perceptions rather than objective assessments of their capabilities. While existing literature confirms that similar stereotypes operate systemically in healthcare education and academic environments [30, 31], we stress these accounts represent specific experiences rather than universal patterns among ethnic minority students. The citations are provided to contextualize these experiences within broader documented patterns of bias, not to suggest these particular students experienced the exact forms of mistreatment described in the referenced studies.

Theme 4. Obstacles related to maintaining religious faith

Subtheme 1: Avoiding physical interaction in mixed genders

Ethnic minority students who follow religious regulations face difficulties in laboratory sessions due to religious boundaries. Female students do not interact with male students, and mixed-gender touching is forbidden in their religion. However, such restrictions do not apply to clinical practice due to necessity and professional context. This highlights the importance of respecting religious and cultural differences and accommodating them where possible. Student #6 expressed the difficulties encountered during laboratory sessions due to religious boundaries. She said, “We are forbidden to have mixed-gender touch in our religion.” She needed special arrangements for laboratory skill practice to comply with her religious regulations. Student #14 explained, “We don’t talk to boys or have male friends. It’s more of a religious thing.” Student #9 stated, “We hope that the school can provide a place for prayer and allow students to wear their traditional clothes.

Subtheme 2: food restrictions limit social interactions

Muslim students face food restrictions and need access to halal food, which is challenging to find on or near campus. This can limit social interactions during mealtime with their classmates, adding to their social isolation. Student #5 shared their experience, stating, “When my friends and I go out to eat, I have to let them know I can’t eat certain foods. That’s why I tend not to eat out with them.”

Subtheme 3: faith practice in praying

Muslim students need to pray five times a day, but finding a quiet place for prayer can be challenging. It is crucial to respect and accommodate religious practices and attire, such as prayer and traditional clothing, for Muslim students. A student participant (#5) emphasized the importance of prayers and stated, “I have to inform my teacher in advance as I need to excuse myself from the lesson for praying. I sometimes go home to pray if I cannot find a quiet place.” Traditional attire, such as hijab and jilbab, is an essential part of Islam. Student #12 stated, “We have to wear this headscarf (she is showing the scarf she is wearing), even in clinical settings.” Student #15 expressed her concern about the allowance of wearing the hijab for her practicum and future work in the hospital. However, she believed that there should be a policy to allow this attire, as some ethnic minority nurses or nurse students are practicing there. Student #9 emphasized the need for a designated prayer room on campus to accommodate Muslim students’ religious practices.

Discussion

This study illuminates the multifaceted challenges faced by ethnic minority students in healthcare education, guided by Pickford’s conceptual framework of academic, emotional, and transactional engagement [24]. This study reveals how systemic barriers across these dimensions create unique obstacles for these students, which are encapsulated in four interconnected themes emerged from their lived experiences: (1) Language barriers resulting in heavy workload and stress, (2) Restriction in social network and interactions, (3) Stereotypes towards ethnic minorities, and (4) Obstacles related to maintaining religious faith. These findings not only confirm the applicability to diverse student populations but also reveal critical gaps in its current formulation. These barriers significantly impact students’ academic performance and career trajectories of ethnic minority students, emphasizing the critical need for tailored support and the cultivation of more inclusive educational environments [32, 33]. Summary of identified themes and subthemes

According to Pickford’s framework [24], it becomes evident that language barriers fundamentally disrupt academic engagement by simultaneously overwhelming the ‘challenge’ condition and undermining the ‘development’ conditions within the framework [24]. Students face a mismatch between curriculum delivery and their linguistic backgrounds, creating unsustainable workloads that strain the ideal balance of challenge and support within the framework. For instance, students like Student #9, who struggled with Chinese proficiency, reported dedicating triple the study time compared to peers, illustrating how linguistic challenges increase workload and hinder learning. Similarly, Student #7’s clinical reprimand for using English exemplifies how linguistic hierarchies distort professional socialization. Further, clinical communication struggles (Student #6) extend beyond classroom settings, affecting the transactional engagement required for professional socialization and highlighting how language barriers can distort engagement opportunities in healthcare education. These experiences align with recent literature indicating that language policies neglecting cultural contexts may inadvertently heighten stress and hinder learning [34, 35]. Therefore, governmental policies should prioritize remedial language teaching for ethnic minority students and continuously evaluate the effectiveness of these interventions [36–38]. Future interventions could integrate language support with stress strategies, such as culturally adapted mindfulness approaches shown effective in recent studies [39, 40].

In addition to linguistic challenges, disparities in social networks and interactions emerge as significant barriers. Racial and cultural disparities often lead to social exclusion, which impacts both academic performance and overall well-being. Such exclusion particularly undermines transactional engagement, especially in the ‘inclusion’ and ‘organization’ conditions of Pickford’s framework. For example, students like Student #3, who felt excluded from peer groups, and Student #8, who was sidelined from clinical teams, missed vital opportunities for collaborative learning—an essential component of professional development [24]. These findings extend current understanding by revealing how healthcare education’s social structures often favor culturally homogeneous groups, creating structural barriers to inclusion. Recent research highlights disparities in clerkship grading linked to ethnic differences, emphasizing the complex social dynamics that influence students’ learning experiences and social relationships [34]. Addressing these issues requires targeted interventions, such as peer mentorship programs designed to bridge social divides while maintaining academic rigor. Promoting inclusive community activities and fostering social integration can help create a supportive environment where ethnic minority students feel valued and engaged [35, 41].

Furthermore, stereotypes and biases encountered by ethnic minority students significantly impact their emotional engagement. Participants reported experiences such as Student #2 being automatically associated with academic failure, or Student #7 being publicly reprimanded for language use, directly violations of the ‘support’ and ‘inclusion’ conditions of Pickford’s framework [24]. These experiences often triggered ‘disengagement spirals’, where protective withdrawal from participation inadvertently reinforces stereotypes and hampers social interactions [42–44]. Additionally, cultural norms intersect with biases, creating compounded disadvantages; for example, Pakistani students reported gendered expectations that influence their educational trajectories. Daughters are often expected to manage household responsibilities, while sons may leave school to work, which fosters misunderstandings and biases that affect interpersonal trust [42, 44]. Promoting inclusive attitudes and challenging stereotypes within healthcare education are essential steps toward fostering a supportive, respectful environment.

Respect for religious beliefs is equally crucial, as it pertains to students’ rights to religious freedom [45–47]. Challenges related to maintaining faith can affect academic performance and personal well-being, particularly in clinical practice settings [46–49], such as Student #5’s dilemma over prayer times or Student #15’s concerns about wearing a hijab, highlight an underexplored aspect of transactional engagement. The current framework lacks mechanisms to address the negotiation of identity and professionalism, which are vital for holistic engagement [24]. For example, conflicts between clinical practices and religious observances (e.g., restrictions on mixed-gender contact described by Student #6) suggest the need for a new dimension, perhaps ‘cultural brokerage’, that explicitly recognizes spiritual and religious commitments as legitimate components of engagement rather than obstacles to overcome.

Overall, our findings advocate for healthcare education environments that transcend passive accommodation, moving toward active inclusion [39]. Institutions must implement support systems that address the intersectionality of language, social networks, stereotypes, and religious identity, as these factors collectively shape educational trajectories [32, 33]. To support the holistic development of ethnic minority students, institutions should stress management and psychosocial support, fostering diversity, cultural competence, and social inclusion [39, 40]. Mentorship programs tailored to specific stressors, such as language barriers, can enhance social mobility, well-being, and academic success, thereby nurturing inclusive and supportive learning environments [50, 51].

While our study primarily focused on educational experiences, these challenges may extend to influence future career transitions. Understanding how early clinical socialization can mitigate feelings of isolation and facilitate smoother professional integration warrants further research. Continuous evaluation of such interventions will be essential for developing evidence-based practices that effectively support ethnic minority students’ long-term success.

Ultimately, fostering truly inclusive healthcare education requires a fundamental rethinking of professional socialization, embracing diverse identities while maintaining clinical rigor. Our adapted framework offers a roadmap for this transformation, emphasizing that diversity can strengthen healthcare education when institutions are committed to equitable engagement for all students.

Limitations

The study was conducted in a single healthcare professional training institution, which may limit the generalization of the findings to other programmes. While the sample proportion (12%) aligns with city demographics, future studies should include multi-institutional data to enhance representativeness. Although individual socioeconomic data were not collected, future research could incorporate such metrics to further contextualize the intersection of stereotypes and material circumstances. Expanding the scope to include a wider range of healthcare training programmes would also yield to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the specific learning challenges and needs of ethnic minority students in various educational contexts.

Conclusions

The growing representation of ethnic minority students in healthcare profession highlights the significant challenges affecting their learning motivation, academic performance, and psychosocial well-being. By examining four key themes that encapsulate these obstacles across various healthcare programmes, this study illuminates barriers that may impede students’ educational and professional growth. Educators and programme developers must acknowledge and address these challenges to foster a supportive and inclusive learning environment.

Future research should further investigate the specific needs shaping the learning experiences of ethnic minority students and develop tailored strategies to meet these requirements effectively. The findings emphasize the urgency of addressing the challenges confronted by ethnic minority students to ensure their smooth integration into the healthcare profession. By fostering an inclusive and supportive educational environment that prioritizes diversity and cultural competence, institutions can enhance the educational experiences and outcomes of ethnic minority students in the healthcare field. Implementing tailored interventions and support mechanisms will be crucial in empowering these students to thrive in their academic and professional pursuits.
