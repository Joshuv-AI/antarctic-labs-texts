Machine learning model to classify chronic leg wounds and identify pyoderma gangrenosum
Authors: Dorothee A Busch, Mats L Richter, Jens Hüsers, Mareike Przysucha, Florian Kücking, Jonathan M Mang, Maurice Moelleken, Joachim Dissemond, Jan Heggemann, Guido Hafer, Carola Berking, Cornelia Erfurt-Berge, Ursula H Hübner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy objectivesChronic wounds represent a significant economic and personal burden. For their successful treatment, the causes must be known and treated. Wounds caused by pyoderma gangrenosum (PG), a rare inflammatory skin disease, are often misdiagnosed. This study, therefore, aims to develop a machine learning model capable of differentiating PG from other wound types, focusing on chronic leg wounds to address this diagnostic challenge.MethodsWe used 3674 wound photographs from three specialised wound centres with the four most common types of foot and leg ulcers and the rare inflammatory differential diagnosis PG. The convolutional neural network classifier ConvNeXt ‘B’ was pretrained on LAION2B, ImageNet12k and ImageNet 1k before being trained and fine-tuned on an 85:15 train, validation split.ResultsThe model achieved an overall high accuracy in multiclass classification of the chronic wounds (unbalanced accuracy 90%, balanced accuracy 87%). The sensitivity for identifying PG was 94%, while the sensitivity forother chronic wound types was 97% for diabetic foot ulcers (DFU), 92% for venous leg ulcers (VLU), 78% for mixed leg ulcers and 74% for arterial leg ulcers.DiscussionThe machine learning model effectively differentiates PG from the most common leg and foot ulcers and was very accurate for classifying DFU and VLU. A higher rate of misclassifications occurred for the other vascular ulcers, that is, mixed and arterial leg ulcers. This aligns with the challenges in clinical practice.ConclusionDespite the limited number of wound images, this novel multiclass wound classification model accurately identified PG and differentiated leg and foot ulcer subtypes, providing a foundation for a diagnostic support system.Keywords: Machine Learning; Decision Support Systems, Clinical; Artificial intelligence; Decision Making, Computer-Assisted; Medical Informatics

Introduction

Importance of chronic wounds for patients and healthcare

A large number of patients are affected by chronic wounds worldwide. They represent a burden for both the individual and the healthcare system.1 In the USA, 10.5 million Medicare beneficiaries and nearly 2.5% of the total population are affected, with higher prevalence among elderly people.1

The characterisation and precise description of a chronic wound is essential for the documentation of chronic wounds and the treatment based on this. Making a correct diagnosis is the first step to a cause-oriented treatment, which varies significantly between the different wound types and differs completely compared with pyoderma gangrenosum (PG).

Venous leg ulcers (VLU) represent the most common (around 80%) leg ulcers and require compression therapy and—if possible—intervention on the insufficient veins as causal therapy to heal.2 An arterial ulcer (ALU) is caused by arterial insufficiency and resulting tissue ischaemia. Treatment is based on measures to promote circulation.2 There is also a combined condition between VLU and ALU, the so-called mixed led ulcer (MLU).

Besides these leg ulcers, the diabetic foot syndrome is another condition with a global prevalence of 6.3% among adults with diabetes mellitus3 with a complex clinical picture in which a combination of neuropathy, angiopathy, arch remodelling and pressure load leads to diabetic foot ulcers (DFU), especially in the forefoot area.2

While these wound types are not uncommon, PG is a rare inflammatory disease with an estimated incidence of 0.3–1 per 100 000.4 Occurring on the lower legs in the majority of cases, it is an important differential diagnosis of leg ulcers.4 Treatment of PG is anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive,4 thus totally different from the causal therapies of leg ulcers as shown above. However, PG is frequently misdiagnosed, which can lead to delayed or inappropriate treatment and poorer clinical outcomes.5 Reliable identification of PG would greatly benefit both clinicians and patients, as PG remains largely a diagnosis of exclusion.6 Improved diagnostic accuracy could facilitate earlier intervention and the initiation of an appropriate therapy.

Patients with non-healing wounds who do not receive a specific diagnosis and are therefore not treated adequately pose a severe medical problem. Besides medical history and basic diagnostics (vascular status, neuropathy), visual assessment is a crucial element of the diagnostic process.7

The question therefore arises whether this critical step can be improved through automated classification of wound images, thereby supporting clinicians when visually inspecting the wound.8 This may open new possibilities, especially for rare differential diagnoses such as PG, which cannot be easily identified using basic diagnostics.

Recent findings in machine learning supported wound image classification

There are several studies that aimed at classifying images of different types of wounds including chronic wounds such as foot and leg ulcers, DFU, pressure ulcers, so-called neuroischaemic ulcers,9 but also surgical wounds and surgical site infections. Data were obtained from the ‘AZH’ dataset, a collection of 730 prefiltered region of interest (ROI) images and 538 whole-wound images from the AZH Wound and Vascular centre in Milwaukee, labelled by a wound specialist.810,12 In some cases,8 10 12 this dataset was complemented by the public Medetec Wound Dataset containing wound photographs (DFU, pressure ulcers, VLU). Other studies made use of selected image datasets from one medical centre9 13 or from several centres.5 14 In all studies, the number of images was rather limited compared with other domains, ranging from 30015 to 8690 images13—most of them trained their models on about 1000 images or less. Therefore, the number of images for training was increased through augmentation. Similarly, models were pretrained on large image databases such as ImageNet.12 16

Accuracy values above 90% were achieved by Rostami et al12 and Guo et al11 in a binary comparison between VLU and surgical wounds, by Xu et al16 and Goyal et al17 between ischaemic and non-ischaemic DFU and by Goyal et al18 between DFU and normal skin. Conducting a multiclass classification including leg ulcers, pressure ulcers, neuroischaemic wounds and surgical site infections, Lo et al9 could receive an average accuracy value of 95.9%. Birkner et al5 were the only ones to compare PG with VLU in an effort to improve the differential diagnosis. Their model reached an accuracy value of 89.9% and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.922.

Nilsson and Velic used a pretrained convolutional neural network (CNN) to distinguish VLU from other ulcers of different mixed entities and already mentioned the potential of multiclass wound identification.15 In our previous work,14 we differentiated between DFU and VLU. To our knowledge, no study so far has distinguished between different kinds of vascular wounds alone or in combination with PG.

Focussing on the wound only (ROI images vs full images) and/or including information about the wound location enhanced the model performance. Anisuzzaman et al10 could increase the accuracy for the multiclass classification through adding the wound location from 64.96% to 82.48% and for the binary classification from 71.25% to 85.0%. The model developed by Patel et al8 exhibited an increase from 85.34% to 86.91%. Both studies made use of the AZH and Medetec wound dataset comprising leg ulcers, pressure ulcers, surgical wounds and, in the case of Patel et al8 also, normal skin and background images.

Aim of our study

Given the background of the previous studies, our aim is to classify wounds that are more difficult to differentiate clinically and to test a new approach for its clinical viability. The goal, therefore, is to employ a state-of-the-art architecture that can (1) distinguish the most important leg and foot ulcer diagnoses and (b) distinguish each of these ulcers from PG, a rare inflammatory disease. Furthermore, it is the aim to measure the performance of the architecture in a multiclass classification task that best resembles the clinical situation.

Materials and methods

The present study pursued a multicentre approach using 3674 anonymised images of the departments of dermatology of the University Hospitals in Essen and Erlangen and the Wound Centre of the Christian Hospital in Melle, a municipal hospital, all of them located in Germany. Images of leg and foot ulcers, that is, with the subtypes VLU, ALU, MLU and DFU and images of PG originated from the University Hospital Essen and Erlangen. The hospital in Melle contributed images of DFU. The images were taken during routine wound care and entered into the digital medical and wound record systems. Inclusion criteria at all sites were International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10-GM) diagnoses, patient age >18 and availability of high-quality, anonymised wound images.

In Essen, the images, except for MLU, were expert selected based on the ICD-10-GM codes19 and clinical data in electronic health record (EHRs) and then extracted manually. Images for MLU were manually selected as they were not represented in the ICD-10-GM system and were added when both venous congestion and reduced arterial blood flow were present. As the images were selected retrospectively from the image database, their age varied from 1 to 20 years.

In Erlangen, the images were extracted via the local Data Integration Centre—a service hub providing routine health data for research recorded in electronic systems.20 The health data contained the images and a core dataset such as the patient’s age and medical diagnosis. For our project, we used the images connected to diagnoses for VLU, ALU PG and DFU that were taken in the time period from 1 January 2021 to 30 March 2023.

In Melle, the images of the DFU patients were selected by clinical experts retrospectively from their records of patients with diabetes mellitus.

The dataset was split into a training and a validation split with a ratio of 85%–15% of the total data point. The model was trained on the data using ConvNeXt ‘B’,21 a CNN architecture inspired by visual transformers. The architecture was chosen as CNNs allow for relatively data-efficient training, and ConvNeXt is the currently best-performing architecture. We choose the ‘B’ variant of ContNext, since it was found to be sufficiently large to achieve high performance, with an average layer saturation of 38.9%22 when training the model from scratch on the dataset, indicating high parameter efficiency for the given task. One key challenge was the relatively small amount of data, making training from scratch impractical, since even small CNNs do not adequately learn discriminative features on the image, given the available data. To optimise performance on the small wound dataset, we choose a multistaged pretraining process, starting with a highly varied large-scale dataset followed by progressively smaller, more specific and higher quality datasets. The rationale behind this process was that initial stages would generate highly generalisable features that were improved by increasingly focusing on higher-quality data throughout the training stages. The weights were initially determined by first training the model as a Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) model23 on the LAION2B dataset.24 CLIP was necessary in this case, as LAION2B has no labels that could be used for classification. This first stage of pretraining was followed by fine-tuning on the ImageNet12K and ImageNet1K classification datasets.

To avoid unproductive layers,25 26 we increased the resolution to 256×256 input resolution while training and evaluating the model. The model was finally trained using mixed precision and a batch size of 16 with a cross-entropy loss. Otherwise, the hyperparameter configuration would have been equivalent to the A2 configuration of ‘ResNet strikes back’ by Wightman et al.27

Preprocessing and data augmentation strategies were also adapted without change from Wightman et al.27 The architecture itself remained unchanged from the original implementation21 except for the softmax output, which was reduced to the appropriate number of classes. Training was conducted for 300 epochs using mixed precision, checkpointing after each epoch. The final checkpoint was selected from this trajectory of checkpoints based on their balanced accuracy on the validation set.

Since the dataset was highly imbalanced, we relied on multiple metrics and evaluation methods to quantify predictive performance. Our primary metrics were accuracy and balanced accuracy. The balanced accuracy weighs the samples based on their class, simulating balanced classes in the ground truth. To further breakdown the characteristics of the model, we further used the confusion matrix computed from the prediction on the validation set. The confusion matrices were computed with absolute values and normalised with respect to ground truth, to further characterise the predictive quality with respect to individual classes. To enhance the explainability of the validation samples, we additionally computed activation heatmaps with GradCam ++,28 highlighting the regions contributing to the predicted class. The latter was addressing the imbalance issue directly. However, in the case of overfitting, anomalous or unreasonable activation heatmaps (eg, the texture of the bed contributing more to the classification than the wound itself) could reveal overfitting scenarios.

Results

In the multiclass classification task of this study, the final model achieved an unbalanced accuracy of 90.4%, which corresponds to a balanced accuracy of 87.4%. The confusion matrices (figure 1) reveal an adequate classification of all wound types; however, with differences among the various types. DFU (97%) could be best classified, followed by PG (94%) and VLU (92%). The least accurate classifications concerned MLU (78%) and ALU (74%) with rather low performance compared with the other wound types. In most cases of misclassification, ALU was classified as VLU, as was MLU. In contrast, VLU was only rarely mixed with other wound classes. PG could be distinguished from other types very well, only exhibiting a small proportion of cases that were taken as VLU by the model. Discussion

We developed a well-performing model that distinguishes between the different types of chronic wounds and is capable of classifying PG correctly, thus supporting a clinically meaningful differential diagnosis very well. It aimed at a multiclass task not unrealistic for clinical settings. We used a new, multicentre dataset that is comparatively large with 3674 wound images from three specialised centres.

Our model offers a new promising approach mixing classical CNNs with visual transformers, which have been pretrained extensively on non-medical data. Even with a rather small dataset, it performed very well, reaching an overall balanced accuracy of 87% for a multiclass classification. We have successfully demonstrated that the classification task at hand benefits significantly from the large-scale pretraining on a generic image-text corpus. Without pretraining, we achieved significantly worse results, barely surpassing performance above chance.

Furthermore, we highlighted that the amount of high-quality downstream data necessary to solve the task can be very minimal when the pretraining resulted in general features. This is particularly important as the medical image data is generally not available in the million or billion data points scale required for training modern neural network classifiers. Further expansion of data integration centres at clinical sites may be helpful in this regard.

With regard to balanced accuracy, this model surpassed the results of multiclass classification tasks achieved by Patel et al8 and Anisuzzaman et al,10 adding anatomic information. Only Lo et al9 outperformed our model (95.9% accuracy) with an imbalanced dataset of 2549 wound images. However, no corrections for imbalance were reported in this paper.9 Only Birkner et al5 compared PG with one other wound type, that is, VLU, and reached an accuracy of 89.9% in a binary classification based on 491 balanced images. In our study, PG classification was correct in 94.1% of the cases with only less than 6% misclassification with VLU. When comparing the AUROC, Birkner et al achieved an AUROC of 0.922, while our model achieved an AUROC of 0.9837 for detecting PG.

Previous wound classification models primarily included normal skin and easily distinguishable wound types (surgical, pressure, venous, diabetic).10 11 Our model introduces a novel approach by categorising different leg wounds, addressing the challenge of differentiating similar-looking wounds in the same body area and thereby representing clinical challenges more realistically.

As the traditional assessment of wound type relies heavily on the visual examination of wound characteristics by the clinician, the diagnosis mainly depends on the experience level of the clinician, with a potential for low accuracy. The study of Birkner et al5 included a comparison between the CNN and dermatologists identifying PG, in which dermatologists achieved a sensitivity of 72.7% and specificity of 88.9% of correctly diagnosing PG compared with VLU. Even with differentiation between more wound types, our model achieved higher sensitivity (94.1%) and specificity (96.5%) for the detection of PG than dermatologists in the study by Birkner et al.5

Limitations

The most common misclassification was between arterial and VLU and between mixed and VLU. There are two explanations for this observation. From a technical perspective, these classes are the smallest in terms of quantity, making misclassification more likely due to a lack of data. From a medical perspective, the three classes overlap semantically, as MLU is pathogenetically a combination of ALU and VLU, which probably also contributes to the misclassification. Distinguishing between ALU and MLU is particularly difficult, as MLUs also exhibit reduced arterial blood flow, resulting in wound patterns similar to ALUs.

The differentiation between these vascular wounds remains challenging for clinicians as well, often requiring additional diagnostics.29 Incorporating this type of information beyond wound images—such as precise anatomical location, palpability of pulses, ankle-brachial index or other clinical parameters—could further enhance the accuracy of the CNN.

Even though our dataset is quite extensive compared with the literature, the performance of the model could be enhanced by using a bigger dataset, especially including more images for ALU, DFU and PG.

The dataset consists primarily of Caucasian skin wounds. This may limit the accuracy of the model for darker skin types, particularly for PG, which often shows inflammation that looks different on darker skin.

A large number of the images of DFU came from one hospital, raising the possibility of overfitting the model due to different types of photographs. It is also possible that the identification of the diabetic foot ulcer was based on the identification of the body part rather than the characteristics of the wound.

Clinical impact

A machine learning model achieving an overall balanced accuracy of 87.4% in distinguishing these wound types is clinically useful and promising. Our model offers immediate clinical utility as a possible point-of-care diagnostic support system, particularly in primary or rural healthcare settings for early detection of PG. Clinicians could interact by uploading images and reviewing probabilistic predictions, supported by visual explanations (eg, activation heatmaps), functioning as a transparent decision-aid tool. However, visual inspection of wounds is only the very first step in the diagnostic process. For models to become realistic and thus clinically useful for wound classification, they must also rely on the causal conditions and other biomarkers, such as lab values.

Outlook

Our model represents the first step towards a support system for clinicians to help identify PG and other wound causes and to initiate further diagnostic measures.

Automated wound classification can be a valuable tool for non-expert clinicians in primary and rural healthcare settings.30 By assisting in the identification of rare differential diagnoses, it can support more accurate and timely treatment. Additionally, the system can suggest further diagnostic measures, such as vascular diagnostics or skin biopsies.

For future real-world integration, we envision seamless integration into EHR systems, allowing automated image input and direct incorporation of model predictions into patient records. This would streamline workflows and accelerate comprehensive diagnostic evaluation and thus adequate treatment.

Conclusion

In summary, our work describes a new approach to multiclass wound classification using a pretrained ConvNeXt ‘B’ model on a multicentre dataset of 3764 wound images. The high-accuracy detection of PG in combination with differentiation between different kinds of leg ulcers represents a new and more nuanced wound classification model than previously reported.

Moreover, our findings lay the groundwork for a diagnostic decision support system to assist clinicians in managing chronic wounds. This may be especially helpful in primary care settings, where early detection of rare diseases can significantly impact patient outcomes.
