Deletion of TOP2B promoter using CRISPR-Cas9 induces senescence in HEK 293T cells
Authors: Neha Saroj, Harshitha Golagana Velangani, Shashi Kiran, Pankaj Singh Dholaniya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDNA topoisomerase II beta (TOP2B) is crucial for the regulation of DNA topology, transcription, and genome stability. While its function has been extensively studied in post-mitotic cells such as neurons and cardiomyocytes, its role in proliferative cells, especially in the context of cellular senescence and aging, remains inadequately understood. Emerging evidence suggests that TOP2B expression declines with age and is implicated in several age-associated disorders, yet the molecular basis of its involvement in senescence remains poorly understood. In this investigation, we have specifically disrupted the promoter region of TOP2B in HEK 293T cells using CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing. The targeted region, spanning −533 to −481, which encompasses binding motifs for NF–Y and SP1, also accounts for a major proportion of TOP2B promoter activity. Knockout of this regulatory segment resulted in a marked reduction of TOP2B expression. The knockout cells exhibited classical features of senescence, including elevated ROS levels, increased senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) activity, and upregulation of key markers such as p53, p21, and p16.To examine the paracrine effects of senescence, conditioned media from TOP2B-deficient cells were given to wild-type HEK 293T cells, which also developed senescence-like phenotypes. These findings suggest that Loss of TOP2B expression is associated with the induction of senescence-like phenotypes in dividing cells and can propagate senescent signaling to neighboring cells. This study highlights a previously underappreciated role of TOP2B in the regulation of cellular aging and opens new avenues for understanding its potential contribution to age-related pathologies in proliferative cell types.Keywords: TOP2B, CRISPR-Cas9, Gene editing, Senescence, Conditioned media, ROS, SA-β-Gal

Introduction

DNA topoisomerases regulate the structure of DNA by creating temporary nicks [1]. They are divided into Type I (TOP1 and TOP1MT) and Type II (TOP2A and TOP2B) topoisomerases. TOP2 is involved in several functions in the cell: DNA replication and repair, transcription, chromatin condensation, and remodeling [1]. In order to perform its function, TOP2 cuts both DNA strands and allows a separate unbroken double-stranded segment to move through the opening, thus having more impact and control on the DNA structure. Due to their DNA gyrase activity, negative supercoils are created in the DNA, which is essential for regulating the supercoiling state of DNA [2]. The catalytic cycle of TOP2 begins with TOP2 binding with a segment of DNA called the G-segment (Gated segment). The binding of a pair of ATP molecules to the N-gate domain then induces closure of this gate and secures the T-segment inside. With Mg2+ ions, the WHD domain's catalytic tyrosines cleave the G-segment, generating transient 5′ phosphotyrosine–DNA intermediates. The G-segment cleavage enables T-segment passage, accompanied by ATP hydrolysis. Following the passage of the T-segment, the enzyme reseals the G-segment and hydrolyzes the second ATP. At this stage, the T-segment exits through the C-gate, and the G-segment is discharged from the N-gate, resetting the enzyme for the subsequent catalytic cycle [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]].

Lower eukaryotes have only one TOP2, but higher vertebrates have two isoforms, TOP2A and TOP2B. Both isoforms have an amino acid sequence similarity of 70% [9]. TOP2A is basically present in cells that are rapidly dividing, whereas TOP2B is expressed in both dividing and non-dividing cells, with high expression in post-mitotic cells [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. The role of TOP2B is specifically studied in neurogenesis [14,15], corticogenesis [15], B-cell, natural killer, and retina development [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. TOP2B-deficient mice survive gestation but die soon after birth from respiratory failure [20]. But it was a decade ago that researchers started to study the association of TOP2B with aging. The decline in TOP2B expression with aging was first identified in the cerebellar granule neurons (CGNs) [21] and later in sheep neuronal cells [22]. Later, its role in life span was also identified in Saccharomyces cerevisiae [23,24]. TOP2B knockout is also associated with age-related diastolic dysfunction in mice [24]. Moreover, drugs that inhibit TOP2 act as promoters of aging [25]. Apart from this, the role of TOP2B is also implicated in several age-related diseases like cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, and metabolic and cardiovascular diseases [26]. In cardiomyocytes, the cardiotoxicity of chemotherapeutic agents is mediated through TOP2B [27]. TOP2B carries out the aforementioned roles through its involvement in essential cellular functions during cell division, like DNA replication, mitosis, and chromosome segregation [28]. TOP2B also acts as a transcriptional regulator and an important modulator of genome stability [29]. It plays a major role in DNA damage and repair, specifically in Homologous Repair (HR) and Non-Homologous End Joining (NHEJ) [30]. During the NHEJ repair, TOP2B interacts with Ku70, Werner helicase, and pol-β-dependent pathways and also initiates signal-dependent transcription of certain genes [30,31]. In CGNs, TOP2B-mediated DNA damage repair is dependent on Ku70 and PARP-1 [32]. During the HR pathway, TOP2B induced transitory DNA breaks, leading to the activation of BRCA1 and BRCA2, and initiating repair [30]. Almost all of these studies were carried out in post-mitotic cells, such as neurons, cardiomyocytes, or differentiated SHSY-5Y cells, which are non-dividing cells. Although TOP2B has been implicated in aging and age-related pathologies, most studies have been limited to post-mitotic or non-dividing cell types, where classical features of senescence are not readily assessed. Consequently, the role of TOP2B in regulating senescence-associated phenotypes in proliferative cells remains unclear. Addressing this gap is essential to determine whether TOP2B directly contributes to cellular aging processes in dividing human cells. In this study, we aim to address this gap by targeting the regulatory region of the TOP2B promoter in a human cell line undergoing proliferation and evaluating its impact on cellular senescence, oxidative stress, and intercellular signaling.Cellular senescence is characterized by a permanent arrest of the cell cycle, which was first observed in fibroblast cells that failed to divide after a finite number of cell divisions [33]. It has been identified as a cellular response to stress, such as DNA damage, nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, or any genotoxic stress [34]. Senescent cells exhibit enhanced resistance to apoptosis due to the activation of cell survival pathways, such as the upregulation of antiapoptotic proteins from the BCL-2 family [35,36]. Other features of senescent cells include morphological changes, oxidative damage (ROS), cell cycle arrest (p53, p16, p21), metabolic changes, including SA-β-Gal accumulation and secretion of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [37,38]. Though there are recent reports and reviews as to how TOP2B could be involved in aging, the molecular mechanism or cellular changes have not been studied extensively [26]. In this study, we aim to specifically knock out the TOP2B promoter region in a dividing cell line like HEK 293T and study its cellular and molecular characteristics. We knocked out the regulatory region (between −533 and −481) of TOP2B, where 80% of its promoter activity is attributed. This region harbours two inverted CCAAT boxes (ICBs) and a GC-box domain where NF–Y and SP1 bind, respectively [39]. We mapped this region in the Homo sapiens genome and also found a similar domain in the Rattus norvegicus genome. This region was targeted with CRISPR-Cas9 in HEK 293T cells, and a significant reduction of TOP2B expression was achieved. During single-cell cloning attempts to isolate knockout clones, we observed that the edited cells failed to divide, suggesting a proliferation defect post-knockout. The effects of TOP2B knockout were evaluated using ROS and SA-β-Gal assays, alongside analysis of senescence markers such as p53, p21, and p16. Additionally, control HEK 293T cells were exposed to conditioned media from knockout cells to study downstream effects. We observed several markers of senescence in these cells. Together, our findings demonstrate that precise disruption of the TOP2B promoter region is sufficient to silence gene expression and induce a senescence-like phenotype in dividing human cells. These results implicate TOP2B as an essential mediator of cellular homeostasis and underscore its broader role in modulating oxidative stress responses and intercellular communication.

Methods and materials

Cell line maintenance

HEK 293T cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM (Cat.#11965092, ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS (Cat. #10270106, ThermoFisher Scientific) and 1X Anti-Anti (Cat. #15240062, ThermoFisher Scientific) with 5% CO2 in a humidified chamber. Before any experiment, cells were trypsinized, plated, and cultured for 24 h.

Designing guide RNA targeting the TOP2B promoter

Four single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were strategically designed using the CRISPick tool (Broad Institute) to target the TOP2B promoter region: CGCAATGACGTTTCCCCCCT, TCGGGTCCCGCCCCTCCAGG, CATGGCGCTGACTGACAGCG, and ATGGCGCTGACTGACAGCGG. The sgRNAs, synthesized as complementary forward and reverse oligonucleotides, were selected such that two sites flank the upstream region of the promoter, while the remaining two flank the downstream region. This design was intended to enable precise excision of the promoter segment through dual CRISPR-Cas9–mediated cleavage (Supplementary Fig. 1A–B). Primers spanning these cut sites were also designed to validate the deletion (Table 1). Off-target analysis of CRISPR guide RNAs was perform through Cas OFFinder tool with up to 3 bases mismatches to ensure high target efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 1C).

Cloning and transformation of the guide RNA

For annealing, 50 ng of each forward and reverse oligos for the guide RNAs were incubated in the annealing buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5- 8.0), 50 mM NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA at 95 °C with a controlled ramp rate of −1 °C per 0.38 s for 10 min. A modified version of the plentiCRISPRv2 vector was used, which was digested with the BsmbI vector (Cat. #R0580, NEB) for 15 min at 55 °C. The annealed sgRNAs were inserted into the vector using the Infusion HD cloning kit (Cat. #638909, Takara) following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. The resulting recombinant vector was then transformed into Stellar competent cells (Cat. #636763, Takara) as per the manufacturer's guidelines, and cells were plated onto LB agar supplemented with ampicillin and incubated overnight at 37 °C. For each sgRNA, 2-5 colonies were picked and screened for the correct clones by Sanger sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Finally, the positive colonies were propagated, and the plasmid was isolated with a Plasmid miniprep isolation kit (Cat. #K0502, ThermoFisher Scientific).

Transfection into cells and knockout confirmation

In a 6-well plate, HEK 293T cells were cultured with a seeding density of 3 × 10^5 (Day 0)and cultured up to 80-90 % confluency before transfection. 1 μg plasmid DNA/well was used for transfection(Day 1). The plasmid with sgRNA was transfected in different combinations-sgRNA1+sgRNA3, sgRNA1+sgRNA4, sgRNA2+sgRNA3, sgRNA2+sgRNA4, and sgRNA1+sgRNA2+sgRNA3+sgRNA4. sgRNAs were first diluted in optiMEM (Cat. #31985070, ThermoFisher Scientific) media, and then PEI-1μg/ml (Polyethylenimine) (Cat. #764892-1G, Sigma-Aldrich) was added in a 1:3 ratio (Vector: PEI). Following 15 min of room temperature incubation, the sgRNA–PEI complex was gently introduced to the cultured cells. After 12 h of incubation, the transfection complexes were discarded, and the cells were incubated in fresh complete DMEM medium for 24 h. After that, the cells were selected with 2.5 μg puromycin for another 24 h. After this, the cells were briefly rinsed with PBS and cultured in fresh complete DMEM media. The cells were sampled at 48, 72, and 96 h after the transfection period (After 12 h of transfection). Total genomic DNA was isolated using a DNA isolation kit (Cat. #K0512, ThermoFisher Scientific). Region-specific primers spanning the promoter were used to amplify the corresponding fragment from genomic DNA by PCR. (Table 1) (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Cell culture conditioned media treatment

The medium from TOP2B-knockout (KO) cells was collected (KO-CM) and spun at 1000×g for 5 min to eliminate cellular debris. Freshly plated cells were cultured with this conditioned medium to assess the effect of TOP2B knockout on neighboring cells for 48 h. For comparison, conditioned media from normal control cells were also used for treatment. Additionally, RNA and protein were also isolated from the treated cells for further characterization.

Total cellular ROS detection

For ROS detection through imaging, the cells were plated on coverslips and subjected to different treatments. Cells were rinsed with PBS at the indicated time point and treated with 25 μM H2DCFDA (Cat. #D399, ThermoFisher Scientific) in serum-free DMEM media for 30 min in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C. Subsequently, the cells were rinsed with serum-free DMEM once and with 60% glycerol mounted on a glass slide and imaged with Axio Observer Z1 with Apotome from Carl Zeiss with a 488 nm filter. For the spectrophotometric assay, cells were seeded in a 96-well plate. At the appropriate time, after a quick PBS-wash, they were treated with 25 μM H2DCFDA in serum-free DMEM for 30 min at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator. Following incubation, the reagent was removed, and fresh serum-free DMEM was added to each well. Fluorescence intensity was recorded using a Tecan plate reader at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm.

SA-β-gal assay

The HEK 293T cells were cultured on coverslips or cell culture plates, rinsed with PBS, and then fixed at RT for 10 min in 4% PFA. After fixing and washing the cells once with PBS, the cells are then treated with freshly prepared SA-β-Gal staining solution, containing 1 mg/ml X-gal (Cat. #3117073001, Sigma-Aldrich), 40 mM citric acid/Sodium Phosphate buffer (pH-6.0), 5 mM Potassium ferrocyanide, 5 mM Potassium ferricyanide, 150 mM Sodium chloride and 2 mM Magnesium chloride for 12-16 h or until we observed the development of blue precipitate. The coverslips were then mounted on a glass slide with 60% glycerol, or the dishes were directly imaged on a brightfield microscope with a stage micrometre. The number of SA-β-Gal–positive cells was determined manually and normalized to the total cell count, then plotted using GraphPad Prism 9.

Western blot

HEK 293T cells, following respective treatments, were trypsinized and pelleted down. The resulting cell pellets were gently resuspended in RIPA lysis buffer (Cat. #R0278, Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. Samples were kept on ice for a total of 20 min, with brief vortexing every 2 min at 4 °C to enhance cell disruption. Lysates were then centrifuged at 14,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was retained as the total protein extract. 10% SDS-PAGE gels were used to separate proteins (40 μg per lane), following transfer to PVDF membranes. Loading uniformity was verified using Ponceau S staining (Supplementary Fig. 3). Membranes were subsequently blocked in a blocking buffer containing 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, following an overnight incubation at 4 °C with gentle shaking in primary antibodies: anti-TOP2B (1:1000, Cat. # ab264158, Abcam) and anti-β-Actin (1:1000, (8H10D10 Mouse mAb) Cat. #3700, CST). After three washes using TBST (0.1% Tween-20), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Donkey anti-rabbit or Goat anti-mouse) at a 1:10,000 dilution for 1 h at room temperature. Following repeated washing, detection and imaging of protein bands were carried out using the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP imaging system.

Immunostaining

Cells were seeded on glass coverslips and processed as described in Section 2.4. Cells were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature to preserve cellular structures, followed by a 10 min permeabilization using 0.25% Triton X-100. The coverslips were then blocked in a solution containing PBS with 1% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody labeling was performed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with gentle shaking using the following antibodies diluted in blocking buffer: Rabbit anti-TOP2B (1:1000, Cat. #ab264158, Abcam) and Mouse anti-Ki-67 monoclonal antibody (1:200, Cat. #MA5-15484, Invitrogen). The next day, the coverslips were rinsed three times with PBS, then incubated for another 1 h at room temperature in the dark with the secondary antibodies: Goat anti-Mouse AlexaFluor™ 488 (1:1000, Invitrogen) and Goat anti-Rabbit AlexaFluor™ 594 (1:1000, Invitrogen). Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 15 min, and membranes were labeled with Dye Aye (Cat. #42364, Sigma-Aldrich). Finally, the coverslips were mounted onto glass slides using 60% glycerol and imaged with Axio Observer Z1 with Apotome from Carl Zeiss.

Mitochondrial membrane potential determination

To understand the effect of TOP2B knockout on the mitochondrial membrane potential, we stained the cells with Rhodamine 123 (Cat. #R8004, Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were treated with 10 μg/mL Rhodamine 123 dissolved in serum-free DMEM and were maintained for 30 min at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Following this, they were stained with Dil dye and counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescence images were captured using Axio Observer Z1 with Apotome from Carl Zeiss. All images obtained were processed and quantified using ImageJ, and the resulting measurements were visualized in GraphPad Prism 9.

Real-time PCR analysis

RNA was extracted from HEK 293T cells following the respective treatments using the PureLink® RNA Mini Kit (Cat. #12183025, ThermoFisher Scientific). 1 μg of RNA was converted into cDNA with the PrimeScript cDNA synthesis kit (Cat. #6100A, Takara) using random hexamer primers in a 20 μL reaction. The cDNA was then diluted 1:10 ratio, and 1 μL of the diluted cDNA was used in a 10 μL reaction for real-time PCR using SYBR Premix Ex Taq™ (Cat. #RR420A, Takara) to amplify the genes listed in Table 1. The thermal cycling protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 1 min and 60 °C for 20 s, and concluded with a melt curve program at 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 15 s, and 95 °C for 15 s. Relative gene expression was calculated by normalizing to GAPDH, and the fold changes compared with the control were plotted using GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Image and data interpretation

All experimental data are represented as mean ± standard deviation. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and statistical difference was determined by one-way ANOVA, considering p-values below 0.05 as significant. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ, and the data were graphically represented in GraphPad Prism 9.0. The mean fluorescence intensity of each image was quantified in ImageJ from three independent replicates per condition, expressed as a percentage, normalized to the control and relative to cell number. Results

TOP2B expression was decreased in HEK 293T cells following TOP2B promoter knockout and treatment with TOP2B–KO conditioned media

To generate the TOP2B knockout (KO), a specific promoter region spanning −533 to −481 was targeted using CRISPR-Cas9 in HEK 293T cells, as detailed in the Methods section. Following transfection, cells were cultured for 24 h and then subjected to puromycin selection for an additional 24 h. At 48, 72, and 96 h post-transfection, RNA and protein were extracted. A significant reduction in TOP2B expression was evident by 48 h at both transcript and protein levels. Immunostaining showed a gradual reduction in TOP2B protein levels over time, with the largest decrease observed at 96 h (Fig. 1A and B). RT-PCR analysis further confirmed a similar temporal decrease in TOP2B transcript levels (Fig. 1C). To evaluate the paracrine effects of the knockout, conditioned media (CM) from TOP2B–KO cells (KO-CM) were collected, centrifuged to remove cellular debris, and untreated HEK 293T control cells were cultured in this media for 48 h. CM from unedited HEK 293T cells (Control-CM) served as the control. Treatment with KO-CM led to a notable reduction in TOP2B expression in recipient cells at both the protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 1D–F). Interestingly, cells treated with Control-CM exhibited a slight increase in TOP2B expression at 48 h, particularly at the protein level (Fig. 1E). Western blot analysis corroborated these findings, showing significant suppression of TOP2B in KO samples at 96 h and in KO-CM-treated cells at 48 h compared to their respective controls (Fig. 1G and H) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

TOP2B knockout impairs proliferation, suppresses Ki-67, increases ROS, and alters gene expression of TOP2B-associated genes

During the generation of single-cell clones from TOP2B–KO cells, we observed a complete failure in clonal expansion, indicating a proliferation defect. This observation was further supported by MTT assay results (Supplementary Fig. 2D). Knockout treatment not only has more cell death after transfection but also fails to divide further as compared with transfected control cells. We also assessed the expression of the proliferation marker Ki-67 by immunostaining. A progressive decrease in Ki-67 expression was observed over time post-knockout, indicating impaired proliferative capacity (Fig. 2A,C).To further evaluate changes in cell physiology, we labeled the cell membrane with the lipophilic fluorescent dye DiIC18 and assessed total cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels using the DCFDA reagent. ROS levels were measured using two independent approaches. First, a plate-based spectrophotometric assay in a 96-well format was used to quantify ROS at 48, 72, and 96 h. TOP2B–KO cells showed a significant increase in ROS at 72 and 96 h compared to both untreated and transfection control cells (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Second, ROS levels were visualized by fluorescence microscopy, which confirmed elevated ROS signals in TOP2B–KO cells at 72 and 96 h relative to controls (Fig. 2B,D). We also assessed the expression of TOP2B-associated regulatory genes, particularly transcription factors known to bind the TOP2B promoter. SP1 expression showed a gradual decline at the mRNA level over time, while NFY-A expression exhibited temporal fluctuations (Fig. 1E). Interestingly, the expression of the TOP2B paralog TOP2A was markedly upregulated at 48 h post-knockout but declined progressively thereafter (Fig. 1E). These findings suggest that TOP2B loss not only impairs proliferation and increases oxidative stress but also disrupts the regulatory network of genes associated with its promoter activity.

TOP2B knockout HEK 293T cells stain positive for SA-β-gal and display altered expression of cell cycle-modulating genes and pathways associated with senescence

In alignment with the observation of decreased Ki-67 expression along with other changes in the cellular integrity, we also assessed the SA-β-Gal activity through a simple staining protocol mentioned in the methods section 2.7. TOP2B–KO cells exhibited significantly elevated SA-β-Gal activity at 72 and 96 h, normalized to cell count.(Fig. 3A and B). To further characterize the senescence-like phenotype, we analyzed the expression of key cell cycle regulators. Quantitative RT-PCR revealed a time-dependent upregulation of p16, p21, and p53, with transcript levels progressively increasing from 48 to 96 h post-knockout (Fig. 3D). Consistent with our protein-level observations, Ki-67 mRNA levels were significantly reduced in TOP2B–KO cells, supporting a sustained loss of proliferative capacity (Fig. 3C). Additionally, we investigated the expression of genes associated with apoptosis resistance. Notably, BCL-2 and FOXO4 exhibited elevated expression following TOP2B disruption (Fig. 3E), suggesting a potential shift toward a senescence-associated survival program. RNA-Seq analysis was performed for TOP2B–KO compared to Control, for KO-CM-treated cells compared to Control, and for TOP2B–KO with respect to KO-CM. Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed if they showed a log2 fold change greater than 1 or less than −1, along with an adjusted p-value below 0.05. 1078, 753, and 515 genes were found to be upregulated in Control Vs TOP2B–KO, Control Vs KO-CM, and TOP2B–KO Vs KO-CM, respectively. Similarly, 1531,705 and 175 genes were downregulated in Control Vs TOP2B–KO, Control Vs KO-CM, and TOP2B–KO Vs KO-CM, respectively (Supplementary Figure 4.1, A). The pattern of differential gene expression (DE genes) common among all the three groups is shown in the heat map (Supplementary Figure 4.1, D). 23 upregulated and 2 downregulated genes were common to all three conditions (Fig. 4.1A and B). Pathway enrichment analysis of the DE genes in each condition was performed. In the TOP2B–KO group, p53 signaling, NF-Kappa B signaling, IL-17 signaling, MAPK signaling, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathways were upregulated, while pathways such as Valine, leucine, and Isoleucine degradation, CGMP-PKG signaling, and other metabolic pathways were downregulated (Supplementary Figure 4.2). A similar trend was observed in cells treated with TOP2B–KO conditioned media. Pathways such as IL-17 signaling, MAPK signaling, p53 signaling, NF-κB signaling, FOXO signaling, and cellular senescence were upregulated, while pathways such as PI3K-Akt signaling, cGMP-PKG signaling, and other metabolic pathways were downregulated (Supplementary Figure 4.3). Collectively, these results indicate that targeted knockout of the TOP2B promoter region not only induces growth arrest but also activates a robust senescence program, characterized by increased oxidative stress, altered cell cycle regulation, and enhanced survival signaling.

TOP2B–KO CM-treated cells demonstrated decreased mitochondrial membrane potential and increased ROS production

To evaluate changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), we employed Rhodamine 123, a cationic lipophilic dye that selectively localizes in functional mitochondria. Cells were stained and visualized using fluorescence microscopy. A marked reduction in MMP was observed in TOP2B–KO cells at 96 h post-knockout, indicating mitochondrial dysfunction. Notably, a similar decrease in MMP was detected in cells treated with conditioned media derived from knockout cells (KO-CM) as early as 48 h, suggesting a transferable, paracrine effect (Fig. 4A and B). The oxidative stress was assessed using DCFDA via two complementary methods: a 96-well plate–based fluorometric assay and direct visualization by fluorescence microscopy. Both approaches revealed a significant elevation in ROS levels in cells treated with KO-CM at 24 and 48 h, relative to control groups (Fig. 4C and D; Supplementary Fig. 1D–E), reinforcing the notion of a stress-inducing secretome from TOP2B-deficient cells. We further examined the expression of transcription factors known to regulate TOP2B promoter activity. SP1 expression was notably reduced in KO-CM-treated cells at 48 h, while no appreciable change was observed in cells treated with control conditioned media (Fig. 4E). In contrast, NFY-A expression increased in response to control CM, but declined in cells exposed to KO-CM at the same time point, suggesting that factors secreted by TOP2B-deficient cells may actively suppress key regulatory components of the TOP2B transcriptional network (Fig. 4E).

TOP2B–KO CM treated cells demonstrated decreased Ki-67 staining and increased SA-β-gal activity

Consistent with observations in TOP2B–KO cells, a marked reduction in Ki-67 expression was detected in both KO-CM and Control-CM treated cells at 48 h post-treatment, with a more pronounced decrease in the KO-CM group (Fig. 5A and B). Membrane counterstaining using the fluorescent lipophilic dye DiI revealed subtle differences in staining intensity between treatment conditions, potentially reflecting early morphological or membrane-associated changes (Fig. 5A). To further investigate the induction of senescence, we assessed SA-β-Gal activity in recipient cells following treatment. Cells exposed to KO-CM exhibited robust SA-β-Gal staining at various time points (Fig. 5C–D), indicative of a senescence-like state. Collectively, these results demonstrate that soluble factors secreted by TOP2B-deficient cells are sufficient to elicit a non-proliferative, growth-arrested phenotype in naïve HEK 293T cells, recapitulating key molecular and cellular hallmarks of senescence observed in directly edited TOP2B–KO populations.

Discussion

Cellular senescence is an extremely complex phenomenon that leads to aging and age-related anomalies. It is a gradual decline in the proliferation capacity of cells, leading to irreversible cell cycle arrest [40]. Different mechanisms leading to senescence or aging have been studied, including mitochondrial dysfunction, stem cell exhaustion, epigenetic changes, loss of proteostasis, genome instability, and telomere dysfunction [41]. The major pathways implicated in cellular senescence include the p53/p21^WAF1/CIP1 and p16^INK4A/RB tumor suppressor axes, as well as DNA damage response (DDR), AMPK signaling, p38/MAPK, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways. [42]. Also, senescent cells are rendered more resistant to apoptosis than any other cells [43]. Thus, senescence emerged to be a complex state caused by DNA damage, excessive stress during DNA replication, epigenetic changes, production of ROS leading to mitochondrial damage, and any other stressors affecting the homeostasis inside the cell. Researchers have studied senescence for a long time, but the role of TOP2B in senescence or aging is not well explored. The role of TOP2B in senescence is largely studied in post-mitotic cells like CGNs, where its expression is shown to decrease during aging in rats [21]. Most of the knockout studies have been done in post-mitotic cells like cardiomyocytes and neurons [18,21,24]. In these cases, i.e., cardiomyocytes and neurons, TOP2B knockout led to age-dependent degeneration and functional decline but did not induce classic senescence hallmarks like SA-β-Gal positivity or cell-cycle arrest, given the non-dividing nature of these cells. Since these cells are post-mitotic and non-dividing, direct phenotypic effects such as cell-cycle arrest cannot be readily observed; instead, these studies focus on the transcriptional consequences of TOP2B activity [44,45]. TOP2B's role as a transcriptional regulator, influencing transcription, is well studied [46,47]. But its role in senescence or aging has not been explicitly studied. Our findings, therefore, expand the role of TOP2B into senescence regulation in dividing cells, revealing a novel function distinct from its previously described maintenance of transcription fidelity and chromatin architecture in non-dividing tissues. In the present research, we sought to understand the role of TOP2B in a dividing cell line like HEK 293T through loss-of-function mutation in its promoter region. Our results reveal that the knockout of TOP2B significantly impacts the expression of TOP2B and its downstream signaling, ultimately affecting cell proliferation, oxidative stress, and senescence. These results offer novel insights into TOP2B's role in controlling cellular functions and highlight its potential involvement in cellular aging and disease processes. The loss of TOP2B via CRISPR-Cas9 caused a substantial reduction in its mRNA and protein expression, with the effects becoming more pronounced over time (48h, 72h, and 96h). Conditioned media from TOP2B-deficient cells reduced TOP2B expression in recipient cells, suggesting paracrine regulation possibly involving SP1 and NF–Y, although the underlying mechanisms remain to be defined. The downregulation of SP1 and NFY-A, transcription factors known to bind to the TOP2B promoter, suggests a possible feedback loop in which TOP2B not only regulates its own expression but also impacts the expression of genes crucial for its regulation, though more studies are needed in this direction. Specifically, SP1 showed a gradual decline in expression over time, while NFY-A expression fluctuated, pointing to a potentially complex and time-dependent regulatory network. Additionally, the observed increase in TOP2A expression at 48 h, followed by a decline, may indicate a compensatory mechanism or isoform switching in response to the loss of TOP2B.

The initial observation that TOP2B knockout HEK 293T cells failed to proliferate in vitro led us to investigate the functional consequences of TOP2B knockout, through assays for cell proliferation, ROS production, and senescence. The decreased expression of Ki-67, a well-known cell proliferation marker, alongside the decline in DiI dye intensity, strongly suggests a reduction in cell proliferation following TOP2B knockout. We also detected a substantial rise in reactive oxygen species levels in TOP2B–KO cells, particularly at 72 and 96 h. This increase in oxidative stress may contribute to cellular damage and could explain some of the phenotypic changes observed, including the induction of senescence. Fluorescence microscopy confirmed these findings, showing a pronounced ROS signal in TOP2B–KO cells.

Furthermore, the accumulation of SA-β-Gal activity in the TOP2B–KO cells at later time points (72h and 96h) supports the induction of a senescence phenotype. This was corroborated by the upregulation of cell cycle regulators p16, p21, and p53, which are canonical markers of senescence [37,48,49]. The observed dysregulation of apoptosis-related genes, such as the increased expression of BCL-2 and FOXO4, suggests that TOP2B knockout cells may also acquire resistance to apoptosis, a hallmark of senescent cells that may contribute to their long-term viability despite the stress [50,51]. Interestingly, conditioned media from the TOP2B–KO cells were able to affect healthy cells in a similar manner to the direct knockout of TOP2B, as indicated by decreased Ki-67 expression, altered DiI dye staining, and increased ROS production. These results imply that secreted factors from the senescent cells may play a role in propagating the senescence phenotype, consistent with the concept of the SASP, which needs further attention. Our data suggest that the cellular milieu in which TOP2B is depleted contains factors that influence neighboring cells, further implicating TOP2B in regulating not only intrinsic cellular processes but also intercellular communication. Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) is a critical marker of cellular health, and our results show a significant decline in MMP in both TOP2B–KO cells and those treated with TOP2B–KO conditioned media. This alteration in mitochondrial function is consistent with the senescence phenotype, where mitochondrial dysfunction often serves as both a marker and a contributor to cellular aging [52]. Apart from this, genes like TOP2A, NFY-A, and SP1 show a significant reduction in their expression after 48 h of conditioned media treatment, indicating a similar response to TOP2B–KO cells. Additionally, increased SA-β-Gal activity in conditioned media-treated cells further corroborates the idea that senescence-associated changes are transmissible and not confined to the knockout cells. RNA sequencing analysis revealed that TOP2B knockout induces upregulation of several key pathways associated with cellular stress responses, including p53, NF-κB, and MAPK signaling. These pathways are well-known regulators of senescence, apoptosis, and inflammation, which may explain the cellular phenotypes observed in this study [[53], [54], [55]]. Additionally, pathways related to immune responses, such as IL-17 signaling and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, were upregulated, suggesting that the immune system may be involved in response to TOP2B depletion [[56], [57], [58]]. These findings are consistent with prior studies that highlight the intersection of DNA damage, inflammation, and senescence in aging cells [59] [60]. Apart from this pathway, valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation is also downregulated in TOP2B–KO cells, thus accumulating inside cells during senescence, a phenomenon well known in senescence and aging [61]. Interestingly, we found that the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway was downregulated in KO-CM-treated cells, further supporting the senescence phenotype [62] [63]. We also observed that FOXO signaling was upregulated in KO-CM cells, as in previous studies, a strong driver of senescence and aging [51]. Together, these findings emphasize the importance of TOP2B for maintaining homeostasis in dividing cells. Finally, while our findings suggest that TOP2B influences both intrinsic cellular processes and intercellular communication, they do not establish whether TOP2B directly regulates canonical senescence pathways or acts indirectly via mitochondrial and transcriptional effects.

Limitations

While our data demonstrate that disruption of TOP2B expression is associated with induction of senescence-like phenotypes, the relative contribution of its canonical enzymatic activity in DNA topology versus its role in transcriptional regulation remains unclear. Future studies dissecting the distinct functions of TOP2B, along with the identification of downstream signaling pathways and secreted mediators such as pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and matrix-remodeling enzymes, will be critical to fully understand its role in cellular senescence. Future studies incorporating DNA damage markers such as γH2AX and cell-cycle analysis would further refine the mechanistic understanding of TOP2B-mediated senescence. Furthermore, HEK 293T cells offer a technically advantageous model due to their high transfection efficiency and rapid proliferation, they are transformed in nature and may not fully recapitulate normal cellular physiology. Consequently, the generalizability of the present findings to non-transformed systems remains to be established. Future studies in primary or non-transformed cell types, such as fibroblasts or epithelial cells, will be essential to validate the role of TOP2B in senescence under more physiologically relevant conditions.

Conclusions

Overall, our study emphasizes the multifaceted function of TOP2B in regulating cellular integrity, cell cycle progression, and senescence. The findings presented here offer new insights into how TOP2B contributes to cellular homeostasis and the development of age-related phenotypes. However, these results represent an early step, and further mechanistic and translational studies are needed to ascertain whether TOP2B is a direct driver of senescence or an upstream modulator of broader stress-response pathways. Future studies focusing on the precise mechanisms by which conditioned media influence cell fate decisions, as well as exploring the intercellular effects of senescent cells, will be essential in understanding the full impact of TOP2B depletion.
