High-definition transcranial direct current stimulation enhances exercise-induced hypoalgesia in patients with chronic low back pain
Authors: Haozhi Zhao, Chen Gong, Kaixia Gao, Beibei Feng, Yafei Wang, Yangyang Lin, Yuling Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryExercise-induced hypoalgesia is often attenuated in chronic pain. This randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial tested whether HD-tDCS over the left primary motor cortex enhances exercise-related hypoalgesic responses, as reflected by pressure pain threshold changes, during aerobic exercise in chronic low back pain. Fifty-two participants received active or sham HD-tDCS while cycling. Pressure pain thresholds and pain-evoked fNIRS responses were assessed before and after intervention. Active HD-tDCS produced larger pressure pain threshold increases than sham stimulation and attenuated pain-evoked activation in somatosensory, motor, and prefrontal regions. Reduced cortical activation correlated with increased pressure pain thresholds, and reduced left dlPFC activation mediated the PPT increase at the tibialis anterior site. These findings suggest that M1 HD-tDCS can augment acute exercise-related pressure pain threshold responses and modulate pain-evoked cortical activity in chronic low back pain.

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) continues to be a major contributor to global disability, representing 8% of all years lived with disability (YLDs) in 2020.1 Epidemiological data from 54 countries further estimate its point prevalence at 11.9% and one-month prevalence at 23.3%, with the number of affected individuals projected to reach 800 million by 2025.1,2 In addition to its health impact, LBP imposes substantial economic costs, with direct and indirect expenses exceeding $100 billion annually.3

Global clinical guidelines consistently recommend aerobic exercise as the first-line nonpharmacological intervention for managing chronic low back pain (CLBP).4,5,6 Exercise-induced hypoalgesia (EIH) is a primary analgesic mechanism underlying aerobic exercise.7 EIH refers to an increase in pain thresholds and a decrease in pain sensitivity occurring during or after exercise.8 Importantly, EIH should not be conflated with the longer-term reduction in clinical pain intensity observed after exercise training programs, and it remains uncertain to what extent the mechanisms underlying these two effects overlap. EIH is thought to involve endogenous pain modulation mechanisms, including the activation of the endogenous opioid and endocannabinoid systems, as well as the enhancement of descending inhibitory pathways.8,9 However, among individuals with chronic pain, this response tends to be diminished and exhibits substantial variability, with some patients even developing exercise-induced hyperalgesia.10,11 Therefore, identifying strategies to enhance EIH is crucial for improving the consistency and clinical utility of exercise-based analgesic interventions.

High-definition transcranial direct current stimulation (HD-tDCS) is a neuromodulatory technique that, when applied over the primary motor cortex (M1), has been shown to influence endogenous pain regulatory processes and reduce pain sensitivity in experimental settings.12,13 Although systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest that the effects of tDCS on long-term clinical pain intensity in individuals with chronic LBP are modest and inconsistent,14 accumulating evidence indicates that HD-tDCS may interact with exercise through complementary mechanisms involved in endogenous pain control.15 From a neuromodulatory perspective, HD-tDCS targeting M1 is more likely to influence central pain regulatory circuits, particularly descending inhibitory pathways, which are considered to contribute to EIH, rather than local intracortical inhibitory mechanisms within M1.16,17 Accordingly, leveraging HD-tDCS as a means to augment exercise-related central pain modulation may represent a rational approach to addressing the commonly observed attenuation of EIH in individuals with CLBP.

To examine how HD-tDCS modulates EIH in CLBP, monitoring cortical responses during pain is essential. Prior studies have shown that repeated noxious stimuli evoke hemodynamic changes in key pain-related areas, such as the primary somatosensory cortex (S1), M1, and prefrontal cortex (PFC).18,19 These changes reflect central pain processing and help reveal mechanisms of neuromodulation. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is a portable, noninvasive method for real-time monitoring of cortical oxygenation dynamics.20 In the context of pain research, fNIRS is a useful tool for assessing the activation and connectivity of pain-related cortical networks,21 offering insights into HD-tDCS effects in clinical populations.

Compared with other chronic pain conditions, relatively few studies have specifically examined EIH in individuals with CLBP, and available evidence suggests that EIH may be attenuated or highly variable in this population.7 Although several studies, including our own, have examined the effects of M1 HD-tDCS on EIH in healthy individuals, the findings remain inconsistent, and most lack objective neurophysiological measures.22,23 More importantly, substantial differences exist between healthy adults and patient populations, limiting the generalizability of these results. Therefore, it remains necessary to validate the effects of HD-tDCS on EIH, specifically in individuals with CLBP. This study aimed to assess whether HD-tDCS enhances EIH in patients with CLBP and to explore related cortical hemodynamic responses via fNIRS. We hypothesized that HD-tDCS would enhance EIH in individuals with CLBP and reduce cortical hemodynamic responses in S1, M1, and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) during noxious stimulation. These findings may guide clinical practice by clarifying the efficacy and neural mechanisms of this combined nonpharmacological approach, aiding CLBP management.

Active HD tDCS increased pressure pain thresholds

Linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects for Group and Time and a random intercept for Subject were used to analyze PPT at each measurement site.

At the lumbar sites, significant Group × Time interactions were observed at both L3 (F = 7.08, p = 0.010, ηp2 = 0.12) and L5 (F = 5.45, p = 0.023, ηp2 = 0.09), with greater pre-to postintervention increases in PPT in the Active group than in the Sham group. Significant Group × Time interactions were also found at the tibialis anterior (F = 5.06, p = 0.029, ηp2 = 0.09), gastrocnemius (F = 6.97, p = 0.011, ηp2 = 0.12), and forearm sites (F = 4.58, p = 0.037, ηp2 = 0.08), again reflecting larger postintervention PPT increases in the Active group. After correction for multiple comparisons across sites using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, all Group × Time interaction effects remained statistically significant (adjusted p < 0.05). fNIRS results

Baseline cortical responses were comparable between groups

To ensure baseline comparability, preintervention cortical activation in the 17 predefined ROI channels was compared between the active and sham groups using model-based simple effects derived from the linear mixed-effects model. No significant between-group differences were observed in any ROI channel at baseline (all p > 0.05; all q > 0.05), indicating good comparability of baseline hemodynamic responses (Figure S3).

Active HD tDCS reduced pain-evoked cortical activation

This study extracted changes in HbO2 levels during the 20–30 s period of the task phase as characteristic values. During the pressure pain test, neural activity was monitored across channels covering the S1, dlPFC, and M1, as defined by standard optode placement and stereotactic registration.

Significant group × time interactions were identified in eight channels after FDR correction, including S1-related channels CH1 (β = −0.063, q = 0.015), CH5 (β = −0.062, q = 0.015), CH7 (β = −0.061, q = 0.015), CH42 (β = −0.054, q = 0.027), and CH48 (β = −0.081, q = 0.015), the dlPFC-related channels CH35 (β = −0.050, q = 0.034) and CH44 (β = −0.049, q = 0.008), and the M1-related channel CH32 (β = −0.067, q = 0.015). These findings indicate that active HD-tDCS combined with aerobic exercise produced greater attenuation of pressure pain-evoked cortical activation than sham stimulation in somatosensory, prefrontal, and motor regions.

Follow-up model-based simple-effects analyses further showed significant pre-post reductions in the active group in 11 channels after FDR correction, including CH32 (estimate = −0.076, q < 0.001), CH44 (estimate = −0.043, q < 0.001), CH1 (estimate = −0.064, q < 0.001), CH7 (estimate = −0.056, q = 0.001), CH42 (estimate = −0.050, q = 0.002), CH40 (estimate = −0.057, q = 0.003), CH48 (estimate = −0.054, q = 0.009), CH5 (estimate = −0.041, q = 0.018), CH35 (estimate = −0.036, q = 0.028), CH10 (estimate = −0.025, q = 0.033), and CH9 (estimate = −0.026, q = 0.043). In contrast, no significant pre-post activation changes were observed in the sham group after correction. In addition, no significant between-group differences were found at baseline in any ROI channel.

At the postintervention time point, the active group exhibited significantly lower pain-evoked activation than the sham group in six channels after FDR correction, namely CH1 (estimate = −0.067, q = 0.004), CH44 (estimate = −0.045, q = 0.004), CH48 (estimate = −0.077, q = 0.004), CH42 (estimate = −0.059, q = 0.011), CH32 (estimate = −0.059, q = 0.016), and CH40 (estimate = −0.048, q = 0.049). From a functional perspective, S1-related channels are associated with sensory-discriminative processing of nociceptive input, dlPFC-related channels with cognitive-affective evaluation and top-down modulation of pain, and M1-related channels with motor regulation and descending pain modulation.

Reduced cortical activation was associated with increased PPT

To further examine the behavioral relevance of the revised fNIRS activation findings, correlation analyses were performed between ΔHbO2 (post-pre) in the eight ROI channels retained in the ROI-based LMM analysis and ΔPPT (post-pre) at each measurement site. Depending on data distribution, Pearson’s or Spearman correlation analyses were applied. To control for multiple comparisons, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was performed separately within each PPT site across the eight channels.

No significant correlations were observed for the L5 or forearm sites after correction. At the L3 site, significant negative correlations with ΔPPT were observed for CH35, CH42, and CH48 after FDR correction (all q = 0.032; Figures 6A–6C). At the tibialis anterior site, CH5 and CH44 were negatively correlated with ΔPPT, and both remained significant after FDR correction (CH5: r = −0.369, q = 0.028; CH44: r = −0.432, q = 0.011; Figures 6D and 6E). At the gastrocnemius site, CH44 and CH48 were also negatively correlated with ΔPPT, and both associations survived FDR correction (CH44: r = −0.391, q = 0.027; CH48: r = −0.371, q = 0.027; Figures 6F and 6G). Left dlPFC activation mediated the effect on PPT

The mediation analysis demonstrated a significant indirect pathway whereby group allocation (Active vs. Sham) influenced the ΔPPT at the tibialis anterior, with channel CH44 serving as the mediating variable (Figure 7). The indirect effect was statistically significant (estimate = 2.25; 95% CI [0.05, 4.97]; p = 0.042), even though the link between the mediator and the dependent variable (path b) was not significant (p = 0.134). A significant association was observed between the independent variable and the mediator (path a: β = −0.048; 95% CI [−0.077, −0.020]; p = 0.001), as well as between group assignment and changes in PPT (path c: β = 6.83; 95% CI [0.74, 12.70]; p = 0.028). These findings provide evidence of a significant indirect effect of group assignment on ΔPPT via reduced pain-evoked activation in the left dlPFC (CH44), suggesting that dlPFC suppression may be involved in the analgesic response to HD-tDCS combined with exercise.

Active and sham stimulation showed comparable tolerability

Across the total sample of 52 participants, no severe adverse effects occurred during the study period. The most commonly experienced sensations during stimulation were mild itching and tingling, reported by 69.2% of the participants in the HD-tDCS group and 50.0% in the control group; however, no statistically significant difference was detected (p = 0.158). Additional complaints following the intervention included transient skin redness (19.2% in the active group vs. 7.7% in the sham group), headache (3.8% vs. 0%), fatigue or drowsiness (both groups: ∼3.8%), nausea (7.7% vs. 3.8%), and slight concentration difficulties (7.7% vs. 3.8%). None of these outcomes differed significantly between the groups (see Table S4 for details).

Exercise intensity was comparable between groups

Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was performed to assess temporal changes in HR and RPE during the exercise session on the basis of measurements obtained every 5 min for both groups (Figure S5). Prior to analysis, boxplot inspection was used to identify and eliminate outliers. The normality assumption was satisfied, as confirmed by the Shapiro-Wilk test (p > 0.05). Since Mauchly’s test indicated a breach in the sphericity assumption across the four repeated measures (p < 0.05), adjustments were made via the Greenhouse-Geisser correction. No statistically meaningful group differences were observed for either HR (F = 0.010, p = 0.921) or RPE (F = 0.511, p = 0.478).

Discussion

Our findings indicate that HD-tDCS exerts a modulatory effect on experimentally measured pain sensitivity in individuals with CLBP. Relative to the sham condition, participants receiving active stimulation exhibited significantly greater pre-to-post increases in PPT, as reflected by significant Group × Time interaction effects. Neuroimaging data acquired via fNIRS further revealed significant group differences in cortical activation within the left M1, dlPFC, and bilateral S1 regions, whereas no significant between-group differences were observed in functional connectivity. Moreover, changes in PPTs were negatively correlated with cortical activation levels, suggesting that increased pain thresholds were associated with reduced cortical activation. Overall, these findings suggest that HD-tDCS combined with exercise may modulate pain sensitivity and pain-related cortical responses in CLBP.

With respect to EIH outcomes, we found significant increases in PPTs across most anatomical sites in both groups postintervention, except at the gastrocnemius and forearm sites in the sham group. Notably, the active group presented significantly greater increases in PPTs than the sham group did, indicating an enhancement of EIH by HD-tDCS. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies and our earlier work in healthy individuals.22,23,24 Unlike prior studies, this study focused specifically on the modulatory effect of HD-tDCS on EIH in CLBP. Taken together, these results support the view that applying HD-tDCS over the left M1 during aerobic exercise can enhance experimentally measured pain sensitivity responses. The synergistic analgesic effect of HD-tDCS and aerobic exercise may involve enhanced motor cortical excitability and the activation of descending pain modulatory pathways, which can modulate pain sensitivity beyond task-specific motor execution. While the exact mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated, both interventions have been shown to engage the corticospinal system and endogenous opioid networks.25,26 In contrast to previous tDCS-EIH studies employing isometric exercise, our use of dynamic aerobic exercise likely elicited broader systemic physiological responses, including endogenous opioid release and enhanced descending inhibition, providing a more favorable neural context for HD-tDCS to exert its effect.27,28 In addition, recent neuroimaging studies suggest that symptom improvement in chronic pain is associated with the reorganization of pain-modulatory networks, including regions showing spatial overlap with μ-opioid receptor expression.29 However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. In the present study, the behavioral outcome primarily reflected pain sensitivity, as measured by pressure pain threshold, rather than clinical pain intensity, pain-related disability, or long-term functional improvement. Therefore, although HD-tDCS combined with aerobic exercise was associated with greater increases in PPT, the present results should be viewed as supporting its potential as an adjunctive strategy for CLBP management, pending confirmation in studies evaluating clinically meaningful long-term outcomes. Clinically, EIH responses in patients with chronic pain vary substantially. This variability may partly reflect the heterogeneous nature of CLBP, including differences in baseline pain severity, pain duration, and psychosocial factors such as pain catastrophizing and depressive symptoms. Some patients exhibit impaired EIH, with exercise occasionally leading to increased pain sensitivity, a phenomenon known as exercise-induced hyperalgesia (EIH impairment).10,11 This phenomenon is thought to underlie the paradoxical worsening of pain in certain patients during physical activity,30,31 contributing to fear of movement, activity avoidance, and ultimately a vicious cycle of pain, disuse, and disability.32 Although aerobic exercise has demonstrated consistent analgesic effects in healthy individuals and certain musculoskeletal pain populations and is widely recommended in clinical guidelines,33 its effectiveness in treating CLBP remains limited by variability in individual responses8. Notably, however, not all individuals with chronic pain demonstrate impaired EIH. Variability in findings across studies may partly reflect differences in assessment methods (e.g., clinical pain ratings versus pressure pain thresholds). While some studies have reported EIH impairment in conditions such as painful diabetic neuropathy and chronic neck pain,34,35 others have reported preserved EIH responses in patients with rheumatoid arthritis and CLBP.36,37 In the present study, PPT increased across multiple sites following the intervention, suggesting enhanced exercise-related hypoalgesic responses at the group level. This pattern may be related to the relatively mild symptom severity of our sample and a more preserved endogenous pain modulatory system. Clinically, EIH responses across different body sites may reflect partially distinct pain modulatory mechanisms. Local PPT changes are thought to be more strongly influenced by segmental spinal mechanisms, whereas remote PPT responses are more dependent on supraspinal descending inhibitory control.38,39,40

In individuals with CLBP, previous studies have reported marked site-specific variability in EIH expression, suggesting that differences between local and remote pain responses are common and may reflect heterogeneity in central pain modulation rather than inconsistent treatment effects.41 From a neuromodulatory perspective, HD-tDCS applied over the primary motor cortex is more likely to affect supraspinal pain control circuits, including descending inhibitory pathways, rather than purely segmental spinal mechanisms. Accordingly, its effects may be more evident in pain responses relying on central inhibition, whereas local responses may exhibit greater inter-individual variability. This view is consistent with evidence that EIH in CLBP is largely determined by central pain modulation rather than uniform peripheral mechanisms,41,42 and data-driven fMRI studies further suggest that symptom improvement is accompanied by intrinsic connectivity reorganization within pain-modulatory networks, including μ-opioid receptor-rich systems.29 Future research should aim to determine whether these experimentally observed effects on pain sensitivity translate into meaningful improvements in clinical pain intensity, disability, and long-term outcomes, while also identifying optimal exercise modalities, intensities, and individual predictors of response.

With respect to neuroimaging outcomes, HD-tDCS combined with MAE significantly attenuated pain-evoked cortical activation in key brain regions implicated in pain processing, including the left M1, bilateral S1, and dlPFC. These findings are consistent with the view that HD-tDCS influences cortical processing of nociceptive input in regions involved in sensory-discriminative and cognitive-affective aspects of pain. Rather than reflecting isolated regional effects, the concurrent modulation of S1 and dlPFC may suggest coordinated changes across sensory-discriminative and cognitive-evaluative components of pain processing. These results align with previous evidence highlighting the critical role of the M1, S1, and dlPFC in the sensory-discriminative and affective-cognitive dimensions of pain.43,44,45 Specifically, reduced dlPFC activation might indicate decreased attentional engagement with nociceptive stimuli, driven by enhanced top-down modulation post intervention. Given the established role of the dlPFC in cognitive-evaluative pain processing and top-down pain regulation,43,44 this finding may reflect reduced cognitive engagement with nociceptive input following HD-tDCS combined with exercise. The observed reductions in the activation of S1 and M1 further support the reversal of maladaptive cortical hyperactivation commonly associated with chronic pain. In particular, reduced S1 activation may reflect attenuated sensory-discriminative processing of nociceptive input, consistent with the recognized role of S1 in pain localization and sensory encoding.45 Notably, the decreased hemodynamic responses in these areas were negatively correlated with improvements in ΔPPTs, reinforcing the link between cortical modulation and behavioral analgesia.44,46 Importantly, mediation analysis revealed that the analgesic effect of HD-tDCS may be partially mediated by reduced activation in the left dlPFC, suggesting a possible cross-regional modulatory pathway originating from M1 stimulation. This finding supports the hypothesis that HD-tDCS enhances “top-down” pain inhibition via the PFC, which is consistent with the role of the dlPFC in the cognitive-emotional regulation of pain. However, we note that mediation is statistical and does not establish causality. In contrast, functional connectivity did not show significant between-group differences in the present study. Although descriptive within-group patterns suggested reduced pain-related connectivity after intervention, these results should be interpreted cautiously and do not provide evidence for a differential FC effect of active stimulation relative to sham. Although between-group differences in functional connectivity were not observed, the concurrent attenuation of activation across sensorimotor and prefrontal regions may still support the possibility that HD-tDCS influences multiple pain-related cortical regions simultaneously. Prior work has demonstrated both positive and negative coactivation among the prefrontal, sensorimotor, and subcortical regions during pain processing.47,48,49 Our findings support the potential of HD-tDCS to modulate pain-related cortical activity. However, future studies employing advanced neuroimaging techniques such as fMRI with larger sample sizes and longer intervention periods are necessary to further investigate these network-level mechanisms.

This study has several key strengths. First, this study is the first to systematically explore the cortical mechanisms underlying enhanced EIH in patients with CLBP by integrating HD-tDCS with fNIRS, thereby addressing the lack of neurophysiological evidence on how neuromodulation modulates exercise-induced analgesia in chronic pain populations. Second, the use of HD-tDCS—offering superior focality and safety compared with conventional tDCS—enhances the precision and reliability of neuromodulatory interventions targeting pain-related cortical regions.

Overall, delivering 2 mA HD-tDCS to the left M1 concurrently with aerobic exercise was associated with greater postexercise increases in PPT in individuals with CLBP, consistent with enhanced exercise-related hypoalgesic responses. This enhancement is associated with attenuated activation in response to pain within the left M1, S1, dlPFC, and right S1. These findings support HD-tDCS combined with aerobic exercise as a promising strategy to increase the reliability of analgesic responses and promote exercise adherence in individuals with CLBP.

Limitations of the study

This study is subject to several limitations. One notable limitation is the modest sample size, which may have reduced the statistical sensitivity, especially in analyses of functional connectivity. Future research with larger participant groups is necessary to confirm these results. Second, the use of a standardized MAE protocol, although aligned with clinical guidelines for CLBP, may have constrained individual variability in physiological responses. Future research should consider personalized exercise regimens tailored to patient-specific characteristics to optimize treatment outcomes and adherence. Third, this was a single-session intervention study, which limits inferences regarding the long-term analgesic effects, optimal intervention frequency, and treatment duration of the combined HD-tDCS and exercise approach.

Accordingly, the present findings should be regarded as evidence of acute modulation of experimentally measured pain sensitivity and cortical responses, rather than proof of sustained clinical benefit. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to determine whether the observed acute enhancement of EIH translates into sustained clinical benefits with repeated or prolonged interventions.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

Participants with chronic low back pain were recruited from the community, outpatient clinics, and online platforms. The inclusion criteria were age between 18 and 45 years, right handedness, right leg dominance, and chronic low back pain lasting for at least 3 months, defined as pain located between the inferior margin of the 12th rib and the lower gluteal fold for more than 3 days per week, with activity limiting pain and a numerical rating scale score of 3 or higher. The exclusion criteria were radicular pain or low back pain secondary to serious medical conditions, such as infection, fracture, or malignancy; cardiovascular comorbidities; professional athletic status; use of analgesics or alcohol within 24 h before testing; severe depressive symptoms, defined as a Beck Depression Inventory II score greater than 29; active menstruation in female participants; and cranial conditions contraindicating electrode placement.

A total of 54 participants were enrolled and randomly assigned to the active or sham stimulation group at a 1:1 ratio. Fifty-two participants were included in the final analyses. The final sample included 25 males and 27 females, with a mean age of 28.22 ± 1.55 years and mean education duration of 17.10 ± 1.20 years. The active group included 26 participants, with 13 males and 13 females, and the sham group included 26 participants, with 12 males and 14 females. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, body mass index, education years, pain duration, pain intensity, disability, pain sensitivity, psychological measures, and physical activity, were generally comparable between groups and are reported in Table 1.

Given the modest sample size, this study was not powered to examine sex-specific intervention effects. Sex distribution was compared between groups at baseline, and no significant between-group difference was observed. Therefore, the potential influence of sex on treatment response should be interpreted as a limitation and warrants further investigation in larger samples. Information on ancestry, race, and ethnicity was not collected because these variables were not prespecified in the approved study protocol.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat sen University, Guangzhou, China, with the approval number 2024ZSLYEC-432. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Method details

Study design

This was a single center, randomized, double blind, sham controlled, parallel group clinical trial. Participants were assigned to receive either active high-definition transcranial direct current stimulation or sham stimulation over the left primary motor cortex during a single session of moderate intensity aerobic exercise. Data collection and intervention were conducted from March to November 2024.

The primary outcome was pressure pain threshold. Secondary outcomes included functional near infrared spectroscopy derived pain evoked cortical activation and functional connectivity. Outcomes were assessed immediately before and after the intervention. Sample size

According to the meta-analysis conducted by Naugle et al.,38 acute exercise exerts a variable effect on PPT in individuals with chronic pain, with reported effect sizes generally ranging from moderate to large (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.5–1.0). Although most available studies employed within-subject designs, these findings provide a reasonable reference for the expected magnitude of acute exercise-induced changes in pain sensitivity. Together with previous reports indicating that the combination of tDCS and exercise may further enhance EIH,23 as well as expert input, we specified an effect size of d = 0.80 as a moderate-to-large planning assumption. Sample size estimation was performed using PASS 2021, assuming a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05, statistical power of 0.80, and a 1:1 group allocation ratio, which indicated a minimum required sample size of 26 participants per group (total n = 52). To account for potential attrition, a total of 54 participants were ultimately enrolled (27 per group).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The eligibility criteria for participants included the following: (1) between 18 and 45 years of age; (2) right-handed and right-leg dominant50,51; and (3) CLBP, defined as posteriorly localized pain between the inferior margin of the 12th rib and the lower gluteal fold, occurring more than three days per week for at least three months with activity-limiting intensity and a pain severity score of ≥3 on a 0–10 numerical rating scale.52 The exclusion criteria included (1) the presence of radicular pain or LBP secondary to serious medical conditions (e.g., infection, fracture, or malignancy); (2) cardiovascular comorbidities (e.g., hypertensive heart disease); (3) professional athletic status; (4) recent use of analgesics or alcohol within 24 h prior to testing; (5) severe depressive symptoms (Beck Depression Inventory-II score >29); (6) active menstruation (for female participants); and (7) cranial conditions contraindicating electrode placement.

Randomization and blinding

Eligible participants were randomly assigned to the active or sham stimulation group at a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated randomization sequence. The sequence was generated by an independent investigator who was not involved in participant recruitment, intervention delivery, outcome assessment, or data analysis. Allocation concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes. Participants were enrolled by one investigator and assigned to the intervention by another investigator who opened the envelope after baseline assessment.

This trial was double blinded with respect to participants and outcome assessors. Participants were unaware of their group allocation throughout the experiment. Outcome assessors who performed pressure pain threshold testing and fNIRS assessment were blinded to group allocation and were not involved in stimulation delivery. The investigator responsible for delivering stimulation did not participate in outcome assessment or statistical analysis. Sham stimulation was used to mimic the sensory experience of active stimulation, with identical electrode placement and brief ramp up and ramp down periods. The success of participant blinding was evaluated after the intervention by asking participants to identify whether they believed they had received active or sham stimulation, and guessing accuracy was compared between groups.

Experimental procedures

All the experimental procedures were conducted in a quiet, temperature-controlled laboratory maintained at 23°C–26°C. The participants were instructed to avoid strenuous physical activity, as well as the intake of caffeine or tobacco, within 24 h prior to the scheduled session. Upon arrival, participants provided demographic information and completed a series of baseline self-report instruments, including the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS, 0–10) for average pain intensity over the past 7 days, International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS), Pain Sensitivity Questionnaire (PSQ), Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK), Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II), and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). Leg dominance was assessed via a standardized bilateral push protocol50, and hand dominance was determined via the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory.53

After familiarization with the testing procedures, baseline pain sensitivity was evaluated. The participants then underwent a single-session intervention combining HD-tDCS and moderate-intensity aerobic exercise (MAE), with group allocation determining the stimulation type (active vs. sham). The entire session lasted approximately 30 min. Primary and secondary outcomes were collected immediately before and after the intervention, with PPT designated as the primary outcome and fNIRS derived cortical activation and functional connectivity as secondary outcomes. In this study, EIH was inferred from the pattern of repeated pre- and postexercise PPT measurements and statistically evaluated using the Group × Time interaction in the linear mixed-effects model; standardized verbal encouragement was provided throughout the intervention to ensure protocol adherence. All PPT measurements were conducted by the same trained assessor to avoid interrater variability. Intervention

HD-tDCS

The neurostimulation protocol utilized the Neuroelectrics Starstim 8 HD-tDCS system. Electrode placement followed the international 10–10 EEG system, with the anode positioned over C3 to target the left M1. Stimulation was delivered for 20 min, incorporating 30-s ramp-up and ramp-down phases. Four cathodes (−500 μA each) were arranged in a standard 4 × 1 ring configuration at F3, Cz, P3, and T7 around the center electrode to help circumscribe the stimulated region and improve focality (Figure S1).54 The return current was evenly distributed across the four cathodes to maintain current balance and reduce current density and sensation at any single return site.55 In the sham condition, electrodes were placed identically, but current was applied only during the initial and final 30s of the session—a standard procedure to mimic the tingling sensation associated with active stimulation without producing lasting neuromodulatory effects.56,57 The stimulation montage and dosing parameters used in EIH and analgesia were prespecified on the basis of prior HD-tDCS literature. Safety procedures included antiseptic skin preparation and baseline impedance checks (<10 kΩ), with participants excluded if they had intracranial metal implants or skin conditions at stimulation sites.58 The impedance and physiological parameters were monitored continuously to optimize current delivery. Poststimulation procedures included skin assessments and systematic documentation of adverse events.

MAE

During tDCS, participants engaged in a standardized 30-min MAE protocol via a power-controlled cycle ergometer. This aerobic exercise protocol has previously been shown to effectively induce EIH.38 Resting heart rate (HR) was measured via a Polar Electro H10 monitor prior to exercise, which also continuously tracked HR during the session. Exercise intensity was individually prescribed on the basis of the American College of Sports Medicine equation (HRmax = 220 − age), aiming for 64%–76% of Hrmax.59 The workup commenced with a 5-min warm-up, assisted by a visual interface displaying the target cadence range (55–65 RPM). Resistance (and thus external power) was dynamically adjusted in real time according to each participant’s heart rate to maintain the prescribed moderate-intensity zone (64–76% HRmax). During the 20-min main phase, heart rate and resistance were regulated through Bluetooth 5.0 closed-loop control. Heart rate was recorded every 5 min along with a self-reported rating of perceived exertion (RPE). The cooldown phase employed a stepwise reduction in resistance (15% every 30s) until the ergometer fully stopped, promoting cardiovascular recovery.

Outcome measures

Pressure pain threshold

PPT was evaluated via a handheld algometer (Wagner FPX, USA) equipped with a 1 cm2 rubber tip, with pressure applied at a constant rate of approximately 1 N/s. The anatomical landmarks for measurement included (1) 3 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle of the right forearm, (2) the tibialis anterior, located 5 cm lateral to the tibial tuberosity, (3) the midpoint of the gastrocnemius muscle between the right heel and the popliteal fossa, and (4) sites 5 cm lateral to the spinous processes at lumbar vertebrae L3 and L5. These sites were prespecified from prior literature to capture primary (local) and secondary (remote) hyperalgesia.60 For clarity, the latter are simply denoted as “L3” and “L5” in this study. PPT was measured bilaterally before and after the intervention. The participants were instructed to signal when pain was initially perceived, at which point the pressure application was immediately stopped, and the corresponding force value (in newtons) was recorded. To minimize observer bias, the algometer screen was hidden from the participants during testing. Each site was tested three times with 15-s intervals between trials, and the means of the three values were calculated for analysis. All the evaluators underwent unified training to ensure reliability and reduce interrater variability. Importantly, previous studies have demonstrated good to excellent within-session test–retest reliability of PPT in both individuals with chronic low back pain and healthy populations, with intraclass correlation coefficients typically exceeding 0.80.61 These findings support the use of PPT as a reliable primary outcome for detecting short-term changes in pain sensitivity.

fNIRS measurement during pressure pain

Cerebral oxyhemoglobin (HbO2) levels were continuously captured via a 49-channel multichannel fNIRS system (NirSmart-3000A; Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China), which emits continuous-wave light at two wavelengths (730 ± 5 nm and 850 ± 5 nm) with a sampling rate of 11 Hz. The optode configuration included 14 sources‒detector pairs placed following the international 10–20 EEG standard, spaced 3 cm apart to enable cortical depth measurements of up to 3 cm.62 Notably, optodes D3, D11, and S7 corresponded to FPz, C3, and C4, respectively (see Figure S2 for details).

During resting-state fNIRS acquisition, participants were instructed to remain relaxed and still in an upright seated posture with forward gaze fixation, while avoiding voluntary movements of the head and upper limbs. Following calibration, resting-state hemodynamic data were acquired for 5 min from predefined bilateral ROIs, including the dlPFC, M1, and S1, identified using standard stereotactic coordinates.

The block-design pressure pain paradigm applied during fNIRS acquisition was adapted from a previously published fNIRS study in individuals with chronic low back pain.19 During fNIRS acquisition, pressure pain stimulation was delivered at an individualized intensity, defined as the average baseline PPT of each participant, to account for inter-individual differences in pain sensitivity. The task protocol comprised six cycles lasting 300 s in total, each consisting of 20 s of noxious mechanical pressure followed by a 30-s rest interval (Figure 2B). During task execution, participants were placed in a prone position with the lumbar area exposed. Manual pressure was applied perpendicularly to a site located 5 cm lateral to the intersection between the Jacoby line and the line connecting the highest points of the iliac crests.

fNIRS data processing and analysis

fNIRS data analysis included signal preprocessing, activation analysis, and functional connectivity assessment. Preprocessing included converting raw light signals into optical density and correcting motion artifacts through dynamic thresholding and spline interpolation. A 0.01–0.2 Hz bandpass filter was applied to remove low-frequency physiological noise, and HbO2 changes were computed via the modified Beer–Lambert law.63 For activation analysis, HbO2 responses during pressure pain stimulation were extracted as characteristic activation values for the 17 predefined ROI channels. The functional connectivity analysis focused on 17 predefined ROIs, and Pearson correlation coefficients (r ≥ 0.5) were calculated between ROI channels to construct functional brain network models and quantify neural coupling strength. The cortical regions and Brodmann area proportions corresponding to each channel are provided in Table S1 for details.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical details of experiments

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 and R version 4.4.2. Sample size estimation was performed using PASS 2021. Unless otherwise specified, n refers to the number of individual participants. Fifty-two participants were included in the final analyses. For pressure pain threshold measurements, each site was tested three times.

Statistical details, including the statistical tests used, exact n values, test statistics, degrees of freedom, p values, FDR adjusted q values, effect sizes, and confidence intervals where applicable, are reported in the results section, figure legends, Tables 1 and 2, and supplemental tables. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation when normally distributed and as median with interquartile range when non normally distributed. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Statistical significance was defined as two tailed p < 0.05 for uncorrected analyses and q < 0.05 for false discovery rate corrected analyses.

Randomization and allocation

Participants were randomly assigned to the active or sham stimulation group at a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated randomization sequence. The sequence was generated by an independent investigator who was not involved in participant recruitment, intervention delivery, outcome assessment, or data analysis. Allocation concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes. No stratification was used.

Sample size estimation

Sample size was estimated using PASS 2021. According to the meta-analysis conducted by Naugle et al.37, showing moderate to large exercise induced changes in pressure pain threshold in chronic pain populations and prior reports suggesting that transcranial direct current stimulation may enhance exercise induced hypoalgesia, an effect size of d = 0.80 was used as a planning assumption. With a two sided alpha level of 0.05, statistical power of 0.80, and a 1:1 allocation ratio, the required sample size was 26 participants per group, yielding a total sample size of 52 participants. To account for potential attrition, 54 participants were enrolled.

Inclusion and exclusion of participants and data

The inclusion and exclusion criteria for participants are described in the experimental model and study participant details section. Briefly, eligible participants were adults aged 18 to 45 years with chronic low back pain lasting for at least 3 months. Participants were excluded if they had radicular pain, low back pain secondary to serious medical conditions, cardiovascular comorbidities, professional athletic status, recent use of analgesics or alcohol within 24 h before testing, severe depressive symptoms, active menstruation in female participants, or cranial conditions contraindicating electrode placement.

The final analyses included participants who completed the intervention and had available pre and post outcome measurements. No imputation was performed for missing data.

Baseline comparisons

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro Wilk test. Baseline continuous variables were compared between groups using independent samples t tests when the normality assumption was met. Non normally distributed continuous variables were compared using nonparametric tests. Categorical variables were compared using continuity corrected chi square tests or Fisher exact tests when appropriate.

Pressure pain threshold analysis

The primary outcome was pressure pain threshold. For each anatomical site, pressure pain threshold was analyzed using a linear mixed effects model. Group, Active versus Sham, Time, Pre versus Post, and the Group × Time interaction was included as fixed effects. Subject was included as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements within participants.

The Group × Time interaction was considered the primary inferential effect because it tested whether pre to post changes in pressure pain threshold differed between the active and sham groups. Effect sizes for Group × Time interactions were reported as partial eta squared. Multiple comparisons across pressure pain threshold sites were controlled using the Benjamini Hochberg false discovery rate procedure.

fNIRS activation analysis

For fNIRS activation analysis, HbO2 values from the 17 predefined ROI channels were analyzed using linear mixed effects models. Group, Time, and the Group × Time interaction was included as fixed effects, and Subject was included as a random intercept. The Group × Time interaction was considered the primary inferential effect for each channel.

Benjamini Hochberg false discovery rate correction was applied across the 17 ROI channels, with q < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Follow up model-based contrasts were used to examine pre to post changes within each group and between group differences at each time point. False discovery rate correction was applied within each family of follow up comparisons.

Functional connectivity analysis

Functional connectivity analyses were treated as descriptive and exploratory. The current fNIRS processing pipeline provided only group averaged connectivity matrices. Therefore, formal subject level Group × Time mixed effects modeling was not feasible for functional connectivity outcomes.

Exercise intensity analysis

Heart rate and rating of perceived exertion during exercise were analyzed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA, with Group as the between subject factor and Time as the within subject factor. Mauchly’s test was used to assess sphericity. Greenhouse Geisser correction was applied when the sphericity assumption was violated. Outliers were inspected using boxplots before analysis.

Correlation and mediation analyses

Associations between changes in cortical HbO2 and changes in pressure pain threshold were examined using Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses, depending on data distribution. Benjamini Hochberg false discovery rate correction was applied separately within each pressure pain threshold site across the selected channels.

Mediation analyses were conducted to explore whether changes in cortical activation statistically mediated the relationship between group allocation and changes in pressure pain threshold. Mediation models were implemented using the mediation package in R. Group allocation was entered as the independent variable, pressure pain threshold change as the dependent variable, and selected cortical activation changes as candidate mediators. Indirect effects and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using nonparametric bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples.
