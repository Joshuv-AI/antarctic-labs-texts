Development and quality appraisal of entrustable professional activities for anticancer therapy nursing: groundwork for competency-based oncology education
Authors: Ying-Siou Lin, Jung-Chen Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe growing global cancer burden has intensified the need for nurses who can safely deliver complex systemic anticancer therapies. Competency-based education offers a structured way to define measurable learning outcomes, and entrustable professional activities (EPAs) translate these competencies into observable, assessable clinical tasks. However, oncology-specific EPA frameworks remain limited, particularly for systemic anticancer therapy nursing. This study aimed to develop a set of oncology-specific EPAs for systemic anticancer therapy (SACT) nursing and to evaluate their structural quality through expert consensus and structured quality appraisal, providing groundwork for competency-based oncology education.MethodsThis multi-phase methodological study was guided by a published 12-step EPA development framework, focusing on the development and pre-implementation phases (preparation, drafting, and quality appraisal). Core systemic anticancer therapy nursing tasks were employed to define the scope of EPA development and quality appraisal of EPA specifications. Draft EPAs were generated using a nominal group process and refined through a modified Delphi survey involving 22 participants. Structural quality was evaluated by a separate panel of six oncology experts using the Traditional Chinese version of the Entrustable Professional Activity Quality (EQual) rubric across three domains. EPAs that did not meet prespecified thresholds underwent iterative revision and re-appraisal.ResultsConsensus procedures produced seven EPAs for systemic anticancer therapy nursing. Initial EQual appraisal identified weaknesses primarily in the “discrete units of work” domain, particularly for a broad EPA on anticancer drug administration. In response, this EPA was decomposed into three phase-specific activities to enhance observability and assessment fidelity. After revision, all seven EPAs met or exceeded overall and domain-level EQual criteria.ConclusionsIntegrating consensus-based development with structured quality appraisal yielded oncology-specific EPAs that are clinically relevant, structurally robust, and ready for piloting in clinical education and practice. The methodological contribution is the integration of consensus-based development with EQual-guided iterative refinement, including a rubric-anchored example of EPA decomposition. These EPAs provide a foundation for pilot implementation in competency-based SACT nursing education. Whether they ultimately support role clarity, feedback specificity, entrustment decisions, and patient-centred care will require future empirical study.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04958-5.Keywords: Systemic anticancer therapy, Competency-based education, EQual rubric, Entrustable professional activities, EPA, Workplace-based assessment, Curriculum development

Background

The global burden of cancer is projected to rise sharply in the coming decades. A 2024 World Health Organization report, drawing on International Agency for Research on Cancer estimates, predicts that annual new cancer cases will increase by 77%, from 20 million in 2022 to more than 35 million by 2050 [1]. Taiwan reflects this trend, where cancer has been the leading cause of death since 1982 [2]. This growing burden, in conjunction with nursing workforce shortages, underscores the urgent need for high-quality, patient-centered cancer care, especially the safe management of increasingly complex systemic anticancer therapy (SACT) regimens. To address evolving clinical and workforce needs, medical and nursing education have increasingly adopted competency-based education (CBE), which focuses on clearly defined learning outcomes and demonstrated competencies rather than time-based training. This approach better prepares healthcare professionals for the complexities of modern practice [3–5]. Of particular relevance to oncology nursing, the ASCO–ONS antineoplastic therapy administration safety standards [6] organize SACT safety around four interlocking domains: a safe care environment; patient consent and education; ordering, preparing, dispensing, and administering; and monitoring for adherence, toxicity, and complications. Taken together, these standards underscore that nursing competence must integrate across these domains within a single patient encounter, and is therefore most meaningfully judged at the level of complete clinical activities rather than discrete tasks.

Cancer care spans the full illness trajectory which is from diagnosis and active treatment through remission, relapse, and end-of-life care, requiring holistic attention to patients’ medical, psychological, social, and emotional needs. Nurses play a pivotal role within multidisciplinary oncology teams by integrating complex treatment regimens with person-centred support and continuity of care [7]. To ensure safe, patient-centred SACT, postgraduate oncology nursing education must foster domain-specific knowledge, critical thinking, self-reflection, interprofessional collaboration, and effective communication. Nevertheless, a fundamental challenge persists. How can postgraduate training be optimally structured such that oncology nurses reliably achieve and demonstrate these competencies in real clinical work?

Within this CBE paradigm, entrustable professional activities (EPAs) were introduced as units of professional work that translate abstract competencies into observable, assessable clinical tasks [3–5]. Nonetheless, despite the widespread adoption of CBE in clinical training, the development of oncology-specific EPAs for SACT nursing remains limited [8]. This shortfall perpetuates a critical gap in how core competencies are defined and assessed to support safe, effective practice in high-stakes treatment settings.

In nursing, the American Association of Colleges of Nursing (AACN) Essentials: Core Competencies for Professional Nursing Education provide a national CBE framework that defines competency as the integration of knowledge, skills, values, and attitudes across four spheres of care and ten competence domains [9]. EPAs offer a practical mechanism to implement the AACN Essentials in clinical settings by converting broad competence domains into clearly specified activities that can be supervised, observed, and evaluated [3–5, 9]. This alignment is particularly salient in high-risk areas such as SACT where failure to achieve the required level of entrustment can directly compromise patient safety.

Multiple cancer nursing competency frameworks have been developed in high-income contexts [10], including the ONS Oncology Nurse Generalist Competencies [11], the EONS Cancer Nursing Education Framework [12], and the UKONS Systemic Anti-Cancer Therapy (SACT) Competency Passport [13]; however, these are generally organized as statements of knowledge, skills, and attitudes, course-level outcomes, or procedural checklists, and do not include supervision-graded entrustment scales or integrate multiple competencies into observable units of professional work [10]. In contrast, EPAs translate discrete competencies into observable, entrustable units of work anchored to a supervision scale, thereby enhancing patient safety and providing a structured means for educators to confirm that learners’ performance meets professional expectations [14–16]. In nursing education, however, a recent scoping review identified 16 areas in which EPAs have been developed; only emergency or intensive care and palliative care represent specialty contexts, and oncology is absent [8]. Taken together, these observations expose two complementary gaps: a structural gap in current cancer nursing frameworks, and a specialty gap in nursing EPA development. An oncology-specific, fit-for-purpose EPA framework for SACT nursing is positioned to address both.

Recent scoping reviews have outlined systematic approaches to EPA development in health professions education. Shorey et al. reported that EPA development typically begins with identifying relevant competencies and practice requirements, progresses through expert workshops and iterative Delphi consensus, and yields EPA units with clearly defined descriptors and supervision expectations [17]. Bramley and McKenna similarly observed that most initiatives employ modified Delphi processes and educator-led workshops involving multiple stakeholders, mapping EPAs to competencies, milestones, and expected levels of performance [14]. Pietsch et al. further demonstrated that nursing EPAs are frequently derived from the nursing care process and refined through expert consensus, with some frameworks specifying supervision levels for implementation [8]. However, these studies also reveal considerable methodological diversity and variable rigor in how EPAs are conceptualized, specified, and quality-assured across contexts [8, 14, 17].

To address these challenges, Hennus et al. proposed 12 practical tips organized across four phases (preparation, drafting, quality control, and curriculum alignment) to guide educators in developing high-quality EPAs that are observable, assessable, and sustainable within curricula [18]. Drawing on experience across health professions, their framework emphasizes conceptual clarity, stakeholder engagement, and formal quality appraisal as cornerstones of robust EPA design [18]. Although the 12-step guide was published shortly after the initiation of our consensus-building work, its principles closely align with our approach and provided a useful lens for structuring the later stages of EPA refinement and quality evaluation.

Taken together, the literature indicates growing interest in EPAs for nursing and provides clear guidance on their development [8, 14, 17–18]; however, a substantive gap remains. To our knowledge, this study is the first to report an oncology-specific, fit-for-purpose EPA framework for SACT nursing that has been systematically appraised using a validated EPA quality rubric. The framework is intended to support the training of newly graduated registered nurses (NGNs) during their first two years of practice on inpatient wards caring for patients receiving SACT and is designed for use by oncology nurse educators, clinical supervisors, and curriculum designers. This study therefore aimed to (1) develop a set of oncology-specific EPAs for SACT nursing using structured consensus methods, and (2) evaluate their structural quality through a formal rubric-based appraisal to ensure that the EPAs are ready for piloting in clinical education and practice. We anticipated that integrating consensus-building with iterative, rubric-guided refinement would yield EPAs that meet predefined quality criteria and provide a robust foundation for competency-based oncology nursing education.

Methods

Study design

This methodological study was conducted from August 2022 to October 2023 and followed the twelve-step EPA development framework proposed by Hennus et al. in 2023 [18]. It focused on the development and pre-implementation phases, integrating preparation and drafting with a structured quality control phase to support later clinical implementation [18]. As the study was limited to these phases, we did not include external stakeholder consultation for interprofessional content validation (e.g., physicians or pharmacists). The EPAs were developed specifically for adult patients receiving SACT in inpatient settings, addressing responsibilities specific to inpatient SACT care, including the recognition and management of SACT-related complications. Outpatient chemotherapy administration, the dedicated HSCT transplant unit, and pediatric oncology were not included in this study’s scope.

The preparation and drafting phases focused on consensus-building activities using the Nominal Group Technique (NGT) to generate and refine EPAs specific to oncology nursing care for patients receiving SACT. The quality control phase employed the Traditional Chinese version of the Entrustable Professional Activity Quality (EQual) rubric as a structured quality check to ensure that the finalized EPAs were robust and aligned with established educational standards [19–20]. Consistent with the twelve-step framework’s adaptive nature, the development process was iterative rather than strictly linear, allowing the research team to revisit and refine earlier stages as needed throughout the study [18].

Setting and participants

The study was conducted at a university-affiliated medical center in northern Taiwan. Data were drawn from inpatient oncology and hematology-oncology wards where SACT is routinely delivered. In this setting, the EPAs are intended for a defined group of learners, whose expected baseline competence we specify here because entrustment decisions depend on the learner’s prior education, clinical exposure, and institutional role expectations. The framework targets NGNs during their first two years of inpatient SACT practice. All NGNs held a baccalaureate nursing degree and a valid Taiwan registered nurse licence. Within three months of hire, they completed an institutional SACT Nursing Training Course combining asynchronous digital modules with preceptor-supervised assessment of core SACT procedures (Supplementary Table 1). On completion, NGNs delivered SACT care alongside their preceptors, with supervision progressively reduced as institutional entrustment criteria were met.

To develop EPAs for these learners, participants were recruited voluntarily and purposively from inpatient oncology and hematology-oncology wards where SACT is routinely delivered, ensuring representation from both contexts. The panel comprised 19 clinical nurse preceptors, two hematology-oncology nurse practitioners, and one oncology nurse practitioner, all holding formal responsibilities for clinical supervision and education [21]. Preceptors were eligible if they had at least three years of clinical experience and prior experience orienting NGNs.

For the EQual evaluation phase, prespecified criteria required (i) representation across oncology and hematology-oncology inpatient SACT, spanning ward-based practice and nursing-department or hospital education and supervisory roles; (ii) expertise in oncology nursing education with completion of EPA-related training; and (iii) independence from the research team. Six experts were recruited accordingly. Two had contributed to the EPA development consensus phases (NGT and modified Delphi); the remaining four had not, comprising a nursing-department EPA development group member, a hematology-oncology unit nurse promoting EPA concepts, a nurse with graduate training in nursing education research, and a nurse responsible for hospital oncology nursing education. Although their EPA implementation experience varied, all could apply CBE and EPA principles using the Traditional Chinese version of the EQual rubric.

EPA development: integrated preparation and drafting

Nominal group meetings were held between August and December 2022, integrating the preparation and drafting phases. Owing to the rotating shifts of clinical nurse preceptors, several meetings were scheduled to accommodate their availability. At the start of each meeting, the first author delivered a brief introduction on the principles of CBE, the EPA concept, and NGT procedures to ensure participants shared an understanding of the EPA framework and the structured consensus process [4–8].

Five NGT sessions were held with 22 participants (3–6 per session). Each session followed a set sequence as follows: silent idea generation, round-robin reporting, clarification, consolidation, and voting. During silent idea generation, participants responded to a standardised prompt presented verbatim: (i) identify routine EPAs within the context of SACT care and (ii) specify each activity’s constituent specifications. Participants independently drafted candidate EPA titles and corresponding specifications that represented core SACT nursing practice.

Candidate EPA titles were collected through structured round-robin reporting and recorded by the research team on a whiteboard. Using the same round-robin format, participants stated the corresponding specifications, which were recorded in parallel; no discussion occurred during either step. Clarification and consolidation followed, during which participants explained their proposed EPA titles and specifications. Titles judged to represent the same activity, based on similarity in both title and specification content, were consolidated into a single EPA; further, overlapping specifications were integrated. The facilitator merged content only after confirming that no participant objected; otherwise, distinct EPA titles and specifications were retained in parallel.

Prioritization was conducted using anonymous weighted voting. Each participant selected five EPAs for further development, assigning 5 points to the first-ranked EPA and 1 point to the fifth-ranked EPA. For each EPA, we calculated the vote count (number of participants selecting the EPA) and the total weighted score (sum of assigned points). EPAs were ranked primarily by vote count and secondarily by total weighted score. To maintain feasibility, the top five EPAs from each session were retained; although no ties occurred at the cut-off, the protocol specified that tied EPAs would also be retained. Borderline EPAs that were not clearly SACT-specific were retained within a session if no participant objected and were carried forward to the pooled cross-session list. “Borderline” titles were those with specifications that did not consistently align with the SACT scope, while titles were classified as non–SACT-specific when their specifications primarily reflected general nursing activities rather than SACT care. Before the title-rating survey, pooled titles were screened to remove broad nursing activities that were not SACT-specific (e.g., general medication administration and routine bedside procedures) and were, therefore, outside this EPA set’s intended scope. At this stage, cross-session pooling was limited to compiling titles and conducting scope screening; specifications were retained verbatim as recorded within each session and were not harmonised across sessions.

After the NGT sessions, we administered a structured survey between 8 and 21 November 2022 to confirm consensus on the preliminary EPA titles (Table 2); only these titles were included as rating items. Of the 22 participants in the NGT sessions, six declined further participation, resulting 16 valid responses. Participants rated each title on two 5-point Likert scales for learning necessity (1 = not at all necessary; 5 = highly necessary) and workplace observability (1 = not observable in practice; 5 = fully observable), with the alternative to provide free-text comments.

We computed descriptive statistics (mean, median, and standard deviation [SD]) using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Mean ratings for learning necessity and workplace observability were utilized to prioritize the preliminary EPA titles (Table 2), and the five highest-rated EPAs across both domains were selected for further development.

Based on the survey findings, the first author drafted specification statements for the selected EPA titles by extracting specification-level content from the NGT records and participants’ free-text comments, consolidating overlapping descriptions such that each statement described a single EPA-related activity. The draft statements were cross-checked with institutional standard operating procedures and relevant international guidelines before inclusion in the modified Delphi survey for consensus rating.

In the subsequent modified Delphi survey, the same 16 participants rated each specification statement for relevance, specificity, and measurability in practice using a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not relevant/specific/measurable; 4 = highly relevant/specific/measurable). The Delphi items, comprising these specification statements, are presented in Supplementary Table 2. Participants also provided free-text comments on potential limitations, patient safety risks, related competency domains, and the knowledge, skills, and attitudes needed for entrustment decisions. These competency domains were mapped to the AACN Essentials: Core Competencies for Professional Nursing Education, which define nursing competence as the integration of knowledge, skills, values, and attitudes across professional practice areas [9]. This mapping ensured that each EPA descriptor was conceptually aligned with AACN competency domains, including patient-centred care, interprofessional collaboration, clinical decision-making, and safety.

We prespecified the consensus criteria and analysis plan. The modified Delphi procedure permitted up to three rounds, with additional rounds undertaken only if any item failed to satisfy the prespecified consensus thresholds. Because all statements met the thresholds across the three rating domains in the first round, only one round was required. Within each rating domain (relevance, specificity, and measurability), consensus was defined as an agreement proportion (ratings of 3–4) of at least 75% and a quartile deviation (QD) of less than 0.6. To support interpretation, we also reported the mean (SD) and median for each item in each domain. Descriptive analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0. Supplementary Table 2 presents the item-level results.

The research team drafted EPA descriptions following the recommended eight-section framework for full EPA descriptions [15]; the framework and the intended content of each section are detailed in Supplementary Table 3. Free-text comments from the modified Delphi survey informed only wording-level refinements, which are reflected in the final item wording. Supplementary Appendix 1 provides the complete EPA descriptions organized under these headings.

A supervision framework was implemented to guide assessment and support progressive autonomy. The entrustment scale ranged from Level 1, where the learner performed the activity with the supervisor under direct supervision, to Level 5, which indicated full independence and the ability to supervise junior trainees. Intermediate levels followed the postgraduate entrustment–supervision scale proposed by ten Cate et al. [22] Level 2 indicated continuous direct, proactive supervision with assistance; Level 3 indicated indirect, reactive supervision with post hoc review; and Level 4 indicated independent practice with supervisor notification as needed. In the NGNs context, Level 1 was defined as an initial co-activity phase to encourage active trainee participation while maintaining patient safety, as entrants had prior internship experience.

Quality appraisal of EPAs using the EQual rubric

A structured quality appraisal was conducted using the EQual rubric described in Study Design Sects. [19–20]. We used the Traditional Chinese version of the rubric, which Lin et al. previously translated, culturally adapted, and psychometrically validated [20].

The six experts described above completed the EQual appraisal. Informed consent was obtained before data collection. Study instructions were provided in Traditional Chinese and supplemented with a brief video tutorial adapted from the original English-language materials available online [19]. Experts had four weeks to evaluate the EPAs using the Traditional Chinese EQual rubric and were invited to provide written feedback, including specific revision recommendations, in a designated comment section. Items were rated on a 5-point anchored scale. EQual rubric scores were analyzed using descriptive statistics (mean, SD) in IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0, consistent with the approach used for consensus survey data. Predefined cut-off scores established by Taylor et al. were applied (overall ≥ 4.07; discrete units ≥ 4.17; entrustable, essential, and important tasks ≥ 4.00; educational tool ≥ 4.00) [19]. EPAs scoring below these thresholds were revised based on quantitative ratings and written qualitative comments, then reappraised by the same six experts using the same procedures.

Comments were organized by EPA and compared with EQual domain anchors to identify revision targets [19–20]. Each comment and revision decision was classified as substantive (structural), substantive (within-boundary), wording-level clarification, or not adopted; the full audit trail is provided in Supplementary Table 4, with item-level worked examples in Supplementary Table 5. For example, where an EPA scored below the “discrete units of work” threshold, it was revised either by tightening its boundaries so that it remained observable as a single unit or, where it could not be observed as one coherent unit, by decomposing it into phase-specific EPAs; low “educational tool” scores prompted clarification of ambiguous wording and closer alignment with intended learning outcomes. Formal thematic coding was not conducted; instead, below-cut-off domains were reviewed against the anchored descriptors to locate specific misalignments. To reduce circular reinforcement and anchoring, appraisers were not provided with consensus-phase raw materials, original EPA versions, or prior ratings in the subsequent round. Pre- and post-revision EPA versions were retained to document structural changes (Supplementary Appendix 1 and 2).

Handling of participant comments and revision principles

Across the three consensus stages, participant comments were handled according to the principles specified in the preceding subsections: in the NGT phase, comparable titles and specifications were consolidated through clarification and group discussion; in the modified Delphi phase, free-text comments informed only wording-level refinement, with additional rounds governed by the prespecified consensus thresholds; and in the EQual phase, comments and revision decisions were classified and acted on according to the rubric anchors and predefined cut-offs, as detailed above and in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of National Taiwan University Hospital (IRB Nos. 202101053RINC and 202303056RINC); all procedures complied with institutional guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. The authors approached potential participants and provided written and verbal information regarding the study’s purpose, procedures, voluntary participation, and data management. Written informed consent was obtained before data collection. Participants could withdraw at any time without consequence. All data were de-identified, and only the research team had access to individual responses

Results

EPA development

Generation and prioritisation of EPA titles

Across the five NGT sessions, participants produced 10, 7, 9, 8, and 13 candidate titles, respectively, which were consolidated within and across sessions and screened for SACT specificity. The NGT process yielded nine consolidated EPA titles for SACT nursing (Table 1). Three activities were prioritized consistently across at least three groups, indicating their salience as core EPAs: “administer anticancer drugs” (five sessions), “manage transfusion therapy for oncology patients” (four sessions), and “care for oncology patients with septic shock” and “care for oncology patients with neutropenia” (three sessions each). Other EPAs reflected unit-specific emphases: hematology groups highlighted bone marrow transplant and tumor lysis syndrome, whereas oncology groups emphasized pain management and acute bleeding.

Refinement and selection of priority EPAs

In the subsequent rating of learning necessity, three titles: “administer anticancer drugs”, “manage transfusion therapy for oncology patients” and “care for oncology patients with septic shock”, received unanimous agreement (mean = 5.00), followed by “care for patients with central venous catheters” (mean = 4.88). All EPAs achieved median scores ≥ 4.00 with low standard deviations, indicating strong consensus among respondents (Table 2).

For workplace observability, “administer anticancer drugs” was rated highest (mean = 4.65), followed by “care for patients with central venous catheters” (mean = 4.53) and “manage transfusion therapy for oncology patients” (mean = 4.35). All EPAs achieved median scores ≥ 4.00 and showed low standard deviations, indicating strong consensus among respondents (Table 2).

Based on these findings and their repeated endorsement in NGT sessions, five EPAs (C1–C5) were prioritised for further development: C1, “administer anticancer drugs”; C2, “manage transfusion therapy for oncology patients”; C3, “care for oncology patients with septic shock”; C4, “care for oncology patients with neutropenia”; and C5, “care for oncology patients with central venous catheters”. To align with local clinical practice, C5 (care for oncology patients with central venous catheters) was refined to “care for oncology patients with implanted vascular access ports”. Participants then evaluated draft specifications for these EPAs to determine their relevance, specificity, and assessability.

Consensus on EPA specifications

All specification statements achieved the prespecified consensus thresholds (agreement proportion [ratings 3–4] ≥ 75% and quartile deviation < 0.6) for each rating domain (relevance, specificity, and measurability in practice) in the first modified Delphi round; therefore, although up to three rounds were permitted, only one round was required. As presented in the Supplementary Table 2, within Administer anticancer drugs, statements related to prescription verification/order clarification and individualized patient education tended to receive comparatively lower measurability ratings than more procedural verification and administration steps. For Manage transfusion therapy for oncology patients, specification statements were consistently strong across relevance, specificity, and measurability, while statements related to patient education tended to be comparatively lower, particularly in measurability.

For Care for oncology patients with septic shock, ratings were highest for clinical intervention-focused statements, whereas psychosocial assessment and communication-oriented statements tended to be comparatively lower, particularly in measurability. Specifications for Care for oncology patients with neutropenia showed consistently strong ratings across domains. For Care for oncology patients with implanted vascular access ports, most statements were highly rated, with comparatively lower specificity for the maintenance-related statement, while still satisfying the prespecified consensus criteria. This pattern is consistent with the well-recognized difficulty of standardizing the direct observation and measurement of relational and affective care tasks in workplace-based assessment.

Free-text comments mainly concerned wording; the research team reviewed all comments, merged overlapping suggestions, and resolved divergent views through discussion before finalizing the revised statements. The Supplementary Table 2 presents the item-level quantitative summaries (including agreement proportion and quartile deviation).

Overall, all specification statements met the prespecified consensus thresholds for relevance, specificity, and measurability in practice in the first Delphi round. Across EPAs, statements involving patient education, communication, and psychosocial assessment tended to show comparatively lower ratings in measurability, although they still met the consensus criteria.

Quality appraisal of EPA structure using the EQual rubric

The developed EPAs were evaluated using the EQual rubric to assess their alignment with established EPA quality standards and identify areas for improvement. In the initial appraisal, C1 (Administer anticancer drugs), C3 (Care for oncology patients with septic shock), and C4 (Care for oncology patients with neutropenia) did not satisfy the overall benchmark score of 4.07, while four EPAs (C1–C4) scored below the 4.17 criterion in the discrete units of work domain. In addition, C4 scored below 4.00 in the entrustable, essential, and important tasks domain, while C2 (Manage transfusion therapy for oncology patients) did not reach the 4.00 threshold in the educational tool domain (Table 3). These results indicated a need for refinement to improve clarity, observability, and educational alignment.

In the initial appraisal, C1 (the broad anticancer drug administration EPA) scored below the predefined cut-off for the “discrete units of work” domain (Table 3). Its breadth, spanning the pre-administration, during-administration, and adverse-effect phases, undermined the ability to observe and entrust the activity as a single coherent unit of work. Consistent with domain 1 anchors that emphasize clearly defined and independently executable units of professional work [19], the research team interpreted the low discrete-units score as an indication that a narrower scope was needed, rather than more procedural detail. C1 was therefore decomposed into three phase-specific EPAs: EPA 1 (Conduct pre-administration care for patients receiving anticancer drugs), EPA 2 (Administration of anticancer drugs), and EPA 3 (Management of adverse effects of anticancer drugs). For each phase-specific EPA, the “specifications and limitations” section was revised to define phase-appropriate inclusions and exclusions, and task statements were rewritten using more principle-based language to reduce redundancy and support consistent interpretation during workplace assessment. This revision aimed to improve observability and assessability while maintaining the intended supervision level, and it increased the EPA set from five to seven titles. Only C1 required a substantive structural change (decomposition); of the remaining four candidates, three received substantive within-boundary revisions and one required wording-level refinement only.

In the second EQual appraisal (Table 4), all seven EPAs met the overall and domain-specific predefined EQual benchmarks, demonstrating clear improvement compared to the initial evaluation. These results suggest that the iterative revisions improved the structural quality and educational utility of the EPA set, supporting their readiness for piloting in clinical education settings.

Overview of EPA evolution across the development process

The candidate EPAs evolved through five stages. First, five NGT sessions produced 10, 7, 9, 8, and 13 candidate titles, respectively. These were consolidated within and across sessions and screened for SACT specificity, yielding 9 unique consolidated titles (Table 1). Second, the structured rating of learning necessity and workplace observability (n = 16), together with their repeated endorsement across NGT sessions, prioritised 5 candidate EPAs for further development (Table 2); the 4 EPAs not selected (post-HSCT care, acute bleeding care, tumor lysis syndrome care, and pain management) showed lower workplace observability scores (see Table 2). Third, all 5 prioritised candidate EPAs met the prespecified Delphi consensus thresholds in Round 1, with revisions limited to wording and no titles renamed. Fourth, the initial EQual appraisal identified the broad anticancer drug administration EPA as scoring below the cut-off for the discrete units of work domain; this EPA was decomposed into three phase-specific EPAs (pre-administration, during administration, and management of adverse effects), increasing the set from 5 to 7. Fifth, all 7 revised EPAs met the EQual benchmarks in the second appraisal. A full audit trail of these transitions is presented in Supplementary Table 4.

Discussion

This study developed seven oncology-specific EPAs for SACT nursing through a structured, consensus-based process with clinical preceptors, followed by rubric-guided quality appraisal. Beyond reporting the EPA set itself, the methodological contribution lies in integrating NGT-based generation and structured prioritization, modified Delphi specification rating, and EQual-anchored iterative revision, illustrated by a worked example in which an overly broad anticancer drug administration EPA was decomposed into phase-specific units to strengthen observability and unit-boundedness [18–19]. Collectively, the resulting EPAs offer a structured reference for describing and defining oncology nursing practice related to SACT nursing and establish a foundation for future piloting and evaluation of competency-based curriculum design and workplace-based learning in postgraduate nursing education. The EPAs developed in this study align with expected practice standards for NGNs in inpatient oncology settings. However, because institutional baseline preparation, prerequisites, and the scope of postgraduate training vary across settings, transferability to nurses with different baselines or training stages should be considered carefully. This study focused on EPA specification and structural appraisal; feasibility, acceptability, and utility in real-world entrustment decisions were outside the scope of this development-stage research.

The development process aligned with established EPA frameworks, which emphasize multi-stakeholder consensus-building through workshops, NGT, and Delphi techniques [9, 17–18, 23]. In this study, combining NGT sessions with subsequent Delphi-style specification surveys produced a high level of agreement within a single consensus cycle, likely reflecting the active participation of experienced clinical preceptors from hematology and oncology units whose knowledge of local workflows and patient needs grounded the EPAs in real-world practice. This contextual grounding may, however, embed institution-specific protocols and resource configurations. Although the preceptors held no formal academic appointments, oncology nursing is well established within Taiwan’s mature health system, and their expertise strengthened the EPA set’s content validity and contextual relevance, though these contributions should be interpreted within the context of a single organizational culture.

Positioning this quality check before later refinement allowed structural issues to be resolved early and prioritised clear articulation of nursing-specific tasks by nursing experts, so that subsequent Delphi or stakeholder processes could focus on clinical appropriateness and interprofessional validation. Although this study did not advance to the curriculum alignment and implementation phases, this sequence provides a methodological platform for future piloting [17].

The framework developed in this study integrated two underlying logics, service provision and disease or patient category, as elucidated by Hennus et al. [24]. As the EPAs were designed for nurses caring for patients receiving SACT, anchoring the activities in disease- and treatment-specific contexts was appropriate and necessary, which may improve alignment with actual clinical scenarios. The specifications emphasized observable and longitudinal clinical activities, such as administering anticancer drugs, monitoring responses, managing complications, and communicating with the healthcare team. This approach enables routine nursing processes to be translated into potentially assessable units of work. Consistent with Hennus et al.’s recommendation that mixed logics are particularly suitable when EPA development is grounded in clinical practice [24], this hybrid design aimed to ensure that the resulting framework reflected oncology care’s complexity while maintaining assessability, as judged through structural appraisal, and may warrant feasibility testing for supervision.

The seven EPAs address high-risk, specialized clinical contexts in oncology nursing and were structured to follow the core logic of the nursing process, including assessing, diagnosing, implementing, and evaluating, as described in prior work on nursing EPAs [14]. This alignment may suggest a perceived congruence between the EPA framework and everyday nursing practice. The EPAs emphasize interprofessional communication and teamwork; for example, specifications for anticancer drug administration, transfusion management, and septic shock care require nurses to consult physicians or other team members when clinical uncertainties arise [9, 14, 23]. The inclusion of clear entrustment and supervision levels, along with explicit guidance on appropriate information sources for judging progress, may improve the clarity of the intended assessment design and support future piloting of formative assessment processes. These features demonstrate how oncology-specific EPAs can be conceptualized as integrated learning and assessment frameworks that aim to align structurally with international quality standards in competency-based nursing education [8–9, 14, 18], while their effectiveness in real-world entrustment decisions requires empirical evaluation in practice settings.

An additional contribution of this study is the explicit use of a validated, culturally adapted EQual rubric in a non-English-speaking context [20]. By demonstrating how the Traditional Chinese version of the EQual can be used to iteratively refine oncology EPAs, this study extends previous research on EPA quality assurance beyond Western settings [19–20]. This approach may be especially valuable for nurse educators in other Asian or multilingual contexts who aim to adapt EPA frameworks while maintaining structural quality.

These findings sit within a broader movement in nursing education towards context-specific competency assessment, in which generic frameworks are adapted into validated, specialty-tailored tools. Cao et al., for example, adapted an existing scale into the Chinese Pediatric Nurses’ Genomic Literacy Scale (CPN-GLS) and established its quality through psychometric validation in a large nurse sample [25], while contextual determinants of nurses’ genetic competency have been mapped in a related systematic review [26]. Our study addresses a parallel need from a different angle: rather than psychometrically validating a self-report scale, we use the EQual rubric to appraise the structural quality of observable professional activities. The two approaches are complementary routes to rigorous, specialty-specific competency tools, one examining what nurses know, the other appraising what nurses can be entrusted to do.

Practical implications

From a practical standpoint, these EPAs are intended as a structured reference for educators, clinical supervisors, and curriculum designers responsible for SACT nursing training. By translating SACT-relevant competencies into observable, entrustment-anchored units of work, the framework offers a vocabulary for articulating supervision expectations, designing workplace-based assessment, and identifying targeted feedback opportunities during clinical practice. Developing these EPAs from the perspective of clinical educators and refining them through an EQual-guided process produced activities that, in this study, satisfied structural quality standards and included the essential EPA elements identified in previous literature [18–20, 23, 27]; this approach may provide a reproducible model for development-stage quality assurance that could inform EPA development across nursing specialties. The extent to which using these EPAs actually influences supervision practice, learner progression, or care quality in real settings remains an empirical question beyond this development-stage study and is reserved for future pilot implementation research.

The seven oncology nursing EPAs provide a structured framework that aligns learning expectations between educators and learners: by defining explicit learning goals and performance expectations, they create a shared reference supporting structured observation, targeted feedback, and progressive entrustment planning during training. For educators, this clarity can sharpen instructional focus, reduce variation in supervision, and specify the knowledge, skills, and attitudes needed for safe, independent practice; for learners, it makes expectations transparent and helps identify experiential gaps requiring further development. Embedding the EPAs in workplace-based learning can also establish a shared pedagogical language among clinical preceptors and link entrustment levels to patient safety [4–7, 17]. Realizing this in practice requires faculty to develop three capabilities: observing learner performance against the EPA specifications, judging the appropriate level of entrustment, and giving EPA-anchored feedback that supports progression. These can be cultivated within existing continuing professional development structures, through structured workshops applying EPA specifications to clinical observation, calibration sessions aligning entrustment-level judgments using shared vignettes, and feedback practice with peer review or video. Because these activities can be integrated into existing preceptor programmes without requiring new infrastructure, the necessary faculty development may be feasible for institutions piloting the EPAs in SACT nursing settings.

These EPAs show structural readiness to support pilot implementation in local contexts (e.g., small-scale trials within a single institution or program). Before wider adoption, further work is needed to evaluate feasibility, assessor training requirements, acceptability to stakeholders, and the reliability of entrustment decisions in routine practice.

Limitations and future research

In terms of the validity framework, this study addresses evidence at the level of content, internal structure, and response process through expert consensus and rubric-based appraisal. Evidence concerning decision consistency, reliability of entrustment in practice, and consequential validity (downstream educational and clinical impact) was beyond the development-stage scope and is reserved for future empirical studies.

This study has several limitations to consider when interpreting its findings. First, it was conducted in a single university medical center, so the resulting EPAs may reflect institutional characteristics and local practice norms (including SACT and anticancer drug administration workflows, staffing ratios, and the availability of electronic decision support) that limit generalizability; transferability to regional hospitals or differently resourced institutions has not yet been established, and future research should include multiple hospitals and academic, community, and regional centers to assess transferability and adaptability across oncology nursing contexts. Relatedly, the entrustment targets are calibrated to Taiwan’s nursing postgraduate-year (NPGY) training structure, in which each EPA defines the summative entrustment goal (Level 5) as attainment upon completion of the NPGY programme; because health systems structure postgraduate training and supervisory progression differently, these supervision-level milestones may require recalibration to local training pathways before the EPAs are applied in other national contexts.

This study examined the foundational phases of EPA development (preparation, drafting, and quality control) described in the twelve-tip framework by Hennus et al. [18], emphasizing structural quality and clinical relevance to create a methodological platform for later phases such as pilot implementation, curriculum mapping, and ongoing evaluation. Consistent with this development-phase scope, the activities were defined and appraised by a purposively recruited, homogeneous panel of hematology and oncology nurses to capture nursing-specific tasks; learner input was not part of this stage by design, and the absence of trainees and interprofessional stakeholders may have limited the range of perspectives. Accordingly, the study did not assess the EPAs’ effects on learner performance or patient care. Incorporating trainees’ perspectives would strengthen the EPAs by confirming their relevance and observability from the learner’s standpoint, and future phases should therefore involve learner panels alongside broader stakeholders, link these EPAs to national or institutional competency frameworks, and examine their integration and effects within comprehensive competency-based curricula [9, 18].

Third, although the EQual rubric provided a rigorous appraisal framework, ratings were generated by a small expert group from a single institution [19–20]. As the same panel conducted both rounds, anchoring, confirmation bias, or learning effects may have influenced re-appraisal, even though appraisers were blinded to the original EPA versions and their previous scores. The lack of an independent panel remains a limitation. Larger, independent, multidisciplinary panels from multiple sites could improve robustness, mitigate institutional bias, and enable cross-institutional benchmarking. Additionally, circular reinforcement may occur when local experts are involved in both development and appraisal. To improve credibility, we reported panel composition transparently, used a validated rubric with predefined cut-offs, and implemented procedures to limit information carryover between stages.

Future studies should employ longitudinal designs in diverse clinical settings to evaluate EPA-based instruction’s implementation, acceptability, and educational outcomes, while considering contextual determinants at the individual, unit, and organisational levels [26]. Key outcomes encompass the reliability of entrustment decisions, learner progression, patient safety indicators, and the effects of faculty development, which collectively inform feasibility and sustainability [18, 22, 27]. Broader transferability requires multi-site, interprofessional validation, including the engagement of physicians and pharmacists as stakeholders to inform curriculum design for EPA-related knowledge, skills, and attitudes [18]. The lower measurability of relational and psychosocial care statements further suggests that such items may benefit from complementary assessment methods, such as case-based discussion, rather than reliance on direct observation alone. Despite these limitations, this study provides a methodological foundation and conceptual model that may support piloting and subsequent evaluation, informing the systematic development, refinement, and operationalization of EPAs within competency-based nursing education.

The present study was confined to the early phases of the Hennus twelve-step process (EPA development and structural appraisal) and did not address implementation, feasibility, or learner outcomes. Building on these EPAs, our team is now working through the later phases of this framework: aligning prerequisite knowledge, skills, and attitudes with the EPAs and the corresponding curriculum; developing faculty training that prepares clinical teachers to observe learner performance against the EPA specifications and to make entrustment-level judgments; and, on this basis, moving toward EPA-aligned workplace-based observation and assessment in routine SACT care. Findings from these subsequent phases will be reported separately.

Conclusion

This study developed seven oncology-specific EPAs for SACT nursing through a structured workflow combining NGT generation, modified Delphi specification rating, and EQual rubric-guided iterative refinement. All seven EPAs met overall and domain-level EQual structural quality thresholds after one revision cycle, including an EQual-anchored decomposition of an initially broad anticancer drug administration EPA into three phase-specific units. The findings establish structural readiness as a foundation for subsequent pilot evaluation. Whether this structural foundation supports reliable entrustment decisions, improved learner progression, or patient-centred care outcomes is not demonstrated by this study and remains a hypothesis for empirical investigation in future implementation research. From a methodological standpoint, this work illustrates how rubric-anchored quality appraisal can serve as an active design tool rather than a post-hoc check, surfacing structural ambiguities such as insufficient unit-boundedness and guiding targeted revision before any field implementation; this approach may be transferable to other specialty nursing contexts seeking to operationalise CBE.
