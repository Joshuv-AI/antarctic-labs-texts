Real-world results of first-line immunotherapy or targeted therapy for metastatic melanoma in Finland: a cohort study
Authors: Kalle E Mattila, Leena Tiainen, Johanna Vikkula, Anna Kreutzman, Mia Engström-Risku, Kai Kysenius, Olivia Hölsä, Sari Hernesniemi, Päivikki Hemmilä, Anssi Pystynen, Siru Mäkelä
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim: First-line (1L) immunotherapy has yielded superior overall survival (OS) in metastatic melanoma (MM) but some patients are ineligible for immunotherapy or need rapid response with 1L targeted therapy (TT).Materials & methods: Retrospective cohort study of real-world patients treated with 1L immunotherapy (144 BRAF wild type, 85 BRAF-mutated) or 1L TT (143 BRAF-mutated) for MM in Finland during 2014–2021.Results: Baseline brain metastases, liver metastases and elevated LDH were less common, 2-year OS rates were higher (60.3–63.5% vs. 33.8%) and more patients were alive without the next-line treatment (38.0–43.8% vs. 23.3%) in patients with 1L immunotherapy.Conclusion: Real-world patients with 1L immunotherapy for MM had favorable baseline characteristics and better treatment outcomes than observed in patients with 1L TT.Keywords: : BRAF, brain metastasis, immunotherapy, lactate dehydrogenase, liver metastasis, melanoma, overall survival, real-world evidence, targeted therapy

Introduction

Cutaneous melanoma is the most aggressive skin cancer prone to spread metastases to lymph nodes and visceral organs. Worldwide, metastatic melanoma (MM) causes over 55000 cancer deaths annually [1]. Due to the exposure to ultraviolet radiation, cutaneous melanomas harbor a high mutational burden leading to the formation of neoantigens that are recognized by the immune system [2]. Immune checkpoint blockade with CTLA-4 (ipilimumab) and PD-1 inhibitors (nivolumab and pembrolizumab) has dramatically improved the survival of patients with MM during the past ten years [1]. In addition to immunotherapy, mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway -targeted therapy (TT) with BRAF and MEK inhibitors (vemurafenib and cobimetinib, dabrafenib and trametinib, encorafenib and binimetinib,) have induced rapid responses in patients with BRAF-mutated MM (40–60% of cutaneous melanomas), even with a high tumor burden and brain metastases [1,3]. Currently, the five-year overall survival (OS) rates of patients with MM have reached 40% and even extended over 50% with first-line (1L) immunotherapy [4–6] and 35% with 1L TT in clinical trials [7,8]. The increase in long-term survivors has also been observed in real-world registry studies. In the Danish Metastatic Melanoma Database (DAMMED), the number of patients who had survived over three-years after treatment initiation raised from 15% in 2007–2011 cohort to 32% in 2012–2016 cohort [9]. In the Netherlands, the four-year survival rate of patients with MM treated with ipilimumab and nivolumab combination was 43% [10]. However, especially acquired resistance to treatment is common leading to the five-year progression-free survival (PFS) rates of 21–36% with 1L immunotherapy and 19–23% with 1L TT in clinical trials [4–8]. Thus, most patients with MM still need subsequent treatment options in clinical trials and in the real world.

Based on the greater likelihood of long-term survival, immunotherapy is the preferred 1L treatment option for eligible patients with MM, and TT with BRAF and MEK inhibitors is commonly used in the second line (2L) for patients with BRAF-mutated MM after disease progression on immunotherapy [11]. Higher disease burden involving more advanced M-stage, ≥3 metastatic sites, brain metastases, liver metastases and elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) has been associated with inferior outcomes of immunotherapy as well as TT [12–14]. Randomized, prospective DREAMseq and SECOMBIT trials have investigated the choice of 1L treatment in patients with BRAF-mutated MM. 1L immunotherapy followed by 2L TT after disease progression has resulted in higher two-year OS rates (71.8–73%) compared with 1L TT followed by 2L immunotherapy after disease progression (51.5–63%) [15,16]. However, rapid response with 1L TT might be a desirable goal for some patients with symptomatic BRAF-mutated MM. Planned switch to immunotherapy after achieving initial response with upfront TT is an attractive treatment strategy especially for patients with high tumor burden or brain metastases, but it needs further validation in prospective clinical trials before being adopted into routine clinical practice [16].

Unlike patients in clinical trials, many patients encountered in routine clinical practice have impaired performance status, significant comorbidities including autoimmune diseases, or symptomatic brain metastases requiring corticosteroid treatment and, therefore, might not be eligible for upfront immunotherapy and clinical trials. Even up to 53–60% of patients have not been ‘trial-like’ in earlier real-world analyses [17,18]. Targeted therapy is feasible for most patients with BRAF-mutated MM who are ineligible for immunotherapy. However, patients with BRAF wild type MM lack TT options. Therefore, chemotherapy is still sometimes used in routine clinical practice for patients with MM unless there are other treatment options available.

Although immunotherapy and TT have been widely available for several years, there are only few studies available comparing the choice of 1L treatment in real-world patients with MM [19,20], including the effects on healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) [21]. In Finland, BRAF inhibitor monotherapy has been reimbursed and available for patients with MM since 2014, PD-1 inhibitors and BRAF plus MEK inhibitors since 2015, and ipilimumab plus nivolumab since 2016. The aim of this retrospective register study was to evaluate the impact of baseline characteristics on treatment outcomes in patients who had received immunotherapy or TT for the 1L treatment of MM in routine clinical practice in Finland during 2014–2021.

Methods

Objectives

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate clinically relevant baseline characteristics of the patients with MM treated in the three largest academic hospitals of Finland. Our hypothesis was that there are differences in relevant prognostic baseline characteristics affecting also treatment outcomes in different treatment groups. In the real world, the patients who are fit for upfront immunotherapy might present with better prognostic characteristics than the patients who are eligible for targeted therapy.

The secondary objectives were to describe event-free survival rates (time to next treatment) and overall survival rates at 24 months in patients treated with 1L immunotherapy and 1L targeted therapy. Time to next treatment (TTNT) was chosen as a surrogate for progression-free survival because radiological response evaluation was not performed according to the RECIST 1.1 criteria in routine clinical practice at our hospitals. Time to next treatment reflects more directly the efficacy of 1L treatment whereas overall survival depends also on the available next-line treatment options. The real-world design allowed us to investigate the benefit from 1L immunotherapy and targeted therapy among all patients eligible for effective 1L treatments in routine clinical practice without the selection of only ‘trial-like’ patients.

Study population

All patients with the diagnosis of cutaneous melanoma (ICD-10 code C43) were retrospectively identified from the electronic medical records of Helsinki, Turku and Tampere University Hospitals between the 1 January 2010 and the 31 December 2021. Helsinki, Turku and Tampere University Hospital districts cover over 50% of the whole population in Finland. The diagnosis of metastatic disease was not available uniformly in a structured format in the used registries. Therefore, metastatic disease status was derived using multiple inclusion and exclusion criteria. Metastatic disease was determined by the presence of at least one of the following criteria: ICD-10 code for metastasis (C78 or C79); TNM stage IV or M1; radiation therapy of the brain (procedure codes AAG50, AA0) or metastases (procedure code WF049); medication for metastatic disease including ipilimumab, nivolumab, pembrolizumab, vemurafenib, cobimetinib, dabrafenib, trametinib, encorafenib, binimetinib, dacarbazine, temozolomide, lomustine, vincristine, cisplatin, carboplatin and paclitaxel; metastatic melanoma and the metastatic site mentioned four-times in the electronic medical records.

From the preliminary cohort, prevalent patients (i.e., diagnosis of metastasis prior to year 2014), patients <18 years of age and patients living outside the studied three hospital districts were excluded. Patients with mentions of local, satellite, or in-transit metastases, sentinel lymph node, stage III, or adjuvant therapy in their electronic medical records were considered to have stage III disease and were excluded from the metastatic melanoma cohort. Also, patients with a record of other cancer before or at the time of metastatic melanoma were excluded from the cohort to ensure that metastases were related to melanoma. Finally, patients with missing data (i.e., missing or incomplete medication records or unknown BRAF V600 status) and patients treated with chemotherapy or BRAF inhibitor monotherapy in the first treatment line were excluded.

For each patient in the final cohort, the first record of metastatic melanoma was set as an index date. Patients were followed from the index date until death or end of study (31st Dec 2021), whichever occurred first. The study patients were stratified into three cohorts based on the choice of 1L treatment and BRAF mutation status. Cohort A included 144 patients with BRAF wild type (WT) MM treated with 1L immunotherapy (38.7% of all patients), cohort B included 85 patients with BRAF-mutated MM treated with 1L immunotherapy (22.8% of all patients), and cohort C included 143 patients with BRAF-mutated MM treated with 1L TT (38.4% of all patients).

Clinical characteristics

Baseline clinical characteristics were obtained from electronic medical records using structurally reported information and text mining. Clinical characteristics included age, sex, Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) [22], LDH levels, BRAF V600 mutation status (mutated, wild type), sites of metastases and number of metastatic sites. M-stage was determined according to AJCC 8th edition [23] using the information of metastatic sites. There were different scales used to assess performance status during the study period. The ECOG performance status could not be reliably retrieved from the electronic medical records and was thus omitted.

CCI was defined based on diagnoses recorded at specialized care, at maximum 4 years before the index date. Melanoma (ICD-10 code C43) and metastases (ICD-10 code C77, C78, C79) were excluded from the CCI calculations. Other characteristics were reported at index using records at maximum ± 3 months away from the index date. If multiple records were available within the three-month period, the record closest to the index date was used.

Processing free-form text

Unstructured, free-form text data was used to define BRAF V600 status, metastatic disease and sites of metastasis. Text data was first split into sentences and then, sentences including uncertainties, negations, or mentions of family members, other relatives, or other cancers were classified as invalid sentences and excluded from further analyses. Valid sentences were finally classified using regular expressions.

Treatment lines

All systemic treatments and the use of radiation therapy for metastatic disease during study period were also obtained. Treatment lines were derived using the data of drug administrations and prescriptions from the University Hospitals and the data of reimbursed medication purchases provided by the Social Insurance Institution. For each patient, preliminary treatment lines were constructed by considering all medication records within 90 days to belong to the same treatment line. Consecutive preliminary treatment lines were merged into one treatment line if the regimen in both preliminary lines were the same and the gap between the lines was less than 6 months. Unusual treatment combinations were inspected by the authors and adjusted manually.

Treatment outcomes

The primary end points of this study included OS and TTNT in different treatment cohorts. Survival status (alive or dead) and the date of death were obtained from the electronic medical records. OS was defined as time from the initiation of 1L treatment until death (event) or the end of follow-up (the 31st of December 2021). In routine clinical practice, radiological response evaluations were not performed according to RECIST 1.1 guidelines and responses were not re-evaluated by a trained radiologist. Therefore, TTNT was used as a surrogate for PFS. TTNT was defined as time from the initiation of 1L treatment until the initiation of 2L treatment (event), death (event), or the end of follow-up.

Healthcare resource utilization

All-cause healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) was assessed from the diagnosis of MM until the end of follow-up. Cumulative number of inpatient days and outpatient visits per patient was estimated using a mean cumulative function (MCF), in which, patients accumulated zero resource use after death.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented with median and range and categorical variables with number and percentages. Differences in the baseline characteristics between treatment cohorts were tested using Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and Chi-squared/Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. The median length of the follow-up was defined using a reverse Kaplan-Meier fit. To protect the anonymity of the patients, all patient numbers below 5 were censored and reported as n <5 as required by the data authority. The proportion of patients with missing data was also reported.

Treatment outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier fit and reported with the median OS and the 24-month OS rate (with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI)). Multivariable Cox regression analysis was performed to study the effect of baseline characteristics on OS and TTNT. Multivariable analyses were performed separately for patients treated with 1L immunotherapy (cohort A and B) and 1L TT (cohort C). The following covariates were included into the multivariable analysis: age (<65 years, ≥65 years), sex (male, female), brain metastases (no, present), liver metastases (no, present), LDH (normal, elevated) and the year of metastatic melanoma diagnosis (2014–2017, 2018–2021). BRAF mutation status (mutated, WT) was also included in the multivariable analysis of the patients with 1L immunotherapy. Additionally, a univariate Cox model was performed on OS and TTNT for each cohort separately to assess the impact of M stage on treatment outcomes. All analyses were performed using R version 4.0.3.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Finnish Social and Health Data Permit Authority Findata (THL/1189/14.02.00/2021). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design of this study. Data was collected, handled and analyzed according to the legislation on the secondary use of healthcare data.

Results

Study population

A total of 2,255 patients with metastatic melanoma during 2010–2021 were identified from the electronic medical records of Helsinki, Turku and Tampere University Hospitals. Out of them, 454 patients with incident and metastatic melanoma in 2014–2021 who had received immunotherapy or TT as the 1L treatment for MM in routine clinical practice were included into the initial study population. However, 82 patients were excluded from further analyses because they had received BRAF inhibitor monotherapy as the 1L treatment with potentially markedly worse treatment outcomes compared with patients treated with BRAF and MEK inhibitor combination in the first line. The excluded patients with 1L BRAF inhibitor monotherapy were older than patients with 1L BRAF and MEK inhibitor combination (median age 70.2 years vs. 65.3 years), but other baseline characteristics were similar. Thus, a total of 372 patients were included into the final study cohort. Patient selection flow-chart is described in Figure 1. Most of the final study cohort fulfilled multiple inclusion criteria: 84 patients (22.6%) fulfilled two criteria, 134 patients (36.0%) three criteria, 137 patients (36.8%) four criteria and 11 patients (3.0%) five criteria (Supplementary Figure S1). Only 6 patients (1.6%) were included just based on medication for metastasis.

The median age of the whole study population was 65.7 (range 19.7–86.5) years. Patients with BRAF-mutated MM treated with 1L immunotherapy were younger than patients with BRAF-mutated MM treated with 1L TT (the median age 62.1 vs. 65.3 years). The number of metastatic sites was lower in patients treated with 1L immunotherapy compared with patients treated with 1L TT (1–2 metastatic sites 84.4–92.2% vs. 66.9%). Correspondingly, M1a and M1b stages were more common and M1c less common in patients treated with 1L immunotherapy compared with patients treated with 1L TT (M1a 48.1–53.3% (cohort A and B) vs. 36.7% (cohort C),M1b 16.3–29.9% (cohort A and B) vs. 10.1% (cohort C) and M1c 11.7–24.4% (cohort A and B) vs. 26.6% (cohort C)) (Table 1).

In the whole study population, there were 53 (15.1%) patients with brain metastases, 72 (20.5%) patients with liver metastases and 98 (30.0%) patients with elevated LDH at the initiation of 1L treatment. The proportion of patients with baseline brain metastases (5.9–10.4% (cohort A and B) vs. 26.6% (cohort C)), liver metastases (9.1–20.7% (cohort A and B) vs. 26.6% (cohort C)) and elevated LDH (21.4%–23.0% (cohort A and B) vs. 41.2% (cohort C)) was smaller in patients treated with 1L immunotherapy compared with patients treated with 1L TT (Table 1).

The proportion of patients who had received any 2L treatment was 34.0, 58.8 and 38.5% in cohorts A, B and C, respectively (Table 1). There were some patients who had been re-challenged in the 2L with the same type of treatment as used in the 1L (3.5% in cohort A, 5.9% in cohort B and 7.0% in cohort C) (Figure 2). Among the patients with BRAF-mutated MM treated with 1L immunotherapy, the proportion of patients who had received 2L TT after disease progression on 1L immunotherapy was 84.0% of the patients who had received any 2L treatment (Figure 2B). In contrast, only 45.5% of the patients with BRAF-mutated MM treated with 1L TT who had received any 2L treatment were treated with 2L immunotherapy after disease progression on 1L TT (Figure 2C).

The proportion of patients who had died after 1L treatment without receiving any 2L treatment was 19.4% (cohort A), 1.2% (cohort B) and 37.8% (cohort C) (Figure 2). The proportion of patients who had received any third-line (3L) treatment was 11.8% (cohort A), 24.7% (cohort B) and 12.6% (cohort C) (Figure 2). Different chemotherapy regimens (e.g., temozolomide, temozolomide-lomustine-vincristine and paclitaxel-carboplatin) were used as a salvage treatment for some patients in the 2L and 3L in this study population.

Time to next treatment & overall survival

After the median follow-up of 39.5 (95% CI: 35.6–43.7) months for all patients, the median TTNT was 18.4 (95% CI: 13.7–27.3) months, 18.4 (95% CI: 12.9–24.1) months and 9.6 (95% CI: 7.6–12.9) months in cohorts A, B and C, respectively (log-rank p < 0.001; Figure 3A). The two-year event-free survival rates were 43.8% (cohort A), 38.0% (cohort B) and 23.3% (cohort C) (Figure 3A). The two-year event-free survival rates were 57.2% (cohort A), 47.5% (cohort B) and 38.0% (cohort C) in patients without poor prognostic characteristics (baseline brain metastases, liver metastases, or elevated LDH) (Figure 3C) compared with 25.9% (cohort A), 31.3% (cohort B) and 13.0% (cohort C) in patients with poor prognostic (Figure 3E).

The median OS was 28.5 (95% CI: 24.7–47.6) months, 39.9 (95% CI: 28.9–not reached) months and 15.9 (95% CI: 12.9–19.3) months in cohorts A, B and C, respectively (log-rank p < 0.001; Figure 3B). The two-year OS rates were 60.3% (cohort A), 63.5% (cohort B) and 33.8% (cohort C) (Figure 3B). The two-year OS rates were 65.4% (cohort A), 66.5% (cohort B) and 49.2% (cohort C) in patients without poor prognostic characteristics (baseline brain metastases, liver metastases, or elevated LDH) (Figure 3D) compared with 46.7% (cohort A), 40.0% (cohort B) and 23.3% (cohort C) in patients with poor prognostic characteristics (Figure 3F).

Multivariable analysis of the association of baseline characteristics with TTNT & OS

Brain metastases and liver metastases at the initiation of 1L immunotherapy were independently associated with inferior TTNT and OS regardless of age, sex, LDH levels, the year of metastatic melanoma diagnosis, and BRAF mutation status in multivariable analysis (Figure 4A, B). The hazard ratio (HR) of the next treatment or death was 2.91 (95% CI: 1.52–5.6) for patients with brain metastases compared with patients without brain metastases and 2.84 (95% CI: 1.67–4.84) for patients with liver metastases compared with patients without liver metastases. The HR of death was 2.43 (95% CI: 1.14–5.21) for patients with brain metastases compared with patients without brain metastases and 2.24 (95% CI: 1.23–4.06) for patients with liver metastases compared with patients without liver metastases. In addition, elevated LDH was also significantly associated with inferior OS in patients with 1L immunotherapy (the HR for death 1.74 (95% CI: 1.02–2.97)) but not with inferior TTNT in multivariable analysis. Patients with 1L immunotherapy and the diagnosis of metastatic disease during 2018–2021 were associated with longer TTNT compared with patients with metastatic disease diagnosed during 2014–2017 (the HR of the next treatment or death 0.51 (95% CI: 0.33–0.78)) but not with longer OS in multivariable analysis.

Multivariable Cox regression models of time to next treatment panel (A & C) and overall survival panel (B & D) in patients treated with 1L immunotherapy and 1L targeted therapy.

Baseline brain metastases and liver metastases were also associated with inferior TTNT and OS in patients with 1L TT regardless of age, sex, LDH levels, and the year of metastatic melanoma diagnosis in multivariable analysis (Figure 4C, D). The HR of the next treatment or death was 1.69 (95% CI: 1.98–2.64) for patients with brain metastases compared with patients without brain metastases and 2.06 (95% CI: 1.18–3.6) for patients with liver metastases compared with patients without liver metastases. The HR of death was 1.86 (95% CI: 1.16–2.97) for patients with brain metastases compared with patients without brain metastases and 2.1 (95% CI: 1.18–3.72) for patients with liver metastases compared with patients without liver metastases. In patients with 1L TT, elevated LDH was not significantly associated with inferior TTNT and OS in multivariable analysis.

Treatment outcomes according to M-stage

Treatment outcomes (TTNT and OS) were better in patients with less advanced disease (stages M1a and M1b according to AJCC 8th edition) compared with patients with more advanced disease (stage M1c and M1d) in patients treated with 1L immunotherapy and 1L targeted therapy. Regarding time to next treatment, the 24-month event-free survival rates were 51.9% (95% CI: 41.4–64.9%) vs. 22.2% (95% CI: 11.5–42.8%), 42.2% (95% CI: 30.5–58.3%) vs. 20.2% (95% CI: 4.4–93.7%) and 35.6% (95% CI: 24.6–51.5%) vs. 13.5% (95% CI: 7.3–24.9%) in cohorts A, B and C, respectively, when stratified according to M-stage (M1a-M1b vs. M1c-M1d) (Figure 5A,C,E). Similarly, the 24-month overall survival rates were 64.4% (95% CI: 54.1%–76.7%) vs. 44.1% (95% CI: 29.6%–65.8%), 73.6% (95% CI: 62.1%–87.1%) vs. 11.6% (95% CI: 1.9–71.9%) and 43.2% (95% CI: 31.3–59.7%) vs. 24.8% (95% CI: 16.3–37.9%) in cohorts A, B and C, respectively (Figure 5B,D,F). When comparing patients with M1c and M1d to patients with M1a and M1b, the univariate HR of the next treatment or death was 2.8 (95% CI: 1.7–4.5, p < 0.001) in cohort A, 2.4 (95% CI: 1.2–4.8, p = 0.015) in cohort B and 2.2 (95% CI: 1.5–3.3, p < 0.001) in cohort C. Similarly, the univariate HR of death was 2.1 (95% CI: 1.3–3.6, p = 0.004) in cohort A, 4.8 (95% CI: 2.1–11.0, p < 0.001) in cohort B and 2.1 (95% CI: 1.4–3.2, p < 0.001) in cohort C when comparing patients with M1c and M1d to patients with M1a and M1b.

Healthcare resource utilization

Accumulation of all-cause inpatient days and outpatient visits per patient are shown in Figure 6. During the first year, patients receiving 1L TT spent 11 days hospitalized (95% CI: 8–13) while patients with 1L immunotherapy and mutated BRAF had only six inpatient days (95% CI: 4–8) and patients with 1L immunotherapy and WT BRAF had seven inpatient days (95% CI: 5–8). After the first year, the accumulation rate for inpatient days was similar across patient groups. On the other hand, patients with 1L immunotherapy had more frequently outpatient visits than patients with 1L TT: altogether 44 outpatient visits (95% CI: 41–48) per patient during the first year for patients with 1L immunotherapy and BRAF WT MM (cohort A), 43 visits (95% CI: 38–49) for patients with 1L immunotherapy and BRAF-mutated MM (cohort B) and 37 visits (95% CI: 34–40) for patients with 1L TT and BRAF-mutated MM (cohort C).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we discovered that patients who had received 1L targeted therapy for metastatic melanoma had more commonly poor baseline characteristics including ≥3 metastatic sites, brain metastases, liver metastases and elevated LDH compared with patients treated with 1L immunotherapy in three academic hospitals in Finland. This finding confirmed our hypothesis that 1L immunotherapy was typically used for patients with lower tumor burden while 1L TT was commonly offered for patients with higher tumor burden and brain metastases. Moreover, 84% of patients with BRAF-mutated MM who had been treated with any 2L therapy had received 2L TT after disease progression on 1L immunotherapy, whereas only 46% of the patients with BRAF-mutated MM who had been treated with any 2L therapy had received 2L immunotherapy after disease progression on 1L TT. Thus, patients with BRAF-mutated MM who had been treated with 1L TT were usually considered ineligible for 2L immunotherapy at the time of disease progression. To our knowledge, this study included the largest cohort of real-world patients with modern systemic therapy for MM and detailed information on clinical characteristics evaluated in Finland.

Based on significant differences observed in prognostic baseline characteristics and subsequent systemic treatments, it was not surprising that patients with 1L immunotherapy had superior OS compared with patients with 1L TT (two-year OS rates 60.3–63.5% vs. 33.8%). TTNT was also longer in patients with 1L immunotherapy compared with patients with 1L TT (two-year event-free survival rates 38.0%-43.8% vs. 23.3%). The two-year OS rate of 60% in real-world patients with 1L immunotherapy in this study was similar to 56% reported by Atkins et al. in patients with BRAF-mutated metastatic melanomas treated with 1L immunotherapy [20] but lower than 80% observed by Betof Warner et al. [19]. This is likely explained partly by differences in the baseline characteristics of different real-world patient cohorts and partly by available treatment options e.g., less regular use of ipilimumab and nivolumab combination in our study cohort. The two-year OS rate of 33.8% in real-world patients treated with 1L TT in this study was low but comparable to 28% observed by Ismail et al. in real-world patients treated with BRAF and MEK inhibitors also harboring commonly poor baseline characteristics [17]. The two-year OS rate in patients with BRAF-mutated metastatic melanoma treated with 1L TT would have been even poorer (30.7%) if patients with BRAF inhibitor monotherapy were included in the final analysis. However, patients with BRAF inhibitor monotherapy were treated during the early years of the study period and do not represent the current clinical practice as BRAF and MEK inhibitor combinations have showed better efficacy and less toxicity [7,8].

After stratifying patients according to the M-stage, we found that 1L immunotherapy yielded generally longer time to next treatment and overall survival compared with 1L targeted therapy in patients with less advanced disease (M1a and M1b) as well as in patients with more advanced disease (M1c and M1d), except for OS in BRAF-mutated MM patients and M1c-d stage (although this subgroup was small). The median OS was not reached in patients with BRAF-mutated MM and M1a-b stage treated with 1L immunotherapy which is likely partly explained by salvage treatment with 2L BRAF and MEK inhibitors after disease progression on 1L immunotherapy. Time to next treatment was slightly longer in patients with BRAF WT MM treated with 1L immunotherapy compared with BRAF-mutated MM treated with 1L immunotherapy. In this study cohort over 80% of patients with 1L immunotherapy had received PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy. Better PFS results with PD-1 inhibitor therapy have also been observed in patients with BRAF WT disease compared with patients with BRAF-mutated disease in clinical trials which has led to the preference of ipilimumab and nivolumab combination therapy in patients with BRAF-mutated MM [13,24].

We observed that baseline brain metastases and liver metastases were independently associated with inferior OS and TTNT in patients with 1L immunotherapy and 1L TT although the negative effect was more pronounced in patients with 1L immunotherapy. After adjusted for other relevant prognostic factors in the multivariable analysis, elevated LDH was independently associated with inferior OS only in patients treated with 1L immunotherapy. Patients with baseline brain metastases, liver metastases, or elevated LDH had inferior OS and TTNT with both 1L immunotherapy and 1L TT as observed in earlier studies [12–14]. Our data showed that patients treated with 1L immunotherapy had longer OS compared with patients with 1L TT regardless of brain metastases, liver metastases and elevated LDH. Pedersen et al. reported that combination immunotherapy with ipilimumab and nivolumab resulted in longer PSF and OS compared with BRAF and MEK inhibitors in a real-world patient population with melanoma brain metastases [25]. However, there might be significant differences in the activity of intracranial disease between patients treated with immunotherapy and TT in retrospective analyses. Our study was limited to assessing merely the presence of brain metastases and not their number and activity (e.g., asymptomatic vs. symptomatic or stable vs. progressive) that have affected the choice of 1L treatment in clinical practice. Because of marked differences in relevant baseline prognostic characteristics between different treatment groups in our study the comparisons between treatment groups should be interpreted with caution.

In this study, patients treated with 1L immunotherapy had more outpatient visits whereas patients treated with 1L TT were more often hospitalized. The higher number of outpatient visits for patients with 1L immunotherapy is likely explained by the intravenous administration of immunotherapy. Higher disease burden and differences in potential adverse events, such as fever related to TT, might contribute to the higher number of inpatient days in patients with 1L TT. Ghate et al. observed similar HCRU patterns between 1L immunotherapy and TT before adjusting for baseline differences, especially brain metastases [21].

The limitations of this study are attributed to its retrospective, real-world design. Study data were automatically obtained from the electronic medical records of three University Hospitals in Finland. Some relevant prognostic characteristics might be underestimated because they have been inconsistently recorded during routine clinical practice, such as the number and sites of metastases, or not available at all, such as the ECOG performance status. Radiological responses were not analyzed in this study due to the lack of standardized reporting of treatment response in routine clinical practice. Therefore, we did not have as complete set of information available as reported from prospectively collected registries such as the Danish Metastatic Melanoma Database and the Dutch Melanoma Treatment Registry [26,27]. While this study reflects the treatment of Finnish patients with MM during study period, potential selection bias should be considered when comparing the results of different real-world studies. Treatment practices in Finland are in line with the European treatment guidelines [11] but there might have been differences in available treatment options in different countries. However, the results observed in this study were comparable with earlier real-world studies [17,19,20] supporting the generalizability of our findings in real-world patients with MM treated outside clinical trials.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we observed that patients who had received 1L immunotherapy for metastatic melanoma had favorable baseline characteristics compared with patients treated with 1L targeted therapy contributing to better treatment outcomes achieved with 1L immunotherapy in Finland consistent with the results from randomized clinical trials [15,16] and real-world reports from the US, Europe, and Nordic countries [17–20]. Patients treated with 1L immunotherapy had longer OS compared with 1L targeted therapy regardless of brain metastases, liver metastases, and elevated LDH which supports the use of upfront immunotherapy for eligible patients with poor baseline characteristics unless there is an acute need of rapid response. However, direct comparison of the outcomes of different 1L treatment options is not justified because of marked differences in baseline prognostic characteristics observed in this study. This study highlights that novel treatment options for patients with metastatic melanoma are urgently needed as over 40% of patients received 2L systemic treatment after disease progression on 1L therapy and patients with BRAF-negative disease lack effective targeted therapies. Our future work will focus on the mechanisms of treatment resistance to reveal new treatment strategies for these patients.
