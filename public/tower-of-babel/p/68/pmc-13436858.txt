Introduction

There are usually a lot of risky and challenging circumstances in the intensive care unit (ICU). In these cases, the abilities under non-technical skills (NTS) are not directly connected to technical competence but are still vital for preserving patient safety, collaboration, and the environment, facilities, and all ICU members. Numerous studies conducted in the ICU have pinpointed and categorized the non-technical skills (NTS) necessary for ensuring safety in the ICU areas.

Critical events encompass a range of unanticipated events in intensive care unit (ICU) settings that may lead to unintended patient outcomes that impact patient morbidity and mortality. These incidents fall into two categories: human and mechanical mistakes. A malfunctioning ventilator, monitor, or other piece of equipment might be the cause of mechanical faults. Airway difficulties, pharmaceutical mistakes, cardiovascular instability, falls from bed, improper ventilator settings, and CVP-related issues are all potential causes of human error. Kaur et al.[1] examined important events and discovered that human error contributed to up to 70.37% of these occurrences, while mechanical errors contributed to 29.62% of these events.

Crisis resource management (CRM) training focuses on developing these high-stakes, unexpected event-handling skills. CRM has become a critical element in achieving successful and effective patient outcomes. Successful and effective critical management should include how to manage the usual daily activities and the critical events in the ICU.

The usefulness of intensivists’ proficiency with NTS and its use in the ICU is discussed in this review. Additionally, it will cover ways to enhance intensivist training to raise NTS.

Reducing mistakes, encouraging early detection of possible crises, and launching suitable solutions all depend heavily on competent leadership, good communication, situational awareness, and resource management. Any deficiency in previous element performance usually leads to exaggerated unwilling outcomes in the ICU.

CRM Curriculum

CRM is the acronym for a collection of guidelines about interpersonal and cognitive behaviors that support the best possible team performance.[2] The main ideas covered in CRM are intended to help healthcare professionals intervene more skillfully by enabling early identification of possible negative consequences. The eight teamwork behaviors that comprise CRM are situational awareness, communication, leadership, planning and anticipating, resource utilization, workload distribution, triage and prioritization, and disruption management [Table 1].[3]

Items of crisis management curriculum

Leadership

A single, easily recognized designated leader is required during times of crisis. This person must possess strong technical knowledge, skill proficiency, and the capacity to maintain composure and organization.[4] The team leader’s role is to keep a close eye on the larger context while giving other team members guidance. Provides precise instructions by making eye contact with team members throughout the crisis, speaking to individuals by name (or by role if names are unknown), and utilizing clear, audible, closed-loop communication. Summarizes the objectives and treatment plan, establishes mutual understanding, and involves all team members in a way that is agreeable to them all. Tracks and adjusts to shifts in the mix of skills and performance of the team to maximize effectiveness.[56]

Effective communication

In times of crisis, team members must communicate, listen, and exchange information before acting on suggestions. It is critical that each team member feels heard as well as understood when others speak. It may be challenging to communicate effectively during a crisis due to the underlying tension. Team members must thus work very hard to communicate clearly. Effective communication requires the transmission of information and instructions verbally in plain, standard language that is prepared and easy to understand. Positive nonverbal cues, such as eye contact and gesturing, are also appropriate. Closed-loop communications facilitate these interactions, and by designating a team member to acquire important history and/or explain instances, the leader and other team members can promote efficient management of family members or carers.[47]

Anticipation and planning

Abrupt, unplanned advances in the medical field can have detrimental effects on patients and their care teams. Teams can prepare for changes to the patient or environment by planning and predicting potential outcomes.[8]

Planning can happen much in advance of a crisis, right before it happens, or even during it. In order to plan and allocate resources in a way that doesn’t endanger the future or the ability of the affected areas to recover, each requires forethought.[910]

Resource utilization

Cognitive, social, and personal resource skills are resources for the efficient handling of a medical emergency; these skills support technical abilities and promote safe work behavior.[11] To find all of the resources in their surroundings, teams should continuously scan their surroundings. The particular requirements of the team and the intricacy of the medical emergency will determine when to deploy each resource. Ideally, in order to influence patient outcomes, teams should request assistance as soon as possible and activate extra resources with enough advance notification.[12] The team leader plays an important role in achieving effective resource utilization according to each ICU environment, team members, and facilities. The success of resource utilization is maximized by frequent training in teamwork in different critical event scenarios.

Workload distribution

The process of dividing up duties and responsibilities among team members in a way that is effective and equitable is known as workload allocation. Analyzing each person’s talents, as well as their job and capacity at the moment, is necessary to allocate tasks in a way that is both fair and maximizes the use of all skill sets.[13]

Teams in medical emergencies have to handle several responsibilities at once. If team members are required to complete too many duties at once or jobs outside their area of expertise, they may easily get overwhelmed. The team leader is responsible for identifying workload imbalances, assessing capacity, prioritizing projects and assigning tasks, involving staff, redistributing as needed, and learning how to delegate and monitor workload metrics.[12]

Situational awareness

Situational awareness refers to the capacity to get accurate information, evaluate it, and anticipate future developments in a medical emergency so that proper action may be performed. Teams are able to quickly recognise and react to changes in a patient’s condition when situational awareness is present.[1415]

Triage and prioritization

Medical emergencies frequently reveal an imbalance between the total quantity of work that needs to be completed and the resources available to do it. Teams should identify patients who require emergency resuscitation quickly, allocate patients to a dedicated patient care area so that their care is prioritized, and start diagnostic and therapeutic procedures as needed.[1617]

Management of disruptions

Teams in the medical field frequently operate in dynamic settings with numerous interruptions. While some disruptions operate as diversions that might impair a team’s performance, others call for quick attention and resources. Teams should evaluate each disruption’s urgency as soon as possible to see if it can wait for attention to be given.[3]

How to Enhance the Effectiveness of NTS in the ICU Teams

Problem-based learning (PBL)

PBL is a powerful active learning strategy that uses the least amount of resources to improve learning outcomes and raise trainee satisfaction. Educators may also utilize PBL to enhance the impact of their presentations in medical schools that follow a standard curriculum.[18]

Simulation-based training (SBT)

Any educational activity that replicates clinical circumstances using simulative aids is known as simulation-based medical education. The use of simulation tools can be used in place of actual patients. Trainers don’t have to worry about upsetting patients when they make errors and learn from them. Before performing in clinical situations, students can benefit greatly from this kind of training by gaining both technical and NTS skills. Furthermore, there is a strong chance that the application of SBT will promote lifetime learning and ongoing professional development.[19] Outside of the hospital’s real clinical areas, the CRM curriculum typically employs SBT to generate a critical event scenario in an atmosphere close to an intensive care unit. After handling a crisis, participants can participate in a debriefing conducted by a professional educator. A crucial part of full-scale simulation is debriefing. The scenario is also recorded on video to spark conversation and ensure that all learning objectives are met. These classes might be planned as a component of the ICU trainees’ curriculum.[20]

In-situ simulations in actual ICU settings

The application of simulated scenarios in a clinical setting, as opposed to training facilities, to enhance clinical treatment and learning is known as in situ simulation. A rising number of ICU staff members are receiving training in clinical skills, emergency management, and resuscitation through in situ simulation in CRM.[21] Delivering in situ simulations has a benefit over standard SBT in that it may foster collaboration among teams as well. Additionally, many of the logistical challenges associated with developing and executing team-based training curricula are reduced or eliminated when training takes place in situ.[22]

Despite the NTS’s proven benefits, many medical practitioners do not place a high weight on these abilities. These abilities are taught to the trainees with little to no prior instruction. Now that there is more emphasis on quality, this mindset is shifting authority and implementing competency-based training.

Continual education and training help team members become more crisis-ready and enhance patient safety in general. It is imperative to acknowledge that NTS ought not to be regarded in a vacuum. For the greatest patient results, an appropriate understanding of technical skills and NTS is necessary. SWAT analysis of different training and learning sessions will empower NTS’s practice of teamwork.

Conclusion

Integrating technical and non-technical skills in ICU practice with frequent well-organized training creates effective daily ICU outcomes.
