Visual outcomes in patients with bilateral early to moderate primary open-angle glaucoma following wavefront-shaping partial-range of field–extend intraocular lens implantation
Authors: Mercè Guarro, Idoia Goñi Guarro, Elena López, Sergi Ruiz, Meritxell Vázquez, Laura Sararols, Sorcha Ní Dhubhghaill, Marc Biarnés
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/aimsTo assess binocular visual performance, visual acuity at different contrast levels and visual disturbances in patients with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) undergoing bilateral cataract surgery with the placement of wavefront-shaping partial-range of field–extend (PROF-Ex) intraocular lens (IOL).DesignProspective, descriptive, single-arm study with 3 months of follow-up.MethodsPatients diagnosed with bilateral early-to-moderate POAG were included and underwent cataract surgery with implantation of a PROF-Ex IOL (AcrySof IQ Vivity, Alcon Healthcare) bilaterally. Postoperative outcomes were measured in binocular conditions for visual acuity (uncorrected and corrected at varying distances and contrast levels), contrast sensitivity and visual disturbances assessed through light distortion analysis in terms of the light distortion index (LDI) and best-fit circle radius.Results27 patients were included, with a mean age of 74.2±8.1 years. Three months after surgery, binocular corrected visual acuities at 100% contrast were −0.01±0.06, 0.11±0.08 and 0.26±0.13 LogMAR for far, intermediate and near distance vision, respectively. Those at 25% contrast were 0.57±0.11 and 0.65±0.12 LogMAR for intermediate and near distance vision, respectively. The mean binocular contrast sensitivity was 1.63±0.11, and the mean LDI was 13.28±7.06. No significant adverse events were recorded.ConclusionsThe wavefront-shaping PROF-Ex IOL technology shows positive outcomes in terms of visual acuity, contrast sensitivity and visual disturbances for patients with bilateral early-to-moderate POAG. Thus, wavefront-shaping PROF-Ex technology lenses can be a viable and effective option for patients with POAG seeking spectacle independence.Keywords: Field of vision, Glaucoma, Optics and Refraction, Ophthalmologic Surgical Procedures, Vision

Introduction

Chronic primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) is a progressive neurodegenerative optic neuropathy that affects visual function through characteristic visual field defects and changes in contrast sensitivity and may affect visual acuity (VA) in later disease stages. Although patients with POAG often retain good VA, many report visual disturbances related to reduced contrast sensitivity—an essential function for daily visual tasks.1 2 As with the general population, patients with glaucoma undergoing cataract surgery increasingly expect reduced spectacle dependence and high postoperative visual quality.

However, several studies have reported that multifocal intraocular lenses (IOLs) may compromise contrast sensitivity because of the division of light energy between multiple focal points.3 4 This decline, particularly in higher spatial frequencies, is more pronounced in multifocal IOLs than in monofocal lenses, with observed reductions in raw scores, greyscale values and mean deviation indices.5,7 Although healthy individuals may tolerate these optical compromises, the reduction in contrast sensitivity is of greater concern in patients with glaucoma because of their pre-existing deficits. Additionally, increased glare and halo effects—often reported with complex multifocal optics—may further exacerbate visual sensitivity issues.8

Recent advancements in IOL design have led to partial-range of field–extend (PROF-Ex) lenses, which aim to provide good vision at far and intermediate distances, with some near-vision improvement.9 In low light, contrast sensitivity decreases, and pupil dilation increases ocular aberrations, affecting visual quality, such as when driving at night. Under these conditions, improved contrast sensitivity and reduced dysphotopsia of PROF-Ex lenses become evident. Several studies have shown that PROF-Ex lenses offer superior optical quality compared with bifocal or trifocal multifocals.10,12

Optical principles underlying PROF-Ex len designs available on the market vary considerably. One of the recent PROF-Ex lens design (AcrySof IQ Vivity DFT015, Alcon, Fort Worth, TX, USA) uses adaptations on the refractive surface to shape the wavefront for focus extension. The technology is based on two smooth surface transition elements responsible for stretching and shifting the wavefront: a slightly elevated plateau (~1 µm) that stretches the wavefront, resulting in a continuous extended focal range and a small curvature change that shifts the wavefront so that all the energy is usable.

In healthy individuals, the PROF-Ex IOLs have demonstrated comparable distance VA to monofocal IOLs, with superior intermediate and near vision, fewer visual disturbances and improved contrast sensitivity compared with diffractive designs.13 14 Although multifocal IOLs are traditionally contraindicated in glaucoma, recent evaluations of PROF-Ex implantation during glaucoma surgery—assessing visual function and contrast sensitivity—suggest potential benefits in selected patients.15 This study aimed to build on these findings by assessing binocular visual performance in patients with early-to-moderate POAG (MD −2 to −12 dB) undergoing bilateral cataract surgery with wavefront-shaping PROF-Ex lenses. Secondary outcomes included contrast-dependent VA and visual disturbances.

Material and methods

This investigator-initiated trial was partially funded by Alcon Healthcare (USA) under grant #64 698 055. For this perspective, descriptive, single-arm study, patients with bilateral POAG undergoing cataract surgery and AcrySof IQ Vivity placement at our centre OMIQ Research – Institut d’Oftalmologia Mèdica i Quirúrgica, in Barcelona (Spain) were followed for 3 months.

The study protocol adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Grupo Hospitalario Quirón Salud-Catalunya (Ref. 2021/58-OFT-HUGC). All participants signed an informed consent before any test was performed.

Patients

Eligible patients had bilateral cataracts with early-to-moderate POAG (according to Hodapp–Parrish–Anderson classification),16 with MD between −2 and −12 dB on two Humphrey field analyser tests within 6 months before surgery and well-controlled intraocular pressure as assessed by a glaucoma specialist (MG). Patients with pseudoexfoliative or angle-closure glaucoma, zonular instability, off-centred lenses post-surgery, other ocular conditions affecting results, prior refractive or glaucoma surgery or recent eye surgery within 6 months were excluded.

Intraocular lens

AcrySof IQ Vivity is a single-piece lens manufactured from a high-refractive index (n=1.55) hydrophobic acrylic material. The lens incorporates advanced wavefront-shaping technology. This technology consists of two smooth-surface transition elements designed to stretch and shift the wavefront. Specifically, one of these transition elements is a slightly elevated smooth plateau (~1 µm high) that stretches the wavefront, creating a continuous focal range. The other element is a small curvature change (across the 2.2 mm region) that shifts the wavefront to use all available light energy. This design allows for the use essentially of all the transmitted light energy that passes through the IOL (Alcon data on file, US Patent 9 968 440 B2, 15 May 2018). The anterior surface is designed with a negative spherical aberration of −0.20 µm for a 6 mm pupil to compensate for the positive spherical aberration of the natural human cornea.17 The IOL has an optic zone diameter and overall length of 6 and 13 mm, respectively.

Additionally, a new classification system for IOLs based on their range of focus capabilities was proposed.18 This study utilised the AcrySof IQ Vivity IOL, which aligns with the PROF-Ex definition from the functional classification proposed by Ribeiro et al.

Clinical tests

Each participant underwent a comprehensive preoperative ophthalmologic examination, which included both objective and subjective refraction, uncorrected distance (4 m) VA (UCDVA) and corrected distance VA (CDVA) using Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS test) optotypes, Goldmann contact tonometry and anterior and posterior segment examinations. Additionally, corneal topography (Pentacam, Oculus Inc., MO, USA), macular and peripapillary optical coherence tomography (OCT, with Spectralis HRA+OCT, Heidelberg Engineering, Germany) and ocular biometry (Lenstar LS 900, Haag-Streit) examination were performed.

The IOL powers were calculated using the Barrett Universal II formula to target post-surgery emmetropia. Follow-up visits were scheduled for 1 day, 1 week, 1 month and 3 months postoperatively. All surgeries were performed by the same experienced surgeon (MG). The surgery outcome variables included were reported from the 3-month visit.

Tests were conducted binocularly. Photopic (~85 cd/m2) VA was measured with ETDRS at 4 m, 66 cm and 40 cm for far, intermediate and near vision, respectively. Contrast sensitivity was evaluated using the Pelli–Robson chart, and VA was assessed at varying contrast levels. Using a light distortion analyser (HLMP-CW47-RU000, Agilent Technologies), dysphotopsias were non-invasively and objectively measured. This device consists of a central white light-emitting diode (LED) surrounded by 240 small white LEDs (HSMW-CL25, Avago Technologies, CA, USA) arranged in 24 hemi-meridians, with a separation angle of 15°, covering a 10° area at a testing distance of 2 m. Peripheral stimuli were presented from the central zone toward the field for random 250–750 ms durations; hemi-meridians were tested in random order.

Participants sat 2 m from the device in a dark room and called out when a small LED appeared different from the central LED. The test was repeated three times monocularly and binocularly; the third measurement after training was used for analysis. The light distortion index (LDI) was calculated as the ratio between the area of points missed by the participant and the whole area examined, expressed as a percentage (%) of the area examined covered by the central-LED induced halo. The best-fit circle radius (BFCRad) was defined as the circle that best fit into the area of distortion delineated by a line joining all the points in each meridian. This variable is expressed in millimetres.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated aiming at a power of 80%, an alpha value of 5% (0.05), an SD of 0.1 logMAR based on CDVA values obtained in a previous pilot study with the same lens, a two-sided 95% CI and a minimum expected difference of 0.04 logMAR. The patient sample size was 25, and assuming a 20% drop-out rate, it was increased to 30.

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics included mean±SD for quantitative and n (%) for categorical data. Paired Student’s t-tests were used for parametric comparisons between preoperative and postoperative values; otherwise, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied for non-parametric analyses.

All statistical tests were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Significance was set at a two-tailed p<0.05.

Results

28 patients were enrolled, and 27 concluded the study, meeting the aim of at least 25 patients with complete follow-up. The study population included 12 (45.8%) female and 15 (54.16%) male patients, respectively. The mean age was 74.2±8.1 (range, 52.00–89.00) years. Of the right eyes, 58% had stage 1 glaucoma and 42% had stage 2. Of the left eyes, 73% had stage 1 glaucoma and 27% had stage 2. The number of preoperative and postoperative glaucoma medications was comparable in both eyes: 1.50±0.71 and 1.42±0.65 (range 1–3), respectively.

Discussion

Glaucoma-related low-frequency contrast loss, combined with age-related decline, raises concerns about presbyopia-correcting IOLs, especially multifocals, due to added light loss.19 In non-glaucomatous cases, newer multifocals may improve light transmission and reduce contrast loss.20 We chose the wavefront-shaping PROF-Ex lens for early-to-moderate bilateral POAG, as it uses all transmitted light.

Currently, few studies have examined the performance of this lens in patients with glaucoma. Kerr et al21 included patients with various types of glaucoma in the early stage. Although their participants underwent bilateral IOL implantation, the data presented were monocular. This aspect should be considered when comparing their results with the present findings because, although monocular and binocular tests were performed on our patients, only the latter were reported. In terms of far vision, outcomes were similar; however, for intermediate and near vision, they only incorporated uncorrected data. The results for the intermediate and near vision were comparable between the two studies (0.08±0.07 vs 0.06±0.16 and 0.23±0.13 vs 0.29±0.10, respectively). Methodological differences between studies were previously noted.

Ferguson et al15 included patients with mid-stage glaucoma and a trabecular micro-bypass stent device (iStent; Glaukos, San Clemente, CA, USA) concurrently implanted with the AcrySof IQ Vivity IOL during cataract surgery. For far vision, the reported CDVA was −0.06±0.07, slightly better than that obtained here. However, UINV and UNVA (0.17±0.12 and 0.31±0.17, respectively) were slightly better in the present study.

In a retrospective study by Dessouky et al,22 patients diagnosed with early-to-moderate POAG were examined. Some of these patients received the same iStent device used in Ferguson et al’s study, and they also had the PROF-Ex intraocular lens (IOL) from Lucidis, Swiss Advanced Vision (SAV-IOL SA) implanted during cataract surgery.

Regarding uncorrected and corrected far VA (0.05±0.09; 0.01±0.06, respectively), our patients had slightly better UCDVA and comparable CDVA. However, Dessouky et al did not specify whether their measurements were taken under monocular or binocular conditions. Given that they evaluated 14 unilateral and 14 bilateral eyes, we assume that they assessed monocular vision, which makes their results not directly comparable with our data.

When comparing our outcomes with those of a study23 involving patients with POAG undergoing placement of a different method of achieving PROF-Ex (TECNIS Eyhance ICB00 lens, Johnson & Johnson Vision Care Inc., Santa Ana, USA), the distance VAs in our cohort were slightly better (−0.01±0.06 vs 0.01±0.04); however, note that we report binocular data. For uncorrected intermediate and near vision, as presented in the study by Nam et al,23 the wavefront-shaping PROF-Ex lens yielded improved results (0.08±0.07 vs 0.34±0.10 and 0.23±0.13 vs 0.50±0.10, respectively).

This difference could be attributed to the fact that the TECNIS ICB00 is a monofocal plus IOL rather than a PROF-Ex lens and thus has different optical properties, despite evidence indicating the lack of effect on retinal sensitivity.24

Our results in bilateral glaucoma were compared with those in healthy individuals based on our literature review. Scheepers et al25 reported binocular DCIVA outcomes (0.04±0.08) slightly better than ours, with similar near vision results (0.22±0.12). Kandavel et al26 found comparable results in far, intermediate and near vision (0.06±0.07; 0.12±0.11 and 0.36±0.12). These findings suggest that the wavefront-shaping PROF-Ex lens yields similar VA in healthy patients and those with moderate POAG under photopic and high-contrast conditions.

In the study by Ferguson et al.15 and Dessouky and Mermoud,22 contrast sensitivity was analysed through the Pelli–Robson test. Ferguson et al reported a binocular value of 1.78±0.17, slightly better than 1.62±0.11 of the present study. We found no data in the literature for comparisons with healthy individuals and the same IOL under study.

Dessouky and Mermoud22 reported 3-month postoperative mean contrast sensitivity of 1.56±0.11 log. No significant differences were found based on the glaucoma control method (p=0.08) or iStent use (p=0.14). A significant correlation existed between mean deviation and contrast sensitivity (p<0.001). The study did not specify whether assessments were monocular or binocular and measured contrast sensitivity under photopic conditions at one spatial frequency, focusing on mean deviation.

When comparing our results with those obtained in phakic cases with glaucoma, whether in early or moderate stages, the wavefront-shaping PROF-Ex lens demonstrated good performance in terms of contrast sensitivity. Bambo et al27 reported 1.76±0.18 and 1.51±0.30 in the early and moderate stages, respectively, using the Pelli–Robson test. In contrast, our unified evaluation of the two groups yielded 1.62±0.11. On the contrary, our results also align with those reported by Guarro et al28 concerning contrast sensitivity obtained with the same test in healthy patients with monofocal lens implantation, specifically Acrysof IQ SN60WF (Alcon), which reported 1.63±0.08.

Bissen-Miyajima et al29 compared PROF-Ex (TECNIS Symfony) and monofocal IOLs in mild-to-moderate POAG. Both had similar visual outcomes, but some PROF-Ex eyes showed reduced photopic contrast sensitivity at 3 months, possibly due to lower visual field sensitivity or the diffractive design of the PROF-Ex IOL.

The same author recently examined visual field effects on acuity and contrast sensitivity in patients with POAG using the same IOLs.30 In PROF-Ex IOL eyes, high-frequency contrast sensitivity (12–18 cpd) correlated with central visual field (30-2, 10-2), whereas in monofocal IOL eyes, 30–2 correlated only with CDVA and 3 cpd contrast sensitivity. PROF-Ex IOL function links to perimetry in high frequencies, unlike monofocal IOLs. Further studies on other lens types are needed.

Our findings regarding refractive outcomes and VA are consistent with those reported by Urcola et al and Liu et al.31 32 However, Liu et al included a heterogeneous sample with many participants having suspected glaucoma or ocular hypertension, which limits direct comparability.32 In contrast, both the study by Urcola et al and the present study involved a more homogeneous cohort of patients with mild-to-moderate glaucoma. Although our improvements in contrast sensitivity are in line with those of Urcola et al,31 Liu et al did not perform it,32 precluding direct comparison in this domain. Additionally, the present study used the light distortion analyser to objectively quantify visual disturbances through the LDI, thereby providing a more precise and objective assessment compared with the subjective questionnaires used in previous studies.

When measuring dysphotopsia, our group previously examined visual disturbances following the same method as used in the present study but in healthy patients. The LDI value was 8.24±3.86, significantly better than those obtained in our study at 13.28±7.06, and the BFCRad value was 22.87±5.55 compared with our 28.79±8.20.13 Hence, besides healthy individuals returning better measures of dysphotopsia with the same lens, same lens, the data were more homogeneous, as the SD obtained in the present study was clearly higher. Despite this, the present results were better than those reported in the study for AT LARA 829MO (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany) and TECNIS Symfony ZXR00 (Johnson & Johnson Vision Care Inc.) in terms of LDI (19.93 and 16.51%, respectively) and BFCRad (34.75 and 31.70 mm, respectively). These results confirm the excellent performance of the wavefront-shaping PROF-Ex lens in glaucoma, comparable with diffractive lenses in healthy eyes, where light distribution affects visual disturbances.13

All postoperative contrast sensitivity and dysphotopsia values improved over preoperative levels, often due to cataract removal.

The small sample size and lack of a control group were limitations of this pilot study, as PROF-Ex IOLs are not yet standard care for glaucoma. Additionally, the short follow-up period limits the interpretation of long-term outcomes. Future studies with a larger study group should include reassessment over an extended period—beyond neuroadaptation and considering glaucoma’s progressive nature—which could provide valuable insights. Although Clareon material has demonstrated some of the lowest glare levels compared with other IOLs, both AcrySof and Clareon materials are mechanically and optically equivalent,33,35 and a recent study reported contrast sensitivity results for Clareon Vivity in a healthy population.35 Since both IOLs share the same design, this study provides valuable insights into their use in patients with early-to-moderate glaucoma, although further research is warranted to explore long-term outcomes.

AcrySof IQ Vivity DFT015 lens offers favourable VA, contrast sensitivity and reduced visual disturbances in early-to-moderate POAG. Its wavefront-shaping design avoids light loss, making it suitable for patients with impaired contrast sensitivity. Although promising, further data are needed to confirm its efficacy in glaucoma populations.
