Predicting postoperative pulmonary function improvement in pediatric scoliosis using 3-dimensional computed tomography analysis
Authors: Jen-Ta Shih, Masafumi Kawai, Satoshi Kato, Takaki Shimizu, Satoru Demura, Noriaki Yokogawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThoracic scoliosis is associated with pulmonary function impairment, yet the predictive value of thoracic deformity parameters remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate whether 3-dimensional computed tomography (3D-CT) analysis predicts postoperative pulmonary function improvement in pediatric patients with thoracic scoliosis.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 67 pediatric patients with thoracic scoliosis who underwent posterior spinal fusion. All patients completed preoperative and 2-year postoperative pulmonary function tests (PFTs), radiographs, and low-dose chest CT scans. 3D-CT was used to assess lung volume (LV), airway dimensions, and rib cage parameters. Patients were grouped into adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS, n = 56) and non-AIS (n = 11) cohorts. Correlations were determined between anatomical deformities and changes in pulmonary function using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.ResultsSurgical correction significantly improved thoracic Cobb angle and PFT values. Preoperative LV and bronchial cross-sectional area (CSA) correlated positively with postoperative FVC%. Rib cage deformity metrics—including apical rotation angle, vertebra-sternum angle, and spinal penetration index—were associated with greater FVC% improvement. Correction of thoracic hypokyphosis toward a more physiological kyphosis was also significantly linked to postoperative pulmonary function gains. Subgroup analysis indicated that non-AIS patients, although presenting with more severe deformities and poorer baseline function, achieved greater improvements postoperatively, yet still had inferior outcomes compared with AIS patients.ConclusionsThis study showed that 3D low-dose CT imaging provides critical insights into the relationship between thoracic deformity and pulmonary function in pediatric scoliosis. Preoperative lung volume strongly predicted postoperative outcomes, and patients with more severe deformities had lower baseline function but greater improvement after surgery. Correction of thoracic hypokyphosis toward an acceptable kyphosis enhanced thoracic cavity configuration and pulmonary capacity. These results highlight the importance of detailed preoperative assessment and precise surgical planning. Incorporating 3D imaging may help tailor corrective strategies, particularly in non-idiopathic scoliosis with complex deformities and potential for greater functional recovery.Trial registrationNot applicable.

Introduction

The association between progression of thoracic scoliosis and pulmonary function impairment is known. Several factors such as greater number of vertebrae involved in thoracic deformity, greater head position of curvature, hypokyphosis, and curve flexibility have been reported as predictive factors for pulmonary function [1, 2]. A number of previous reports have described that there is an inverse relationship between thoracic Cobb angles and pulmonary function [3, 4]. Scoliosis may result in restrictive pulmonary function because of the thoracic cage deformity in lung volume, displacement of intrathoracic organs, obstruction of rib movement, and impairment of respiratory muscle mechanics [5].

Pulmonary function is also reported to be indirectly associated with scoliosis, supporting the hypothesis that decreased pulmonary function is due to chest wall deformities or other factors rather than scoliosis itself [6]. Nevertheless, many pulmonary function tests (PFT) and analysis of plain radiographs are reported in scoliosis patients, although few reports include lung volume and airway analysis using 3-dimensional (3D) low-dose computed tomography (CT).

Historically, scoliosis in the pediatric population has been classified into idiopathic, congenital, neuromuscular, or syndromic categories. Management trends for syndromic scoliosis have mirrored those for idiopathic scoliosis [7]. However, syndromic scoliosis presents increased risk of complications during surgical deformity correction; complication rates are significantly higher than those in patients with adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) and comparable to those in neuromuscular scoliosis [8, 9]. Studies on pulmonary function in other types of scoliosis remain sparse, highlighting the need for further research in these areas.

The purpose of this study was to examine associations between perioperative radiographic parameters and pulmonary function in pediatric patients surgically treated for scoliosis using low-dose CT-based imaging analysis, and to determine whether these parameters were associated with changes in pulmonary function. The study also focused on differences between AIS patients and those with non-AIS types of scoliosis.

Patients and methods

Study population

A series of consecutive pediatric patients with primary thoracic, right-convex scoliosis withe T7-T10 level apex, who underwent primary posterior correction and fusion surgery at a single institute between March 2010 and March 2021, were enrolled. Patients with a history of thoracic or spine surgery, existing lung or airway diseases requiring medical management (such as asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), or an inability to comply with pulmonary function tests (PFTs) were excluded. All participants completed standing radiographs of the whole spine, PFTs, and preoperative low-dose CT scans of the entire thorax and spine for surgical planning. Included patients were divided into two groups for comparison: the AIS group and the non-AIS group.

Surgical techniques

In all cases, posterior corrective fusion was performed. First, the posterior elements were exposed. Then, soft tissue release, grade one osteotomies in all facet joints, and peri-apical grade two osteotomies in rigid curve were performed. Subsequently, segmental pedicle screws were inserted. Deformity correction was performed by placement of an over-contoured rod on the concave side, followed by cantilever reduction of the thoracic hump with an under-contoured convex rod. The coronal and sagittal corrections were adjusted using compression and distraction technique. Finally direct vertebral rotation was performed to maximize axial correction, as previously described [10].

Spinal and rib cage parameters

The main thoracic (MT) and proximal thoracic (PT) Cobb angles were measured on standing coronal radiographs, whereas thoracic kyphosis (TK; superior endplate of T2 to inferior endplate of T12) and the lumbar curve (LC; superior endplate of L1 to superior endplate of S1) were measured on sagittal radiographs. All measurements were obtained using the Cobb method [11]. Changes in these parameters were calculated as postoperative values minus preoperative values.

The axial CT image at the apical level was used to measure the apical rotation angle (ARA), the vertebra–sternum angle (VSA) (Fig. 1), and rib cage parameters as described by Farrell et al. [12], including the hemi-thoracic width ratio (HWR), rib hump index (RHI), endothoracic hump ratio (EHR), and spinal penetration index (SPI) (Fig. 2). The apical rotation angle (ARA) was defined as the angle between the vertebral body bisectional line and a vertical reference line on the axial CT image. The vertebra–sternum angle (VSA) was defined as the angle between the vertebral body bisectional line and the line connecting the midpoint of the sternum to the center of the spinal canal. The HWR was defined as the ratio of the convex (W1) to concave (W2) horizontal distance from the vertebral bisectional line. The RHI was calculated as the ratio of the anteroposterior distance between the most posterior points of each hemithorax (H1) to the mediolateral distance from the spinal canal to the posterior point of the convex hemithorax (H2). The EHR was defined as the ratio between two distances measured on axial CT images: the distance (E1) between a line connecting the most posterior points of both hemithoraces and a parallel line passing through the anterior vertebral body, and the distance (E2) between the latter line and a parallel line through the inner midpoint of the sternum. Finally, the SPI was calculated as the proportion of the chest cavity area occupied by the vertebra (S1/S2). Lung volume and airway parameters

Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of the chest CT was performed and analyzed using Synapse Vincent 3D image analysis system (Fujifilm Corp., Tokyo, Japan). This system produces high-quality 3D images and isolates the location and shape of the lungs and bronchial tree, respectively (Fig. 3). The lung volume (LV) of each lobe was calculated. The mean cross-sectional area (CSA) of the bronchus was determined by dividing the volume by the length of each segment and the lumen areas of the primary and secondary bronchus were summed to represent an airway parameter.Fig. 33D reconstruction of lung lobes and bronchi on the software. PFTs

PFTs were performed for all patients preoperatively and at two years postoperatively. Spirometry was conducted in a sitting position following the joint American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society standards [14]. The following PFT parameters were measured: forced vital capacity (FVC), defined as the volume of air that can be exhaled forcibly after a full inspiration; forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), defined as the volume of air that can be exhaled forcibly in the first second after a full inspiration. Pulmonary function tests (PFTs) were reported relative to predicted values, and arm span was used to estimate corrected standing height. Percent-of-predicted values, such as FVC% (FVC/predicted FVC ratio) and FEV1% (FEV1/predicted FEV1 ratio) were calculated according to the spirometric reference values [15].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). The paired t-test was used to analyze differences between pre- and postoperative parametric data. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to determine correlations. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to analyze differences between groups. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the study hospital (Approval number: 1893). The IRB waived informed consent of included patients or their parents or guardians because the research involved analysis of anonymized retrospective data with no more than minimal risk to the privacy of the pediatric patients. All procedures were performed in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Results

Sixty-seven patients (59 girls, 8 boys, mean age 14.8 years; range, 10–19 years) were included. Among the study cohort, 56 patients were diagnosed with adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS), while 11 had non-idiopathic scoliosis of syndromic origin. The syndromic group comprised six girls and five boys, including two cases of Prader–Willi syndrome, two of Marfan syndrome, three of Neurofibromatosis, two of Noonan syndrome, and one of Syringomyelia. Comparisons of radiographic and pulmonary function parameters between the AIS and non-AIS groups are presented in Table 3. Significant differences were found between the two groups in age, body size, and PF. The non-AIS group had a tendency toward hyperkyphosis (TK: 24.9 vs. 38.8, p = 0.004), and had greater deformity in the coronal and axial planes (MT: 61.5 vs. 76.5, p = 0.013; LC: 36 vs. 43.5, p = 0.042; ARA: 13 vs. 19.4, p = 0.013). These differences were also retained at two years postoperatively. Given significantly lower values of LV and CSA, the non-AIS group had poorer pulmonary function preoperatively (FVC%: 79.9 vs. 68.1, p = 0.04). At two years postoperative follow-up, although the AIS group had better pulmonary function the difference became nonsignificant in multivariable analysis. (FVC%: 82.1 vs. 73.3, p = 0.06). Discussion

The present study retrospectively evaluated spinal and thoracic cage deformity, lung volume, and airway dimensions based on reconstructed 3D-CT analysis to better understand the correlation between pulmonary function and these parameters in pediatrics scoliosis patients. Comparison of measurements preoperatively and at 2 years postoperatively revealed significant correction of scoliosis and improvement in PFT, demonstrating the benefits of surgery on pulmonary function outcomes. After analyzing the correlation between postoperative changes in pulmonary function and the measured parameters, the measurements were stratified by AIS and non-AIS groups to understand differences associated with different preoperative characteristics of each subgroup. These differences may influence postoperative pulmonary function outcomes helping surgeons identify the key factors of surgery and postoperative care protocol in patients with scoliosis of different patterns.

Several studies have investigated the associations between pulmonary function and scoliosis correction. Nakashima et al. [16] reported that postoperative pulmonary function improvement in a case series of 146 adult scoliosis patients was limited to those with severe preoperative impairment. Similarly, a retrospective study by Ledonio et al. [17], which included 20 AIS patients, found a strong correlation between scoliosis correction and improved total lung capacity in cases with significantly reduced pulmonary function, but not in cases with normal pulmonary function. However, in contrast, Lorete et al. [18] reported that cardiorespiratory function did not improve even 2 years after posterior surgical correction in 20 patients with severe AIS Lenke type 1 A scoliosis. Results of the present study, aided by reconstructed 3D-CT scans, showed that improvement in forced vital capacity (FVC%) correlated moderately with rib cage deformity. In another study, these parameters were also associated with the volume of the right lung [19].

Furthermore, thoracic kyphosis (TK) has become increasingly recognized as a key factor in optimizing both pulmonary function and sagittal balance. Normal kyphosis ranges from approximately 20° to 40° in adolescents [20], while in adults it averages 48°–50° in women and about 44° in men [21, 22]. Accordingly, a TK angle of less than 20° is defined as hypokyphosis, whereas an angle greater than 40° is generally classified as hyperkyphosis. In another retrospective study involving 39 patients with AIS, surgical correction of kyphosis did not correlate directly with improvements in pulmonary function [23]. However, the present study demonstrated a significant positive association between postoperative TK angles and the enhancement of pulmonary function at two-year follow-up. Specifically, patients with more pronounced apical deviation due to scoliosis before surgery and those exhibiting less hypokyphosis after surgery experienced greater improvements in their pulmonary function.

Findings of the present study suggest that the degree of thoracic kyphosis correction plays a crucial role in respiratory outcomes of scoliosis patients receiving surgical correction. By surgically correcting the spine from a hypokyphosis status to an acceptable thoracic kyphosis, the shape and volume of the thoracic cavity are improved, thereby enhancing lung capacity and function. Therefore, the pulmonary function of scoliosis patients may improve substantially if their spinal deformity is surgically corrected to an optimal thoracic kyphosis, highlighting the need for careful preoperative planning and precise surgical techniques to achieve the desired spinal alignment and maximize postoperative pulmonary function.

The health-related quality of life of patients with scoliosis is influenced by the scoliosis subtype [24]. Previous studies of pulmonary function in patients with scoliosis other than AIS have been limited and have only been followed for a short period [25, 26]. In the present study, as explained in the Methods section above, pediatric patients with scoliosis were divided into two subgroups: AIS and non-AIS. Preoperatively, non-AIS patients had smaller body size and poorer pulmonary function. However, these parameters differed between the subgroups without significance after two years of surgery. In other words, surgical correction can have greater benefits for non-AIS patients than for AIS patients. The ventilatory dysfunction in thoracic scoliosis is not only restrictive, but often also has an obstructive component [12, 25]. Impacted pulmonary bronchial development due to spinal-thoracic deformity has been reported in patients with AIS [27]. Preoperative CT data in Table 3 shows that CSA and LV are lower in the non-AIS group, accompanied by greater rotational and axial deformity. Although no apparent deterioration was noted in FEV1% preoperatively (mean > 80% in both subgroups), a significant improvement in FEV1% was noted two years postoperatively.

Computed tomography (CT) scans have been used in previous studies to provide accurate measurements of lung anatomy and lung volume (LV) in patients with AIS [28, 29]. In this study, we also used 3D CT reconstruction image analysis software to calculate the preoperative LV. The results showed that there was an extremely positive correlation between preoperative LV and both preoperative and 2 year postoperative pulmonary function. These results applied not only to AIS patients but also to non-AIS patients. In other words, the preoperative LV can help surgeons to predict the pulmonary function performance of the scoliosis patient even after the operation due to the high correlation between LV and lung function. In some studies, the postoperative LV was also calculated using the same method at follow-up, but the results were inconclusive due to the inconsistent timing of the postoperative scan [30, 31].

Airway narrowing due to spinal morphology is considered an obstructive component of ventilatory dysfunction in AIS patients [12]. Farrell et al. [32] examined preoperative 3D-CT reconstructed airway models of 34 scoliosis patients and found that loss of kyphosis leads to narrowing of the bronchus intermedius and its trifurcation. In the present study, the AIS group was relatively hypokyphotic compared to the non-AIS group. CSA as a proxy for the airway parameter showed a positive correlation with preoperative pulmonary function including FVC (L), FVC (%) and FEV1 (L) in the AIS group. After the surgical correction, the TK curve decreased in the AIS group. At the same time, the influence of CSA on postoperative lung function decreased. From this we hypothesize that the preoperative CSA is useful for predicting the preoperative pulmonary function of patients with thoracic hypokyphosis. Meanwhile, scoliosis patients may benefit from surgical correction associated with obstructive lung disease.

The impact of scoliosis deformity affects the non-AIS group more than the AIS group. The non-AIS group had higher VSA and ARA and lower HWR than the AIS group. These deformity parameters also showed a correlation with preoperative lung function and posterior pulmonary function in the non-AIS group. Scoliosis patients with severe deformity tend to have deteriorated pulmonary function preoperatively. Although pulmonary function improved more in scoliosis patients with severe deformity after surgery, pulmonary function outcomes were still worse than in patients with less deformity. This cohort would require more postoperative rehabilitation efforts to maintain or improve their pulmonary function [33, 34].

Limitations

This study has several limitations, including, first, that the sample was small with uneven numbers in subgroups. However, the different pulmonary function results between patients with AIS and those with syndromic scoliosis patients were worth investigating. Second, no CT data were available at two-years postoperatively to compare the differences between the subgroups. Ongoing routine CT follow-up may place adolescent patients under additional radiation exposure, so patients only underwent radiographs exams and PFT at specific limited timings. Third, the improvements in pulmonary function may be overestimated due to normal growth of the patients’ bodies within two years’ follow-up. Nevertheless, the patients’ pulmonary function was recorded in not only the absolute numbers but also the percentage, which was adjusted by increased height and weight.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that 3D low-dose CT imaging offers valuable insights into the relationship between thoracic deformity and pulmonary function in pediatric scoliosis. Comprehensive assessment of pulmonary function before and after surgery shows that preoperative lung volume is a strong predictor of postoperative respiratory performance. Patients with more severe thoracic deformities tend to have lower baseline pulmonary function but show greater improvement following surgical correction, highlighting the importance of detailed thoracic assessment for both surgical planning and long-term prognosis in pediatric scoliosis. In particular, the findings suggest that the degree of thoracic kyphosis correction plays a crucial role in respiratory outcomes. By surgically correcting the spine from a hypokyphosis status to an acceptable thoracic kyphosis, the thoracic cavity configuration and lung capacity can be improved, thereby enhancing postoperative pulmonary function. In clinical practice, incorporating 3D imaging into preoperative evaluation can help identify patients who may benefit most from corrective surgery and allow surgeons to tailor their approach to optimize not only spinal alignment but also respiratory outcomes, with special attention to achieving optimal thoracic kyphosis. This is particularly relevant for non-idiopathic scoliosis patients, who often present with more complex deformities and greater potential for functional recovery.
