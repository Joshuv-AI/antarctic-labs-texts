A “rete” posterior cerebral artery: Frozen in transition or acquired type?
Authors: Marialuisa Zedde, Takahiro Ota, Rosario Pascarella
Published: 2026

Introduction

The so-called “rete” or twig middle cerebral artery (MCA) is characterized by the replacement of the M1 segment with a plexiform network of arteries. It has been described both as a consequence of a focal intracranial arteriopathy[1234] and as an embryologic remnant in either Asian[1] and western[56] populations.[789] In addition, a morphologically similar “rete-like” pattern has been described not only on MCA but also on the internal carotid artery (ICA) terminus and A1 anterior cerebral artery.[10] Two cases showed the simultaneous presence of anterior choroidal artery (AchoA) and posterior cerebral artery (PCA) “rete mirabile” with subarachnoid hemorrhage.[1112] The authors proposed this pattern as caused by an interruption in the embryological process ending with the adult PCA.[1112] However, a true “rete mirabile” with a functional role has been documented only in lower mammals, but not in humans.[13] In another case,[14] a detailed interpretation of the rete PCA as a remnant of the AchoA origin was proposed. There are no reported cases of association among rete PCA and ischemic stroke and rete PCA and systemic diseases, including genetic or acquired prothrombotic conditions. In fact, in humans, the “rete-like” collaterals are probably a secondary event and not a congenital pattern.[13]

Among acquired prothrombotic conditions, antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) has been associated with different neurovascular manifestations, involving both large and small arteries.[15] APS has been associated (weak evidence) with moyamoya arteriopathy, which is one of the potential differential diagnoses of the rete or twig MCA, but the moyamoya pattern does not involve other arteries,[16] and this association has been recently proposed as coincidental.[17]

We report a case of a “rete-like” pattern on the PCA in a patient with APS diagnosed after ischemic stroke, raising a diagnostic reasoning about embryological or acquired origin of this pattern and its relation to the true “rete.”

Case Description

We are proposing the case of a 63-year-old male with a nonrelevant past medical history. The patient went to the neurological attention through the emergency department, with left-sided weakness. A noncontrast computed tomography (CT) scan of the brain was unsignificant [Figure 1a]; however, the follow-up on day 2 [Figure 1b] and 5 [Figure 1c] showed a hypodense lesion in the subcortical white matter underlying the right precentral sulcus, as for a recent small subcortical infarction. Laboratory tests revealed triple positivity for IgM anti-beta-2 glycoprotein 1, anticardiolipin antibodies, and lupus anticoagulant. A presumptive diagnosis of APS was made, and oral anticoagulation with Warfarin (target INR 2–3) was started. After 2 months, the patient underwent brain magnetic resonance (MR) imaging and MR angiography (MRA) [Figures 2 and 3]. MRA [Figure 3] imaged a “replacement” of the right P2–P3 PCA by a tiny and twisted network of arteries.

Discussion

The presented case offers the chance of some considerations. The stroke pattern was small vessel disease (SVD)-related in both episodes, involving the intermediate perforators of the right MCA in the first episode and the P1 PCA perforators in the second one. The role of APS in chronic and acute SVD has been extensively described with or without classic vascular risk factors.[14] The recurrence of ischemic stroke while on subtherapeutic anticoagulation supports the APS as the main etiology.[14] On the other hand, being the association of APS with intracranial arteriopathy questionable and not demonstrated,[17] the “rete-like” PCA cannot be proposed as APS-related (e.g., as a consequence of an extreme development of collateralization in a chronic occlusion). Moreover, a similar evolution of a segmental atherosclerotic stenosis seems unlikely.

We propose, as the most likely hypothesis, the incidental discovery of a “rete-like” PCA pattern in a patient with APS and suggest an embryological origin resulting from an interrupted or halted stage in the transition from fetal to adult PCA configuration. As expected, this pattern encompasses both the AchoA and the PCA in our case and in the three previously described cases.[111214] The PCA and AchoA are closely interrelated during embryonic development. In fact, the P1 PCA originates from the posterior division of the ICA, whereas the P2–P4 PCA is derived from the primitive AchoA, which is the rostral division of the ICA.[13] The connection between these two different arteries is termed “distal annexation.”[16] The formation of a “rete-like” PCA has been postulated to arise from a remnant of this distal annexation between the primitive AchoA and the PCA during embryogenesis,[1214] being “rete” a network of multiple arteries or arterioles that interconnect and compensate for absent or hypoplastic segments of the parent artery.[18] As previously introduced, “rete mirabile” represents the normal adult configuration in lower mammals,[1920] but it is probably misnamed in humans,[13] including the association with other vascular anomalies.[2021] Beyond ICA, a rete pattern has been described on the vertebral artery, PCA, and posterior inferior cerebellar artery.[12141922] In the early embryonic stage, the primitive AchoA supplies the temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes with cortical branches, which later develop into the P2–P4 PCA during distal annexation.[2324] Hyperplastic AchoA with persistent cortical branches is a rare anomaly resulting from incomplete distal annexation between the primitive AchoA and the PCA.[232425] In the “rete-like” PCA, it was speculated that the persistence of the embryonic anastomoses between the primitive AchoA and the PCA was the underlying cause.[14] In the current case, both AchoA and PCA contribute to a small network of interconnected vessels, supporting the hypothesis of an incomplete distal annexation process. In a previous report,[11] a dual rete in the PCA and in the AchoA was described, differing from our case. One possible explanation for this discrepancy is that the distal annexation process in our case was interrupted at a later stage than in the previously reported case.[11] In addition, in the final configuration, the annexed cortical AchoA branches evolve into the P2–P4 PCA, but when distal annexation is incomplete, the AchoA retains varying degrees of cortical branches from the temporo-parieto-occipital lobes, manifesting as “hyperplastic” variants of the AchoA. In our case, the AchoA [Figures 5-7] has probably interrupted its regression at a later phase, and the network includes anastomoses between the AchoA and posterior choroidal arteries [Figure 7].

A “rete” pattern has been described in association with subarachnoid hemorrhage (34.2%), but also as an incidental finding (20%).[26] Subarachnoid hemorrhage can result from an associated aneurysm, the rupture of a fragile artery within the network, or hemodynamic stress.[27] In our case, no aneurysm was identified, and we propose that it is merely an incidental finding. Although in pigs, the “rete mirabile” provides an animal model for arteriovenous malformations,[1928] the “rete” usually supplies normal brain tissue without an arteriovenous shunt. Another potential differential diagnosis is pure arterial malformation,[19293031] but the involvement of both the AchoA and PCA in our case does not support this hypothesis.

The main limitation of the presented case is the lack of a digital subtraction angiography. In fact, catheter angiography was not performed due to the risk in a patient with APS and recurrent ischemic stroke under anticoagulation, being an incidental nonpathological finding, the main suspicion. However, we acknowledge that a better definition of the angioarchitecture of the network could support our conclusion. Finally, this finding has been reported as incidental and not related to the clinical presentation of the patient, and a conservative approach was proposed.

Conclusions

The so-called “rete” pattern in the PCA is a rare finding, and it may help to better understand the embryological process of distal annexation between the primitive AChoA and the PCA in its steps. Noninvasive imaging can identify this pattern, and it has to be recognized to avoid misinterpretation as causally linked to cerebrovascular events. However, this hypothesis, although suggestive, is not final, and, when feasible, catheter angiography should be suggested to exclude alternative acquired causes.
