Telework and mental health among caregiving workers in Japan: a cross-sectional study using data from the 2024 JACSIS and implications for age-friendly workplaces
Authors: Takeshi Miura, Kazumi Kubota, Maya Minamizaki, Yuka Kanoya, Takahiro Tabuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTelework is promoted as a strategy to support work-care balance; however, its mental health implications for workers providing childcare and/or older adult care remain unclear. We aimed to examine whether the association between caregiving roles and poor mental health differs according to teleworking frequency, with implications for developing age-friendly work environments.MethodsWe analyzed the cross-sectional data of 12,692 employed adults (aged 20–59 years) from the 2024 Japan coronavirus disease 2019 and Society Internet Surveys. Caregiving status was classified into no caregiving, childcare only, older adult care only, and dual caregiving. The telework frequency was none, 1 day/week, 3 days/week, or daily. Poor mental health was defined as ≥ 1 mentally unhealthy day in the past 30 days. Logistic regression was used to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) stratified by telework frequency and adjusted for demographics, socioeconomic status, health conditions, and job-related and social support factors.ResultsRelative to no caregiving, adjusted odds of poor mental health were higher for older adult care (OR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.43–2.16) and dual caregiving (OR = 2.62, 95% CI: 1.89–3.62). Among dual caregivers, odds were highest when teleworking 1 day/week (OR = 4.23, 95% CI: 2.24–7.99), imprecise at 3 days/week (OR = 2.18, 95% CI: 0.77–6.11), and not significant for daily teleworking. For older adult care, teleworking 3 days/week showed elevated, though non-significant odds (OR = 1.87, 95% CI: 0.85–4.07).ConclusionsOlder adult care and dual caregiving were linked to poorer mental health, and teleworking effects were nonlinear. Therefore, integrating flexible work plan with supportive job conditions may be beneficial in managing poor mental health among caregivers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07406-w.Keywords: Caregiving, Mental health, Telework, Older adult care, Work-family conflict, Age-friendly environments, Workplace policy, Japan

Background

In Japan, the number of employees who provide family care while continuing to work (working caregivers) has steadily increased [1, 2]. Traditionally, older adult care responsibilities tend to arise after individuals have completed the most intensive periods of child rearing and career development. However, demographic and societal changes, including increased life expectancy, delayed marriage and childbirth, have shifted the timing of caregiving. Consequently, an increasing number of individuals in their prime working years are simultaneously responsible for raising children and caring for aging parents, leading to a growing burden of managing multiple caregiving roles within the household [3–5].

Dual caregiving may place individuals at greater risk of psychological strain due to heightened role overload and work-family conflict [6]. These pressures affect caregivers’ well-being and may also influence labor force participation, job stability, and productivity at the population level. In this context, workplace support for balancing employment and caregiving has emerged as a crucial policy issue, and fostering an age-friendly society requires attention to caregivers’ mental health and well-being [7–9].

Telework has expanded rapidly in Japan since the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic and is usually promoted as a strategy to support work-care balance [10]. Teleworking may help caregivers to remain employed while responding more flexibly to family needs by reducing commute time and providing greater control over daily schedules [11, 12]. However, the benefits of teleworking vary across individuals [13]. Telework can blur the boundaries between work and home, potentially increasing the psychological burden, particularly when caregiving demands are high or workplace autonomy is limited [14–16]. Recent discussions emphasize that teleworking should include a broader set of support, including leave systems, workplace accommodations, and community-based care services.

Despite the increasing attention given to working caregivers, empirical evidence on whether teleworking mitigates the mental health burden associated with caregiving remains limited [17, 18]. In particular, few studies have focused on workers who simultaneously provide childcare and older adult care, a group that is likely to experience substantial role strain. Understanding how mental health varies according to the caregiving role and teleworking frequency is essential for designing policies and workplace environments that adequately support diverse caregiving situations. Consequently, in this study, we aimed to examine the association between caregiving roles, teleworking frequency, and mental health among employed adults in Japan.

Methods

Study design

We used data from the 2024 Japan COVID-19 and Society Internet Survey (JACSIS). The JACSIS is a nationwide web-based panel survey that monitors health, lifestyle, and social conditions in Japan. Participants were recruited from a large online panel administered by Rakuten Insight Inc. (Tokyo, Japan; https://member.insight.rakuten.co.jp/). Sampling was designed to reflect the distributions of sex, age groups, and residential regions across all 47 prefectures in Japan [19].

Data were collected between December 2024 and January 2025. Participants included in the analysis were individuals aged 20–59 years who were currently employed. We excluded students, and those who were not currently employed (e.g., retired, homemakers, or unemployed), or engaged only in irregular work arrangements. After applying these inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final analytical sample comprised 12,692 participants. The survey was conducted using a required-response format, meaning that participants were required to provide responses to all items before proceeding; therefore, no missing values were generated for individual variables.

Outcome variable

Poor mental health was assessed using the mental health item (response range, 0–30 days) of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) HRQOL-4 (Healthy Days Measures), which asks the following: “Now thinking about your mental health, which includes stress, depression, and problems with emotions, how many days during the past 30 days was your mental health not good?” [20].

This item has often been summarized to distinguish individuals with no recent mental health problems from those reporting any recent psychological distress. This measure is used descriptively to capture the presence or absence of recent mental health problems, including the application of a threshold of one or more days [21].

In the present study, we focused on whether employed caregivers experienced any mental health problems during the past 30 days. Caregiving-related psychological strain may lead to short-term functional impairment, including work absences and reduced work productivity, even when symptoms are transient [22–24]. From the perspective of labor force participation and work functioning, identifying whether mental health problems occurred at least once during this recent period is highly relevant.

In our study, the distribution of mentally unhealthy days was markedly concentrated at 0 days (Additional file 1), suggesting that the assumptions of linear regression were unlikely to be fully satisfied. In addition, our primary interest was whether employed caregivers had experienced any recent mental health problems that could affect work functioning. Therefore, poor mental health was operationalized as a binary outcome by dichotomizing the number of mentally unhealthy days during the past 30 days as 0 days versus ≥ 1 day. To examine the robustness of this operationalization, we also conducted sensitivity analyses using alternative cut-offs (≥ 3 days and ≥ 5 days), as well as a supplementary analysis in which the outcome was modeled as a continuous variable.

Explanatory variable

The explanatory variable was the caregiving status. Childcare was identified using the question, “During the past month, on average, how many hours per day did you spend on childcare?” Respondents who reported working more than zero hours per day were classified as providing childcare.

“Do you currently provide care or assistance in daily activities (e.g., bathing, shopping, cooking) for a family member with illness or disability?” was asked to assess older adult care. The binary responses of providing care or not providing care were recorded. Respondents who reported that they provided care were classified as those who provided older adult care. Although the age of the care recipient was not explicitly specified in this item, the term “caregiving” generally refers to care provided to older family members in the Japanese context. Nevertheless, this measure may have also included a subset of respondents who provided care to younger family members with illness or disability.

Dual caregiving was defined as meeting the criteria for both childcare and older adult care. Based on these responses, the caregiving status was categorized into four mutually exclusive groups: no caregiving, childcare only, older adult care only, and dual caregiving.

Teleworking refers to working from home. The telework frequency of the respondents was measured using a survey question. Based on the reported usual weekly work patterns, teleworking was categorized as follows: no teleworking; 1 day per week (including respondents who reported teleworking 2–3 times per month or once per week); 3 days per week (including those who reported teleworking 2–3 times per week); and daily teleworking (including those who reported teleworking 4–5 times or 6–7 times per week).

Covariates

The covariates included demographic, socioeconomic, health-related, psychosocial, and support factors. In the demographic-adjusted model (Model 2), we adjusted for age, sex (male/female), household size, marital status (married vs. unmarried), educational attainment (university graduate vs. less), household income (categorized based on survey cutoff points), smoking habits (current smoker vs. not), alcohol consumption habits (yes/no), and morbidity status. Morbidity was coded as present if the respondents reported any of the following chronic conditions: diabetes, pneumonia, bronchitis, asthma, atopic dermatitis, angina pectoris or myocardial infarction, stroke (including cerebral infarction or cerebral hemorrhage), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic hepatitis or liver cirrhosis, cancer or malignant tumors, epilepsy, depression, or mental disorders other than depression. All psychosocial items were rated on four-point Likert scales. To aid interpretation, quantitative job demands were coded such that higher values reflected lower perceived demands; job control was coded such that higher values reflected lower control; and supervisor, coworkers, and family/friend support were coded such that higher values reflected greater perceived support.

In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), we included work-related and social support variables based on the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) theory [25]. Quantitative job demands were assessed using items measuring the extent to which respondents felt they had to handle a tremendous amount of work, could not complete their work within the allotted time, and needed to work very hard (Cronbach’s α = 0.8126). Job control was evaluated using items assessing whether respondents could work at their own pace, determine the order and methods of their tasks, and express their opinions regarding workplace policies (α = 0.7902).

Supervisor support was assessed based on how easily respondents felt they could talk with their supervisor, how much they could rely on them when facing difficulties, and the extent to which their supervisor listened when they discussed personal concerns (α = 0.8499). Coworker support was measured in the same manner, asking about ease of communication, perceived reliability during difficulties, and willingness to listen among workplace colleagues (α = 0.8380). Family and friend support was assessed based on how easily respondents could talk with their spouse, family members, or friends. How much they could rely on them when facing difficulties, and the extent to which they felt heard when discussing personal concerns (α = 0.8657). All constructs were measured using multi-item scales capturing psychosocial burden and available supportive resources.

Statistical analysis

First, we conducted descriptive analyses for all variables to examine the distribution of participant characteristics and scale scores across caregiving status groups (no caregiving, childcare-only, older adult care-only, and dual caregiving).

We estimated the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for poor mental health using logistic regression analysis. Teleworking may alter the association between caregiving and mental health; hence, the analyses were stratified by teleworking frequency (no teleworking, 1 day/week, 3 days/week, daily). Within each stratum, the caregiving groups (childcare only, older adult care only, and dual caregiving) were compared with the reference group of participants with no caregiving responsibilities.

Three models were used in this study. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors, including age, sex, household size, marital status, educational attainment, household income, smoking habit, alcohol drinking habit, and the presence of ≥ 1 chronic condition. Model 3 was adjusted for work-related and social support variables based on the JD–R theory: quantitative job demands, job control, supervisor support, coworker support, and family/friend support. Multicollinearity among explanatory variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). The VIF values ranged from 1.02 to 2.45, indicating no evidence of serious multicollinearity.

To examine potential dose-response trends across teleworking frequency, we conducted tests treating teleworking frequency as an ordinal variable. We also conducted sex-stratified analyses (male and female) using the fully adjusted logistic regression model to assess whether the observed associations were consistent across sex groups. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the fully adjusted logistic regression analyses using alternative definitions of poor mental health (≥ 3 days and ≥ 5 days in the past 30 days) to examine whether the direction of the associations was robust to different cutoff points. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided α = 0.05. All analyses were performed using JMP, Version 18.2.1 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). This cross-sectional study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and all relevant national regulations and institutional guidelines. The dataset was de-identified before the analysis. Ethical approval was obtained from the Shimonoseki City University Research Ethics Committee (No. 2513–0806) and Tohoku University Graduate School of Medicine Ethics Committee (rapid review dated March 26, 2025; protocol 2024-1-1035). All participants were provided with an information sheet describing the study procedures, potential risks, data handling, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Participants who agreed to take part in the study provided electronic informed consent by checking the consent box prior to participation. No personally identifiable information was included in the analytical dataset. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Results

Basic characteristics by caregiving status

In total, 12,692 participants were included in the study. Among them, 8,933 (70.3%) reported no caregiving responsibilities, 3,108 (24.4%) provided childcare only, 468 (3.6%) provided care for older adults only, and 183 (1.4%) provided dual caregiving. The mean age was 39.8 (Standard Deviation [SD] = 11.5), 39.8 (SD = 7.4), 44.3 (SD = 12.1), and 40.3 years (SD = 9.3) in the no caregiving, childcare only, older adult caregiving, and dual caregiving groups, respectively (p < 0.001). Regarding the outcome, 9,003 participants (70.9%) reported 0 days of poor mental health, whereas 3,689 participants (29.1%) reported at least one day of poor mental health during the past 30 days.

The proportion of men was 62.7% (n = 5,602), 61.6% (n = 1,916), 57.9% (n = 271), and 70.0% (n = 128) in the no caregiving, childcare only, older adult caregiving, and dual-caregiving groups, respectively (p < 0.05). The proportions of married participants were 35.3% (n = 3,157), 94.9% (n = 2,950), 34.2% (n = 160), and 89.6% (n = 164), respectively (p < 0.001). Mean household size was 2.1 (SD = 1.2), 3.8 (SD = 1.9), 2.4 (SD = 1.2), and 4.0 (SD = 2.44), respectively (p < 0.001).

Regarding educational attainment, 21.4% (n = 1,914), 14.5% (n = 451), 27.6% (n = 129), and 13.1% (n = 24) had completed high school or less and 60.3% (n = 5,383), 67.3% (n = 2,091), 48.5% (n = 227), and 70.5% (n = 129) had university degrees or higher (p < 0.001), in the respective groups.

Household income also differed across the groups (p < 0.001). For example, annual income ≥ 12 million JPY was reported by 28.6% (n = 2,550), 24.8% (n = 771), 29.1% (n = 136), and 22.9% (n = 42) in the no caregiving, childcare only, older adult caregiving, and dual caregiving groups, respectively.

Current smoking was reported by 20.9% (n = 1,886), 17.5% (n = 543), 32.3% (n = 151), and 36.6% (n = 67) of the participants, respectively (p < 0.001). Current alcohol consumption was observed in 53.5% (n = 4,778), 56.4% (n = 1,753), 61.8% (n = 289), and 73.8% (n = 135) of the participants, respectively (p < 0.001). One or more chronic conditions were found in 48.2% (n = 4,309), 48.1% (n = 1,495), 68.4% (n = 320), and 71.0% (n = 130) of the participants, respectively (p < 0.001).

The mean scores for the work-related and social support factors in the full sample were as follows: quantitative job demands (mean = 2.38, SD = 0.78), job control (mean = 2.39, SD = 0.74), supervisor support (mean = 2.36, SD = 0.75), coworker support (mean = 2.43, SD = 0.74), and family or friend support (mean = 2.79, SD = 0.80). These scores varied significantly across the caregiving groups (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

In Model 1 (Crude model), compared with participants who reported no caregiving responsibilities, the odds of poor mental health were lower among those providing childcare only (OR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.70–0.84, p < 0.001). In contrast, higher odds were observed among those providing care for older adults only (OR = 1.93, 95% CI: 1.60–2.33, p < 0.001) and those providing dual caregiving (OR = 2.70, 95% CI: 2.02–3.63, p < 0.001).

In Model 2 (adjusted for demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors), the association with childcare was attenuated and not statistically significant (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.79–1.01, p = 0.066). The odds of poor mental health remained higher among those providing care for older adults only (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.45–2.15, p < 0.001) and dual caregiving (OR = 2.70, 95% CI: 1.97–3.69, p < 0.001).

In Model 3 (Fully adjusted model including work-related and social support factors), the odds of poor mental health were modestly reduced; however, they remained elevated among those providing care for older adults only (OR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.43–2.16, p < 0.001) and those providing dual caregiving (OR = 2.62, 95% CI: 1.89–3.62, p < 0.001). Childcare only was associated with a small, and statistically significant decrease in odds of poor mental health (OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.77–0.99, p < 0.05) relative to no caregiving (Table 2).

The fully adjusted model (Model 3) demonstrated adequate model fit (likelihood ratio χ² = 1665.64, df = 19, p < 0.0001; pseudo R² = 0.1089; AICc = 13,674.3; BIC = 13,823.2).

Discussion

The results of this study suggest that caregiving for older adults and dual caregiving may be associated with a higher likelihood of experiencing at least one mentally unhealthy day within the past 30 days. In contrast, the association was weaker among individuals providing childcare alone. In the stratified analyses by teleworking frequency, the association was strongest among dual caregivers who reported teleworking one day/week. In contrast, the estimates were less precise at three days and were not statistically significant for daily teleworking. These associations persisted even after adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors. However, after further adjusting for work-related factors, including quantitative job demands, job control, and support from supervisors, coworkers, and family or friends, the associations were attenuated and no longer statistically significant in some cases. This pattern suggests that a broader work environment may influence how caregiving responsibilities relate to mental health and that caregiving roles alone may not fully account for the observed differences.

In particular, dual caregiving is described as involving cumulative time pressure, emotional labor, and complex scheduling demands, given that childcare and older adult care place different, often competing responsibilities on caregivers [26–28]. These findings are consistent with prior research, indicating that dual caregivers may face a more substantial psychological burden than those responsible for a single caregiving role, especially when caring for older adults, which tends to entail greater unpredictability and emotional strain [29, 30]. In the sex-stratified analysis, older adult care and dual caregiving were associated with higher odds of poor mental health in both men and women, suggesting that the mental health burden related to caregiving is not limited to one sex. In Japan, women have traditionally assumed a greater share of unpaid family caregiving; however, our findings indicate that employed men may also experience substantial psychological strain when caring for older adults or managing dual caregiving responsibilities [31, 32]. These findings suggest that, although gendered roles in family care may still be relevant, caregiving-related mental health should be considered an issue affecting both male and female workers, highlighting the importance of examining caregiving circumstances across sexes.

Stratified analysis by teleworking frequency further revealed that the association between caregiving and poor mental health was not uniform across the teleworking frequency groups. Notably, among individuals who telework approximately 1 day/week, dual caregiving was associated with the highest odds of poor mental health. One possible interpretation is that partial or limited teleworking may not provide sufficient flexibility to coordinate work and caregiving demands meaningfully and may instead blur boundaries, heightening the sense of simultaneous responsibilities [33–35]. In contrast, more frequent telework may allow for greater schedule adjustment or reduced commuting time. However, the extent to which such arrangements are beneficial likely varies depending on job characteristics, workplace norms, and support availability [36]. These observations suggest that the effects of teleworking on caregiver well-being are not uniform and may depend on how teleworking is structured and supported.

Overall, these findings suggest that promoting teleworking is an important component of supporting employed caregivers; however, teleworking alone may be insufficient. More effective support may come from approaches that combine flexible work arrangements with workload adjustments, opportunities for greater autonomy in task management, and access to supportive communication with supervisors and colleagues. Such workplace environments may be significant for individuals managing dual caregiving responsibilities, whose day-to-day schedules may leave a limited capacity to accommodate additional strain.

In the context of age-friendly environments and Sustainable Development Goal 11, our findings suggest that teleworking is most beneficial when embedded in supportive employment ecosystems that combine flexible work with realistic workload targets, greater worker autonomy, responsive supervision, and access to community-based care services. In rapidly aging societies, such as Japan, aligning workplace policies with the local care infrastructure may help sustain caregivers’ employment and mental health, thereby supporting aging in place.

In Japan, the Childcare and Family Care Leave Act provides a legal framework that supports workers in balancing their employment and caregiving responsibilities. Recently, the scope of this legislation has expanded. Notably, the 2025 revision of the Act strengthened employers’ obligations to offer more flexible work arrangements and implement workplace-based support measures to prevent caregiving-related job loss [37, 38]. These policy developments indicate a growing national commitment to support work-care reconciliation. However, the effectiveness of such policies likely varies substantially across workplace cultures, organizational resources, employment status, workers’ awareness, and perceived accessibility of available support.

Furthermore, for individuals engaged in dual caregiving, who usually face particularly intensive and overlapping care demands, the benefits of these policies are yet to be fully realized. Therefore, future research should evaluate how the 2025 policy reforms may influence caregiving workers’ mental health and employment stability across diverse occupational contexts. Such evaluations will be crucial for identifying conditions under which flexible work arrangements and workplace support strategies can function most effectively, and providing insights for the development of more tailored measures to address the needs of those carrying multiple caregiving roles.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not establish temporal ordering among caregiving roles, teleworking practices, and mental health. Second, the survey was conducted using a required-response format, meaning that participants were required to answer all items before proceeding. Although this design minimized missing data, it may have introduced response bias, particularly for sensitive variables such as mental health, if participants provided less accurate responses in order to complete the survey. Third, caregiving was measured based on the self-reported presence of childcare and/or older adult care responsibilities and did not consider caregiving intensity, duration, task complexity, or the care recipient’s level of dependency and need for continuous support, all of which may vary substantially and influence psychological burden. In particular, the duration of caregiving could not be assessed in this dataset, although psychological strain may emerge or accumulate after prolonged periods of caregiving and may not be evident in the initial stages. Additionally, although the caregiving item did not explicitly specify the age of the care recipient, the term “caregiving” generally refers to care provided to older family members in the Japanese context. However, it is possible that a subset of respondents were providing care for younger family members with illness or disability. Such individuals may require medical or intensive care support even at younger ages, and the present dataset did not allow us to distinguish these situations. As a result, some degree of exposure misclassification may have occurred. If younger care recipients with substantial care needs were included in the caregiving category, the observed association between caregiving and poor mental health may have been overestimated. Fourth, teleworking frequency was measured only weekly; qualitative aspects, such as task autonomy while teleworking, expectations for availability, digital resources at home, and workplace norms, were not assessed and could modify associations. Fifth, poor mental health was measured using a single CDC HRQOL item, which may not fully reflect the multidimensional aspects of psychological well-being. In addition, this measure captures the number of mentally unhealthy days rather than clinically diagnosed mental disorders and therefore should be interpreted as an indicator of recent psychological distress rather than a diagnostic measure. Sixth, the sample is drawn from an online panel using quota sampling. The analyses were adjusted for key sociodemographic factors, sampling weights were not applied, and selection bias was possible; therefore, generalizability to populations with limited digital access or non-standard employment arrangements may be limited. Seventh, reduced precision in some teleworking strata, particularly among dual caregivers teleworking 3 days/week and among daily teleworkers, may lead to wide CI. Finally, residual confounding by unmeasured workplace factors (e.g., schedule predictability, availability of formal care leave, or organizational culture) cannot be ruled out. Longitudinal studies with richer measures of caregiving demands and workplace contexts are needed to clarify the mechanisms and inform policies.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study suggests that caregiving for older adults and dual caregiving may be associated with greater odds of poor mental health among employed Japanese adults. In contrast, the association may be weaker when childcare is the only factor. The magnitude of these associations differed according to teleworking frequency, indicating that teleworking may not uniformly mitigate caregiving-related stress. The attenuation of these associations after adjusting for work-related factors suggests that the mental health impact of caregiving is influenced by the ability to work remotely, as well as workplace conditions, such as workload, job control, and support from supervisors and colleagues. These findings imply that teleworking is more supportive when implemented in conducive workplace environments. Policies integrating flexible work arrangements with improvements in workplace support may benefit workers with dual caregiving responsibilities. Future studies that further examine caregiving-related mental health from multiple perspectives, including the quality and duration of caregiving, availability and use of workplace support programs, and gender roles in care provision, are warranted.
