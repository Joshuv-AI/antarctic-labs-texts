The impact of fly ash and slag on the microscopic interface of recycled concrete and its destruction evolution
Authors: Chen Chen, Zi’er Wei, Jingming Zhang, Zheng Chen, Fujun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe interfacial transition zones in recycled concrete are the main factors affecting their durability and strength. The interfacial transition zones (ITZS) in recycled concrete are the main factors affecting durability and strength, yet their response to combined use of fly ash and slag remains insufficiently understood. This study investigates the influence of different replacement levels of recycled aggregate, fly ash, and slag on ITZS properties through both microscopic and macroscopic testing, combined with numerical simulation of the failure process. The results show that the minimal ITZS effect occurred when the recycled aggregate content was 40% and slag content was 10%, under which the compressive strength reached 32.8 MPa and the splitting tensile strength reached 3.21 MPa, representing reductions of only 4.5% and 5.2% compared with ordinary concrete, respectively. Microscopic observations revealed that cracks initiate predominantly in porous ITZS regions and propagate inward, a process well captured by the cohesive zone model simulations. These findings indicate that a balanced combination of recycled aggregate, fly ash, and slag can achieve mechanical performance comparable to conventional concrete, offering a viable approach to promote sustainable utilization of industrial by-products in structural applications. Specifically, under these conditions, the compressive strength reached 32.8 MPa and the splitting tensile strength reached 3.21 MPa, representing reductions of only 4.5% and 5.2% compared with ordinary concrete, respectively. These results indicate that partial substitution of recycled aggregate combined with moderate slag content can produce concrete with mechanical performance comparable to that of conventional concrete, while promoting sustainable use of industrial by-products in structural applications. In addition, the failure of recycled concrete was demonstrated based on microscopic results, from which the failure of recycled concrete was found to be a gradual failure from the outside to the inside. The failure zone first appeared in the interfacial transition zones with large porosities and large area, where internal cracks were generated and gradually spread. Introduction

Cement concrete materials are the largest carbon emitters in the building materials industry, and their life cycle carbon emissions are enormous1. With the acceleration of urbanization, demolition of old buildings has produced a large amount of construction solid waste2. If not properly disposed, it will cause environmental damage and waste resources. Steel mills discharge large amounts of industrial waste, such as fly ash and slag, every year, which occupies land and is a major source of environmental3–5. Concrete can “eat waste and swallow slag,” improve the resource utilization of large-scale solid waste, and carry out green and low-carbon design of concrete materials. It is of tremendous research significance Reducing the carbon footprint of concrete and achieving the goal of “double carbon6”.

The preparation of recycled coarse aggregate ( RCA ) from construction waste by crushing, screening, sorting, and other processes and its promotion and application in concrete materials have become a more mature and effective means of saving resources and improving the environment6. However, owing to the layer of old mortar attached to the surface of RCA, some aspects can be improved compared with natural aggregate porosity and water absorption7. With an increase in the RCA replacement rate, the strength of recycled concrete becomes significantly lower than that of ordinary concrete, and multiple interface transition zones (ITZS) become more extensive, which restricts the application and development of high-volume recycled coarse aggregate concrete.

Pani et al.8 found that the performance of recycled concrete made from recycled coarse aggregate (80%-100%) and natural fine aggregate was lower than that of ordinary concrete. In comparison, the performance of recycled concrete made from 50% to 20% recycled coarse aggregates was similar to that of ordinary concrete. Ekka et al. replaced natural aggregate (NCA) with 30%, 50%, and 100% recycled coarse aggregate. The w/c ratio is 0.43. The strength of fly ash concrete from 30% recycled aggregate concrete can be increased by 6.23%. Abdelkader et al. conducted a mix-ratio test of fly ash and recycled concrete. After ten days of curing, recycled aggregate concrete containing 20% FAA and 90% RCA could replace cement to achieve better compressive, splitting tensile, and bending strengths. However, the 40% fly ash content affecteds the performance of recycled aggregate concrete. Asahina et al.9 evaluated the research requirements for modeling critical pre-crack propagation under uniaxial stress conditions and successfully predicted the interfacial debonding performance and fracture behavior of recycled concrete under uniaxial tension. Li et al. proposed that microcracks usually first appear around weak old and new ITZ and then expand into old and new cement mortar, and as the relative strength of new and old cement mortar increases, the location of microcrack initiation gradually shifts from the new ITZ to the old ITZ. Ma et al.10 studied the bending failure process of thoroughly graded concrete using a random sequential stacking scheme and a random aggregate model of polygonal and polyhedral particles. Bai et al.11 found that the performance difference between recycled aggregate (RA) and natural aggregate (NA) was due to the old mortar attached to RA. It was found that peeling off old mortar can improve the performance of recycled aggregate, but this is not the only way to promote its application of recycled aggregate. Controlling the water-cement ratio, adjusting the water content of the aggregate gate, and using different mixing methods can improve the performance of recycled aggregates. Recent studies have further highlighted the potential of using industrial by-products and recycled aggregates in structural concrete12,13. Revilla-Cuesta et al.13 demonstrated that ladle furnace slag can be effectively incorporated in concrete mixtures, influencing both compressive strength and modulus of elasticity. Chen et al.14 investigated the particle characteristics of supplementary cementitious materials, which directly affect workability and hydration. Santamaría et al.15 and Fiol et al.16 validated that high proportions of recycled aggregates, when combined with slag or other by-products, can produce sustainable structural concrete meeting building code requirements. Yin et al.17 explored surface treatments to improve durability, while Yan et al.18 discussed the environmental implications of waste reuse in cementitious systems. Nukah et al.19 provided a systematic meta-analysis on cement replacement materials, emphasizing slag’s role in sustainable design. Collectively, these works confirm the versatility of slag and recycled aggregates, and their suitability for both mechanical performance and environmental objectives in modern concrete technology.

Beyond the incorporation of fly ash and slag, various surface treatments, nano-modifications, and binder system designs have been adopted to optimize the interfacial transition zone (ITZS) and matrix densification. Siliceous fly ash remains the most widely used waste material in cement composites, often as part of ternary or quaternary binder systems, where its synergistic interaction with other pozzolans or nano-additives significantly influences fracture toughness and crack resistance. For example, recent studies have demonstrated that fly ash combined with nano-SiO2 can improve crack-bridging capacity and alter crack propagation patterns under mode I fracture loading20–22. Modern developments in alkali-activated and thermally activated binders have also shown potential for improving both the strength and fracture properties of recycled concretes. Alkali-activated thermally activated recycled cement, particularly when modified with nano-silica, has been reported to enhance hydration kinetics and microstructural compactness, thereby improving mechanical performance and reducing crack susceptibility23,24. Furthermore, studies using non-contact measurement techniques such as digital image correlation have provided deeper insights into crack initiation and propagation in such modified systems25. Incorporating these findings into the current research framework helps position our work within the broader context of SCM utilization, highlighting both the potential and the challenges associated with designing sustainable, high-performance concrete mixtures.

The research project is designed to probe into the effects of fly ash and slag on ITZS of recycled concrete. With the application of macroscopic and microcosmic experiment methods, efforts were made to ascertain the minimum effects that differentiated mixing amount of fly ash-recycled aggregate and slag-recycled aggregate had on ITZS. Besides, cohesive force simulation model was established based on characteristics of ITZS to gain insight into the failure mechanism of recycled concrete.

Methods

All experimental work was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, following the requirements of relevant Chinese standards (GB/T 50081 − 2019 and GB/T 50080 − 2016). The mixing process involved first blending the dry materials, including cement, fly ash, slag, and aggregates, in a pan mixer for 2 min, followed by the gradual addition of water and a polycarboxylate superplasticizer, and continued mixing for 3 min to ensure homogeneity. Fresh concrete was cast into steel molds in two layers, each compacted on a vibrating table to remove entrapped air. After 24 h, the specimens were demolded and transferred to a standard curing room maintained at 20 ± 2 °C and relative humidity above 95% until the designated test age. Compressive strength tests were carried out on 100 × 100 × 100 mm cubic specimens at 28 days using a hydraulic testing machine with a loading rate of 0.5 MPa/s, while splitting tensile strength tests were performed on cylindrical specimens (100 mm diameter × 200 mm height) in accordance with the same standard. For microstructural characterization, thin slices were prepared and polished for optical microscopy to observe the interfacial transition zones (ITZS), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed using a field-emission instrument operated at 15 kV to identify hydration products and assess ITZS morphology. In addition, a cohesive zone model (CZM) was implemented in ABAQUS to simulate the crack initiation and propagation within multiple ITZS structures, with input parameters calibrated from the experimental results.

Microscopic results and analysis

Slice test

Compared to ordinary concrete, recycled concrete has a more complex aggregate and mortar interface structure. During the processing of recycled coarse aggregate, some old mortar peels off the surface of the aggregate, and the aggregate without mortar adhesion is similar to that of natural aggregate33.

Therefore, the interface area between recycled and ordinary concrete was selected for microscopic morphology observation34–39.

Figure 5 shows the microscopic slice diagram of concrete. Fig. 5a, c show the slice effect diagram of recycled concrete and ordinary concrete, respectively, and Fig. 5b, d show the corresponding pore structure diagrams. The blue area in the Figure represents the pores. A comparative analysis of Fig. 5a, c shows that the surface of the recycled aggregate was higher than that of the natural aggregate, and the interior of the natural aggregate was denser. By comparing and analyzing Fig. 5b, d, it can be concluded that the porosity of the transition zone between the recycled aggregate and new mortar is higher. Compared with the contact surface in Fig. 5d, it can be seen that the porosity of the interface transition zone of recycled aggregate is high. The closer it is to the aggregate boundary, the denser the blue area of the cementation area. Owing to the high porosity of the interfacial transition zone inside the recycled concrete, the large pores in the concrete are the reason for the significant reduction in mechanical properties and durability. Therefore, it can be preliminarily determined that the interfacial transition zone between recycled aggregate and new mortar is where recycled concrete is damaged40.

SEM test

To further explore the changes in the internal interface area of recycled concrete, the effects of fly ash and slag on the interfacial transition zone and hydration degree of recycled concrete were investigated using SEM scanning electron microscopy5,41–44.

Figure 6a–f show the experimental groups B8, A4, and A1, respectively. From the observations of the microstructures of recycled concrete and ordinary concrete, it can be observed that there are a large number of hydration products and a small amount of acicular calcium vanadate (AFt) in the concrete, and the hydration products are mainly flocculent (C-S-H gel) and calcium hydroxide (C-H). Figure 6a shows a recycled concrete test block comprising 80% recycled aggregate and 20% slag powder. A crack can be observed in the microscopic morphology diagram, which may be due to mechanical crushing of the test block. After increasing the magnification, as shown in Fig. 6b, the ITZ between the recycled aggregate and the new mortar is visible in the diagram40. The incomplete hydration of cement near the ITZ is accompanied by a small amount of AFt, with high porosity, poor compactness, and loose structure, which is unfavorable for the strength and durability of recycled concrete32,45.

Micromorphology of recycled aggregate and natural aggregate concrete.

Figure 6c shows the recycled concrete test block under the conditions of full replacement of recycled aggregate and 20% fly ash content. Its microscopic morphology shows poor spatial structure and high porosity, and the degree of hydration is poor with AFt generation. The magnification effect diagram is shown in Fig. 2d, which clearly marks the ITZ area at the outline of the recycled aggregate, and clearly shows the loose spatial structure of the interfacial transition zone and some unreacted fly ash particles. The high porosity of the interfacial transition zone is unfavorable for its strength and durability35.

Figure 6e, f show ordinary concrete configured with natural aggregates. Ordinary concrete has a denser microstructure, smaller pores, and richer hydration products than those of recycled concrete. Based on the above conclusions, it is concluded that both fly ash and slag cause incomplete hydration of cement, resulting in a larger internal pore structure in recycled concrete ITZS46. Owing to RCA’s complex and diverse internal structure of RCA, the ITZS cement hydration must be improved, and it is difficult for cement particles to fill the space on the aggregate surface30.

Simulation and destruction

ITZS model establishment

Ignoring the constitutive relationship of the ITZS, ITZS can be simplified as a simple interface failure without considering the complexity and diversity of the problem. Therefore, a cohesive zone material ( CZM ) have been proposed to study the failure mechanism of multiple interface structures47,48,42.

Figure 7 shows the ITZS structural model of recycled concrete. As shown in Fig. 7, when the recycled aggregate was completely replaced, there were three interface forms: the old aggregate-old mortar interface, old aggregate-new mortar interface, and old mortar-new mortar interface.

The ITZS of recycled concrete was simulated, and a random polygonal aggregate was generated by coding and assembling it into 100 mm × 100 mm × 100 mm recycled concrete slices. The composite material accounted for 60% of the total volume fraction. The maximum particle size of the aggregate was less than 20 mm and the minimum particle size of the recycled aggregate was 5 mm. The aggregate model was covered with a 2 mm old mortar layer, which was consistent with the experimental conditions. Finally, the CZM of the interface damage factor was introduced and combined with the basic theory of fracture mechanics. The CZM is a simplified model of the31 interaction force between molecules and atoms36,37. The crack tip was assumed to be a small cohesive zone composed of two crack interfaces, and the relationship between the traction force T and the relative displacement U between the two crack surfaces was used to achieve fracture49.

Destruction analysis

A longitudinal unidirectional coupling load was applied to the upper part of the member, and the lower part was fixed on the test platform. According to the set cohesive element failure criterion and damage evolution standard, failure occurs when the internal unit of the specimen reaches the failure fracture energy, that is, failure occurs when the energy required for fracture is reached between the units. Finally, the crack propagation process and the final fracture morphology of the recycled concrete were observed.

Figure 9a shows the failure evolution diagram of the cohesive element recycled concrete. After observing the failure trend, the failure cracks could be divided into two types: through cracks and internal cracks. The failure mechanism of recycled concrete is gradual failure from the outside to the inside. Failure first occurred in the ITZS, which had large outer pores and small areas. With an increase in loading time, each failure zone gradually connected to produce cracks, and the failure mainly occurred in the ITZS area. In summary, the reason is that the porosity in the interface transition zone is high, resulting in poor adhesion between the new mortar and recycled aggregate. Part of the interface transition zone with high porosity is destroyed, and internal cracks are generated until the component was completely is destroyed48,49,43.

Uniaxial compression failure diagram of fully replaced recycled aggregate.

Figure 9b shows the actual compressive failure diagram of the recycled concrete. The simulated failure area of recycled concrete tendeds to be consistent with the actual failure area. Failure occurred in the interface transition zone. Failure first occurred in the outer interface transition zone, and internal cracks occurred in the inner part. When multiple damaged interface transition zones were connected, penetrating cracks were generated. Both simulated and actual failures were interface transition zone failures. In Fig. 9, clear differences in crack morphology can be observed between the different material configurations. For specimens incorporating 100% recycled aggregate with high fly ash content, cracks were generally wider (average width 0.35–0.50 mm) and more irregular in path, often following the highly porous ITZS and branching into the adjacent new mortar. In contrast, specimens containing moderate recycled aggregate content (40%) and slag showed narrower cracks (average width 0.15–0.25 mm) with straighter propagation paths, suggesting a denser and more uniform ITZS structure. The morphology of cracks in slag-containing mixes also exhibited fewer secondary branches and less surface spalling compared with those in fly ash-rich mixes, indicating a relatively higher resistance to crack bifurcation. These observations are consistent with the microstructural analysis in Sect. 3.3, which showed that higher ITZS porosity and incomplete hydration in fly ash-rich mixes promote easier crack initiation and more tortuous crack propagation. It can be seen that the interface transition zone is the main reason for the failure and strength reduction of recycled concrete38. These observations are consistent with previous findings that the ITZS is often the critical initiation site for cracking in recycled concrete, due to its higher porosity and weaker bond strength compared to the bulk matrix. Bai et al.11 and Li et al.50 demonstrated through meso-scale modeling and experimental testing that cracks tend to nucleate in the ITZS and propagate inward under uniaxial loading, which aligns with the gradual outside-to-inside failure pattern observed in our simulations and experiments. Tang et al.39 and Zhang et al.51 further reported that cohesive zone models can accurately capture this localized damage evolution in multi-interface systems, confirming the suitability of our simulation approach. These studies collectively support the conclusion that ITZS properties govern the fracture behavior and ultimate failure mode of recycled aggregate concrete.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the effects of fly ash and slag on the interfacial transition zones (ITZS) and failure mechanisms of recycled concrete through a combination of experimental testing and numerical simulation. The main findings are summarized as follows:

(1) The strength of recycled concrete varies non-linearly with recycled aggregate content, showing an initial increase, followed by a decrease, a rise, and finally a decline. The ITZS effect is minimal at 20% and 60% recycled aggregate. With fly ash, the least ITZS influence occurs at 100% recycled aggregate and 20% fly ash, while slag minimizes ITZS impact at 40% recycled aggregate and 10% slag.

(2) Fly ash and slag reduce cement hydration, decreasing C-S-H gel formation and increasing ITZS porosity, with fly ash having a greater effect than slag. High ITZS porosity leads to stress concentrations, accelerating damage.

(3) Cohesive element recycled concrete failure starts in the ITZ due to high porosity, initiating cracks. These propagate under load, connecting failure zones and forming penetrating cracks, ultimately leading to structural failure.

Overall, the results confirm that controlling the replacement levels of recycled aggregate, fly ash, and slag can significantly influence ITZS characteristics and, consequently, the mechanical performance of recycled concrete. The proposed mix combinations demonstrate potential for producing sustainable concrete with acceptable strength and durability, which can be applied in non-prestressed structural elements, pavements, and other secondary structural components. Nevertheless, this study was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions with a fixed water–binder ratio and limited mechanical performance indicators. Environmental exposure effects, long-term durability, and large-scale structural behavior were not evaluated. Future research should investigate these aspects, explore a wider range of replacement levels, and consider additional durability parameters to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the material’s long-term performance in real-world applications52–61.
