Approaching Serious Illness Conversations in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Using Telehealth
Authors: Erica Scirocco, Sabrina Paganoni, Kate Brizzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose of ReviewGiven the lack of consensus on serious illness conversations (SIC) in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) by using telehealth, we aim to provide practical strategies in this setting.Recent FindingsOver the past 5 years, there has been substantial growth in telehealth, especially after the global COVID-19 pandemic. In ALS, telehealth has become an increasingly used tool for providing clinical care, especially as the disease progresses, when travel becomes challenging and geographic constraints arise. As ALS advances, clinicians often have SIC with individuals living with ALS and their caregivers using telehealth. In the literature, few recommendations are available to improve telehealth communication in the neuropalliative setting.SummaryWe present 3 case scenarios showcasing telehealth strategies for SICs, with specific considerations for individuals living with ALS. We provide a strategy, CARE in ALS, to support telehealth communication in individuals with speech impairments. We hope to provide practical guidance for health care clinicians in this specific setting.

Multidimensional Challenges in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a neurodegenerative disease marked by progressive motor neuron degeneration, ultimately leading to death within 2–5 years.1 Individuals living with ALS experience progressive functional decline that can result in significant physical, emotional, social, spiritual, and existential distress.2

Why Palliative Care Matters

It is widely recognized that palliative care is a critical aspect of ALS care, delivered both by the ALS clinic team (referred as “primary palliative care”) and a palliative care specialist (“specialty palliative care”) as part of a continuum of care with complementary strengths.3,4 Effective palliative care relies on skillful communication, referred to as serious illness conversations (SIC), which guide patients and caregivers in realms such as symptom management (e.g., sialorrhea, pain, or spasticity), quality of life (QoL), psychological needs, and end-of-life care.4,5 These conversations are part of advance care planning to address expectations, goals of care, and preferences, including the desired place of death.6,7 The American Academy of Neurology recommends that SICs in neurologic disorders be initiated as early as possible when patients can participate in a meaningful way.8 However, SICs remain underused in both neurologic and non-neurologic contexts because of barriers such as: (1) clinicians' limited awareness of the terminal stages; (2) clinicians' insufficient skills in conducting these discussions; (3) clinicians and/or patients' hesitation to start the conversation; and (4) lack of time to initiate SICs.9-15

The Journey of Serious Illness Conversations in ALS

Studies have shown that individuals with ALS are willing to discuss advance directives16 particularly when these discussions are part of ongoing conversations with a short follow-up period.17,18 However, one of the main challenges in facilitating these conversations is the burden of travel.19

Growing Role of Telehealth

After the global COVID-19 pandemic,20-23 telehealth in ALS has grown substantially.24-27 Considering the varying degrees of mobility limitations and the potential geographic distance from specialized clinics,28,29 telehealth in ALS has emerged as an increasingly used tool that maintains a high quality multidisciplinary approach.30 As part of telehealth visits in ALS, palliative care concerns are frequently discussed.31-33

Benefits and Complexities of Telehealth in ALS Care

A recent scoping review of telehealth for home-based palliative care in different medical settings found improvements in care coordination, increased patient-clinician interactions, optimized time efficiency, and strengthened relationships, with the main barrier being the complexity of technology involved.34 Although both patients and clinicians recognize challenges in virtual care, such as the lack of in-person connection, there are also many advantages, particularly from the patient perspective. These advantages include: (1) the comfort of staying at home, that provides feelings of safety and eliminates the travel burden; (2) the opportunity to have more frequent telehealth consultations as the disease progresses, compared with the difficulty of attending in-person clinic visits; and (3) the participation of several family members, including those living far away.35 A telehealth survey of patients conducted at Mayo Clinic reported that telehealth visits using video were preferred twice as much as phone consultations for mental health, care planning, and education discussions.36 This suggests the benefit of seeing the clinician during virtual visits, creating a stronger sense of presence and connection during the visit. In addition, telehealth offers relatively rapid access to care.17,18,37 Many studies have explored telehealth use in neuromuscular diseases such as ALS.25-27,30,32,33,35,38,39 In ALS, main barriers to telehealth are identified in the following: (1) physician licensure and legal liability; (2) billing challenges; (3) need for mutual trust to establish a virtual interpersonal relationship; and (4) limited access to technology, particularly for the older individuals and in rural areas.

Practical Considerations to Guide ALS Serious Illness Conversations via Telehealth

There is no literature and scientific consensus on how to address SIC in ALS clinical practice. Here, we present 3 real-world cases from our clinical experience illustrating the use of telehealth for SICs in ALS, during which patient's understanding of the disease, values, and care priorities are explored. Although the cases are inspired by actual patient experiences, all identifying information has been modified to protect confidentiality. Each case highlights key strategies for successful telehealth SIC encounters and specific considerations for patients with ALS for both primary and specialty palliative care clinicians.

Three Case Scenarios for Serious Illness Conversations in ALS via Telehealth

Case 1: General Principals of Telehealth in Serious Illness Conversation

Mike is a 66-year-old retired engineer with limb onset ALS who presents for a telehealth palliative care visit. He was diagnosed 1.5 years ago, and his disease has been progressing over the past year. He is now wheelchair-dependent and uses noninvasive ventilation at night. His swallowing and speech are minimally affected.

Before engaging in a discussion with Mike through telehealth, ensure a professional environment. This includes checking your internet connection, testing both video and audio, positioning the webcam at eye level for optimal engagement, using an appropriate background, choosing a quiet space free from interruptions, and turning off your phone if possible. Confirm the technical setup with the patient by asking:“Can you hear and see me clearly?”“Let me provide you with the direct link to our appointment in case we get disconnected.”“Are you somewhere you feel comfortable talking about your health information? Is there anyone else you wanted to include on the visit today, perhaps a family member or a caregiver?”“Is there anything else you'd like to adjust before we begin?”“Please feel free to interrupt me at any time if you are experiencing any technical issue during the visit.”

Case 2: SIC Through Telehealth in the Setting of Communication Challenges

Margaret is a 56-year-old nurse with bulbar onset ALS who lives 2 hours away from an ALS multidisciplinary clinic. She has dysarthric speech which can be understood about 75% of the time. She types in the chat on the telehealth platform to clarify when needed. She has some mild swallowing challenges and she doesn’t think she wants a feeding tube in the future. She notes that conversations about the future feel scary and is not sure how much she wants to know.

Many patients with bulbar onset ALS will require alternative strategies for communication. In this example, Margaret uses the chat function in the telehealth platform to assist with communication and may require extra time to type her thoughts. Other tools that can be used include boogie boards, which can be held up to the screen, or type-to-speech applications on a cell phone or other augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) device. Understanding how patients with bulbar onset ALS disease communicate before initiating serious conversations is important to facilitate effective dialog.

Margaret is initially hesitant to engage in conversations about the future. For patients who are ambivalent about such discussions, helpful strategies have been outlined in the study of Back et al. to conduct SICs and guide patients toward informed medical decisions.46 These strategies include the following:Naming patient's ambivalence by explicitly acknowledging the patient's mixed feelings about receiving information. It shows the patient that their internal conflict, wanting to know but also fearing the information, is recognized and normal. This can help them feel understood and reduce anxiety about expressing uncertainty.Exploring the reasons behind reluctance by inviting the patient to explain why they might be hesitant to discuss their prognosis. It's a way to uncover fears or concerns and to give the patient control over the conversation. This approach opens the door for understanding the underlying emotions and reasoning behind their reluctance.Weighing the pros and cons of knowing prognosis by helping the patient consider both the potential benefits and downsides of receiving information. For example, the clinician might help the patient see, how knowing more about course, stage, and prognosis could help with planning or making decisions, while also acknowledging that it could be emotionally challenging. This balanced approach empowers the patient to make a choice that aligns with their needs and readiness.Recognizing the difficulty of these conversations by acknowledging that discussing prognosis is hard to normalize the patient's feelings, and privately assessing whether the patient needs to receive details about their clinical condition immediately, based on their current clinical condition. This ensures that the patient is not overwhelmed with information unless it is necessary for their understanding and decision-making process.Validating patient's concerns to foster a more open dialog, by acknowledging what is true or reasonable from the patient's perspective and by providing actionable steps, to build trust and reduce resistance.

At a follow-up visit, Margaret is having more difficulty with speaking. She is now typing to communicate.

As Margaret's disease progresses, she relies increasingly on assistive communication devices. Although the tools described above can continue to be useful, for patients who lose the ability to use their hands, additional assistance may be needed. In this case, having a caregiver present can be helpful, as a support in feeling more at ease in expressing needs. This is particularly critical for telehealth services and should be explored before the visit, “Would you feel more comfortable having a caregiver with you during the visit?” Although open ended questions are often encouraged in SICs, yes/no questions may be very appropriate in certain settings.

Margaret has had more swallowing challenges including some episodes of choking. She notes more openness to thinking about a feeding tube.

Margaret's feelings about a feeding tube changed over time. This is a common experience described in individuals with ALS. As ALS progresses, patients' care goals often shift. This process, known as “frame shift” or “response shift”, involves “reframing” and reprioritization, allowing focusing on what patients perceive as most important for their quality of life in their present.47 As physical function deteriorates in ALS, it has been shown that the patient focus shifts from physical abilities to factors like family, friends, spiritual support, and existential concerns, such as meaning and purpose in life. To support them during this shift in perspective, questions like “What matters most to you now?” can help focus the discussion on crucial aspects of maintaining their quality of life. If the patient opens the discussion, consider asking, “If time were short, what matters most to you?” This question can indirectly address the experience of death, which can facilitate a less distressing contemplation of mortality. The conversation should then center on aspects that are crucial for maintaining a good quality of life, supporting the natural mechanism of frame shift. Before concluding the discussion, it is important to validate the emotions involved, expressing gratitude for the patient's openness, by stating “I really appreciate you sharing all this important information with me.” Acknowledging the difficulty of these conversations helps foster a supportive environment, enhances the therapeutic alliance, and encourages continued engagement in future discussions.

Case 3: Using SIC in Telehealth for Goals of Care

Mary is a 75-year-old retired teacher with mixed limb and bulbar ALS presents for a telehealth visit. She lives alone in Boston and has caregivers that help her during the day. She has a son who lives an hour away and a daughter who lives in Texas. She states that she is not ready to complete advance directives but would like to learn more.

Mary lives alone and in-person visits are challenging. Her children struggle to attend appointments because of the distance. Telehealth offers a valuable solution by improving access to clinical care and enabling greater family involvement. As Mary expressed her desire to learn more about advance care directives during a telehealth visit, the clinician can begin exploring the topic, emphasizing that the discussion is always available whenever Mary feels ready. In ALS and in all serious illnesses, future planning is fundamental and often required by patients and their caregivers. During a screen share session, clinicians can either draw live using a virtual whiteboard or prepare figures in advance, highlighting key considerations along the ALS disease roadmap such as challenges in speech and swallowing, mobility, and breathing. As an example, visual aid can be integrated into the conversation to enhance understanding of the various domains of the ALS Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R) scale. A clinician might say, “Looking at your most recent ALSFRS-R score, we can see that some of your functions are starting to change. This figure shows the four key areas we assess with the ALSFRS-R scale (bulbar, fine motor, gross motor, and respiratory) and it helps illustrate which changes you might expect in the future.” Although comprehending the disease can help patients and their families in coping with its implications, it is equally important to provide emotional support throughout the process by stating, “Figures can serve as general guides, however, every person's journey with ALS is distinct. I want to ensure that I am here to support you through both the positive and challenging moments that lie ahead.” It is important during these sensitive conversations to explore the role of hope by asking questions such as “What brings hope to your life?” By first defining what hope means for the patient, clinicians can help empower individuals to focus on what is achievable in the present and what is already part of their lives. This approach can foster the mentioned frame shift coping process. For example, if a patient expresses joy about their grandchildren (a certainty in the present), hope might manifest as the desire to see them weekly or attend their graduation at the end of the month (a possible near-future expectation).

At a follow-up visit, because of hand weakness and bulbar symptoms, Mary has started using eye-gaze technology. At times, family members try to fill the space by talking for her. She now has a caregiver or a family member with her 24/7. Mary has had further progression of her disease and expresses her wishes to complete advance directives and learn about what hospice provides. She hopes to see her grandchildren graduate.

Mary's bulbar ALS requires her to use eye-gaze technology. Although family members can contribute positively to discussions, it is essential to ensure that Mary's wishes remain central. Clinicians can help make sure that Mary is able to speak for herself, following the CARE in ALS strategy (Figure). Clinicians should ask if Mary wishes to have their caregivers interacting and by addressing her directly with questions like, “Mary, does this sound reasonable to you?” Telehealth also allows multiple team members to contribute their expertise simultaneously enabling all participants to see each other's faces in real time, fostering personal and engaging communication. During the telehealth visit, the clinicians can address the caregivers' emotional wellbeing,48 because they often experience feelings of burden, hopelessness, and depression,49 that can profoundly affect both the patient and the entire family.50 Clinician may use statements to validate emotions such as, “It sounds like you've dealt with some difficult experiences,” to create a safe space for discussion. In cases where patients, like Mary, may not have family or caregivers nearby, it becomes even more essential for clinicians to adopt these strategies to ensure that the patient feels heard and supported. Clinicians can also explore potential alternative support systems, such as local community resources or virtual support groups, to enhance the patient's network and address their emotional needs.

Mary is now ready to discuss advance care directives, which can be discussed through telehealth using the ‘share screen’ modality. The screen can also be shared while explaining the disease progression with graphical images,51 or procedures like feeding tube placement, or again while presenting the Physician's Orders for Life Sustaining Treatment (POLST) form.52,53

Conclusion

Telehealth is a growing tool in medical practice, facilitating communication and connection for individuals with ALS, especially in the setting of mobility challenges or geographic constraints. This study presents 3 real-world cases illustrating the use of telehealth in SICs with individuals with ALS. Optimizing telehealth for SICs require platforms that provide reliable, high-quality audiovisual communication, low latency, user-friendly access across devices, and secure Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant connections. Additional features such as pan-tilt-zoom camera control, picture-in-picture tool, and background blurring can further improve connection and visual engagement.

Evidence from other fields supports the efficacy of telehealth in delivering palliative care. For instance, a randomized multisite clinical trial in patients with advanced cancer demonstrated comparable improvements in QoL whether early palliative care was delivered in-person or through telehealth.54 This suggests that timely access to palliative care is more crucial than the mode of delivery, although in-person visits may be preferred when feasible and whenever the benefits outweigh the harms. However, for individuals with ALS, in-person visits may become increasingly challenging as the disease progresses. Given the benefits telehealth offers to patients and their families, further development of evidence-based strategies to enhance virtual communication is essential.

The limitations of telehealth for ALS SICs must also be acknowledged, particularly in circumstances where in-person consultations are preferred, such as when assessing respiratory status or swallowing ability is necessary to inform the timing of interventions and prognosis. Therefore, it is essential to continue developing both telehealth and home visit options. This dual approach ensures that a variety of care modalities are available to individuals with ALS, thereby alleviating the burden of travel for necessary assessments and enhancing overall patient care.

Palliative care visits conducted through telehealth, particularly in contexts where communication is impaired, can be time-consuming. Such visits typically range from 30 to 60 minutes or longer to ensure comprehensive assessment and support for the patient's needs. The sustainability of telehealth-delivered palliative care will depend on reimbursement policies that recognize the value of time spent on complex, multidisciplinary care. Continued advocacy for stable reimbursement frameworks is essential to support rapid and ongoing access to palliative care for individuals with ALS.

The scope of our study is limited to 3 case scenarios within ALS care, and patient experiences in other neurodegenerative conditions may differ significantly. Despite these limitations, this work provides valuable insights into the practical application of telehealth for SICs in this specialized context drawn from literature search and real-world experience.

Our findings lay the groundwork for future initiatives aimed at offering practical guidance to health care clinicians in this field.
