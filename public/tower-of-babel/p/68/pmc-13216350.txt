Combating small extracellular vesicle–mediated immunological barriers in the tumor microenvironment via strategically activatable PEGylated peptides
Authors: Chan Ho Kim, Hyewon Ko, Jae Ah Lee, So Hee Kim, Minsung Park, Chang Hyun Lee, Sol Shin, Jung Min Shin, Soyoung Son, Jeongjin Lee, Heegun Kang, Sohyun Cho, Joonsuk Chang, Jiyeon Kim, Won Ho Jang, Min Jun Kim, Joshua A Jackman, Jae Hyung Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor cell-derived small extracellular vesicles (TDEs) play a critical role in immune evasion, including the establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), and form a substantial barrier to effective cancer immunotherapy. Here, we introduce the ExoPERM (pH-enabled rupture of exosome membranes) strategy, a pH-responsive system based on small extracellular vesicle (EV)-disrupting alpha-helical peptides that selectively target small EVs localized within the TME. By incorporating a pH-sensitive linker through PEGylation, this system ensures systemic stability while allowing the selective release of peptides within the mildly acidic TME. Mechanistic investigations revealed that the peptide preferentially ruptured small EVs at pH 6.5, effectively preventing EVs’ PD-L1 interactions with PD-1 on CD8⁺ T cells and contributing to the restoration of their effector functions. Furthermore, this targeted disruption significantly attenuated TDE-induced activation of cancer-associated fibroblasts, thereby remodeling the dense fibrotic stroma that physically restricts immune cell penetration. In vivo studies have demonstrated enhanced CD8⁺ T-cell tumor infiltration and activation, notably when combined with immune checkpoint blockade or adoptive T-cell transfer. These synergistic effects were robustly validated across murine melanoma and colorectal cancer models, successfully converting immunologically cold tumors into T-cell-inflamed hot tumors. The ExoPERM strategy provides a promising option for overcoming TDE-mediated resistance in cancer, suggesting a distinct approach to small EV inhibition.Subject terms: Drug development, Cancer microenvironment, Immunotherapy, Drug delivery

Introduction

Compared with conventional chemotherapy, cancer immunotherapy has brought about a new era in cancer treatment, offering a promising avenue for enhancing durable clinical responses and long-term survival rates.1–3 Despite the clinical success of representative strategies such as immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) and adoptive T-cell transfer (ACT), the lack of therapeutic benefits in a generalized population of patients represents a significant unmet need.3–7 This heterogeneous therapeutic response can be attributed primarily to the tumor microenvironment (TME), which has been demonstrated to hamper antitumor immunity and drive resistance to immunotherapy.6

Among the various mechanisms of immune evasion within the TME, tumor-derived small extracellular vesicles (TDEs) have recently been identified as significant contributors.8,9 Small extracellular vesicles (EVs) are lipid bilayer–enclosed particles that are typically 50–150 nm in diameter.10,11 TDEs, a population enriched in tumor-associated cargos, mediate intercellular communication and modulate stromal and immune cell behavior to facilitate tumor progression and dampen antitumor immunity.10,12–14 Although TDEs are predominantly immunosuppressive across many tumor models, they can also perform immune-stimulating functions depending on their molecular cargo, reflecting the heterogeneous immunological roles of these vesicles.15 In regard to the considerable impact of TDEs on resistance to immunotherapy, various strategies have been developed with the objective of inhibiting them.12,16,17 The prevailing approach is to interfere with the biogenesis machinery, from the formation of multivesicular bodies to the release of small EVs.18–21 However, these strategies encounter several obstacles, including the functional inconsistencies of proteins involved in small EV biogenesis across different cell species, the potential toxicity to normal cells, and the necessity for the intracellular delivery of inhibitors.

To circumvent these constraints, we developed an innovative strategy that directly targets secreted TDEs in a previous study.22 This strategy leverages an alpha-helix-structured peptide (AH-Pep) that specifically recognizes and ruptures lipid bilayer-enveloped vesicles within the 50–150 nm range on the basis of membrane curvature.23 Notably, the membrane disruption activity of AH-Pep is enhanced under mildly acidic conditions, which are characteristic of the TME. In addition, as it relies on the structural characteristics of small EVs, this approach is independent of complex small EV biogenesis pathways, implying broad applicability across different cancer types. Initially, developed for antiviral use, AH-Pep has been shown to exhibit similar specificity for TDEs and to elicit rupture, thereby preventing exosomal PD-L1 (exoPD-L1) from binding PD-1 on T cells.12,22,24 This blockade restored CD8⁺ T-cell activation and enhanced antitumor immune responses.

Despite these encouraging outcomes resulting from the synergistic effect with anti-PD-1 antibodies, the intratumoral administration of this proof-of-concept study warrants further investigation because of its potential limitations in clinical applicability. In the present study, an effective systemic delivery formulation of AH-Pep was developed by applying the widely used technique of PEGylation (polyethylene glycol modification) in drug delivery.25 PEGylation has been extensively acknowledged for its ability to prolong the half-life of therapeutic agents, which has resulted in the FDA approval of numerous PEGylated drugs.26,27 Its safety and efficacy profiles are well documented, making it a valuable tool for enhancing drug stability. However, conventional PEGylation can also obscure the active sites of drugs, potentially decreasing their therapeutic efficacy.28,29

Herein, we designed a strategically activatable PEGylated peptide (PEG-SA-Pep) by introducing TME responsiveness via a pH-sensitive linker,30 enabling the triggered release of the active peptide at tumor lesions (Fig. 1). This design also benefits from the acid-enhanced membrane disruptive properties of AH-Pep, which increase vesicle-rupturing potency.22 This modification ensures that PEG-SA-Pep remains stable under physiological conditions but becomes activated within the mildly acidic TME (pH 6.5–6.8), an approach designated as the ExoPERM (pH-enabled rupture of exosome membranes) strategy. By leveraging the difference in pH between physiological and TME conditions, the ExoPERM strategy minimizes off-target effects and enables the selective disintegration of TDEs. Furthermore, by circumventing the complexities of intracellular intervention and small EV biogenesis pathways, this strategy offers a direct and potent method for counteracting TDE-mediated immunosuppression.

The present study investigated the potential synergy of the ExoPERM strategy with current cancer immunotherapy regimens, focusing on its ability to overcome the immunosuppressive challenges posed by TDEs. To this end, PEG-SA-Pep is evaluated in combination with two prominent T-cell-based immunotherapies, namely, ICB using anti-PD-1 antibodies (aPD-1) and ACT. These findings suggest that PEG-SA-Pep restores CD8⁺ T-cell effector function and enhances T-cell infiltration within the TME, effectively complementing the limited efficacy of monotherapies. Overall, this study suggests a novel therapeutic rationale for addressing TDE-mediated immunosuppression through the ExoPERM strategy, which could expand therapeutic benefits for patients with cancers refractory to immunotherapy.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of PEG-SA-pep

To enable the systemic delivery of AH-Pep, we engineered two PEGylated variants: PEG-SA-Pep, a pH-responsive formulation designed for selective release in the TME, and PEG-NA-Pep (nonactivatable PEGylated peptide), which serves as a nonresponsive control. PEG-SA-Pep incorporates a carboxy-dimethyl maleic anhydride (CDM) linker between the PEG moiety and the peptide (Supplementary Fig. 1a). This CDM linker undergoes hydrolysis under mildly acidic conditions (pH 6.5–6.8) characteristic of the TME, triggering the selective release of AH-Pep. Conversely, PEG-NA-Pep features a stable amide bond to PEG and lacks pH sensitivity. This design strategy enables a comparative analysis of pH-responsive versus nonresponsive formulations in a controlled experimental framework, elucidating the impact of pH-triggered AH-Pep release on therapeutic efficacy.

The synthesis of PEG-SA-Pep was carried out in two steps. First, a PEG-CDM was synthesized, which was subsequently verified using ¹H NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Afterward, the PEG-CDM was conjugated to AH-Pep to yield PEG-SA-Pep. The successful conjugation of the PEG derivatives and AH-Pep was assessed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and MALDI‒TOF mass spectrometry (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d), which revealed that PEG-mediated enhancement of hydrophilicity and effective conjugation occurred. To assess the impact of AH-Pep modification on its secondary structure, which is crucial for its biological activity, circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was performed. The results revealed that both PEG-NA-Pep and PEG-SA-Pep maintained the characteristic alpha-helix conformation of bare AH-Pep (Supplementary Fig. 1e), supporting its intended therapeutic application to disrupt TDEs.

Following structural characterization, we evaluated the pH-dependent release profiles of AH-pep under physiological (pH 7.4) and TME conditions (pH 6.5) (Fig. 2a). PEG-NA-Pep, with a stable amide linker, showed minimal peptide release across all the tested pH conditions, confirming its pH-insensitive nature. In contrast, the release dynamics of PEG-SA-Pep differed, reaching threefold greater release at 24 h at an acidic pH of 6.5 and maintaining negligible release at a physiological pH of 7.4. To further verify the chemical specificity of linker cleavage, we performed HPLC analysis, which confirmed that SA-linker cleavage occurs selectively under TME-mimetic acidic conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2). This pH-responsive release profile, coupled with the preserved α-helical structure of AH-Pep, supports the suitability of PEG-SA-Pep for targeted therapeutic intervention within the TME.

In vitro pH-responsive disruption of TDEs and restoration of T-cell function

Following its physicochemical characterization, we next evaluated the ability of PEG-SA-Pep to counteract TDE-mediated immunosuppression in vitro. TDEs were isolated from the culture supernatant of B16F10 murine melanoma cells and subsequently used for in vitro functional assays. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed a mean particle diameter of approximately 147 nm, and western blotting confirmed the presence of exosomal markers, such as CD9, along with PD-L1 in the isolated vesicles (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b).

As shown in Fig. 2b, as measured by NTA, PEG-SA-Pep significantly disrupted TDEs under mildly acidic conditions (pH 6.5), confirming that its pH-sensitive design enables effective payload release. This behavior mirrors that of bare AH-Pep, which reduced the TDE concentration by approximately 50% at physiological pH and exhibited greater disruption at acidic pH, likely due to enhanced hydrophobic interactions with TDE membranes. In contrast, PEG-NA-Pep, which lacks pH sensitivity, induced minimal TDE disruption with no significant difference between pH conditions, likely because steric hindrance from PEG impedes peptide‒TDE interactions. In addition, representative NTA images revealed vesicle aggregation following AH-Pep treatment, with PEG-SA-Pep exhibiting a similar pattern at acidic pH, which is consistent with membrane destabilization, a finding further corroborated by the significant reduction in intact TDEs through fluorescence-based vesicle integrity analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3c and 4). Notably, further characterization revealed that the membrane-disrupting activity of AH-Pep is exhaustible upon interaction (Supplementary Fig. 5), underscoring the necessity for a delivery system capable of ensuring sustained availability of the peptide within the TME. To exclude potential off-target membrane damage, we confirmed that despite their inherent membrane-disruptive nature, the peptides caused neither plasma membrane damage nor intracellular lysosomal disruption in normal cells, thereby underscoring their curvature-dependent selectivity and negligible cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Given that PEG-SA-Pep effectively disrupted TDEs under acidic conditions, we next examined whether this membrane destabilization could impair the immunosuppressive function of exoPD-L1. The binding of TDEs to PD-1 on CD8+ T cells via exoPD-L1 is a well-established immune escape mechanism.12,24 ELISA confirmed that intact TDEs robustly promoted PD-1/exoPD-L1 interaction, which was significantly reduced by AH-Pep, mirroring the trends observed in the NTA measurements (Fig. 2c, d). PEG-NA-Pep, which lacks pH responsiveness, had a negligible effect on PD-1/exoPD-L1 binding regardless of the pH. In contrast, PEG-SA-Pep displayed marked pH-selective activity—substantially reducing PD-1/exoPD-L1 binding under mildly acidic conditions (pH 6.5) but showed negligible effects at physiological pH (7.4), which is consistent with its pH-selective vesicle-disrupting activity.

To further investigate the effects of TDE rupture on T-cell interactions, we performed confocal microscopy analysis to visualize and quantify the association between TDE and splenic CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 2e, f). Intact TDEs exhibited strong fluorescence signals on T cells, indicating substantial vesicle association. In line with earlier observations, AH-Pep treatment markedly reduced TDE accumulation across pH conditions. Notably, compared with AH-Pep, PEG-SA-Pep reduced TDE accumulation at a pH of 6.5 to levels similar to those of AH-Pep, whereas the effects of PEG-NA-Pep were minimal regardless of the pH, indicating that the activity was pH responsive. This reduction reflects both vesicle disintegration and disrupted PD-1/exoPD-L1 binding.

Extensive evidence indicates that interactions between TDEs and T cells play a regulatory role in T-cell functions.31–34 To evaluate this effect, we analyzed Ki-67 and granzyme B (GrB) expression in CD8⁺ T cells by flow cytometry (Fig. 2g, h). TDEs markedly suppressed the proportions of Ki-67⁺ and GrB⁺ CD8⁺ T cells to approximately 10% and 30%, respectively, which is consistent with a functionally exhausted phenotype. This suppression was substantially reversed by bare AH-Pep, restoring both proliferation and effector activity. Under mildly acidic conditions, compared with the control, PEG-SA-Pep significantly increased the proportions of Ki-67⁺ and GrB⁺ CD8⁺ T cells, resulting in 3.4- and 1.8-fold increases, respectively. In contrast, PEG-NA-Pep had minimal effects. Furthermore, these findings were corroborated by flow cytometry analysis of the expression of the key exhaustion markers PD-1 and lymphocyte-activating gene-3 (LAG-3) on splenic CD8⁺ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 7). These findings suggest that PEG-SA-Pep neutralizes TDE-induced CD8+ T-cell exhaustion and that structural disruption of vesicles and reduced exoPD-L1 engagement may contribute to this effect.

In vivo pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of PEG-SA-Pep

To ensure sufficient stability in circulation prior to in vivo evaluation, we first assessed the serum stability of each peptide over 48 h (Supplementary Fig. 8). HPLC analysis revealed that unmodified AH-Pep was rapidly degraded, whereas the PEGylated variants, including PEG-NA-Pep and PEG-SA-Pep, maintained remarkable stability throughout the incubation period. These results demonstrate that PEGylation confers substantial protection against proteolytic degradation, thereby minimizing the premature loss of PEG-SA-Pep during systemic circulation. Motivated by these favorable stability profiles, we next examined the in vivo pharmacokinetics and biodistribution following systemic administration of the peptides in tumor-bearing mice. All the tested peptides exhibited a time-dependent decrease in plasma concentration (Supplementary Fig. 9). Although PEGylation typically prolongs circulation, compared with PEG-NA-Pep, PEG-SA-Pep decreases faster, which may be attributable to efficient tissue distribution and tumor-selective activation. This observation is supported by the results of the time-course biodistribution analysis, in which PEG-SA-Pep rapidly accumulated in tumors, peaking at 6 h post-injection (Fig. 3a). Compared with AH-Pep (58.74 ± 10.39) and PEG-NA-Pep (94.29 ± 10.55), PEG-SA-Pep exhibited a substantially greater area under the curve (AUC) for tumor accumulation (184.6 ± 12.4), representing 3.14- and 1.96-fold increases, respectively. Further quantitative analysis of tumors and major organs at 6 and 24 h post-injection revealed that AH-Pep accumulated predominantly in the liver and kidneys, suggesting rapid clearance (Fig. 3b, c). In contrast, the tumor accumulation of PEG-SA-Pep was sustained at both time points, in line with its previously noted tumor-selective distribution. PEG-NA-Pep showed tumor accumulation levels comparable to those of AH-Pep, suggesting that PEGylation alone may confer limited tumor-targeting benefit in the absence of a pH-responsive feature. This pattern may reflect selective tumor retention, possibly aided by pH-responsive cleavage that facilitates peptide persistence within the TME.

Encouraged by the high in vivo tumor accumulation of PEG-SA-Pep, we next investigated its efficacy in eliminating TDEs in vivo (Fig. 3d). To this end, B16F10 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously administered AH-Pep (15 mg/kg), PEG-NA-Pep (15 mg/kg), or PEG-SA-Pep (5, 10, or 15 mg/kg) (Fig. 3e). After two doses, the plasma levels of exoPD-L1 were quantified by ELISA (Fig. 3f). As expected, compared with the PBS-treated tumor-bearing controls, the tumor-free mice presented lower plasma exoPD-L1 levels. Neither AH-Pep nor PEG-NA-Pep significantly decreased exoPD-L1 expression compared with that in the PBS controls, indicating limited in vivo efficacy. In contrast, treatment with 10 mg/kg PEG-SA-Pep led to a dose-dependent reduction in exoPD-L1 levels. At the highest dose tested (15 mg/kg), compared with no treatment, treatment with PEG-SA-Pep decreased the level of circulating exoPD-L1 to levels comparable to those in tumor-free mice. These findings suggest that PEG-SA-Pep effectively depletes circulating immunosuppressive small EVs in vivo, likely owing to its enhanced tumor accumulation and efficient interaction with TDEs.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of PEG-SA-Pep in combination with ICB

Having established that PEG-SA-Pep achieves favorable tumor accumulation and selectively neutralizes small EVs in a pH-dependent manner, we next investigated whether this targeted intervention could overcome TDE-mediated immunosuppression and potentiate the efficacy of aPD-1 therapy. We conducted antitumor efficacy studies in B16F10 melanoma-bearing C57BL/6 mice and administered treatments either as monotherapy or in combination with aPD-1 (Fig. 4a). Consistent with our hypothesis, monotherapy with PEG-SA-Pep, PEG-NA-Pep, or bare AH-Pep failed to elicit significant tumor suppression, demonstrating that TDE elimination alone is insufficient for effective tumor growth control (Fig. 4b, c). This limited therapeutic effect likely reflects the preferential targeting of the peptides toward small EVs rather than tumor cells directly, as supported by the minimal cytotoxicity observed in B16F10 tumor cells and NIH3T3 fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Notably, a remarkable synergistic effect was observed when PEG-SA-Pep was coadministered with aPD-1. This combination resulted in profound tumor growth inhibition, significantly outperforming aPD-1 monotherapy and all the other combination treatments (Fig. 4b, c). In contrast, combining aPD-1 with either AH-Pep or PEG-NA-Pep offered no significant therapeutic benefit over aPD-1 alone. This outcome reflects the poor preferential tumor accumulation observed for both bare AH-Pep and PEG-NA-Pep, with the latter further compromised by its noncleavable PEG shield. These results underscore that pH-responsive cleavage and subsequent TDE disruption are critical for potentiating ICB. The superior efficacy of the PEG-SA-Pep + aPD-1 regimen was further supported by a substantial reduction in the weights and sizes of excised tumors at the experimental endpoint (day 20 postinoculation; Fig. 4d, e). Encouragingly, no significant systemic toxicity was observed across all treatment groups, as reflected by body weights and histological analysis of major organs (Supplementary Fig. 11). Moreover, although compared with the control treatment, the PEG-SA-Pep treatment markedly attenuated the correlation between the exosomal PD-L1 level and tumor burden, the plasma total EV concentration decreased only modestly (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). This selective depletion, in which tumor-specific markers are significantly reduced while the total EV pool is substantially preserved, suggests that PEG-SA-pep has minimal off-target effects.

To investigate the immune mechanisms responsible for this synergistic effect, we analyzed the tumor immune landscape. Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) revealed that compared with aPD-1 monotherapy, the PEG-SA-Pep + aPD-1 combination led to a significant increase in the frequency of CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes among CD45⁺ immune cells, with a 1.84-fold increase (Fig. 4f). Concurrently, this regimen induced a significant decrease in the proportion of immunosuppressive CD4⁺CD25⁺Foxp3⁺ Tregs (Fig. 4g, Supplementary Fig. 14) and alleviated the exhausted phenotype of CD8⁺ T cells, characterized by the marked downregulation of PD-1 and LAG-3 expression (Supplementary Fig. 15), indicating fundamental reprogramming of the TME toward a more inflamed, antitumorigenic state. To assess whether this local immune reinvigoration was associated with a systemic response, we evaluated the proliferative capacity of splenocytes. Following ex vivo stimulation with the melanoma-associated antigen GP100, splenocytes from the PEG-SA-Pep + aPD-1 group exhibited a markedly increased frequency of Ki-67⁺ proliferating cells, supporting the induction of robust, tumor-specific systemic immunity (Fig. 4h). Collectively, these results suggest that the pH-responsive disruption of TDEs by PEG-SA-Pep, central to our ExoPERM strategy, contributes to alleviating immunosuppressive pressure within the TME, thereby eliciting a potent, systemic antitumor T-cell response that synergizes with aPD-1 therapy.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of PEG-SA-Pep in combination with ACT

While our findings demonstrated that PEG-SA-Pep enhances CD8⁺ T-cell responses when combined with aPD-1, effective immunotherapy, particularly in solid tumors, relies not only on T-cell activation but also on the successful infiltration of these cells into tumor tissue. The dense stromal architecture and fibrotic matrix of the TME often serve as major barriers to effective T-cell penetration into the tumor core. We therefore sought to determine whether PEG-SA-Pep could also reduce these physical constraints. To this end, CD8⁺ T cells isolated from CT26 tumor-bearing mice were adoptively transferred into tumor-bearing recipients pretreated with PEG-SA-Pep, after which the intratumoral distribution of the transferred cells was subsequently assessed (Fig. 5a).

Immunofluorescence imaging of tumor sections revealed differential intratumoral localization of Dil-labeled transferred T cells across treatment groups. In mice treated with PBS, AH-Pep, or PEG-NA-Pep, T cells primarily accumulated at the tumor periphery and exhibited minimal infiltration into the tumor core (Fig. 5b, c). This restricted distribution likely contributed to the limited therapeutic efficacy observed in these groups. In contrast, compared with the other groups, the PEG-SA-Pep pretreatment group exhibited markedly enhanced T-cell penetration into deeper tumor regions, with the intratumoral fluorescence intensity increasing by more than threefold. These findings suggest that PEG-SA-Pep not only promotes T-cell recruitment but also facilitates T-cell spatial distribution within the TME, potentially improving the effectiveness of ACT. TDEs have been reported to activate cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), thereby contributing to fibrotic stroma through α–smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and collagen deposition, hindering T-cell access in solid tumors.35–38 Therefore, we hypothesized that PEG-SA-Pep–mediated disruption of TDEs may attenuate CAF-driven fibrosis, resulting in a less fibrotic TME that permits greater T-cell infiltration. To validate this hypothesis, we first performed in vitro experiments using the murine fibroblast line NIH3T3. As expected, treatment with TDEs induced robust upregulation of α-SMA in NIH3T3 cells, which was consistent with fibroblast activation toward a myofibroblast-like state. Notably, this activation was effectively abrogated by PEG-SA-Pep under mildly acidic conditions (pH 6.5), confirming that the pH-responsive disruption of TDEs prevents fibroblast activation (Supplementary Fig. 16). Corroborating these findings in vivo, compared with the PBS treatment, the PEG-SA-Pep treatment reduced α-SMA levels by approximately 75%, whereas the AH-Pep and PEG-NA-Pep treatments did not significantly alter α-SMA expression (Fig. 5d, e). These results indicate that PEG-SA-Pep mitigates TDE-induced fibrosis, thereby alleviating the physical barriers within the TME.

To further assess whether PEG-SA-Pep–mediated remodeling of the TME could enhance the efficacy of ACT, we preconditioned tumor-bearing mice with PEG-SA-Pep prior to the adoptive transfer of CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 5f). Despite IL-12 coadministration, ACT alone resulted in only modest tumor growth suppression, likely because of insufficient T-cell infiltration and persistent fibrotic barriers typical of solid tumors (Fig. 5g, h). Pretreatment with AH-Pep or PEG-NA-Pep similarly failed to improve therapeutic outcomes, yielding a tumor growth profile comparable to that of ACT alone. In contrast, PEG-SA-Pep preconditioning resulted in a notable therapeutic benefit, with consistently delayed tumor progression in all treated mice. This enhanced antitumor efficacy is likely driven by PEG-SA-Pep–induced stromal remodeling, which improves tissue accessibility and promotes the intratumoral accumulation of transferred CD8⁺ T cells, an effect that may be further supported by IL-12–mediated activation. These findings support the use of PEG-SA-Pep as a TME-modulating approach to increase the efficacy of ACT.

Discussion

TDEs have emerged as pivotal players in cancer immunosuppression, leading to the vigorous exploration of small EV inhibition strategies.9,16,39 Initial efforts have concentrated on impairing small EV biogenesis via various modalities.19,24,40,41 However, these approaches risk compromising normal cell function because of the nonspecific disruption of EV pathways.42 Consequently, safer alternatives have begun to focus on targeting secreted small EVs directly, aiming to selectively neutralize tumor-promoting vesicles with a reduced impact on normal cellular functions.22,43,44 However, it is important to note that the immunomodulatory effects of TDEs can vary depending on their molecular cargo and biological context.15 While these vesicles are widely characterized for their immunosuppressive effects, several studies have also reported that EV-mediated immune-stimulating effects are driven by specific cargo compositions.45–47 This heterogeneity underscores the importance of accounting for tumor-specific EV phenotypes when TDE-directed therapeutic approaches are considered.

To address this need, we engineered PEG-SA-Pep as a pH-responsive, systemically deliverable formulation of our previously developed AH-Pep peptide.22 Our design circumvents the common limitations of PEGylation by incorporating a pH-sensitive linker that maintains a protective PEG shell during systemic circulation and enables selective peptide activation in the acidic TME. Specifically, the 5 kDa PEG size was selected to optimize the balance between pharmacokinetic stability and immunogenic safety, while N-terminal-specific conjugation was employed to mechanistically cap the membrane insertion trigger and ensure product homogeneity.48,49 This TME-specific activation is expected to minimize off-target effects and facilitate local small EV membrane disruption within the acidic tumor milieu, mediated by peptide-induced membrane rupture. Importantly, this rupture mechanism is further facilitated by the preferential interaction of the peptide with the high membrane curvature characteristic of small EVs, which distinguishes them from planar cellular membranes.22,23 By confining this activity to the acidic TME, the ExoPERM strategy provides functional selectivity toward the collective immunosuppressive small EV pool enriched within the tumor bed—including TDEs and abundant small EVs derived from stromal and immune cells (e.g., CAFs and tumor-associated macrophages)—rather than discriminating among specific subtypes. This TME-constrained activation thus offers broad therapeutic benefits by eliminating protumorigenic vesicles regardless of their cellular origin.

In in vitro functional T-cell assays, PEG-SA-Pep effectively restored CD8+ T-cell effector function in the presence of TDEs (Fig. 2g, h), which is consistent with the hypothesis that the pH-dependent release of free AH-Pep contributes to this restoration. This pH-dependent behavior also promoted selective tumor accumulation of PEG-SA-Pep and significantly reduced plasma exoPD-L1 levels (Fig. 3a, f), thereby limiting PD-1 engagement and mitigating TDE-mediated immunosuppression. The therapeutic potential of PEG-SA-Pep in combination with ICB was effectively demonstrated. Notably, while compared with aPD-1 monotherapy, the use of PEG-SA-Pep as a single agent elicited limited tumor suppression, compared with aPD-1 monotherapy, coadministration with aPD-1 led to markedly greater tumor regression (Fig. 4b–e). These observations suggest that TDE disruption functions primarily to reverse immune evasion rather than as a direct cytotoxic intervention. In line with this rationale, the robust antitumor efficacy of the combination therapy was accompanied by favorable alterations in the tumor immune landscape, including enhanced TILs and reduced Treg populations (Fig. 4f–h). Accordingly, these effects likely arise from the disruption of immunosuppressive TDEs by PEG-SA-Pep, which reduces exosomal PD-L1–mediated signaling and may alleviate additional TDE-derived suppressive cues within the TME, thereby supporting improved effector T-cell activity and enhancing the efficacy of aPD-1. In a field where resistance to ICB remains a major hurdle, PEG-SA-Pep offers a translationally viable approach to enhance the therapeutic benefit and durability of ICB, particularly in aPD-1-refractory tumors.

Furthermore, PEG-SA-Pep demonstrated noteworthy synergistic effects on ACT by significantly increasing the intratumoral accumulation of transferred CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 5b, c). This improvement was paralleled by remodeling of the fibrotic stroma, as evidenced by reduced α-SMA expression (Fig. 5d, e). This observation aligns with our in vitro analysis confirming that PEG-SA-Pep effectively abrogates TDE-mediated fibroblast activation. Consequently, the proposed dual mechanism, in which both physical and immunosuppressive barriers are targeted, likely provides a compelling rationale for the enhanced tumor control observed when PEG-SA-Pep with ACT (Fig. 5g, h) through the neutralization of TDEs, which are known causes of activated CAFs and the promotion of extracellular matrix remodeling.50,51 These findings are of particular clinical significance given the recent accelerated approval by the FDA of lifileucel (Amtagvi), a TIL therapy for advanced melanoma in early 2024, which marked a milestone in extending ACT to solid tumors.52 As TILs and other cell-based therapies continue to expand into diverse solid tumor indications, strategies to enhance T-cell infiltration and function will be crucial for maximizing therapeutic outcomes.53,54 While our findings support the functional impact of TDE disruption on stromal remodeling and T-cell infiltration, future studies utilizing transcriptomic profiling will be valuable to fully elucidate the complex molecular networks modulated by the ExoPERM strategy.

In conclusion, this study establishes an ExoPERM strategy, a pH-responsive approach for the selective disruption of immunosuppressive EVs, including TDEs, within the TME. Using PEG-SA-Pep as a representative implementation, we provide evidence that the ExoPERM strategy neutralizes exoPD-L1 while sparing normal cellular functions, offering a mechanistically distinct and potentially safer alternative to nonspecific biogenesis inhibitors. This strategy overcomes two major barriers to cancer immunotherapy—immune suppression, which limits ICB efficacy, and fibrotic stroma, which restricts ACT. By addressing both, it facilitates the transition of immunologically cold tumors into T-cell–inflamed hot tumors. Its modular design, enabling variation of linkers, therapeutic payloads, and combination partners supports broad adaptation and integration with established treatment regimens. Given recent clinical advances in both antibody- and cell-based immunotherapies, strategies such as the ExoPERM strategy, which enhances T-cell infiltration and function, may broaden the reach of current treatments, particularly in tumors resistant to existing modalities.

Despite the functional efficacy of the ExoPERM strategy, our current approach provides environment-mediated selectivity rather than cell-of-origin specificity. As such, the strategy acts upon the collective small EV population within the tumor niche, and distinguishing TDE-specific effects from the broader TME-associated small EV landscape remains to be fully elucidated. Future investigations exploring the incorporation of TDE-targeting motifs into the ExoPERM strategy will be valuable for refining targeting precision and minimizing potential off-target effects. Additionally, while our study utilized established melanoma and colorectal cancer models, further validation across a broader range of clinically relevant tumor types is warranted to generalize the therapeutic applicability of the ExoPERM strategy. Overall, this platform offers a promising TME-responsive approach to overcome small EV-mediated immune evasion and enhance the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Materials

mPEG (Mn: 5 kDa) was purchased from Sigma‒Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). 3-(4-Methyl-2,5-dioxo-2,5-dihydrofuran-3-yl) propanoic acid (CDM) was obtained from Ambeed (Arlington Heights, IL, USA). For the synthesis of the control peptide (PEG-NA-Pep), PEG-succinimidyl glutarate (Mn: 5 kDa) was purchased from creative PEGWorks (Durham, NC, USA). Oxalyl chloride was obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry (TCI; Tokyo, Japan). To label TDEs, the fluorescence dye DiO’ and DiOC18(3) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc. (Rockford, IL, USA).

Peptides

AH-Pep (SGSWLRDVWDWICTVLTDFKTWLQSKL) was chemically synthesized and supplied in lyophilized form at >90% purity by Anygen (Gwangju, Korea). The molar concentration of the peptide solutions was determined by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm using ultraviolet‒visible (UV‒Vis) spectroscopy (Optizen 3220UV, KLAB).

Cell lines and animals

The murine melanoma cell line B16F10 (KCLB-8008) was obtained from the Korean Cell Line Bank and cultured in DMEM (Capricorn Scientific) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Capricorn Scientific) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Capricorn Scientific). The cells used in all the in vitro experiments were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

For in vivo experiments, 5–6-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Orient Bio and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sungkyunkwan University (no. SKKUIACUC2024-05-16-1).

Synthesis of PEG-CDM

The carboxyl group of CDM was activated using oxalyl chloride to conjugate it with the hydroxyl group of mPEG, forming PEG-CDM via an ester bond. Briefly, 50 mg of CDM was dissolved in 2 ml of dichloromethane (DCM), followed by the addition of 46.3 μl of oxalyl chloride and 8 μl of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF). The mixture was stirred on ice for 10 min and then at room temperature for 1 h. After evaporation under vacuum conditions, 180 mg of PEG dissolved in 4 ml of DCM with 6 μl of pyridine was added to the reaction mixture, which was then stirred overnight at room temperature. To quench the reaction, 10 ml of a saturated NH4Cl solution was added. The resulting mixture was transferred to a separating funnel, and the bottom organic layer containing the product was collected. The product was precipitated in diethyl ether and then washed by centrifugation at 2500 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. After the supernatant was discarded, the precipitate was vacuum-dried to obtain the final PEG-CDM product.

Preparation of PEG-SA-Pep

To achieve the selective conjugation of the N-terminal amine of AH-pep to PEG-CDM, the pH of the solvent system was carefully controlled to inhibit side chain amine activation during the reaction process.55 Briefly, 20 mg of PEG-CDM was dissolved in 6 ml of PBS (pH 7.4), and 5 mg of AH-Pep was dissolved in 1 ml of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The AH-Pep solution was added dropwise to the PEG-CDM solution under continuous stirring. The reaction was allowed to proceed overnight at 4 °C. The resulting mixture was dialyzed against distilled water, with the pH adjusted to 8 using NaOH. The final solution was lyophilized to obtain PEG-SA-Pep. As a control, PEG-NA-pep was synthesized using the same procedure, except that PEG-succinimidyl glutarate was used instead of PEG-CDM.

Release test of PEG-SA-Pep under various pH conditions

To determine the in vitro release profile of AH-Pep liberated from PEG-SA-Pep, a dialysis method was employed. Briefly, each peptide sample was dispersed in 100 mM phosphate buffer (PB) at a pH of 7.4 or 6.5, and the solution was transferred into a dialysis membrane tube with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 12–14 kDa. The dialysis tube was then immersed in PB at the corresponding pH (7.4 or 6.5) and gently shaken in a water bath at 100 rpm for predetermined time points. At each point, the amount of released AH-Pep was quantified using a UV‒Vis spectrometer (Optizen 3220 UV, Mecasys Co., Daejeon, Korea), and the release profile was plotted accordingly.

Isolation of TDEs

For in vitro isolation of TDEs, a tangential flow filtration (TFF) system equipped with an Omega membrane filter capsule with a 300 kDa molecular weight cutoff (Pall Corporation) was used.56 Briefly, B16F10 cells at approximately 70% confluency were washed twice with PBS and cultured in serum-free DMEM for 24 h. The cell culture supernatants were then collected and subjected to sequential centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min and 2000 × g for 20 min to remove cell debris and large vesicles, respectively. The supernatant was further filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter before processing with the TFF system. For in vivo experiments, cell-free plasma was obtained from blood samples for TDE isolation. TDEs from mouse plasma were purified using a Total Exosome Isolation Kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

In vitro TDE rupture test

The concentration of TDEs, which is critical for evaluating the efficiency of TED rupture, was measured using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; NanoSight LM10; Malvern Panalytical). Each peptide was prepared by suspending PB at a concentration of 6 μM and incubating it for 6 h. TDE suspensions (1 × 109 particles ml−1 in PBS) were subsequently incubated with or without peptides at room temperature, resulting in a final concentration of 3 μM AH-Pep. After incubation, the suspensions were filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter to remove aggregates prior to analysis by the NTA system.

PD-1/PD-L1 binding assay

To quantify the binding affinity between PD-L1 on small EVs and PD-1, plasma was obtained from WT or B16F10 tumor-bearing mice. Small EVs were isolated following a previously described protocol. Small EVs were then coincubated with each peptide under various pH conditions. The binding capacity of PD-L1 to PD-1 was subsequently measured using a mouse PD-L1 ELISA kit (catalog no. DY1019-05; R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and as previously reported.22

Isolation and stimulation of CD8+ T lymphocytes

Spleens were harvested from C57BL/6 mice, mechanically dissociated, and passed through a 40 μm cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Red blood cells were then lysed using RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend, San Diego, CA). CD8+ T lymphocytes were isolated from single-cell suspensions using a mouse naïve CD8a+ T-cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec) and LS Columns (Miltenyi Biotec) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Freshly isolated naïve CD8+ T cells were seeded and activated for 48 h with anti-CD3 antibody (5 μg/ml) and anti-CD28 antibody (2 μg/ml) in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 1× nonessential amino acid solution (MilliporeSigma, catalog no. M7145) and 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol (MilliporeSigma, catalog no. 60-24-2).

Cellular uptake of TDEs by CD8+ T lymphocytes

Splenic CD8+ T cells were isolated and activated as described previously and seeded at 1 × 106 cells per well in a 12-well plate with glass bottoms precoated with fibronectin (2 μg/cm2; MilliporeSigma, catalog no. F1141). To track the cellular uptake of TDEs, the lipophilic dye DiO was used to label the membranes of the TDEs. Briefly, 10 μL of DiO dye stock solution (3 mg/mL, DMSO:ethanol = 1:9, v/v) was added to 1 mL of the TDE suspension. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The labeled TDEs were then passed through a 0.8-μm filter and purified using a PD-10 column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA) by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 2 min to remove unbound dye.

DiO-labeled TDEs were subsequently preincubated with or without 3 μM of each peptide for 10 min before being added to the cells. After 3 h of incubation, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Following fixation, the cells were rinsed with PBS, and the nuclei were stained with DAPI. Coverslips were mounted onto glass slides for imaging using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems). To quantify the uptake of DiO-labeled TDEs, the cells were also analyzed by flow cytometry (MACSQuant VYB, Miltenyi Biotec), and the fluorescence intensity was assessed using FlowJo software (BD Life Science, Franklin Lakes, NJ).

In vitro CD8+ T lymphocyte exhaustion assay

CD8+ T cells were isolated from mouse spleens and stimulated as previously described to evaluate T-cell exhaustion induced by TDEs. TDEs were preincubated with or without 3 μM of each peptide for 10 min in PBS adjusted to pH 7.4 or 6.5 and then added to the stimulated CD8+ T cells. The cells were incubated for 48 h. To assess intracellular Ki-67 expression, 1 × 106 cells were collected by centrifugation at 350 × g for 5 min and permeabilized with chilled 70% ethanol. After a 1-h incubation at −20 °C, the cells were washed twice with PBS containing 1% FBS. The cells were then stained with anti-mouse Ki-67-PE/Dazzle 594 (clone no. 16A8; BioLegend) for 30 min at room temperature, after which they were washed twice and analyzed by flow cytometry (MACSQuant VYB; Miltenyi Biotec).

For intracellular GrB staining, isolated CD8+ T cells were incubated with TDE suspensions for 48 h, followed by treatment with monensin (1 μg/ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for an additional 6 h to promote intracellular accumulation. After treatment, 1 × 106 cells were harvested and blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody (clone no. 2.4G2; BioLegend) for 15 min on ice to prevent nonspecific Fc receptor binding. The cells were then fixed and permeabilized with Cytofix/Cytoperm buffer (BD Biosciences), followed by two washes with 1× Perm/Wash buffer (BD Biosciences). The cells were subsequently stained with anti-human/mouse Granzyme B-FITC (clone no. QA16A02; BioLegend) and analyzed by flow cytometry (Guava easyCyte; Millipore, Sigma).

To label representative exhausted T-cell markers, isolated CD8+ T cells were incubated with TDE suspensions for 48 h. After treatment, 1 × 105 cells were harvested and blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody (clone no. 2.4G2; Biolegend) for 15 min on ice to prevent nonspecific Fc receptor binding. The cells were then stained with PE/Dazzle 594-conjugated anti-CD233 (LAG-3) (catalog no. 125223; Biolegend) and FITC-conjugated anti-CD279 (PD-1) (catalog no. 135213; Biolegend) for 30 min at room temperature, after which they were washed twice and analyzed by flow cytometry (MACSQuant VYB; Miltenyi Biotec).

In vivo pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of PEG-SA-Pep

To investigate the in vivo behavior of AH-Pep, Cy5.5-labeled AH-Pep was prepared. Briefly, AH-Pep and Cy5.5-maleimide were dissolved in DMSO at concentrations of 25 mg/ml and 10 mg/ml, respectively. Each solution was added dropwise to 3 ml of PBS under stirring, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C. The reaction product was purified by dialysis against distilled water using a 2 kDa MWCO membrane and subsequently lyophilized to obtain the dried Cy5.5-labeled AH-Pep. To prepare Cy5.5-labeled PEG-SA-Pep or PEG-NA-Pep, Cy5.5-labeled AH-Pep was PEGylated according to a previously described procedure.

For pharmacokinetic analysis, B16F10 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with Cy5.5-labeled peptides at equivalent concentrations. Blood samples were collected at predetermined time points, and the Cy5.5 fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments) to determine the peptide circulation profiles.

To assess biodistribution, major organs, including the liver, lungs, kidneys, spleen, heart, and tumor, were harvested from the mice at defined time points. The tissues were homogenized using a homogenizer kit (InnogeneTech, Gimpo, Korea), and the fluorescence intensity was measured with a microplate reader to evaluate the distribution and clearance of the Cy5.5-labeled peptides.

In vivo evaluation of PD-L1 expression on TDEs in plasma

When the average tumor volume reached approximately 800 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups. To evaluate the dose-dependent effects of PEG-SA-Pep on plasma PD-L1 levels, each peptide formulation was intravenously administered twice. One day after the final injection, blood was collected, and the plasma was separated. Plasma-derived TDEs were isolated using a total exosome isolation kit (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. PD-L1 levels associated with TDEs were quantified using a mouse PD-L1 ELISA kit (no. DY1019-05; R&D Systems).

In vivo antitumor efficacy of PEG-SA-Pep

On day 12 after subcutaneous inoculation of B16F10 cells, tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to eight treatment groups: G1 (PBS), G2 (AH-Pep), G3 (PEG-NA-Pep), G4 (PEG-SA-Pep), G5 (aPD-1), G6 (AH-Pep+aPD-1), G7 (PEG-NA-Pep+aPD-1), and G8 (PEG-SA-Pep+aPD-1). The mice received intravenous injection of peptides at 15 mg/kg in 200 μl of PBS and intraperitoneal injection of aPD-1 at 5 mg/kg aPD-1 in a 100 μl volume. The tumor volumes and body weights were measured daily. The tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume (mm3) = 0.5 × length (mm) × width2 (mm2). Mice were euthanized according to animal care guidelines if the tumor diameter exceeded 20 mm, if the tumor burden reached 15% of body weight, or if body weight loss exceeded 20%. All the mice were euthanized by day 21, prior to reaching the maximum allowable tumor burden. The tumor, major organ, and blood samples were harvested for subsequent analyses.

In vivo evaluation of the antitumor immune response

To analyze CD8+ T cells within CD45+ TILs, tumor tissues were enzymatically dissociated using a tumor dissociation kit and a GentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec). The resulting suspensions were sequentially filtered through 70- and 40-μm cell strainers to obtain single-cell suspensions. TILs were enriched using CD45 Microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The isolated TILs were first blocked with Trustain FcXTM (catalog no. 101320, Biolegend) and subsequently stained with PE/Dazzle 594-conjugated anti-CD3 (catalog no. 100245, Biolegend), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-CD8a (catalog no. 100726, Biolegend), PE/Dazzle 594-conjugated anti-CD233 (LAG-3) (catalog no. 125223, Biolegend) and FITC-conjugated anti-CD279 (PD-1) (catalog no. 135213, Biolegend) for flow cytometric analysis.

To investigate the population of Tregs, which are characterized by the expression of CD25+Foxp3+ cells among CD4+ T cells, the cells were stained with Pacific blue-conjugated anti-CD25 (catalog no. 102021; Biolegend), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-Foxp3 (catalog no. 320011; Biolegend) and PE/Dazzle 594-conjugated anti-CD4 (catalog no. 100455; Biolegend).

To assess the antigen-specific proliferation of splenic T cells, the spleens were mechanically dissociated and passed through a 40 μm cell strainer to generate single-cell suspensions. Isolated splenocytes were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 12-well plates supplemented with RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% AA, 1× nonessential amino acid solution (catalog no. M7145; MilliporeSigma), and 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol (catalog no. 60-24-2; MilliporeSigma). Cells were stimulated with GP100, a melanoma-associated antigen. After 48 h, the splenic T cells were stained with PE/Dazzle 594-conjugated anti-Ki-67 (catalog no. 652409; Biolegend) to evaluate proliferation. All labeled cells from tumors and spleens were analyzed by flow cytometry (MACSQuant VYB, Miltenyi Biotec), and the data were processed with FlowJo™ software (BD Life Science).

Isolation and activation of adoptive T cells

For the polyclonal T-cell-adoptive transfer experiment, CT26 mouse colorectal cancer cells were subcutaneously inoculated into BALB/c mice. Seven days post-inoculation, tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLNs) were harvested from CT26 tumor-bearing mice in accordance with a previously described protocol.57 The TDLNs were mechanically dissociated to generate single-cell suspensions, and CD8+ T cells were isolated using magnetic microbeads, as described above. Prior to adoptive transfer, isolated CD8+ T cells were activated for 48 h with anti-CD3 (5 μg/ml) and anti-CD28 (2 μg/ml) antibodies in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 1× nonessential amino acid solution (MilliporeSigma, catalog no. M7145) and 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol (MilliporeSigma, catalog no. 60-24-2).

Tumor infiltration capacity of adoptive T cells

The CT26 tumor-bearing mouse models were established as described above. On day 5 postinoculation, the mice were randomly divided into four groups: G1 (PBS), G2 (AH-Pep), G3 (PEG-NA-Pep), and G4 (PEG-SA-Pep). Each peptide was administered intravenously at a dosage of 15 mg/kg in 200 μl of PBS. Activated CD8+ T cells were isolated from the TDLNs of CT26 tumor-bearing mice and labeled with Dil, a lipophilic dye used for membrane staining. On day 15, the tumor tissues were harvested, embedded, and cryosectioned. The tissue sections were fixed in ice-cold acetone and blocked with PBS containing 1% BSA for 1 h. For α-SMA staining, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-α-SMA-FITC. Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; TCS SP8 HyVolution; Leica Microsystems CMS GmbH, Germany).

In vivo antitumor efficacy of PEG-SA-Pep with ACT

To study ACT in a CT26 tumor-bearing mouse model, CT26 cancer cells were subcutaneously inoculated into BALB/c mice. On day 7 postinoculation, tumor-bearing mice were randomly assigned to six treatment groups: G1 (PBS), G2 (T cells + PBS), G3 (T cells + IL-12), G4 (AH-Pep + T cells + IL-12), G5 (PEG-NA-Pep + T cells + IL-12), and G6 (PEG-SA-Pep + T cells + IL-12). Peptides were administered intravenously at a dosage of 15 mg/kg in 200 μl of PBS. One day prior to adoptive transfer, the mice were lymphodepleted via intraperitoneal injection of cyclophosphamide at 4 mg/h. Activated CD8+ T cells isolated from the TDLN of CT26 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected at a dosage of 2 × 106 cells/h. Recombinant IL-12 was administered intratumorally at 60 ng/h. The tumor volume was measured daily until day 23 using the formula: volume (mm3) = 0.5 × length (mm) × width2 (mm2).

Statistics

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Detailed statistical methods and p values are provided in the figure legends. Significance levels are denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001; n.s indicates “not significant.” The number of experimental replicates (n) is indicated in the figure legends, with each sample representing an independent biological replicate or a separate experiment, as specified. The data were assumed to follow a normal distribution, although this was not formally evaluated. Both the data collection and the analysis were performed in a blinded manner to the experimental conditions. Investigators were blinded to group allocation during the experiments and outcome assessments.
