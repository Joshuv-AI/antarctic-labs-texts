Comparative Study on Effective Dose and Cancer Risk in Dual-tracer Hybrid Imaging – International Commission on Radiological Protection and Personalized Models
Authors: Mehrnoosh Karimipourfard, Hojjat Mahani, Sedigheh Sina, Masood Shobeiry, Chai Hong Yeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:Dual-tracer hybrid imaging is used for concurrent evaluation of two distinct molecular biomarkers, enhancing diagnostic capabilities. However, concerns about increased radiation exposure to patients arise with this technique. This study investigates the representative radiation doses and cancer risks associated with dual-tracer imaging and explores personalized dosimetry approaches.Materials and Methods:A literature-based protocol identification was performed to obtain the required dosimetric inputs. Dose coefficients were applied to convert tracer activities into effective dose estimates. Internal dose assessments using reference phantom models were compared with personalized dosimetry results obtained through GATE-based Monte Carlo simulations specific to dual-tracer positron emission tomography. In addition, complementary computed tomography (CT) imaging was performed, and cancer risks were quantified using the BEIR VII risk model.Results:Whole-body CT dose estimates closely matched those from the MIRDct tool, with a maximum discrepancy of 9.2%. Among the tracers evaluated, 201Tl resulted in the highest effective dose and cancer risk, while 81mKr and 13N-ammonia exhibited the lowest doses. Personalized dosimetry for dual-tracer imaging with ¹⁸F-fludeoxyglucose (FDG) and 68Ga-PSMA showed a maximum deviation of 5.6% from phantom-based models. The total effective dose reached up to 34 mSv.Conclusions:The study emphasizes the importance of addressing increased radiation doses and harmonizing dual-tracer hybrid imaging protocols. Personalized dosimetry enhances patient-specific risk assessments and safety by providing more accurate estimates of radiation exposure and associated cancer risks.

INTRODUCTION

Molecular imaging techniques such as single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) and positron emission tomography (PET) provide detailed information on physiological functions within the human body.[12] These functional images are crucial for diagnosing, staging, and monitoring treatment outcomes of numerous diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular disorders, neurological conditions, and renal pathologies.[13] Hybrid imaging, which combines functional imaging with anatomical modalities like computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging, enhances lesion localization and enables attenuation and scatter correction.[14] Conventional SPECT/CT and PET/CT imaging typically use a single tracer like 99mTc, 18F, and 177Lu to target specific molecular pathways.[5] However, certain physiological processes, such as perfusion and metabolism, are interdependent and require simultaneous acquisition of two molecular datasets under identical conditions.[6] Another example is the requirement for simultaneous measurement of amyloid-β and tau in Alzheimer’s disease.[7] Dual-tracer molecular imaging has emerged as a solution for such scenarios, enabling the simultaneous evaluation of two biomarkers.[8] Compared to sequential acquisitions, dual-tracer SPECT or PET imaging significantly reduces motion artifacts and image registration errors.[89] Nevertheless, the use of two radiotracers raises considerable concerns about increased radiation exposure to the patients.[9]

Over the years, several studies have explored the clinical benefits of dual-tracer imaging. Kadrmas et al.[10] demonstrated the feasibility of rapid dual-tracer PET for tumor imaging, highlighting the substantial informational value contained in dual-tracer PET data. In 2007, Ho et al.[11] showed that dual-tracer PET using 18F-fludeoxyglucose (FDG) and 11C-ACT improved detection of hepatocellular carcinoma metastasis compared to single-tracer 18F-FDG PET. In 2009, Berman et al.[12] successfully utilized a dual-tracer approach with 201Tl/99mTc for stress/rest myocardial perfusion SPECT. The Society of Nuclear Medicine and Molecular Imaging (SNMMI) published a guideline in 2012[13] recommending dual-tracer protocols such as 99mTc-sestamibi/99mTc-pertechnetate and 99mTc-sestamibi/123I for parathyroid imaging, associated with effective dose tables for common tracers. Lam et al. (2013)[14] highlighted the prognostic value of 99mTc-MAA/99mTc-sulfur colloid dual-tracer SPECT imaging. Liu et al. (2016)[15] conducted a dosimetric analysis on the radiation exposure associated with dual-tracer PET imaging using 18F-FDG and 11C-ACT, cautioning that the elevated radiation dose necessitates careful consideration before use. Chu et al. (2021)[16] performed a cost-effectiveness analysis and concluded that dual-tracer PET/CT, while clinically beneficial, may not be suitable for routine staging in all hepatocellular carcinoma patients. Evans et al.[17] achieved promising results using 18F-FDG and 18F-NaF scintigraphy for the identification of culprit carotid plaques and pathophysiology. More recently, Boudousq et al.[18] utilized 99mTc-sestamibi/123I-sodium iodide for SPECT/CT and planar subtraction imaging of primary hyperthyroidism.

Despite these advances, quantitative data on radiation exposure from dual-tracer imaging remain scarce. To date, only one study[15] has reported radiation dose estimates for dual-tracer 18F-FDG/11C-ACT PET/CT using the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) 103 tissue-weighting coefficients.[19] Therefore, the present study aims to address this gap by performing a targeted literature-based protocol identification of dual-tracer SPECT/CT and PET/CT imaging studies, extracting the reported tracer pairs and their corresponding average administered activities. The associated radiation doses were subsequently estimated using dose coefficients derived from the comprehensive dosimetric models developed by Andersson et al.[20] These models incorporate updated dosimetric values for 338 radiopharmaceuticals, grounded in the recommendations of ICRP Publication 106,[21] biokinetic data from ICRP Publication 128,[22] and relevant data from the literature. In addition, an experimental CT dose assessment was conducted using the tube current modulation (TCM) technique, which is common in dual-tracer imaging protocols. The total effective dose from both internal and external exposures was then computed, and the lifetime attributable risk (LAR) of cancer was estimated using the BEIR VII model.[23] Finally, personalized dosimetry for 18F-FDG/68Ga-PSMA PET was conducted using GATE Monte Carlo (MC) simulation to compare with ICRP phantom-based estimates. MATERIALS AND METHODS

Identification of dual-tracer imaging protocols

A targeted literature-based protocol identification was conducted to evaluate effective dose estimates in dual-tracer imaging protocols. Comprehensive full-text journal articles indexed in PubMed through the end of 2024 were identified using a range of keywords, including “dual-tracer”, “multi-tracer”, “dual-isotope”, “multi-isotope”, “multiple tracers”, “PET/CT”, “SPECT/CT”, and “molecular imaging”. Duplicate records were removed, and inclusion criteria were limited to human studies. Animal studies, phantom-based simulations, and computational-only models were excluded. Another crucial inclusion criterion was the availability of compound-specific dose conversion factors (i.e., from administered activity to effective dose) for both tracers used in each protocol. It is important to note that this study does not aim to conduct a traditional systematic review. Instead, the objective was to identify and utilize existing dual-tracer imaging protocols to calculate “representative” effective doses using ICRP models, experimental CT dose measurement, and GATE MC simulation.

Following the protocol identification, detailed information on radiopharmaceuticals and their respective administered activities was extracted. It is critical to note that accurate dose estimation necessitates precise identification of the radiopharmaceutical compounds, not merely the radioisotopes. Other protocol details, such as tracer administration timing, acquisition sequence, and image reconstruction settings, were considered irrelevant for effective dose calculation and were excluded, except for the number of CT scans, which directly impact the external exposure. In cases where studies reported a range of injection activities, the mean value was adopted for analysis. The dual-tracer imaging modalities included PET/PET, PET/SPECT, and SPECT/SPECT, each integrated with CT imaging. Computed tomography dose assessment

In this study, the radiation dose from low-dose CT was evaluated as part of the dual-tracer hybrid imaging protocol. Most dual-tracer imaging protocols incorporate TCM in whole-body CT imaging, enabling more personalized scans and reducing radiation exposure.[15] In order to assess radiation dose from the CT component in dual-tracer hybrid imaging, a phantom study was carried out using a standard computed tomography dose index (CTDI) body phantom and the SoliDose 400 pencil ionization chamber (RTI Electronics, USA). The phantom was scanned using a low-dose CT protocol on a Philips Ingenuity TF PET/CT scanner, which features a helical CT component with 128 channels and 4.0 cm axial coverage. where ETotal is the total effective dose, ECT represents the whole-body effective dose from CT imaging, while ETracer, i denotes the effective dose associated with tracer i. A key assumption in this model is that the presence of one tracer does not alter the biodistribution of the other when injected independently, particularly during simultaneous acquisitions.

To estimate internal doses from molecular imaging, rigorously derived dose coefficients for dual-tracer molecular imaging were used. These were based on models outlined by Andersson et al.[20] and ICRP Publication 128[22] and included radiopharmaceutical-specific coefficients from Tolvanen et al.,[58] Sandström et al.,[59] Sandgren et al.,[60] and Meyer et al.[61] for 11C-choline, 68Ga-DOTATATE, 68Ga-PSMA, and 68Ga-FAPI, respectively. These coefficients were derived using ICRP/International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU) computational phantoms and tissue weighting factors (wT) from ICRP 103 for both male and female patients. Effective doses were calculated by multiplying the radiotracer’s activity reported in the literature by the corresponding dose coefficients. For studies reporting activity concentrations in MBq/kg, average body weights of 80 kg for males and 64 kg for females were assumed.[62] Each tracer dose was computed individually and then summed with the CT dose to obtain the total effective dose. Finally, the LAR of cancer incidence and mortality per 100,000 individuals was estimated using the total effective dose and the “all cancers” row in tables 12D-1 and 12D-2 of the BEIR VII model for both genders.[23]

Personalized internal dosimetry in dual-tracer imaging

To determine personalized effective doses from dual-tracer imaging, an MC simulation was performed using the GATE platform. For this purpose, whole-body unenhanced PET/CT scans with both 370 MBq 18F-FDG and 200 MBq 68Ga-PSMA tracers were acquired from a 55-year-old male patient (75.0 kg) using the Philips Ingenuity TF 64-slice PET/CT scanner equipped with LYSO crystals and an 18-cm axial field-of-view (FOV). Data acquisition times varied between 30 and 300 s per bed position, with 18F-FDG scans conducted at 20, 60, and 90 min post-injection and 68Ga-PSMA scans at 5, 30, and 90 min post-injection to estimate cumulative activity [Figure 2]. Reconstructed attenuation-corrected PET images had a matrix size of 144 × 144 with a voxel resolution of 4 mm × 4 mm × 4 mm, while CT images were acquired at a resolution of 512 × 512 × 320.

Fused 18F-fludeoxyglucose (left) and 68Ga-PSMA (right) positron emission tomography/computed tomography scans of the patient

For MC simulation, a voxelized phantom and corresponding voxelized source for the patient were created using separate PET data from each tracer and CT images. All CT images were resampled to a 144 × 144 matrix to match the PET image dimensions. An experienced nuclear medicine physician segmented the trunk organs, muscles, and bones on each slice. Motion-corrected PET-CT images were registered using the B-spline registration method, with PET and CT images treated as fixed and moving images, respectively. The B-spline grid size was set to 3 × 3 × 3 per axis to align subdivisions within the fixed image space. Modeling and simulation were conducted using the GATE v8.1 MC framework.[63646566] The dose actor was used to record the voxel dose rate, while a MATLAB script generated the S-value map using organ-labeled CT images. Statistical uncertainties of dose rates were kept below 0.1% at the voxel level. Using the MIRD schema,[34] internal organ doses were calculated by multiplying the time-integrated activity (TIA) by the absorbed dose rate per unit TIA (i.e., S-value), obtained from GATE MC modeling. Each organ dose was then adjusted by a dose conversion factor and summed to estimate the patient’s effective dose.

DISCUSSION

This study evaluates representative effective radiation doses associated with dual-tracer hybrid imaging by aggregating doses from both the CT component and the emission tomography units. As anticipated and corroborated by Table 3 and Figure 3, findings confirm the expected linear relationship between tube current and radiation dose in CT imaging. Higher tube currents are associated with thicker body sections and vice versa. The whole-body TCM CT imaging results in an effective dose of approximately 11.5 mSv/scan. Liu et al.[15] reported an effective dose of 21.20 mSv for two non-contrast whole-body CT scans using the VirtualDose software, which is similar to our experimental result of 23 mSv (2 × 11.5 mSv/scan), with a difference of about 7.8%. Our findings align with the MIRDct-calculated effective dose of 10.4 mSv (9.2% difference) for the ICRP reference male adult phantom (arms up). These results support the use of 11.5 mSv/scan as a representative dose for typical whole-body dual-tracer CT protocols. Nevertheless, diverse CT imaging protocols can result in varying effective doses, underscoring the necessity for individualized internal and external dosimetry assessments in dual-tracer imaging.

Table 4 includes a study (no. 10) where 18F-FDG, typically used for PET scanning, was utilized in SPECT imaging. In study no. 10, only one of the two back-to-back annihilation photons was captured using a high-energy collimated SPECT camera, a method employed due to the high cost of PET scanners. Referring to Table 4, it is evident that the majority of the investigated dual-tracer imaging studies are focused on SPECT/SPECT imaging. This trend is primarily attributable to the wider availability of dose coefficients for SPECT tracers and, more importantly, to the relatively simpler signal separation methodologies available for SPECT tracer pairs compared with those used in PET-based dual-tracer imaging. In SPECT/SPECT imaging, 201Tl produced the highest radiation doses compared to other SPECT radiopharmaceuticals. Consequently, optimizing protocols that use 201Tl in cardiac/brain dual-tracer imaging appears necessary. Although the use of 201Tl has declined over time due to its long half-life and higher radiation exposure associated with its low-energy emissions, it remains valuable in specific contexts due to its linear correlation with myocardial blood flow compared to 99 mTc-labeled tracers.[6768] In contrast, 123I SPECT results in the lowest radiation doses among SPECT tracers. Likewise, 13N-ammonia and 11C-labeled tracers yield the lowest radiation doses in PET/PET imaging compared to 18F-FDG, underscoring their potential for dual-tracer PET imaging from a radiation dosimetry standpoint.

In PET/SPECT imaging [Table 4], the radiation dose from 99 mTc-sestamibi in study no. 39 was approximately 15.5 times higher than that from 99 mTc-DSMA in study no. 41 (3.94 vs. 0.26 mSv), primarily due to differences in injected activity [Table 1]. Similar discrepancies were observed in SPECT/SPECT and PET/PET imaging [Table 4]. For instance, the effective doses from 18F-FDG ranged from 8.74 mSv (study no. 34) to 0.41 mSv (study no. 30). These findings underscore the need for harmonizing dual-tracer imaging protocols and establishing local diagnostic reference levels. Notably, some dual-tracer imaging protocols do not involve CT scans, as demonstrated in study no. 12.

For dual-tracer PET imaging, Liu et al.[15] estimated radiation doses for 18F-FDG and 11C-ACT using OLINDA/EXM software and ICRP 106 biokinetic models. The maximum variation between their results and ours was <25%, which is acceptable given their use of personalized dosimetry versus our reference phantom approach. For females, the LAR of cancer incidence and mortality ranged from 72 to 559 and 64–259 per 100,000 persons, respectively [Figure 4]. The BEIR VII model attributes the higher LAR of cancer incidence and mortality in females compared to males primarily due to their increased sensitivity to radiation and longer life expectancy, which result in distinct dose–response relationships. As age increases, the LAR of cancer incidence and mortality decreases. This can be attributed to several factors: (1) a shorter remaining lifespan, (2) decreased cellular proliferation, (3) higher competing mortality risks, and (4) age-related biological changes.

In our dual-tracer 18F-FDG and 68Ga-PSMA PET study, the ICRP-based effective dose estimate (7.02 mSv) was 5.6% higher than the patient-specific MC estimate (6.68 mSv). A similar pattern was noted for 68Ga-PSMA. These differences reflect the limitations of using a reference phantom, which does not account for patient-specific anatomy or tracer kinetics. Although the observed differences (≤8.0%) are within clinically acceptable limits, this finding highlights the value of personalized dosimetry for protocol optimization. Nevertheless, the MC-based method is computationally intensive and requires multiple time-point PET/CT scans, posing significant practical challenges. Emerging deep learning models offer promising solutions by accelerating simulations and generating late-time-frame images while maintaining accuracy.[6970]

This study is the first to comprehensively evaluate the total effective dose in dual-tracer hybrid imaging. The findings reveal a significant increase in radiation dose for dual-tracer imaging compared to single-tracer protocols. In our previous study,[2] the radiation dose from a single-tracer 18F-FDG PET was found to range from 5.72 to 6.04 mSv, while Li et al.[71] reported a dose of 6.1 mSv. This contrasts with the higher average dose of 11.04 mSv observed for the dual-tracer 18F-FDG/68Ga-PSMA PET. Despite its clinical benefits, dual-tracer imaging results in a total effective dose of up to 34 mSv for patients. There is also substantial variability in molecular imaging doses, along with the high radiation exposure, primarily due to differences in dual-tracer imaging protocols. The complexity increases further when multiple tracers are used, reinforcing the need for harmonized protocols. It is important to remember that dual-tracer imaging can be performed sequentially or simultaneously. In sequential acquisitions, each tracer imaging session is paired with its own CT scan, resulting in two separate CT radiation doses contributing to the increased total effective dose.

The ICRP model, however, comes with some limitations: (1) lack of accuracy as not being personalized, (2) not including all radiopharmaceuticals currently used in clinical settings, and (3) not providing external dose conversion factors (to account for CT dosimetry). Thus, for fast internal dosimetry with acceptable accuracy, the ICRP model is recommended. In contrast, the personalized model is suggested for newly developed tracers and high accuracy. Both ICRP and personalized methodologies indicate that high radiation exposure in dual-tracer hybrid imaging, with an increasing number of tracers, leads to a higher radiation dose. One potential approach is the use of long axial FOV (or total-body) PET or high-sensitivity SPECT scanners, which can significantly reduce the radiation dose while maintaining image quality.[72] In addition, machine and deep learning models offer the possibility of improving image quality even at very low radiation doses for PET, SPECT, or CT scans.[69707172] Combining these advanced algorithms with high-sensitivity PET or SPECT cameras could effectively address the radiation dose challenges in dual-tracer imaging protocols.

CONCLUSIONS

This study presents the first comprehensive evaluation of representative effective doses in dual-tracer hybrid imaging, encompassing a wide range of modalities and radiopharmaceutical combinations used in clinical practice. The findings highlight three critical issues: (1) considerable variability in dual-tracer imaging protocols, particularly in terms of administered activities and the number of CT scans performed; (2) elevated radiation exposure levels, with total effective dose reaching 34 mSv in certain protocols; and (3) the importance of personalized internal dosimetry for accurate estimation of tracer-specific radiation doses. These results underscore the urgent need for harmonizing dual-tracer imaging protocols and adopting advanced scanners with higher sensitivities. The integration of machine learning and deep learning approaches offers a promising avenue to optimize image quality while minimizing radiation dose. Currently available dose conversion factors (coefficients) apply to a broad array of radiopharmaceuticals, facilitating effective dose estimation across diverse tracer combinations and imaging modalities. Future research should prioritize expanding these dose coefficients to include newly developed radiotracers and evaluating effective doses for multitracer applications. Such efforts will be essential for improving patient safety and enhancing the clinical utility of dual-tracer molecular imaging.
