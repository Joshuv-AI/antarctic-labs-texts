Marination with espresso, filter, and Turkish coffee: A novel approach to improving the tenderness of spent hen meat
Authors: Nazik Meziyet Dilek, Ali Samet Babaoglu, Halime Alp, Kubra Unal, Mustafa Karakaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explored the use of espresso, filter, and Turkish coffee as natural meat tenderizers, chosen for their rich bioactive compounds, antioxidant capacity, acidic and phenolic content, which can enhance tenderness and sensory quality of meat. The effects of coffee marination on the physicochemical, microstructural, textural and sensory properties of spent hen breast meat were evaluated. Color parameters (L*, a*, b*) were significantly influenced by coffee type (P < 0.05), whereas cooking loss, drip loss, water-holding capacity, and marinade absorption were unaffected (P > 0.05). SEM analysis showed that espresso caused the greatest disruption of muscle fibers, while Turkish coffee induced the least damage. Texture analysis revealed a pronounced tenderizing effect of espresso, likely due to its unique physicochemical characteristics, suggesting that coffee marination could serve as a practical and functional strategy to improve meat quality. The panelists found that the filter coffee group had the greatest overall acceptance scores of any treatment.Keywords: Coffee marination, SEM, Spent hen meat, Meat quality, Tenderization

Introduction

The expansion of the poultry industry has resulted in a significant increase in the number of spent chickens (Kalaikannan et al., 2007). The term “spent chicken” refers to hens that have reached the end of their egg-laying capacity (Polizer Rocha et al., 2019). Compared to broiler meat, spent hen meat exhibits notable differences in sensory qualities, including odour (US: odor), color, flavor, and texture, making it less desirable for human consumption and more commonly utilized in animal feed production (Navid et al., 2011). Despite the fact that their meat is tougher, primarily due to the higher collagen content that develops over multiple laying cycles (Archile-Contreras et al., 2011)-spent chickens remain a valuable source of protein and omega-3 fatty acids (Lee et al., 2003).

When it comes to meat product quality, tenderness is the most crucial sensory factor (Xiong et al., 2020). Numerous techniques, such as blade tenderization or vacuum mixing (Patil et al., 2024), plant-based enzymes (Botinestean et al., 2018), and marinating, can be employed to soften and reduce the harshness of leftover chicken meat.

Marination is a process applied not only to impart a more tender and crisp texture to meat but also to enrich its flavor and aroma. In the marination process, meat pieces are immersed in a liquid mixture known as the marinade, which contains, in proportions determined by the recipe, ingredients such as salt, vegetable oil, organic acids, vinegar, phosphates, and spices. Through this process, the product can acquire a richer profile in terms of flavor and aroma components, along with a crisper texture. Marination can be carried out by directly immersing the meat pieces into the marinade or through methods such as tumbling and injection (Ehsanur Rahman et al., 2023).

Unlike industrial practices, food and beverage establishments operating in the field of gastronomy may use, in addition to conventional marinade ingredients, special sauces, plant-based products, or other components specific to certain geographical regions. Accordingly, alternative marinade formulations unique to a particular locale or culture can be encountered in world cuisines. For instance, teriyaki sauce-based marinades are used in Japan for marinating fish and meat, while barbecue sauce is employed in marination processes in the cuisines of Central and North America (Nerurkar et al., 1999). Marinating meat and meat products with coffee-based marinade solutions is also among the novel methods recently adopted in the field of gastronomy (Uzun, 2022). Vu et al. (2023) stated that coffee is used to marinate foods such as braised fish and barbecued ribs, and its use is expected to increase in the future as the coffee industry moves toward intensive product research and development.

Coffee, obtained by roasting and grinding the seeds of plants belonging to the genus Coffea of the family Rubiaceae and then brewing them with water, has more than 100 varieties (Farah and dos Santos, 2015). Among the coffee species, Coffea arabica Linn. (arabica), Coffea canephora Pierre ex Froehner (robusta), and Coffea liberica are of commercial importance (Ferreira et al., 2019). Of these, C. arabica is known for having the highest drinking quality among coffee types, although it is not as strong or resistant as C. canephora (Clifford, 2012). Roasting is a critical process in coffee production, profoundly influencing the final flavor profile. Recent empirical evidence indicates that both roast degree (color) and roasting time significantly affect sensory characteristics, with color being the stronger predictor of flavor outcomes. Specifically, darker roasts tend to exhibit higher bitterness and lower acidity, fruitiness, and sweetness, while the development time—defined as the period after the first crack—has a more pronounced effect on flavor than the time to first crack (Münchow et al., 2020). These findings highlight the importance of precisely controlling roasting parameters to achieve desired coffee flavor profiles.

Coffee is a rich source of bioactive compounds such as phenolic acids (caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, p-coumaric acid, ferulic acid, gallic acid, vanillic acid, cinnamic acid), phenolic acid esters, caffeine, trigonelline, cafestol, and kahweol (Komes and Bušić, 2014). The process of transferring the chemical content of ground coffee particles into water to prepare the beverage is defined as brewing, and coffee extraction is influenced by factors such as particle size, solid-to-water ratio, contact surface area, and water temperature (Pictet, 1987). Depending on the desired beverage type, brewing is carried out using one of the boiling, extraction, or pressure methods (Clarke and Vitzthum, 2008). Coffee brewing preferences vary among cultures; for instance, filtered coffee is popular in America, concentrated espresso is preferred in Sicily and Italy (Clarke and Vitzthum, 2008), while Turkish coffee is more commonly consumed in Turkey (Özgür, 2012).

Although there is no single definitive method for brewing Turkish coffee, according to the Turkish Coffee Culture and Research Association, for one cup of Turkish coffee, 1 cup (75 mL) of cold water and 7 g of ground coffee are placed in a cezve, sugar is added if desired, and the mixture is stirred until a homogeneous consistency is achieved before being heated. It is then brewed at 80–95°C (Yönet Eren and Ceyhun Sezgin, 2018). Espresso is obtained by compressing finely ground coffee and passing high-pressure hot water vapor through it in a porous environment, resulting in a coffee that is denser and stronger in flavor than filtered coffee. To achieve the desired taste in a standard 30 mL espresso, 7.5–8.0 g of coffee is used. The water vapor pressure should be 9 bar, the water temperature 85–94°C, and the brewing process should be completed within 15–27 seconds (Caprioli et al., 2015). Filter coffee, an extraction method, is obtained by steeping ground coffee beans in hot water for a certain period, followed by the separation of solids from the liquid. Compared to coffee prepared by boiling (e.g., Turkish coffee), it has a more acidic and pronounced aroma. For consumption readiness, coffee should be extracted at 90°C for 7–9 min, then filtered, and its temperature should be maintained at 83°C (Sánchez-González et al., 2005).

In this study, coffee types obtained using different brewing methods were used in the marination of spent hen meat to improve various physicochemical and textural properties. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have been reported in the literature on the marination of spent hen meat in coffee-based marination solutions. Therefore, the results obtained from this study are expected to make a significant contribution to the literature.

Materials and methods

Materials

Espresso, filter and Turkish coffee and salt were purchased from a market in Konya, Turkey. A total of breast muscles obtained from 15 spent chickens were used. (72 weeks old, same breed and feeding conditions) were obtained from a local farmer in Konya. Following slaughter, the carcasses were promptly and safely taken in plastic bags to the laboratory, where they were refrigerated for no more than 30 min. Before being used, the breast portions of every chicken were physically separated from the carcasses and kept in a refrigerator at 4°C for 16 hours. Analyses were conducted on post-mortem samples at 16 hours. Subsequently, the fresh breast meat measuring 60 mm by 30 mm by 20 mm and weighing 20.00 ± 0.50 g in the direction of the muscle fibers was sliced into pieces. The breast meat from 15 spent chickens was cut into two equal pieces and then randomly distributed into 4 groups, with each group containing 7 pieces of breast meat (28 pieces of breast meat in total) (each group was marinated with a different marinade).

Quantification of phenolic compounds via high-performance liquid chromatography with diode-array detection (HPLC-DAD)

In this study, phenolic compound reference standards were characterized under two different analytical conditions. The quantitative determination of these compounds was carried out using external calibration curves constructed from six successive dilutions (25, 50, 75, 100, 200, and 300 μg/mL) prepared at accurately defined concentrations for each reference material. Following the preparation of these calibration solutions, all calibration standards were analyzed using an HPLC-DAD system under instrumental parameters identical to those applied to the samples to be examined.

Analytical Condition 1: For the determination of fourteen phenolic compounds listed in Table 1, an ACE 5 C18 reversed-phase column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 µm) was employed. The mobile phase consisted of a binary gradient system comprising acetonitrile (A) and 1.5 % (w/w) acetic acid solution (B). The initial composition was set to 15 % A and 85 % B, followed by a linear gradient to 40 % A and 60 % B over a total run time of 29 min. The system configuration included a 1260 DAD WR detector (monitoring at 250, 270, and 320 nm), a 1260 Quaternary Pump (flow rate of 0.7 mL/min), a 1260 Autosampler (injection volume of 10 μL), and a G7116A thermostatted column compartment maintained at 35°C.

Marinades and marination process

Individual breast portions were put in plastic containers. For the control group (C), the marinade solution was prepared using 250 mL of distilled water containing 1 % NaCl. For the treatment groups, each marinade contained 1 % NaCl and was prepared using 250 mL of espresso coffee (1:2) for group E, 250 mL of filter coffee (1:15) for group F, and 250 mL of Turkish coffee (1:10) for group T. Subsequently, the breast meat samples assigned to each group were marinated at 4°C for 24 hours.

pH

The pH values of the marinated meat samples, coffee types and marinades were measured using a pH meter (Testo 205 pH-Temperature-Messgerat, AG Postfach 1140, 79849, Lenzkirch) (Lambooij et al., 1999).

Color

The color of the surfaces of the spent hen meat samples was measured using a chromometer (CR-400 Minolta Co, Osaka, Japan). The L* (lightness), a* (redness), and b* (yellowness) values were created using data from the International Commission on Illumination CIELab (Commision Internationele de I'E Clairage), which is based on three-dimensional color measurement.

Cooking loss

The cooking loss (CL) values of samples in each treatment group were ascertained in accordance with the methods outlined by Unal et al. (2022) with some modifications. Approximately fifteen grams of weighted meat samples were cooked for 20 min at 200°C in an oven. The cooking loss values (%) were calculated using the formula below:

CL (%): (uncooked meat weight-cooked meat weight)/(uncooked meat weight) × 100

Drip loss

The Hu et al. (2008) approach was utilized to calculate the drip losses of the meats used in the investigation. After being chopped to around 2 × 3 × 5 cm in size, the meat samples were weighed individually, put in drying papers, put in polyethylene bags, and kept in the refrigerator (4 °C) for 24 h. At the end of this time, the drip losses (%) of each spent hen meats were computed by taking into consideration the samples' weight loss.

Water holding capacity

The water holding capacity of the samples in each group was determined using the methodology, with a few minor adjustments, outlined by Gómez‐Guillén et al. (2000). After being weighed, the 2 g of small-cut spent hen meat sample (W1) was put in a tube and covered with filter paper. After that, the tubes were centrifuged for 10 min at 4°C using a Nuve, NF-800-R Model, Türkiye, at 7245 g force. Following the determination of the weight of the wet filter paper (W2), the filter sheets were dried at 60°C until they reached a constant weight (W3).

Marinade absorption

According to Li et al. (2020), the marinade absorption was calculated by dividing the difference between the marinated and unmarinated weights by the nonmarinated weight.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

A scanning electron microscope (EVO LS 10, ZEISS, UK) was used to analyze samples of leftover spent hen meats for microstructural characteristics. Banerjee et al. (2017) employed sample fixation, ethanol dehydration, freeze-drying, and spray coating techniques to get the samples ready for analysis. The raw samples were loaded into the SEM apparatus and photographed after being gold-plated to improve conductivity and image quality using a gold plating machine (Cressington Sputter Coater, 7002, 7002-220, USA).

Texture properties

The samples were cooked for 20 min at 200°C in an oven. Then, all samples were allowed to cool to room temperature for analysis.

The textural characteristics (hardness, springiness, cohesiveness and chewiness) of cooked meat samples were measured using a texture instrument (TA-HD Plus Texture Analyzer, UK) equipped with a 50 kg load cell in order to analyze the texture profile. Samples measuring 3 × 3 × 1 cm were sliced using a cylindrical probe with a 36 mm diameter (SMS P/36, 36 mm diameter, TA.XT Plus Stable Micro Systems Ltd.) (Herrero et al., 2007).

The mean force (N) required to cut off only the breast of chicken meat is measured using the Meullenet-Owens Razor Shear (MORS) test. To ascertain the textural properties of samples, a texture analyzer (TA-HD Plus Texture Analyser, UK) fitted with a 50 kg load cell was employed in conjunction with a MORS knife. The MORS blade positions of the breast meats were selected in accordance with the guidelines provided by Cavitt et al. (2005).

Sensory properties

Sensory evaluations of samples were carried out according to Yıldız-Turp and Serdaroglu (2010) methods with some modifications by a sensory panel consisting of 20 semi-trained and pre-informed panelists from Selçuk University, Department of Food Engineering. The samples were served after cooking in the airfryer at 200°C for 20 min. Randomly selected 3-digit numbers were used to code the samples. Cooked samples were served to the panelists with water and bread to be consumed between samples.

Statistical analyses

The MINITAB® release 16.0 software was used to perform an Analysis of Variance on the gathered data, which were shown as tables prepared in compliance with the experimental design. The Tukey Multiple Comparison Test was used to assess the significance of the group mean differences. Two replications and three duplicate samples of each parameter were analyzed.

Results and discussion

Phenolic compounds of coffee samples

Table 2 showed the phenolic compounds contained in different coffee types. In the E group samples, the compound detected at the highest concentration was syringic acid (454.7 mg/L), followed by (+)-catechin (267.4 mg/L) and l-ascorbic acid (132.4 mg/L). Among the phenolic acids, chlorogenic acid was found at 40.3 mg/L, while gallic acid (8.82 mg/L), trans-caffeic acid (1.68 mg/L), and rosmarinic acid (1.41 mg/L) were present at lower levels. In terms of flavonoid profile, (–)-epicatechin was measured at 34.1 mg/L, whereas other flavonoids such as rutin, myricetin, quercetin, and apigenin were not detected. Likewise, p-coumaric acid, resveratrol, oleuropein, the anthocyanin derivative cyanidin chloride, as well as hesperetin, kaempferol, baicalein, and chrysin were not identified in this group. Compared to the E group, the F group samples exhibited generally higher phenolic content. The concentration of syringic acid increased to 725.3 mg/L, and (+)-catechin rose to 418.9 mg/L. The amount of l-ascorbic acid was measured at 234.05 mg/L, significantly higher than that in the E group. Chlorogenic acid (67.47 mg/L), gallic acid (14.7 mg/L), and pyrogallol (5.9 mg/L) also showed increases. Notably, quercetin was detected for the first time in this group at a concentration of 4.9 mg/L. However, the (–)-epicatechin level decreased to 23.6 mg/L, and 3,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid was not detected. Other flavonoids and several phenolic compounds also gave negative results in the F group. The T group samples contained the highest l-ascorbic acid concentration (430.3 mg/L) among all three groups, with a statistically significant difference. The syringic acid level was measured at 375.5 mg/L, lower than in the E and F groups. In this group, pyrogallol concentration dramatically increased to 54.4 mg/L. Trans-caffeic acid (19.3 mg/L), vanillic acid (13.32 mg/L), and gallic acid (23.5 mg/L) also reached their highest levels in the T group. Regarding flavonoids, (+)-catechin concentration decreased to 8.0 mg/L, while quercetin was found at 4.86 mg/L. Resveratrol (1.47 mg/L) and baicalein (15.57 mg/L) were detected only in the T group, reflecting its unique phenolic composition. Marked differences in phenolic composition were observed among the three groups. The F group exhibited the highest total phenolic content overall (particularly rich in syringic acid and catechin), whereas the T group was dominant in ascorbic acid and certain phenolic acids. The E group, although showing relatively lower concentrations, presented a more balanced profile. The detection of bioactive compounds such as resveratrol and baicalein exclusively in the T group suggests that processing conditions or raw material variations significantly influence the metabolite profile. The catechin/epicatechin ratio varied dramatically between groups, which may indicate differences in oxidative conditions or enzymatic activity. Overall, it can be concluded that each group possesses distinct bioactive potentials, offering selective applicability for specific functional uses.

pH

In the present study, the pH value of the spent hen meat was found to have 5.79. Furthermore, the pH values of espresso, filter and Turkish coffee used in the marination of spent hen meat were 5.18, 5.68 and 5.65, respectively. Diviš et al. (2019) found that the pH values of coffee beans roasted at a medium level ranged from 5.65 to 5.93. Following the marinating process, the average pH values of the marinade samples were measured as 6.18, 5.94, 6.04 and 6.01 for the control, espresso, filter and Turkish coffee groups, respectively. Sengun et al. (2021) stated that the pH values of marination liquids related to compounds used in marination.

As indicated in Table 3, the pH values of the spent hen meat were found to be significantly affected (P < 0.05) by the marinating process using espresso, filter and Turkish coffee. The pH values of the espresso coffee samples were found to be lower in comparison to the other groups, which exhibited statistically similar pH values. This finding can be attributed to the lower pH value of espresso coffee compared to that of filter and Turkish coffee used in the marinade. Kim et al. (2020) reported that the pH value of samgyetang samples significantly decreased as the concentration of soy sauce used in the marination increased. Sengun et al. (2021) observed no significant differences in the pH values of meat samples marinated with fruit vinegars (P > 0.05). Additionally, Suradi et al. (2019) determined no significant differences in the pH values of duck meat marinated with green tea extracts at different concentrations. Karatepe et al. (2025) revealed that the low pH value of acidic rhubarb juice (Rheum ribes L.) reduced the pH level of chicken breast meat. The differences between the studies could be explained by the types of meat, the marination formulations and the marination conditions.

The observed decrease in pH values in the coffee-marinated samples can influence the structural integrity of muscle proteins and connective tissue. Acidic marinades promote the protonation of amino acid side chains, leading to unfolding and partial denaturation of myofibrillar proteins, which in turn enhances water retention and tenderness (Sharedeh et al., 2015). Moreover, the reduction in pH facilitates collagen solubilisation by disrupting the cross-links within collagen fibrils, thereby improving meat tenderness (Ulloa-Saavedra et al., 2024). In the present study, the slightly lower pH observed in the espresso-treated group may have contributed to greater collagen destabilisation and a more open muscle matrix structure. Additionally, coffee polyphenols, such as chlorogenic acids, can interact with muscle proteins through hydrogen bonding or covalent linkages, potentially altering protein conformation and influencing the overall meat matrix interactions (Zhang et al., 2024). These effects collectively explain how coffee acidity and phenolic compounds may modulate meat quality beyond simple pH changes.

Color properties

As can be seen in Table 3, the effects of marination with different coffee types on the color properties of spent hen meat were statistically significant (P < 0.05). Control samples marinated with water (C) showed elevated L* values in comparison to the other samples. Conversely, the Turkish coffee exhibited a significantly lowest L* value (P < 0.05). The espresso and filter coffee samples demonstrated an intermediate level of L* values, showing no significant variation from the other samples. In relation to the values of the a* parameter, the process of marinating spent hen meat in filter coffee resulted in a higher a* value when compared to the values obtained from the espresso and Turkish coffee groups. Control samples displayed lower a* values in comparison to the other samples. The increase in redness values of the coffee-marinated samples could be related to the formation of melanoidins during the roasting of coffee beans. At temperatures exceeding 100°C, beans begin to brown due to reactions such as the Maillard reaction, the browning reaction and the Strecker decomposition, resulting in the formation of various substances, including melanoidins (Bicho et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2022). Additionally, the decrease in L* values and increase in a* values could be associated with the oxidative reaction of the coffee polyphenols taken up by the spent hen meat through the marinating process (Mau et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2024).

These compounds may interact with meat proteins and pigments during marination, influencing colour development and stability (Estevez, 2011; Lund et al., 2011). Melanoidins can form complexes with myofibrillar proteins, reducing light reflectance and resulting in lower L* values, while polyphenols may bind to heme iron or myoglobin, enhancing redness (a*) and protecting pigments from oxidation (Jongberg et al., 2013). Such interactions contribute to the overall colour stability and visual appeal of the marinated meat

The differences in the effects of different types of coffee on the L* and a* values of the samples are possibly attributable to variations in particle size, roasting degree or the bioactive components of the coffee types.

Significant and similar increases in b* values were observed in samples marinated with different coffee compared to the control sample (P < 0.05). These observations are in accordance with Kim et al. (2020) who reported that L* values were decreased, a* and b* values were increased in samgyetang samples marinated with soy sauce. Similarly, Zhang et al. (2023) established that adding black tea to marinated beef samples resulted in a decrease in L* values and an increase in a* values. Wang et al. (2018) found that the color of chicken wings depends on the type of tea used for marination. Similar L* values were also observed by Onopiuk et al. (2021) for pork meat marinated with natural plant extracts, varying between 40.14 and 46.84.

Cooking loss (%), drip loss (%), water holding capacity (%) and marinade absorption (%)

In terms of cooking loss, drip loss, water holding capacity and marinade absorption values, the marination with espresso, filter and Turkish coffee had no significant effect on these values (P > 0.05) (Table 4). Cooking losses ranged between 32.10 % and 34.14 %, which is consistent with the results of Söbeli and Kayaardı (2025), who found values between 32.65 % and 26.35 % in the chicken meat samples marinated with green tea and thyme extracts. Similarly to our results, Caliskan et al. (2023) informed that the cooking loss values of chicken meat marinated with white and oolong tea were not statistically significant. Sengun et al. (2021) also reported that marinating meat in different vinegars had no statistically significant effect on its cooking yield or marination absorption values (P > 0.05). Furthermore, Fencioglu et al. (2022) found that marinating beef in different vinegar types had no significant effect on its cooking loss values. Our results are in accordance with those of Suradi et al. (2019), who observed that water holding capacity and cooking loss values of duck meat marinated with different levels of green tea extract were not significantly different (P > 0.05). Gök and Bor (2016) stated that cooking loss values increased with marination, and that cooking loss was higher with red grape juice marination than with black carrot juice marination in turkey breast meat samples. Karatepe et al. (2025) also revealed that cooking and drip losses increased, while water holding capacity decreased, in chicken breast fillets marinated with rhubarb juice (Rheum ribes L.). Ismail et al. (2018) reported that marinating beef using 20 % and 60 % red and black tea had no significant effect on cooking loss values. The different results of the study on meat marination could be related to the composition of the marinade, the type of meat, the marination process, and other conditions.

SEM results

As shown in Fig 1, the observed scanning electron microscope (SEM) images clearly indicate that marination with different kinds of coffee had an effect. A tighter/compact structure image was observed in the samples marinated with water (free of coffee types – Control). Regarding the effect of marination process, treatment of coffee types generally occured deteriorations in the structure of the spent hen meats. The E treatment had a significant effect on the muscle of samples due to the broken and separated fibers. The lowest deformation of the muscle fibres was monitored in T group among coffee types. Samples treated with the filter coffee have similar irregular muscle fibres. However, it was seen that espresso coffee (E) and filter coffee (F) were more effective in breaking the inter-fibrillar bonds. This situation could be attributed to polyphenols of coffee types can affect through collagen solubilization and tenderization. Xu et al. (2023) claimed that phenolic compounds interacted with meat proteins, changed the microstructure in the marinaded meat. As seen as Table 2, the highest syringic acid and chloragenic acid values were found in espresso and filter coffee samples. Some researchers reported similar deformations of meat structure in marinated samples compared to the unmarinated group (Çimen et al., 2024; Dilek et al., 2023; Unal et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025).

In this study, the effects of marination with different coffee types such as Turkish, espresso and filter coffee on the textural properties of spent hen meat were investigated using Texture Profile Analysis (TPA) and Meullenet-Owens Razor Shear (MORS) tests. The obtained data, summarized in Table 5, revealed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) in the textural parameters of the marinated spent hen meat, particularly in hardness, shear force, and shear energy.

In terms of TPA parameters, hardness values varied significantly among the groups (P < 0.05). Control samples marinated with water (C) and those treated with Turkish coffee (T) exhibited similarly high hardness values. In contrast, the espresso group (E) showed a significantly lower value, while the filter coffee group (F) presented an intermediate level that was not significantly different from the other treatments. This suggests that marination with espresso coffee was more effective in reducing meat hardness, possibly due to intrinsic compositional features that may have influenced proteolysis and connective tissue softening. On the other hand, the comparable hardness values of the Turkish coffee and control groups imply a limited tenderizing effect, possibly related to differences in particle size, roasting degree, or bioactive compound content specific to Turkish coffee.

No significant differences were observed in springiness, cohesiveness, or chewiness values among the groups (P > 0.05), indicating that the type of coffee used for marination did not substantially influence these specific textural attributes. Nevertheless, numerical trends pointed to the Turkish coffee group having slightly higher springiness and chewiness values, which may reflect subtle effects on meat elasticity and mastication energy, albeit not statistically confirmed.

MORS analysis further supported the TPA findings. Significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed in both maximum shear force (MORSF) and shear energy (MORSE). The T group exhibited the highest MORSF value (13.64 ± 0.60 N), similar to the control (12.24 ± 0.50 N), suggesting greater structural resistance during shearing. Conversely, the E group had the lowest MORSF (9.30 ± 1.07 N), aligning with the lower TPA hardness, while the F group again showed intermediate values. A similar pattern was observed for MORSE, where Turkish coffee samples required the greatest energy for shearing (61.32 ± 1.61 N.s), and espresso samples the least (45.10 ± 5.78 N.s). These outcomes indicate that espresso marination contributed to a more tender structure, as both lower shear force and energy reflect decreased mechanical resistance and potentially improved eating quality.

The observed reduction in meat hardness and shear force in the espresso-marinated samples may be mechanistically associated with the unique physicochemical composition of espresso coffee. To clarify this, the phenolic compound profiles of the coffee types were analyzed. Espresso extract contained high levels of syringic acid (454.7 mg/L) and (+)-catechin (267.4 mg/L), as well as moderate concentrations of chlorogenic acid (40.3 mg/L) and epicatechin (34.1 mg/L). These phenolic constituents possess strong antioxidant and protein-modifying activities that can promote oxidative softening of myofibrillar and connective tissue proteins. Catechins and hydroxycinnamic acids can interact with muscle proteins, altering hydrogen bonding and crosslink structures, which facilitates proteolysis and leads to lower hardness and shear resistance.

Moreover, espresso typically exhibits a higher extraction efficiency and a slightly lower pH compared with the other coffee types, which may further enhance the tenderizing effect by promoting collagen solubilization and myofibrillar swelling. In contrast, Turkish and filter coffees, though richer in total phenolics (e.g., higher ascorbic and chlorogenic acid contents), likely contain larger insoluble particles and fewer readily extractable low–molecular-weight compounds, limiting their diffusion into the meat matrix during marination. Overall, the superior tenderizing action of espresso coffee thus arises not merely from its caffeine content, but from the synergistic effects of specific phenolic constituents and acidity, which collectively facilitate structural weakening of meat tissue.

Overall, both TPA and MORS data consistently demonstrate that marination with coffee types has a measurable impact on the textural properties of spent hen meat. In particular, espresso coffee showed a notable tenderizing effect, likely driven by their physicochemical properties. This is consistent with previous studies suggesting that plant-derived acids, enzymes, and polyphenols can modulate meat structure through proteolysis or collagen solubilization (Anaduaka et al., 2023; Mohd Azmi et al., 2023). Meanwhile, Turkish and filter coffee appeared less effective in altering texture, possibly due to differences in composition, processing, or their interaction with meat proteins.

There is a lack of literature investigating similar marination treatments in spent hen meat, which limits direct comparison with previous findings. Nevertheless, the present results provide novel insights into the potential use of coffee for meat tenderization and highlight promising avenues for future research.

These findings highlight the potential of coffee, especially espresso coffee, as functional ingredients in meat processing to improve the tenderness of spent hen meats—an underutilized poultry resource often associated with tough meat. The use of such alternative products in meat marinades not only improves meat quality but also contributes to product diversity by offering products with different flavors. Furthermore, by offering a natural alternative to synthetic additives, it contributes to both clean label approaches and sustainable production. Future studies should focus on elucidating the specific chemical interactions responsible for textural modifications and assess the sensory and nutritional impacts of such marination approaches.

Sensory properties

The sensory analysis conducted to determine the consumer acceptability and edibility of the samples marinated with different coffee types had a positive effect (P < 0.05) on the odour (US: odor), flavor, texture and general acceptability. When evaluated in terms of these parameters, the treatment groups generally received higher scores compared to the control samples. Espresso and filter coffee marinades positively influenced the color characteristics of the samples, whereas those marinated with Turkish coffee scored lower than the control group (P < 0.05). There are studies in the literature reporting that the marination process improves the sensory parameters of chicken meat. In agreement with our results, Unal et al. (2022) reported that chicken meat samples marinated in grapefruit and lemon juice had higher scores in terms of texture than the control sample. Similarly, Sengun et al. (2019) determined that the chicken meat samples marinated with koruk juice were appreciated in terms of textural properties and koruk juice could be a potential marinating ingredient to improve the sensory properties of chicken meat. Augustynska-Prejsnar ´ et al. (2023) stated that marinating chicken meat samples with apple and lemon juices increased the sensory properties in terms of flavour, juiciness, and texture comparison to the control sample. This findings were consistent with the findings of the study which declared that marinating meat with acidic solutions makes the texture softer (Istrati et al., 2015). In different studies, the variations in the sensory parameter scores could be derived from the type of meat, the composition and concentration of the marination solution, and the marination time.

Conclusion

Marination with different coffee types demonstrated potential to enhance the technological quality of spent hen breast meat, particularly in improving tenderness and surface characteristics. These findings highlight the feasibility of using coffee-based marinades—especially espresso—as a natural, clean-label approach to increase the market value of low-quality poultry meat. Beyond their technological benefits, coffee-derived compounds could also influence sensory perception, such as flavor and color acceptability, which warrants further consumer-based and sensory evaluation studies. Future research may also explore optimization of marination conditions and scaling potential for industrial applications within the poultry processing sector.
