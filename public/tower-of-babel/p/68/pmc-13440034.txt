Different pattern: a new EEG-based method for mental performance detection
Authors: Ugur Ince, Serkan Kirik, Irem Tasci, Prabal Datta Barua, Mehmet Baygin, Burak Tasci, Sengul Dogan, Turker Tuncer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study focuses on detecting mental performance from EEG signals. It provides both classification and explanation results. For this purpose, we developed a new feature extraction method called Different Pattern (DiffPat) within an Explainable Feature Engineering (XFE) framework.New methodThe proposed approach utilizes an EEG mental performance dataset. The DiffPat algorithm extracts the differences between EEG channels in order to improve the efficiency of the feature extraction process. To develop the identified features into a more accurate set of features, the iterative Neighborhood Component Analysis (INCA) feature selection method was utilized iteratively. After selecting the features with INCA, the K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) classifier was used to classify the features. AI-based explainable results utilizing XAI methods in conjunction with Directed Lobish (DLob) symbolic language were generated to produce cortical connectome diagrams and interpretive sentences that describe the relationship between the brain’s neural networks.ResultsThe DiffPat-driven model achieved 84.48% accuracy with leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation, which serves as the primary subject-independent performance estimate. A complementary subject-aware 10-fold cross-validation, where all segments of each subject were confined to a single fold, yielded 99.87% accuracy.Comparison with existing methodsThe DiffPat-based XFE model can explain the results and provides an advance in both feature engineering and neuroscience.ConclusionsBecause the model can both classify and explain the results, it has strong potential for mental performance analysis and other EEG applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03591-1.

Introduction

Electroencephalography (EEG) provides a way to monitor and measure brain activity by recording the electrical signals in the brain which result from mental activities and help us understand how we think or feel [1, 2]. Although this modality is often used for the early diagnosis of neurological diseases, it has also been widely applied in cross-disciplinary areas of artificial intelligence, education, health and human-machine interactions [3–5]. In this context, EEG signals provide comprehensive and valuable information about mental processes by directly measuring the electrical activity of the brain [6]. In particular, the detection of cognitive disorders and emotion recognition are among the popular research areas using EEG signals [7]. In recent years, mental performance measurement with EEG signals has also become an increasingly popular approach [8]. The number of applications using objective measures of mental states and performance is numerous and very important. As such, several potential application areas have arisen, such as identifying the abilities of individuals, the early diagnosis of cognitive disorders and improving educational processes [9, 10].

Several spatial and geometric approaches have been proposed for EEG feature extraction in the literature. Common Spatial Patterns (CSP) [11] and its variants extract discriminative spatial filters by maximizing variance ratios between classes; however, CSP relies on frequency band pre-selection and requires training-phase optimization of spatial filter parameters, resulting in O(Ch3) computational complexity for covariance matrix decomposition. Riemannian geometry-based methods [12] operate on the manifold of symmetric positive definite (SPD) covariance matrices and have shown strong performance in BCI applications; yet they require covariance matrix estimation, which introduces quadratic complexity O(Ch2 × L) and sensitivity to short epochs and noise. Functional connectivity measures such as phase locking value (PLV) [13] and coherence [14] capture inter-channel relationships but require frequency decomposition and are computationally intensive for real-time applications. Pattern-based approaches including Local Binary Pattern (LBP) adaptations for EEG [15] capture local signal texture but typically operate on individual channels independently without modeling cross-channel interactions.

In contrast, the proposed DiffPat method offers several distinct advantages over these existing approaches: (i) DiffPat is entirely deterministic and training-free—it requires no parameter optimization, spatial filter learning, or covariance estimation; (ii) it operates with linear time complexity O(L), making it significantly more efficient than CSP (O(Ch3)), Riemannian (O(Ch2 × L)), and connectivity-based methods; (iii) unlike single-channel pattern methods (e.g., LBP), DiffPat inherently captures inter-channel relationships through its transition table mechanism (Eqs. 6–7), generating a 32 × 32 connectivity-like representation without explicit frequency decomposition; (iv) the transition table structure directly supports explainability, as each feature maps to a specific channel-to-channel interaction, enabling DLob-based symbolic interpretation and cortical connectome visualization—a capability absent in CSP, Riemannian, and most connectivity-based methods. These properties position DiffPat as a lightweight, transparent, and interpretable alternative to existing spatial feature extraction paradigms.

The advent of portable EEG devices has greatly increased the use of EEG signals [16, 17]. Although the technology has improved, EEG signal analysis still has many challenges including their multidimensional, complex and noisy nature [18]. As a result, most of the currently used feature extraction and classification techniques suffer from either low accuracy or lack of transparency regarding how they reach their conclusions. Due to these deficiencies, there is a growing need to develop new techniques that can achieve high levels of accuracy and explain why certain conclusions were reached. To address this need, an innovative model called Different Pattern (DiffPat) based Explainable Feature Engineering (XFE) was created to detect mental performance from EEG signals. The DiffPat-XFE model provided a simple and efficient way to obtain high levels of accuracy in detecting mental performance while at the same time achieving explainability of the classification results.

Although many studies reported high accuracy rates, two significant gaps remain: First, nearly all of the studies presented herein lacked the use of explainable AI (XAI) methodologies; such limitations are particularly relevant to clinical and practical applications which require high levels of interpretability. Second, the computationally intensive approaches used by many of the studies were capable of reporting high accuracy, but may not be applicable to real-time or resource constrained environments. This research addresses both gaps by proposing a lightweight feature engineering approach that captures spatial patterns while maintaining computational efficiency and, crucially, providing XAI-based interpretability through the Directed Lobish (DLob) symbolic language framework.

Motivation and our method

The main motivation of this research is to introduce a next-generation feature engineering model and evaluate the classification capability of this feature extraction function for mental performance detection. In particular, the proposed function for extracting the features should be able to classify well with respect to mental performance. Specifically, the primary goal of the proposed function for feature extraction is to distinguish between the different EEG channels. The resulting feature vector becomes richer when the function can identify differences between EEG channels. As stated previously, the primary goal of the DiffPat method for feature extraction is the generation of explanatory (explainable) features. Each feature in the DiffPat method is generated based on the information provided by two EEG channels.

Unlike CSP which requires supervised spatial filter optimization, Riemannian methods which depend on covariance matrix estimation, and connectivity measures which require frequency decomposition, DiffPat achieves spatial pattern extraction through a fully deterministic channel ranking and transition mapping procedure. This design ensures both computational efficiency and full transparency of the feature generation process. Deep learning models have a variety of dynamic architectures that are capable of delivering high levels of classification performance. A drawback of deep learning models is their use of back-propagation as a method of training those architectures. Back-propagation is computationally complex and can deliver a level of computational complexity related to the exponential nature of the time required to train those models. An alternative feature extraction technique was developed to enable the identification of features at a variety of locations. Channel transformation was then utilized to enable the identification of those locations. Therefore, a transformation-based feature engineering model was created. That model employs a transformer to select features. This type of feature extraction model is novel and provides both high classification performance and a deep transformer model inspiration. Through utilization of the proposed feature extraction model, an Explainable Feature Engineering (XFE) model was developed.

The proposed XFE model combines DiffPat-based feature extraction, Iterative Neighborhood Component Analysis (INCA) [38], as a feature selection method, the k-Nearest Neighbor (kNN) [39] as a classifier, and directed lobish (DLob) [40] to generate XAI results from the selected features for each EEG signal, as it employs the DiffPat feature extraction function to extract features from each EEG signal. The most relevant features are selected by employing an iterative self-organized INCA feature selection function. To further evaluate the high classification capability of the selected features, the kNN classifier is utilized. Moreover, the symbolic language sentence that defines the selected features is produced by employing the DLob symbolic language and the identities of the selected features.

Therefore, in this context, the proposed DiffPat-based XFE model produces both classification and interpretability results for detecting mental performance.

Material

An EEG mental performance dataset was collected specifically for this research. The dataset consists of EEG recordings acquired during a cognitive assessment task designed to evaluate mental performance capabilities.

Cognitive assessment protocol

A 60-item cognitive assessment battery was administered to evaluate mental performance across multiple domains. Each item was worth 2 points, yielding a maximum score of 120 points. The assessment items were carefully curated from publicly available cognitive test questions, drawing inspiration from established intelligence scales (e.g., WISC - Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children) while using only open-source, copyright-free materials to ensure ethical compliance. Data acquisition

EEG data was collected from all 32 channels on the Emotiv Flex wireless EEG Headset. This headset has a sampling rate of 256 Hz. Its 32-channel configuration allows for full spatial coverage across all of the cortical areas (frontal, central, temporal, parietal, and occipital) and allows for a high level of detail regarding patterns of cortical activity.

Participants were seated in a controlled laboratory setting that is free from distractions and noise and were asked to keep their heads and bodies as still as possible to limit motion artifact while recording the data. Only finger movement was allowed for input selections (via either keyboard or touch screen) in order to limit movement of the participant’s body during recording. All testing sessions were administered individually to the participant and consisted of the same standard instructions for each participant.

Data labeling

Participant performance was categorized using a threshold-based criterion: Participants with scores that were lower than 90 points (or less than 75% accuracy) were classified at a low level of mental performance (Class 0); whereas participants who scored 90 points or greater were classified as having high mental performance (Class 1). A 90-point score was used as the cutoff to distinguish the average from below-average performance in the standard test battery.

DiffPat XFE architecture

In this section, we provide details about the presented DiffPat-driven XFE model. In this model, features were extracted using DiffPat. The most informative features were selected through the INCA feature selection method. To demonstrate the high classification capability of the selected features, the kNN classifier was utilized. In the XAI generation phase, DLob symbols were extracted based on the identities of the selected features. Subsequently, a cortical connectome diagram was generated using the extracted data. The general overview of the presented DiffPat-based XFE model is demonstrated in Fig. 2.Fig. 2The graphical outline of the presented DiffPat-driven XFE model

The graphical outline of the presented DiffPat-driven XFE model

The overall structure of the proposed DiffPat-driven XFE model is shown in Fig. 2. It conceptually resembles the attention mechanism in transformer architectures because it models the dynamic relationships among EEG channels. However, the presented DiffPat feature extractor is not a neural or transformer network. It does not use trainable layers, backpropagation, or weight optimization. DiffPat follows a fully deterministic and rule-based transformation. It ranks the EEG channels based on their relative amplitudes and identifies temporal differences between consecutive time windows.

By means of this ranking/transition analysis, DiffPat generates a 32 × 32 transition matrix which describes the frequency of interaction between channels (attention-like behavior) in the EEG domain, with no training. The resulting matrix is then flattened to create a 1024-dimensional feature vector that provides a structured, interpretable description of the brain’s spatiotemporal activity.

As opposed to the black box nature of most conventional transformer-based or convolutional feature extraction methods, DiffPat explicitly defines all of its individual computational operations; i.e., channel ranking, transition mapping, and the creation of the vector. This structure ensures full transparency and reproducibility. The design supports the explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) goal of the study. The later stages, such as INCA feature selection and DLob-based interpretation, maintain interpretability throughout the decoding pipeline. In this context, the inspiration from transformers refers to the conceptual modeling of inter-channel dependencies, not to the use of deep or trainable architectures. The pseudocode for the DiffPat-driven XFE method is provided in Algorithm 3.

Different pattern-based feature extraction

The primary innovation of this model is the introduction of DiffPat. DiffPat is a simple yet effective method, as it identifies different points using an overlapping block, where the length of each block is equal to 2 × Ch, with Ch representing the number of channels.

In this feature extraction function, the process begins with a channel transformation applied to the EEG signals. Next, blocks are created, and the differing values are identified. Subsequently, the identities of these differing values are selected, and a transition table is computed for these values. To generate the feature vector, a matrix-to-vector transformation is applied. The steps of this phase are outlined below for clarity.

Step 1: Generate two channel vectors. 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${v_1} = signal\left( {i,:} \right),i \in \left\{ {1,2, \ldots ,len - 1} \right\}$$\end{document}2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${v_2} = signal\left( {i + 1,:} \right)$$\end{document}

Herein, v: vector and len: length of the signal. These vectors represent consecutive time windows in the EEG signal. By comparing adjacent windows, we can detect temporal changes in spatial patterns.

Step 2: Apply channel transformation to generate the identities of the vector. 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$id{x_h} = argsort\left( { - {v_h}} \right),h \in \left\{ {1,2} \right\}$$\end{document}4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$tr\left( {c:c + Ch - 1,h} \right) = id{x_h},c \in \left\{ {1,Ch + 1, \ldots ,len \times Ch - Ch + 1} \right\}$$\end{document}

Where idx: the qualified identities, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$argsort\left( . \right)$$\end{document}: the qualification function and tr: transformed signal. Step 2 defines the signal transformation. Here, channels are ranked by amplitude (highest to lowest). The “identity” of a channel is its rank position. For example, if channel Cz has the highest amplitude, it gets rank 1. This transformation converts raw amplitudes into relative orderings, making the method robust to baseline differences between subjects.

Step 3: Find the different identities of the transformed signal. 5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q = \left\{ {k{\rm{|}}b\left( {k,1} \right) \ne b\left( {k,2} \right)} \right\}$$\end{document}

Here, q is the set of the row indices of b for which the two columns differ. The positions where there was a change in the rank of the channel from one window to another will be identified. For instance, if channel Cz was at rank 1 in window 1, but at rank 5 in window 2; this indicates a spatial shift in activity and is exactly the type of dynamic change that you would like to detect.

Step 4: Extract the transition table deploying the generated different indices. 6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T = \left[ {\matrix{ 0 & \cdots & 0 \cr \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \cr 0 & \cdots & 0 \cr } } \right]$$\end{document}7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T\left( {b\left( {q\left( j \right),1} \right),b\left( {q\left( j \right),2} \right)} \right) = T\left( {b\left( {q\left( j \right),1} \right),b\left( {q\left( j \right),2} \right)} \right) + 1,j \in \left\{ {1,2, \ldots L\left( q \right)} \right\}$$\end{document}

where T: transition table with a size of Ch × Ch and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L\left( . \right)$$\end{document}: length computation function. The transition table T counts how often each channel-to-channel transition occurs. Entry T(i,j) represents how frequently channel i’s rank position transitioned to channel j’s position. High values indicate strong functional relationships similar to connectivity but derived from amplitude dynamics rather than phase/coherence.

Step 5: Convert the generated transition table to a vector and obtain the feature vector. In this research, the used EEG dataset has 32 channels. Therefore, the length of the extracted feature vector is equal to 1024 (=322). 8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$fv\left( p \right) = T\left( {a,b} \right),\left( {a,b} \right) \in \left\{ {1,2, \ldots ,Ch} \right\},p \in \left\{ {1,2, \ldots ,C{h^2}} \right\}$$\end{document}

Herein, fv: feature vector. The 32 × 32 transition matrix is flattened into a 1024-element feature vector, where each element represents the strength of a specific channel-to-channel transition pattern. This rich representation captures the full network dynamics during mental performance tasks.

These five steps (Steps 1–5) define the proposed DiffPat feature extractor.

Feature selection

The second phase of the introduced DiffPat-driven XFE model is feature selection. In this phase, a self-organized and iterative feature selection function, called the INCA feature selector, is employed. INCA iteratively selects features and generates multiple feature vectors. The classification performance of each selected feature vector is evaluated using the kNN classifier. In the final step, the output with the highest classification accuracy is selected using a greedy algorithm.

In this context, INCA proves to be an effective feature selector, as introduced by Tuncer et al. [38] in 2020. The steps of this feature selection process are explained below.

The performance gap of 15.39% points between the two protocols is attributable to the higher sensitivity of LOSO to individual subject variability, where single outlier subjects with atypical EEG patterns disproportionately affect the overall average, whereas in 10-fold CV, the grouping of 3–4 subjects per test fold provides a natural variance-smoothing effect.

Discussions

A new generation DiffPat-based XFE model is presented in this research, capable of generating both classification and interpretable results. An important methodological clarification concerns the two validation protocols employed in this study. Both 10-fold CV and LOSO CV were implemented with subject-aware data partitioning, ensuring that no subject’s segments appear in both training and test sets within any fold. In 10-fold CV, subjects were distributed across folds via round-robin assignment, creating a leave-N-subjects-out evaluation with approximately 3–4 test subjects per fold. Despite both protocols being subject-independent, a substantial performance gap (15.39% points) was observed. This gap arises because LOSO evaluates each subject individually, fully exposing inter-subject EEG variability. In contrast, 10-fold CV averages performance across multiple test subjects per fold, which smooths out the impact of individual outliers. This observation is consistent with the broader BCI literature, where LOSO evaluations consistently yield lower accuracy than grouped cross-validation even when both are subject-independent [41, 42]. We designate the LOSO result (84.48%) as the primary performance estimate, as it represents the most conservative and clinically relevant evaluation of the model’s generalization capacity to entirely new individuals.

To produce classification results, DiffPat is integrated with INCA and kNN, while for generating interpretable results, DiffPat is combined with INCA and DLob.

As described in the methodology, INCA has been employed as the feature selector due to its self-organized and iterative nature. To identify the best shallow classifier for this feature selector, the MATLAB Classification Learner Toolbox was used. This toolbox includes various shallow classifiers. To provide comparative insights into the classifiers, the best classification results from different classifier families were selected and are showcased in Fig. 6.Fig. 6Comparison of the classifier with 10-fold CV. Comprehensive statistical validation metrics

To provide a thorough validation for whether the classification performance reflected true learning rather than memorization or overfitting; a detailed statistical validation was performed as shown in Table 10. Three separate pieces of evidence demonstrate that overfitting did not occur. First, the huge effect size (Cohen’s d = 182.5), shows that there is a very large difference between how well the classification worked and what would be expected by random guessing. With the model being able to classify at an 84.30% rate and random guessing occurring at a 50% rate, this indicates that the model was classifying significantly better than would have been possible through simple pattern recognition and therefore was learning real information. Second, bootstrap sampling using 10,000 iterations generated a 95% CI of [84.19%, 84.41] based on a 0.022 range (or span). The narrowness of this CI along with low SE (0.056%) suggests that model performance estimates are quite robust in their consistency of estimation across many random data splits and therefore do not have high variability as one would expect if the model was fit to noise. Third, statistical power analysis indicated an ideal “detection probability” (power = 1.00), which further supports the fact that the number of participants (n = 37 subjects; n = 3949 total segments) far exceeds what is necessary to obtain valid statistical inferences. The highly significant p-value (<0.001) definitively rejects the null hypothesis of chance-level classification. Together, these statistics specifically the combination of a large effect size, small confidence interval ranges, and perfect statistical power provide strong evidence that DiffPat-driven models capture specific, generalized EEG signature (mental performance) trends as opposed to memorizing specific aspects of data sets. The model is able to perform with “true” discriminative capability based on neural biomarkers versus memorization of dataset characteristics.

Comparison with decomposition-based Methods

Recently, a number of studies used decompositions and time-frequency analysis for classification of EEG signals, including empirical wavelet transform (EWT), variational mode decomposition (VMD) and other decomposition techniques. Although such approaches are beneficial as they provide insight into frequency-specific neural activities, the DiffPat method is better suited for detecting mental performance due to its decomposition-free approach to processing multi-channel EEG data.

Decomposition techniques (EWT, VMD, EMD), extract intrinsic mode functions (IMFs) or frequencies from EEG signals prior to extracting features. Unlike the decomposition-free DiffPat method that extracts information from multi-channel EEG data by transforming channels and calculating the differences between them. The basic difference gives the DiffPat technique a number of advantages over the decomposition based techniques:Computational Efficiency: Typically, the computational performance of decomposition techniques depends on their respective optimization techniques (iterative optimization e.g. VMD, or adaptive filtering e.g. EWT) which results in an O (N log N) or even higher complexity per channel. The computational complexity of DiffPat is given by O(L) with L being the signal length. Therefore, it is much faster than decomposition techniques for real-time applications.Mode Mixing Prevention: EWT, EMD based techniques have problems with mode-mixing i.e. different frequency components are combined into single IMF. In contrast to that, DiffPat does not need to separate the channels in a temporal-frequency manner since it focuses on spatial (inter-channel) differences.Multi-Channel Integration: Unlike decomposition methods where each channel is processed individually and then features are extracted and combined, DiffPat has the possibility to capture cross-channel relationships due to its transition table mechanism (Eqs. 6-7). That allows for a richer connectivity information as demonstrated by our cortical connectome-diagrams (Figure 4b).Frequency Resolution Trade-offs: Methods using time-frequency decomposition are subject to the uncertainty principle - if the time-resolution increases the frequency resolution decreases and vice versa. Since DiffPat operates in the spatial domain it has no such trade-off between time and frequency resolution and maintains all temporal information and extracts spatial patterns.

Conclusions

This study developed an innovative feature extraction technique called DiffPat, which was paired with an XFE model for detecting mental performance from EEG data. The proposed DiffPat-XFE model achieved a subject-independent classification accuracy of 84.48% under LOSO cross-validation, the primary evaluation metric for subject-independent generalization. A complementary subject-aware 10-fold cross-validation yielded 99.87%, confirming the high discriminative capacity of DiffPat features. Both protocols employed subject-aware partitioning and nested INCA feature selection, ensuring no data leakage at any stage.

The incorporation of DLob symbolic language improved the interpretability of the model, as a diagrammatic representation of the cortical connectome was created, which illustrated activity in central and frontal brain areas. This is consistent with the increasing demand for understandable AI in neuroscience applications.

Due to its small footprint and linear computational complexity, this model can be used on a larger number of EEG data sets than previously possible. The model performed better than other feature extraction models; namely, BP, ChMinMaxPat, and TTPat.

The DiffPat-driven XFE framework demonstrates that effective EEG analysis requires balancing two fundamental principles: classification accuracy and interpretability. The spatial pattern extraction philosophy underlying DiffPat, which focuses on inter-channel transitions rather than isolated spectral features, offers broader applicability to any EEG-based task where neural signatures manifest as distributed cortical network patterns.

The methodological contribution extends beyond mental performance detection. Any cognitive state classification task involving multi-regional brain coordination could benefit from this approach. The interpretability-by-design principle addresses a fundamental clinical BCI challenge: the need for both algorithmic performance and neurophysiological understanding.

The lightweight computational profile combined with transparent feature generation positions DiffPat as a promising candidate for real-time, resource-constrained environments, pending further validation on diverse datasets and populations. Future research should investigate the applicability of DiffPat to other cognitive state classification tasks and larger, more heterogeneous cohorts to validate the generalizability of the reported findings.
