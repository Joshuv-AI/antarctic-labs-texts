Colloidal Synthesis and Analysis of CNT-Cu2S for Stability and Capacity Increase Alleviation in Sodium-Ion Storage
Authors: Youngho Jin, Joon Ha Moon, Honggyu Seong, Geongil Kim, Hyerin Yoo, Taejung Jung, Jaewon Choi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the growing interest in energy storage, significant research has focused on finding suitable anode materials for sodium-ion batteries (SIBs). While developing high-capacity nanosized metal sulfides, issues like low stability and rapid initial capacity decline are common. Instead of maintaining steady capacity, they also tend to exhibit an increase in discharge capacity as cycling continues. We introduce CNT-Cu2S, featuring Cu2S nanoplates integrated onto the surface of MWCNTs, and assess its electrochemical properties for SIBs. Cu2S initially exhibited a rapid decrease in capacity and then showed increased capacity. In contrast, CNT-Cu2S demonstrated a stable capacity of 344.8 mAh g–1 at 2.0 A g–1 over 800 cycles, close to the theoretical capacity with capacitive behavior. This paper carried out analysis using data from in situ EIS and overpotential data from GITT to explain the different outcomes between the Cu2S and CNT-Cu2S experiments. These results show that CNT-Cu2S is a suitable anode material for SIBs.Keywords: Copper sulfide, Multiwalled carbon nanotubes, Sodium-ion batteries, Anode, In situ EIS, Overpotential

As research on energy storage is gaining huge attention, the development of electrode materials for secondary batteries is constantly evolving.1−3 Sodium-ion batteries (SIBs) are key solutions that can diversify battery technologies with their appropriate voltage range and cost benefits.4−6 By reducing reliance on costly materials such as lithium in lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), SIBs are especially well-suited for large-scale energy storage applications.7 They offer a cost-effective solution to the challenges posed by intermittent renewable energy sources.8 SIBs operate through the shuttling of sodium ions between the cathode and anode electrodes during charging and discharging processes, making anode materials vital elements for battery performance. To achieve high capacity and cycle stability, sodium ions must ideally insert and desorb from the electrode materials. Recently, researchers have focused on metal sulfides such as ZnS, Sb2S3, and SnS2 due to their high theoretical capacities and affordability.9,10 Sodium storage in metal sulfides typically involves a conversion reaction. Research is currently focused on synthesizing nanosized metal sulfides as active materials, but metal sulfides tend to experience a rapid decrease in capacity in the earlier cycles, followed by an increase in capacity after additional activation sites have formed.11−13 Although conversion reaction provides high capacity, it induces structural changes in the electrode material, leading to reduced stability.14 This is mainly due to the aggregation of nanomaterials, which reduces reaction sites, and the inherent poor conductivity of metal sulfides.15−17

Copper sulfides (Cu2S), known for their thermal stability and resource abundance, have seen widespread applications in sensors, supercapacitors, and LIBs.18−23 When used as an anode material for SIBs, Cu2S exhibits a high specific theoretical capacity of 336.8 mAh g–1 through a conversion reaction.24,25 However, studies have shown that when Cu2S is used alone as an active material, there is a significant decrease in cycle stability and a rapid decrease in initial capacity.26 Developing strategies to mitigate these challenges is gaining momentum. Kim et al. synthesized Cu2S@NC using a conventional polyol process and achieved a cyclability of 284.99 mAh g–1 at a current density of 5.0 A g–1 for SIB anodes.27 In this study, the Cu2S nanoplate was prepared through a straightforward colloidal method, employing oleylamine as a surfactant by controlling the reaction temperature and time. Our goal was to prevent aggregation by synthesizing the Cu2S nanoplate on the surface of multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) and to tackle the issue of low conductivity by combining it with carbon materials.28,29 MWCNTs are preferred due to their stability, high conductivity, and large surface area.30,31 Thus, carbon nanotube-based nanomaterials are receiving considerable attention in SIBs.32 Tricoli et al. synthesized ZnS-CNT heterojunctions that enhance electron/ion transfer and ensure structural stability with a reversible capacity of 314 mAh g–1 at 5.0 A g–1 after 500 cycles for SIBs.33 Mai et al. prepared a ZnSe/MWCNT composite through hydrothermal reactions followed by a grinding process, maintaining a capacity of 382 mAh g–1 after 180 cycles at a current density of 0.5 A g–1 as an anode for SIBs.34 In this study, the nanoplate Cu2S, anchored on the surface of MWCNTs (CNT-Cu2S), was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and Raman spectroscopy. Its electrochemical properties were evaluated through cyclic voltammetry (CV) and galvanostatic charge/discharge tests as an anode for SIBs. While Cu2S showed a rapid decrease and then increase in capacity, CNT-Cu2S maintained a stable capacity, achieving a discharge capacity of 408.8 mAh g–1 after 450 cycles at a current density of 0.5 A g–1. At a higher current density of 2.0 A g–1, CNT-Cu2S attained a discharge capacity of 344.8 mAh g–1 after 800 cycles, which is close to the theoretical capacity and demonstrates excellent electrochemical performance due to the combination of Cu2S and MWCNTs. To further investigate these phenomena, we used in situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT). With these strategies, including the nanosized morphological design and carbon composite, we aim to mitigate volume expansion and shorten the diffusion pathways for sodium ions. This study provides a solution that overcomes the shortcomings of metal sulfides in the anode application of SIBs.

The nanoplate Cu2S was synthesized using a colloidal method, employing oleylamine as a surfactant, copper(II) chloride as a copper source, and 1-dodecanethiol as a sulfur source. More details of the synthesis process are available in the Supporting Information. Following the synthesis of the copper–dodecanethiol complex, Cu2S was subsequently produced. The formation of the copper–dodecanethiol complex was confirmed through scanning electron microscopy (SEM), infrared (IR) spectra, and X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Figure S1).35,36 Sharp XRD peaks were observed at lower angles corresponding to (0k0) (k = 2–9) diffraction orders. These peaks suggest a layered structure with a large d-spacing, attributed to stacked layers of Cu and S atoms separated by two layers of dodecane chains.37,38 According to Bragg’s law, the calculated kd values for 8 reflections showed an average of 35.88 Å, indicating a double-layered stacked structure consistent with twice the alkane chain length of dodecanethiol (Table S1). The morphology of the nanoplate Cu2S was examined using SEM and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping images (Figure 1a and Figure S2). To enhance electronic conductivity and prevent aggregation, multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) were incorporated to facilitate the growth of Cu2S on the surface of carbon materials. The morphology of CNT-Cu2S, with the Cu2S nanoplate anchored on MWCNTs, is presented in Figure 1b. As shown, Cu2S is widely attached to the surfaces of MWCNTs. The XRD patterns of Cu2S and CNT-Cu2S closely matched that of the chalcocite structure (JCPDS no. 9-328) (Figure 1c). The carbon content in CNT-Cu2S was determined using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), which can be seen in a plotting curve (Figure 1d) showing mass loss during heating due to the combustion of MWCNTs and oxidation of Cu2S to CuO. After calculation, we confirmed that CNT-Cu2S consisted of 86.0% Cu2S and 14.0% MWCNTs. The XRD pattern of CNT-Cu2S after the TGA test showed the formation of CuO (JCPDS no. 48-1548) (Figure 1d, inset). The E2g vibration mode and A1g breathing mode were observed in both MWCNTs and CNT-Cu2S in the Raman spectra, where the ID/IG ratios were 1.22 and 1.43, respectively (Figure S3a).39 The chemical bonding and oxidation states of CNT-Cu2S were further characterized by XPS. The Cu 2p spectrum showed several peaks. The dominant peaks matched Cu+ 2p3/2 (932.48 eV) and Cu+ 2p1/2 (952.48 eV), while small peaks associated with Cu2+ 2p3/2 (934.28 eV) and Cu2+ 2p1/2 (954.76 eV) were observed as a result of the oxidation of Cu2S (Figure S3b).23,24,27 The S 2p spectrum displayed peaks at S 2p3/2 (160.9 eV), S 2p1/2 (161.9 eV), C–S–C 2p3/2 (163.3 eV), C–S–C 2p1/2 (164.3 eV), and SOX (167.5 eV) (Figure S3c).40 Additionally, the C 1s spectrum revealed peaks corresponding to C—C/C=C (284.2 eV), C–S (285.68 eV), C=O (286.2 eV), and O—C=O (288.6 eV), as shown in Figure S3d.27,41 XPS results indicated the binding energies and chemical bonding of Cu2S and MWCNTs. The electrochemical performance of Cu2S and CNT-Cu2S as anode materials for sodium-ion batteries (SIBs) was evaluated by cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s–1 over several cycles (Figure 1e,f). The Cu2S and CNT-Cu2S demonstrated several redox peaks, indicating multiple phase transitions during the first charge/discharge processes. As the current was swept cathodically, processes such as the formation of a solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer around 1.8 V, the insertion of sodium ions (sodiation) (to ∼0.5 V), and conversion reactions (from 0.5 to 0.01 V) occurred continuously, as reported in several papers.11,26 Additionally, the extraction of sodium ions (desodiation) and reverse conversion reactions occurred during the anodic process. In the case of bare Cu2S, the shape of the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves altered significantly before stabilization, whereas in the case of CNT-Cu2S, only the intensity of peaks changed, maintaining a relatively constant voltage position, and the shape of the CV curves remained almost unchanged. As the cycles progressed, reduction peaks (around 1.5, 0.75, 0.5, and 0.1 V) and oxidation peaks (around 1.3, 1.5, 1.75, and 2.1 V) became apparent. Ultimately, Cu2S and CNT-Cu2S exhibited similar CV curves, though they displayed different characteristics during the cycling process. To measure the kinetic behavior of Cu2S, CV was conducted at various scan rates (0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 mV s–1) (Figure S4a). Oxidation peaks (O1, O2, and O4) and reduction peaks (R1, R2, R3, and R4) were shifted in the opposite direction due to the overpotential, but the overall shape of the CV curve was well maintained. The b value is determined by calculating the slope of the log(peak current)–log(scan rate) plot within the range of 0.5 to 1.0 (Figure S4b). A b value closer to 0.5 indicates a diffusion-controlled reaction, while a b value closer to 1.0 means a capacitive-controlled reaction. The overall redox peaks were closer to 1.0, indicating a capacitive reaction. Based on these results, Cu2S would exhibit capacitive behavior. Galvanostatic charge/discharge (CD) tests were also conducted to analyze the electrochemical properties and assess the cycle performance by applying a constant current. As the charging and discharging of Cu2S advanced at a current density of 0.5 A g–1, the position of the plateau altered markedly during the initial 140 cycles, but the CD curve stabilized thereafter (comparing the 140th and 300th cycles, the CD curves showed similarities) (Figure 2a). Conversely, in CNT-Cu2S, plateaus consistently appeared at a specific position throughout the cycling process, and the CD curves stabilized after 10 cycles (comparing the 10th and 20th cycles, the CD curves showed similarities) (Figure 2b). The results of the CD and CV tests were found to be similar. The positions of the plateaus and the capacities of Cu2S and CNT-Cu2S were similar in the initial discharge process, but their behaviors diverged, beginning with the first charging process. Cu2S exhibited discharge capacities of 433.1, 144.1, 337.3, and 346.5 mAh g–1 and charge capacities of 151.3, 144.3, 335.5, and 344.8 mAh g–1, corresponding to the first, 25th, 140th, and 300th cycles, respectively. Only after these cycles had significantly progressed did their behavior start to show similarity. The CNT-Cu2S displayed discharge capacities of 460.4, 356.5, and 360.1 mAh g–1 and charge capacities of 317.4, 345.0, and 352.0 mAh g–1, corresponding to the first, 10th, and 20th cycles, respectively. These phenomena were reflected in the cycling performance. It was confirmed that the capacity of CNT-Cu2S remained constant (408.8 mAh g–1 after the 450th cycle), while the capacity of Cu2S initially decreased rapidly (101.9 mAh g–1 after the sixth cycle), gradually increased, and then stabilized (362.6 mAh g–1 after the 450th cycle) (Figure 2c). Upon increasing the current density to 2.0 A g–1, both Cu2S and CNT-Cu2S exhibited a similar tendency. Initially, Cu2S displayed a discharge capacity of 13.6 mAh g–1 at the 10th cycle, which increased to 335.0 mAh g–1 by the 800th cycle. In contrast, CNT-Cu2S demonstrated a discharge capacity of 344.8 mAh g–1 at the 800th cycle without experiencing a rapid increase in capacity. The divergence in trends between Cu2S and CNT-Cu2S was evident from the first cycle, leading to the observation of electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) at specific voltages within the charge/discharge process (Figure 3a,b,d,e).43 Galvanostatic CD tests and then in situ EIS were performed to analyze the specific section of the Nyquist plot; we added the “–” symbol in front of the discharge voltage (e.g., −1.6 V in Figure 3a) to distinguish it from the charge voltage. The Nyquist plot revealed a semicircle in the high-frequency range and an inclined line in the low-frequency range, and the analysis was further refined using a proposed equivalent circuit (Figure 3a, inset). This equivalent circuit comprised the ohmic resistance (Re) and the resistance associated with Na-ion diffusion through the SEI layer (RSEI). The component Rct//CPEct represented the charge transfer process between the electrode and the electrolyte interface, and the Rb//CPEb segment indicated the electronic conductivity of the active materials and the ionic conductivity, reflecting the low-frequency semicircle pertaining to the bulk phase. Comparative analysis of the Nyquist plots for the discharge and charge processes showed that Cu2S and CNT-Cu2S exhibited distinct trends. The Rct and Rb values for Cu2S initially displayed a slight increase due to volume expansion and interaction with sodium ions but exhibited high resistance values after reaching the fully discharged state (Figure 3c). Conversely, CNT-Cu2S experienced an increase in resistance during the discharge process but thereafter demonstrated lower resistance values (Figure 3d). It was further noted that the overall resistance values for CNT-Cu2S were consistently lower than those for Cu2S throughout the cycle. To elucidate the reasons behind these differences, ex situ TEM at the fully discharged state was performed. As previously mentioned, Cu2S underwent multiple phase changes during the discharge process and formed an SEI layer (Figure 3g). The lattice fringes with interplanar distances of 0.236, 0.244, and 0.377 nm corresponded to Na2Cu4S3, Na3Cu4S4, and Na2S phases, respectively (Figure S5). It is hypothesized that the formation of a suitably thick SEI layer on CNT-Cu2S, compared to Cu2S, contributed to the lower resistance values (Figure 3h,i). The specific capacity of SIBs is associated with the insertion/desertion of sodium ions into the electrode materials. The changes in overpotential were noted through the use of the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) during the capacity increase process. To determine the overpotential (η) using the GITT method, a procedure involving the application of a constant current corresponding to a current density of 0.1 A g–1 for 10 min, followed by a relaxation period of 60 min, was carried out from the first to the 10th cycle (Figure 4a,d). The η was calculated by measuring the difference between the voltage at the end of the current pulse (Emeas (measured voltage) or CCV (close-circuit voltage)) and the voltage at the end of the relaxation period (Eeq (equilibrium voltage) or QOCV (quasi-open-circuit voltage)) (Figure S6 and eq 3).44

The observed voltage deviation after the reaction and relaxation could be attributed to the reaction η, which relates to stress and diffusion behaviors.26 The Cu2S, which displayed a high η value during the first discharge, was observed to stabilize as the η value diminished progressively across the cycles (Figure 4b). Additionally, the η value for charge (desodiation) initially exceeded that for discharge (sodiation) in the first cycle (Figure 4c). Nevertheless, as the cycles progressed and the capacity increased, the η values eventually matched those of the discharge process. For CNT-Cu2S, both of the η values for charge and discharge rapidly stabilized, exhibiting lower values compared to those of pure Cu2S (Figure 4e,f). As the η value declined, the capacity increased, suggesting that the capacity was preserved where the η value became stabilized. By incorporation of Cu2S with MWCNTs, the η value promptly stabilized, and CNT-Cu2S demonstrated a consistent capacity. This outcome verified that incorporating MWCNTs enhances the cycling performance of Cu2S as anode materials for SIBs with the formation of an effective SEI layer and the reduction in overpotential.

In summary, Cu2S and CNT-Cu2S were synthesized using a straightforward colloidal method in a single-step process. Initially, the nanoplate Cu2S demonstrated a high discharge capacity but underwent a rapid decrease in initial cycles. Through CD curve analysis, it was determined that Cu2S achieved a certain discharge capacity after sufficient cycling. Conversely, CNT-Cu2S delivered a reversible capacity of 408.8 mAh g–1 at a current density of 0.5 A g–1 after 450 cycles and maintained stable cycling performance at a current density of 2.0 A g–1 over 800 cycles. To discern the differences in cycle behaviors, in situ EIS was conducted, revealing variant Nyquist plots after the fully discharged state. Based on these observations, ex situ TEM at the fully discharged state was performed, suggesting that an appropriate thickness of the SEI layer was formed, which is presumed to be a factor in reducing resistance. Additionally, the overpotential was calculated using GITT measurements, and the changes in overpotential were analyzed over 10 charge/discharge cycles. This can suggest strategies to mitigate capacity increases for conversion-based materials in SIBs.
