Maximizing surgical precision in gracilis muscle reconstruction: anatomical Variants, advanced Imaging, and novel surgical strategies
Authors: Ingrid C. Landfald, Paloma Aragonés, Dina Dehghani, John Coleman, Jakub Adamek, Yaashmithaa A/P. Siva Kumar, Łukasz Olewnik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe gracilis muscle (GM) is a reliable flap in reconstructive microsurgery due to its consistent anatomy and low donor-site morbidity. However, morphological, vascular, and neural variations may complicate surgical planning and outcomes.MethodsA narrative review was conducted based on anatomical studies, imaging reports, and surgical case series sourced from PubMed and Scopus (up to 2025), with emphasis on anatomical variants, imaging modalities, and reconstructive strategies.ResultsKey variants include supernumerary heads, accessory slips, multiple vascular pedicles, and diverse innervation patterns. These factors affect flap design, harvesting, and reinnervation success. Advanced imaging techniques such as CTA, MRA, Doppler ultrasound, and ICG angiography enable precise preoperative mapping. We propose two clinical frameworks—the Gracilis Functional Flap Classification (GFFC) and the Gracilis Innervation-Based Surgical Planning Algorithm (GISPA)—to integrate anatomical variation into individualized surgical planning.ConclusionsGM anatomical variability significantly influences reconstructive outcomes. The integration of GFFC and GISPA, supported by imaging and emerging technologies, enhances surgical precision and functional results in microsurgical applications.

Introduction

The gracilis muscle (GM) is a reliable standard donor in reconstructive microsurgery owing to consistent landmarks, dependable vascular supply, and low donor-site morbidity. Its slender morphology and long distal tendon enable tension-free inset across indications such as dynamic facial reanimation, restoration of elbow and finger flexion after brachial plexus injury, dynamic graciloplasty, and TUG-based breast reconstruction [1, 26, 28]. Recent high-resolution anatomical studies have refined our understanding of GM neuroanatomy, revealing a compartmentalized internal architecture with reproducible intramuscular and extramuscular innervation patterns [20–22] (Kurtys et al., 22,Olewnik et al., 2022). These insights challenge the traditional uniform model and directly inform flap segmentation, nerve coaptation strategy, and multivector planning. Advanced imaging, including MR neurography, further facilitates preoperative mapping and intraoperative verification (Chhabra et al., 7).

Objective. Building on contemporary anatomical evidence, we introduce two clinically oriented tools: the Gracilis Functional Flap Classification (GFFC), which systematizes intramuscular and extramuscular innervation patterns, and the Gracilis Innervation-Based Surgical Planning Algorithm (GISPA), which operationalizes those patterns into stepwise decision-making for free functional muscle transfer.

Methodology: This study is based on a comprehensive review and synthesis of existing anatomical and clinical evidence. We conducted a focused literature search (PubMed, reference cross-checking) to identify key studies on gracilis muscle anatomy, innervation patterns, and outcomes of free functional muscle transfer (FFMT). Priority was given to recent high‐resolution cadaveric studies of intramuscular innervation (e.g. Kurtys et al. 22) and extramuscular nerve supply (e.g. Kurtys et al. 23), as well as pivotal clinical series on gracilis flaps in facial reanimation, brachial plexus injury, Volkmann’s contracture, and pelvic floor reconstruction. The findings from these sources were critically analyzed and integrated to form the new GFFC and the GISPA. All included data were drawn from published, peer-reviewed research,no new patient or cadaver experiments were performed for this framework. Additionally, representative clinical scenarios (drawn from literature and the authors’ experience) are presented to illustrate the practical application of the GFFC and GISPA in surgical planning.

Surgical anatomy and morphological variants of the gracilis muscle

General morphology and function

The GM is a long, strap-like structure located superficially in the medial thigh. It originates from the inferior pubic ramus and the pubic body and inserts distally onto the medial surface of the proximal tibia via the pes anserinus [24]. The muscle typically measures between 35 and 42 cm in length [20], and functionally contributes to hip adduction, knee flexion, internal rotation of the leg, and minor gait stabilization. Its reliable anatomical configuration and low donor-site morbidity make it highly suitable for dynamic and static reconstructive procedures [20]. Innervation patterns and neuromuscular compartments

The GM exhibits considerable variation in both its extramuscular and intramuscular innervation patterns, which are crucial for planning nerve coaptation and optimizing functional outcomes in free functional muscle transfers (FFMT) [22, 23].

Extramuscular innervation variations

Extramuscular nerve entry shows four reproducible patterns (Types I–IV) with clear operative consequences. Proximal-entry configurations (I/IV) favour dual coaptation and segmented harvest; distal-only (II) limits segmentation and often increases graft length; accessory inputs (III/IV) can enable independent motor territories when preserved. Intramuscular innervation variations (neuromuscular compartments)

Intramuscular nerve branching demonstrates that the gracilis is compartmentalized rather than uniform. A three-type intramuscular innervation scheme (Types I–III) provides a reproducible map of neuromuscular territories that directly informs segmental harvest, dual-innervation, and multivector designs. In brief, Type I (~ 71%) features a direct proximal motor entry and is most amenable to true segmentation; Type II (~ 24%) shows an indirect proximal course allowing conditional segmentation with meticulous mapping; Type III (~ 6%) lacks proximal branches (distal-only input) and is best used as a full-length unit with longer grafts [20, 22]. Vascular anatomy variations

The GM is a Mathes–Nahai Type II flap: one dominant pedicle with variable secondary inputs; pedicle level/length variability affects reach, inset orientation, and need for supercharging (CTA/DUS recommended preoperatively) [12, 26, 27, 34] (Mathes and Nahai, 26).

Surgical applications of the gracilis muscle (GM)

Due to its consistent anatomy and minimal donor-site morbidity, the GM is widely used across various reconstructive disciplines. Its versatility spans dynamic facial reanimation, upper limb reanimation, ligament reconstruction, breast reconstruction, pelvic and perineal repair, and limb salvage. These applications require awareness of anatomical variations to ensure flap viability and functional outcomes. A critical step in these procedures is the precise surgical harvesting of the GM and its tendon. Facial reanimation (Smile Restoration).

FGMT remains the gold standard in dynamic smile reanimation. The muscle is harvested and transferred between the zygomatic arch and oral commissure, with neurovascular anastomosis to facial vessels and either the ipsilateral masseteric nerve or a cross-facial nerve graft [32]. Dual innervation strategies combining both nerves yield superior results in spontaneity and symmetry [17, 36]. Recognition of extramuscular variants [20, 23] Types III–IV) enhances surgical planning and coaptation strategies.

Upper limb reanimation (Elbow Flexion Restoration)

Gracilis-based FFMT is a reliable technique to restore elbow flexion in traumatic brachial plexus injury. The muscle is anchored proximally and connected distally to flexor tendons, typically reinnervated by the spinal accessory or intercostal nerves [10, 15]. Dual nerve variants may support coaptation optimization (Kurtys et al. [23].

Knee ligament reconstruction (ACL/PCL grafting)

Gracilis tendons are commonly used for ACL or PCL grafts, often in combination with semitendinosus, due to favorable length and biomechanical strength. Accessory fascial insertions can complicate harvest, emphasizing the need for meticulous dissection [41]. Recovery of muscle strength is typically achieved within 6–12 months [42].

Breast reconstruction (transverse upper gracilis flap)

The transverse upper gracilis flap, incorporating skin, fat, and muscle, serves as an alternative to abdominal flaps in breast reconstruction. Success rates exceed 90% when preoperative CTA or MRA is used to identify perforators and vascular pedicle patterns [24, 40]. Dual pedicle variants may require tailored vascular strategies.

Pelvic, perineal, and genitourinary reconstruction

Pedicled gracilis flaps are valuable in reconstructing perineal, pelvic, and genitourinary defects, particularly in irradiated fields or chronic wound settings [5]. Their robust vascularity supports healing in compromised tissue beds. Intraoperative indocyanine green angiography enhances flap perfusion assessment [9, 31, 37].

Extremity trauma and limb salvage

GM flaps provide reliable coverage in traumatic and chronic limb conditions, such as osteomyelitis or soft-tissue defects. Flow-through configurations allow simultaneous soft-tissue reconstruction and revascularization [14].

The surgical versatility of the GM spans a wide range of reconstructive applications, each influenced by distinct anatomical variations. Clinical and surgical implications

The significant anatomical variability of the GM directly influences surgical planning, execution, and clinical outcomes. In order to address these variations systematically, we introduce the GFFC and the GISPA. These novel frameworks integrate anatomical variations into structured clinical decision-making tools, enhancing surgical precision and optimizing patient outcomes.

Gracilis functional flap classification (GFFC)

The GFFC categorizes anatomical variations of GM into five clinically relevant types, each with direct implications for surgical planning. This systematic framework integrates morphology, tendon anatomy, vascular variants, and neural patterns, guiding individualized surgical approaches and improving functional outcomes [30].

The GFFC facilitates individualized surgical planning by systematically accounting for anatomical diversity, thus reducing operative risks, enhancing flap survival, and maximizing functional outcomes across various reconstructive procedures. Gracilis innervation-based surgical planning (gispa) — condensed clinical workflow.

Step 1 — Preoperative mapping. Use MR neurography or high-resolution ultrasound to identify extramuscular (Types I–IV) and intramuscular (Types I–III) patterns; confirm vascular pedicle configuration [6, 7, 11].

Step 2a — Extramuscular pattern. Type I: proximal entry → short grafts; dual coaptation feasible. Type II: distal-only entry → limited segmentation; plan for longer grafts. Type III: accessory inputs → preserve to enable independent motor territories. Type IV: proximal ± accessory → strong candidate for dual coaptation [20, 22].

Step 2b — Intramuscular pattern. Type I (~ 71%) allows true segmentation and dual-innervation/multivector designs; Type II (~ 24%) enables conditional segmentation and suits single-vector tasks; Type III (~ 6%) has distal-only input and is best used as a full-length unit [20, 22].

Step 3 — Indication–type matching. Facial reanimation: intramuscular Type I with extramuscular Type I/IV → dual coaptation (masseteric nerve + CFNG). Elbow/finger flexion (BPI): intramuscular Type II → single-vector reconstruction with the shortest donor path. Dynamic graciloplasty: intramuscular Type III as a full-length unit.

Step 4 — Flap design & coaptation. Minimize graft length; separate coaptation sites in dual-innervation to avoid neuroma; align slips with target vectors.

Step 5 — Intraoperative verification. Nerve stimulation to confirm compartment viability and refine segmentation/tension.

Step 6 — Early rehabilitation. EMG-guided activation and targeted physiotherapy to expedite reinnervation and coordination.

Complications and risk mitigation

While gracilis-based reconstructions are generally safe, certain anatomical variations increase risk of complications. Awareness and targeted preventive strategies reduce these risks significantly:

Partial flap necrosis

Unrecognized vascular variations (dual pedicles, early bifurcation) are major risk factors for flap ischemia. Preoperative CTA/MRA mapping and intraoperative intraoperative indocyanine green fluorescence angiography are crucial for identifying vascular variants and ensuring complete perfusion, significantly reducing the risk of ischemic complications [16, 25].

Venous congestion

Aberrant venous drainage or small venae comitantes predispose gracilis flaps to venous congestion. Preoperative DUS and careful vein selection, including secondary venous anastomoses ("supercharging"), effectively mitigate congestion risks [13].

Hematoma and seroma formation

Accessory muscle slips and variant pedicle branches increase donor-site dead space, predisposing to hematomas or seromas. Rigorous hemostasis, meticulous pedicle ligation, and routine closed-suction drains minimize these fluid collections and prevent complications [35].

Nerve coaptation issues and neuroma formation

Variants such as distal-only nerve entry (Type II extramuscular) or dual-branch innervation complicate nerve coaptation. Preoperative MR neurography, intraoperative nerve stimulation, and careful management of nerve stumps (tension-free coaptation and fibrin glue adjunct) reduce neuroma risk and optimize functional reinnervation outcomes [22, 38].

Donor-site morbidity

Supernumerary gracilis heads or accessory slips may increase donor-site functional deficits post-harvest. Preoperative strength assessments targeted postoperative rehabilitation, and precise donor-site closure techniques (with optional soft tissue filler) significantly reduce morbidity [24].

Lymphedema risk

Proximal dissection near inguinal lymphatics, particularly in bilateral harvests (TUG flaps), increases lymphedema risk. Preoperative lymphatic mapping and lymphatic-sparing techniques minimize this complication, preserving long-term limb health [16].

Through proactive anatomical awareness, comprehensive preoperative planning, and careful intraoperative execution, complications associated with gracilis-based reconstruction can be minimized, resulting in high success rates and optimal functional outcomes. Complications, anatomical risk factors, and mitigation strategies

Advanced imaging modalities for preoperative planning

Advanced imaging modalities play a critical role in the preoperative evaluation of GM anatomy, directly influencing surgical strategies, minimizing complications, and optimizing reconstructive outcomes. Precise preoperative mapping using these modalities is essential for leveraging anatomical variations described within the GFFC and GISPA frameworks.

Magnetic resonance neurography (MRN)

MRN provides high-resolution imaging of peripheral nerve anatomy and pathology, offering unparalleled visualization of extramuscular and intramuscular nerve branching patterns of GM [7]. MRN reliably identifies dual innervation variants (Kurtys Types III–IV), distal-only nerve entries (Type II), and distinct compartmental patterns, facilitating tailored nerve coaptation strategies critical for successful functional muscle transfers [7, 23]. Routine integration of MRN is recommended for procedures demanding precise nerve mapping, such as facial reanimation and upper limb reconstruction.

Computed tomography angiography (CTA)

CTA is indispensable for detailed vascular assessment and precise mapping of GM arterial anatomy, especially in complex reconstructive cases. It provides high-resolution three-dimensional visualization of the dominant pedicle, variant vascular anatomy (dual pedicles, high bifurcations), and musculocutaneous perforators critical for flap planning, particularly in TUG breast reconstructions and complex pelvic-perineal reconstructions [24, 25]. Preoperative CTA significantly reduces ischemic complications and partial flap necrosis by enabling meticulous vascular planning and targeted surgical approaches [16].

Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA)

MRA serves as an alternative or complementary modality to CTA, particularly advantageous due to its non-ionizing radiation and excellent soft-tissue resolution. It effectively delineates vascular variants, muscular morphology, and adjacent anatomical structures. MRA is particularly beneficial in repeated imaging scenarios, young patients, and in cases where ionizing radiation exposure must be minimized [16, 25]. Similar to CTA, preoperative MRA facilitates tailored surgical strategies and risk mitigation in gracilis-based reconstructions.

Doppler ultrasound imaging

DUS provides a rapid, non-invasive assessment of superficial vascular anatomy and musculocutaneous perforators of the GM, particularly useful for quick bedside assessments or intraoperative validation of pedicle and perforator locations. Preoperative DUS evaluation is recommended in straightforward or revision ACL graft harvesting, or where precise identification of superficial pedicles and venous outflow pathways are critical [13]. Its integration into surgical planning notably reduces venous congestion complications by enabling secondary venous channel identification and facilitating tailored flap design.

Indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence angiography

ICG fluorescence angiography is a vital intraoperative imaging modality that provides real-time assessment of flap perfusion, directly identifying areas at risk of ischemia or congestion. Its routine use significantly reduces partial flap necrosis risks by enabling immediate identification and correction of suboptimal perfusion or venous congestion through intraoperative vascular adjustments [13, 31]. Incorporation of ICG angiography is strongly recommended, particularly in reconstructions with complex vascular anatomy, dual-pedicle variants, or compromised recipient vessels.

Emerging technologies: augmented reality (ar) and artificial intelligence (ai)-assisted imaging

Emerging technologies, including AR and AI-assisted surgical planning, offer significant advancements in gracilis surgery. AR enhances intraoperative navigation by overlaying precise vascular and neural maps directly onto the surgical field, facilitating accurate flap harvest and minimizing anatomical misinterpretations. AI-based algorithms aid in automatic identification of key anatomical structures, streamlining preoperative imaging interpretation and surgical planning processes. These cutting-edge tools represent future directions with considerable potential to further improve surgical precision, reduce operative time, and enhance functional outcomes in GM reconstructions [29, 33].

By systematically employing these advanced imaging modalities, surgeons can accurately predict anatomical variations, plan individualized surgical strategies, and significantly enhance clinical and functional outcomes in GM-based reconstructions. Limitations

While the GFFC and the GISPA offer valuable tools for surgical decision-making, several limitations must be acknowledged.

Retrospective nature of current evidence

The current understanding of GM variability and its integration into reconstructive algorithms is largely based on retrospective cadaveric studies, case reports, and small clinical series. Prospective validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is necessary to confirm the reproducibility, predictive accuracy, and generalizability of both frameworks [23, 36] (Kurtys et al., 2023a, Roy et al., 36).

Limited access to advanced imaging

GFFC and GISPA rely on imaging modalities such as CTA, MRN, MRA, and ICG angiography, which may not be universally accessible particularly in resource-limited settings. Furthermore, their interpretation requires radiological expertise, which may limit implementation across institutions [16, 25].

Patient-specific confounders

Even with optimal preoperative planning, individual factors such as age, prior surgery, radiation exposure, comorbidities, and tissue quality can influence flap viability and functional recovery. These confounders complicate the isolated assessment of anatomical impact on clinical outcomes [5, 31].

Lack of long-term outcome data

Most existing studies report short- to mid-term outcomes (typically within 6–12 months). There is a notable absence of robust longitudinal data regarding donor-site morbidity, neuromuscular function, and quality of life, particularly in dual-innervated or compartment-based reconstructions [8, 32].

Surgeon learning curve and technical variability

The adoption of GFFC and GISPA requires advanced microsurgical training, familiarity with neurovascular anatomy, and experience with high-resolution imaging. Inter-surgeon variability in interpretation and execution may affect consistency in clinical outcomes. Structured cadaveric dissection courses and AR/VR-based training may help mitigate this learning curve [33].

Future directions and emerging innovations

The GFFC and GISPA frameworks establish a foundation for precision-based GM reconstruction. However, continued research and technological integration are essential to fully realize their clinical potential.

Prospective multicenter validation

In order to confirm the clinical value and generalizability of GFFC and GISPA, large-scale, prospective multicenter studies are urgently needed. These should evaluate diverse patient populations and surgical indications to assess reproducibility and outcome prediction [22, 23, 36].

AI-Enhanced imaging and analytics

AI has the potential to automate the identification of anatomical variants on CTA, MRA, and MRN scans, streamlining preoperative planning. AI-supported imaging could improve detection accuracy and standardize surgical strategies, especially in high-volume centers [3, 4].

Augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR)

AR allows intraoperative projection of vascular and neural maps onto the surgical field, facilitating real-time navigation and flap dissection. VR-based training platforms simulate complex reconstructions and may improve surgical preparedness and reduce intraoperative errors [29, 39].

3D Printing and biomechanical modeling

Patient-specific 3D-printed models based on imaging data enable surgeons to simulate complex flap designs, evaluate pedicle configurations, and plan segmental muscle transfers. Combined with biomechanical analysis, this may significantly enhance precision and flap function [19].

Tissue engineering and regenerative medicine

Advances in bioengineered GM constructs pre-vascularized and pre-innervated offer potential alternatives to autologous grafting. These constructs could reduce donor-site morbidity and expand reconstructive options in patients with contraindications for muscle harvest [19].

Closed-loop neuromodulation

Implantable EMG-guided stimulators, designed to support functional recovery in dynamic reconstructions, may enhance neuromuscular integration and minimize atrophy in denervated or reinnervated gracilis flaps [32].

Robotic-assisted microsurgery

Robotic platforms with enhanced precision and tremor reduction can support microsurgical tasks, particularly in delicate GM transfers for facial or upper limb reanimation. Robotic integration may also reduce fatigue and enable minimally invasive harvests [13].

Educational innovations

Cadaveric workshops, AR/VR simulations, and structured microsurgical training modules can help standardize the application of GFFC and GISPA, shortening the learning curve and ensuring reproducible clinical results [39].

Global accessibility and cost considerations

Cost-effectiveness analyses and equitable access to imaging technologies and surgical training are needed to extend the benefits of GFFC- and GISPA-based planning to low-resource settings. Telemedicine and cloud-based planning tools may facilitate this transition [16].

Through focused exploration and clinical implementation of these emerging directions and technological innovations, future gracilis-based reconstructions promise transformative improvements in surgical precision, patient safety, and functional outcomes, ultimately advancing the frontier of reconstructive surgery. Conclusion

The gracilis muscle remains a cornerstone in reconstructive microsurgery due to its reliable anatomy and low donor-site morbidity. However, anatomical variations spanning morphology, vascular pedicles, and innervation significantly influence surgical outcomes.

This review introduced two integrative frameworks, GFFC and GISPA, that translate anatomical diversity into structured, individualized surgical strategies. Supported by advanced imaging and emerging technologies such as AI, AR, and 3D modeling, these tools enhance surgical precision and functional recovery Future multicenter validation, combined with innovations in tissue engineering, robotics, and education, may further optimize gracilis-based reconstruction. Embracing anatomy-driven planning offers a transformative step toward precision surgery and personalized microsurgical care.
