Emergence of PALB2 Reversion Mutations as a Mechanism of Resistance to Niraparib in Breast Cancer: A Case Report
Authors: Maako Kawamura, Hiroshi Tada, Hidekazu Shirota, Miki Dobashi, Noriko Takenaga, Hiroyuki Yasojima, Narumi Harada‐shoji, Keigo Komine, Kenichi Nakamura, Minoru Miyashita, Hisato Kawakami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case study describes the clinical course of a 39‐year‐old woman with metastatic breast cancer harboring a germline PALB2 mutation who was treated with a PARP inhibitor. She initially demonstrated a clinical benefit with reduced tumor markers and favorable imaging findings. However, disease progression occurred after eight months. Liquid biopsy‐based genomic profiling identified three PALB2 reversion mutations that restored homologous recombination, leading to treatment resistance. The case illustrates both the therapeutic potential of PARP inhibitors in PALB2‐mutated cancers and the emergence of resistance. It emphasizes the importance of liquid biopsy‐based genomic profiling for understanding tumor evolution and guiding treatment strategies.Keywords: breast cancer, CGP test, PALB2, PARP inhibitor, reversion mutation

Introduction

Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) results from defects in the homologous recombination repair pathway, which is crucial for repairing DNA double‐strand breaks [1]. This deficiency causes the accumulation of DNA damage and genomic instability—hallmarks of many cancers, particularly those of the breast and ovaries. HRD is commonly associated with pathogenic variants of BRCA1 and BRCA2, genes that play key roles in maintaining genomic integrity [2]. Additionally, pathogenic variants in homologous recombination genes, such as PALB2, ATM, and CHEK2, have been reported to cause HRD. The therapeutic efficacy of poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors is well documented in cancers harboring HRD [3, 4]. These inhibitors exploit the compromised DNA repair capacity of HR‐deficient cells, inducing widespread genetic damage that leads to genotoxic cell death. Consequently, PARP inhibitors have become an established treatment option in clinical practice, particularly for cancers with BRCA1/2 mutations. Additionally, PARP inhibitors may be effective in tumors with HRD caused by pathogenic variants in genes other than BRCA1/2. For instance, the Olaparib Expansion Trial (TBCRC 048) demonstrated the efficacy of PARP inhibitors in patients with metastatic breast cancer harboring germline PALB2 mutations [5]. Despite these findings, evidence supporting the efficacy of PARP inhibitors in cancers with non‐BRCA HR gene mutations remains limited. Consequently, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved PARP inhibitors only for patients with metastatic breast cancer harboring BRCA1/2 mutations. With recent advances in cancer genomics, more individuals carrying pathogenic variants in HRD‐related genes are being identified [6, 7].

Here, we report the case of a breast cancer patient harboring a germline PALB2 mutation who was treated with the PARP inhibitor niraparib. Although this patient initially exhibited a favorable response to therapy, she eventually exhibited disease progression. Genomic profiling of samples obtained via liquid biopsy revealed the emergence of PALB2 reversion mutations, which were associated with acquired resistance to niraparib and subsequent disease progression.

Case Presentation

A 39‐year‐old woman was diagnosed with metastatic luminal B/HER2‐positive breast cancer 13 years prior to the presentation at our hospital (Figure 1A). The patient had no family history of breast or ovarian cancer. She underwent mastectomy and axillary lymph node dissection following neoadjuvant chemotherapy, comprising eight cycles of fluorouracil, epirubicin, and cyclophosphamide, followed by four cycles of docetaxel. Her postsurgical adjuvant therapy included tamoxifen; however, recurrence was confirmed 7 years postsurgery. The patient subsequently received nine different lines of therapy, including anti‐HER2 and endocrine treatments. In October 2023, Comprehensive Genomic Profiling using the FoundationOne CDx on a skin metastasis biopsy identified a PALB2 p.Arg239Aspfs*40 variant with a 51.5% variant allele frequency (VAF). Additionally, the HRD signature was positive (Table 1). Subsequent germline testing, conducted by FALCO Biosystems, confirmed this to be a germline variant of PALB2. In Japan, PARP inhibitors are unapproved for reimbursement under the national health insurance for breast cancer patients with PALB2 mutations. However, based on evidence supporting PARP inhibitor efficacy in PALB2‐mutant breast cancer (the TBCRC 048 study), the patient was enrolled in a basket clinical trial (the BELIEVE trial, NCCH1901) in June 2024 and was assigned to the niraparib cohort [5, 8]. Thus, she commenced niraparib therapy at a dose of 200 mg once daily. At trial enrollment, her disease had progressed to involve skin and multiple bone metastases as well as pleural dissemination with pleural effusion.

The patient received niraparib monotherapy for 8 months, demonstrating a clinical benefit (Figure 1B,C). At 4 months, CT scans revealed reduced pleural thickening, marked resolution of the pleural effusion, and visible shrinkage of skin metastases. Additionally, a slight decrease in the levels of the tumor marker, CA15‐3, was noted. After 8 months of niraparib therapy, the patient's CA15‐3 levels began to rise. CT imaging at 10‐month follow‐up revealed stable pleural lesions and progressive enlargement of the skin metastases. Thus, suspecting niraparib resistance, we performed a Guardant360 liquid biopsy. This revealed the primary PALB2 p.Arg239Aspfs*40 variant (VAF 47.3%), alongside three newly acquired somatic mutations: PALB2 p.Ser254fs (VAF 1.7%), PALB2 p.Asn241fs (VAF 0.2%), and PALB2 p.G232_R244delinsDLISPK (VAF 0.1%) (Figure 2). According to Guardant Health, these somatic mutations occurred on the same allele as the primary germline PALB2 variant, all of which were estimated to restore protein function. These reversion mutations occurred between 21 bp and 44 bp from the original germline mutation and resulted in a 14–15 amino acid change in PALB2 (Figure 2). Therefore, we concluded that drug resistance developed with the disease's progression and the emergence of reversion mutations. Consequently, niraparib treatment was discontinued.

Discussion

This case highlights key aspects of managing breast cancer associated with germline PALB2 mutations, particularly the potential efficacy of PARP inhibitors and the emergence of resistance via reversion mutations. Currently, PARP inhibitors are not approved for PALB2‐mutant breast cancer by the U.S. FDA or in Japan, underscoring the need for formalized regulatory processes and standardized protocols for PALB2 testing and treatment guidance. In our case, a PARP inhibitor was effective in a patient with a germline PALB2 mutation—an off‐label scenario not addressed in current guidelines.

PARP inhibitors induce antitumor effects by inhibiting single‐strand break repair through the accumulation of double‐strand breaks in HR‐deficient cells via replication fork collapse [3, 4]. However, resistance inevitably develops under long‐term treatment. Previously reported resistance mechanisms in BRCA1/2 mutations include HR function restoration due to secondary mutations in HR‐related genes, replication fork stabilization, activation of drug efflux pathways, and loss of PARP1 [9]. The PALB2 reversion mutation identified in this case represents a classic example of resistance acquisition based on HR function restoration among these mechanisms. Multiple reversion mutations were detected via liquid biopsy, and all are presumed to restore DNA repair function through the recovery of the open reading frame. The coexistence of multiple mutations reflects genetic heterogeneity within the tumor and supports the treatment challenges associated with metastatic tumors.

The germline variant was a single‐base deletion in exon 4 of the PALB2 gene, causing a frameshift and premature stop codon, yielding a truncated, nonfunctional protein (Figure 2, second row). Liquid biopsy later identified three additional somatic PALB2 variants that restored the open reading frame and HR function (Figure 2, third to fifth rows). These reversion mutations, selected under therapeutic pressure, diminished niraparib efficacy and drove disease progression. From a therapeutic perspective, new strategies are being explored to overcome PARP inhibitor resistance. Preclinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of novel drugs targeting other DNA‐damaging repair pathways such as ATR or WEE1, suggesting that these approaches restore or maintain sensitivity to PARP inhibitors and mitigate resistance driven by genomic evolution [9].

Reversion mutations, especially in BRCA1/2, are known resistance mechanisms to PARP inhibitors and platinum‐based chemotherapy. Similarly, PALB2 reversion mutations, though less frequently reported, have been identified as a resistance mechanism in breast and prostate cancers [10, 11, 12]. Unlike BRCA1/2, its clinical features, prognosis, and treatment response to PARP inhibitors remain poorly understood. Our case provides valuable documentation of pre‐ and post‐treatment genetic testing confirmation and treatment evaluation. These often arise polyclonally, reflecting the genetic heterogeneity of metastatic disease. Distinct subclones can develop unique reversion mutations independently across metastatic sites. In our case, the identified three PALB2 reversion mutations restored the reading frame downstream of the germline variant, preserving DNA repair function. Liquid biopsy revealed several genomic alterations, including high tumor mutational burden and gene amplifications not seen in earlier tissue biopsies [13]. These findings underscore the advantage of liquid biopsy in capturing tumor heterogeneity across space and time, supporting its role in precision oncology. Despite early therapeutic success—such as control of pleural thickening and effusion—the patient eventually exhibited a mixed response, with skin metastasis progression and rising tumor markers. This outcome illustrates the difficulty of managing metastatic breast cancer with high genetic diversity and evolving resistance mechanisms. Serial blood sampling for genomic profiling is critical for monitoring tumor evolution, identifying resistance pathways, and guiding therapy adjustments.

On the other hand, this case study also has limitations. As a single case, it does not directly verify functional recovery through reverse mutation, which constitutes an interpretative constraint. Currently, PALB2 testing is not widely adopted in breast cancer care, partly due to the absence of approved targeted therapies like PARP inhibitors. Establishing robust testing and treatment strategies for individuals with pathogenic PALB2 variants is essential. Further research is needed to optimize screening and expand therapeutic options to improve outcomes in this patient population.
