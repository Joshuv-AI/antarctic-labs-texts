A comprehensive China topsoil dataset of high-resolution microstructure, topographical characteristics and physical properties
Authors: Xueying Li, Huanyu Duan, Xiaofan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoil microstructure regulates the transport and reaction of air, water, and nutrients within terrestrial ecosystems. However, high-resolution soil microstructural data remain scarce due to the inherent heterogeneity and opacity of soils as well as the technical and financial limitations of advanced imaging. In this study, we present a novel 3D soil microstructure dataset that integrates high-resolution imaging data of soils across China (CHARM3D) with comprehensive measurements of topological and physical properties to quantitatively characterize soil morphological parameters. The dataset comprises high-resolution X-ray computed tomography (XCT) reconstructions of 120 soil samples from 30 sites across six major Chinese ecosystems, with morphological, topological, and physical parameters derived through advanced image processing and pore-scale modeling. A case study analyzing representative samples from six ecosystems illustrates the dataset’s ability to provide key parameters for reactive transport modeling, including pore connectivity, permeability, and porosity distribution. The dataset provides a general framework for characterizing porous media structure and supporting the calibration of soil hydraulic models, with potential applicability to other porous materials such as deeper soil horizons and rock samples.

Background & Summary

Soil microstructure, defined as the arrangement of microscopic solid particles and the spatial organization of pore networks1, plays a crucial role in regulating key soil processes, including fluid flow and solute transport, carbon and nutrient cycling, as well as root growth and microbial activity2–7. For example, soil microstructure significantly influences hydraulic conductivity and serves as an important indicator of soil parameterization for local and global Earth system models1. Moreover, soil microstructure could affect soil biogeochemistry by regulating microbial functions, which is crucial for sustaining ecosystem services such as agriculture and soil and water conservation8–11. The study of soil microstructure, also referred to as soil morphology, focuses on the geometry of soil at the microscale, which is described using topological parameters, as well as on physical properties of soil that are traditionally addressed in pedology12,13. Soil morphology provides critical insights into both the functional dynamics of ecosystems14 and the relation of microscopic transport mechanisms with macroscopic transport parameters and phenomena in soils15–19.

Existing soil datasets are inadequate for detailed quantification of soil microstructure and are unable to provide reference parameters for modeling coupled hydro-bio-chemical processes in subsurface. For instance, Zhang et al. (2021) introduced a classification scheme for Earth’s critical zones, considering several key factors such as climate, parent material, soil type, groundwater depth, geomorphology, and land use, providing a broader context for soil studies20. Recently, Batjes et al. (2024) presents the “World Soil Information Service (WoSIS) 2023 snapshot”, which provides quality-assessed and standardized soil profile data for global soil mapping and environmental modeling21. Doetterl et al. (2025) emphasize the need for a deeper understanding of soil formation, weathering, erosion, and redistribution across diverse climate zones to improve soil carbon predictions22. However, comprehensive soil datasets, particularly those that capture microscopic-level structural information essential for developing predictive models of subsurface ecological processes, are still limited23.

The limited availability of microstructural soil data is primarily due to the considerable difficulties associated with obtaining and characterizing detailed soil structure at the microscopic level. The inherent heterogeneity of soil across spatial, temporal, chemical, physical, and biological dimensions necessitate the collection of numerous region-specific microstructure samples to accurately capture its variability23,24. Additionally, the opaque nature of soil complicates the retrieval of undisturbed samples, as excavation and handling can alter its original structure. Even when intact samples are successfully obtained, high-resolution microstructural analysis relies on advanced imaging techniques, which are both costly and require specialized expertise. Moreover, describing soil microstructure typically involves mathematical and geometric frameworks, such as fractal principles25, morphological quantification methods12, and Minkowski functionals17,26–28. However, the direct application of these theoretical models in soil science remains complex and challenging. These limitations collectively hinder the development of comprehensive soil microstructure datasets, thereby reducing the accuracy of hydrological and biogeochemical models.

Despite these challenges, recent advancements in high-resolution imaging and measurement techniques have significantly improved morphological quantification of soil and rock samples, as well as the analysis of transport behaviors29–33. These innovations hold promise for overcoming current barriers in soil microstructure characterization and enhancing the predictive capabilities of soil-related models. Among these techniques, X-ray computed tomography (XCT) stands out as a highly effective, nondestructive method that yields reliable 3D microstructure data34–39. These data are essential not only for analyzing intrinsic soil microstructures but also for supporting longitudinal studies that monitor the evolution of soil structure in relation to various physical and biogeochemical processes26,40. Previous studies have reviewed the theory and applications of XCT in hydrology and hydrocarbon reservoirs38, improvements in resolution41, and reconstruction techniques for soil samples42. For example, Schlüter et al. (2020) characterized pore scale structural and biogeochemical heterogeneities by extracting the 3D pore networks through XCT imaging43. Menon et al.44 and Diel et al.45 used XCT to quantify soil aggregates and measured parameters such as particle sizes, shapes, contact points, and free volume, which help assess aggregate stability and crack dynamics under wetting-drying cycles44,45. More recent developments have focused on real-time X-ray velocimetry, which utilizes high-resolution XCT with rapid imaging rates34. Bultreys et al. (2016) used time-resolved XCT to study pore-scale transport processes, enabling real-time observations of fluid flow and reactive transport in geological materials30. This technique enables the capture of dynamic ecological processes in soil, including infiltration, nutrient transport, and solute mixing in underground systems46. Moreover, the combination of XCT with pore scale modeling has long been recognized as a promising approach for studying coupled physio-biochemical processes in soils40. One of the earliest examples includes the work of Coles et al. (1991, 1998) and Spanne et al. (1994), who employed synchrotron XCT to visualize fluid transport and soil topological structure47,48. These 3D datasets were subsequently used for direct simulations of permeability, flow pathways, and transport dynamics41. Recent work by Sanematsu et al. (2019) introduces a pore-scale model based on XCT-derived geometries and Lagrangian tracking to simulate nanoparticle transport and assess the influence of particle size, surface forces, flow rate, and mineralogy49. In a word, the use of XCT-derived pore structures into numerical pore-network models has become a widely adopted practice38.

Quantifying morphological characteristics such as phase fractions, particle sizes and shapes, and pore networks has significantly advanced the ability to describe soil microstructure by integrating mathematical and geometric frameworks into software equipped with advanced image processing techniques. Specialized software, such as Avizo 4.1 and VG Studio Max 3.4, is widely used for high-resolution 3D analysis. Additionally, open-source programs like ImageJ/FIJI50 provide powerful alternatives, offering plugins tailored for specific applications, enhancing their versatility in soil microstructure analysis. Notable plugins include SoilJ51 for soil structure analysis, BoneJ52 for bone morphometry, and MorphoLibJ53 for general morphological analysis, making them essential tools for processing and interpreting 3D porous media. Integrating high-resolution XCT with advanced imaging processes enables the development of comprehensive soil microstructure datasets, though it demands considerable effort for in-situ sample collection. While Weller et al. (2022) introduced the open-access Soil Structure Library (https://structurelib.ufz.de/) for global contributions, national-level comprehensive soil structure datasets are still lacking23.

We developed a first 3D soil microstructure dataset using the high-resolution XCT technique across China (CHARM3D). 32 sampling sites were selected from ecosystem stations within the Chinese Ecosystem Research Network (CERN)54, representing 6 typical ecosystems: forests, grasslands, deserts, agricultural fields, wetlands, and lakes. A total of 120 soil samples were collected, which covered a wide range of soil orders within the Chinese Genetic Soil Classification System (CGSCS)55 (Fig. 1). We reconstructed soil microstructures and quantified morphological parameters, including: (1) topological characteristics of pore network structures, derived from XCT imaging; (2) soil physical properties, such as soil organic matter and permeability, which was estimated through image processing method and pore-scale modeling approach, respectively. These parameters were used as indicators to evaluate soil functions, with a focus on pore networks and the influence of pore connectivity on permeability. This study offers valuable insights for calibrating soil hydraulic models in regional studies.

Methods

The CHARM3D soil microstructure dataset was generated through three main steps: in-situ field sampling, laboratory XCT scanning, and morphological analysis. First, sampling sites were selected based on soil taxonomy and ecosystem types. Second, the 3D microstructure of the samples was captured through XCT scanning and processed using image analysis techniques. Finally, key morphological features were investigated through three kinds of analytical approaches: (1) pore geometry quantified using Minkowski functionals, pore throat analysis as well as network metrics derived from network analysis; (2) measurement of soil organic matter; and (3) permeability estimation using pore-scale modeling via computational fluid dynamics (CFD) methods.

Field sites and sampling strategy

In this study, 30 sampling sites across China were selected based on the maps of CERN and the Soil Regionalization of China. The sampling included six distinct ecosystem types: agricultural ecosystems (9 sites, 9 × 4 samples), forest ecosystems (7 sites, 7 × 4 samples), grassland ecosystems (7 sites, 7 × 4 samples), desert ecosystems (2 sites, 2 × 4 samples), lake ecosystems (3 sites, 3 × 4 samples), and wetland ecosystems (2 sites, 2 × 4 samples) (Table 1). The sampling sites were grouped to facilitate comparisons among different ecosystem types (Fig. 2). A total of 120 in-situ undisturbed soil core samples were collected using soil sampling rings and carefully packed with polystyrene. All soil samples were collected during dry weather conditions in the summer season. Sampling was avoided during or immediately after rainfall or freezing events to minimize the influence of short-term climatic factors on soil structure. No sieving or removal of stones or plant roots was performed before XCT scanning to preserve the original soil microstructure. Four parallel samples were obtained from each site. The 120 soil core samples represent a diverse range of soils classified according to the CGSCS55, including Alfisols, Semi-Alfisols, Primarosols, Ferrallisols, Desert soils, Alpine soils, Aqueous soils, Pedocals, and Anthrosols, the latter reflecting soils influenced by long-term farming and irrigation practices. The topsoil (0–10 cm) was sampled using the soil ring sampling method. We used PVC rings (5 mm wall thickness, 10 cm inner diameter, 10 cm height) instead of stainless steel to prevent interference with XCT scanning results from metal walls. Before each sampling, we selected flat ground and cleared non-soil surface materials to minimize sampling errors. GPS devices were then used to record the longitude, latitude, altitude, and other information of the sampling points. At each sampling site, four parallel samples were collected at 50 cm intervals in a horizontal line. Samples were sealed with Styrofoam and wrapped in shockproof materials during transportation to preserve in-situ soil pore structure. Samples were refrigerated at 5 °C upon arrival and scanned within 24 hours. The sampling workflow is shown in Fig. 2.

XCT scanning and image processing

All soil samples are scanned using X-ray computed tomography (XCT) immediately upon arrival at the laboratory. XCT is a non-destructive imaging technique that provides detailed three-dimensional (3D) structural information of materials by capturing a series of two-dimensional (2D) radiographs from multiple angles and reconstructing them into a 3D tomographic dataset40. The scanning process involves the use of an industrial XCT system (ZEISS METROTOM 1500 225 kV G3), which enables high-resolution imaging of the in-situ soil columns.

XCT imaging relies on the differential attenuation of X-rays as they pass through a sample40. As illustrated in Fig. 3(a), a vacuum X-ray tube generates high-energy electrons by accelerating them towards a tungsten target. When these electrons collide with the target, X-ray photons are emitted due to Bremsstrahlung radiation and characteristic X-ray emission. In Fig. 3(b), a cone-shaped X-ray beam with an initial intensity I0 is emitted from the source and directed onto a uniformly rotating sample. After completing the scanning process, the reconstructed data are further analyzed and processed using VG Studio MAX 3.4 for image processing. Each soil sample generates 3,000 image slices, each with dimensions of 3024 × 3024 pixels. A region of interest (ROI) with 46.31 mm diameter and 74.10 mm height is extracted, and the corresponding 3D volume data were exported as 16-bit grayscale image slices in TIFF format. To minimize noise, the images are processed using a three-dimensional median filter with a kernel size of 2 × 2 × 2. Binary images were generated from the processed XCT images using the Phansalkar thresholding method (Phansalkar et al., 2011) implemented in ImageJ. The thresholding parameters were set to the recommended values (k=0.25, r=0.5, p=2, q=10) and applied consistently to all images to ensure methodological consistency and reproducibility across the large XCT dataset.

The morphological analysis of 3D soil microstructure

In this section, we present the methodology for quantifying 3D soil microstructures. Soil topological analysis is first introduced as a robust approach for characterizing soil geometry. This includes the calculation of Minkowski functionals using ImageJ, pore throat analysis with Avizo to evaluate pore connectivity, and network analysis to assess pore connectivity, flow, and transport properties. Then we introduce two fundamental soil physical properties that are difficult to calculate using traditional methods: soil organic matter (SOM), determined via image analysis, and permeability, estimated using pore-scale simulations in COMSOL Multiphysics 6.3.

Soil topological analysis

Minkowski analysis

Pore throat analysis

Pore throats, the interconnected channels between soil pores, are key morphological features that characterize soil pore connectivity61. They serve as crucial indicators for understanding the transport of water, gases, and nutrients within soils, providing valuable insights for improving agricultural practices62,63. The throat radius and coordination number are two fundamental parameters used to quantify the distribution of pore throats in soil samples. The throat radius characterizes the size of channels between pores, which directly impacts fluid flow properties64. The coordination number, a key structural metric of the pore network, represents its connectivity, with higher values generally indicating enhanced pore connectivity and improved soil permeability61.

This study adopted pore throat analysis to quantify pore network characteristics by calculating the throat radius and coordination number using Avizo software27. High-resolution 3D soil images were processed to extract pore throat information. First, the image segmentation function in Avizo was utilized to differentiate soil pores from solid components. Next, the Axis Connectivity analysis tool was used to assess pore connectivity and determine the coordination number, which represents the number of neighboring pores connected to each pore. In the pore network model, the coordination number is defined at the pore scale as the number of throats connected to a given pore (i.e., the node degree). Two pores are considered connected when at least one throat is identified between them. Accordingly, the coordination number is automatically computed by Avizo from the extracted pore throat topology of the binary images, without the application of user-defined connectivity thresholds. Finally, the Generate Pore Network Model analysis tool was applied to identify the connecting channels (throats) between pores and calculate the throat radius for each pore65.

Network analysis

To further investigate soil morphology, we applied network analysis by modeling soil as a system of interconnected pores61,66. In our framework, pores are treated as nodes in a complex network that transmits information. The idea of network analysis was originally developed to study social interactions by quantifying relationships and pathways67,68. Wang et al.61 borrowed this idea to study flow transport properties in soil pore structures and link soil topological properties with flow transport behavior61. In this section, we applied Wang et al.’s61 method to compute three global indicators of the pore network, i.e., clustering coefficient (C), average distance (D), and average degree (K), as well as one local indicator, node betweenness centrality (NBC), for each pore61.

Before calculating the four indicators for soil samples from different ecosystem types, we determined the representative elementary volume (REV) for each sample based on the distribution of pore coordination numbers across six ecosystems. The REV is defined as the smallest control volume of the soil structure that can yield flow and transport parameters representative of the entire sample17,69. We extracted cubic sub-volumes of varying sizes from the soil samples and plotted the distribution of pore coordination numbers for each sub-volume (see the extraction method in Fig. 5). Since the coordination number is defined at the pore scale, each sub-volume yields a distribution of coordination numbers rather than a single value. As the cube size increased, the cumulative coordination number curves progressively converged and exhibited minimal variation, indicating that the pore connectivity statistics became insensitive to further increases in volume. This convergence of the cumulative coordination number distribution was therefore used as the criterion for identifying the REV. Based on these observations, we finally selected N=2 as the REV size with a resolution of 11.50 mm for each soil sample, except for the lake ecosystem, where N=3. Calculation of soil physical properties

Analysis of soil organic matter

Soil organic matter (SOM) plays a central role in soil biogeochemical processes and ecosystem functioning, including nutrient regulation and soil aggregate stabilization73,74. In this study, SOM is operationally quantified as the volumetric proportion of organic material within the soil matrix based on XCT image segmentation. While recent studies have introduced imaging-based methods to address the limitations of traditional physical fractionation, such as structural disruption and loss of spatial context, this study used a machine learning-based identification method with ilastik 1.4.075 (https://www.ilastik.org/) to automate the classification and detection of SOM in intact soil structures. High-resolution CT images of the scanned soil were first denoised and contrast-enhanced to improve image quality. The processed images were then imported into ilastik 1.4.0, where regions within the images, such as organic matter, minerals, and air pores, were manually annotated to generate a training dataset. ilastik 1.4.0 extracted features such as color, texture, and shape from the annotated data and trained a machine learning model. By iteratively optimizing the training data and adjusting model parameters, the trained model could automatically distinguish organic matter from other soil components based on the extracted features76. The total volume of organic matter identified by the image segmentation was summed and normalized by the total soil volume to obtain an imaging-based volumetric proportion of SOM.

Permeability calculation

Permeability is an intrinsic physical property of the soil pore structure that quantifies the flow and solute transport behavior17. In this study, we simulated flow transport in each soil sample and calculated soil permeability using combined software tools: Avizo 4.1 (https://www.thermofisher.com/avizo), Netfabb 2024 (https://www.autodesk.com/products/netfabb), JHNY-DPM V377,78 and COMSOL Multiphysics 6.3 (https://www.comsol.com/release/6.3). The REV structure of each soil sample from high-resolution 3D XCT images was extracted using Avizo 4.1 and exported as an STL mesh file. Netfabb 2024 was used to repair defects and irregularities in the reconstructed pore structure, ensuring a simulation-ready triangular mesh. The corrected STL file was imported into COMSOL Multiphysics 6.3 for permeability calculations. We simulated the incompressible flow transport process by solving the Navier – Stokes equation. Permeability was calculated by solving Darcy’s law79. Given the low Reynolds number, the Pervasive Flow Interface was applied as the interface of the flow within the soil pore structure. As shown in Fig. 6, the inlet boundary condition is a fixed velocity of 10−4 m/s, while the outlet boundary condition is a fixed pressure of 0. All walls are treated as no-slip boundaries. The computational domain corresponds to the REV size of the samples, as determined from the previous network analysis of each ecosystem.

The CHARM3D dataset, including high-resolution 3D microstructure data, topographical characteristics, and physical properties, is publicly available at 10.5281/zenodo.15089552 and currently includes data from six samples representing six distinct ecosystems81. The CHARM3D dataset provides 3D soil microstructures in TIFF format, which can be used as direct input for reactive transport models or as raw data for calculating morphological parameters. Data Overview

The CHARM3D is consist of 120 in-situ soil core samples collected across 30 sites in China, representing 6 distinct ecosystem types: agricultural, forest, grassland, desert, lake, and wetland ecosystems. Figure 7 demonstrates the overview of the soil micro-structure dataset. It is divided into two linked components, each identified by a unique sample ID to facilitate data integration and further synthetical applications. The first dataset has 3.88 TB of high-resolution 3D soil column microstructures (TIFF format) along with site-level soil classification, environmental characteristics (temperature, humidity, landscape), and geographic coordinates. The second dataset, compiled through 3000 hours of postprocessing, comprises 600 GB of sample-specific soil structure data, encompassing morphological parameters and key physiochemical properties such as soil texture, organic matter content, and permeability. This dataset provides a comprehensive soil microstructure archive, which promotes multi-scale modeling of eco-hydrological processes in subsurface systems. Future studies can use this high-resolution dataset for comparative analyses, improving soil property predictions and land management strategies.

Technical Validation

In this section, we document the quality-control and validation procedures applied to ensure the reliability, reproducibility, and usability of the CHARM3D dataset. This section focuses on segmentation robustness, representativeness of extracted soil microstructures (REV), and numerical verification of derived products.

Validation of soil topological properties

Robustness checks of Minkowski functionals for 3D soil microstructure

Minkowski functionals and related topological parameters were computed from binarized 3D pore-solid volumes following the workflow described in Methods. As a basic technical validation, we screened the derived outputs for plausibility and logical consistency to detect potential processing artifacts (e.g., values outside physically meaningful bounds or violations of definitional relationships among parameters). Figure 10 summarizes the parameter distributions for six representative samples selected from six ecosystem categories. The resulting patterns are qualitatively consistent with typical microstructural characteristics expected for these ecosystems, supporting the plausibility of the released topological parameters.

Plausibility and consistency check of Minkowski-functional and related morphological parameters computed from six representative soil samples (Soil 15, 114, 65, 18, 42, and 37) selected from different ecosystem categories to span a broad range of microstructural heterogeneity. Box plots summarize (a) Area, (b) Circularity, (c) Convex Area, (d) Convexity, (e) Euler Number, (f) Perimeter Density, (g) Perimeter, and (h) Porosity derived from the corresponding image stacks. Ecosystem labels are used only for sample identification.

We then used correlation analysis as an internal-consistency and sanity-check approach for the Minkowski-derived and related morphological descriptors. Specifically, Pearson correlation matrices were computed within each representative sample to screen for potential processing artifacts and implausible parameter relationships (e.g., correlations that contradict definitional constraints or physical expectations, or abrupt sign inversions that may indicate segmentation/preprocessing failures). Figure 11 provides the correlation heatmaps for the six representative samples. These matrices are presented for quality-control transparency and user-side plausibility checking.

Plausibility checks for network-derived connectivity metrics

To validate that the pore-network analysis provides a plausible representation of soil pore connectivity, we extracted REVs from soil samples spanning six ecosystems and computed standard indicators, including clustering coefficient (C), average shortest-path distance (D), and average degree (K). These metrics are reported in Table 4. As technical validation, we screened the outputs for plausibility and internal consistency to detect potential extraction artifacts (e.g., excessive fragmentation into small isolated components, boundary-truncation effects, or values inconsistent with the REV size and voxel resolution). We also checked that repeated REV sampling within a sample yields stable indicator values within an acceptable variability range.

Further, NBC was computed as a diagnostic measure of network organization and as a quality-control to identify overly centralized connectivity that can arise from segmentation noise or topological bottlenecks introduced during network extraction. Figure 12 provides example network visualizations colored by NBC and the corresponding pore-radius and throat-length distributions for the representative samples. These distributions are included to support user-side plausibility checking (e.g., confirming physically meaningful ranges and identifying potential outliers) of the released pore-network indicators.

Summary

The Minkowski functionals and their correlations can robustly differentiate soil microstructural characteristics across ecosystems. The consistent ecosystem-dependent patterns confirm the technical reliability of the XCT-derived morphological metrics and their suitability for comparative soil structure analysis. The network-based metrics, including clustering coefficient, average distance, degree, and NBC, consistently capture differences in pore connectivity and network organization among ecosystems. This confirms that the extracted pore network models provide reliable representations of soil connectivity at the REV scale.

Soil physical properties

Physical checks for SOM

SOM volume fractions were derived from the segmented 3D volumes as described in Methods. As a plausibility check, all SOM fractions fall within physically meaningful bounds and are broadly consistent with ranges reported for comparable soils in the literature84–86, supporting that the SOM quantification is physically reasonable under the fixed segmentation workflow.

Numerical verification for permeability estimates

Figure 13 illustrates example 3D velocity streamlines from pore-scale flow simulations computed in COMSOL (v6.3) using the REV geometries and boundary conditions described in Methods. The streamline visualizations are provided as a sanity check of the released simulation outputs, confirming that flow is confined to connected pore pathways and that the resulting velocity fields are physically plausible under low Reynolds number conditions. Differences in streamline patterns primarily reflect variations in pore-space geometry and connectivity captured by the segmented volumes. The overall behavior is consistent with established understanding of flow through porous media and hydraulic testing concepts87,88 and with reported controls on hydraulic variability in soil systems89.

Porosity-permeability consistency check

Figure 14 illustrates the relationship between porosity and permeability across the representative samples and serves as a plausibility check of the permeability estimates. A strong positive correlation is observed, with higher porosity generally corresponding to increased permeability, reflecting the fundamental role of pore space availability in facilitating fluid flow. Importantly, the porosity-permeability relationship is not expected to be purely one-to-one because permeability is controlled not only by porosity, but by the combined effects of pore connectivity and pore geometry. Overall, these results support that the permeability values are broadly consistent with topological parameters derived from the same REV-scale pore-space representations.

The SOM measurements and pore-scale permeability simulations collectively validate the dataset’s capacity to link soil microstructure with key physical and hydraulic properties. These results demonstrate that the dataset is suitable for investigating structure-function relationships relevant to hydrological modeling and ecosystem processes.
