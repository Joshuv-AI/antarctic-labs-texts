In Situ Colorimetric LAMP Based on One-Step Modified Filter Paper to Screen Human Papillomavirus (HPV)16/18 from Clinical Samples
Authors: Tingting Jiao, Peng Sun, Shuang Tian, Yingjun Tan, Chunyan Wang, Guangjun He, Liujia Shi, Yang Zhang, Jianhua Li, Yin Gu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer is among the most common malignant tumors in women. The development of rapid screening techniques plays an important role in early screening for cancer treatment. We have developed an HPV screening method, which effectively combines the high-efficiency nucleic acid enrichment of chitosan-modified filter paper and the rapid visual detectability of colorimetric LAMP, along with the enhancement of the tolerance ability of the pH-sensitive LAMP reagent to acidic original samples, making the detection of HPV 16/18 easy to carry out and reliable, which is helpful for the epidemiological prevention and control strategies of HPV-induced cancer. This technique can simultaneously exhibit the “in situ amplification” capability of chitosan-modified filter paper and the nontemperature cycle dependence of visual LAMP detection. Therefore, DNA extraction and amplification can be performed efficiently and quickly within a single reaction where all DNA is concentrated in the QF paper disc. By embedding amino-modified filter paper into the plastic chip, a simple and reliable disposable chip was prepared for rapid HPV16 and HPV18 detection from clinical endometrial samples, and the results were 100% consistent with clinical diagnosis. More importantly, even after the sample was diluted 100-fold, HPV16/18-infected cells could be accurately identified, showing the advantages of the system in early cancer screening. Moreover, for endometrial samples containing plenty of cells, the filter paper could be used to enrich cells by filtration, preventing the acidic fluid from impacting pH-induced colorimetric LAMP detection and realizing direct amplification for HPV identification without nucleic acid extraction. This easy-to-operate system that can analyze a wide range of samples will be suitable for routine on-site HPV screening, dramatically extending the applications and utility for rapid, near-patient nucleic acid testing.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the second most common cancer in the female population worldwide, especially in developing countries that lack diagnostic facilities. The lack of universal screening programs leads to a high incidence in those countries. Currently, cervical cancer affects 265,000 women each year worldwide, and more than 90% of these deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1,2 The main cause of cancer is human papillomavirus (HPV). HPV is a nonenveloped double-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) virus with more than 100 genotypes. Those genotypes have been subdivided into the following groups: high risk (HR), probable high risk (pHR) and low risk (LR).3 Among those high-risk types, HPV16/18 types could integrate into the host genome with strong aggressive ability, leading to cervical neoplasia. Despite the regional differences in incidence statistics, HPV16/18 have been associated with ∼70% of cases of cervical cancer globally.4,5

The traditional method for screening cervical cancer is based on cytological testing for routine diagnosis (The Pap (Papanicolaou) test), which relies on the experience of operators for morphological observation.6,7 The participation of too many subjective factors leads to variable levels of sensitivity in screening. These methods are usually carried out in fully and well-equipped laboratories with good infrastructure, reliable electricity supply, and highly trained staff. Currently, the nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) of HPV DNA is the gold standard for HPV genotype identification since the viral genome can be detected in 99.7% of studied cervical cancer cases and exhibits higher sensitivity than conventional cytological screening tests.8−10 Over the past decade, precise HPV genotype identification has been achieved by nucleic acid analysis of the virus.11,12 Various NAAT methods for viral nucleic acid detection, such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR), linear array testing (hybridization), and loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), have been employed to diagnose HPV.13−16 However, PCR-based detection is time-consuming and requires expensive equipment and skillful technicians.17,18 The process of the linear array test is also cumbersome and time-consuming and therefore difficult to implement in viral endemic areas.

Therefore, it is important to develop a simple, rapid, and cost-effective method for the early detection of HPV DNA. Compared with PCR-based detection, isothermal amplification methods are better suited for on-site rapid detection, with the use of a much simpler and less expensive heating instrument to run the nucleic acid assay.19−21 These methods include LAMP,22−26 nucleic acid sequence-based amplification (NASBA),27 recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA),28,29 helicase-dependent amplification (HDA),30 and strand displacement amplification (SDA).31 Among these isothermal techniques, LAMP has been most widely researched for POC applications.21,23,32,33 The LAMP assay can be performed by examining the turbidity or color change of the reaction solution.34−36 For colorimetric detection of the LAMP assay, pH-sensitive dyes and metal ion indicators have been reported as color indicators in the literature.32,37,38 The result can be obtained quickly by observing the color change with the naked eye, which can further reduce the dependence on instruments.39

Although the process of HPV genotype identification could be greatly simplified although a visual LAMP assay, pretreatment of raw samples still relies on auxiliary equipment, such as centrifuges, for cell enrichment, separation, lysis and nucleic acid extraction, which increases the dependence of whole-process detection on equipment and reduces the potential for popularization and application. In an early study, our group reported several applications of nucleic acid analysis based on chitosan-modified filter paper that enabled the trapped DNA to be employed for downstream analysis with minimal loss.40−42 These “in situ amplification” approaches have been demonstrated to be highly appropriate for integration into a “sample-in-answer-out” system, as the DNA extraction and amplification can be accomplished in a single reaction chamber. Meanwhile, all the DNA extract concentrated from large-volume samples can be used for analysis without the need of elution, thereby enhancing the detection sensitivity and simplifying the microchip structure.

Toward the end, the focus of this study is to establish a reliable, nonlaborious and cost-effective laboratory protocol based on the WarmStart colorimetric LAMP methodology combined with chitosan-modified QF paper. The high-efficiency nucleic acid enrichment and simplicity of “in situ amplification” combined with the rapid visual detectability of colorimetric LAMP make HPV 16/18 detection easy to implement and reliable, helping epidemiological prevention and control strategies for HPV-induced cancer.

Materials and Methods

Design and Fabrication of Microdevices

The disposable plastic microchips for nucleic acid extraction and chromogenic LAMP detection with one-step modified quartz fiber (QF) filter paper were fabricated as previously described. Briefly, as depicted in Figure 1B, the DNA extraction microchip is made up of two layers (top and bottom): an upper PMMA (poly(methyl methacrylate)) layer with a thickness of 2.5 mm and an ARseal adhesive tape (Adhesives Research, Inc., GlenRock, PA). The microchip consists of three 15-μL reaction chambers with inlet and outlet channels for DNA extraction and amplification. A circular groove (3 mm in diameter, 200 μm in depth) upstream of the reaction chamber was used to place one-step modified QF paper.

Application and verification of “in situ amplification” DNA extraction with Amino filter Paper. (A) Schematic of the DNA capture mechanism. At a pH of approximately 5, DNA molecules are actively adsorbed onto the chitosan-modified fibers. Once DNA is on the fibers, the physical entanglement of the long-chain molecules with the fiber matrix can also assist capture. At a pH of 9, although DNA was not actively absorbed onto the fiber, DNA molecules remained bound due to the physical trapping of these long-chain DNA molecules within the fiber matrix against washing and elution. (B) Exploded view of the microchip. The microfluidic chip consists of an upper ARseal adhesive tape layer and a lower PMMA layer. A 3 mm-diameter disc of chitosan-modified QF filter paper was loaded in different chambers to exclude the presence of false positives or false negatives.

The test chamber of the chip was used for the extraction and detection of different samples, such as cells and endometrial samples. Control chamber (with template obtained from QIAamp DNA Micro kits or Standard samples) and NTC (no template control) chamber was set for positive and negative control, respectively. In order to evenly control the sample flowing through the filter paper when adding samples manually, we extended the inlet and outlet pipelines of the test chamber to avoid the fluid from short-circuiting when the liquid pressure is too high. A chitosan-modified QF paper of 3 mm in diameter was inserted between the single-sided adhesive tape membrane and the PMMA layer in the chamber.

We designed a custom-made Plexiglas clamp for the operation of chip. A syringe pump (PHD 22/2000, Harvard Apparatus, MA) was connected to the chip outlet through PTFE tubing and controlled the fluid flow through the chip chamber.

Modification of Quartz Filter Paper with Chitosan

All reagents were purchased and prepared as previously reported.43 In the process of QF filter paper modification, a piece of quartz filter paper (diameter of 47 mm) was treated with 20 mL methanol consisting of 5% GPTMS (3-Glycidyloxypropyltrimethoxysilane) and chitosan (0.001%, prepared with 0.1% acetic acid, pH 5.0), followed by 3 h incubation on a shaker. Here we conducted the surface silanization of the filter paper and the condensation reaction of chitosan with the epoxy group simultaneously, instead of realizing them independently in two steps in our earlier research, thereby distinguishing and defining the “one-step” and “two-step” modified filter papers.40 After modification, the filter paper was washed three times with methanol and acetic acid (0.1%, pH 6.0). The samples were dried thoroughly at 50 °C in a vacuum drying oven. Afterward, discs of 3 mm-diameter filter paper were cut out and stored in a sealed Petri dish at room temperature until further application.

Evaluation of the Chitosan-Modified Quartz Filter for DNA Extraction

Standard bacteriophage λ-DNA was utilized to validate the nucleic acid capture efficiency of the one-step modified QF paper. A series of standard samples were prepared by adding 5, 10, 25, and 50 ng of λ-DNA to 3 mm-diameter filter discs and then amplified to generate the standard curve for quantification. DNA samples were prepared with MES (2-(N-morpholino)-ethanesulfonic acid) at a pH value of 5.0 to a concentration of 4 ng/μL. Standard sample (5 μL) consisting of 20 ng of λ-DNA was pipetted onto the surface of the QF paper placed in the chamber (Figure 1B). Subsequently, ARseal adhesive tape was used to seal the chamber. 200-μL of Deionized water (DI water) was aspirated at a rate of 200 μL/min through the reaction chamber in order to wash the QF paper disc. At last, the microchip was disassembled in the UV3 HEPA PCR cabinet (Analytik Jena US LLC, Upland), and the disc was removed with disposable sterile forceps for real-time PCR analysis.

The same standard samples prepared in 1× Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) and DI water (pH = 7.0) were equally trapped by chitosan-modified QF paper for verifying the electrostatic capture mechanism. As a control, unmodified QF paper was employed to capture DNA concurrently. In order to test the nucleic acid retention capacity of the modified QF paper during elution, λ-DNA (20 ng) was first seized on the filter discs and subsequently washed with 200-μL of DI water. Subsequently, the discs were rinsed under a variety of elution conditions, for instance, 1 mL of DI water, 100 μL of PCR mixture (pH 8.5), and 100 μL of 1× Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0).

A pair of primers amplifying a 101-bp fragment was employed for real-time PCR analysis of λ-DNA. The 25 μL of PCR mixture prepared for each reaction was consisted of 0.425 μL of each primer (10 μM), 12.5 μL of 2× Premix Ex TaqII PCR MasterMix (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan), 11.65 μL of deionized water and the disc containing DNA templates. Real-time PCR was performed in a LightCycler96 (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The thermal cycling protocol comprised the activation of Taq polymerases at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles at 95 °C, 60 and 72 °C (with each lasting for 30 s), and a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min. Standard curves for quantification were generated using a series of diluted λ-DNA with QF paper. A set of primers amplifying the L1 gene from HPV genomic DNA was employed to verify the on-chip DNA extractions. The sequences amplified a 291 bp fragment and a 292 bp fragment, respectively. The mixture proportion and thermal cycling protocol of PCR were as described above. Standard curves for quantification were generated using a series of diluted HPV16/18 L1 gene standard DNA (National Institute of Metrology, China) with filter paper.

Evaluation of DNA Extraction from Cell Samples

Caski cells (FengHui Bio, Changsha, China) and HeLa cells (Institute of Basic Medical Sciences Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, Beijing, China), which contain specific genes of the HPV16 and HPV18 genotypes, respectively, were used to evaluate the capture efficiency from rough samples. The lysis of the two kinds of cervical epithelium cells was performed in an Eppendorf tube by mixing cells (10 μL cell culture suspension each) with 190 μL lysis buffer (0.1% CTAB (cetyltrimethylammonium bromide), 1.5 M NaCl, 100 mM MES, pH 5.0) and incubating at room temperature for 15 min. The lysate was drawn into the test chamber of the microchip at a flow rate of 200 μL/min, followed by washing with 200 μL of 1% Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate (SDS) and 200 μL of deionized water at the rate of 200 μL/min. In the end, the modified QF paper disc was taken out from microchip for real-time PCR analysis. The trapped DNA was quantified with the aid of a calibration curve created by adding serially diluted HPV16/18 L1 gene standard DNA to the QF paper discs. At least three repetitions were made for each data point to generate standard deviations. Conventional DNA extractions employing QIAamp DNA Micro kits (QIAGEN, Germantown, MD) were also performed by following the user manual.

Procedure of On-Chip DNA Extraction and Chromogenic LAMP Detection from Clinical Samples

Cervical epithelial cell samples were collected from volunteer patients. For clinical sample collection, a sterile saline cotton ball was inserted into the cervix and rotated after 5 s to take the cervical epithelial cells, followed by rinsing with sterile saline. Next, half of the cell suspension was mixed with lysis buffer and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The lysate was aspirated into the test chamber for DNA extraction at a rate of 200 μL/min, followed by rinsing with 50 μL of 1% SDS and 200 μL of deionized water at a speed of 200 μL/min. For comparison, the remaining cell suspension was subjected to DNA extraction with QIAamp DNA Micro kits (QIAGEN, Germantown, MD), and the eluent (equivalent to the volume of filter paper) was used for LAMP detection.

A 15-μL LAMP mixture for on-chip DNA extraction and LAMP detection was composed of 7.5 μL WarmStart Colorimetric LAMP 2X Master Mix (NEB, Franklin Lake, NJ), 4 μL LAMP primer mixture (Figure-SI-2), 0.5 μL Bst2.0 WarmStart DNA Polymerase (NEB, Franklin Lake, NJ), and 3 μL DI water or eluent. Once the LAMP mixture was loaded into the microchip, the inlets and outlets of the microchip were sealed with ARseal tape, and the chip was placed in a thermostat water bath (Chang’an Scientific Instrument, Beijing, China) at 65 °C for 30 min. Then, the microchip was removed for observation by the naked eye.

Results and Discussion

Mechanism of “In Situ Amplification” through DNA Extraction

As presented in Figure 1A, the capture process of “in situ amplification” for nucleic acid analysis via a “two-mode” method was used to extract nucleic acids. At a pH below 5, the positively charged chitosan layer can adsorb anionic DNA onto the fiber surface. Meanwhile, the physical entanglement of nucleic acid chains with the fibers can also enhance the nucleic acid capture efficiency, thereby achieving highly efficient DNA extraction. Chitosan is deprotonated when the pH is above 7; thus, electrostatic adsorption with DNA molecules is not possible. However, the physical trapping of nucleic acid molecules within the QF fiber matrix effectively prevents elution and enables in situ amplification.

In a previous study, two-step modified filter paper in which epoxy groups and amino groups were successively covalenced on the surfaces of filter fibers was utilized for forensic DNA typing of trace evidence and multiplex detection of pathogenic microorganisms.40,41 In this research, we achieved the one-step modification of a mixture of GPTMS and chitosan groups on filter paper by optimizing the concentration ratio of methanol to H2O and GPTMS to chitosan. Less chitosan was used for the modification of quartz filter paper, reducing the original modification time (more than 24 h) to less than 3 h, while still achieving excellent performance in nucleic acid extraction and enrichment ability.

To verify the characteristics of one-step modified filter paper on DNA extraction, we first evaluated the DNA capture efficiencies of the modified and unmodified QF filter paper. We directly pipetted 20 ng of λ-DNA onto the surface of the modified QF paper disc, followed by rinsing with DI water. The pH of the loading solution was carefully adjusted to 5, ensuring that chitosan becomes cationic for DNA extraction and DNA still remains intact from depurination.40 As shown in Figure 2A, the DNA capture efficiency of the modified filter paper was 96.9%, while unmodified QF paper can merely offer a DNA capture efficiency of 35.4%. This significant difference in the capture efficiency between the modified and unmodified QF paper clearly demonstrated that the ability to capture nucleic acids was enhanced due to the grafting of chitosan. In addition, the capture efficiencies of 24.6% and 30.6% obtained at pH 7.0 and 9.0, respectively, were similar to the capture rate of the unmodified QF paper, illustrating that under alkaline conditions, DNA fragments were captured in the QF fiber matrix only through physical entanglement rather than electrostatic adsorption.

Evaluation of DNA capture by one-step modified quartz filter paper. (A) Comparisons of the DNA capture efficiencies of λ-DNA among unmodified quartz filters (QFs) and chitosan-modified QF at pH 5, 7, and 9. (B) Elution of captured λ-DNA from filter paper in different conditions. Approximately 0.4, 9.5, and 7.9% of the captured DNA was washed off with DI water, TE buffer, and PCR buffer, respectively. (C) Comparisons of the Ct values of real-time PCR among one-step modified filter paper, two-step modified filter paper, one-step modified filter paper with additional enzymes, unmodified filter paper and the control without any filter. (n = 3, error bars represent one standard deviation).

To achieve “in situ amplification”, the modified QF paper needs to prevent DNA from being eluted at a pH higher than 7, so that no DNA is released when loading the amplification mixture. We first captured 20 ng of λ-DNA on the modified filter paper discs and then used DI water (1 mL), PCR mixture (100 μL,pH 8.5) and 1× TE buffer (100 μL, pH 9.0) to elute DNA. As depicted in Figure 2B, eluting with 1 mL of DI water fails to release any DNA and 99.6% of the DNA remained on the disc. When the discs were eluted with PCR mixture or 1× TE buffer, only 7.9% (1.60 ng) and 9.5% (2.20 ng) DNA were respectively detected in the eluants, while more than 90% of the DNA were still captured within the filter paper discs. These results confirmed that this novel modified fiber could enable in situ amplification.

In addition, the low absorption to DNA polymerases of chitosan-modified filter paper is also a key point for achieving nucleic acid analysis through in situ amplification. As shown in Figure 2C, PCR amplification with one-step modified filter paper demonstrated Ct values similar to those of the modified filter paper previously used,40 but slightly higher than other conditions. This may be due to the fact that the filter paper itself may adsorb a part of the nucleic acid amplification polymerase, resulting in a reduction in amplification efficiency. Furthermore, with additional DNA polymerase provided, the PCR efficiencies of the modified filters can be comparable to that of the controls without added filter discs (Figure 2C). Our team has been conducting nucleic acid analysis based on chitosan-modified filter paper for many years, and a variety of filter paper materials (such as glass fiber, Fusion 5 filter paper and glass fiber) are used for the nucleic acid extraction after modification. We found that glass fiber and Fusion 5 filter paper would produce a relatively high PCR inhibition effect, and the reason might be due to the large amount of nonsilica impurities (such as fiber binder).Therefore, high-purity silica fiber (QF filter paper) was used in this study, and the method of directly coating chitosan on the filter paper surface was replaced by covalent bond bonding, which significantly reduces the PCR inhibition effect.41,43 The low inhibition of nucleic acid amplification by the modified fiber could enable more sensitive tests in our system.

Evaluation of Chitosan-Modified Filter Paper for Low-Copy Number (LCN) DNA Extraction

An important advantage of “in situ amplification” is the enhanced sensitivity, which is achieved through the concentration of nucleic acid from a continuously flowing diluted sample into a single reaction. To assess the concentrating ability of the modified QF paper, a series of diluted HPV16/18 L1 gene templates with concentrations ranging from 2000, 1000, 500, down to 100 copies/mL were trapped by the modified QF paper and quantified to ascertain the concentration factors. Although a large volume of samples (1 mL) was used for extraction, we did not perform a low flow rate (such as ∼10 μL/min) for higher capture efficiency, which is too time-consuming for practical applications. In this study, a flow rate of 200 to 300 μL/min was selected for sample loading, so that even 1 mL of samples can be loaded within 5 min. As Figure 3A,B shows, the capture efficiency of the HPV16 and HPV18 L1 genes with chitosan-modified filter paper can reach more than 80 and 60%, respectively, at concentrations greater than 500 copies/mL. Furthermore, 64.0 and 50.6% nucleic acid capture efficiency can be achieved from the HPV16 and HPV18 L1 genes, respectively, even with only 100 copies of template in 1 mL of buffer.

DNA capture from low-copy HPV L1 gene samples. (A) Capture of diluted HPV16 L1 gene samples with concentrations ranging from 2000, 1000, 500, down to 100 copies/mL. (B) Capture of diluted HPV18 L1 gene samples with concentrations ranging from 2000, 1000, 500, down to 100 copies/mL (n = 3, error bars represent one standard deviation).

In addition, the volume of the 3 mm-diameter filter paper disc is only approximately 2.83 μL (0.4 mm in thickness), and the concentrated concentrations of HPV16 and HPV18 L1 genes enriched from 100 copies/mL could be calculated as 22.65 × 103 and 17.91 × 103 copies/mL, respectively, representing a more than 180-fold concentration rate from low-copy samples by chitosan-modified filter paper. According to our previous study on RPA detection of SARS-CoV-2,42 the nucleic acids captured by chitosan-modified filter paper were mostly concentrated on its side in contact with the upstream fluid, and only part of the filter paper was utilized for nucleic acid capture, indicating that a higher concentration efficiency can be achieved in reality.

Additionally, the limit of detection (LOD) of aminated filter paper for HPV identification was tested after 50, 100, 200, and 1000 copies of the HPV 16 and HPV 18 L1 genes were extracted from 1 mL of buffer. For comparison with aminated filter paper, the same sample was extracted by a QIAGEN kit for fluorescence PCR analysis. As shown in Figure S1, the Ct values of the HPV16 and HPV18 L1 genes (1000 copies/mL) extracted by aminated filter paper were 24.17 ± 0.23 (HPV16) and 28.16 ± 0.50 (HPV18), respectively. In contrast, the Ct values of fluorescence PCR using the QIAGEN kit were only 28.82 ± 0.98 (HPV16) and 32.51 ± 1.27 (HPV18), indicating an obvious disparity in ΔCt between our method and conventional DNA extractions using QIAamp DNA Micro kits (4.65 and 4.35 for the HPV 16 and HPV 18 L1 genes, respectively.) Meanwhile, the LOD of chitosan-modified filter paper for the detection of both HPV genotypes reached 50 copies/mL, and conventional DNA extractions were only 100 copies/mL and 200 copies/mL for HPV16 and HPV18, respectively.

On-Chip DNA Extraction and Chromogenic LAMP Detection

Another important advantage of chitosan-modified filter paper was that a bind–wash–elute protocol was not necessary for extracting DNA from various sample types for downstream amplifications, in which all bound DNA molecules can be condensed in a small disc for amplification, resulting in high sensitivity as well as uncomplicated microchip structures. As shown in Figure 4A,B, the whole extraction time of modified filter paper is less than 50% compared with traditional methods. Otherwise, reagent components used for nucleic acid extraction by modified filter paper mainly rely on cationic surfactants, without any organic solvents, which are hard to remove completely; thus, a further reduction in amplification efficiency in microchips was achieved.44

Evaluation of cell sample lysis and capture efficiency. (A, B) Processing protocol of cell culture samples tested on the microchip. Cell culture samples (10 μL) were mixed with lysis buffer and incubated for 10–20 min. The lysates were then loaded into the sample chamber of the microchip for DNA extraction. (C) Comparison of DNA extractions from two kinds of cell samples using the one-step modified quartz filter paper embedded in the microchip and the QIAampDNA Micro kit (n = 3, error bars represent one standard deviation).

As illustrated in Figure 4C, by using our modified filter paper, DNA extractions from 10 μL Caski and HeLa cells could produce 33.14 ± 2.46 and 30.80 ± 1.21 ng of DNA, respectively, while the QIAGEN kit only provided 7.87 ± 1.26 and 9.59 ± 0.56 ng of DNA with an elution volume of 50 μL. Due to the fact that the entire disc with extracted DNA can be directly utilized in the amplification buffer, the template concentration extracted from 10 μL of Casik cell culture (11.71 ng/μL, filter paper volume in 2.83 μL) was much higher (∼73×) than that provided by the QIAGEN kit (0.16 ng/μL, using 2 μL of DNA elution). According to the same calculation principle, the extracted DNA concentration ratio of 10 μL of HeLa cells from chitosan-modified filter paper (10.88 ng/μL) to the QIAGEN kit (0.19 ng/μL) corresponded to a factor greater than 57.

Next, we performed on-chip DNA extraction and chromogenic LAMP detection from a series of raw samples with 100, 500, and 2000 cells. To avoid false positive results, the same batch of modified filter paper was placed in a control chamber (QIAGEN or positive control) and an NTC (no template control) chamber for each experiment. Cell cultures were first mixed with lysis buffer and then incubated for 10 to 20 min. Then, the lysates were loaded into the extraction and detection chamber of the microchip, followed by purification and washing (Figure 5A). After DNA extraction, a mixture of 12 μL LAMP reagent and 3 μL DI water was loaded in the chamber for the subsequent reaction. For comparison, a total of 15 μL of LAMP mixture contained 3 μL of eluent acquired from the QIAamp DNA Micro kit loaded in the control chamber. The eluent was replaced with 3 μL DI water for the NTC control. As shown in Figure 5B, a significant color shift (from red to yellow) can be observed both for Caski and HeLa cells even when only 100 cells are extracted by modified filter paper. In contrast, at least 500 Caski cells should be utilized when traditional DNA extraction methods are implemented. For HeLa cell detection, over 2000 cells are needed. In addition to the excellent enrichment ability for nucleic acid detection, the whole process of nucleic acid extraction can be utilized in the simplified microchip instead of multistep centrifugation and ethanol drying by traditional methods, which can only add diluted eluent for detection. Due to these advantages, chitosan-modified filter paper is more suitable for real-time and rapid detection in the field.

Cell samples with a series of numbers ranging from 100, 500, and 2000 were mixed with lysis buffer and incubated for 10–20 min. The lysates were then loaded into the sample chamber of the microchip for chromogenic LAMP detection. On-Chip Chromogenic LAMP Detection from Clinical Samples

Ten clinical cervical samples were collected from volunteer patients to verify the effect of chitosan-modified filter paper on HPV genotype identification. All samples were tested according to the process shown in Figure 5A, and the results for all samples detected by the modified filter paper were consistent with the clinical diagnosis (Table S3). This result demonstrated that the rapid screening of HPV infection under outpatient conditions can be achieved efficiently based on this novel method.

However, in the initial stage of cancer, due to the limited number of viral particles available, the inefficiency of traditional elution methods for nucleic acid extraction lead to an insufficient concentration of obtained samples for downstream analysis, thereby reducing the sensitivity of detection and the early diagnosis of the disease. Thus, achieving a high enrichment of low copy number samples for nucleic acid detection is a formidable challenge for the early diagnosis of cervical cancer.

To demonstrate the high DNA enrichment ability of chitosan-modified filter paper for raw clinical sample detection, we diluted the cell suspension samples from HPV16- and HPV18-positive patients 25 times, 50 times, and 100 times, respectively, followed by on-chip extraction by modified filter paper. Even if the sample was diluted 100 times, a significant color shift could be observed by the naked eye when DNA extraction was performed by chitosan-modified filter paper (shown in Figure 6). In contrast, no color change was observed in 100-fold diluted HPV16- and HPV18-positive samples in the control chamber performing DNA extraction by the traditional method; thus, it was difficult to predict the early stage of the disease.

On-chip chromogenic LAMP detection with diluted clinical samples. (A, B) Clinical samples were diluted to 1/25, 1/50 and 1/100 for HPV16 and HPV18 chromogenic LAMP detection, respectively. The conventional DNA extractions were compared with one-step modified QF filter paper in processing the same handled clinical samples.

On the other hand, cervical samples containing many epithelial cells can be used for direct amplification without DNA purification, further reducing the detection time. However, the LAMP mix undergoes a mutable color shift with pH indicator dyes in an acidic environment, so a direct LAMP procedure will not likely be implemented with endometrial samples obtained from the endometrium (pH 4–4.5). To demonstrate our hypothesis, test buffer (1× PBS solution, pH = 5) was prepared to simulate the environment of endometrial samples. As shown in Figure S2, compared with the NTC group (loaded with 3 μL ddH2O and 12 μL LAMP mix), regardless of whether 3 μL of test buffer contained Caski cells, a significant color shift (red to yellow) occurred even without heating. Even if the test buffer is diluted 100 times, weakly positive results can be observed. This result shows that directly utilizing endometrial samples for pH-induced colorimetric LAMP detection leads to false positive results.

For the test of chitosan-modified filter paper, 30 μL test buffer with or without 5000 Caski cells was pumped through filter paper, rinsed with 30 μL of ddH2O, and then loaded with 15 μL LAMP mix for amplification. As we expected, a positive color change only occurred in the modified filter paper group with Caski cells after heating. Therefore, the adverse impact of acidic conditions on the pH-induced colorimetric LAMP mix can be eliminated by filter paper via a filtration effect, which was similar to the “in situ” nucleic acid extraction mentioned above but without the aid of electrostatic adsorption.

Since no nucleic acid extraction was needed and a microchip with simplified structure was designed for in situ amplification, this fully integrated microdevice directly shows potential to become a more dependable and economical visual LAMP analyzer for the rapid screening and diagnosis of HPV infection.

Conclusions

In summary, we developed a colorimetric LAMP combined with “in situ amplification” of nucleic acid extraction utilizing amino-modified filter paper for rapid HPV virus detection. This combined approach was capable of being conveniently integrated into a microfluidic chip because only 2 or 3 steps are needed for sample pretreatment and the system is dependent on nonvariable temperature cycles. Cell capture/lysis, DNA extraction, and LAMP amplification from endometrial epithelial samples can be realized in a simplified-structured microchip, without performing centrifugation and temperature cycling.

The main advantage presented by this method is the visual detection coupled with the high capture efficiency and “in situ amplification” capability because (i)Excellent nucleic acid capture ability is capable of extracting and concentrating DNA from a continuous flow of large-volume samples with minute quantities of DNA.(ii) The “in situ amplification” is capable of utilizing all the concentrated and captured nucleic acids for nucleic acid amplification analysis without any loss of DNA, thereby providing enhanced sensitivity. (iii) Through colorimetric LAMP detection, the results can be quickly identified by the naked eye, reducing the dependence on experimental equipment. Other merits of our method comprise its low cost and convenient portability; in addition, the method can be easily integrated with other functional units. Thus, we consider our method an ideal candidate to be popularized in remote or underdeveloped areas where the corresponding analysis equipment for rapid HPV infection screening is lacking.
