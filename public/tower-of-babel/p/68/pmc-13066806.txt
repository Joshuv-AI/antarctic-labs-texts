Genetic Dissection of Resistance to Pseudomonas amygdali pv. tabaci in Soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merr.] by Linkage Analysis
Authors: Seo Yoon Yang, In-Jeong Kang, Ji-Min Kim, Sungtaeg Kang, Sungwoo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWildfire, caused by Pseudomonas amygdali pv. tabaci (Pat), is a severe bacterial disease of soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merr.]. Enhancing resistance to wildfire is an important breeding goal, yet genetic studies remain limited. This study aimed to identify quantitative trait loci (QTL) associated with partial resistance to Pat using a Daepung × Taekwang recombinant inbred line (RIL) population. Sixty RILs were evaluated for resistance to the Pat isolate ‘W14-M1’. Two QTL associated with resistance to Pat were identified on chromosomes 4 and 5, explaining 14.1% and 19.1% of the phenotypic variance, respectively. Daepung contributed the resistance alleles at both loci. The identified genomic regions overlapped with previously reported resistance QTL for bacterial, fungal, and nematode pathogens. These findings indicate that Daepung is a valuable source of resistance to Pat, and the detected QTL may facilitate the development of molecular markers for pyramiding multiple disease resistance traits in soybean breeding program.Keywords: linkage analysis, Psuedomonas amygdali pv. tabaci, quantitative resistance locus, soybean

Soybean is an important food crop serving as a key source of protein and oil, with considerable economic value (Pagano and Miransari, 2016). As of 2025, Korea produced 156,145 tons of soybeans on 74,015 ha (Statistics Korea, 2025), and demand continues to rise due to its extensive use in food, animal feed, and as an industrial applications. To meet this growing demand, the Korean government has implemented policies to increase soybean self-sufficiency by expanding cultivation into former rice paddy fields (Yu et al., 2019). However, highly humid conditions typical of paddy soil create an environment favorable for the occurrence and spread of various soil- and airborne soybean diseases. This poses a significant challenge to protecting soybean yields from biotic stress.

Biotic stresses, including diseases and pests during the growing season, is a major constraint on soybean production (Savary et al., 2019). Between 2010 and 2019, annual soybean yield losses in the United States and Canada were estimated at approximately 1.1 million tons due to these infestations (Allen et al., 2017; Bradley et al., 2021). The economic impact of these diseases is particularly severe in specific regions; notable examples include soybean rust in South America (Yorinori et al., 2005) and Japan (Hossain et al., 2023), as well as bacterial pustule in East Asia (Kang et al., 2021).

Bacterial pustule of soybean caused by Xanthomonas citri pv. glycines is the most common soybean disease nationwide in Korea and has therefore been extensively studied among soybean bacterial diseases (Kang et al., 2021). Along with bacterial pustule, wildfire of soybean caused by Pseudomonas amygdali pv. tabaci (formerly Pseudomonas syringae pv. tabaci) was first reported in Korea in 2009 (Myung et al., 2009) and has become an increasingly prevalent bacterial disease affecting soybean. The pathogen primarily manifests during the late growth stages, with symptoms worsening in the late summer and early fall season when humidity levels increase, and temperatures remain relatively moderate. This infection results in irregular brown spots on the leaves and, in severe cases, premature leaf drop (Kang et al., 2016). In particular, the humid conditions of soybean-growing fields create an environment highly conducive to the occurrence of various diseases caused by airborne or soilborne pathogens.

Wildfire can occur in multiple crop species, including cowpea, eggplant, tobacco, and tomato, and may cause up to a 16% yield loss in tobacco alone (Joshi et al., 2020; Kakar et al., 2020). Toxin produced by P. amygdali pv. tabaci (Pat) is responsible for the symptom of wildfire in soybean (Turner and Taha, 1984). The disease is categorized into two types based on tabtoxin production: wildfire, caused by tox+ (toxin-producing) strains, and angular leaf spot, caused by tox− (non-toxin-producing) strains. Studies reported that bacterial pustules serve as natural infection sites for the wildfire bacterium, suggesting an association between the incidence of wildfire and bacterial pustule progression (Chamberlain, 1956; Hartman et al., 2015).

Due to its high incidence and severity, wildfire of soybean has been closely monitored in Korea. A nationwide investigation of major soybean-producing areas reported a wildfire incidence of 63% in 2014 and 32% in 2015 (Kang et al., 2016). While the impact of wildfire on soybean yields in Korea has not been officially reported, artificial inoculation experiments on a wildfire-susceptible soybean cultivar, ‘Taekwang’, demonstrated a yield reduction of 13% compared to non-inoculated controls (unpublished). The disease typically manifests during the late growth stages, becoming more severe in early fall under high humidity and moderate temperatures, leading to characteristic brown spots surrounded by yellow halos on the leaves and, in severe cases, premature leaf drop (Kang et al., 2016).

Despite the high occurrence rate and significant impact of wildfire on soybean production, its epidemiology and the life cycle of the causal agent remain poorly understood, with limited information available. Previous studies, including Hong et al. (2012), have indicated that delayed soybean planting can reduce the incidence of bacterial diseases, including wildfire, and that different planting dates have varying effects on resistant and susceptible cultivars.

Wildfire is the second most prevalent bacterial disease of soybean in Korea, frequently occurring in soybean fields and causing damage alongside bacterial pustule. Although studies on the wildfire of soybean remain limited, several Korean cultivars have shown resistance to Pat (Kang et al., 2015a). To date, only one study has identified nine quantitative trait loci (QTL) for resistance to Pat using linkage analysis (Seo et al., 2018). The lack of genetic studies on resistance to Pat has resulted in limited information regarding soybean varieties that exhibit complete resistance, as well as the genetic loci associated with resistance genes for wildfire in soybeans.

For this study, a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population derived from ‘Daepung’ × ‘Taekwang’ was utilized to identify QTL for resistance to wildfire in soybean. Daepung is a high-yielding Korean cultivar developed for fermented soy food and tofu production, originating from a cross between ‘Baekwoon’ × ‘Sinpaldal2’. Daepung has also been reported to exhibit relatively high levels of phenolic acids compared with other cultivars (Oh et al., 2019). Daepung is lodging-tolerant and resistant to bacterial pustule (Park et al., 2005). Taekwang is an older elite cultivar derived from a cross between ‘Gwanggyo (SS77011)’ × ‘Dongsan 53’ (Kim et al., 1992). Previous studies identified Daepung and Taekwang as resistant and susceptible to Pat, respectively (Kang et al., 2015a). The first artificial crosses between Daepung and Taekwang were performed to develop a mapping population at Dankook University, resulting in five F1 seeds obtained in the fall of 2012. An F6-derived population consisting of 134 RILs was developed by the single-seed descent method. In 2019, these seeds were obtained and propagated at the university experimental farm of Chungnam National University, Daejeon.

Young leaves were harvested from various plant species, then frozen and desiccated. DNA extraction was performed as described in Heo et al. (2022), utilizing the detergent Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide. Genotypic data from the Axiom 180K SoySNP Array (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was obtained for 131 genotypes, covering 169,028 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Prior to genetic analysis, the samples underwent pre-processing. First, the data set was filtered to exclude SNPs with missing or heterozygous alleles for either parent or that did not exhibit polymorphism between the two parents. Second, imputation was employed to supplement the filtered SNP data, resulting in a total of 21,243 SNPs. Subsequently, 2,168 SNPs were excluded because over 10% of the entire population were either missing or heterozygous. Then six RILs were removed due to missing values and heterozygosity exceeding 10% of the total SNPs and five RILs were filtered out due to seed unavailability, resulting in a final dataset of 19,075 SNPs across 123 RILs.

Owing to experimental capacity associated with phenotypic evaluation, it was not practical to phenotype all 123 RILs. Therefore, a representative subset of 60 RILs was selected to ensure efficient and accurate phenotypic assessment. Genetic relatedness among the 123 RILs was calculated using a neighbor-joining approach using TASSEL (version 5), and the two most genetically similar lines were iteratively paired in the phylogenetic tree. From each pair, one RIL was retained based on lower missing genotype rates and lower heterozygosity. By excluding genetically redundant lines, this procedure reduced the number of RILs evaluated while preserving the overall genetic variation of the population. The selected 60 RILs were subsequently used for phenotyping assays and QTL analysis.

During a nationwide field survey in 2014–2015, Pat isolates were collected and analyzed to investigate the occurrence and severity of wildfire in Korea (Kang et al., 2016). Among these isolates, W14-M1, collected in 2014 from Muan-gun, Jeollanam-do, was used for inoculation in this study. The isolate was stored at −80°C until use. Prior to inoculation, the isolate was thawed and plated on tryptic soy agar (TSA) four days before inoculation, followed by incubation for one day at 25°C in the dark. Individual colonies were then selected and spread on TSA plates, followed by an additional incubation under the same conditions for one more day. The cultured strains were then harvested with a cotton swab and plated again, followed by incubation for one day. For inoculum preparation, the cultured strains were suspended in a sterile MgSO4 solution (0.95 g MgSO4, 1 L of distilled water), and the inoculum concentration was adjusted to an optical density (OD600) of 0.4. Silwet® L-77 was then added to a final concentration of 400 μl/L before inoculation.

The phenotypic assays were conducted with a completely randomized design and were replicated three times. In a single replication, seeds of the selected 60 RILs and the two parents were sown in pots and grown to the V4 stage (single plant per pot) in a chamber (26°C, 14/10 h day/night). The individual V4 plants were inoculated by spraying the inoculum back and forth across the trifoliate with an air compressor (KAC-20SA; Keyang, Seoul, Korea) at a blowing pressure (1 kPa). After inoculation, disease development was induced for 7 days in a humidified room at 27°C with 90% relative humidity, maintained by a centrifugal humidifier (HR-50; Faran, Yongin, Korea). Seven days after inoculation, the inoculated leaves were photographed using a digital camera (ILCE-6000; Sony, Tokyo, Japan) to obtain RGB images. The percentage of diseased leaf area relative to the total leaf area (a single trifoliate) was calculated using the software Image J (Fig. 1). The final phenotype of each RIL was determined by averaging the percentage of diseased area (lesions) over three replicated experiments. A disease severity score (DSS) was assigned based on the percentage of diseased leaf area as follows; 0–5% (DSS 1), 6–15% (DSS 2), 16–50% (DSS 3), 51–75% (DSS 4), and 76–100% (DSS 5). Early-defoliated leaves were considered 100% diseased and recorded as DSS 5 (Fig. 2).

The genetic analysis program IciMapping version 4.2 (Meng et al., 2015) was used to construct a genetic map of the Daepung × Taekwang RIL population. From the pre-filtered 19,075 SNPs, SNPs with high redundancy were excluded again using the BIN function, leaving 1,760 SNPs. The SNPs were categorized into 20 chromosomes using the MAP function, and genetic distances between SNPs were calculated based on recombination frequencies between SNPs. Inclusive composite interval mapping was conducted to determine the statistical association between marker genotypes and phenotypes using the BIP function. Logarithm of odds (LOD) threshold for statistical significance was determined by a 1,000-permutation test (P < 0.05). Additive effects were calculated by dividing the difference in the averaged phenotypic data between the two groups of the lines with differing alleles (Daepung vs. Taekwang allele) for the identified loci (i.e., SNPs) by 2 (i.e., the number of alleles for each locus).

Daepung, Taekwang, and 60 RILs of the Daepung × Taekwang population were tested for resistance to Pat isolate ‘W14-M1’. Resistant reactions to wildfire show few spots, while susceptible reactions form dark brown irregular spots on the leaves, and over time, the spots merge and form a yellow band around them. The resistant parent, Daepung, exhibited 1% disease leaf area (DSS 1), and the susceptible parent, Taekwang, was moderately susceptible, with an average of 33% of the leaves diseased (DSS 3). Of the 60 RILs, none, 7, 47, 5, and 1 RILs had scores of 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5, respectively (Fig. 2). The broad-sense heritability for resistance to Pat was calculated as 0.22 in this population. Even though the same inoculation method was applied and the post-inoculation conditions (temperature and humidity) were carefully monitored to minimize environmental variation, the standard deviation of the lesion area (%) across the three replications ranged widely, from 1 to 48. This high degree of variation implies that the expression of resistance to Pat is heavily influenced by environmental factors (Table 1).

A high-density genetic map was constructed for QTL analysis using the 19,075 filtered SNPs. The average inter-marker distance per chromosome ranged from 1.2 to 2.5 cM, reflecting an even distribution across most of the genome (Supplementary Table 1). However, chromosomes 11 and 20 exhibited a notable lack of polymorphism, resulting in reduced map coverage. On these chromosomes, polymorphic SNPs were clustered exclusively within the 5–11 Mbp region on chromosome 11 and the 30–55 Mbp region on chromosome 20, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1). Consequently, the genetic maps for these two chromosomes represented only limited intervals.

The distribution of polymorphic SNPs on chromosomes 11 and 20 differed from other chromosomes, with polymorphisms localized to specific regions. This restricted distribution may be attributed to the high degree of genetic relatedness between the two parental lines. Pedigree analysis revealed that ‘Baekwoon’, a parent of Daepung, was derived from ‘Gwanggyo’, also served as a parental line for Taekwang. Consequently, Daepung and Taekwang shared Gwanggyo in their lineage, leading to significant genomic conservation in these regions. This observation suggests that the target genetic variations that confer resistance to Pat are likely to be located outside these specific common genomic regions.

Through inclusive composite interval mapping, two QTL for partial resistance to Pat were mapped to chromosomes 4 and 5, explaining a total of 33.2% of the phenotypic variance (Table 2, Fig. 3). QTL-04 was located within the 3.3–5.0 Mbp region of chromosome 4, adjacent to marker AX-90451056. The phenotypic variance explained by this marker at this locus was 14.1%. The additive effect of the Daepung allele at this locus on chromosome 4 was −0.26, indicating that disease severity increased by 0.26 (i.e., increased susceptibility) when one allele was substituted with the Taekwang allele. QTL-05 was located at 40.9–41.3 Mbp on chromosome 5, associated with the peak SNP markers AX-90323460 and AX-90397243. This QTL explained about 19.1% of the phenotypic variance. The negative additive effects at both QTL suggest that the resistant alleles are provided by the Daepung. The QTL-04 overlaps with a QTL for resistance to soybean cyst nematode identified by Vuong et al. (2011), located between 3.3 and 5.3 Mbp. QTL-05 is located near known disease-resistance QTL against P. sojae and Indonesian soybean dwarf virus, which are located approximately at 40.5 Mbp and 41.6 Mbp, respectively (Qin et al., 2017; Uchibori et al., 2009).

Most previously reported QTL associated with partial resistance have exhibited small effects and limited statistical significance, suggesting that some minor loci might have remained undetected under stringent significance criteria. Lande and Kruglyak (1995) first introduced the concept of ‘suggestive linkage’, referring to weak but potentially meaningful association between genotype and phenotype that do not meet genome-wide significance. Subsequently, Van Ooijen (1999) proposed the use of chromosome-wide LOD thresholds, in which the analysis of each chromosome is treated as an independent experiment, and the corresponding threshold depends on its genetic map length. In the present study, no genomic locus did not exceed the 95% genome-wide LOD threshold of 3.6 (α = 0.05). Therefore, a relaxed, chromosome-wide threshold at the 95% confidence level, suggested for map length between 100 and 150 cM in Van Ooijen (1999), was applied to indicate provisional significance. The chromosome-wide LOD threshold (i.e., 2) identified the two QTL with relatively low LOD scores (2.3 and 2.8) (Fig. 3, Table 2). Applying chromosome-wide thresholds increases the likelihood of detecting minor-effect QTL, although this comes at the expense of a higher type I error rate. Despite this limitation, such an approach facilitates the identification of loci that may represent genuine effects when repeatedly detected across experiments, thereby contributing to the elucidation of complex architectures underlying traits such as partial resistance to Pat. Several studies often applied an alternative, relaxed LOD threshold when detecting QTL in cases where linkage analyses did not identify highly significant associations (Lee et al., 2013; Sato et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2020).

The pathological responses of Daepung have been well documented in previous studies. While Daepung exhibits susceptibility to the Phytophthora sojae isolates 40468, 40412, and 2457 (Jang et al., 2020; You et al., 2023) and moderate susceptibility to bacterial pustule (Kim et al., 2022), it shows strong resistance to eight Cercospora sojina Hara isolates (Kang et al., 2015b). The present study extends this profile by identifying its resistance to Pat isolate W14-M1. Collectively, these findings suggest that pyramiding resistance genes from Daepung with other sources will facilitate the development of cultivars with broad-spectrum resistance, ensuring stable soybean production against multiple diseases under field conditions.

Several studies reported the development of new cultivars by pyramiding multiple diseases-resistance genes introgressed from different genetic sources. Bacterial blight, blast, and sheath blight resistance genes/QTL were introgressed into the background of ‘ASD16’ and ‘ADT 43’, respectively, to develop multiple disease-resistant rice cultivars by marker-assisted backcross breeding (Ramalingam et al., 2020a). The newly developed rice cultivars exhibited enhanced resistance to the three target diseases in addition to desirable agronomic traits similar to their parents. Ramalingam et al. (2020b) also developed several breeding lines by pyramiding disease resistance genes for Phytophthora rot and powdery mildew contributed by soybean varieties ‘CO 3’ and ‘JS 335’. The improved lines exhibited high levels of resistance to the two diseases, increased nodulation, and improved agronomic traits compared to the susceptible parental lines. Maruthasalam et al. (2007) incorporated three resistance genes, chi11, tlp, and Xa21, into popular indica rice varieties to improve resistance to sheath blight (Rhizoctonia solani Kühn) and bacterial leaf blight (Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae) via transformation. The transgenic lines exhibited significantly smaller lesions, restricted pathogen spread, and increased browning at the infection site compared to non-transgenic controls. Likewise, three resistance genes to Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xa4, xa5, Xa21), blast resistance gene (Pi40) to Magnaporthe grisea, and a tolerance gene (Bph18) to brown planthopper (Nilaparvata lugens) were incorporated into the japonica rice variety ‘Jinbubyeo’ (Suh et al., 2015). These gene-pyramided lines exhibited strong disease resistance and similar agronomic features to the recurrent parent Jinbubyeo.

The heritability of resistance calculated from this mapping population was relatively low (Table 1). In genetics and breeding, heritability represents the proportion of phenotypic variation attribute to genetic differences among individuals (Greene et al., 2008). Since quantitative traits are often affected by environmental factors in addition to genetics, maintaining a strictly controlled environment is crucial for accurate phenotypic assessments. The results of this study are expected to serve as a reference for the development of soybean varieties resistant to wildfire in the breeding program. The identified QTL can be used for the development of genetic markers associated with resistance to wildfire, which would increase the efficiency of breeding for disease resistance using marker-assisted selection.

In conclusion, this study first identified two QTL conferring resistance to Pat, which explained approximately 14.1 and 19.1% of the phenotypic variance, respectively. Analysis of additive effects confirmed that Daepung harbors resistance alleles at the two loci. Notbably, these identified genomic loci overlap with previously reported QTL associated with resistance to various bacterial, viral, fungal, and nematode pathogens. This finding suggests that these genomic loci are putative targets of selection, despite the modest levels of statistical significance. Pyramiding multiple resistance genes from diverse genetic sources against single or multiple pathogens enables the development of robust soybean cultivars resilient to biotic stresses. This approach is considered the most effective strategy for achieving durable protection and maintaining stable yield.
