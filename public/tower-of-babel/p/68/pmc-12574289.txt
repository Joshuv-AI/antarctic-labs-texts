Distal renal tubular acidosis triggered by clozapine in a patient with a heterozygous ATP6V0A4 c.1029 + 5G > A variant: a case report and literature review
Authors: Takuya Kumagai, Ayano Saito, Shihori Ino, Kazuhisa Yoshizawa, Masahiro Takeshima, Naoto Takahashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDistal renal tubular acidosis (dRTA) is characterized by impaired acidification in the distal tubules and may be associated with genetic factors, such as ATP6V0A4 variants. The role of clozapine in triggering dRTA, particularly in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility, remains poorly understood.Case presentationA 24-year-old woman with schizophrenia developed dRTA following the reintroduction of clozapine; a previous episode had resolved after discontinuation of the drug. Investigations confirmed impaired urinary acidification consistent with dRTA. Genetic testing identified a heterozygous ATP6V0A4 variant (c.1029 + 5G > A). She responded well to sodium bicarbonate therapy, which enabled the continuation of clozapine treatment.ConclusionThis case highlights a potential gene–drug interaction, in which an ATP6V0A4 variant predisposed the patient to clozapine-induced dRTA. Recognizing genetic susceptibility is essential when dRTA arises in the context of medications not typically associated with the condition, to inform both diagnosis and management.Keywords: Distal renal tubular acidosis, Clozapine, ATP6V0A4, Gene-drug interaction

Introduction

Clozapine is an atypical antipsychotic widely recognized for its superior efficacy in improving psychiatric symptoms in patients with treatment-resistant schizophrenia. It has been shown to significantly improve total, positive, and negative symptoms in the short term, while primarily improving positive symptoms over the long term [1]. Due to its clinical benefits, clozapine is recommended in treatment guidelines as the first-line therapy for treatment-resistant schizophrenia, particularly when patients fail to respond to at least two other antipsychotic agents [2].

However, its use has been associated with serious adverse effects, including agranulocytosis and myocarditis [3]. These life-threatening complications led to the withdrawal of clozapine from the market in the 1970s; it was later reintroduced with strict monitoring protocols.

Distal renal tubular acidosis (dRTA) is a disorder characterized by impaired hydrogen ion secretion in the distal tubules, resulting in a normal anion gap metabolic acidosis with an inappropriately high urinary pH [4]. The condition is commonly associated with autoimmune diseases such as Sjögren’s syndrome, as well as genetic disorders and nephrotoxic drugs, including lithium and amphotericin B [5]. Although drug-induced dRTA is well-documented, clozapine has not been definitively established as a causative agent. Furthermore, the role of individual genetic susceptibility in such adverse drug reactions remains to be fully elucidated.

Herein, we report a rare case of recurrent dRTA in a 24-year-old woman, closely associated with the reintroduction of clozapine.

Case presentation

A 24-year-old woman with a 13-year history of schizophrenia had previously achieved psychiatric stability on clozapine, which had been initiated several years earlier. Her family history included depression in her father and schizophrenia in her maternal grandfather. There was no family history of renal disease, hearing loss, or recurrent kidney stones. Her past medical history was significant for atopic dermatitis, bronchial asthma, and acute appendicitis. She was hospitalized with hypokalemic myopathy at the age of 22, and the diagnosis of distal renal tubular acidosis (dRTA) was made based on blood gas analysis (pH 7.334, bicarbonate (HCO3−) 19.0 meq/L) and urinalysis (urine pH > 5.5, positive urine anion gap). Clozapine was presumed to be the causative agent, and her treatment was switched to alternative antipsychotics, along with electroconvulsive therapy (ECT). Although her acid-base status was not monitored during the clozapine-free interval, a venous blood gas analysis performed just prior to its reintroduction confirmed that the dRTA had resolved (pH 7.420, HCO3− 25.3 meq/L). Due to the persistence of her psychiatric symptoms, a decision was made to reintroduce clozapine five months before the current presentation. The medication was gradually titrated, and nephrology follow-up was initiated at the time of reinitiation. During routine follow-up, progressive metabolic acidosis was detected, prompting further investigation and management. At the time of evaluation, she was receiving the following oral medications: clozapine 300 mg/day (6.0 mg/kg), clonazepam 0.5 mg/day (0.012 mg/kg), eszopiclone 2 mg/day (0.04 mg/kg), valproic acid 600 mg/day (12 mg/kg), olanzapine 15 mg/day (0.25 mg/kg), and potassium gluconate 15 g/day (60 mEq/day).

At the time of referral, the patient’s vital signs were as follows: Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was full, body temperature was 36.5 °C, blood pressure was 112/70 mmHg, heart rate was 78 beats per minute, and SpO₂ was 99%. On physical examination, the patient’s vital signs were stable. Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were unremarkable. There were no signs of volume depletion, or of fluid overload.

Laboratory findings on admission are summarized in Table 1. Venous blood gas analysis revealed metabolic acidosis, with a pH of 7.26 and a bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) level of 12.6 mmol/L. The calculated anion gap was 9.4 mEq/L, consistent with a normal anion gap acidosis. Serum creatinine was 0.63 mg/dL. Serum sodium, potassium, and chloride levels were 140 mEq/L, 3.6 mEq/L, and 118 mEq/L, respectively. Urinalysis showed a persistently high urine pH of 8.0. Urinary sodium, potassium, chloride, and urea nitrogen levels were 47 mEq/L, 52 mEq/L, 64 mEq/L, and 353 mg/dL, respectively. A positive urine anion gap of 35 mEq/L and a urine osmolality gap (UOG) of 15.2 mEq/L (with urine osmolality < 100 mOsm/kg) were consistent with a diagnosis of dRTA. Other laboratory results revealed no significant abnormalities (Table 1). The clozapine trough level was 404.7 ng/mL, within the therapeutic range of 350–600 ng/mL [6]. Computed tomography (CT) of the kidneys showed no structural abnormalities or evidence of nephrocalcinosis.

The patient experienced mild diarrhea, occurring twice daily; however, this was unlikely to be the primary cause of the metabolic acidosis given its mild severity and the presence of a positive urine anion gap.

There was no history of exposure to medications known to induce dRTA, such as amphotericin B, lithium, or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Importantly, her other medications had remained stable for several months before and during the onset of dRTA, with clozapine being the only relevant medication reintroduced prior to the recurrence. Autoimmune screening, including anti-SSA and anti-SSB antibodies, was negative, and there were no sicca symptoms, effectively ruling out Sjögren’s syndrome, a common autoimmune cause of dRTA. Given the recurrent nature of her dRTA and the strong temporal association with clozapine reinitiation in the absence of other identifiable causes, we explored the possibility of an underlying genetic predisposition. Genetic sequencing revealed a heterozygous c.1029 + 5G > A variant near a splice site in the ATP6V0A4 gene (Fig. 1). This gene encodes the a4 subunit of the V0 domain of the V-ATPase complex and is known to be associated with hereditary forms of dRTA.

Considering the patient’s clinical course, specifically, the recurrence of dRTA following clozapine rechallenge and its resolution after discontinuation, along with the identification of a heterozygous ATP6V0A4 variant, it was hypothesized that clozapine likely induced dRTA in a genetically predisposed individual.

As the patient’s schizophrenia remained stable on clozapine, we opted to continue the medication, accompanied by close monitoring and management of the acidosis with oral bicarbonate supplementation. She was started on oral sodium bicarbonate at a dose of 2 g/day. By day 7, venous blood gas analysis showed improvement in the metabolic acidosis, with a pH of 7.35 and HCO₃⁻ of 15.5 mmol/L. By day 14, the blood gas analysis had normalized, with a pH of 7.41 and HCO₃⁻ of 25.4 mmol/L. With ongoing sodium bicarbonate therapy, the patient has experienced occasional mild episodes of metabolic acidosis during follow-up. However, these episodes have been significantly less severe than those observed prior to initiating bicarbonate treatment, and clozapine therapy has been successfully maintained with regular blood gas monitoring.

Discussion

This case report describes a 24-year-old woman with schizophrenia and a heterozygous ATP6V0A4 variant (c.1029 + 5G >A), who developed distal renal tubular acidosis (dRTA) following the reintroduction of clozapine. The clinical course, marked by the recurrence of dRTA upon clozapine rechallenge and its resolution after discontinuation two years earlier, strongly supports a causal relationship between clozapine and the onset of dRTA in this genetically susceptible individual. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the final step of urinary acidification is mediated by the V-ATPase proton pump located on the apical membrane of renal α-intercalated cells. This pump actively secretes protons (H⁺) into the urine, a process that is critically dependent on the integrity of all its components, including the a4 subunit encoded by the ATP6V0A4 gene [7]. Consequently, a loss-of-function variant in ATP6V0A4 compromises the pump’s efficacy, resulting in impaired proton secretion and the clinical manifestation of dRTA.

The ATP6V0A4 gene encodes the a4 subunit of the V-ATPase proton pump, which is predominantly expressed in α-intercalated cells of the renal collecting duct. This pump plays a crucial role in maintaining acid-base balance through the secretion of protons (H⁺) for urinary acidification

To contextualize our findings, we compared this case with previous reports. Primary dRTA caused by ATP6V0A4 variants is typically inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. As shown in the upper part of Table 2 [7–23], patients with homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations classically present in infancy or early childhood with severe metabolic acidosis, failure to thrive, and a high incidence of nephrocalcinosis and sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL). In contrast, our patient, who carries a heterozygous variant, presented in adulthood and lacked both SNHL and nephrocalcinosis, distinguishing her clinical picture from that of typical recessive dRTA.

However, there are a very few number of reports, summarized in the lower part of Table 2, describe patients with heterozygous ATP6V0A4 variants who develop dRTA. A common feature among these cases is a later age of onset and a lower prevalence of SNHL compared to the classical recessive form. This suggests that heterozygosity for an ATP6V0A4 variant may not be sufficient to cause overt disease on its own but rather acts as a predisposing factor, creating a state of reduced renal acid-secreting capacity, consistent with incomplete dRTA (idRTA) [23].

The most remarkable feature of our case, when compared to other heterozygous carriers, is that a specific pharmacological agent, clozapine, was identified as the definitive trigger for overt dRTA. Discussion of triggers in previously reported heterozygous cases is limited; where mentioned, they have been attributed to physiological stressors such as pregnancy or other clinical events like hypokalemic periodic paralysis [23]. However, in many other reports, a clear precipitant was not identified. Our report is therefore the first to clearly demonstrate that a specific medication can serve as the trigger, highlighting a clinically significant gene-drug interaction that unmasked the latent renal tubular defect.

The pathophysiology of this case can be best explained by a “two-hit” model, where a pharmacological trigger acts on a pre-existing genetic susceptibility. We postulate that the patient had an underlying idRTA due to ATP6V0A4 haploinsufficiency (the first hit). Clozapine administration then likely precipitated overt dRTA (the second hit) through at least two possible, non-mutually exclusive mechanisms. First, clozapine may have increased the systemic acid load, overwhelming the patient’s limited renal acidification capacity. Clozapine is known to cause severe metabolic disturbances; it can induce hyperglycemia leading to diabetic ketoacidosis, and has also been reported to cause lactic acidosis, even in overdose cases without secondary hypotension [24]. Mechanistically, clozapine may impede Akt kinase activation, shifting cellular metabolism towards glycolysis and increasing lactate production [25]. Either of these metabolic derangements would present a substantial acid challenge to the kidneys. Second, clozapine may have caused direct renal tubular injury. Although no previous cases have reported dRTA in association with clozapine, it has been linked to other forms of kidney injury, such as acute tubulointerstitial nephritis (AIN) [26]. A subclinical or low-grade tubulointerstitial injury could have further compromised the function of α-intercalated cells, thereby reducing the net H+ secretion below the threshold required to maintain acid-base homeostasis. These two mechanisms may have acted synergistically.

A crucial question is why the patient did not develop dRTA during her initial course of clozapine therapy years earlier. The precise reason remains unclear, but we can speculate on several possibilities. Although the patient’s serum clozapine concentration was within the therapeutic range (404.7 ng/mL, reference range: 350–600 ng/mL) at the onset of dRTA, the adverse effect on renal function could be related to the cumulative duration of exposure rather than the absolute drug level. Alternatively, another subclinical factor, such as the patient’s mild diarrhea, might have coincided with the clozapine reintroduction, creating an additional stressor or “third hit” that was necessary to overwhelm her compensatory mechanisms. This remains a limitation of our report, but it highlights the complex interplay of factors that can lead to disease manifestation in genetically susceptible individuals.

The c.1029 + 5G > A variant identified in our patient is located at a canonical splice donor site. Such variants are known to disrupt normal splicing, potentially leading to exon skipping or intron retention. This would likely result in an aberrant mRNA transcript, producing a non-functional or unstable ATP6V0A4 protein. The resulting haploinsufficiency would reduce the number of functional V-ATPase proton pumps in the apical membrane of α-intercalated cells, thus impairing maximal H+ secretion and creating the underlying vulnerability to acidosis.

In conclusion, this case of recurrent dRTA following clozapine exposure in a patient with a heterozygous c.1029 + 5G > A variant in the ATP6V0A4 gene underscores a clinically significant gene–drug interaction. The development of dRTA in this patient strongly suggests that her underlying heterozygous variant was a key predisposing factor, with clozapine serving as a critical trigger. Therefore, for patients who present with dRTA induced by a drug not typically associated with this side effect, clinicians should evaluate for an underlying genetic susceptibility. This approach may aid in understanding individual susceptibility and informing future therapeutic decisions. Further research is warranted to better define the spectrum of gene–drug interactions contributing to the development of dRTA.
