Optimizing automated external defibrillator incremental deployment to improve out-of-hospital cardiac arrest coverage: a spatial optimization study
Authors: Pengfei Cheng, Ding Luo, Hua Zhang, Baichao Xu, Pengyu Yang, Huachen Xue, Xiaobing Zhai, Ying Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•More defibrillators do not always produce proportional coverage gains.•Coverage gains level off after an optimal deployment threshold.•Restaurants are priority sites for expanding public defibrillator access.Keywords: Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, Automated external defibrillator, Maximal covering location problem, Public access defibrillation

Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) remains a leading cause of sudden death worldwide, with survival critically dependent on early defibrillation.1, 2 In China, OHCA incidence is estimated at 95.7 per 100,000 population, yet survival to hospital discharge remains markedly low at 1.2–2.8%, with favorable neurological outcomes achieved in fewer than 1% of cases.3, 4 Public access defibrillation (PAD) programs through strategic placement of automated external defibrillators (AEDs) have been widely advocated to improve early defibrillation rates.5 However, real-world AED utilization during OHCA remains disappointingly low globally, with on-site AED defibrillation reported in fewer than 0.1% of OHCA cases in China.3

A critical barrier to effective PAD implementation is the spatiotemporal mismatch between AED placement and OHCA occurrence.6 Spatially, AEDs are often concentrated in high-visibility locations such as commercial centers and transportation hubs, whereas epidemiological evidence consistently shows that 70–80% of OHCA events occur in residential or community settings.7 Current guidelines for AED deployment predominantly rely on population density or land use classification rather than actual OHCA distribution, potentially leading to suboptimal placement strategies.8 Moreover, few studies have systematically evaluated the incremental coverage gains from adding AEDs to existing networks or identified deployment thresholds at which marginal benefits diminish9—questions critical for resource-constrained settings where indiscriminate expansion may incur substantial costs without proportional benefit.10, 11

The maximal covering location problem (MCLP) model has been increasingly applied to optimize emergency resource allocation, including AED deployment.12 By maximizing coverage of demand points within a specified service distance using a limited number of facilities, MCLP is particularly well-suited for AED network planning where rapid response is critical.12 Building on this approach, the present study employed GIS combined with MCLP optimization to simulate incremental AED deployment under multiple response distance scenarios, aiming to: (1) quantify existing AED network coverage; (2) identify optimal incremental deployment locations; (3) determine coverage saturation thresholds; and (4) characterize priority venue types. These findings support more efficient AED deployment strategies in resource-limited settings.

Method

Study area and setting

This study selected Haikou City, Hainan Province, as the study area. Haikou, the provincial capital, is located on the northern coast of Hainan Island, covering an area of 2284.49 km2 with a resident population of 3.03 million. The city is divided into four administrative districts: Longhua, Xiuying, Meilan, and Qiongshan.13 Compared with economically developed coastal cities in eastern China, Haikou has relatively limited economic resources and less developed emergency medical infrastructure. Therefore, findings from this study may offer practical guidance for AED deployment in cities with similar resource constraints.

Data sources

OHCA cases were identified from the Haikou Emergency Medical Service (EMS) registry following Utstein-style reporting guidelines.14 All OHCA events recorded between January 2015 and December 2021 were eligible for inclusion. Inclusion criteria was: cardiac arrest occurring outside medical facilities, confirmed OHCA diagnosis by EMS personnel, and complete address-level location data. Cases involving trauma, suicide, traffic-related injuries, or occurring within medical institutions were excluded. AED location data were obtained from the Haikou AED Map, the official registry of publicly accessible AEDs jointly maintained by the Haikou EMS Center and the local Red Cross Society.

Candidate locations for new AED placement were derived from point-of-interest (POI) data obtained from the Amap (Gaode) open platform, accessed in October 2025. After geocoding, a total of 17,004 publicly accessible venues across the study area were retained as candidate sites for the optimization model. Each candidate venue was classified into one of 12 location categories with reference to the venue types specified in the Guidelines for the Configuration of Automated External Defibrillators in Public Places issued by the National Health Commission of China in 2021,15 in combination with its registered business type in the POI database. The categories comprised restaurants, industrial or workplace, shopping malls, home or private residence, transportation hubs, sports or recreation event, parks/scenic areas, hotels, government agencies, health care or medical facility, educational institution, and other POI. All identified candidate venues were eligible for selection; no prior filtering or exclusion was applied. The number of candidate venues available within each location category is summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Spatial optimization model

AED deployment optimization employed the maximal covering location problem (MCLP) framework, which maximizes the number of OHCA events covered within a predefined response distance given a fixed number of additional AEDs.16 Existing AED locations were preserved throughout all simulations, reflecting real-world constraints where previously installed devices cannot be relocated.

OHCA events were represented by their geographic coordinates, and an event was considered covered if at least one AED was located within the specified Euclidean distance threshold. The MCLP model was solved iteratively under increasing deployment scenarios, with coverage outcomes recalculated after each step. Incremental deployment continued until marginal coverage gains became minimal, enabling identification of coverage saturation thresholds. At each increment, candidate sites were selected solely to maximize coverage, irrespective of category; the selected sites were subsequently aggregated by category to derive the location types prioritized for deployment. The MCLP was formulated and solved in Python (version 3.9) using the spopt module of the PySAL library. Spatial data processing and mapping were performed in QGIS 3.40.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was OHCA coverage rate, defined as the proportion of OHCA events located within the specified distance of at least one AED. Distance thresholds of 100 m, 200 m, 300 m, 400 m, and 500 m were applied, where lower thresholds reflect immediate bystander retrieval on foot and upper thresholds represent scenarios involving assisted transportation.17 Secondary outcomes included newly covered OHCA events at each deployment step and cumulative coverage across deployment scenarios. Coverage saturation was defined as the point at which further AED deployment yielded minimal marginal gains.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and event characteristics. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages and compared using chi-square tests. A chi-square goodness-of-fit test was used to assess the mismatch between the current distribution of AEDs and the distribution of OHCA cases across location categories. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Hainan Medical University (HYLL-2021-102). All data were anonymized prior to analysis.

Results

Epidemiological characteristics of OHCA

During the study period, 5792 OHCA events were included after excluding 364 records with incomplete data. Patients were predominantly male (67.21%), with OHCA occurrence increasing markedly with age; individuals aged ≥30 years accounted for 88.72% of cases. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved in 530 cases (9.15%), while 5262 patients (90.85%) were pronounced dead at the scene.

Home or private residence were the most common OHCA location, accounting for 3440 events (59.39%), followed by public streets (594 events, 10.26%), educational institution (242 events, 4.18%), hotels (233 events, 4.02%), and nursing homes (142 events, 2.45%). Detailed epidemiological characteristics are provided in Supplementary Tables S2–S7.

Baseline AED distribution and spatial mismatch

A total of 403 AEDs were registered across the study area, corresponding to a density of 1.33 per 10,000 inhabitants. AED deployment was concentrated in home or private residence (27.30%), educational institution (11.17%), government institutions (10.17%), and community neighborhood committees (10.17%). Complete AED location data are provided in Supplementary Table S8.

Stratifying OHCA occurrences by the Utstein location categories14 and comparing each category with its corresponding AED deployment revealed a substantial mismatch between the two distributions. (Table 1). Although home or private residence ranked first for both OHCA events (59.39%) and AED deployment (27.30%), street or highway—accounting for 10.26% of OHCA events—had no registered AEDs. Conversely, educational institution was overrepresented in AED deployment relative to OHCA burden. The distribution of AEDs differed significantly from OHCA occurrence across location types (chi-square goodness-of-fit test, χ2 = 513.2, df = 8, P < 0.001).

Baseline AED coverage

The existing AED network demonstrated limited OHCA coverage across all distance thresholds (Table 2). At 100 m, only 843 OHCA events (14.55%) were within AED coverage. Coverage increased to 37.34% at 300 m and 45.68% at 500 m. Spatial mapping confirmed that substantial high-density OHCA areas remained outside AED coverage (Fig. 1).

Optimization performance and saturation thresholds

Using the MCLP model with existing AEDs preserved, incremental deployment simulations revealed a consistent nonlinear relationship between additional AEDs and coverage gains across all distance thresholds (Table 2; Fig. 2). Coverage increased rapidly during initial deployment phases, followed by progressively diminishing marginal returns approaching radius-specific saturation points.

Total OHCA coverage rate (%) as a function of the number of additional AEDs deployed under MCLP optimization, stratified by coverage radius (100–500 m). Each curve represents a different assumed response distance. Markers indicate the saturation point for each radius, defined as the point beyond which marginal coverage gains fall below 0.1% per additional AED.

At 100 m coverage radius, adding 100 optimally placed AEDs increased coverage from 14.55% to 37.24%. Near-saturation (96.44%) required approximately 1467 additional AEDs. At 300 m, coverage reached 77.76% with 100 additional AEDs and approached saturation (99.07%) at 499 AEDs. At 500 m, the most efficient gains were observed, with coverage reaching 91.33% after 100 AEDs and saturation (99.43%) at 308 AEDs. Detailed coverage data for all distance thresholds are provided in Supplementary Tables S9–S13.

Spatial distribution of optimized AED placements

The MCLP model generated spatially explicit placement recommendations for each coverage scenario. Across all distance thresholds, optimized AED locations were preferentially allocated to high-density OHCA clusters that remained uncovered by the existing network. Although the total number of AEDs required to achieve saturation varied substantially across coverage radii (ranging from 308 at 500 m to 1467 at 100 m), the optimized configurations consistently expanded coverage into underserved areas, particularly in central urban districts with concentrated OHCA incidence. Detailed spatial distributions of optimized AED placements are provided in Supplementary Figs. S1–S6 (downtown areas) and Supplementary Figs. S7–S11 (citywide coverage).

Location types prioritized by optimization model

The MCLP-optimized deployment demonstrated a strikingly different location pattern compared to existing AED distribution (Table 3). Restaurants were consistently identified as the highest-priority location, accounting for 56.50–65.29% of recommended placements across all coverage radii, despite currently hosting only 1.24% of registered AEDs. Industrial or workplace (7.43–13.57%) and shopping malls (4.71–7.96%) were also prioritized. In contrast, home or private residence, educational institution, and government institutions—which dominate current deployment—received substantially lower priority in optimized solutions. Discussion

Using multi-year, Utstein-compliant OHCA registry data from a resource-constrained coastal city in China, this study provides a data-driven evaluation of the effectiveness of the existing urban AED network and the potential gains achievable through incremental optimization. Three principal findings emerge. First, the current AED deployment offers limited effective coverage for real-world OHCA events, indicating a spatial mismatch between device placement and emergency demand. Second, incremental AED deployment yields substantial coverage gains initially but exhibits pronounced diminishing marginal returns, with coverage approaching saturation beyond context-specific thresholds. Third, AED placements optimized using an MCLP framework align more closely with empirically observed OHCA risk patterns than conventional placement strategies.

Current AED deployment offers limited effective coverage, indicating clear spatial mismatch. AED density (1.33 per 10,000) is lower than Denmark (9.2), Canada (6.68), Hong Kong (1.94), and Xuzhou (3.87).6 In many low- and middle-income regions, budget competition has slowed PAD maturation.18 However, limited coverage cannot be attributed to device numbers alone. Existing networks are often deployed by administrative convenience rather than empirical OHCA patterns: in our setting, 45.4% of AEDs serve public buildings accounting for only 12.9% of OHCA, whereas streets/highways (10.3% of OHCA) have no coverage. Similar redundancy alongside underserved high-risk areas was reported by Ringh et al.19 Further increases in quantity may incur substantial costs without proportional coverage gains11; improving PAD effectiveness depends on correcting spatial inefficiency, not merely adding devices.

Using a GIS-based MCLP framework, we identified a pronounced non-linear relationship between AED quantity and coverage. At baseline, 14.6% of OHCA were covered within 100 m—higher than Shanghai (4.6%)9 but below Canada (23%) and Denmark (22%).20, 21 Raising the response distance to 500 m increased baseline coverage to 45.7%, but this reflects inflated theoretical accessibility with practical trade-offs in routes and time-to-defibrillation. Incremental deployment delivered substantial early gains but increasingly produced overlapping service areas; coverage plateaued near saturation (e.g., ∼99% with ∼499 additional devices at 300 m; saturation requiring 308 devices at 500 m versus 1467 at 100 m). This explicit saturation-and-radius framework is transferable: rather than location-specific coordinates, it lets other cities derive their own deployment optimum using locally appropriate service distances. Markedly fewer AEDs were needed than in Shanghai,9 underscoring the role of local urban form. These findings challenge the assumption that more AEDs yield proportional coverage,22, 23 providing a quantitative basis for staged, rational investment without restructuring existing networks.

Optimized scenarios revealed venue-type priorities that depart from Western paradigms. Restaurants consistently ranked highest (57–65% of newly sited devices), followed by factories and enterprise settings; shopping malls remained mid-priority. Residential areas—despite hosting most existing AEDs—received lower priority, as did transportation hubs and government/educational institutions, which yielded limited marginal gains. This does not contradict evidence that most OHCA occur in residences24, 25; rather, consistent with the saturation dynamics above, residential zones have entered the diminishing-returns regime, whereas restaurants and enterprise settings remain in the high-marginal-yield regime, where the model redirects incremental devices toward under-served OHCA demand. In this high-density context, restaurants fulfill that role, whereas Western paradigms center on transportation and commercial hubs shown to yield reduced marginal benefits beyond baseline.26, 27 This indicates that venue priorities derived in high-income Western cities cannot transfer directly to high-density Asian contexts. Restaurants and workplaces are widely distributed, operate extended hours, and are embedded in daily activity, enhancing spatial and temporal accessibility26, 27; prior work likewise identifies them as high-efficiency targets, though the mix varies locally.19, 28 Sun et al.27 found cafés, restaurants, and retail stores were major Toronto hotspots. Enterprises further support AED awareness, training, and responsibility assignment, and their even distribution lets each device extend coverage into underserved areas.28

From a policy perspective, expanding AED networks without strategic targeting may yield limited returns relative to its cost.8, 29 Conversely, a Copenhagen simulation showed that digitally optimized deployment increased bystander defibrillation and 30-day survival while achieving comparable coverage with one-fifth the AEDs required by guideline-based placement.8 Building on this principle, our findings support a staged, data-driven strategy for developing AED networks: guideline-based siting at high-traffic and OHCA-history sites offers a reasonable starting point when local data are limited,8, 29 then transition to incremental, OHCA-data-guided expansion.10 Simultaneously, in terms of the specific knowledge this study adds to the field, optimization in this high-density tropical island setting favored restaurants and enterprise venues over the transport hubs and public buildings emphasized in Western, high-income evidence, suggesting that placement priorities may be shaped by local urban morphology, climate, and culture, and that recommendations derived from high-income settings may not transfer directly. Such a staged framework is particularly relevant for rapidly urbanizing, resource-constrained cities—especially those in comparable Asian, tropical, and island regions—where maximizing the marginal benefit of each device is essential for sustainable PAD program development, and where wholesale adoption of high-income-country recommendations may be inappropriate.19 More importantly, by aligning AED placement with real-world OHCA data, resources can be channeled toward underserved populations, advancing both the efficiency and—importantly—the equity of prehospital defibrillation access.30

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, coverage used Euclidean rather than network distance, potentially overestimating accessibility; future work should incorporate road-network and time-dependent data. Second, candidate sites were drawn from a point-of-interest database, so venue priorities partly reflect category density—restaurants’ prominence reflects their accessibility and ubiquity rather than intrinsic clinical risk. Third, temporal access constraints related to opening hours were not explicitly modeled, potentially overestimating coverage during nighttime hours when substantial OHCA events occur.19 Fourth, as a single-city study, our specific venue priorities may not generalize, though the analytical framework remains broadly transferable; multi-city validation is warranted.

Conclusion

Existing AED networks provide limited effective OHCA coverage due to spatial mismatch between placement and demand. MCLP-guided incremental deployment yields substantial early gains but pronounced diminishing returns, approaching saturation beyond context-specific thresholds. Optimization prioritizes restaurants and commercial/industrial settings over residential areas and transportation hubs—a venue ordering diverging from Western paradigms and underscoring that priorities must be calibrated to local OHCA epidemiology. These findings support phased, evidence-guided expansion that maximizes emergency access rather than symbolic device counts, a strategy especially relevant for resource-constrained settings.
