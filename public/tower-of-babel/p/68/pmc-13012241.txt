The CCL17–CCR4 Axis Is Critical for Mutant STAT6-Mediated Microenvironmental Remodeling and Therapeutic Resistance in Relapsed/Refractory Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma
Authors: Madelyn J. Abraham, Cynthia Guilbert, Natascha Gagnon, Christophe Gonçalves, Alexandre Benoit, Kelly Tran, Owen Hiltebrand, Ryan N. Rys, Samuel E.J. Preston, Ryan D. Morin, Wilson H. Miller, Nathalie A. Johnson, Sonia V. del Rincon, Koren K. Mann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study finds that, in rrDLBCL, STAT6D419 mutations enhance CCL17-CCR4 signaling to recruit CD4+ T cells and drive doxorubicin resistance. Using mouse-modelling and PhenoCycler imaging of human biopsies, CCR4 was identified as a promising therapeutic target.

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common lymphoma in adults. Frontline treatment is R-CHOP poly-chemoimmunotherapy (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone), leading to complete remission in 60% of patients. DLBCL that relapses or is refractory to R-CHOP [relapsed/refractory DLBCL (rrDLBCL)] is associated with poor outcomes. Thus, it is critically important to better understand mechanisms of therapeutic resistance, with the overall goal of identifying treatment modalities with increased efficacy for patients with rrDLBCL.

Signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6) is a transcription factor with emerging relevance in rrDLBCL. STAT6 is mutated or overactivated in many hematologic malignancies, including cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (1), primary mediastinal B-cell lymphoma (PMBCL; refs. 2–5), follicular lymphoma (FL; refs. 6–9), and classic Hodgkin lymphoma (10–12). In these cancer types, STAT6 phosphorylation is increased, and the downstream signaling promotes tumor cell survival, proliferation, and resistance to apoptosis, leading to the classification of STAT6 as a potential therapeutic target in lymphoma. In DLBCL, STAT6 is not frequently overactivated, except in the case of relapsed/refractory disease, in which mutations at the D419 gain-of-function hotspot are enriched, occurring in 2% to 4% of patients at initial diagnosis but in 7% to 16% of patients at relapse (13–15). STAT6 mutations in DLBCL are associated with poor prognosis (16), GCB cell of origin, and EZB LymphGen classification (17). Moreover, D419 mutations are associated with increased STAT6 activation, as measured by increased STAT6 phosphorylation, nuclear translocation, and transcription of target genes (6, 7, 18). Indeed, we have shown that the presence of STAT6D419 mutation in DLBCL cell lines leads to increased IL4/13-dependent phospho-STAT6 nuclear residency. This correlates with increased expression of STAT6 transcriptional targets, including the chemokine CCL17, which is chemoattractive to CCR4+ immune cells, including CD4+ T cells. Concordantly, in human rrDLBCL, we found that increased tumor-cell derived CCL17 is associated with increased CD4+ T-cell invasion, specifically in STAT6-mutant samples (14).

Overall, previous research has demonstrated that hyperactivation of STAT6, whether resulting from increased microenvironmental IL4/13 or due to gene mutation, leads to tumor cell autonomous and microenvironmental changes. However, the clinical significance of these findings in rrDLBCL remains unclear. For instance, it is unknown whether the CD4+ T-cell infiltrate in STAT6D419-mutant tumors is skewed toward a specific polarization state or whether these T cells contribute to disease progression and therapeutic resistance. Moreover, although we hypothesize that CCL17 is the main chemoattractant driving CD4+ T-cell recruitment, this has not been experimentally characterized. Recent publications have asserted that the spatial organization of tumors is just as important as their cellular constitution (19, 20), but it is unknown how STAT6D419 mutation induces tumor restructuring and whether CCL17–CCR4 plays a role. Finally, STAT6D419 mutations are enriched in rrDLBCL, but in vitro data indicate that STAT6D419-mutant tumor cells do not have increased resistance to R-CHOP (14). It is still unknown whether STAT6D419 mutations might contribute to therapeutic resistance due to changes in the tumor microenvironment (TME).

In this study, we developed an immunocompetent mouse model that recapitulates key features of STAT6D419-mutant rrDLBCL, including increased phospho-STAT6 expression and CD4+ T-cell infiltration. Using flow cytometry and ex vivo assays, we show that STAT6D419N tumor cells are chemoattractive to CCR4+ CD4+ T cells, leading to in vivo resistance to doxorubicin. Importantly, blocking CCL17-driven CD4+ T-cell recruitment with a CCR4 inhibitor resensitizes these tumors to doxorubicin. In human STAT6D419-mutant DLBCL biopsies, we observe elevated phospho-STAT6 and CCL17 expression, along with increased interactions between phospho-STAT6+ tumor cells and CCR4+ CD4+ T cells compared with STAT6WT biopsies. Thus, our data identify CCR4 as an attractive therapeutic target for patients with rrDLBCL with overactivated STAT6 signaling.

Materials and Methods

All reagents and tools used in this study can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

Cell culture

MEFARF−/− and Phoenix-AMPHO (CRL-3212) cells were grown in DMEM (Wisent, 319-005-CL) media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS: Wisent, 080-450) and 0.5% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S: Wisent, 450-201-EL). To generate mitotically inactive MEFs for co-culture, MEFARF−/− cells were given 40 Gy accumulated irradiation using a MultiRad225 (Faxitron) using a 0.5-mm Cu filter and set to 225 kV and 17.8 mA. Following irradiation, cells were frozen in FBS supplemented with 10% DMSO. Eμ-Myc cells were co-cultured with irradiated MEFARF−/− (iMEF) cells in 50% DMEM/50% Iscove’s media (Wisent, 319-105-CL), supplemented with FBS (10%), P/S (0.5%), and β-mercaptoethanol (50 nmol/L: Sigma, M3148). Eμ-Myc cells were maintained in culture for a maximum of 10 days to prevent genetic drift. All cell lines were routinely tested for Mycoplasma contamination using the Myco-Sniff Mycoplasma PCR Kit (MP Biomedicals). The Eμ-Myc and MEFARF−/− cells used in this study were generated in the laboratory of and were a kind gift from Dr. Jerry Pelletier (2015, Goodman Cancer Institute; ref. 21). Cell lines were not reauthenticated in the last year.

To produce murine STAT6WT and STAT6D419N lymphoma cell lines, Phoenix-AMPHO packaging cells were transfected with pMIG plasmids containing a GFP reporter and wild-type (WT) or mutant mSTAT6 (aspartic acid to asparagine mutation at position 419, using mutagenesis primers FWD: GGT​GGT​CAT​CGT​GCA​TGG​TAA​CCA​AAA​CAA​CAA​CGC​CAA​AGC​TAC and REV: CAT​AGG​ATG​GTA​GCT​TTG​GCG​TTG​TTG​TTT​TGG​TTA​CCA​TGC). The retrovirus supernatant was used to transduce Eμ-Myc cells by spinoculation (centrifugation at 800 × g for 30 minutes at room temperature with 6 μg/mL polybrene, every 12 hours for two consecutive days). Successfully transduced GFP+ cells were sorted using a BD FACSAria Fusion Cell Sorter at the Lady Davis Institute Flow Cytometry Facility and were maintained in cell culture.

Mouse modeling

All mouse experiments were performed in accordance with an Animal Use Protocol approved by the McGill University Animal Care Committee. Mice were housed in the animal facility of the Lady Davis Institute for Medical Research.

1 × 106 mSTAT6WT or mSTAT6D419N Eμ-Myc cells in 0.1 mL of sterile PBS were injected into 8 to 10-week-old female C57BL/6 mice via the tail vein on day 1. Tumor cells homed to lymph nodes, and disease burden was monitored using a VEVO-3100 ultrasound machine, with Vevo LAB software used for tumor volume calculation. Mice were sacrificed at indicated time points or if they showed signs of weight loss and hind-leg paralysis.

Where indicated, mice were treated with doxorubicin (2 mg/mL stock solution; Jewish General Hospital Oncology Pharmacy) or AZD2098 (MedChemExpress, HY-U00064). Doxorubicin was given as three doses of 3 mg/kg or one dose of 10 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection. AZD2098 was purchased as powder and was prepared at 2 mg/mL in 10% DMSO and 90% corn oil. Mice were given twice weekly gavage at a dose of 10 mg/kg.

At the indicated time points, mice were humanely sacrificed using isoflurane anesthesia, CO2, and cervical dislocation. Tumors were harvested and processed for flow cytometry or were saved in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks.

Immunohistochemistry

For Ki67 staining, FFPE tumor and lymph node sections were deparaffinized using three changes of xylene and were hydrated through graded alcohols. Antigen retrieval was performed for 20 minutes at 95°C, using a PT Link Antigen Retrieval machine (Agilent) with Tris-EDTA pH 9.0 buffer. Endogenous peroxidases were quenched with 4.5% H2O2 for 15 minutes, and slides were blocked with 2% donkey serum for 30 minutes. They were then stained with Ki67 antibody (1:400; Cell Signaling Technology, 12202S) for 30 minutes at 37°C and incubated in anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Dako, K4003) for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by 1 minute DAB exposure (Vector Laboratories, SK-4105). Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, blued with blueing buffer, and mounted with coverslips and Permount (Fisher Scientific, SP15-500). For pSTAT6-CD4 co-staining, slides were prepared with the same deparaffinization, antigen retrieval, and quenching/blocking steps. Then slides were incubated with CD4 antibody (1:50; Invitrogen, 14-0042-82) for 30 minutes at 37°C and incubated in anti-rat secondary antibody (Abcam, ab214882) for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by 2 minutes of DAB exposure. Following DAB exposure, slides were again quenched and blocked. They were then incubated overnight (O/N) at 4°C with pSTAT6 antibody (1:250; Cell Signaling Technology, 56554S) and then incubated in anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 1 hour, followed by a Magenta Red (Agilent, GV92511-2) detection protocol. Slides were then counterstained with hematoxylin, blued with blueing buffer, and mounted. For CCL17 and CCL22 staining, slides were prepared with the same deparaffinization, antigen retrieval, and quenching/blocking steps. Then slides were incubated with CCL17 (1:100; Abcam, ab182793) or CCL22 (1:100; Abcam, ab124768) antibody O/N at 4°C and incubated in anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by 1 minute and 30 seconds DAB exposure.

For all slides, images were acquired at 20X in brightfield with an AxioScan 7 (Zeiss) and analyzed using QuPath 0.5.1-x64 software. Where applicable, staining was quantified based on percent positivity for a given marker, based on an intensity cutoff, or was quantified as H-score. H-score is calculated by indexing each cell within the tissue as having negative, weak, moderate, or strong staining for the given marker, based on three different intensity cutoffs. Then, the following formula was used to score the tissues, of a maximum of 300:H‐Score=[0 × (% of negative cells)] + [1× (% of weak staining cells)] + [2 × (% of moderate staining cells)] + [3 × (% of strong staining cells)]

Flow cytometry

Cervical lymph node (cLN) tumors were harvested at the indicated time points and were gently crushed in a 1-mL Eppendorf tube using a pestle. Following crushing, cells were passed through a 70-μm cell strainer. Single cells obtained from tumor dissociation were counted, and 2 × 106 cells per sample were plated in a 96-well V-bottom plate. Cells were incubated with fixable aqua live/dead stain (30 minutes; Invitrogen L34966) and Fc block (30 minutes) and then incubated with fluorescently conjugated antibodies (30 minutes). For intracellular staining of transcription factors, cells were fixed and permeabilized (eBioscience, 00-5521-00) and then incubated in fluorescently conjugated intracellular staining antibodies diluted in permeabilization buffer. The flow antibodies used in this study can be found in Supplementary Table S2. Data were acquired on an LSRFortessa (BD; Lady Davis Institute Flow Cytometry Core), and FlowJo v10.10 (BD) was used for all flow cytometry data analysis. Gating for flow cytometry can be found in Supplementary Fig. S3.

Ex vivo chemoattraction

For experiments with chemoattraction towards tumor cells in vitro, Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6WT or Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6D419N cells were plated at a confluence of 3 × 105 cells/mL, overtop of iMEF cells. After 24 hours, tumor cells were stimulated with 5 ng/mL of murine (m) IL4 (PeproTech, 214-14-20) or vehicle control. After 1 hour, cells from a duplicate plate were collected for RNA isolation and Ccl17 and Ccl22 qPCR analysis. In tandem, spleens from female tumor-naïve C57BL/6 mice were harvested and dissociated. Red blood cells (RBC) were lysed for 10 minutes on ice (eBioscience, 00-4300-54). Splenocytes were treated with either 10 μmol/L AZD2098 (MedChemExpress) or vehicle control for 1 hour at room temperature. Following tumor cell incubation in mIL4 and splenocyte incubation in AZD2098, 1 × 106 bulk splenocytes were plated overtop the stimulated or nonstimulated Eμ-Myc tumor cells using a 5-μm pore transparent membrane (Sarstedt, 83.3930.500). Splenocytes were allowed to migrate toward Eμ-Myc tumor cells for 16 hours. Migrated splenocytes were collected and stained with aqua live/dead stain (30 minutes). They were then incubated with Fc block (30 minutes) and stained with fluorescently conjugated antibodies to detect B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, dendritic cells (DC), macrophages, NK cells, and eosinophils. Cells were not fixed in order to preserve tumor cell endogenous GFP expression. The entire sample was acquired on an LSRFortessa, and FlowJo was used for analysis. The plated tumor cells were distinguished from migrated cells via expression of GFP and CD19, and tumor cells were excluded from the migrated cell counts.

For chemoattraction toward tumor supernatants, cLN tumors and spleens were harvested at day 11 after Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6WT and Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6D419N tumor cell injection. cLNs were crushed in a 1-mL Eppendorf tube with 500 μL total volume of PBS and were incubated on ice for 20 minutes. Following incubation, tubes were spun down at 300 × g for 10 minutes, and tumor supernatants were collected (see below). Spleens were also dissociated with 10-minute RBC lysis. Following dissociation, CD4+ T cells were purified from splenocytes (Miltenyi, 130-104-454) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Following purification, CD4+ T cells were treated for 1 hour at room temperature with either 10 μmol/L AZD2098 or vehicle control. While CD4+ T cells were incubating in AZD2098, equal volumes of tumor supernatants or PBS control were plated in a 12-well plate. 5 × 105 CD4+ T cells were plated overtop the Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6WT and Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6D419N tumor supernatants using a 3-μm pore transparent membrane (Sarstedt, 83-3931.300). CD4+ T cells were allowed to migrate toward tumor supernatants for 16 hours and then were collected for CD4+ T-cell quantification and phenotyping by flow cytometry.

Chemokine/cytokine profiling

At day 11 after Eμ-Myc injection, Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6WT and Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6D419N tumors were dissociated, as described above. Tumor samples were allowed to incubate on ice on 20 minutes and then were spun down at 300 × g for 10 minutes. The supernatant was collected and was either used fresh for chemoattraction experiments or stored at −80°C for cytokine profiling. The cell pellet was counted. To determine the cytokine/chemokine constitution of tumor supernatants, 44-Plex Mouse Cytokine/Chemokine profiling was performed with Eve Technologies, and cytokine/chemokine expression was normalized to total cell number in each tumor.

In vitro doxorubicin sensitivity assay

Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6WT or Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6D419N cells were plated at a confluence of 3 × 105 cells/mL, overtop of iMEF cells. To determine whether CD4+ T cells affect Eμ-Myc doxorubicin sensitivity, after 24 hours, tumor cells were co-cultured with 3 × 105 CD4+ T cells (purified from spleen, as above), either directly, or indirectly using a 0.4-μm pore transparent membrane (Sarstedt, 83.3931.041). After 4 hours, cells were treated with doxorubicin for 16 hours at the indicated concentrations. Cells were then collected, and the proportion of live tumor cells was determined using flow cytometry, whereby live tumor cells were defined as the CD45+ CD4− CD19+ GFP+ L/D Aqua- population.

DLBCL patient tissues

The patient cohort used for PhenoCycler imaging consisted of FFPE preserved samples obtained from patients treated in Montreal, Quebec, at the Jewish General Hospital. All biopsies were obtained in the context of routine clinical care. Biobanking of patient plasma at the time of relapse and secondary use of FFPE samples for this project were approved by the research ethics board (11-047, 15-036). All patients provided informed written consent for tumor and plasma biobanking, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients were all treated with curative intent R-CHOP–like regimens. Using banked specimens, 1-mm cores of rrDLCBL FFPE tissue were arranged into a tissue microarray (TMA) at the Jewish General Hospital Research Pathology Core. TMA sections (4 μm) were mounted onto SuperFrost slides and were stained with a 52-plex PhenoCycler antibody cocktail, following the protocol described below. Cores from 39 patients were included in the TMA. However, cores from nine patients were either exhausted prior to staining or detached during the staining protocol, so tissue from a total of 31 patients was analyzed. For STAT6D419 mutant samples, whole sections from six patients were mounted onto SuperFrost slides, with two sections from two different patients per slide.

Patient metadata included age, sex, cell-of-origin, and STAT6 mutational status. Targeted sequencing and exome sequencing of the patient plasma, to determine STAT6 mutational status, was previously reported by Rushton and colleagues (22). Briefly, plasma samples were prepared into sequencing libraries using custom adapters containing unique molecule identifiers. Libraries were enriched using a custom set of probes targeted to genes known to be involved in rrDLBCL pathology, including STAT6, and sequenced using 150-bp reads on Illumina MiSeq or HiSeq2500 platforms. FFPE samples were prepared and previously sequenced using whole-exome capture. All sample somatic mutations were identified using Strelka2 and a custom bioinformatics pipeline previously described by Rushton and colleagues (22).

PhenoCycler staining

PhenoCycler staining was performed as described previously (23–25). FFPE lymphoma tissue sections were baked for 1 hour at 60°C, deparaffinized using xylene, and hydrated through graded alcohols. Antigen retrieval was performed for 20 minutes at 95°C, with Tris-EDTA pH 9.0 buffer. Tissue autofluorescence was quenched by bathing slides in a solution of 4.5% H2O2 and 20 mmol/L NaOH prepared in PBS, sandwiched between LED lights at 25,000 lux, for 45 minutes at room temperature. Slides were then washed with PBS (3 × 5 minutes) and hydration buffer (Akoya Biosciences; 2 × 5 minutes). Slides were equilibrated in staining buffer (Akoya Biosciences; 30 minutes) whereas the antibody staining cocktail was prepared in staining buffer supplemented with N, G, J, and S blockers (Akoya Biosciences) and oligonucleotide-conjugated antibodies diluted at the concentrations indicated in Supplementary Table S3. Antibody staining occurred in two steps. The first step used antibodies optimized for 30-minute incubation at 37°C. After the first staining step, slides were washed in three changes of staining buffer before proceeding. The second step used antibodies optimized for O/N incubation at 4°C. After O/N staining, slides were washed in three changes of staining buffer and fixed with 1.6% paraformaldehyde for 10 minutes, ice-cold methanol for 5 minutes, and proprietary fixative solution (Akoya Biosciences) for 20 minutes. Slides were then stored until imaging with a PhenoCycler-Fusion instrument (up to 2 days).

The slides were mounted with a flow cell (Akoya Biosciences) to allow for automated fluidics that wash on and off fluorescently conjugated oligonucleotides that are complementary to the oligonucleotides that are conjugated to each antibody (aka “reporters”) while allowing the slide to stay mounted to a fluorescent microscope. To image slides, a reporter plate was prepared that included solutions containing 1X PhenoCycler-Fusion buffer, 3 μg/mL DAPI (Thermo Fisher, D1306), 1 mg/mL salmon sperm DNA (Invitrogen, AM9680), and up to three different reporters, as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The reporter plate solutions were washed onto the tissue cyclically, using the PhenoCycler-Fusion instrument, until all antibodies in the staining panel were visualized. The final outcome was qptiff files with highly multiplexed immunofluorescent images. The following markers were excluded from analysis because of low-quality staining: Tbet and GATA3.

Following staining, the flow cell was removed using 24-hour incubation in Citrisolv (Decon Labs, 1601). The Citrisolv was washed off, using grading alcohols, and then hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed. Slides were then mounted and imaged with an AxioScan 7 (Zeiss).

PhenoCycler data analysis

Multiplexed qptiff images were uploaded to Enable Medicine, where cell segmentation was performed using DeepCell, with DRAQ5 as a nuclear marker and β-actin as a membrane marker. Quality control was used to exclude tumor cores of poor quality or individual cells that showed evidence of staining artifacts (i.e., increased signal sum, decreased signal variation, and abnormalities in cell size or nuclear signal). Unsupervised clustering was then performed using expression of the lineage markers CD11b, CD11c, CD14, CD15, CD20, CD21, CD31, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD68, FoxP3, Ki67, MPO, PAX5, pan-CK, and αSMA to identify major cell types. We used Leiden clustering, set to detect 50 neighbors, with a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection minimum distance of 0.1 and a clustering resolution of 0.5. The accuracy of clustering was visually confirmed using Voronoi diagrams, and Leiden subclustering was used to further refine clusters to ensure accurate cell classification. Subclustering was also used to subclassify cells based on the expression of markers of interest. Spatial neighbor calculations were performed in Enable by calculating the proportion of interacting cells for chosen cell types.

Results

The immunocompetent mSTAT6D419N-Eμ-Myc lymphoma recapitulates critical features of STAT6D419-mutant rrDLBCL

Using in vitro models, we have previously demonstrated that STAT6D419 lymphoma cells have a similar proliferative rate and therapeutic sensitivity as STAT6WT lymphoma cells (14). However, our previous data also indicated that STAT6D419 mutation may function to induce therapeutic resistance via modulation of the TME. Thus, we developed an immunocompetent mouse model of STAT6D419N lymphoma to study how mutated Stat6 affects microenvironmental dynamics. To do this, we generated Eμ-Myc lymphoma cell lines overexpressing either murine (m) Stat6WT or Stat6D419N. These lymphoma cells were injected into C57BL/6 mice via tail vein, where they home to the cLN and inguinal lymph node (iLN) and spleen and produce an orthotopic model of B-cell lymphoma (Fig. 1A). Disease burden was followed via ultrasound (Fig. 1B), and mice reached endpoint at 14 days after injection, when the primary cLN tumor site exceeded 100 mm3 and the secondary iLN tumor site reached 50 mm3. Critically, both Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6WT tumors and Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6D419N tumors grew at the same rate in the left and right sides of both cLN and iLN (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Fig. S1A and S1B), and neither genotype provided a survival advantage in the context of primary disease. This is an expected result, as both mSTAT6WT and mSTAT6D419N tumors are driven by Myc overexpression, and we did not anticipate Stat6 mutation to override this aggressive phenotype.

We aimed to determine how Stat6D419N mutation may affect the TME over the course of tumor formation. Therefore, to characterize these tumors during their development, lymph nodes were harvested at early-, mid-, and late-disease. At early and late disease, Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6WT and Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6D419N tumors had a similar percentage of Ki67+ cells within the cLN (Fig. 1D and E). However, at mid-disease, Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6D419N tumors had significantly less Ki67+ tumor cells, despite no difference in tumor weight (Fig. 1F). To characterize this discrepancy, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) co-staining for CD4 and phospho-STAT6 (Fig. 1G). Consistent with patient samples (14), we found that Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6D419N tumors had increased CD4 and phospho-STAT6 positivity (Fig. 1H), and these two metrics were positively correlated (Fig. 1I). This could be further recapitulated with flow cytometry, in which we found that Eμ-Myc-mSTAT6D419N tumors were composed of an increased proportion of CD4+ T cells (represented as % of CD45+ cells within the tumor mass) and had a significantly increased total abundance of CD4+ T cells (Fig. 1J). These differences were not observed at early or late disease (Supplementary Fig. S2A and S2B). No differences in proportion or abundance of CD8+ T cells were observed at any time point (Supplementary Fig. S2C–S2E).

IHC also validated that mSTAT6D419N tumors had significantly increased expression of CCL17 as compared with mSTAT6WT tumors at both mid- and late-disease (Fig. 1K). We further performed IHC for CCL22, a chemokine also under STAT6 transcriptional control with significant functional overlap to CCL17, but saw no significant differences in mid- or late-disease expression (Supplementary Fig. S2F and S2G). These findings demonstrate that the Eμ-Myc mouse model of STAT6D419N-mutant lymphoma replicates the human disease it is designed to model, including increased phospho-STAT6 positivity, increased CD4+ T-cell invasion, and increased expression of CCL17.

mSTAT6D419N-Eμ-Myc tumors have increased CCR4+ Th1 cells and increased hallmarks of inflammation

We next questioned whether the CD4+ T cells in mSTAT6D419N tumors were skewed toward any specific polarization state. In FL, increased CCL17 is associated with increased invasion of regulatory T cells (Treg; ref. 26). However, in DLBCL, STAT6 mutations have been associated with a GC-like TME that is enriched in both Tregs and T follicular helper cells (Tfh; ref. 27). In this model, we found neither Tregs nor Tfh cells were increased in prevalence or number in mSTAT6D419N tumors as compared with STAT6WT tumors (Fig. 2A and B; Supplementary Fig. S3A). Therefore, we next looked at inflammatory T helper cells (Th1) and anti-inflammatory T helper cells (Th2), and found that Th1s, but not Th2s, were increased in prevalence and number in mSTAT6D419N tumors (Fig. 2C and D; Supplementary Fig. S3B).

One potential mechanism in which a STAT6-mediated increase in CCL17 can drive CD4+ T-cell recruitment is via the recruitment of CCR4+ immune cells. Therefore, we next examined the expression CCR4 on CD4+ T cells in Eμ-Myc tumors. We found that CCR4+ CD4+ T cells were increased in both prevalence and number in the mSTAT6D419N TME (Fig. 2E). We further examined the expression of CCR4 on Th1 and Th2 cells to better mechanistically understand the observed increase in Th1 cells in mSTAT6D419N tumors. Indeed, a higher percentage of Th1 cells in mSTAT6D419N tumors were CCR4-positive compared with those in mSTAT6WT tumors. Additionally, Th1 cells in mSTAT6D419N tumors exhibited a significantly higher mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CCR4 expression (Fig. 2F). In contrast, Th2 cells from both tumor types exhibited a similar CCR4 prevalence and MFI (Fig. 2G).

To explore the broader impact of these TME changes on tumor cells, we isolated Eμ-Myc cells from mSTAT6WT and mSTAT6D419N mid-disease tumors, using CD19 microbeads, and performed bulk RNA-seq (Fig. 2H). Gene set enrichment analysis indicated that mSTAT6D419N tumor cells had increased hallmarks of inflammation (Fig. 2I), consistent with the elevated presence of Th1 cells. We additionally found that 25% of Th1 cells in both mSTAT6WT and mSTAT6D419N tumors expressed IFNγ (Supplementary Fig. S4A), suggesting that the increased number of Th1 cells in mSTAT6D419N tumors could lead to increased inflammation within tumors. Th1 cells were also positive for activation or degranulation markers, CD69 and CD107a (Supplementary Fig. S4A–S4C), whereas the expression of these markers was lower CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. S4D–S4F). These findings demonstrate that mSTAT6D419N mutation leads to the increased presence of IFNγ-expressing CCR4+ Th1 cells, which may contribute to an enhanced inflammatory response signature in mSTAT6D419N tumor cells.

mSTAT6D419N tumors are chemoattractive to CCR4+ CD4+ T cells

To experimentally validate that STAT6D419N tumor cells attract CD4+ T cells, we tested the ability of Eμ-Myc cells in culture to attract various CCR4+ immune cells, including CD4+ T cells, eosinophils, DCs, macrophages, NK cells, and CD8+ T cells. In this experiment, mSTAT6WT and mSTAT6D419N Eμ-Myc cells were stimulated with IL4 to induce STAT6 activation. One hour following stimulation, bulk splenocytes from non–tumor-bearing mice were plated overtop the Eμ-Myc cells using a 5-μm pore membrane. After 16 hours, migrated cells were collected and characterized by flow cytometry (Fig. 3A). qPCR confirmed that mSTAT6D419N cells produced significantly more Ccl17, but not Ccl22, in response to IL4 stimulation than mSTAT6WT cells (Fig. 3B).

Within the splenocyte input, only 4% of cells were positive for CCR4, mostly consisting of CD4+ T cells. However, following migration toward Eμ-Myc cells, the proportion of CCR4+ cells increased 10-fold (Fig. 3C). Whereas the proportions of CCR4+ cells that migrated toward both stimulated and unstimuated mSTAT6WT and mSTAT6D419N cells were similar, significantly more CCR4+ cells migrated toward IL4 stimulated Eμ-Myc cells than unstimulated Eμ-Myc, predominantly consisting of CD4+ T cells (Fig. 3D). Moreover, IL4-stimulated mSTAT6D419N cells induced significantly greater migration of CCR4+ CD4+ T cells as compared with mSTAT6WT cells. This migration was blocked by pretreating splenocytes with the CCR4 inhibitor AZD2098 (28). These findings demonstrate that IL4 stimulated mSTAT6D419N cells can attract a higher number of CCR4+ CD4+ T cells, highlighting the functional impact of increased CCL17 production by mSTAT6D419N tumor cells.

In vivo, there is extensive crosstalk between tumor cells, immune cells, and stroma that is not recapitulated with splenocytes from tumor-naïve mice migrating toward tumor cells stimulated with a single cytokine. Thus, we next modeled the complexity of an intact TME ex vivo. To do this, mice were injected with either mSTAT6WT or mSTAT6D419N cells, and at mid-disease, tumors and spleens were harvested and dissociated. CD4+ T cells were enriched from the tumor-bearing spleens, and tumor supernatants were collected from lymph nodes (Fig. 3E). Thus, with this model, splenic CD4+ T cells have been polarized due to tumor exposure, and tumor supernatants represent the secretome of an intact TME. Chemokine/cytokine profiling of tumor supernatants demonstrated that mSTAT6D419N tumors expressed higher levels of many cytokines/chemokines, including CCL17, and the Th1-associated cytokines IFNγ, IL2, TNFα, and IL12 (Fig. 3F). The supernatants were then used as chemoattractants to CD4+ T cells derived from tumor-bearing spleens (Fig. 3G).

Significantly more CCR4+ CD4+ T cells migrated toward mSTAT6D419N supernatant compared with mSTAT6WT supernatant, regardless of whether the CD4+ T cells were derived from mSTAT6WT or mSTAT6D419N tumor–bearing mice (Fig. 3H). This migration of CCR4+ T cells toward mSTAT6D419N supernatants, but not mSTAT6WT supernatants, was markedly reduced when CD4+ T cells were pretreated with AZD2098, indicating the role of CCR4–CCL17 interaction in transmigration of CD4+ T cells toward STAT6D419N supernatant. We further phenotyped the migrated T cells and found that an increased number of Tregs, Tfh, Th1, and Th2 cells migrated toward mSTAT6D419N supernatants than migrated toward mSTAT6WT supernatants, but this was effectively blocked with AZD2098 treatment (Fig. 3I). Notably, Tregs seemed to show drastically increased migration toward the tumor supernatant relative to PBS control, but we postulate that this is due to the presence of factors like IL2 and TNFα in tumor supernatants promoting Treg proliferation. All together, these results suggest that the chemoattractive effect of mSTAT6D419N supernatant is not specific to any CD4+ T-cell subset but that the observed increase in Th1 cells in mSTAT6D419N tumors in vivo may be due to increased retention, proliferation, or polarization of Th1 cells in the TME. Moreover, these experiments collectively demonstrate that STAT6D419N tumors possess a heightened ability to attract CCR4+ CD4+ T cells.

mSTAT6D419N Eμ-Myc tumors are resistant to doxorubicin treatment

In vitro, mSTAT6D419N-Eμ-Myc tumor cells do not have altered sensitivity to doxorubicin (Supplementary Fig. S5A). However, our data indicate that mStat6D419N mutation drives increased frequency of CCR4+ CD4+ T cells, leading to evidence of enhanced tumor cell inflammatory signaling, which is known to correlate with doxorubicin resistance in human patients with DLBCL (29–32). We found that direct or indirect co-culture of Eμ-Myc tumor cells with CD4+ T cells is not sufficient to induce doxorubicin resistance (Supplementary Fig. S5B and S5C); thus, we hypothesized that an intact TME may be required to observe the impact of Stat6D419 mutation on therapeutic response and that our mouse model of Stat6D419-mutant lymphoma may not recapitulate the results previously obtained in vitro.

To investigate this, mice were injected with Eμ-Myc mSTAT6WT or mSTAT6D419N tumor cells on day 1 and then were given 3 mg/kg doxorubicin at days 11, 13, and 15 after injection (Fig. 4A). We found that mSTAT6WT tumors returned to baseline size by day 15 after injection, indicating the efficacy of doxorubicin treatment for these aggressive Myc-driven tumors. In contrast, mSTAT6D419N tumor volume did not decrease during doxorubicin treatment, and tumors continued to grow immediately upon the cessation of treatment (Fig. 4B). Additionally, ultrasound monitoring of the secondary tumor site, the iLN, revealed that 100% of mice with mSTAT6WT tumors had sustained disease clearance in the iLN, whereas 40% of mice with mSTAT6D419N tumors had bilateral iLN relapse (Fig. 4C). Although not achieving statistical significance (Gehan–Breslow–Wilcoxon test P = 0.0764), this led to decreased overall survival (OS) of mSTAT6D419N tumor–bearing mice treated with doxorubicin (Fig. 4D), which we believe to be biologically significant, given the aggressive nature of Eμ-Myc tumors.

We next tested another treatment paradigm, by giving each mouse a single high-dose doxorubicin treatment of 10 mg/kg at day 11 after injection, which represents a time point in which tumors have established, and our data indicate that mSTAT6D419N tumors have CCR4+ CD4+ T-cell invasion (Fig. 4E). Like our findings with 3 × 3 mg/kg doxorubicin, we found that 100% of mice bearing mSTAT6WT tumors decreased to baseline size, whereas only 50% of mice bearing mSTAT6D419N tumors decreased in size (Fig. 4F). This was recapitulated in the iLN, where 100% of mice bearing mSTAT6WT tumors had durable disease clearance in the iLN, whereas 50% of mice bearing mSTAT6D419N tumors had bilateral iLN relapse, and another 10% had unilateral iLN relapse (Fig. 4G). Accordingly, mSTAT6WT tumor–bearing mice had a trend toward improved OS (Gehan–Breslow–Wilcoxon test P = 0.1211; Fig. 4H). Tumors collected at disease endpoint showed no difference in tumor burden or skewing in the proportions of CD4+ T cells, CCR4+ CD4+ T Cells, CD8+ T cells, or CCL17 expression between mSTAT6WT and mSTAT6D419N tumors (Supplementary Fig. S6A–S6F). These results indicate that the mSTAT6D419N-driven changes in the TME at day 11 are sufficient to reduce the tumor response to doxorubicin but that changes in the TME are not retained or exaggerated at disease relapse in this mouse model.

Treatment of mSTAT6D419N Eμ-Myc tumors with the CCR4 inhibitor AZD2098 resensitizes them to doxorubicin

We next aimed to investigate how inhibiting CCR4 would affect the growth of Eμ-Myc tumors, following our discovery that mStat6D419N mutation in Eu-Myc tumor cells enhances the recruitment of CCR4+ CD4+ T cells. To do this, we treated Eμ-Myc tumor–bearing mice with AZD2098 and monitored tumor growth and changes in the TME over time (Fig. 5A). AZD2098 successfully blocked the mid-disease invasion of CD4+ T cells into mSTAT6D419N tumors (Fig. 5B). Further analysis showed that AZD2098 did not affect the numbers of Tregs or Tfh cells in either mSTAT6WT or mSTAT6D419N tumors. However, as previously noted, Th1 cells were more abundant in mSTAT6D419N tumors compared with mSTAT6WT, and treatment with AZD2098 resulted in a modest decrease in Th1 cells in mSTAT6D419N tumors. Consistent with CCR4’s role in Th2 cells (33, 34), AZD2098 treatment reduced Th2 cells in both mSTAT6WT and mSTAT6D419N tumors (Fig. 5C). Despite these shifts in TME composition, AZD2098 treatment did not significantly alter the growth rate of either mSTAT6WT or mSTAT6D419N tumors (Fig. 5D).

Encouraged by AZD2098’s ability to reduce CD4+ T-cell infiltration in mSTAT6D419N tumors to levels comparable with mSTAT6WT, we hypothesized that the addition of AZD2098 could improve the response to doxorubicin in mSTAT6D419N tumors (Fig. 5E). As in previous experiments, 100% of mSTAT6WT tumors responded completely to doxorubicin alone, whereas only 20% of mSTAT6D419N tumors responded (Fig. 5F). Remarkably, combining AZD2098 with doxorubicin resulted in a complete response of 100% of mSTAT6WT tumors and 80% of mSTAT6D419N tumors. Whereas mSTAT6D419N tumors treated with doxorubicin alone resumed growth immediately after treatment cessation, tumors treated with the combination of doxorubicin and AZD2098 did not regrow until approximately 1 week after treatment (Fig. 5G). Additionally, 80% of mSTAT6D419N tumors treated with doxorubicin alone showed bilateral iLN relapse, whereas those treated with the combination showed only unilateral relapse, indicating a minor reduction in systemic disease (Fig. 5H). Overall, the combination treatment significantly improved survival in mice bearing mSTAT6D419N tumors but not in those with mSTAT6WT tumors (Fig. 5I). These findings demonstrate that inhibiting the CCL17–CCR4 signaling axis resensitizes mSTAT6D419N Eμ-Myc tumors to doxorubicin treatment.

PhenoCycler imaging of STAT6D419-mutant rrDLBCL patient samples shows increased expression of phospho-STAT6, CCL17, and CCR4

Of these STAT6D419-mutant tissue biopsies, three were relapse biopsies from patients with DLBCL, one was a diagnostic DLBCL biopsy from a patient who proceeded to relapse, one was a diagnostic PMBCL biopsy, and one was a diagnostic FL biopsy from a tumor that proceeded to transform into DLBCL (GCB) involving the central nervous system. The inclusion of non-rrDLBCL STAT6-mutant lymphoma samples provided us with the unique opportunity to compare how STAT6 mutations could affect the TME in diverse pathologies in which STAT6D419 mutations have been previous observed.

In tumor cells, we found that phospho-STAT6 was more highly expressed in STAT6D419-mutant biopsies than STAT6WT biopsies (Fig. 7D), whereas Ki67 had similar staining intensity (Fig. 7E), indicating that the observed differences between STATD419-mutant tissues and STAT6WT tissues is not due higher background staining intensity. Concordant with their status as STAT6-transcriptional targets, we found that CCL17 and CCL22 had significantly increased staining intensity in STAT6D419-mutant tumor cells, and both markers were positively correlated with phospho-STAT6 expression (Fig. 7F and G).

In STAT6WT biopsies, CCR4 was expressed at its highest level on DCs and macrophages (Fig. 7H). In STAT6D419-mutant biopsies, CCR4 was expressed highly on all immune cells of the TME. We directly compared the expression of CCR4 on CD4+ T cells in STAT6WT- and STAT6D419-mutant biopsies and found that CCR4 had a trend toward increased expression on CD4+ T cells from STAT6D419-mutant rrDLBCL biopsies and significantly increased expression on CD4+ T cells from the other lymphoma types. Regardless of lymphoma type, CCR4 expression on CD4+ T cells was shown to positively correlate with phospho-STAT6 expression (Fig. 7I).

Finally, we asked whether CD4+ and CCR4+ CD4+ T cells have increased proximity to phospho-STAT6+ tumor cells in STAT6D419-mutant samples. To do this, we measured the proportion of total tumor cells versus phospho-STAT6+ tumor cells that have direct cellular contact with CD4+/CCR4+ CD4+ T cells (Fig. 7J). When these measurements were performed, we found that the proportion of total tumor cells interacting with CD4+ T cells or CCR4+ CD4+ T cells was unchanged between STAT6WT- and STAT6D419-mutant tissues. However, we found that the proportion of CD4+ T cells interacting with phospho-STAT6+ tumor cells was significantly increased in STAT6D419-mutant biopsies, and the proportion of CCR4+ CD4+ T cells interacting with phospho-STAT6+ tumor cells trended towards increased in STAT6D419-mutant biopsies. Thus, these results strongly imply that the CCL17/CCL22–CCR4 axis is overactivated in STAT6D419 lymphoma tissues, leading to alterations in TME organization and favoring interactions between CD4+ T cells and phospho-STAT6+ tumor cells.

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrate that mutation of STAT6 at residue D419 is sufficient to drive microenvironmental remodeling in the early stages of tumor development in an aggressive Myc-driven model of lymphoma, largely via the CCL17–CCR4 axis. Using the immunocompetent Eμ-Myc mouse model, we showed that STAT6D419N-mutant tumors have increased invasion of CCR4+ CD4+ T cells, coupled with resistance to doxorubicin treatment. Moreover, ex vivo functional assays demonstrated that STAT6D419N tumor cells are directly chemoattractive to CCR4+ CD4+ T cells, and in vivo treatment with the CCR4 inhibitor AZD2098 reduced CD4+ T-cell infiltration and resensitized tumors to doxorubicin.

Clinically, STAT6 mutations are uncommon at initial diagnosis but enriched at relapse. This may arise due to newly acquired mutations at disease relapse or through outgrowth of treatment resistance clones following the selective pressure of R-CHOP. In the latter scenario, it is likely that STAT6 mutations confer a survival advantage under treatment pressure (16), consistent with our findings that STAT6D419N tumors are resistant to doxorubicin in vivo. In human STATD419 tumor samples, we confirmed elevated expression of phospho-STAT6 in tumor cells relative to STAT6WT rrDLBCL samples. As predicted by our mouse modeling, we observed increased tumor cell expression of CCL17 and increased cellular interactions of phospho-STAT6+ tumor cells with CD4+ T cells. Unlike our mouse models, we also found that CCL22 is increased in STAT6D419-mutant tumor cells, demonstrating potent activation of this signaling axis.

Given the increase in phospho-STAT6 expression observed in human and murine STAT6D419 DLBCL tumors (13), we initially hypothesized that STAT6D419-mutant tumor cells would be more chemoattractive to IL4-producing CD4+ T cells, such as Th2 or Tfh cells, which may then function to maintain STAT6 phosphorylation. Unexpectedly, we found that STAT6-mutant lymphoma tumor cells have an increased abundance of CCR4+ Th1-polarized CD4+ T cells. Although CCR4 expression is typically associated with Th2 cells and Tregs, its expression on Th1 cells has also been described (33, 35, 36). Indeed, the mStat6D419N TME has increased Th1-associated cytokines, mStat6-mutant tumor cells display evidence of an inflammatory gene signature, and IFNγ expression is increased in CD4+ T cells in STAT6D419 lymphoma biopsies. Concordant with previously published literature in DLBCL and other solid tumors, this inflammatory TME is associated with therapeutic resistance to doxorubicin (29–32, 37–39).

Pharmacologic blockade of CCR4 with AZD2098 reduced CD4+ T-cell invasion and restored doxorubicin sensitivity. We further found that AZD2098 reduced Th2 cells in both mSTAT6WT and mSTAT6D419N tumors but only reduced Th1 cells in mSTAT6D419N tumors. Given that doxorubicin resistance was only observed in mSTAT6D419N tumors, we postulate the restoration of therapeutic sensitivity achieved with AZD2098 is due to the reduction of Th1 that is specific to mSTAT6D419N, rather than the shared reduction of Th2 cells.

CCR4 has been established as a therapeutic target in mycosis fungoides (MF) and Sezary syndrome (SS) due to tumor cell upregulation of CCR4. Indeed, the humanized CCR4-targeted mAb mogamulizumab is FDA-approved for the treatment of relapsed/refractory MF and relapsed/refractory SS (40, 41). CCR4 has also been tested as a therapeutic target in DLBCL. In a phase I clinical trial (NCT03309878), mogamulizumab was tested in combination with the anti–PD-1 pembrolizumab for the treatment of rrDLBCL (42); however, due to the presence of serious adverse events, the trial was discontinued prior to phase II. Our data suggest that STAT6-mutant rrDLBCL may benefit from mogamulizumab, warranting future trials to test it in safe combinations with rrDLBCL therapies, such as salvage chemotherapy. To this end, our preclinical mouse model of mSTAT6D419N-mutant lymphoma may be further used to determine whether STAT6 D419 mutations confer resistance to chemotherapies such as gemcitabine, cisplatin, or etoposide, which are all commonly used for the treatment of rrDLBCL, and to determine whether CCR4 inhibition augments the response to these chemotherapies.

Whereas our results indicate a critical role for the CCL17–CCR4 axis in STAT6D419-mediated microenvironmental remodeling, other mechanisms may also underlie the observed doxorubicin resistance. STAT6 regulates genes such as CD23, CISH, IL2RA, BCL-6, BCL-XL, CDK2 (4, 6, 43), which influence B-cell activation, resistance to apoptosis, and cell cycling. Moreover, our results found that expression of Myc transcriptional targets are altered in STAT6D419N tumors. Future studies will investigate how altered regulation of these genes affects treatment resistance within an intact TME and will investigate how STAT6D19 mutation might cooperate with other genomic alterations that are common in lymphoma to promote drug resistance. As such, our study did not address specifically why CCR4+ CD4+ T cells engender therapeutic resistance in STAT6D419N mutant tumors or whether CCR4-expressing myeloid components of the TME play a role. Additional studies will use in vivo CD4-depleting antibodies to determine the functional significance of CD4+ T cells to Eu-Myc lymphoma growth and will use single-cell transcriptomic approaches on murine and human samples to determine the specific phenotype of CCR4+ CD4+ T cells and myeloid cells and how they might drive tumor growth and chemoresistance.

Our findings highlight the importance of investigations focused on the intricate interplay between DLBCL tumor cells and the TME. Indeed, the full functional impact of STAT6D419 mutations could only be realized by utilizing an immunocompetent mouse model. Many gene mutations are predicted to modify the interaction of DLBCL with its TME; thus, future studies should take a TME-centric approach to better understand how this relationship affects response to immune-based therapies, such as immunomodulatory agents, bispecific T-cell engagers, and chimeric antigen receptor T cells. Overall, a better understanding of the mechanisms of therapeutic resistance and immune escape in DLBCL could improve clinical outcomes, particularly for those with relapsed and refractory diseases.
