Evolutionary dynamics of repetitive elements and genome size in Tetrigidae (Orthoptera: Caelifera)
Authors: Muhammad Salman, Ping Wang, Nian Liu, Xuanzeng Liu, Weian Deng, Yuan Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRepetitive elements (REs), involving both satellite DNA (satDNA) and transposable elements (TEs), significantly influence the evolutionary dynamics of the host genome. The order Orthoptera, known for its large genomes, serves as an ideal model for studying the relationship between repeat elements and genome size. We conducted a comparative repeatome analysis across ten Tetrigidae species, using low-coverage short reads in RepeatExplorer2 and dnaPipeTE. Our findings revealed that RE content constitutes between 42.82% (in Thoradonta yunnana) and 60.86% (in Saussurella cornuta) of their genomes, with major components including LINEs, LTR/Gypsy, LTR/Copia, and DNA transposons (Maverick and Helitron). We observed a strong positive correlation between repeat and TE content with genome size, suggesting genome expansion in Tetrigidae is likely driven primarily by repetitive DNA, particularly transposable elements. TE divergence analysis, calculated using RepeatMasker, indicates that species with large genomes, such as S. cornuta (2.836 Gb) and Formosatettix gonggashanensis (1.465 Gb), have experienced both ancient and recent bursts of TE activity. Satellitome analysis revealed a diverse range of satDNA families, with Macromotettixoides jiuwanshanensis having the highest number (34 families) and Phaesticus moniliantennatus showing the greatest abundance (16.76%). Additionally, a fossil-calibrated phylogeny based on 13 mitochondrial protein-coding genes (PCGs) enabled ancestral state reconstructions of genome size, repeat content, and TE composition, revealing lineage-specific patterns of genomic evolution. This study highlights the critical role of repetitive DNA, particularly TEs, in driving genomic complexity and expansion, offering key insights into the evolutionary dynamics of Tetrigidae.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17670-2.Keywords: Transposable elements, TE evolutionary patterns, Genome size, CorrelationSubject terms: Mobile elements, Comparative genomics, Molecular evolution

Introduction

Repetitive DNA sequences are ubiquitous across eukaryotic genomes, serving a vital function in genome size differentiation and evolution 1–3. These sequences are broadly categorized into tandemly repeated regions, such as satellite DNA, and interspersed repeat components, which include transposable elements (TEs) 4,5. Together, they comprise what is collectively known as the “repeatome” 6,7. The satellite repeats were first identified by Kit in the guinea pig and mice genomes 8. These sequences are typically organized in tandem arrays and are predominantly located in heterochromatic regions, often associated with important structural and functional genomic regions like centromeres and telomeres 5,9,10. Recently, the complete set of satDNA within a genome is referred to as the “satellitome 11. Satellite DNA constitutes a significant portion of many insect genomes and plays important roles in genome organization and evolution. Its abundance and dynamics have been studied across diverse insect orders, including Diptera 12, Neuroptera 13, Hemiptera 14, Coleoptera 15, Hymenoptera 16, Lepidoptera 17, and Orthoptera, which exhibit particularly high proportions of satellite repeats 18,19.

Transposable elements (TEs) are mobile genetic sequences that play a pivotal role in eukaryotic genome evolution 20–22. These elements are broadly classified into two categories based on their transposition mechanisms. Class I TEs (retrotransposons), including SINEs, LINEs, and LTR elements, propagate via an RNA intermediate through a “copy-and-paste” mechanism involving reverse transcription. In contrast, Class II TEs (DNA transposons) such as Maverick and Helitrons employ a “cut-and-paste” strategy that does not require RNA intermediates 20,23,24. A defining characteristic TEs is their ability to mobilize and replicate within host genomes, leveraging the host’s cellular machinery to increase their copy number across generations. This self-propagating potential allows even a single TE insertion to expand substantially, contributing to structural variation and influencing genomic function and evolution 25–27.

The expansion of REs, involving TEs and satDNA, is now recognized as a major contributor to genome size variation across eukaryotes 28,29. In the animal kingdom alone, genome sizes vary by an astonishing 6,600-fold, ranging from 0.02 to 132.83 pg 30. Insects display a vast diversity in nuclear genome sizes, from 0.09 pg (Mayetiola destructor; Diptera: Cecidomyiidae) 22 to 21.96 pg (Bryodemella tuberculata; Orthoptera: Acrididae) 31. Grasshoppers, in particular, are notable for their larger genome sizes, which are closely linked to a high TE content and associated with genome gigantism 26,30,32–35. The family Tetrigidae Rambur 1838 is an ancient and widespread group within Orthoptera, comprising over 300 genera and 2,000 known species 36. Commonly referred to as pygmy grasshoppers or grouse locusts, they are the second most diverse group of Orthoptera after the Acrididae. 37,38. These insects have a relatively uniform body structure and are typically small, averaging about 1 cm in length 39,40. Generally inconspicuous, they inhabit moist environments, with some species even adapted to semi-aquatic habitats. Despite their diversity, Tetrigidae remains one of the least-studied families within Orthoptera 41.

In this study, we investigated the REs in ten species of the family Tetrigidae. We employed RepeatExplorer2 (RE2), TAREAN 42, and dnaPipeTE pipelines 3 on low-coverage short-read data to identify genomic repeats, including transposable elements and satellite DNA. RepeatMasker was utilized to estimate the divergence and abundance of TEs and satellite repeats. Additionally, we performed mitochondrial genome assembly using MitoZ and conducted a fossil-calibrated phylogenetic analysis in a Bayesian framework (BEAST2) based on 13 mitochondrial PCGs. This integrated approach aimed to explore the evolutionary patterns of REs, evaluate their contribution to genome size variation, and offer valuable insights into genome evolution in Tetrigidae grasshoppers.

Results

Fossil-calibrated phylogeny of Tetrigidae

We reconstructed a time-calibrated phylogeny of ten Tetrigidae species using 13 mitochondrial protein-coding genes (PCGs), assembled and annotated with MitoZ (Supplementary Table 5), within a Bayesian inference framework implemented in BEAST2. The resulting chronogram (Fig. 1a) reveals two major, well-supported clades, with high posterior probabilities (pp) indicated by node labels and divergence times represented by 95% highest posterior density (HPD) intervals as node bars. The crown divergence of the group is estimated at approximately 115.96 million years ago (Mya) during the Early Cretaceous (95% HPD: 113.30–126.33), followed by a deep split forming two primary lineages. The first clade includes T. yunnana, Macromotettix guangxiensis, M. jiuwanshanensis, P. monilantennatus, and Gibbotettix guangxiensis, which diversified during the Paleogene, with their most recent common ancestor dated to around 43.25 Mya (Fig. 1a). Within this group, T. yunnana diverged earliest (~ 26.46 Mya), indicating an early separation from the rest. The second clade comprises Xistrella cliva, Tetradinodula bambusae, F. gonggashanensis, S. cornuta, and Austrohancockia guangxiensis, with a shared ancestor estimated at 43.52 Mya. Notably, the split between T. bambusae and F. gonggashanensis has moderate support (pp = 0.772), the only node in the tree not strongly supported. All other nodes show high support (pp = 0.99–1.00). The terminal pair S. cornuta and A. guangxiensis forms a well-supported sister lineage, diverging around 41.06 Mya (95% HPD: 38.48–45.09). The integration of HPD intervals and a detailed Mesozoic–Cenozoic geological timescale enables a clear understanding of divergence timing in relation to major geological events. This chronogram offers a strong foundation for future investigations on the evolutionary history, ecology, and comparative genomics of Tetrigidae.

Genome size estimation and RE abundance

Genome size estimation using k-mer analysis with Jellyfish v2.3.0 and GenomeScope revealed notable variation across the ten Tetrigidae species. The largest genome was found in S. cornuta (2.836 Gb), more than twice the size of the smallest genome observed. M. jiuwanshanensis (1.479 Gb) and F. gonggashanensis (1.465 Gb) followed, representing the next largest genome (Fig. 1b). At the lower end, T. bambusae had the smallest genome (1.029 Gb), with slightly higher sizes in A. guangxiensis (1.033 Gb) and T. yunnana (1.044 Gb). Other species, such as M. guangxiensis (1.358 Gb), X. cliva (1.151 Gb), G. guangxiensis (1.115 Gb), and P. moniliantennatus (1.135 Gb), had genome sizes within the intermediate range (Fig. 1b). This variation provides insight into genome evolution within Tetrigidae and supports further investigation into its relationship with repeat content.

We utilized the RepeatExplorer2 pipeline for quantification of DNA repeat content, using low coverage (0.1–0.5 ×) Illumina short reads (Supplementary Table 2). The results revealed a highly repetitive genomic content across the Tetrigidae species, with the highest repeat percentage observed in our largest genome S. cornuta at 60.86%, followed by G. guangxiensis at 60.12% and 59.25% in F. gonggashanensis (Fig. 1c). On average, 53.84% of the genome in the ten selected species consisted of repetitive elements. The REs percentages for M. jiuwanshanensis, M. guangxiensis, X. cliva and T. bambusae were 58.14%, 54.97%, 54.83% and 51.94%, respectively (Fig. 1c). Additionally, the lowest repeat content was observed in T. yunnana (42.82%), while P. moniliantennatus (46.80%) and A. guangxiensis (48.63%) had slightly higher percentages (Table 1). RE2 clustering summary revealed a dominance of REs in Tetrigidae species, with supercluster boxes skewed toward the repeat-rich left side; box height represents read count, while the x-axis shows the proportion of repeats (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Comparative analysis of REs and their correlation with genome size

The comparative analysis of ancestral genome size estimates suggests a moderate baseline, with S. cornuta showing a noticeable increase (2.836 Gb), followed by M. jiuwanshanensis and F. gonggashanensis, whereas T. bambusae retained a relatively compact genome (~ 1.029 Gb) throughout its evolutionary history (Fig. 2a). The overall genomic repeat content in Tetrigidae species includes both Class I and Class II repeat elements, with both types abundantly represented. Class I elements were particularly dominant, including LTR retrotransposons such as Ty1_copia, Ty3_gypsy, and Bel-Pao, as well as non-LTR elements like LINEs and Penelope. Among Class II elements, Maverick and Helitron transposons were identified (Fig. 2b). Among all repeat types, LINEs and Ty3_gypsy elements were the most consistently prevalent across species. LINEs accounted for 49.41% of total repeats in M. guangxiensis, 35.54% in T. yunnana, and 31.90% in T. bambusae of total repeat content. Similarly, Ty3_gypsy comprised 20.12% of repeats in S. cornuta and 14.5% in G. guangxiensis (Fig. 2b). Maverick elements made up 21.15% in M. jiuwanshanensis, while Helitron elements constituted 14.28% in S. cornuta. Satellite DNA emerged as the dominant tandem repeat, with P. moniliantennatus showing the highest proportion at 41.62% and M. guangxiensis the lowest at 4.71% of total repeat content. Other tandem repeats identified included 45S rDNA and 5S rDNA (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 3).

The RE2 comparative clustering analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1) revealed distinct repeat structure profiles among Tetrigidae species. The repeat landscape showed that most repeat clusters were shared across species, suggesting relatively recent divergence within the Tetrigidae family. Only a small fraction of clusters were species-specific, primarily comprising satDNA, which is known as one of the most evolutionarily dynamic components of REs. Species such as S. cornuta, M. jiuwanshanensis, and F. gonggashanensis exhibited species-specific top-clusters, largely consisting of satDNA, LINEs, Ty3_gypsy, Helitron, Maverick, and unknown repeats (Supplementary Fig. 1b). In contrast, closely related species such as T. yunnana and M. guangxiensis, as inferred from the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1a), showed nearly identical repeat landscapes, further supporting their recent evolutionary divergence. Additionally, the height of the peaks in the clustering plots was normalized to genome size, with taller peaks representing a higher read numbers in that specific cluster relative to the genome size (Supplementary Fig. 1c).

We employed dnaPipeTE with the Orthoptera-TElib, reference library to enhance TE annotation across Tetrigidae species. The analysis revealed substantial variation in repeat composition, with DNA transposons and LINEs as the dominant TE classes in most genomes. S. cornuta, which has the largest genome, showed the highest DNA transposon content (27.28%), along with notable levels of LINEs (9.49%) and LTRs (7.51%). The highest LINE content was observed in M. guangxiensis (36.66%) followed by F. gonggashanensis (25.59%), and T. bambusae (24.61%) (Supplementary Table 4). SINEs were consistently low across species, ranging from 0.44% in F. gonggashanensis to 4.04% in S. cornuta. Helitrons were also restricted, varying from 0.87% in G. guangxiensis to a peak of 11.65% in P. moniliantennatus (Supplementary Table 4). These patterns indicate that proliferation of specific TE classes, primarily LINEs and DNA transposons, contributes to genome size variation and structural diversity in Tetrigidae.

We performed Pearson correlation analysis to evaluate how genome size is associated with repeat content. The analysis revealed a strong positive relationship between genome size and both total repeat content (r = 0.971, p < 0.001) and transposable element content (r = 0.952, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2c). These findings suggest that species with larger genomes generally have a higher abundance of repetitive sequences. Among TE categories, significant correlations were found for LTRs (r = 0.928, p < 0.001), DNA transposons (r = 0.947, p < 0.001), and SINEs (r = 0.928, p < 0.001), whereas LINEs showed a moderate, non-significant association (r = 0.446, p = 0.1964) (Supplementary Fig. 3). Satellite DNA exhibited only a weak, statistically non-significant correlation (r = 0.228, p = 0.5262), while unclassified repeat elements revealed a strong association with genome size (r = 0.914, p < 0.001). These findings underscore the substantial role of repetitive DNA, particularly TEs, in driving genome size expansion within Tetrigidae lineage.

TE divergence and evolutionary dynamics

Repeat landscapes for TE divergence activity were generated using RepeatMasker, based on dnaPipeTE output (see “Methods”), to determine whether specific TEs reflect recent activity or ancient proliferation events. The Kimura 2-parameter (K2P) genetic divergence metric was used to estimate the timing of TE insertions. Across all species analyzed, DNA transposons and LINEs comprised the majority of the TE content, with LTRs and other classes contributing smaller proportions. Most species showed divergence peaks concentrated between 25–45% K2P, indicating that the majority of TE insertions are relatively ancient (Fig. 3). However, a few species, including S. cornuta, P. monilantennatus, and F. gonggashanensis, exhibited additional peaks in the 0–10 percent Kimura, suggesting recent TE activity, primarily involving DNA transposons families TcMar-Mariner and TcMar-Tc1. Alongside these recent bursts, older peaks associated with LINE/RTE-BovB, LTR/Gypsy and RC/Helitron were also observed, reflecting ancient proliferation events that still constitute a significant portion of their genomes (Fig. 3). Species such as M. guangxiensis, M. jiuwanshanensis, T. yunnana, and T. bambusae showed TE landscapes dominated by ancient insertions, with divergence profiles primarily influenced by LINE families CR1, RTE-BovB and LTR Gypsy and Copia elements. Notably, species with larger genome sizes, such as S. cornuta (2.836 Gb) and F. gonggashanensis (1.465 Gb), displayed broad TE divergence distributions, suggesting both ancient and more recent TE accumulation that may contribute to their expanded genome sizes (Figs. 1b and 3). In contrast, A. guangxiensis, G. guangxiensis, and T. yunnana exhibited narrow, steep divergence peaks, indicating more homogeneous TE landscapes likely resulting from a single or limited wave of proliferation. This pattern may help explain their relatively smaller genome sizes due to reduced TE accumulation over time. Overall, the TE divergence analysis reveals that a combination of ancient retrotransposon expansion and more recent DNA transposon activity has shaped genome size variation and complexity across the family Tetrigidae.

We further used the time-calibrated phylogeny inferred with BEAST2 to reconstruct the ancestral states of genome size and repeat element composition across the studied taxa, employing the phytools R package with the fastAnc and contMap functions. The color gradient in the visualization reflects trait contraction (blue) and expansion (red) across nodes. Ancestral reconstruction of RE abundance revealed higher repeat levels in lineages with larger genomes, particularly in S. cornuta (RE = 60.86%, genome size = 2.836 Gb), compared to consistently lower RE content in T. yunnana (RE = 42.82%, genome size = 1.044 Gb) (Figs. 4 and 1b). These patterns support the interpretation that genome expansion in Tetrigidae is largely driven by repeat amplification. A similar pattern was found in TE abundance, highlighting their contribution to genome structure variation across different evolutionary lineages (Fig. 4). Reconstruction of satDNA content indicated limited ancestral presence, with a pronounced increase observed only in P. moniliantennatus (15.38%), while most other lineages showed stable or reduced levels (Fig. 5a). Further analysis of repeat classes showed that LTRs, DNA transposons, and LINEs contributed most to the divergence in repeat composition, particularly in S. cornuta (Supplementary Fig. 4). In contrast, SINEs remained low and relatively consistent across taxa. The ‘Unknown’ repeat category displayed high variability, with a notable peak in M. jiuwanshanensis (Supplementary Fig. 4). These reconstructions reveal a dynamic and lineage-specific pattern of genome and repeat evolution, reflecting both deep ancestral signatures and recent amplification events that collectively contribute to genomic diversity within Tetrigidae.

Satellitome dynamics: gain, loss, and divergence of satellite repeats

The RE2 pipeline, which includes the TAREAN (Tandem Repeat Analysis) tool, was used to evaluate satellite DNA in the genomes. The Tetrigidae species exhibited a high abundance of satellite repeats, with P. moniliantennatus showing the largest proportion at 15.38% of the total genomic content, and M. guangxiensis the lowest at 2.04% (Fig. 5b). The number of satDNA families varied across species, with M. jiuwanshanensis having the highest count at 34 families, followed by G. guangxiensis with 32 families and F. gonggashanensis with 31 families, while the lowest count was observed in P. moniliantennatus with 12 families (Fig. 5c). In total, we identified 232 satellite DNA families across the studied species. Intra-specific similarity analysis using CD-HIT-EST (90% identity threshold) showed no clustering among satDNA sequences within the same genome, confirming that TAREAN-identified families are non-redundant and represent distinct satellite repeats per species. The comprehensive satDNA characterization, including monomer lengths ranging from 14 bp in XclSat16 to 7,087 bp in MguSat02, along with other parameters such as GC + AT content, abundance, and divergence, is summarized in Supplementary Table S6. The satDNA family PmoSat01 showed the highest abundance, comprising 5.16% of the total genome in P. moniliantennatus, followed by ScoSat07 at 4.08% in S. cornuta (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 6). We have found a non-significant moderate positive correlation between genome size and the number of satDNA families (Pearson correlation: r = 0.406, p > 0.05).

The overall patterns of gain, loss, and homogenization of satDNA repeats were assessed using z-score values for abundance and divergence across the Tetrigidae family, based on RepeatMasker output. Positive z-scores for abundance and negative z-scores for divergence indicated gains, whereas negative z-scores for abundance pointed to satDNA losses. Results showed that the G. guangxiensis genome experienced the highest gain, with 35 satDNA families, including notable gains in species-specific families such as GguSat04, GguSat05, and GguSat07, along with substantial amplifications of TbaSat11, FgoSat12, and AguSat04 (Fig. 5d). Similarly, M. guangxiensis, T. bambusae, X. cliva, and S. cornuta exhibited gains in 29, 26, 23, and 14 families, respectively, while A. guangxiensis, M. jiuwanshanensis, and F. gonggashanensis each showed gains in 22 satDNA families (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 5). Interestingly, P. moniliantennatus, the species with the highest satDNA abundance, showed the lowest gain across the family Tetrigidae, with only 13 satDNA families. The gains in this species were primarily limited to species-specific repeats, such as PmoSat01, PmoSat02, and PmoSat05, suggesting that certain satellite repeats are conserved and allowed to propagate within the genome, while others are gradually lost over time (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 6). Additionally, color-enhanced repeat profiles for the satDNA family TbaSat11 revealed the highest read depth coverage, considering continuous gap-free alignment, in T. bambusae, followed by G. guangxiensis, with the lowest coverage in S. cornuta (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 6).

We generated satellitome landscapes for the ten most abundant satDNA families in each selected species using output from the calcDivergenceFromAlign.pl script provided with RepeatMasker. The resulting divergence profiles showed low sequence divergence of satDNA from their consensus sequences, supporting the idea that satellite DNA is one of the most dynamic components of the repeatome. In the satellitome landscape of P. moniliantennatus, the most abundant satDNA family, PmoSat01, exhibited a recent amplification, with a concentration of repeats clustered within the 0–7% Kimura divergence range (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 7). Other families, such as PmoSat03 and PmoSat07, displayed divergence peaks between 0–1% K2P, suggesting a recent set of copies with minimal deviation from the consensus sequence. Meanwhile, PmoSat05 exhibited a double-peak pattern, with a first peak on 4% and a second at 15–17% Kimura, indicating both recent and older conservation of this repeat family. Similarly, the repeat landscape of M. guangxiensis showed that MguSat02, MguSat13, and MguSat14 exhibited divergence peaks at 0–2%, indicating the presence of recent copies. The satDNA family TbaSat11 was consistently mapped across all species, with recent copies observed in T. bambusae and G. guangxiensis, along with older peaks in F. gonggashanensis, M. jiuwanshanensis, and T. yunnana (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 7). In our study, most satDNA families clustered on the left side of the graphs, suggesting an abundance of recent copies and reinforcing the idea that satDNA are among the most evolutionarily dynamic and active components of genomic repeats.

Discussion

Repetitive elements are an important component of eukaryotic genomes, influencing genome architecture and evolution through both TEs and satellite repeats 1,21,23,43. Orthoptera is one of the most diverse insect groups, with an evolutionary history spanning over 300 million years and major diversification events during the Cretaceous and Cenozoic periods 44. These insects are characterized by highly repetitive and exceptionally large genomes 30–32. Our time-calibrated phylogeny, reconstructed using 13 mitochondrial PCGs in the Bayesian framework BEAST2, estimated the crown age of Tetrigidae to be approximately 116 million years ago in the Early Cretaceous, comparable to previous estimates 37. Similar time-calibrated phylogenies using Bayesian methods have recently been reported for other groups, including Acrididae 45 and the suborder Ensifera 46, further supporting the evolutionary timing and divergence patterns within Orthoptera. Our comparative repeatome analysis of ten Tetrigidae species revealed a considerably high proportion of REs, averaging 53.84%, with S. cornuta reaching 60.86%. We observed abundant Class I retrotransposons (LINEs, LTRs), Class II DNA transposons (Tc1, Mariner, Helitron), and a substantial proportion of satDNA, consistent with previous findings in Orthoptera, which are characterized by large, repeat-rich genomes 18,28,34,44–48. Notably, species sharing a common ancestor and more recent divergence times, such as T. yunnana, M. guangxiensis, S. cornuta, and F. gonggashanensis, exhibited broadly similar repeat landscapes, suggesting that closely related lineages tend to preserve shared RE and TE profiles (Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast, smaller clusters exhibited species-specific differences, primarily driven by satellite repeats, which are among the most rapidly evolving genomic components 1,28,49. RE accumulation can differ between sex chromosomes in heterogametic species, as shown in Drosophila, where the X and Y chromosomes exhibit noticeably different TE content 50. In Orthoptera, many Caelifera species follow an XO sex determination system, with females being XX and males XO, which may contribute to sex-specific differences in genome size and repeat composition. In our study, only female (XX) individuals were analyzed, reducing this source of variation. Although karyotype data are lacking for the studied species, recent chromosome-level assemblies in Tetrigidae consistently report a 7-chromosome karyotype, where chromosome 5 is annotated as the X chromosome 51–53, providing insights into the potential chromosomal organization of the studied species.

Genome size variation among organisms has been a long-debated question for genome biologists. Various mechanisms have been proposed as probable causes of genome size expansion in eukaryotes 54–56, including whole genome duplication (WGD) 57–59, piRNA silencing and the accumulation of repetitive content, particularly TEs 22,30,60, and satDNA 18,34. Insects exhibit a vast range of genome sizes, from the smallest (0.07 pg) in Clunio tsushimensis 61 to the largest (21.96 pg) in Bryodemella tuberculata, with Orthoptera being the only insect group known for such large genomes 31. Among assembled insect genomes, the Antarctic midge (Belgica antarctica) possesses the smallest, at 99 Mb, with less than 1% of its genome composed of TEs. In contrast, Schistocerca gregaria has one of the largest sequenced insect genomes, totaling 8.82 Gb, with repetitive elements accounting for over 62.55% of its genome 62,63. This considerable genome size variation has been attributed primarily to the expansion of repetitive elements, particularly TEs, across Orthoptera and other insects 32–34. Our findings further demonstrate that REs, including TEs, contribute substantially to the overall genome composition in Tetrigidae, with both showing strong positive correlations with genome size. (Fig. 2c). This pattern extends to major TE classes and satellite repeats, which also demonstrated moderate to strong positive correlations (Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting a significant association between repeat accumulation and genome size expansion in Orthoptera 28,30,34,44–46.

The evolutionary patterns of TE insertions are key to understand their role in genome size dynamics, as they may reflect either ancient accumulation or recent activity. Our findings indicate a combination of both ancient and ongoing TE proliferation contributing to genome expansion in Tetrigidae. Similar patterns have been reported across other Orthoptera lineages 28,44–46,64, particularly within the Acrididae family, where recent TE amplifications have been linked to substantial genome size variations throughout evolutionary history 45,62,65. Moreover, genome size evolution in eukaryotes is shaped by the balance between TE insertions and deletions, with transposition serving as a major mechanism of genome amplification 66. Retrotransposons which follow copy-and-paste pattern, particularly LINEs, LTRs, and SINEs, contribute significantly to genomic expansion and complexity 67,68. In contrast, most DNA transposons, which use a cut-and-paste mechanism, typically persist as older genomic copies and have limited contribution to ongoing genome enlargement, as seen with TcMar-Tc1, Mariner, Maverick, and Helitron elements 69.

Ancestral state reconstructions revealed phylogenetic conservation among closely related species, reflecting consistent patterns across traits such as genome size and TE expansion, both in our study and in previous investigations in Orthoptera 44–46. The similarity in RE profiles among these taxa likely reflects conservation of gene content and gene order in their genomes 64,70. Similarly, species with lower repeat content may have experienced stronger evolutionary constraints through known genome defense and TE silencing mechanisms, including piRNA pathways and DNA methylation 30,71, resulting in more compact genomes. These findings underscore the lineage-specific dynamics of repeat elements and their contribution to genome size evolution in Orthoptera.

Satellite DNA is abundantly found in Orthoptera, where it constitutes a considerable part of the genome and plays key roles in chromosomal organization, structural variation, and genome evolution 72–74, making its analysis essential for understanding genome architecture and evolutionary dynamics within this group. In the present study, satDNA abundance varied notably across species, contributing significantly to genome architecture. In this study, the highest satDNA proportion was observed in P. monilantennatus, where it comprised 15.38% of the genome. Among the 232 identified satDNA families, PmoSat01-159 alone contributed 5.61% of the genome in this species. This high satDNA diversity is consistent with previous satellitome studies in Orthoptera, which have reported similarly extensive and variable satellite repeat content 11,34,74–80. The number of satDNA families can vary widely among species, reflecting lineage-specific evolutionary dynamics. Notably, a recent study reported an exceptional 246 satDNA families in Meconema thalassinum, representing the highest number recorded in any Orthoptera species and even among all insects to date 18. This surpasses the previous maximum of 165 families identified in the beetle species Chrysolina americana 81. In our study, we also observed variation in satDNA family numbers across Tetrigidae species, with M. jiuwanshanensis showing the highest number (34 families) and P. monilantennatus the lowest (12 families) (Fig. 5c). Similar patterns have been reported in other grasshopper species, including Ruspolia dubia (104 families), Ruspolia yunnana (84 families) 18, Pyrgomorpha conica (76 families) 48, Locusta migratoria (62 families) 11, Ronderosia bergii (53 families) 82, and Xya riparia (14 families) 83. No significant correlation between the number of satDNA families and either genome size or satDNA abundance, which is consistent with previous findings 18,84.

Satellite repeats, as being the most dynamic and fast-evolving genomic component display species-specific divergence and expansion patterns 73,85. The satellitome landscapes observed in our study, generally revealed species-specific peaks, with recent burst activity at lower Kimura divergence levels (0–10%), suggesting an ongoing homogenization process due to the relatively short period of divergence 28. In our satellitome analysis, TAREAN identified several satDNA families with large monomer lengths (1.5–7.1 kb). Previous studies have shown that TEs can form tandem dimers, potentially giving rise to satellite-like structures 86. To investigate possible TE origins, we conducted BLASTn searches of all satDNA consensus sequences against the Orthoptera-TElib, a curated TE database. Fourteen families (~ 6%) showed significant similarity to known TEs, including LTR/Gypsy, LINE/L1-Tx1, DNA/hAT, and Helitron elements (Supplementary Table 7), suggesting TE-derived origins for a subset of repeats. The majority, however, showed no TE similarity, supporting their classification as true satellites. Additionally, RepeatProfiler confirmed the conserved presence of TbaSat11-2095 across species, underscoring the potential evolutionary persistence of some satDNA families and warranting further investigation. Overall, these analyses underscore the influence of REs on the evolutionary dynamics and genetic diversity within the Tetrigidae family.

Conclusions

The comparative repeatome analysis revealed high repetitive content in Tetrigidae genomes (42.82%–60.86%), dominated by LINEs, LTRs, and DNA transposons. Genome size showed strong positive correlations with both total repeat and TE content, indicating that repeat expansions, particularly of TEs, largely drive genome enlargement. TE divergence patterns suggest both ancient and recent proliferation events. Satellite DNA was highly diverse, with most families showing low divergence, consistent with recent amplification and ongoing homogenization. Fossil-calibrated phylogeny based on mitochondrial genes enabled ancestral reconstructions, revealing lineage-specific shifts in genome size and repeat content. Together, these findings offer valuable insights into the evolutionary dynamics of the Tetrigidae family, emphasizing the role of REs, especially TEs, as a major driving force behind genome expansion, and laying the groundwork for future research into grasshopper evolutionary processes and genomic complexity.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, genome size estimation and next generation sequencing

Adult specimens from ten Tetrigidae species were collected from natural populations at various locations across China. Detailed geographical information and collection dates are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The samples were kept alive until they were stored at -80 °C for further DNA extraction and NGS sequencing. The k-mer based genome size was estimated using Jellyfish v2.3.0 87 and GenomeScope v1.0 88. The k-mer coverage was counted using illumina reads in Jellyfish with options (count -C -m 21 -s 10G -t 64). Subsequently, heterozygosity, duplication rate and genome size were estimated using GenomeScope.R script.

We extracted genomic DNA from the hind leg samples of the ten selected species using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s protocols. The extracted DNA was then sonicated to produce fragments approximately 350 bp in length, which were used to prepare libraries fixed onto a microarray via bridge PCR. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, with coverage up to 50 × and paired-end read lengths of 150 bp.

Identification of repeat elements across species

The repeat identification was performed using RepeatExplorer2 (https://repeatexplorer-elixir.cerit-sc.cz/galaxy/ last accessed October 2024) 42. The unassembled paired-end Illumina reads were randomly sampled to three million reads to achieve the recommended 0.1–0.5 × coverage for RE2 (Supplementary Table 2), using SeqTK (https://github.com/lh3/seqtk/, last accessed October 2024). The sampled reads were then uploaded to the RE2 Galaxy server, where FastQC was employed to assess the quality of the reads, available under RepeatExplorer Utilities. Pre-processing was performed using the “Preprocessing of FASTQ paired-end reads” tool, which includes adapter detection, cut-adapt filtering, removal of single-end reads, quality filtering, read trimming, and interlacing the reads into a single FASTQ file. For individual analysis, interlaced FASTA files from pre-processing were used as the input dataset in RE2 pipeline with REXdb database selected to “metazoa version 3.0”, following the default protocols 42.

In the comparative clustering analysis, reads in the interlaced FASTA file were assigned a three-letter species-specific name, comprising the first letter from genus and the first two letters from species name (e.g., SCO for Saussurella cornuta), using the “FASTA read name affixer” tool in the RE2 Galaxy platform. Next, reads were sub-sampled to 500,000 for each species using the “Read sampling” tool, as required for comparative clustering analysis in the RE2 pipeline. Subsequently, all files were concatenated into a single dataset using the “Concatenate datasets” option. The comparative clustering analysis was then performed by running RE2 pipeline was ran in comparative mode with the following options: REXdb database selected as “metazoa version 3.0,” group code length as “3,” and queue as “long”, with all other settings left as default. The output files of the analysis were downloaded and manually inspected for incorrect annotations. We utilized the RepeatExplorerUtilities’ “Visualization of comparative clustering” tool to visualize the results.

TE characterization and divergence analysis

We further utilized dnaPipeTE (https://github.com/clemgoub/dnaPipeTE, last accessed October 2024) 3 with -RM_lib option to employ “Orthoptera-TElib” 89 library as a reference database for de-novo annotation of transposable elements. The quality control of Illumina paired-end reads was performed using Trimmomatic v0.39 90 with the parameters: LEADING:3, TRAILING:3, ILLUMINACLIP:TruSeq3-PE.fa:2:30:10, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20, and MINLEN:36. Then, we used only the forward-end reads for each species with approximately 0.15 × genome coverage in the dnaPipeTE software, using the following operations: singularity shell –bind ~ Project:/mnt ~ /dnaPipeTE/dnapipete.img, and python3 dnaPipeTE.py -RM_lib Orthoptera-TElib_v3.fa -output output_dir -input filtered_1.fastq.gz -genome_size < estimated_size > -sample_number 2 -genome_coverage 0.15 -cpu 16 -RM_t 0.3. The output from the dnaPipeTE pipeline was manually inspected and used for downstream analyses and visualizations. Using the RepeatMasker files included in the dnaPipeTE output, we first generated an .align file (ProcessRepeats -a Trinity.fasta.cat.gz), then calcDivergenceFromAlign.pl script was used from the RepeatMasker suite to estimate genetic divergence and construct Kimura divergence-based evolutionary landscapes for TE classes across all studied genomes.

Phylogenetic analysis and ancestral state reconstructions

Mitochondrial genomes were de novo assembled and annotated for ten selected species using MitoZ v3.6 (https://github.com/linzhi2013/MitoZ, accessed April 2025) 91. PhyloSuite v1.2.3 was used to extract 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs), and MAFFT v7.453 (L-INS-i) was employed for multiple sequence alignment. Divergence time estimation was performed in BEAST2 v2.7.7 92 under a Bayesian framework, incorporating fossil calibration points from the Paleobiology Database (PBDB) to constrain Tetrigidae node ages. The analysis implemented a relaxed molecular clock model with Yule process as tree prior, running 100 million MCMC generations and ‘log every’ option at 10,000. Convergence diagnostics were evaluated in Tracer v1.7.2, and the first 25% of MCMC samples were excluded as burn-in. Subsequently, TreeAnnotator v2.7.7 generated the maximum clade credibility (MCC) tree and which is visualized in FigTree v1.4.4. To investigate trait evolution, we reconstructed ancestral states for genome size, repeat content, and TE composition using the phytools R package. Ancestral values at each internal node were inferred using the fastAnc function under a maximum likelihood framework, and the distribution of continuous traits across the tree was visualized with the contMap function.

Satellitome analysis and repeat profiling of satDNA families

The RE2 pipeline involves TAREAN, a comprehensive platform for characterizing satellite repeats in the genome. The satDNA families consensus sequences were extracted from the RE2 output files and named according to the nomenclature, which contains the species code followed by the word “Sat” and satellite sequence number (e.g., ScoSat01) 11,13. To assess the potential TE origin of satDNA families, all consensus sequences were compared against the Orthoptera-TElib database 89 using BLASTn v2.9.0 + (evalue < 1e − 5, max_target_seqs = 1). High-confidence hits were retained using a minimum alignment identity of 80% and alignment length of at least 80 bp. Redundancy and potential homology among satDNA sequences were further examined using CD-HIT-EST v4.8.1, applying a sequence identity threshold of 90% (-c 0.9, -n 8, -d 0). We used RepeatMasker v4.1.6 (https://repeatmasker.org/, last accessed November 2024) with options -a and “RMBlast” to estimate divergence and abundance of the satDNA families in their respective genomes. A customized library containing satDNA consensus sequences from all the species was mapped against 3 million randomly sampled interlaced reads of each species using the -lib option. The “calcDivergenceFromAlign.pl” script was utilized to calculate the divergence summary, and subsequently, summary files were utilized to create satellitome landscapes.

RepeatProfiler v1.1 (https://github.com/johnssproul/RepeatProfiler, last accessed November 2024) was utilized to generate and compare DNA repetitive profiles of satDNA consensus sequences using the “-corr” option. The satellite DNA sequences FASTA file was mapped to 3 million randomly sampled reads from each species to generate satDNA repeat profiles across species. The “pre-corr” flag was used to create a “user_groups.txt” file for correlation analysis, with other settings kept as default.
