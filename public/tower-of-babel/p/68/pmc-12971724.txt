Interconverting the Joint Awareness Score and the Forgotten Joint Score: A Crosswalk Analysis
Authors: Emma N. Horton, Lauren K. Holbrook, David F. Scott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient awareness of their artificial joint is a crucial component of satisfaction after total joint arthroplasty. The Forgotten Joint Score (FJS) is widely employed to evaluate this phenomenon; however, English-speaking patients may struggle to distinguish the answer choices—“never,” “almost never,” “seldom,” “sometimes,” and “mostly.” The 6-question Joint Awareness Score (JAS) demonstrates proof-of-concept as a streamlined alternative to the 12-question FJS in total hip and knee arthroplasty patients. We hypothesized that the JAS would show acceptable internal consistency, correlate strongly with the FJS, and yield a near-linear crosswalk.MethodsTo facilitate cross-comparisons and data pooling between the 2 measures, we performed a crosswalk analysis between the JAS and the FJS. The study included patients undergoing primary total hip and knee arthroplasty who completed both the FJS and JAS at their preoperative, 6-week, 6-month, and 1-year postoperative visits.ResultsA total of 684 patients participated in the study, including 424 knees and 260 hips. The internal consistency of the JAS, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha, ranged from 0.76 to 0.89. The Pearson correlation coefficient between the JAS and FJS ranged from 0.75 to 0.88 across all time points. Crosswalk analyses demonstrated a near-linear relationship between the FJS and JAS with Spearman correlation coefficients ranging from 0.88 to 0.90.ConclusionsOur results demonstrate a nearly direct conversion between the FJS and the JAS. The JAS is a shorter and more user-friendly alternative to the FJS for American English-speaking patients.

Introduction

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are essential in orthopaedic research, providing valuable insights into treatment efficacy and patient satisfaction [[1], [2], [3]]. Among these, the Forgotten Joint Score (FJS) was specifically developed to address the ceiling effects commonly seen in earlier PROMs [4]. Ceiling effects occur when more than 15% of patients score at the maximum possible value, limiting a tool's ability to detect subtle differences among high-performing individuals [[5], [6], [7]]. This is a growing concern given the increasing demand for total joint arthroplasty in younger and more active patients and improvements in surgical techniques and implants resulting in a higher proportion of maximum scores.

Along with addressing ceiling effects, the FJS was designed to quantify a patient's awareness of their artificial joint—a crucial component of satisfaction and quality of life after total joint arthroplasty [4,8]. The FJS, initially developed in Norwegian, is now used worldwide and has been translated and validated in numerous languages [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. However, the FJS has only been validated in British English and has not been validated in American English [18,19]. While utilizing the FJS in our American English-speaking joint replacement center, we observed that the FJS has several limitations. Our patients struggle to distinguish the answer choices—“never,” “almost never,” “seldom,” “sometimes,” and “mostly”—in particular, the difference in meaning between the middle 3 answer options. The semantics of the translation of the FJS to English is problematic in this regard. Another limitation is that the FJS commonly delivers “no score” when a patient answers 3 or more questions as “N/A,” which can be particularly common at the preoperative or early postoperative visits due to limited joint function. Additionally, the FJS instructions specifically refer to a prosthetic joint rather than the joint in general, which complicates the assessment of preoperative baselines.

To address these limitations, we developed the 6-question Joint Awareness Score (JAS) as a streamlined alternative to the 12-question FJS. The JAS features simplified language and fewer response options while maintaining the FJS’s core concept of measuring joint awareness. A pilot study of 174 total knee arthroplasty (TKA) patients demonstrated the JAS's initial effectiveness [20]. With crosswalk analyses becoming increasingly popular for interconverting PROMs, we conducted a crosswalk analysis between the JAS and FJS for both TKA and total hip arthroplasty (THA) patients to further validate the new PROM [21]. We hypothesized that, in THA and TKA patients, the JAS would demonstrate acceptable internal consistency and a strong correlation with the FJS, and that equipercentile linking would allow near-direct score conversion between measures.

Study design

The WCG Institutional Review Board approved the prospective collection of clinical outcomes data as part of an institutional hip and knee registry. The present study included all consenting patients from our registry study undergoing primary TKA or THA between April 2020 and October 2024. The study included 684 participants from the hip and knee registries, comprising 52.8% men (361/684) and 47.2% women (327/684). Participant ages ranged from 28 to 91 years, with a mean age of 67.1 years. Data analyses

For correlation analyses, a 2-sided power calculation (α = 0.05, 80% power) indicated that approximately 84 patients would be required to detect a conservative correlation of r = 0.30; the final sample (n = 684) exceeded this threshold. We evaluated sample adequacy in terms of precision and crosswalk error rather than relying solely on a classical power framework. Using a binomial approximation for the sampling variability of empirical percentile ranks, the standard error across central percentiles (P = .25–.75) was approximately 0.017–0.019; at P = .50, the corresponding 95% confidence interval was 0.463–0.537, reflecting a precision of approximately ±3.7 percentile points. Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA). Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to assess the internal consistency of the FJS and JAS preoperatively and 6 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively. Values of Cronbach’s alpha greater than 0.70 were considered acceptable, indicating good reliability based on established literature [23,24]. Ceiling and floor effects were assessed for the FJS and JAS across all time points. The ceiling effect was defined as the percentage of participants achieving the highest possible score, while the floor effect was defined as the percentage of participants achieving the lowest possible score [25]. Construct validity was evaluated using the Pearson correlation coefficient across all time points. Confidence intervals (95% CI) for the Pearson correlation coefficients were also computed. Content validity was assumed because the analysis is based on the widely adopted FJS.

The crosswalk analysis was performed in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, http://www.R-project.org/). A crosswalk between the FJS and JAS was constructed using pooled scores from preoperative and 6-week, 6-month, and 1-year postoperative visits. The equipercentile equating method was used to establish bidirectional mappings between the scales (FJS to JAS and JAS to FJS) with set maximum and minimum boundaries to ensure predicted scores remained within the valid scale range. The crosswalk was developed for TKA and THA individually, as well as for the combined TKA and THA cohorts.

Validation of the crosswalk addressed 2 objectives. The first objective was to assess bias by comparing crosswalk-derived scores for inputted predicted values with the actual population averages. Input scores were divided into 13 bins. For each bin, the mean actual outcome was compared to the corresponding crosswalk-predicted outcome. The mean absolute deviation was calculated to quantify the bias introduced by the crosswalk. The second objective was to evaluate the strength of association between crosswalk-derived scores and actual individual outcomes. Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to assess this relationship, accounting for bidirectionality across all cohorts.

TKA-only subsample

Cronbach’s alpha for the FJS was 0.93 preoperatively, 0.97 at 6 months, and 0.96 at 1 year postoperative. For the JAS, it was 0.76 preoperatively, 0.89 at 6 months, and 0.87 at 1 year postoperative. The Pearson correlation between the FJS and JAS was 0.75 (95% CI: 0.70-0.79) preoperatively, 0.88 (95% CI: 0.85-0.90) at 6 months, and 0.85 (95% CI: 0.81-0.88) at 1 year postoperative (Table 5).

Ceiling effects for the FJS were 0.3% preoperatively, 3.9% at 6 months, and 9.5% at 1 year postoperative, with floor effects of 17.1%, 2.5%, and 0.9%, respectively. The JAS ceiling effects were 0.2% preoperatively, 7.0% at 6 months, and 15.8% at 1 year postoperative, with floor effects of 35.8%, 10.6%, and 3.0%, respectively (Table 6).

THA-only subsample

For the FJS among the THA-only subsample, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.95 preoperatively, 0.97 at 6 months, and 0.98 at 1 year postoperative. For the JAS, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.77 preoperatively, 0.87 at 6 months, and 0.87 at 1 year postoperative. Pearson correlation coefficients between the FJS and JAS were 0.80 (95% CI: 0.75-0.84) preoperatively, 0.86 (95% CI: 0.81-0.90) at 6 months, and 0.79 (95% CI: 0.71-0.84) at 1 year postoperative (Table 5).

The FJS demonstrated ceiling effects of 0.4% preoperatively, 10.8% at 6 months, and 21.5% at 1 year postoperative, with floor effects of 32.0%, 1.9%, and 0.7%, respectively. The ceiling effect for the JAS was 0.4% preoperatively, 21.9% at 6 months and 29.9% at 1 year postoperative, while the floor effect was 43.8%, 3.9%, and 0.0% at the respective visits (Table 6).

Discussion

The present study builds upon the promising findings from an initial pilot study, which evaluated the JAS as a viable alternative to the FJS in a cohort of 174 primary TKA patients [20]. Increasing the sample size with the inclusion of both primary THA and TKA patients strengthened the evidence supporting the JAS as an effective alternative to the FJS. Furthermore, the addition of a validated crosswalk with strong correlation coefficients indicates that the JAS and FJS are nearly analogous in this patient population.

Crosswalks facilitate the conversion of scores from one PROM to another through an algorithmic process [21]; however, there is not a standardized methodology to achieve a crosswalk. A recent systematic review by Ackerman et al. identified 35 crosswalks that effectively converted PROM scores in the context of joint arthroplasties using a variety of methodologies, including item response theory (IRT), linear equating, and equipercentile [21]. IRT assumes unidimensionality—that a single latent trait is being measured by the instrument [26,27]. Yet the concept of joint awareness is complex, and a patient’s score may be influenced by a combination of traits, such as pain, stiffness, and function [4,20,28,29]. This complexity rendered the IRT methodology inappropriate for the present study. In comparison, linear equating models assume a linear relationship between scores, which can lead to scores extending beyond the original scale range [30]. These models are ill-suited for finite outcome measures like the JAS and FJS. Given these limitations, the present study opted for the equipercentile method, which is better suited for preserving the integrity and finite range of scores for multidimensional surveys such as the FJS and JAS. While there is no standardized crosswalk method, the systematic review by Ackerman et al. identified 3 hip/knee arthroplasty PROM crosswalks that performed equipercentile analyses along with other crosswalk methods [21]. These studies favored the equipercentile approach because it demonstrated less systematic bias and provided the closest approximation of predicted to observed scores [21,[31], [32], [33]]. The equipercentile approach also maintains consistency with previous crosswalks for joint arthroplasty PROMs [[34], [35], [36], [37], [38]].

Beyond methodological considerations, the practical implementation of PROMs presents additional challenges. The collection of PROMs can be hindered by respondent burden and fatigue, which arises when patients find participation challenging, time-consuming, or emotionally taxing [39]. To ensure accurate data collection and minimize survey burnout, PROMs must be concise, user-friendly, and written in accessible language [40]. The JAS addresses these needs. The simplified response options—“never/rarely,” “sometimes,” and “always/frequently”—circumvent the potential for patient confusion inherent in the FJS’s 5-point Likert scale. The 6-question JAS survey improves interpretability and reduces cognitive burden for patients compared to the longer 12-question FJS, while still achieving the goal of evaluating the important concept of “forgetting” one's joint. Additionally, the JAS’s scoring algorithm generates a complete score even when patients find certain questions nonapplicable, addressing a fundamental limitation of the FJS where such cases can result in a “no score.” This feature is particularly beneficial during preoperative or early postoperative visits, where functional limitations are common. Furthermore, the JAS refers to the “joint” in general terms rather than specifying an “artificial joint” as the FJS does, making it more versatile for both preoperative and postoperative assessment.

Another distinction between the JAS and FJS is the selection of activities used to assess joint awareness. The JAS takes a more targeted approach, asking patients specifically about their joint awareness during discrete, high-demand functional tasks. For example, the JAS inquiries about joint awareness when getting in and out of a vehicle—a pivotal motion that can reveal limitations in joint performance. In contrast, the FJS uses a more general prompt, asking about joint awareness while “traveling in a vehicle.” This broader wording could encompass the high-demand actions of entering and exiting the vehicle but may also capture the relatively lower demands of sitting inside. By focusing on the specific functional task of transitioning in and out of a vehicle, the JAS question is designed to better distinguish between satisfactory joint performance and self-reported limitations. This tailored approach is intended to enhance the JAS's ability to detect subtle differences in joint awareness, particularly among patients with higher physical functioning. However, this specificity may limit the applicability of the JAS to patients with reduced physical function or those engaged in less physically demanding activities. Thus, the JAS may be better suited for patients with higher functional demands. With the outcomes of total joint arthroplasties continuing to improve and patients undergoing these procedures at increasingly younger ages and with higher physical demands, there is a need for such an instrument [[41], [42], [43]].

The findings of the present study may be influenced by environmental bias, as this single-institution study involved surgeries performed by one surgeon in a relatively homogenous patient population. Since all TKA patients underwent unrestricted kinematic alignment and all THA patients underwent a direct anterior approach, the generalizability of our results to other surgical techniques may be limited. External validation across diverse clinical populations will be necessary. Future multicenter studies across geographic borders will be crucial to validate the JAS and its crosswalk to the FJS before widespread adoption.

Another potential limitation of the present study is survey fatigue because patients completed both the JAS and FJS sequentially at each assessment time point along with other PROMs. This raises concerns about potential errors in test reliability. However, Thompson et al. demonstrated nearly perfect test-retest reliability for the English version of the FJS in Australia, with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.97, and similar strong reliability has been reported in studies using other translated versions of the FJS, suggesting that the simultaneous completion of these surveys is likely feasible without compromising the quality of the data [[13], [14], [15],44].

Furthermore, the reduction in the number of questions and answer choices in the JAS compared to the FJS (6 vs 12), while improving efficiency, may sacrifice the collection of valuable information. This simplification likely contributed to the JAS producing a higher proportion of patients reaching both ceiling and floor effects at almost every time point, which may undermine the survey’s ability to detect meaningful differences within the population [45]. However, the increase in the ceiling and floor effects compared to the FJS may be counterbalanced by the decrease in survey fatigue that is likely produced by halving the number of questions. The concise 6-item questionnaire makes the JAS notably shorter than other traditional joint arthroplasty PROMs, potentially leading to improved response rates while reducing patient burden [46]. Research staff should carefully consider these trade-offs when selecting outcome measures for their studies.

Conclusions

The JAS is an improved and shortened alternative to the FJS for evaluating joint awareness, validated for use in an American English-speaking hip and knee arthroplasty population. There is a direct interconversion between the 2 scores, allowing use of the JAS in place of the FJS without loss of continuity in longitudinal data collection. However, multicenter studies are necessary to ensure validity across diverse patient populations before implementation of broader clinical use.
