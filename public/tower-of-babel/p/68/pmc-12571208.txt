Fe3⁺‐Coordinated Chitosan‐Hydrogel Networks for Highly Deformable and Enhanced Performance Triboelectric Nanogenerators
Authors: Hai Anh Thi Le, Thien Trung Luu, Habtamu Gebeyehu Menge, Dukhyun Choi, Yong Tae Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTriboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) have attracted a lot of attention recently because they can power wearable electronics and small energy‐harvesting devices. However, the use of rigid materials has limited applicability in TENGs, particularly in flexible electronic devices. Hence, to address this challenge, flexible materials are gaining interest as an alternative to conventional rigid materials. This work introduces a hydrogel‐based TENG that is easy to fabricate, offers high production efficiency, superior mechanical flexibility, and self‐healing capabilities. The incorporation of Fe3+ ions into a crosslinked network of chitosan (CHI) and polyacrylic acid (PAA) macromolecular chains significantly enhances the hydrogel's conductivity and the TENG's output performance. Under a 2 N load, a single‐electrode mode of hydrogel‐TENG (H‐TENG) produced output voltage and current of 150 V and 6 µA, respectively. Moreover, the hydrogel retains its good mechanical stretchability (250%), good electrical conductivity, strong adhesion, and robust self‐healing ability. After just 5 min of self‐healing, this H‐TENG device also exhibited effective and sustained self‐healing performance of the electrical output signal. Due to its high output voltage, stretchability, self‐healing property, and sensitivity to mechanical stimuli, this H‐TENG holds great promise as a wearable energy harvesting device and a highly accurate self‐powered body motion sensor.Keywords: chitosan, deformation, Fe3+ networks, hydrogel, self‐healing, triboelectric nanogenerators

Introduction

The triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG), first described by Wang et al. in 2012,[ 1 ] is a device that uses the triboelectric effect and electrostatic induction to transform mechanical energy into electrical energy.[ 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] In an era of energy crisis, which relies too much on depleting fossil fuels, TENG represents a source of green energy offering the advantages of low cost, environmental friendliness, and high efficiency.[ 7 , 8 ] TENG's holds remarkable potential of in powering next‐generation technologies such as artificial intelligence, the internet of things, and wearable sensor electronics.[ 9 , 10 , 11 ] Besides, the demand for wearable electronic devices has increased rapidly along with growing interest in biomedical signal monitoring, tactile sensing, and human body motion detection.[ 12 ]

TENGs have been widely utilized in wearable technology owing to their exceptional qualities like high output efficiency, stretchability, biocompatibility, and healing capacity.[ 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ] The integration of TENGs in wearable electronics has driven efforts to overcome the mechanical restrictions of conventional TENGs. Traditional TENGs typically employ electrodes comprised of stiff materials, which have a limited tolerance for deformation.[ 18 ] To develop flexible TENG devices, flexible materials have been created to replace standard hard electrodes, including conductive paper, silver colloids, carbon nanotubes, and commercial fabrics.[ 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 ] Among these alternatives, hydrogels have emerged as promising candidates to replace of traditional electrodes.[ 24 , 25 , 26 ] Hydrogels are soft, water‐rich materials that exhibit high flexibility, high elasticity, strong biocompatibility, high electrical conductivity, and especially self‐healing ability. TENG using hydrogel shows great potential in harvesting energy from body movement, sensing, and receiving small external stimuli.[ 27 ] Besides, when using hydrogel, TENG will operate in single‐electrode mode with many accompanying benefits, such as a compact, flexible design, which can be seamlessly integrated into electronic circuits quickly, allowing energy collection from motion without considering electrode shape and position.[ 28 ] Many studies have been conducted to integrate various salt solutions into hydrogel networks. However, hydrogels are composed of numerous complex components. Furthermore, the relationship between chitosan (CHI) and polyacrylic acid (PAA) remains limited, and the salt solution does not serve as a dynamic coordination bond. The mechanical properties and performance of hydrogels interconnected by saline solutions remain challenging.[ 29 , 30 , 31 ] Compared to divalent cations such as Ca2⁺ or Zn2⁺, Fe3⁺ offers distinct advantages in hydrogel systems owing to its higher charge density and stronger ligand‐binding affinity. The trivalent nature of Fe3⁺ enables the formation of multiple coordination sites per ion, which increases the effective crosslinking density within the polymer network. This leads to enhanced mechanical strength, improved toughness, and more efficient stress dissipation under deformation. Moreover, the strong electrostatic interactions between Fe3⁺ and polar functional groups accelerate network recovery after mechanical damage, thereby promoting rapid self‐healing. From an electrical perspective, the high valence state of Fe3⁺ contributes to increased ionic conductivity, facilitating faster charge transport and boosting triboelectric output. Literature reports have shown that Fe3⁺‐coordinated hydrogels exhibit superior stretchability, resilience, and conductivity compared to analogous systems incorporating Ca2⁺ or Zn2⁺.[ 32 , 33 ] These combined attributes make Fe3⁺ an ideal choice for designing high‐performance, robust hydrogels for wearable TENG applications.

In this study, we present a stretchable and flexible TENG, whose electrode layer is a multifunctional hydrogel made out of CHI, PAA, and incorporated Fe3+ ions by a simple casting method, and the Fe3+ is well used as a dynamic metal‐ligand coordinator to reinforce the hydrogel network, and shows no evident cytotoxicity toward human.[ 31 , 34 ] Tailoring CHI into the PAA‐Fe3+ ionic crosslinking network facilitated the hydrogel formation. The exceptional conductivity of the hydrogel and strain sensitivity are due to the presence of Fe3+ ions, which also boost the TENG device's output efficiency. The hydrogel's mechanical characteristics were sustained both before and after self‐healing, and the output voltage was recovered after 5 min (45%) and after a day (67%). These TENGs have also been demonstrated to function as energy receivers and sensors to identify minute human movements as well as text‐like tactile sensations.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of CHI‐PAA and CHI‐PAA‐Fe Hydrogel

The synthetic strategy of CHI‐PAA‐Fe3+ multifunctional hydrogel is illustrated in Figure 1a. Multiple linkages involved in the network endow the hydrogel with self‐healing properties and excellent mechanical resilience. The ionic cross‐linking, hydrogen bonding, and coordination bonding of Fe3+ ions lead to the formation of a double‐network structure. When Fe3+ ions are introduced, the color of the resulting CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel changes to orange, which was originally translucent white. The interaction between the CHI‐PAA and CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogels' structural components was ascertained using the results of both the FT‐IR and UV–vis spectroscopy. Figure 1b represents the FT‐IR spectra of the CHI, PAA, CHI‐PAA, and CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogels. The characteristic absorption spectra of CHI are observed at 3400 cm−1 (─OH stretching) and the and at 1600 cm−1 (C═O stretching of amide group), while, pure PAA exhibits absorption peaks at 3400, 1700, 1300 cm−1 corresponding to the stretching vibrations of ─OH and C═O in pure PAA.[ 34 , 35 ] When the picks were moved to 1600–1700 cm−1 on the spectrum of CHI‐PAA and CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogels, it proved that NH2 and NH3 + were present in the hydrogels' structural makeup.[ 36 , 37 ] The absence of the carboxyl group's elongation peak at ≈1700 cm−1 in the PAA spectrum of the hydrogels can be attributed to the development of ionic interactions between the carboxyl groups of AA and Fe3+. Furthermore, the hydroxyl group (OH−) ≈3600–3300 cm−1 in PAA‐CHI is greatly diminished. The connectivity between PAA and CHI is stated to be limited. In the presence of Fe3+, the hydrogel network of PAA and CHI was significantly reinforced by dynamic coordination bonds, as illustrated in Figure 1a. Increased hydrogen bonding in hydrogels enhances crosslinking, resulting in improved mechanical characteristics and self‐healing capabilities.[ 38 , 39 ] Furthermore, in Figure 1c the UV–vis spectra of the CHI‐PAA and CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogels are contrasted. There were no peaks between 300 and 400 nm for the CHI‐PAA hydrogel. Strong peaks were found at 370 nm after incorporation of Fe3+ ions into the CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel. Additionally, based on the outcomes of Figure 1d, the hydrogels demonstrated their transparency. Furthermore, as clearly shown in the photograph, the addition of Fe3+ decreased the transparency of the hydrogel. As shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information), the presence of Fe ions was confirmed by SEM, elemental mapping, and digital imaging of the CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel.

Mechanical Properties of CHI‐PAA and CHI‐PAA‐Fe Hydrogels

The hydrogels' mechanical characteristics were next investigated and shown in Figure 2 . Figure 2a‐i depicts the hydrogel's recovery phases. The hydrogel was cut into two pieces. At the point of contact of the two pieces, apply little water, subsequently, the two pieces are put in contact with each other and held for 1 min. The hydrogels were then kept in sealed bags for 1 day, and the characteristics of both original and healed hydrogels were then examined. These hydrogels also exhibited sensitive conductivity when Fe3+ ions were added to the hydrogel network. Figure 2a‐ii and Figure S2 (Supporting Information) illustrate the results of CHI‐PAA‐Fe and CHI‐PAA hydrogel. Comparison of CHI‐PAA hydrogel in the absence and presence of Fe3+ ions indicated that strong conductivity and brightened LEDs were obtained from the hydrogel only in the presence of Fe3+ ions. Notably, the electrical conductivity of the CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel was retained even after self‐healing. This demonstrates that the hydrogel can also act as a flexible sensor to directly monitor different movements when combining the two properties of this hydrogel's good elasticity and electrical conductivity. The stress‐strain curves of the original and healed CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogels are depicted in Figure 2b. The results show that the elongation capacity of the hydrogel samples is almost preserved after healing. Figure 2c is a stretch image of the CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel healing point, where the joint has almost completely healed after 1 day. These findings highlight the remarkable self‐healing ability of CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel, as well as its ability to retain elongation properties. The formation and tightness of hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, and coordination bonds in the hydrogel network formed by the PAA, CHI chain, and Fe3+ ions have also been proposed to explain this self‐healing ability. Based on the outstanding conductivity, the strain‐sensing ability of CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel was also investigated and demonstrated in Figure 2d. The hydrogel is designed to adhere to the finger, as shown in Figure 2d‐ii, and when the finger bends repeatedly, the sensor's ΔR/Ro is synchronized to the movements in a stable and fast manner (Figure 2d‐i). The strain sensing was also preserved with the CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel after being healed (Figure S3, Supporting Information). With these results in mind, this simple hydrogel preparation strategy will result in a multifunctional H‐TENG device for harvesting energy from body movement, sensing, and receiving small external stimulation.

Performance Analysis of CHI‐PAA and CHI‐PAA‐Fe Hydrogel

Figure 3a depicts the H‐TENG operating principle in longitudinal contact friction mode, with the PDMS serving as the electronegative material and the CHI‐PAA‐Fe Hydrogel acting independently as both electrodes and a positive triboelectric layer with a surface area of 2 × 2 cm2. When the PDMS comes into contact with the hydrogel layer, electrons are transferred from the hydrogel layer's surface to the PDMS, causing the hydrogel layer's surface to be positively charged and the PDMS membrane to be negatively charged. During contact release, the two layers of hydrogel and PDMS of opposite charge separate, creating an electron flow in the external circuit. When the two layers come in contact and the electric field returns to zero, the direction of electron flow will be reversed. Figure 3b,c depict the output performance of the TENG device when using different electrode layers, namely traditional Al electrode, CHI‐PAA, and CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogels with 2 N compressive force. The results show that the output voltage and current values of H‐TENG using CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel as an electrode (150 V and 6 µA, respectively) are significantly higher than those of CHI‐PAA hydrogel (20 V, 0.2 µA) and aluminum electrode (30 V, 0.3 µA). We also tested the transferred charge of these samples, which shows the same trend, reaching more than 15 nC for CHI‐PAA‐Fe (Figure S4, Supporting Information). When Fe3+ ions are added, the hydrogel becomes electrically conductive and acts as an electrode layer in H‐TENG. In contrast, the CHI‐PAA hydrogel is not electrically conductive and exhibits high resistance (Figure S5a, Supporting Information). The CHI‐PAA hydrogel, which operates independently without an electrode layer, did not produce high voltage values even when in contact with the PDMS layer. At 2 N load and 5 Hz frequency, the CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel demonstrated outstanding high output performance, with peak voltage and current values of 150 V and 6 µA, respectively. The H‐TENG demonstrated higher mechanical stability and durability compared to the optimum hydrogel, as verified by an endurance test. The H‐TENG endurance was evaluated via 10 000 contact‐separation cycles at a force of 2 N and a frequency of 4 Hz (Figure S6, Supporting Information). Figure 3d shows that the open‐circuit voltage rises with increasing input force from 2 to 5 N and peaks at 5 and 10 N. It can be seen that the CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel can give high output efficiency when the applied load is a small value. The H‐TENG is tested for its capacity to charge at the same operating frequency using capacitors of 1, 10, 22, and 47 µF, each demonstrating a brisk charging rate (Figure S7, Supporting Information). And 32 bulbs were successfully illuminated with a contact area of just 2 cm2 when H‐TENGs were linked to the LED system in series to verify the energy receiver's functionality (Figure 3e; Video S1, Supporting Information). With the output performance results of the above H‐TENGs, it is shown that the CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel can act as an independent electrode layer.

a) H‐TENG design layout and the mechanism of triboelectric generation; b,c) Triboelectric output performances of the S‐TENG using CHI‐PAA hydrogel, CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel, and aluminum as an electrode; d) Voltage of CHI‐PAA‐Fe/H‐TENG with the area of 4 cm2 under different applied force; e) Photograph of 32 colorful LEDs lightened by hand tapping a 2 cm2–sized H‐TENG.

The remarkable output efficiency of the CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel can be seen when operating as an electrode in the H‐TENG system. Besides, we also tested the ability to recover the output performance of this H‐TENG system before and after the self‐healing process. The results are illustrated in Figure 4 , after 5 min of healing, the output voltage and current were recovered by 40% and 67%, respectively. And after 1 day, the recovery rate for voltage was 67% and for current was 83%. Moreover, the transferred charge of H‐TENG was measured after the self‐healing time, and it was confirmed that the transferred charge results were also effectively recovered after healing (Figure S8, Supporting Information). The conductivity measurements in Figure S5b (Supporting Information) also confirm the results well. Compared with the intact recovery of the tensile properties in Figure 2b, the output voltage efficiency of H‐TENG using the CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel is not 100% restored, besides that, the healing and recovery rate is not 100% but fast output performance recovery along with high voltage value at low load (≈100 V at 2 N) is also the highlight of this hydrogel. Furthermore, the long‐term hydration retention of hydrogel is also one of the important factors. Figure S9 (Supporting Information) shows that the output performance remains consistent without any noticeable degradation even after 21 days of storage.

Applications

Application for Self‐Powered Sensors

To enhance the service life, working efficiency, and minimize the dehydration of the hydrogel, H‐TENG was developed by wrapping the CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel between two PDMS membranes of negative voltage and good elasticity, as depicted in Figure 5a. The working mechanism of this TENG system is depicted in Figure 5b. Here, the H‐TENG is designed to operate in single‐electrode mode, which is more optimized for practical applications, and the CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel acts as a conductor.[ 40 , 41 ] When the PDMS surface is in contact with a triboelectric positive layer (e.g., human skin, metals, etc.), opposite charges are generated due to the electrification effect between these two contact layers. When the two layers are separated, positive charges are generated in the CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel electrode layer due to the inductive effect of the negative charges on the PDMS, resulting in current to flow through the electrode and downward. When the two layers come into contact again, electrons flow back from the ground to the electrode, and an alternating current is generated by repeating this cycle of contact and separation.

Sensing performance of the stretchable H‐TENG. a) Illustration of the hydrogel‐based TENG fabrication procedure; b) Working principle of the TENG in single‐electrode mode; c–e) Triboelectric output voltage of the sensor generated by finger bending c), elbow bending d), and airflow blowing onto the sensor e).

Based on two common applications, self‐powered sensors and energy harvesting devices, we present the diverse applications of H‐TENGs. First, H‐TENG sensors can detect movement in various parts of the human body. The H‐TENG sensor is designed to be attached to the fingers and elbows, and the voltage produced when bending the knuckles and elbows ranges from 0.5 to 1.5 V (Figure 5c,d). The received signals demonstrate the fast and stable sensing performance of the H‐TENG device. Furthermore, the H‐TENG exhibits high reliability in micro‐impact detection, such as respiratory rate monitoring. Continuous breathing created airflow pressure on the surface of the H‐TENG device, and Figure 5e shows that the H‐TENG detected this breathing by obtaining an output voltage in the range of 0.2–0.6 V during each breath. In addition to the strain and pressure sensors for body movement, we also tested the ability of the handwriting recognition sensor in the self‐powered H‐TENG device. Figure 6a is an illustration of the H‐TENG used for writing and a practical testing process. Handwriting was recorded on the surface of the H‐TENG device, and various strokes and letters generated voltage signals with different peaks and values depending on the pressure of the pen tip on each stroke. The output voltage was repeated corresponding to a specific phrase such as “MJU,” as shown in Figure 6b,c. To test the repeatability of the signal voltage, force, and stroke were controlled, and the output voltage signal was confirmed to be identical with no significant differences. The above results demonstrate the fast and sensitive recognition ability along with the high recognition ability of the self‐powered H‐TENG sensor device, showing good potential in the applications of wearable sensor electronics in the future.

H‐TENG for sensing various handwritten contents. a) Illustration of handwriting on the surface of H‐TENG. b,c) Voltage signals capable of detecting various handwritings. The same alphabet produced similar voltage signals.

Application for Energy Harvesting devices

In addition to providing sensing applications and capturing biomechanical energy from the deformation of body movements, the H‐TENG also showed the ability to capture energy from external applications. Figure 7a,b shows the voltage output results obtained when force was applied to a computer keyboard and a mobile phone screen (Videos S2 and S3, Supporting Information). The human finger acts as a positive triboelectric layer. When force was applied to the H‐TENG, an electric friction effect occurred, and the force from the finger acting is reflected in the magnitude of the friction voltage. For the applications on the keyboard and on the phone screen, the voltage values obtained were 40−100 V and 20−25 V, respectively, depending on the different forces acting on the H‐TENG. The resulting voltage and current from the H‐TENG devices were sufficient to light up LED bulbs. These findings demonstrate that the H‐TENG using CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel is flexible, has a simple structure, and is suitable for a wide range of applications. Overall, this device shows significant potential for the application in wearable and self‐powered flexible electronic devices.

Energy harvesting efficiency of H‐TENG. Voltage values generated when the triboelectric touch effect was applied to the H‐TENG surface. a) H‐TENG attached to the surface of a computer keyboard; b) attached to the surface of a mobile phone.

Conclusion

In summary, the addition of Fe3+ ions into the hydrogel matrix has resulted in CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogel layer with outstanding properties such as electrical conductivity, good elasticity, rapid deformation sensing, and self‐healing. This hydrogel effectively functions as a stand‐alone electrode in the TENG device. H‐TENG with size 2 × 2 cm2 in vertical contact mode with PDMS negative voltage layer generated a maximum voltage of 200 V and current of 6 µA at 5 N and 5 Hz loads. Especially at a low load of 2 N, H‐TENG provided a voltage up to 150 V and a current of 6 µA. Along with self‐healing, the mechanical properties and output efficiency of the TENG device are also restored. To enhance durability, expand the range of applications, and limit the dehydration of the hydrogel, the flexible H‐TENG device has been designed with a hydrogel layer structure sandwiched between two layers of elastic PDMS. H‐TENG works as a flexible sensor device that can detect body movements such as finger and elbow flexion, monitor breathing, and even recognize handwriting strokes on the H‐TENG surface. With that, H‐TENG can easily derive energy from those deformations and movements. The results also indicate a variety of applications that capture energy from the mechanical impact of typing and touching, such as when the H‐TENG device is attached to the keypads and mobile phone screens. Therefore, this H‐TENG device represents a potent candidate with a wide range of applications and is an excellent choice for wearable self‐powered sensors and flexible energy harvesters.

Experimental Section

Materials

Chitosan (CHI, degree of deacetylation = 80%), poly(acrylic acid) (PAA, M W = 100,000 g/mol, 35 wt.% aqueous solution), Iron (III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3 6H2O, 97%) were used as received from Sigma‐Aldrich.

Preparation of CHI‐PAA and CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogels

The CHI‐PAA and CHI‐ PAA‐Fe hydrogels were prepared by radical polymerization. Initially, the CHI solution was prepared by dissolving 0.25 g CHI in 10 mL of deionized DI water (1% CH3COOH) and stirring to obtain a homogeneous solution. Then, 1 mL of PAA (35 wt.%) was added to the CHI solution, and stirring was continued. After 30 min, 1 mL of FeCl3 solution was added with different concentrations (0 and 5 wt.%). The solution was continuously stirred until the mixture became homogeneous. The solution is then sonicated to remove air bubbles generated during stirring. The mixed solution was introduced into the mold and reacted at 60 °C for 8 h to form the CHI‐PAA and CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogels. The obtained CHI‐PAA and CHI‐PAA‐Fe hydrogels were stored in sealed bags.

Self‐Healing Performance

The hydrogel samples were cut from the middle. And apply a small amount of water to the cut of the two hydrogel pieces, then bring the two pieces into contact, hold for 1 min, and place in a plastic bag for 24 h at room temperature. The self‐healing efficiency of the hydrogels, as verified by the tensile strength method and measuring the electrical signal, was compared with the original hydrogel.

Fabrication of H‐TENG

H‐TENG is composed of PDMS at the top and bottom layers and the hydrogel layer (1 × 4 cm2) is sandwiched between two layers of PDMS (2 × 6 cm2) to prevent the dehydration of the hydrogel. A copper wire was used as the electrical conductor, the H‐TENG is then vacuum‐pumped to remove all air bubbles.

Characterizations and Measurements

Fourier transform infrared (FT‐IR) spectra were used to determine the characteristic interactions between CHI, PAA, and Fe3+ ions. FT‐IR measurements were recorded in an FT‐IR spectrophotometer (NICOLETiS5, Thermo Scientific). The light absorbance of the samples was measured with a UV–Vis–NIR spectrometer (DH‐2000‐BAL, Oceans Optics). The hydrogels were cut into 5 mm dumbbell shapes. Stretching speed was set to 60 mm min−1. A universal testing machine (Autograph AGS‐X, Shimadzu) was used to measure the mechanical properties of the hydrogel. Each sample was tested 5 times to get the results. Sheet resistance was measured using a four‐point probe (Pro4, Signatone) with a 0.4 mm probe tip diameter and 1.0 mm tip spacing. To measure the electrical signal, voltage, and current for the TENG were measured using a digital waveform (MDO3052, Tektronix), a low‐noise current preamplifier (SR570, Stanford Research System), and a galvanometer (system galvanometer 6514, Keithley) with an input impedance of 40 MΩ. The resistance characteristics of the developed hydrogel were measured using an LCR meter (LCR‐6100, GwINSTEK). The surface morphologies of the hydrogels were analyzed using field‐emission scanning electron microscopy (FE‐SEM, LEO SUPRA 55, Carl Zeiss).
