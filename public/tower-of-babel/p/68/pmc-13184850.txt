Ventricular Tachycardia as the Initial Presentation of Tuberculous Myocarditis
Authors: Arnau Subira-Ingla, Jaume Francisco-Pascual, Alba Santos-Ortega, Maria Nazarena Pizzi, Albert Roque, José F Rodríguez-Palomares, Ignacio Ferreira-González, Gerard Oristrell-Santamaria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocardial involvement in tuberculosis is exceptionally rare and may closely mimic cardiac sarcoidosis, particularly when presenting with ventricular arrhythmias.Case SummaryA 47-year-old man from a tuberculosis-endemic region presented with sustained monomorphic ventricular tachycardia and biomarker elevation. Coronary angiography was normal. Multimodal imaging demonstrated extensive inflammatory nonischemic myocardial involvement and mediastinal lymphadenopathy. Initial lymph node biopsy showed nonnecrotizing granulomas, leading to a working diagnosis of probable cardiac sarcoidosis and initiation of corticosteroid therapy. Delayed lymph node culture later identified Mycobacterium tuberculosis, prompting revision of the diagnosis and initiation of antituberculous therapy. Despite microbiologic control, recurrent ventricular tachycardia required antiarrhythmic optimization and catheter ablation.DiscussionThis case highlights the diagnostic overlap between tuberculous myocarditis and cardiac sarcoidosis and underscores the importance of microbiologic confirmation in patients from endemic regions.Take-Home MessagesTuberculosis should be considered in granulomatous myocarditis with ventricular arrhythmias. Multimodal imaging combined with extracardiac tissue culture may be decisive in establishing the correct diagnosis.Key words: cardiac magnetic resonance, cardiomyopathy, electroanatomical mapping, positron emission tomography, ventricular tachycardia

Tuberculous Myocarditis Presenting With Sustained Monomorphic Ventricular Tachycardia

Multimodality imaging with cardiac magnetic resonance and [18F]FDG PET/CT demonstrated active granulomatous myocardial inflammation with extracardiac lymphadenopathy. Delayed lymph node culture confirmed Mycobacterium tuberculosis, allowing definitive diagnosis and targeted therapy. CMR = cardiac magnetic resonance; LGE = late gadolinium enhancement; [18F]FDG PET/CT = fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography–computed tomography; ICD = implantable cardioverter-defibrillator; MMVT = monomorphic ventricular tachycardia.

History of Presentation

A 47-year-old man originally from Pakistan, an active smoker with no relevant medical history, presented to the emergency department with a 2-week history of palpitations and chest tightness. He denied syncope, dyspnea, fever, viral prodromal symptoms, or constitutional complaints. He reported completion of routine vaccinations in his country of origin, including Bacillus Calmette–Guérin vaccination, and last international travel 6 months before presentation.

On arrival, he was hemodynamically stable, with a blood pressure of 110/75 mm Hg, heart rate of 150 beats/min, and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. Physical examination was unremarkable. A 12-lead electrocardiogram showed a regular wide-complex tachycardia with right bundle branch block morphology, superior right-axis deviation, negative precordial transition in V3, and atrioventricular dissociation, consistent with sustained monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (MMVT) likely arising from the inferolateral-apical left ventricle (Figure 1).

Admission Electrocardiogram

12-lead electrocardiogram on admission showing a regular wide-complex tachycardia at 150 beats/min with right bundle branch block morphology, superior right-axis deviation, negative precordial transition (R to S) in lead V3, and atrioventricular dissociation, consistent with sustained monomorphic ventricular tachycardia of inferolateral-apical origin.

Past Medical History

The patient had no known cardiovascular disease, no prior hospitalizations, and was not taking any regular medications. There was no history of immunosuppression.

Investigations

Laboratory testing showed normal renal and thyroid function and normal lactate (<2 mmol/L). High-sensitivity troponin I level was elevated (623-723 ng/L; reference p99 <54 ng/L), and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide was increased (1,161 pg/mL; normal <150 pg/mL). Chest radiography demonstrated bilateral mediastinal widening (Figure 2).

Chest Radiography at Presentation

Chest radiography showing normal cardiothoracic index and marked bilateral mediastinal widening. No pleural effusion or signs of pulmonary vascular redistribution are present.

Point-of-care echocardiography revealed preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) without regional wall motion abnormalities. Coronary angiography showed no obstructive coronary artery disease.

After 4 100-mg boluses of procainamide, sinus rhythm was restored. The postconversion electrocardiogram showed sinus tachycardia (100 beats/min), normal PR interval, narrow QRS complexes, flattened T waves in V4 to V6, normal QTc, and frequent monomorphic premature ventricular complexes with an inferolateral axis (Figure 3).

Postconversion Electrocardiogram

Electrocardiogram after pharmacologic cardioversion showing sinus rhythm at 100 beats/min, PR interval of 160 ms, narrow QRS complexes (80 ms), flattened T waves in leads V4 to V6, normal R-wave progression, normal corrected QT interval (QTc 430 ms, Bazett), and frequent monomorphic premature ventricular complexes with an inferolateral axis.

The patient was admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit for continuous telemetry monitoring. Despite beta-blocker therapy (bisoprolol 5 mg/d), he exhibited a high ventricular ectopic burden with recurrent episodes of sustained MMVT, prompting escalation to intravenous antiarrhythmic therapy.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) demonstrated mild left ventricular hypertrophy with preserved systolic function (LVEF 52%) and extensive nonischemic late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). Enhancement was predominantly intramyocardial in the septal and inferoseptal segments, forming a “hook sign” pattern, with additional subepicardial involvement of the anterior, anterolateral, lateral, and inferolateral walls (Figure 4A). Native T1 was globally elevated (1,099 ms; septal 1,190 ms; normal <1,050 ms), extracellular volume fraction was increased (35%), and T2 mapping demonstrated patchy myocardial edema (up to 70 ms; normal <55 ms) (Figure 4B). Right ventricular function was preserved, and there was no pericardial involvement. Multicompartment mediastinal lymphadenopathy was present. Overall, these findings were consistent with an active inflammatory cardiomyopathy.

(B) Native T1 mapping (top) in basal, mid, and apical short-axis slices showing globally (1,099 ms) and septally (1,190 ms) increased values, consistent with myocardial fibrosis (normal <1,050 ms). Given the concern for granulomatous disease, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography–computed tomography ([18F]FDG PET/CT) showed heterogeneous myocardial uptake with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 12 and bulky hypermetabolic supraclavicular and mediastinal lymphadenopathy (SUVmax = 28), without other organ involvement (Figure 5).

[18F]FDG PET/CT During the Acute Phase

Fused [18F]FDG PET/CT images in coronal (top left), axial (top right), and short-axis (bottom) planes demonstrating extensive heterogeneous myocardial uptake involving the anterior, anterolateral, mid-septal, and basal septal regions (SUVmax = 12). Additional focal uptake is observed in the right ventricular outflow tract and pulmonary infundibulum. Hypermetabolic supraclavicular, right paratracheal, and subcarinal lymphadenopathy are present (SUVmax = 28). No pulmonary, pleural, or pericardial uptake is observed. [18F]FDG PET/CT = fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography–computed tomography.

Additional testing revealed mildly elevated serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (68.7 U/L; normal <63 U/L), a positive interferon gamma release assay for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and negative human immunodeficiency virus serology.

Management

Because cardiac sarcoidosis was strongly suspected, histologic confirmation was pursued. Given the limited diagnostic yield and procedural risk of endomyocardial biopsy, a right supraclavicular lymph node biopsy was performed. Histopathology demonstrated nonnecrotizing granulomatous lymphadenitis (Figure 6), with negative initial microbiologic stains and polymerase chain reaction testing for M. tuberculosis.

Supraclavicular Lymph Node Biopsy

Hematoxylin and eosin–stained section of a right supraclavicular lymph node demonstrating nonnecrotizing granulomatous lymphadenitis (red arrow) with multinucleated giant cells of Langhans type (green arrow).

Based on the 2014 Heart Rhythm Society consensus criteria, a diagnosis of probable cardiac sarcoidosis was made.1 High-dose corticosteroids (prednisone 40 mg/d) were initiated, and a secondary-prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was implanted given recurrent sustained ventricular tachycardia. During the short course of corticosteroid therapy, a similar arrhythmic burden persisted, mainly in the form of frequent nonsustained ventricular tachycardia episodes detected by the ICD. In parallel, treatment for presumed latent tuberculosis infection was started with a 3-month regimen.

One month after discharge, lymph node culture returned positive for M. tuberculosis. The diagnosis was revised to tuberculous myocarditis with isolated myocardial and nodal involvement. Corticosteroids were rapidly tapered, and standard 4-drug antituberculous therapy was initiated.

Despite antimicrobial therapy, the patient experienced recurrent sustained MMVT episodes treated by antitachycardia pacing. Antiarrhythmic therapy was optimized with the addition of mexiletine to bisoprolol and amiodarone. Because symptomatic sustained MMVT persisted, catheter ablation was performed. During the electrophysiology study, a sustained MMVT with a slightly different morphology from the clinical tachycardia—characterized by a superior axis, left bundle branch block pattern, and negative precordial concordance—was inducible and successfully ablated from the septoapical region using radiofrequency energy. This difference in morphology was considered to reflect a different exit site from the same septoapical inflammatory substrate (Figure 7).

The earliest endocardial electrogram precedes QRS onset by only 5 ms, suggesting an intramyocardial focus or re-entry. Radiofrequency applications delivered from this region and the opposing left ventricular aspect successfully terminated the arrhythmia (red circle). Additional ablation was performed targeting late potentials in the septal right ventricular outflow tract adjacent to the basal septal intramyocardial scar identified on cardiac magnetic resonance (green circle). A second ventricular tachycardia was inducible but was nonsustained and not reproducibly mappable. Outcome and Follow-Up

After completing 6 months of antituberculous therapy, the patient remained asymptomatic without further ventricular arrhythmias. At 12 months, repeat CMR showed mildly reduced LVEF (42%) with global hypokinesia and decreased LGE extent and intensity (Figure 8). Follow-up [18F]FDG PET/CT demonstrated near-complete resolution of myocardial inflammatory activity (SUVmax = 1.84, comparable to blood pool) and marked reduction in lymph node uptake (SUVmax = 3.7) (Figure 9).

He remains under follow-up in cardiomyopathy and arrhythmia clinics. Guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure was initiated. Due to amiodarone-induced hyperthyroidism, amiodarone was replaced with sotalol, with plans to progressively withdraw mexiletine.

Discussion

Cardiac tuberculosis most commonly involves the pericardium, whereas myocardial involvement is exceedingly rare.2 Tuberculous myocarditis may be asymptomatic; when clinically apparent, it may manifest with heart failure, conduction abnormalities, ventricular arrhythmias, or sudden cardiac death, frequently mimicking other granulomatous cardiomyopathies, particularly cardiac sarcoidosis.3 This case illustrates how tuberculous myocardial inflammation can closely resemble cardiac sarcoidosis across clinical presentation, imaging phenotype, and histopathology.

Multimodal imaging was central to diagnosis and management. CMR demonstrated extensive nonischemic LGE with associated myocardial edema, supporting active inflammation rather than a purely scar-related arrhythmic substrate. In granulomatous myocarditis, LGE distribution is often patchy and may involve subepicardial, midmyocardial, or transmural layers; the septal “hook sign,” while frequently associated with sarcoidosis, is not disease-specific. [18F]FDG PET/CT complemented CMR by identifying metabolically active myocardial inflammation and guiding biopsy toward accessible extracardiac lymph nodes, thereby avoiding higher-risk endomyocardial sampling.4

Although myocarditis guidelines recommend endomyocardial biopsy in high-risk presentations such as sustained MMVT,5 its diagnostic yield in suspected tuberculous myocarditis is extremely low. In addition, no published cases to date have demonstrated M. tuberculosis by myocardial culture or polymerase chain reaction, further limiting the expected diagnostic yield of endomyocardial biopsy. In contrast, microbiologic confirmation is more commonly achieved from extracardiac sites, making lymph node biopsy a safer and more informative diagnostic strategy.6

The initial diagnosis of probable cardiac sarcoidosis was reasonable using contemporary consensus criteria1; however, nonnecrotizing granulomas do not exclude tuberculosis, particularly in patients from endemic regions. Early negative stains or molecular tests should not preclude ongoing microbiologic investigation, as delayed culture positivity may ultimately establish the diagnosis and substantially alter management.

Antituberculous therapy remains the cornerstone of treatment, although the optimal duration for myocardial involvement is not well defined. Most reported cases describe treatment courses ranging from 6 to 12 months.2,6 Adjunctive corticosteroids have been used in selected cases, but evidence supporting their routine use is limited. Paradoxical clinical worsening after the initiation of antituberculous therapy has been described and warrants close monitoring.7

Sustained MMVT places myocarditis in a high-risk category, justifying ICD implantation for secondary prevention irrespective of etiology.5 To our knowledge, this represents the first documented case of catheter ablation for ventricular tachycardia in the setting of tuberculous myocarditis. Experience from other granulomatous cardiomyopathies, particularly cardiac sarcoidosis, suggests that ablation may be most effective once inflammatory activity has decreased, an approach supported here by the subsequent metabolic resolution on [18F]FDG PET/CT.3 Notably, the induced ventricular tachycardia demonstrated a focal activation pattern with minimal prematurity at the earliest endocardial site and colocalized with regions of increased metabolic activity on [18F]FDG PET/CT, in the absence of a corresponding scar on CMR. This pattern supports an inflammation-driven arrhythmic mechanism rather than a fixed scar-related substrate, consistent with active granulomatous myocardial involvement.

Serial imaging provided objective markers of disease activity and therapeutic response. [18F]FDG PET/CT demonstrated near-complete metabolic quiescence, whereas follow-up CMR showed reduced LGE burden with evolving ventricular dysfunction. Together, these findings highlight the complementary roles of CMR and [18F]FDG PET/CT in guiding treatment decisions and longitudinal follow-up.

Conclusions

Tuberculous myocarditis, although rare, should be actively considered in patients presenting with granulomatous inflammatory cardiomyopathy and ventricular arrhythmias, particularly in those from endemic regions. Early pursuit of microbiologic confirmation from extracardiac tissue may prevent misdiagnosis and inappropriate long-term immunosuppression.
