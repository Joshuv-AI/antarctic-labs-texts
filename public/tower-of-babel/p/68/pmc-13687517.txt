A Time-Domain Model for the Generation of Spontaneous Otoacoustic Emissions in Resonant Cavities
Authors: Hero P. Wit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A long-standing question in hearing science is whether spontaneous otoacoustic emissions (SOAEs) arise from a specific active site on the basilar membrane—they have a local origin—or whether they derive from distributed active elements working together in a resonant cavity—a global picture. This paper uses numerical modelling to investigate the difference between local and global activity in the ear. The cochlea is modelled as a graded array of discrete oscillators, each of fixed natural frequency and coupled only by the surrounding fluid. We examine what happens when the damping parameters of the system are varied in particular ways. Do the continuous oscillations that emerge at the middle ear carry the tell-tale frequency of a specific oscillator—pointing to a local origin—or is the oscillation one that arose from the global activity of multiple interacting oscillators? The basic oscillating element considered here is made up of two feedback-coupled masses, a general arrangement first proposed by Neely and Kim. An important parameter is the amount of undamping of the element, such that high undamping (low damping) energizes the system and tends to generate spontaneous oscillations. We use the time-domain state-space formalism to describe the interacting system of oscillating elements. We find that the entire system can be made local or global depending on the size and distribution of the undamping parameters. Thus, if a single element is made to continuously oscillate by increasing its undamping, a spontaneous otoacoustic emission (SOAE) will emerge at the oscillator’s natural frequency—it can be considered to have a local origin. On the other hand, if all the elements are provided with sufficient undamping, an SOAE can again emerge even though each individual element remains below its oscillation threshold—the conclusion being that the oscillation now arises from global coupling. In this manner, our numerical modelling shows that even when undamping is supplied irregularly all along the array (but not enough to make any individual element continuously oscillate), one or more SOAEs can still be produced. When such a system is examined, we see an underlying pattern of standing waves, but even so, we see no clear evidence of a more or less regular process for the reflection of waves from irregularities at the apical side of a resonant cavity—which is what the generally accepted global model of SOAE generation by coherent reflection calls for.

Otoacoustic emissions were discovered almost 50 years ago by Kemp [1, 2] as “evoked cochlear mechanical responses” (ECMRs). Even when no evoking stimulus is present, weak tones can be detected emerging continuously from the inner ears of frogs, lizards, birds, and mammals—so-called spontaneous otoacoustic emissions (SOAEs).

Two broad classes of models have been used to understand the generation of SOAEs. At one extreme, there are “local” models, where isolated micro-mechanical elements permanently oscillate, independently of the other elements. At the other, there are “global” models, in which none of the elements oscillates in isolation, but when coupled by the inner ear fluid, the system produces one or more self-sustaining oscillations. The difference between the two classes, and the implications for how SOAEs are generated, was a few years ago extensively described by Shera [3].

Figure 1 is a slightly adapted version of Fig. 3 in Shera’s 2022 [3] paper. It illustrates how an SOAE is generated by the human ear, functioning as a global oscillator. The red hair cell borders a “resonant cavity.”

Shera [3] describes the operation of the global oscillator in Fig. 1 below as follows: “The region between the stapes and the peak of the traveling wave (the red hair cell in Fig. 1) acts as a “resonant cavity” enclosing a non-linear gain medium powered by hair cells. Partial reflection of forward- and backward-traveling waves (the green line in Fig. 1) occurs at each end of the cavity. Standing waves occur at frequencies where the round-trip phase changes by an integral number of cycles. Standing-wave amplitudes are stabilized when the round-trip gain matches the losses due to internal damping and emission into the ear canal.”

Examples of local models are given in [4–10]. A notable feature of all these local models is that self-sustaining oscillators cluster in frequency plateaus [11], and each plateau is taken to being the source of an SOAE (strictly speaking, these models, except [7], do not generate SOAEs, because they lack a connection to the tympanic membrane).

An earlier effort to illustrate the similarities and differences between the two classes of model was set out in the intermezzi of an earlier paper [9]. The present contribution is a continuation of that earlier work, and here the characteristics of the two classes of systems are investigated and illustrated in more detail. We find that the difference between them can be simply categorized in terms of where and how undamping—the source of the SOAEs energy—is applied within an array of micro-mechanical elements. If undamping, large enough to make the system oscillate continuously at some particular frequency, is applied to just one oscillator in the array, the signal detected in the ear canal will carry its signature frequency, conclusively indicating that the SOAE has a local origin. On the other hand, even if the undamping applied to each of the elements is not large enough to make any one of them oscillate, the broader system can still continuously oscillate if an irregularity is introduced into the larger set of individual undamping values—as we will show later. In such a case, the SOAE can be said to have a global origin.

In more detail, our modelling will also compare the situation in which the array of micro-mechanical elements has one isolated irregularity in its undamping parameters with the situation where the undamping parameters are irregular all along the array. In these two situations, the underlying mechanisms that create continuous oscillations are compared and explored. Of particular interest, we search here for “coherent reflection,” which is a key component in the global standing wave paradigm [3, 12–14].

The micro-mechanical elements we use to investigate the two classes of behavior are based on a pair of actively coupled masses, a configuration first proposed by Neely and Kim [15]. Our cochlear model comprises an extended array of such pairs.

To keep the presentation clear and comprehensible, details of this system are placed in Appendices 1 and 3.

If a global oscillator is defined as a system of two or more oscillators that do not oscillate in isolation, but undergo a self-sustaining oscillation when coupled, then the system in Appendix 1 can be regarded as an example of an elementary global oscillator.

When the solutions reveal continuous oscillation of oscillator 1 (the stapes and entire middle ear), then this shows the presence of an SOAE exiting the inner ear into the external ear canal.

Following Elliott et al. [16], the micro-mechanical elements j in Fig. 2 are chosen to be Neely & Kim elements [15], as investigated in Appendix 1. Importantly, note that there is no elastic and/or dissipative coupling between the elements (as there is, for instance, in the similar model of Fruth et al. [17]). The only coupling here is through the fluid.

Although it may be surprising that a self-sustaining single frequency oscillation can arise in a nonlinear time-domain state-space model due to a single irregularity in an array of feedback gain parameters, this was in fact demonstrated two decades ago by Elliott et al. [16] in their Fig. 14. In their case, the irregularity was a single step from γ = 0.850γ=0.850 down to γ = 0.849γ=0.849, while the frequency of the self-sustaining oscillation was “very similar to the characteristic frequency at the position of the step.” In our situation, the frequency of the impulse response for element 100, for no fluid coupling and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _{0,100} =0.6$$\end{document}γ0,100=0.6 is 3.10 kHz. This is to be compared with 3.14 kHz for the elements in Fig. 4.

It is of interest to note that Vignali [18] repeated the calculation that was used to obtain Fig. 14 in Elliott et al. [16], and that his figure 6.3, with a standing wave pattern, is directly comparable with our Fig. 4a (the standing wave pattern in Vignali’s figure is more clearly visible, because he calculated BM velocity, not displacement).

It should further be noted that the single irregularity in Elliott et al. [16] (see above), and also in Vignali [18], is a single step in an array of γ γ-values. Whereas in our case, it is a single anomaly of γ γ. Our case can be interpreted as two steps: one step up, followed by one step down.

Roughness Helps Energize the System

To investigate the effect of the strength of the irregularities on the generated SOAE, the same calculations, as for Fig. 7g, were repeated for different standard deviations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta _{sd}$$\end{document}δsd of the irregular \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _{0,j}$$\end{document}γ0,j-array, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _c$$\end{document}γc was kept at 0.42, except for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _{0,100} $$\end{document}γ0,100, being 0.522 throughout. For each arrangement, the RMS profile along the array of elements was calculated. The results are shown in Fig. 8.

It can be concluded from Fig. 8 that the stronger the irregularities, the larger the amplitude of the self-sustaining oscillations of the elements, including the strength of the SOAE at oscillator 1. Furthermore, at least for this choice of parameters, irregularities are needed in order to evoke an SOAE. Without irregularities, there are no self-sustaining oscillations.

However, while the result is clear graphically, the mechanism behind it is not immediately apparent. How and why should roughness in a set of oscillators lead to amplification of the SOAE? The answer is by no means clear to us, and we discuss this point later.

Creating Multiple SOAEs

To elucidate the role that damping parameters play in the generation of SOAEs, we illustrate what happens when undamping is increased at two more isolated positions in the array. Here, the calculations for the same irregular array of damping parameters used to obtain the results in Fig. 7 were repeated after making two changes: increasing the standard undamping term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _{0,60}$$\end{document}γ0,60 from 0.436 to 0.52, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _{0,124}$$\end{document}γ0,124 from 0.493 to 0.57. This change resulted in the generation of three SOAEs at frequencies of 2.20, 3.18, and 5.48 kHz, rather than the previous single SOAE at 3.18 kHz.

The results are shown in Fig. 9.

After comparing Figs. 7 and 9, the main conclusion to be drawn is that Fig. 9 is effectively a three-way composite of the single arrangement in Fig. 7, leading to 3 resonant cavities instead of 1. Each cavity generates its own single-frequency SOAE, a process which Zweig [21] compared to how a laser creates a single-frequency (or wavelength) light beam.

Discussion

Global Versus Local

If Figs. 4 and 5 are compared it can be seen that for both conditions—global and local—an SOAE is generated within a resonant cavity. For the global situation, a necessary condition is that wave reflection at the basal end of the cavity needs to be sufficiently strong to maintain the self-sustaining oscillation.

For the global case, however, if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _{0,j}$$\end{document}γ0,j is given a value smaller than 0.32, it can never produce any self-sustaining oscillation—all the oscillators are damped. As Shera [3] explains: “the round-trip gain no longer matches the losses due to internal damping and emission into the ear canal.” Or, in terms of our model, the undamping by feedback pressure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_a$$\end{document}Pa in the Neely and Kim micro-mechanical element (Fig. 16 in Appendix 1) is too small.

Quite different is the situation for the single self-sustaining local oscillator: even for all \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _{0,j}$$\end{document}γ0,j being zero, except for element 100 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _{0,100}$$\end{document}γ0,100 remains at 1.1), a self-sustaining oscillation is generated, although it has a much smaller SOAE amplitude and smaller standing wave in the resonant cavity. This can be verified by comparing the 3D-plots in Figs. 5 and 12.

It can be concluded that in the global situation, the creation of a resonant cavity is a necessary condition for the generation of an SOAE. On the other hand, with a local oscillator, the resonant cavity is merely a by-product.

Cochlear Standing Waves in Resonant Cavities

More than 30 years ago, Wit et al. [24] introduced wavelet analysis as a tool for time-frequency analysis of click-evoked otoacoustic emissions from human ears. They found that the introduction of a slight irregularity in the frequency-to-place map of the inner ear gave rise to outcomes that looked remarkably similar to those obtained from actual ears. They also showed that adding periodicity to the frequency-to-place map to emulate Bragg reflection, as proposed by Strube [25] to explain the relation between time delay and frequency in click-evoked otoacoustic emissions, gave unrealistic results.

In related work, Wit et al. [26] showed how the presence of small irregularities in the array of central frequencies of the individual filters in a bank of linear bandpass filters leads, in a rather straightforward way, to(quasi) periodicity in (synthesized) spectra of click-evoked otoacoustic emissions.

One year earlier, Shera and Zweig [12] expanded on Strube’s [25] “cochlear washboard” proposal to explain periodicity in the spectrum of evoked otoacoustic emissions in terms of coherent scattering off irregularities, creating a backward-travelling wave that interferes with the stimulus tone.

Two years later, Zweig and Shera [13] elaborated this model and showed that “coherent reflection filtering” can also generate SOAEs and explain periodicity in their spectra. Essential in the coherent reflection filtering paradigm is, in contrast with Kemp’s [27] original standing wave model, where the backward travelling wave originates from a point reflection, that this wave “necessarily arises over a region equivalent to the span of many hair cells.” This is illustrated in Fig. 13, which is a somewhat modified version of Fig. 8 in [14].

Applied to the present model, this means that reflection needs to occur at more than a single micro-mechanical element. In this way, reflected waves that are in phase amplify each other ( hence the term “coherent reflection”).

Shera and Zweig [13] describe coherent reflection as follows: “...wave reflection is largely localized to a relatively broad region containing many reflectors, which are arrayed, in general, at a superposition of many spatial frequencies. Although each of the many reflected wavelets originates from a different location within the peak region, wavelets reflected by a particular spatial frequency, denoted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{K}$$\end{document}K^, undergo a phase change due to scattering by the irregularity that exactly compensates for the phase change due to wave propagation forth and back to the point of reflection. Such wavelets therefore reflect coherently, and their sum dominates the net backward-traveling wave.”

Now, how does this situation apply to the global/local distinction we have made?

In the case of a local oscillator, Fig. 5 shows that when an SOAE is generated by a local oscillator, its source is a single element in the array of micro-mechanical elements. This single element bounds a resonant cavity—in which the standing wave with the frequency of the SOAE is generated—at its apical (low frequency) end. And although amplification (the green region in Fig. 13) generates a stronger SOAE, it is not needed, as is shown in Fig. 12.

A similar situation applies in the global case (Fig. 4). In this case, reflection occurs at a single element (j=100), which is at the apical end of a resonant cavity. There is no region of reflection spanning more than one element. And the wave propagating in the apical direction starts immediately at element 101, next to the apical border of the resonant cavity (see Figs. 4b and 7c). With the local case, an essential difference is that sufficient amplification (undamping) and reflection at the stapes are needed in order to generate an SOAE.

It is clear from Fig. 8 that increasing the strength of the irregularities in the array of undamping parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _{0,j}$$\end{document}γ0,j amplifies the standing wave and the SOAE (Fig. 8b and c). By setting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta _{sd}$$\end{document}δsd at zero for different regions of the array of irregularities, we found by trial and error that if irregularities are restricted to the region from element 85 to element 130, it amplifies exactly as much as when irregularities are present all along the array. In other words, irregularities for j=2-84j=2-84 and j=131-351j=131-351 play no role. But having irregularities only for elements 100–105 is enough to amplify the displacements of the elements, as can be seen in Fig. 14b and c.

Instead of introducing irregularities, the displacements can also be made larger by increasing the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _{0,100}$$\end{document}γ0,100. In Fig. 14d and e, it can be seen that increasing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _{0,100}$$\end{document}γ0,100 from 0.6 to 0.643 gives exactly the same RMS value as for the situation with irregularities present all along the array.

It is shown in Fig. 14b and c that irregularities from element 85 to element 130 create the same amplitude for the standing wave and the SOAE as irregularities all along the array. To investigate this in more detail, the RMS value of oscillator 1 (the SOAE) was calculated step-by-step, starting at element 86 and including progressively more irregular elements. The result, as shown in Fig. 15d, is that the level of the SOAE increases from a value for no irregularities all along the array to a value for irregularities all along the array.

Figure 15d shows a more or less gradual increase for the amplitude of the generated SOAE if the number of irregular elements increases from 12 (at element 97) to 45 (at element 130). By adding one more irregular element, we see no systematic increase in amplitude at regular intervals, which we would expect if the phase of the wavelet reflected by this element positively contributed to the amplitude of the standing wave. Furthermore, it can be concluded from Fig. 15 e, f, and g that there is no correlation between the profiles for the array of irregularities and the increase of SOAE amplitudes.

In this context, it is worth looking again at Fig. 14b and c, which show that more irregularities mean stronger reflection, not more amplification. If it were a case of amplification, then one would expect a contribution of the irregularities in the region between elements 2 to 85, where the standing wave is created. Yet we see that only 5 irregularities (elements 101–105) contribute almost as much to the amplitudes of the standing wave and the SOAE as all the other irregularities.

Furthermore, Fig. 15d shows that the irregularities contribute in a complex way. In some regions (for instance, 110–115), increasing the number of contributing irregularities even decreases the amplitude of the SOAE.

Finally, the coherent reflection explanation calls for reflections from a particular spatial frequency: a structure that is periodic across position in space. Such behavior is also not seen in Fig. 15.

In the end, it must be concluded that the time-domain model, as presented here, gives a clear indication that reflection at one or more irregularities is essential for the generation of spontaneous otoacoustic emissions. But how the reflected signals add up to create a sufficiently strong standing wave is not immediately clear.

Ku et al. [28], applying the same time-domain state-space model [16] as we do here, observed “the salient features of Zweig and Shera’s theory.” Among other things, they calculated the statistics of unstable frequencies (self-sustaining oscillations) for random spatially dense variations in gain all along the array of micro-mechanical elements. They found that the average spacing of adjacent unstable frequencies agreed with the preferred minimum distance in human SOAE data. They gave no information about how reflections from individual irregularities could add up to contribute to the amplitude of a self-sustaining oscillation.

Since Ku et al. [28] do not give any information about how reflections from individual irregularities add up to contribute to the amplitude of a self-sustaining oscillation (and we could not find other published time-domain models that investigate the generation of SOAEs by reflection from many irregularities) we do not really know what might be convincing evidence of the presence of coherent reflection, at least as intended by Zweig and Shera [13].

Frequency Plateaus

Figure 9 shows that the three peaks in panel f are generated in three partly overlapping resonant cavities. Also in Fig. 10, standing waves, generated in separate resonant cavities, can be seen. Each of these waves corresponds to an SOAE-peak in panel a. Similarly, clear distinct standing wave patterns—and corresponding frequency plateaus—can also be seen in Fig. 3 of Epp et al. [29], where the generation of SOAEs is also modelled with a one-dimensional array of micro-mechanical elements. However, in this case, while the elements have velocity-dependent damping and a velocity-dependent feedback stiffness term [30], they are different from the Neely and Kim element.

Frequency plateaus are also generated in the model by Fruth et al.[17] for the mammalian ear (see their Fig. 6). In this one-dimensional model, the 3500 (!) micro-mechanical elements are Hopf oscillators that are coupled hydrodynamically and via elastic and dissipative elements. The latter type of coupling is the same as in the model for the lizard ear by Vilfan and Duke [4]. In the light of the present work, it appears relevant that Fruth et al. [17] find that SOAEs in their model “are elicited by synchronization of local oscillators, which is in agreement with the SOAE generation mechanism found in other vertebrates. This is in contrast to the notion of SOAEs arising of a global phenomenon from wavelike excitations.”

The conclusion from the different approaches seems to be that frequency plateaus are a universal phenomenon underlying the formation of SOAEs, independent of the model used.

Spacing Order

Spectra with multiple SOAEs from a human ear show spacing order [31]) and a preferred minimum distance (PMD) on a logarithmic frequency scale. Braun [23] determined the PMD between spectrally neighboring SOAEs to be 1 semitone (100 Cent), or a frequency ratio Δ fΔf=1.06 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta f = 2^{(PMD/1200)}$$\end{document}Δf=2(PMD/1200). It can be concluded from Braun’s Fig. 1 that there is no perfect order: the distributions in this figure have a long, gradually decaying tail, without local maxima at multiples of the PMD. Shera [14] found the same value for the PMD with his Fig. 2 having a maximum at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{SOAE}=17$$\end{document}NSOAE=17. Using the relation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta f=(2N_{SOAE}+1)/(2N_{SOAE}-1)$$\end{document}Δf=(2NSOAE+1)/(2NSOAE-1) this also yields a preferred minimum ratio Δ fΔf of 1.06.

A frequency ratio of 100 Cent corresponds with 1 semitone in musical terms. This prompted Bell and Jedrzejczak [32] to suggest that “the inner ear may play a role in constructing a natural theory of music.”

Order in the spectra with multiple SOAEs in the present work was investigated by repeatedly calculating SOAE spectra, each time for a newly generated irregular array of damping parameters. Just like for spectra of human SOAEs, Fig. 11a and b reveal a PMD, but at a frequency ratio of 1.35 (or 520 Cents). This corresponds well with the positions of the maxima in the distributions for human, owl, and anolis in Fig. 3B of [22]. But not with the distributions given by Braun [23] or Shera [14] (although Fig. 11c shows a small local maximum at a ratio 1.10, not 1.06).

More light can possibly be thrown on the discrepancy between our results and those of Braun [23] and Shera [14] by repeating the model calculations with other sets of parameters. For instance, a larger standard deviation of the array of damping parameters generates more spectral peaks. What remains to be explained is the difference between Braun’s and Shera’s distributions and that of Bergevin et al.

Non-mammalian Ears

The question naturally arises as to whether the global oscillator paradigm is restricted to just mammalian ears, since it is generally considered that, for the lizard ear at least, a local oscillator model is appropriate. Traveling waves and resonant cavities do not seem to be present in non-mammalian ears.

Thus, when Vilfan and Duke [4] and Gelfand et al. [5] modelled the generation of SOAEs in the ears of lizards, they constructed an array of coupled active elements (in the first case, 80 Hopf oscillators and in the second, 110 Van der Pol oscillators). In both models, the SOAEs are simply peaks in the power spectrum of the oscillator displacements.

Slightly differently, in the model of the lizard ear by Wit and Bell [7], all the inner ear oscillators were active Hopf oscillators, and these were considered to directly drive the basilar papilla. The motion of the papilla was then coupled to the tympanic membrane by incompressible inner ear fluid, which in turn created SOAEs.

Moreover, even the most elementary ears of frogs emit SOAEs (for a review, see Van Dijk et al. [33]). Their most likely source is the amphibian papilla: an arrangement of hair cells, covered with tectorial membrane, located on the dorsal wall (top) of the amphibian recess. However, unlike for lizards, there is no comparable model for the generation of SOAEs in frogs, although Bell [34] sets out a possible “local oscillator” mechanism: oscillations of hair cells are directly transported to the tympanic membrane by fast pressure waves in the inner ear fluids.

Conclusions

By modelling the cochlea as a graded array of micro-mechanical elements—two feedback-coupled masses with variable degrees of counteraction of their damping—our numerical computations provide a number of insights into how spontaneous emissions might arise. The model replicates a number of SOAE properties.

One notable aspect is the way it may help resolve the question of whether SOAEs are of local or global origin, suggesting that the distinction is really a matter of how the irregular array of damping parameters is arranged.

Another point of interest is the appearance in the model of a standing wave within a resonant cavity. All elements within the cavity oscillate at the same frequency, which is the frequency of the generated SOAE.

Our time-domain model is also able to simulate roughness, and although the results may be indicative, the interpretation is not clear-cut. The calculations show that increased roughness increases the size of SOAEs, but our model is limited in explaining how such an amplification arises.

In summary, the present model shows a number of interesting features that help explain some aspects of cochlear mechanics, but at the same time, it also gives rise to a number of unanswered questions which need explanation by further exploration.
