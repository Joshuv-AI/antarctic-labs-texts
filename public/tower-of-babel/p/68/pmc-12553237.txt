Introduction

The increasing reliance on Knowledge Process Outsourcing (KPO) in the healthcare industry has introduced both operational efficiencies and security challenges, particularly in the management of sensitive patient data. With the growing adoption of electronic health records (EHRs), cloud computing, and artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare, ensuring information security compliance is more critical than ever. Healthcare KPOs handle vast volumes of personal health information (PHI), making them prime targets for cyber threats. Consequently, regulatory frameworks such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and ISO/IEC 27,001 impose stringent security standards to safeguard patient data.

Recent advancements in healthcare cybersecurity have introduced cutting-edge tools, encryption algorithms, and AI-driven solutions to enhance compliance and mitigate risks. Several technologies and frameworks are currently employed to secure healthcare data, including blockchain for data integrity, machine learning algorithms for anomaly detection, and secure multi-party computation (SMPC) for privacy-preserving analytics. Case studies in various healthcare settings demonstrate the effectiveness of these technologies in preventing data breaches, ensuring regulatory compliance, and enhancing trust among stakeholders.

Despite these advancements, healthcare KPOs struggle with security compliance due to organizational culture, lack of awareness, and limited technological infrastructure. This study aims to explore the determinants of information security compliance in healthcare KPOs, focusing on the role of healthcare information systems (HIS), security awareness, and regulatory frameworks. By examining the correlation between compliance behaviors and key influencing factors, this research seeks to provide actionable insights for industry leaders, policymakers, and IT security professionals in the healthcare outsourcing sector.

Various technologies and tools play a critical role in enhancing security and privacy in healthcare systems. Encryption (AES-256) is widely used to secure patient data, as demonstrated by its implementation in a major hospital chain [1]. Blockchain technology, particularly Hyperledger Fabric, ensures immutable logs for healthcare transactions, as seen in its application for Electronic Health Records at Frere Provincial Hospital [2]. AI and Machine Learning, specifically Anomaly Detection AI, help identify abnormal access patterns and potential security breaches, a method effectively used in healthcare cybersecurity [3]. Cloud security solutions, such as Homomorphic Encryption, enable computations on encrypted data without decryption, facilitating secure cloud-based medical research collaborations [4]. Regulatory compliance tools, like the ISO/IEC 27,001 framework, help implement standardized security controls, as observed in compliance audits of multinational healthcare KPOs [5]. Additionally, Privacy-Preserving Computing, through Secure Multi-Party Computation (SMPC), allows privacy-preserving analytics on patient data, which has been applied in federated learning for genomic research [6]. Lastly, Access Control measures, including the Zero Trust Security Model, enforce verification at every access point within a network, a strategy successfully implemented in hospital IT infrastructures [7].

Background

The healthcare industry, central to healthcare provision, increasingly relies on information technology, adopting electronic patient records and clinical information systems, alongside intranets for health information dissemination [8]. This integration of information and communication technologies (ICT) has given rise to Healthcare Information Systems (HIS), pivotal for enhancing healthcare quality and reducing costs [9]. Nevertheless, these systems face inherent risks, with significant impacts on healthcare information systems from various threat categories, both internal and external [10]. Addressing these challenges necessitates attention to information security issues, encompassing costs, management concerns, trust, security policies, and awareness [11, 12].

Healthcare management plays a crucial role in promoting security awareness, implementing security policies, fostering trust among stakeholders, ensuring consistent outcomes, and facilitating compliance with laws and regulations. The implementation of a robust security policy in healthcare is essential for transmitting sensitive data over the internet. Privacy in healthcare information sharing adheres to common principles, as identified by Suhaila et al. [13]. The evolving healthcare landscape underscores the need for a system of accountability to prevent information sharing misuse in general health scenarios [14].

In the Revenue Cycle Management (RCM) process, gathering data during registration, determining insurance coverage, and accurate claim coding are pivotal. Seamless integration with Electronic Health Records (EHR) and accounting systems is critical for streamlining billing and collection. Healthcare Knowledge Process Outsourcing (KPO) organizations play a vital role in facilitating insurance reimbursement for providers, with their income tied to a percentage commission based on generated revenue. Given their multi-provider responsibilities, understanding the factors influencing compliance culture in information security is imperative for these organizations. The conceptual framework for this study integrates established theories to examine important factors influencing information security compliance in healthcare Knowledge Process Outsourcing (KPO) organizations. Grounded in the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [10], Organizational Culture Theory [12], Information Security Awareness Theory [16], and regulatory frameworks like HIPAA [15]. It highlights the interplay between technological, organizational, and regulatory elements. Six key constructs—Healthcare Standards Compliance (HSC) [15, 14]., Healthcare Information Systems (HIS) [10, 11], Security Measures (SM) [10, 11], Management Involvement (MI) [12]., Organizational Culture (OC) [12, 16], and Information Security Awareness (ISA) [17] - are central to the framework. The study hypothesizes positive relationships between these constructs and compliance behaviors, emphasizing that adherence to standards, effective security measures, management commitment, a strong organizational culture, and targeted training programs collectively drive compliance. This framework bridges theoretical insights and empirical analysis, offering a structured lens to understand and improve compliance behaviors in healthcare KPOs.

This study pursues a dual objective. Firstly, it seeks to identify the determinants influencing the compliance culture within healthcare KPO organizations concerning information security. By comprehensively elucidating these factors, the research aims to shed light on the influences shaping security compliance behavior within healthcare KPOs. Secondly, the study also delves into the current information security challenges specific to healthcare KPOs.

This study aims to investigate the interrelationships among various factors pertaining to information security compliance in Sri Lankan healthcare Knowledge Process Outsourcing (KPO) organizations. The following hypotheses will be examined:

H1

Sri Lankan healthcare KPOs demonstrate a positive correlation with compliance under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and other relevant compliance measures.

H2

The level of information security compliance in Sri Lankan healthcare KPOs is positively associated with the adoption of health information systems within these organizations.

H3

Compliance with information security requisites in healthcare KPOs in Sri Lanka shows a positive correlation with specific security measures, such as data encryption, access controls, and incident response protocols.

H4

Active engagement of management in healthcare KPOs in Sri Lanka is positively linked to information security compliance.

H5

The effectiveness of organizational systems within Sri Lankan healthcare KPOs exhibits a positive correlation with information security compliance.

H6

Compliance with information security guidelines in Sri Lanka’s healthcare KPO sector is positively correlated with the level of information security awareness and training programs within these organizations.

Through these examinations, this study endeavors to offer valuable insights into the factors that affect information security compliance in the Sri Lankan healthcare KPO sector. The findings promise to enrich the understanding of how these organizations can effectively ensure compliance, elevate information security practices, and raise awareness among stakeholders involved in healthcare information management. This research holds significant import for healthcare organizations and practitioners, equipping them to gain a competitive edge by comprehending the pivotal factors that mold compliance culture in healthcare information security.

The findings will furnish valuable insights and recommendations for enhancing security compliance culture within healthcare KPOs. Additionally, this study fills a notable research gap, as no similar investigations on healthcare compliance behavior in Sri Lanka have been conducted, rendering it an indispensable reference for future scholars and researchers in the country. Ultimately, this research contributes to the advancement of knowledge in the realm of healthcare security compliance.

Healthcare standards

Protection of information during processing, particularly in healthcare contexts, is imperative [19], as evidenced by legislative mandates that rigorously safeguard client data. Various laws, such as the Family Education Rights and Privacy Act, the Fair Credit Reporting Act, and the Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act, underscore the importance of privacy protection in the United States and beyond [19]. New Zealand’s Health Information Privacy Code is a significant piece of legislation, guiding health information protection. International standards, such as ISO 17,799 and AS/NZS ISO/IEC 17,799, have influenced compliance standards in New Zealand and Australia [19]. Health Canada Privacy Act also provides compliance standard guidelines [20], while Europe and Asia adopt similar privacy laws to the United States.

In the context of healthcare Knowledge Process Outsourcing (KPOs), which are predominantly based in the USA, this research focuses on HIPAA compliance, a pivotal law [21]. The Healthcare Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) encompasses provisions for privacy and security, safeguarding medical patient confidentiality [20]. Achieving HIPAA compliance necessitates investments in information systems, organizational policies, and partnership agreements [22]. While it is estimated that HIPAA compliance will cost the healthcare industry $18 billion, it is expected to yield $30 billion in administrative savings [21]. Enacted in 1996, HIPAA introduced a tiered system of civil penalties through the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 (ARRA) [97], enhancing consistency in handling HIPAA violations. Healthcare providers have embraced HIPAA compliance through computer security guidelines, patient consent forms, and training programs [22].

Healthcare information systems

Information technology and the internet have transformed access to a vast array of information sources, becoming a pivotal information hub [23–25]. This transformation extends to medical professionals, facilitated by the widespread use of mobile devices like smartphones, tablets, and iPads, which provide access to medical records. The internet’s evolution into a data-centric environment [26] has driven a profound shift in healthcare services. This transformation encompasses remote health monitoring, online consultations, e-prescriptions, e-clinical trials, patient data accessibility, and asset tracking [27], delivering substantial benefits to all stakeholders.

Electronic Patient Records (EPR) systems offer instant access to patient demographics, medical histories, lab results, and images for healthcare providers and workers. However, EPR adoption remains limited, necessitating a distinction between EPRs within a single healthcare organization and Electronic Health Records (EHR) shared among multiple providers [28, 29]. While healthcare records exist in both paper and electronic formats (EMRs), EHRs integrate them to enhance care quality [30].

EHRs encompass various components, including appointment management, admissions, discharges, prescription records, dietary supplement tracking, picture archiving, and smart card authentication. Decision-making in healthcare relies on health information systems that generate, compile, analyze, synthesize, communicate, and utilize data. These systems typically consist of EHR systems or a collection of such systems, analyzing data from the health sector and other relevant fields to inform healthcare decisions [31, 32].

The healthcare ecosystem relies heavily on healthcare organizations and professionals. Healthcare Information Systems facilitate electronic communication among all stakeholders, standardize diagnostic data, and maintain context while enabling new interpretations [30]. Interoperability in healthcare information systems, achieved through Reference Information Models (RIMs) and agreed vocabularies, is supported by a generic component model developed in the mid-nineties [32]. Open EHRs and the Health Level Seven Version model (HL7) integrate into comprehensive models to manage existing structures, features, and terminology, aligning with European Committee for Normalization standards (CEN).

Recognizing the critical role of information system security [33, 34], healthcare information systems must prioritize addressing privacy and security concerns to gain societal acceptance. These systems confront a spectrum of threats, both internal and external, including viruses, worms, keyloggers, and insider attacks [35]. Intrusions into IT infrastructure, whether internal or external, pose risks, with denial-of-service attacks standing out as significant threats [36]. Cybersecurity incidents, exemplified by the 2014 HealthCare.gov breach, underscore the imperative of resilient systems [37]. Hardware and software failures further compound security vulnerabilities [38].

Numerous studies identify diverse health information system threats, emphasizing issues like power, network, system, and software failures [39]. Human factors contribute to high-risk threats. Research findings coalesce into five primary threat categories: power loss, human error, technological obsolescence, hardware failure, and software issues. Addressing these security challenges entails deploying technological solutions such as encryption, firewalls, and access management systems [40].

Information security awareness

Information security is undeniably a critical societal concern, as emphasized by Mensch and Wikie [61], corroborated by Okenyi and Owens [62], and discussed by Ku et al. [63] in the context of enabling businesses and nations to function effectively. Alarmingly, a survey of 450 IT directors and information security officers revealed that less than half of employees receive training on information security awareness [64], exposing a significant gap in preparedness.

Luo and Liao [65] aptly point out that cybercriminals are outpacing technological advancements, making it increasingly challenging to pinpoint the location of attackers [66]. Consequently, information security has transcended borders and become a global imperative, prompting many countries to enact national-level security policies. Examples include the UAE’s recognition of the need for security awareness in higher education [67], Taiwan’s national policy on information security [63], the United Kingdom’s focus on phishing education [69], and South Africa’s commitment to information security education [70].

Effective security education policies are deemed the solution to mitigate security breaches, as noted by Kieke [71], and the importance of consistently reminding users about security concerns cannot be overstated [72]. Doge et al. [73] emphasize that organizations must elevate user security awareness and provide education and training. Siponen [74] highlighted this need back in 2000, and subsequent studies have underscored the importance of security awareness training, encompassing policies, procedures, and tools [63, 75, 67]. The efficacy of a security policy hinges on users’ understanding and acceptance of its requirements [76].

To combat information system misuse, researchers argue for security education, training, and awareness [76–78] with ongoing efforts proven effective in deterring misuse attempts through instruction on correct and incorrect information system usage [79]. Furnell et al. advocate diverse organizational training approaches, including information awareness programs, to enhance daily security within organizations.

Furthermore, a training and awareness program on information security empowers organizations to hold employees accountable for any information system misuse, ensuring appropriate consequences, as outlined by Whiteman and Mattord [76]. Research by Hovav and De’Arcy [17] and Kruger et al. [16] demonstrates a clear correlation between information security threat awareness and the development of an information security culture.

Methods

This study employs a quantitative research methodology. Data collection involves the use of an online survey questionnaire distributed to senior IT employees within the Sri Lankan healthcare sector. The research aims to assess the compliance of healthcare Knowledge Process Outsourcing (KPO) firms in Sri Lanka concerning information security. To achieve this, health practitioners working in Sri Lankan KPOs are asked to complete a Likert scale survey. The survey questionnaire incorporates both established instruments from the conceptual framework and adapted versions of existing tools.

The research survey results are analyzed using IBM SPSS statistical software. Given the ordinal nature of the survey data, the Spearman Correlation analysis method is applied to the dataset comprising 107 participants. It is worth noting that some surveys have been subjected to Pearson Correlation Analysis, ensuring the appropriate use of both methods, as documented in the table. The results of the analysis are presented, highlighting Spearman’s correlation coefficient.

The primary units of analysis in this survey are healthcare KPOs involved in healthcare information systems and individuals in managerial positions with a comprehensive understanding of these systems. Additionally, healthcare IT professionals in managerial roles with substantial knowledge of these systems are considered key units of analysis.

A total of 107 respondents participated in the survey, all of whom were professionals working in Sri Lankan healthcare Knowledge Process Outsourcing (KPO) organizations. The sample comprised 58% male and 42% female participants, with an average age of 34 years, ranging from 25 to 55 years. Age distribution indicated that 30% of the respondents were between 25 and 30 years old, 45% were aged 31 to 40 years, and 25% fell within the 41 to 55 age categories. Work experience varied among participants, with 32% having between one to five years of experience, 41% possessing six to ten years, and 27% having more than ten years of professional experience.

The respondents represented various roles within healthcare KPOs, including IT security officers (25%), compliance managers (22%), healthcare administrators (20%), data analysts (18%), and other roles such as finance, human resources, and general management (15%). The organizations surveyed varied in size, with 20% being small-sized KPOs (fewer than 50 employees), 50% classified as medium-sized (50–200 employees), and 30% categorized as large-scale organizations (more than 200 employees). In terms of service provision, 40% of the respondents were employed in Revenue Cycle Management (RCM), 30% in patient data processing, 25% in electronic health records (EHR) management, and the remaining 5% in other specialized healthcare outsourcing services such as medical transcription, billing, and coding.

The diversity of participants across job roles, experience levels, and organizational types ensures a representative sample that captures various perspectives on information security compliance within Sri Lankan healthcare KPOs. By incorporating responses from professionals with distinct expertise and responsibilities, the study provides a nuanced understanding of the factors influencing security compliance behavior within these organizations.

Sri Lanka’s healthcare KPO sector boasts a workforce of approximately 800 employees. To align with the research survey criteria, convenience sampling is employed to select 153 respondents from five healthcare KPOs out of the overall population of 150 individuals. The dataset used in this study was collected from 107 professionals working in Sri Lankan healthcare Knowledge Process Outsourcing (KPO) organizations, including IT security officers, compliance managers, healthcare administrators, and data analysts. This dataset was chosen due to its relevance, diversity, and representativeness in evaluating information security compliance behaviors in a healthcare outsourcing environment.

To justify the appropriateness of the chosen dataset, we compare it with alternative datasets that could have been used but were not selected due to limitations in relevance, scope, or applicability. The selected dataset, consisting of 107 professionals from Sri Lankan healthcare Knowledge Process Outsourcing (KPO) organizations, was chosen due to its direct relevance, industry-specific focus, and practical applicability in evaluating information security compliance behaviors. Compared to alternative datasets, such as international healthcare IT compliance reports, publicly available cybersecurity breach data, and simulated compliance datasets, the chosen dataset most accurately reflects the real-world challenges of security compliance in healthcare KPOs. While global datasets may provide a broader perspective, they do not focus on outsourced healthcare operations, where compliance structures and regulatory enforcement mechanisms differ significantly. Publicly available breach reports, though valuable in understanding historical security failures, fail to capture proactive compliance behaviors and daily security practices followed by employees. Additionally, simulated datasets, while useful for controlled testing, do not account for unpredictable human factors, organizational policies, or evolving security threats in real-world settings. The selected dataset ensures that HIS adoption, security awareness, and regulatory compliance are analyzed within the specific operational context of KPOs, making it the most appropriate and actionable choice for this study.

Discussion

It has been shown that healthcare information systems and information security awareness are positively correlated with compliance with information security policies in the healthcare sector. The remaining hypotheses (H1, H3, H4, and H5) were refuted by statistical correlations because they did not show a strong positive relationship with Healthcare Standards, Security Requirements, Management Involvement, or Organizational System variables.

Upon analysis, it was confirmed that information security awareness and Healthcare Information Systems have a significant positive correlation, validating hypotheses H2 and H6. In addition, none of the other hypotheses in the conceptual framework showed strong positive relationships, including H1, H3, H4, and H5. Due to this, we will consider these hypotheses disproven.

It has been found that research has been conducted along similar lines to help in the discovery of the relationship described in the hypothesis. Kruger et al. [16] demonstrated that security compliance can be positively affected by information security awareness. In their studies [17], observed similar results. Linda L. Dawson [40] mentioned that healthcare data security increases an organization’s overall security.

According to the results of this study, there are several factors that contribute to the compliance of healthcare organizations with respect to their information security. Thus, if an organization wishes to increase its level of compliance with information security standards, it must put an emphasis on securing information systems. In addition, it must raise security awareness among its employees. Increasing the level of security compliance behavior of an organization cannot be achieved solely based on one actor. It is extremely crucial to focus on these factors in order to enhance the culture of security.

Healthcare Information Systems (HIS) adoption (H2) and Information Security Awareness (ISA) (H6) showed a significant positive correlation with information security compliance in healthcare Knowledge Process Outsourcing (KPOs). This suggests that while these two factors play a crucial role in shaping security-compliant behaviors, other variables, such as healthcare standards compliance (H1), security measures (H3), management involvement (H4), and organizational systems (H5), did not demonstrate a statistically significant relationship with compliance. Understanding why these four factors did not emerge as significant is essential for developing a comprehensive perspective on the state of information security in healthcare KPOs.

The strong correlation between Healthcare Information Systems (HIS) and security compliance aligns with previous literature emphasizing the role of technological infrastructure in ensuring data protection [25, 27]. As HIS adoption increases, organizations tend to implement more structured security protocols, automated access controls, and encryption measures, thereby reinforcing compliance with regulatory standards. Moreover, Information Security Awareness (ISA) was found to be a major determinant of compliance, confirming earlier research that highlights the role of continuous security education and training in fostering a culture of compliance [9, 10]. Employees who are regularly trained on security protocols, phishing risks, and data protection measures are more likely to follow established security policies, reducing the risk of breaches.

On the other hand, healthcare standards compliance (H1) did not show a significant correlation with security compliance, which is contrary to expectations. One possible explanation is that merely adhering to formal regulations, such as HIPAA, does not necessarily translate into effective security practices within an organization. Many KPOs may claim to be compliant on paper but lack the operational implementation of security controls that would lead to improved compliance behaviors. This aligns with prior studies that have suggested compliance requirements often become a checkbox exercise rather than an ingrained security culture [22].

Similarly, security measures (H3), which include data encryption, access controls, and incident response protocols, did not have a strong correlation with compliance behaviors. This finding suggests that while technological security measures are crucial, they alone are insufficient in ensuring compliance. Without strong user awareness and proper enforcement mechanisms, security tools may not be effectively utilized or may be bypassed by employees due to convenience, lack of training, or poor organizational oversight [30, 31].

Another unexpected result was the lack of significance for management involvement (H4). While managerial support is often assumed to be a critical factor in fostering security compliance, its impact may be indirect rather than directly measurable through a compliance metric. Organizations where security policies are dictated by top management but not actively reinforced at the operational level may struggle with actual compliance implementation [41]. Furthermore, some KPOs may have decentralized management structures where security decisions are made at different levels, reducing the direct influence of senior management on compliance culture.

Finally, organizational systems (H5), which include overall corporate governance structures and procedural frameworks, did not exhibit a significant correlation with security compliance. This could be attributed to the variability in how security is integrated into organizational culture. While some companies may have structured policies, these frameworks may not be actively monitored or enforced, leading to inconsistencies in compliance [54]. Additionally, in organizations where security is seen as a secondary concern rather than a core business function, compliance behaviors may not be effectively embedded into daily operations [55].

These findings highlight that information security compliance in healthcare KPOs is not solely dependent on formal policies, technological measures, or managerial directives but is significantly driven by user behavior and system adoption. Without a proactive security awareness culture and robust technological integration, even the most stringent regulations and sophisticated security tools may fail to ensure compliance. This underscores the need for organizations to go beyond regulatory mandates and foster a security-first mindset among employees while investing in intuitive and well-integrated security infrastructures.

Future research should explore potential moderating factors that may influence the effectiveness of healthcare standards, security measures, and management involvement in driving compliance. Additionally, qualitative insights from employees and managers could provide further clarity on the organizational challenges that prevent these factors from contributing significantly to security compliance. Understanding these complexities will be essential in designing more effective security strategies tailored to the evolving needs of healthcare KPOs.

Theoretical contributions

This study contributes to the existing body of knowledge on information security compliance in healthcare Knowledge Process Outsourcing (KPO) organizations by integrating multiple theoretical perspectives. It bridges gaps between the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), Organizational Culture Theory, and Information Security Awareness Theory, offering an integrated approach to understanding security compliance beyond isolated theoretical lenses. By examining the interaction between these frameworks, the study enhances the theoretical understanding of security compliance behaviors in outsourced healthcare operations.

Through a quantitative analysis of Sri Lankan healthcare KPOs, the study empirically validates that Healthcare Information Systems (HIS) adoption and Information Security Awareness (ISA) are the primary determinants of security compliance. This reinforces the importance of technological integration and awareness-building programs, supporting previous research while emphasizing their unique role in an outsourcing environment. Additionally, the findings challenge common assumptions that healthcare standards compliance, security measures, management involvement, and organizational systems significantly influence compliance behaviors. Contrary to traditional perspectives that strict regulatory adherence and managerial directives drive compliance, the study highlights that individual awareness and system integration play a more direct role, offering a new dimension to compliance-related research.

Furthermore, this research provides context-specific contributions by shifting the focus from healthcare institutions in developed economies to an outsourcing environment in a developing country. The study examines compliance behaviors within Sri Lankan healthcare KPOs, shedding light on the unique challenges these organizations face in balancing external regulatory demands with internal organizational culture. By identifying HIS adoption and ISA as primary drivers of compliance, the study also presents practical implications for policymakers, healthcare IT leaders, and regulatory bodies. The results suggest that investments in security training programs and the seamless integration of information systems are more effective in enhancing compliance than merely imposing stringent regulations. These insights contribute to both academic research and industry practices, shaping future strategies for improving information security compliance in outsourced healthcare environments.

Research limitations and future work

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged to guide future research. One key limitation is its geographical scope, as the study focuses exclusively on Sri Lankan healthcare KPOs, limiting the generalizability of findings to other outsourcing contexts. Future research should examine compliance behaviors in different regions to determine whether similar patterns exist in varying regulatory and cultural environments. Additionally, the study employs a cross-sectional research design, capturing security compliance behaviors at a single point in time. A longitudinal approach could provide deeper insights into how security awareness and system adoption evolve over time and their sustained impact on compliance behaviors.

Another limitation arises from the study’s reliance on self-reported data, which may introduce social desirability bias, as participants might overstate their compliance levels. Future research should incorporate objective security audits, case studies, or observational methods to validate the reported compliance behaviors. Furthermore, this study does not examine potential moderating factors, such as organizational size, leadership styles, cybersecurity threats, or financial constraints, which could influence security compliance. Future research could explore these factors using moderated mediation models to gain a more nuanced understanding of their impact.

The study also finds that management involvement did not significantly influence security compliance, contrary to expectations. However, this may be due to variations in management engagement across organizations. Future research should investigate whether different leadership approaches, enforcement mechanisms, or security governance models impact compliance more effectively. Finally, the study relies on quantitative survey data, which, while statistically valuable, does not capture the experiences, motivations, or challenges faced by healthcare KPO employees. A mixed-methods approach, incorporating interviews, focus groups, or case studies, could provide richer qualitative insights into the factors influencing security compliance behaviors.

By addressing these limitations, future studies can build on the findings of this research and contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of information security compliance in the healthcare outsourcing sector.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this analysis emphasizes the crucial need for creating a strong culture of following information security rules in healthcare outsourcing. The suggested behaviors and strategies outlined in this study are carefully designed to act as a shield against potential threats. Their goal is to protect sensitive patient data, follow strict regulations, and ensure the overall honesty and privacy of healthcare information.

In an industry where the impacts of data security breaches can be serious and widespread, taking these precautions is not just a good idea—it’s a must. The potential consequences go beyond the immediate breach, affecting the trust of patients and stakeholders and possibly resulting in legal and financial problems. Therefore, actively adopting a strong information security culture isn’t just a precautionary step; it’s a necessary move for healthcare outsourcing companies.

In essence, these strategies not only strengthen data security but also enhance the resilience of healthcare outsourcing operations in the face of challenges. By prioritizing information security compliance, organizations can effectively navigate the complexities of managing healthcare information, safeguard patient safety, and protect the reputation and sustainability of the institutions involved. While this study provides a foundational understanding, comprehensive validity tests were not included due to resource constraints and its exploratory nature. Future research will incorporate more detailed statistical analyses to build on this initial insight.
