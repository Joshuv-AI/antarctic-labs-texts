Multi-scale elastic rock physics template for deep carbonate reservoir parameter prediction: a case study of the Shunbei Oilfield
Authors: Chuantong Ruan, Yuchun Gao, Jingwu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the depletion of conventional oil and gas resources, global attention has shifted to deep unconventional reserves. Carbonate reservoirs account for a substantial portion of worldwide hydrocarbon reserves. However, their heterogeneous pore systems comprising pores, cracks, and vugs pose significant challenges for parameter prediction. Deep carbonate reservoirs exhibit complex pore structures, pronounced heterogeneity, and ambiguous seismic responses. To address these, we integrate the microscopic Gurevich squirt flow model with the mesoscopic Biot–Rayleigh theory, yielding a multi-scale elastic rock physics framework. This framework quantifies interrelations among microcracks, porosity, and P-wave dispersion. It also incorporates effects of fluid viscosity, temperature, and pressure on seismic wave attributes. Using the Voigt-Reuss-Hill average, differential effective medium theory, and Batzle–Wang equations, we construct a multi-scale elastic rock physics template for the Shunbei area, centered on P-to-S-wave velocity ratio and P-wave impedance. By combining this framework with seismic data, the template enables prediction of reservoir porosity and crack porosity. Results demonstrate that the template-based method effectively delineates high-quality reservoirs. Predictions align well with well-test data from three wells, validating the approach.

Introduction

Carbonate reservoirs account for over 60% of global oil reserves and more than 40% of natural gas, highlighting their significance in petroleum exploration1. As exploration efforts progressively target deeper strata (typically > 4500 m), focus shifts from shallow to deep formations. These deep reservoirs exhibit distinct geological and petrophysical characteristics relative to their shallow counterparts, thereby limiting the applicability and accuracy of conventional prediction methodologies2.

Rock physics provides the essential theoretical link between seismic observations and reservoir properties, forming the foundation for quantitative subsurface characterization3. Laboratory measurements directly constrain the elastic behavior and wave propagation characteristics of rocks within the experimental frequency band. Building on Birch’s foundational studies of pressure-dependent P-wave velocities4,5, subsequent investigations have developed and empirically validated theoretical frameworks describing the stress sensitivity of seismic wave speeds6,7. Parallel research has established robust empirical and theoretical relationships between wave velocity, porosity, and thermal conditions8. while additional studies have quantified the impact of temperature on seismic attenuation and microcrack evolution9–11. Collectively, these works demonstrate that elastic velocity and attenuation are strongly governed by in situ pressure, pore architecture, and fluid phase, thereby providing a reliable physical basis for reservoir property prediction.

Early rock physics formulations typically assumed homogeneous pore geometries and described macroscopic, global fluid flow. This model was subsequently refined through the introduction of squirt flow, wherein wave-induced pressure differentials force fluid from compliant, low-aspect-ratio pores into stiffer pore networks, generating measurable dispersion and attenuation12. The squirt-flow framework was later augmented by incorporating grain-contact pore structures13,14 and explicit crack-connectivity parameters15, significantly improving its applicability to fractured reservoirs. To address pronounced heterogeneity, Ba et al. integrated mesoscopic dual-porosity mechanisms into the Biot–Rayleigh model16. More recent extensions have further generalized classical poroelastic theory to accommodate wave propagation in media saturated with viscoelastic, non-Newtonian fluids17.These developments establish a multi-scale theoretical foundation for modeling frequency-dependent seismic responses in complex reservoirs.

Rock physics templates (RPTs) have emerged as a cornerstone of quantitative seismic interpretation. Following their initial formulation18, RPTs have been systematically coupled with elastic moduli and the Biot–Gassmann framework to establish deterministic relationships between petrophysical properties and seismic responses, enabling robust fluid discrimination and porosity estimation19–21. Subsequent applications have extended RPT methodology to characterize thin-bed heterogeneity and fracture intensity22, validate multi-scale carbonate models against production data23, evaluate source-rock organic richness24, identify fluid phases25, detect tight-gas accumulations26, and predict fracture networks using impedance–Poisson’s ratio crossplots27. This broad applicability underscores the utility of RPTs as a bridge between theoretical rock physics and practical reservoir evaluation.

Although data-driven machine learning techniques have seen rapid adoption across geoscience applications28–30, purely empirical approaches often lack physical interpretability and may extrapolate poorly beyond training conditions. In contrast, physics-based rock physics models explicitly encode the governing relationships among elastic properties, pore architecture, fluid characteristics, and seismic wave propagation31.

Carbonate reservoirs are the product of extended diagenetic and tectonic evolution, frequently exhibiting elevated temperatures, intricate pore-network architectures, and strong permeability–storage heterogeneity32. These complexities constrain the reliability of conventional single-model approaches for property prediction33,34. To address this, the present study integrates the microscopic Gurevich squirt flow model and mesoscopic BR theory into a unified multi-scale framework that explicitly incorporates coexisting stiff and compliant pore systems. Using VRH averaging and DEM theory, we develop frequency-dependent elastic rock physics templates linking seismic attributes (P-wave impedance and VP/VS ratio) to total porosity and crack porosity. Calibrated with ultrasonic and seismic data, these templates predict reservoir quality in three Shunbei wells, with predictions consistent with production tests.

Overview of the study area

The study area is located in the Shunbei Oilfield, in Xinjiang’s central-western Tarim Basin, which marks a breakthrough in marine carbonate exploration. With average depths > 7300 m, it features ultra-deep burial, high pressure, and high temperatures, a milestone for Sinopec35. Occupying the Shuntuoguole Low Uplift, it is bordered eastward by the Manjiaer Sag, westward by the Awati Sag, northward by the Shaya Uplift, and southward by the Katak Uplift. The structure trends higher north-east and lower south-west, with gentle relief (Fig. 1). Formed during Caledonian-Hercynian orogeny, it underwent multi-phase faulting, fracturing, and fluid alteration, yielding diverse reservoirs. Lower Paleozoic strata form a complete source-reservoir-cap assemblage36,37, with high hydrocarbon potential. Predominantly carbonate lithology, primary intervals are Ordovician Yijianfang and Yingshan Formations. Tectonics and dissolution control crack, pore, and vug reservoirs.38Fig. 1Structural location of Shunbei oil and gas field in Tarim basin.

This study targets the Shunbei-5 well area (~ 150 km2), where fault-zone breakthroughs occurred recently. The Shunbei-5 exploratory well penetrates the Yingshan Formation in the compressive-shear segment of NW-trending Fault Zone 5, along the Shuntuoguole Low Uplift’s northern margin. Multi-scale rock physics modeling

Rocks consist of a solid matrix and internal pore fluids. The equivalent elastic moduli of rocks primarily include four aspects: the matrix modulus, the dry-frame modulus, the pore-fluid modulus, and physical factors (such as pressure, temperature, and frequency). Rock physics models idealize rocks under assumptions, establishing mechanical relations among moduli. For deep carbonates’ high-temperature/pressure and complex pores, we develop a multi-scale framework to characterize properties and investigate wave responses under varying conditions.

BR equations

The BR equations integrate Biot’s dual-phase theory with Rayleigh’s bubble equation, forming a dual-porosity wave model for pores differing in porosity, permeability, and compressibility. The dominant pores form the background; minorities are inclusions. Compressibility differences induce inter-pore fluid flow during wave propagation. Rock pore structure is commonly idealized as a dual-pore system comprising intergranular (stiff) pores and interconnected microcracks (soft pores), which exhibit distinct geometrical and mechanical properties. To align with the dual-frame, single-fluid formulation of the BR model and enable seamless coupling with the Gurevich squirt-flow model41, this study adopts a configuration in which the background phase consists solely of stiff pores and the inclusions represent soft pores.

Gurevich model

Pore types in rocks exhibit varying elastic compliances. During wave propagation in heterogeneous media, stiff pores induce minimal fluid pressures, whereas compliant microcracks deform readily, yielding elevated pressures. This differential induces squirt flow42,43, resulting in wave dispersion and attenuation. Building on the Mavko–Jizba framework, Gurevich et al.13 introduced a frequency-dependent squirt flow model that distinguishes between fluid-saturated cracks and dry stiff pores by incorporating crack aspect ratio into the effective frame modulus. In this formulation, cracks are embedded within an otherwise dry, stiff matrix, and their presence modifies the bulk and shear moduli of the equivalent dry rock frame. Here, Kfl denotes the fluid bulk modulus, while J0 and J1 represent the zero-order and first-order Bessel functions of the first kind, respectively.

We refer to K* f as the effective fluid modulus. By substituting the fluid bulk modulus Kfl with K* f, which is derived from Kfl, we ultimately obtain the equivalent frame bulk and shear moduli of the modified rock, where the soft pores are fluid-saturated and the stiff pores remain dry. BRG theory

Similar to the BR model, the background phase primarily contains stiff pores, whereas the inclusions are composed predominantly of soft pores. To facilitate integration with the Gurevich model, a minimal number of stiff pores are are added to the inclusions and the Gurevich model is used to calculate the equivalent dry-frame modulus of the inclusions. The schematic diagram of the multi-scale rock physics model is shown in Fig. 3. The Gurevich squirt flow model assumes a uniform crack aspect ratio, under which smaller cracks close completely at high confining pressure. This simplification limits its applicability in heterogeneous media. To facilitate integration with the mesoscale BR framework, this study adopts context-specific equivalent crack aspect ratios: a larger value for the bulk rock matrix (α) and a smaller one for soft-pore inclusions (α₂). Under global rock pressurization, inclusion cracks close gradually, yielding α ≈ 102 α₂; conversely, direct inclusion loading accelerates closure, corresponding to the reduced α₂.Fig. 3Schematic diagram of the combination of Gurevich theory and BR model.

To delineate wave propagation in complex poroelastic media, we incorporate squirt flow mechanisms within inclusions and propose the multi-scale BRG framework, tailored for heterogeneous pore architectures. This model unifies macroscopic Biot theory (quantifying fluid-elastic wave coupling), mesoscopic Rayleigh theory (modeling heterogeneity-induced local flow), and microscopic Gurevich theory (simulating squirt flow dynamics) into a cohesive system. The combination enables comprehensive quantification of elastic wave dispersion and attenuation across the full frequency bandwidth in porous systems. Leveraging BRG, we develop a multi-scale rock physics template that explains the impact of various pore morphologies, including fractures, vugs, and intergranular voids in deep carbonate reservoirs on seismic attributes, thereby facilitating quantitative inversion of reservoir parameters such as porosity and crack porosity.

Calibration in the ultrasonic frequency band

The rock physics template was calibrated against laboratory measurements prior to quantitative reservoir inversion. A limestone core sample representative of the target reservoir lithology39 was selected. Ultrasonic pulse-transmission experiments were conducted at 1 MHz under stepwise increasing confining pressures to quantify crack-content effects on elastic-wave propagation. Sample properties were: porosity is 2.5%, permeability is 0.023 mD, and bulk density is 2.66 g/cm3. Tests were performed at a fixed pore pressure of 10 MPa and temperature of 140 °C. For the oil-saturated case, the core was oven-dried, jacketed in the ultrasonic apparatus, and saturated with kerosene to the target pore pressure. Confining pressures were applied sequentially (20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, and 50 MPa), and waveforms recorded. P- and S-wave velocities (Vp, Vs) were derived from first-arrival traveltimes. Crack porosity was inverted from the measured Vp and Vs using the method of Zhang et al.48, yielding crack porosity and density distributions. The analysis focused on crack-development effects on wave propagation. Figure 8 illustrates the variation of crack porosity with confining pressure, as well as the relationships between crack porosity and Vp, and Vs. Results indicate that Crack porosity decreases monotonically with increasing confining pressure, while Vp and Vs decline and attenuation increases with rising Crack porosity. Conclusions

This study addresses the prediction of reservoir parameters in ultra-deep carbonate reservoirs, characterized by extreme burial depths (> 7 km), strong heterogeneity, and complex multi-scale pore systems. A multi-scale rock physics framework was developed by integrating microscopic Gurevich squirt-flow theory, mesoscopic BR dual-porosity theory, VRH averaging, DEM modeling, and Batzle–Wang fluid equations into a unified saturated single-fluid BRG model. The framework accurately describes elastic-wave propagation and dispersion across the full frequency spectrum in heterogeneous porous media, tailored to the geological complexities of deep Chinese carbonate reservoirs. Ultrasonic (1 MHz) laboratory data and seismic (~ 35 Hz) field data were used to calibrate frequency-dependent elastic rock physics templates (RPTs) based on Vp/Vs and P-wave impedance (Ip). These templates quantify total porosity and crack porosity. The calibrated multi-scale RPT library was applied to 2D seismic profiles and 3D volumes in the Shunbei area, yielding quantitative predictions of porosity and crack porosity. Results show strong agreement with well-test data and production rates from three wells, effectively delineating high-quality reservoir zones and supporting optimal well placement.

The proposed BRG framework offers a multi-scale approach for characterizing deep carbonate reservoirs, the following limitations should be acknowledged in practical applications: (1) Pore space is idealized as stiff pores and uniform-aspect-ratio cracks, whereas natural systems exhibit continuous crack-geometry distributions. (2) The scale-separated formulation assumes localized fluid exchange, restricting applicability to reservoirs where crack porosity is substantially lower than total porosity. (3) Calibration was limited to ultrasonic and seismic frequencies, excluding logging-band responses. (4) Thermomechanical effects on fluid properties are included via the Batzle–Wang equation, but dynamic coupling to evolving crack geometry and density remains unaddressed.

Future work should (1) implement pressure-dependent, continuous aspect-ratio distributions; (2) incorporate dual-matrix/dual-fluid formulations for complex pore–fluid systems; (3) integrate thermally induced cracking mechanisms; (4) develop hybrid physics-informed machine learning workflows to enhance predictive accuracy; and (5) advance multi-attribute inversion schemes that jointly constrain dispersion, attenuation, and ancillary field data to reduce non-uniqueness. Subsequent efforts will emphasize quantitative cross-plot analysis, R2 calculation, and uncertainty assessment using a larger set of well data. In addition, the proposed method will be extended to additional field blocks to obtain statistically robust validation metrics.
