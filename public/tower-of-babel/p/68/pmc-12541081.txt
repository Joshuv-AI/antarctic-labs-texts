Numerically induced attentional biases in horizontal, vertical, and two-dimensional shapes
Authors: Ryo Hishiya, Masami Ishihara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious studies have demonstrated that numerical magnitudes can induce attentional biases mainly in a horizontal space. The present study aimed to clarify spatial-numerical association in horizontal, vertical, and two-dimensional square stimuli composed of visually aligned strings of relatively smaller Arabic numerals (i.e., 1 or 2) or larger numerals (i.e., 8 or 9). Neurologically and psychiatrically healthy participants, all of whom were Japanese speakers, were instructed to identify the veridical center of the stimuli. The results indicated that, with horizontal stimuli, participants placed their subjective midpoint farther left when smaller numbers were presented compared to larger numbers, consistent with the predicted left-to-right mental number line. With vertical stimuli, however, smaller numbers induced biases upwards, inconsistent with the predicted bottom-to-top representation. For the square, the stimuli with number strings elicited biases with a stronger vertical component, whereas the stimuli without number strings elicited biases with a stronger horizontal component. The upward biases for the square stimuli with number strings may reflect the activation of object-based processing rather than the cognitive function of numerical processing. These findings suggest that distinct mechanisms may predominate over spatial-numerical associations with regard to the biases in the two-dimensional plane.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

The association between space and numerical information has been repeatedly observed in studies on human information processing. Analyses of reaction times obtained from experiments with binary response buttons have shown that individuals recruited from left-to-right reading and writing cultures exhibit faster responses to a presented smaller number when pressing the left side button compared to when pressing the right side button. Meanwhile, they exhibit an opposite response pattern for a larger number1,2. This phenomenon is well-known as the Spatial-Numerical Association of Response Codes (SNARC) effect. It suggests the existence of a left-to-right spatial property of number magnitude in human internal representation (i.e., mental number line)1. Akin to such a spatial-numerical association, ordinal information have also shown a spatially characterized correspondence3,4. Moreover, SNARC-like effects have been observed with non-numerical magnitude, including luminance5,6, pitch7,8, loudness9, and weight10. Recently, studies have even attempted to explore spatial correspondences of risks11,12. These findings suggest that a number of magnitude-related information have spatial characteristics.

These space-numerical associations have also been observed in studies of newly enrolled first-grade children13, preschool-aged children14–17, prelinguistic infants18,19, and even newborns20,21. Furthermore, behavioral evidence indicates that chimpanzees22, gorillas, orangutans23, rhesus monkeys24, Clark’s nutcrackers25, chicks26–28, and even bees29 are also likely to exhibit an association between space and quantity. The observation that both humans and non-humans demonstrate a left-to-right spatial mapping of magnitude-related information suggests that a mental number line may be an inherent functional characteristic.

However, human spatial representations of numerical information are susceptible to cultural conventions. For example, individuals from right-to-left reading and writing cultures show reversed mental number lines compared to those observed in left-to-right reading and writing cultures1. Additionally, in bilinguals, the strength of the associations between space and number varied depending on the presented language30. Notably, in some cultures around the world, individuals are exposed to a variety of reading and writing directions in daily life. For example, Japanese speakers can use mainly two directional patterns in their reading and writing. One of the writing and reading directions in Japanese is to write horizontally from left to right and move from top to bottom to keep reading on a new line, which is the same as when reading an English text. Another pattern of reading/writing direction is vertically oriented from top to bottom and move from right to left when beginning on a new line. Despite such unique cultural conventions, Japanese speakers exhibited a left-to-right spatial representation in their manual responses to the parity judgement for Arabic numerals, akin to Western people31. These findings suggest that the spatial representation of numerical information in Japanese speakers aligns with what has been typically observed in Western culture. However, studies in which Chinese speakers participated have shown that differences in notation (Arabic numerals or Chinese characters) can influence the strength and/or direction of spatial-numerical associations32,33, implying the importance of considering cultural characteristics in studies of numerical cognition.

Approaches to clarify the mechanism of the spatial-numerical association are not limited to reaction time tasks applying binary assigned response buttons. As one of the approaches, for example, the line bisection task has also been employed to examine the perceptual-motor characteristics concerning the association between spatial and numerical information. The line bisection task, a well-established neuropsychological test, has been employed to elucidate spatial-attentional function in neurological patients34. Specifically, when patients with right hemisphere damage attempt to bisect a given physical horizontal line, their subjective midpoints are typically biased to the right side of the true midpoint35,36. This rightward bias indicates the presence of a left hemispace neglect, suggesting that these patients have also difficulty attending to the left hemispace of their body midline. In contrast, the subjective midpoints determined by neurologically healthy individuals tend to be slightly biased to the left side of the true midpoint37. This leftward bias was first referred to as “pseudoneglect” by Bowers & Heilman (1980)38, which has been interpreted to imply that visuospatial attention is predominantly controlled in the right hemisphere. Interestingly, according to a study of animal behavior, chicks and pigeons have also exhibited pseudoneglect-like behavioral tendencies, showing a preference for the left hemispace when they were allowed to freely peck at food39. In humans, the underlying mechanism of attentional control may be related to the speed of visuospatial processing, which is modulated by anatomical lateralization40. Additionally, according to the EEG study, the amplitude of the event-related potential (N1) in the right hemisphere was correlated with biases in the landmark task41. A further study reported by Bjoertomt et al. (2002) also demonstrated that repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) applied to the right posterior parietal region induced a shift in the perceived midpoint of a horizontal line presented in near peripersonal space42. These neurological findings suggest that the functional asymmetry in hemispheric specialization, particularly the role of the right hemisphere, may contribute to the occurrence of pseudoneglect.

Studies of the line bisection task have the potential to provide evidence not only concerning functional characteristics of visuospatial attention but also those of the association between spatial and numerical information. For example, when the line to be bisected was composed of strings of Arabic numerals, the participants’ subjective midpoint was shifted depending on the numerical magnitude, despite the numerical information being irrelevant to the task of line bisection43. This modulation of the participants’ subjective midpoint aligns with the left-to-right orientation of the mental number line. This shift by numerical magnitude occurred even when the stimuli were a line composed of words meaning “2” or “9” in French44 or when numbers were presented at both ends of a line43,45. Likewise, it has been reported that both in adults and in children around 5 years old, biases can be induced by presenting magnitudes through non-symbolic dots positioned at both ends of a line14. Additionally, in the grayscale task, when participants were required to determine which of two vertically aligned stimuli was darker, smaller numbers presented onto the grayscale stimulus also induced a greater leftward bias relative to larger numbers46. Thus, numerical magnitudes presented visually could shift the perceived midpoint of lines. Moreover, when patients with left hemispace neglect were asked to identify the midpoint between two auditorily presented numbers, their responses tended to be biased to the right side from the true midpoint, suggesting hemispatial neglect in the representational horizontal space47,48. This rightward bias observed in the patients is a similar phenomenon to what is observed in physical line bisection tasks, and neurologically healthy individuals also exhibited a similar directional bias when comparing the performance of physical horizontal lines and mental number lines49. Furthermore, magnetic stimulation to the right posterior parietal cortex induced a shift in the midpoint of mental number estimation, suggesting that this region is involved in the spatial representation of numbers50, akin to the finding for the landmark task by Bjoertomt et al. (2002)42. These findings can be considered as supporting evidence for a framework of A Theory Of Magnitude (ATOM), which posits that magnitude-related and spatial information share common perceptomotor processes within a given environment51.

Akin to findings in the horizontal direction, behavioral evidence has demonstrated the presence of SNARC effects in the vertical direction52–54, suggesting that numerical information should be associated with the space even in the vertical dimension (i.e., a bottom-to-top mental number line). This bottom-to-top representation was observed even among Japanese individuals, who have a unique reading and writing culture (cf., two directional patterns; see text above for more details), in the parity judgement task with a binary response31. In addition, during the processing of auditorily presented smaller numbers, individuals’ gaze tended to shift downward relative to the condition of larger numbers in magnitude comparison tasks55. Compared to the reported spatial-numerical associations in the horizontal plane, however, evidence about a bottom-to-top mental number line is not always consistent. For example, the upward bias was not observed in the vertical mental number line task, in which three numbers were sequentially presented on images designed to evoke representations of either a vertical or horizontal axis, and participants were asked to judge whether the first or second interval was smaller or larger56. Additionally, in a study in which participants were asked to identify the location where a number stimulus was presented by relying on their memory after the stimulus had disappeared, the horizontal deviation from the true location was biased depending on the numerical magnitude, whereas no vertical bias was observed57. Given the discrepancy between each task on the emergence of vertical spatial-numerical associations, further investigations into the mental number line of vertical direction are needed. Distinct mechanisms of visuospatial processing concerning the horizontal and vertical axes may probably be involved in such differential patterns observed in spatial-numerical associations.

With respect to the vertical line bisection task, akin to the bias observed in the horizontal line bisection task (i.e., pseudoneglect), an upward bias (i.e., altitudinal bias) has been reported when participants are required to bisect presented vertical lines58,59, and also in the landmark task60. On the one hand, the mechanism underlying altitudinal bias is supposed to involve the ventral stream of visual processing. This assumption is supported by findings showing that the object-based task (associated with the ventral visual stream) induced an upward bias for the vertical line bisection task, even though the spatial-based task (associated with the dorsal visual stream) did not60. Likewise, attentional biases were shifted upward in the facial memory task, which was also intended to activate the ventral visual stream, compared to other tasks, such as the spatial memory task designed to activate the dorsal visual stream61. The ventral and dorsal visual pathways have been demonstrated to possess functionally distinct characteristics in human perceptual-behavioral processing62,63. The two visual pathways may be respectively specialized for processing in the upper and lower visual fields64. Thus, activation of the ventral visual stream is possibly related to the occurrence of altitudinal biases. On the other hand, the functional asymmetry in hemispheric specialization, particularly the lateralization of the right hemisphere, may also be involved in altitudinal biases, akin to the occurrence of horizontal biases. One possible explanation for visuospatial biases observed in the horizontal dimension is that attentional distribution tends to be biased towards the contralateral side of the more activated hemisphere (i.e., activation-orientation hypothesis)65,66. It has been reported that the biases observed in the line bisection task can be shifted by inducing spatial attention through prior cues before the presentation of the lines65 or by manipulating the visual field in which the line is presented37,66,67. From the perspective of these findings, supporting the activation-orientation hypothesis, it was also suggested that visuospatial attention tends to be more biased towards the left hemispace when the right hemisphere is asymmetrically activated relative to the left hemisphere. For both horizontal and vertical lines, a previous study revealed that no significant differences with respect to brain activities in fMRI measurements were found during the landmark task for lines of each dimension. Specifically, the right inferior parietal cortex (rIPC) was activated regardless of line direction68. Given this finding, it is also possible to assume that there is a common mechanism in the rIPC underlying visuospatial judgment for both horizontal and vertical dimensions. However, upward biases were found to increase when vertical lines were presented in the right visual field or participants responded with their right hand59,60. These results show contradictory evidence to the assumption that the right hemisphere is involved in the occurrence of upward bias. Presumably, upward biases are linked to the activation of the left hemisphere rather than the right hemisphere, dissimilar to the functional mechanism when the leftward biases are observed.

Furthermore, studies considering different axes also suggest a dissociation between the mechanism of leftward biases and that of upward biases. For example, when horizontal lines were presented in the lower visual field, participants’ subjective midpoints tended to exhibit stronger leftward biases compared to when presented in the upper visual field67. This pattern of leftward bias may reflect that the dorsal visual stream, which is likely to be responsible for processing the stimuli in the lower visual field64, is preferentially linked to the function of the right hemisphere. However, the phenomenon that horizontal biases were modulated by the vertical positions of line presentation appears to be influenced by the time duration of stimulus presentation (for a long duration, stronger leftward biases in the lower visual field; for a short duration, stronger leftward biases in the upper visual field)69. Thus, the mechanism of bias variations induced by vertical position remains unclear. Similar bias variations have also been observed in the horizontal line bisection manipulated in the place of the radial direction (i.e., near peripersonal vs. far extrapersonal). Subjective midpoints for horizontal lines tended to exhibit leftward biases when the lines were presented in near peripersonal space, whereas those tended to exhibit rightward biases when the lines were presented in far extrapersonal space49,70,71. These findings have also been interpreted to suggest that the processing of stimuli in the near peripersonal space, as well as in the lower visual field, may activate the right hemisphere by linking to the processing of the dorsal visual pathway, resulting in the emergence of leftward biases71. When the stimuli are displayed in far extrapersonal space, the left hemisphere may be dominant over the right hemisphere due to the activation of the ventral visual pathways, resulting in the emergence of rightward biases.

To elucidate visuospatial attentional function, most studies of the line bisection task have examined horizontal and vertical biases independently, by employing one-dimensional stimuli. However, one previous study concluded that the mechanisms underlying the horizontal and vertical biases are distinct by investigating them in two-dimensional planes such as the square, diamond, and circle stimuli72. The study demonstrated no significant correlation between deviations of horizontal and vertical line bisection. In addition, the leftward bias was modulated by the aspect ratio of the square, whereas the upward bias remained unaffected72. More specifically, when participants bisected the rectangles, the biases gradually shifted to the left as the aspect ratio of the rectangles approached 100% (i.e., a square). For the inconsistent tendency across two dimensions, the authors concluded that distinct mechanisms underlie the biases in each dimension72. These studies give rise to a new research question: Are functional differences in the mechanism underlying visuospatial attention between horizontal and vertical dimensions related to the differences in the manifestation of spatial-numerical associations observed along these dimensions? However, to our knowledge, by inducing visual attention with strings composed of numbers like the study by Fischer (2001)43, no studies have examined spatial bias for vertical lines or two-dimensional planes to directly compare horizontal and vertical spatial-numerical associations. To solve the above-mentioned issue, employing the line bisection task or a similar task that requires identifying the subjective center has the advantage of examining the influence of spatial representations of numbers on visuospatial attention, without the influence of binary response demands.

Then, the present study aims to examine the association between numerical magnitude and spatial representation by employing horizontal, vertical, and two-dimensional stimuli (i.e., a square) composed of strings of Arabic numerals (i.e., 1, 2, 8, or 9), which were presented in a within arm’s reach (i.e., viewing distance of 45 cm) (Fig. 1). Besides the stimuli with the number strings, two types of stimuli without the number strings serving as a control were also employed: one with a black background (i.e., dark stimulus), and the other with a grey background (i.e., light stimulus). The light stimulus was included to compare the influence of luminance differences on biases with the dark stimulus because the luminance of the stimuli with the numbers varied depending on the shape of the numbers. Participants in the present experiment were asked to identify the center of these stimuli, and deviations between their subjective center and the veridical center were analyzed. The fundamental hypothesis of our study is that horizontal and vertical biases will be modulated by the magnitude of the presented numbers. Smaller numbers (i.e., 1 and 2) are expected to induce biases to the left or lower directions, whereas larger numbers (i.e., 8 and 9) are expected to induce biases to the right or upper directions, regardless of the stimulus shape. In particular, using two-dimensional stimuli allows us to make a direct comparison of the strength of the spatial-numerical association across both dimensions (i.e., horizontal and vertical axes). However, the horizontal bias, unlike the vertical bias, may not be observed because the aspect ratio of the two-dimensional stimuli used in the present study was 100%, which is expected to reduce a leftward bias72. If the aspect ratio effect emerges, then the mechanisms underlying both dimensional biases will be discussed from the perspective of the exploratory correlation analysis between horizontal and vertical deviations within individuals.

The horizontal stimuli measured 4 mm in height and 130 mm in width, and the vertical stimuli measured 130 mm in height and 4 mm in width. The square stimuli were 130 mm on each side. In each figure as an example of a stimulus with the number, strings of “9” are displayed within a white border. In addition, further behavioral evidence of the spatial-numerical association has shown a possibility that the spatial coding of numbers may be required to be at a certain time. In studies on classical stimulus-response compatibility effects, such as the Simon effect, the compatibility effect was found to be greater for faster responses when the trials of participants’ responses were grouped into bins based on their response speed73,74. Conversely, in the SNARC effect, the compatibility effect was either stable across response speed bins in the parity judgment task73,74 or stronger for slower responses than for faster responses in the magnitude comparison task73. Additionally, when two numbers with different magnitudes were presented at each end of a line, biases in the line bisection task were elicited by the numerical magnitude only when participants were instructed to deliberately and accurately bisect the line, which is designed for them to rely on “vision for perception”75. This variation of subjective midpoints did not occur when participants performed ballistic pointing to bisect the line, which is designed to rely on “vision for action”75. These findings suggest that a certain temporal period may be required for numerical information to be associated with spatial representation2,76. Moreover, there is an argument that the influence of numerical information on a manual pointing movement may occur during the motor preparation stage77 or the motor execution stage78. Thus, the time of participants’ responses is possibly involved in the expression of spatial-numerical association. It is then assumed that participants who respond more slowly in each centering task will show stronger spatial-numerical associations compared to those who show faster responses. Following this assumption, the differences in subjective midpoints between the stimuli with smaller or larger numbers may be greater in participants who responded more slowly.

Lastly, previous studies have reported that the starting position before bisecting the line (i.e., the direction towards the veridical midpoint of the presented line) modulated biases79,80. Specifically, leftward biases increased when participants started their hand movement for line bisection from the right side79, and upward biases increased when participants’ hands moved towards the upper field80. In addition, as discussed earlier, the visual fields where the stimulus is presented also influence biases, and participants may attempt to identify the center of the stimuli by relying on clues such as dust on the display if the stimuli were continuously displayed at the same position. Accordingly, the starting positions (i.e., mouse positions) and stimulus positions in the present study were respectively varied across one of four quadrants (i.e., upper-left, upper-right, lower-left, lower-right) from the veridical center of the display by referring to the previous studies72 (see details in Methods and Materials section).

Results

A total of 37 participants (24 females; mean age = 18.97 years, SD = 0.90 years), including four left-handed participants (one female), completed three counterbalanced tasks: the horizontal centering task, the vertical centering task, and the square centering task. All participants reported that they had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological and psychiatric illnesses. Participants performed each task by moving a continuously visible mouse cursor and clicking the mouse button with either their left or right hand, depending on the trial block, for a total of 576 trials across six experimental blocks (96 trials per block: 6 stimulus types × 4 stimulus positions × 4 starting positions). Trial exclusion criteria on the basis of the coordinate and response time (see the Statistical Analysis section) determined the exclusion of 3.2% of trials in the horizontal centering task, 2.8% of trials in the vertical centering task, and 3.8% of trials in the square centering task. Descriptive statistics for each stimulus type are summarized in the table (Supplementary information).

Horizontal deviations in the horizontal centering task

In the integrated data of all stimulus types, when participants used their left hand, the deviation from the veridical midpoint showed a leftward bias [M = -0.73 mm, SD = 1.70 mm] and this bias was observed in 62.2% of the participants. A one-sample t-test showed that the deviation was significantly different from zero [t (36) = 2.61, p < .05, d = 0.428]. In contrast, when participants used their right hand, the deviation was not significantly different from zero [M = -0.32 mm, SD = 1.59 mm; t (36) = 1.24, p = .22, d = 0.204], and the proportion of participants who exhibited a leftward bias was 56.8%.

The left panel of Fig. 2 illustrates the deviations in the horizontal centering task. A two-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted on the horizontal deviations with response hand (i.e., left and right) and numerical magnitude (i.e., smaller numbers and larger numbers) as within-participants factors. The analysis showed a significant main effect of the response hand [F (1, 36) = 21.50, p < .001, η2p = 0.374], indicating that the subjective midpoints were positioned more to the left side of a given line when participants used their left hand [M = -0.66 mm, SD = 1.78 mm] compared to those when they used their right hand [M = -0.18 mm, SD = 1.66 mm]. A significant main effect of the numerical magnitude was also found [F (1, 36) = 13.12, p < .001, η2p = 0.267], indicating that the deviations for the smaller number stimuli [M = -0.55 mm, SD = 1.73 mm] exhibited greater leftward biases compared to those for the larger number stimuli [M = -0.29 mm, SD = 1.69 mm]. Thus, as expected, participants positioned their subjective midpoint for the smaller number stimulus at more left side relative to that of the larger number stimulus. No significant interaction was observed between the response hand and the numerical magnitude [F (1, 36) = 1.08, p = .31, η2p = 0.029] (Fig. 2).

In addition, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted on the horizontal deviations with response hand (i.e., left and right) and luminance (i.e., dark and light) as within-participants factors. The analysis showed a marginally significant main effect of the response hand [F (1, 36) = 3.59, p = .07, η2p = 0.091; left hand: M = -0.87 mm, SD = 1.71 mm; right hand: M = -0.63 mm, SD = 1.59 mm], and no significant main effect of the luminance was observed [F (1, 36) = 1.27, p = .27, η2p = 0.034; dark: M = -0.70 mm, SD = 1.57 mm; light: M = -0.79 mm, SD = 1.68 mm]. Thus, no modulation of biases due to the luminance factor was observed. No significant interaction was observed between the response hand and the luminance [F (1, 36) = 0.68, p = .42, η2p = 0.018] (Fig. 2).

Vertical deviations in the vertical centering task

In the integrated data of all stimulus types, the deviation from the veridical midpoint showed an upward bias even when participants used either their left or right hand [left: M = 3.07 mm, SD = 2.47 mm; right: M = 2.67 mm, SD = 2.39 mm]. The proportion of participants who exhibited an upward bias was 89.2% in each hand. A one-sample t-test revealed that the deviations with both hands were significantly different from zero [left: t (36) = 7.55, p < .001, d = 1.241; right: t (36) = 6.79, p < .001, d = 1.117].

The left panel of Fig. 3 illustrates the deviations in the vertical centering task. A two-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted on the vertical deviations, with the same design as the horizontal centering task, to examine the influence of numerical magnitude. The analysis showed a marginally significant main effect of the response hand [F (1, 36) = 3.69, p = .06, η2p = 0.093], indicating that the subjective midpoints tended to be positioned more to the upper side of a given line when participants used their left hand [M = 3.15 mm, SD = 2.57 mm] compared to those when they used their right hand [M = 2.78 mm, SD = 2.36 mm]. A significant main effect of the numerical magnitude was also found [F (1, 36) = 10.92, p < .01, η2p = 0.233], indicating that the deviations for the smaller number stimuli [M = 3.06 mm, SD = 2.40 mm] elicited greater upward biases compared to those for the larger number stimuli [M = 2.86 mm, SD = 2.42 mm]. Thus, contrary to our expectations, participants positioned their subjective midpoint of the smaller number stimulus more upper side relative to that of the larger number stimulus. No significant interaction was observed between the response hand and the numerical magnitude [F (1, 36) = 0.23, p = .64, η2p = 0.006] (Fig. 3).

The analysis showed a significant main effect of the response hand [F (1, 36) = 4.80, p < .05, η2p = 0.118; left hand: M = 2.92 mm, SD = 2.36 mm; right hand: M = 2.44 mm, SD = 2.53 mm], but no significant main effect of the luminance was observed [F (1, 36) = 0.00, p = .99, η2p = 0.000; dark: M = 2.68 mm, SD = 2.33 mm; light: M = 2.68 mm, SD = 2.40 mm]. Thus, no modulation of biases due to luminance was observed again. Horizontal and vertical deviations in the square centering task

In the integrated data across all stimulus types, when participants used their left hand, the deviation from the veridical center showed a tendency of leftward bias [M = -0.47 mm, SD = 1.52 mm], and this bias was observed in 70.3% of the participants. When participants used their right hand, the deviation also tended to be biased to the left side [M = -0.26 mm, SD = 1.40 mm], and the proportion of those who exhibited a leftward bias was 59.5%. A one-sample t-test on the deviation showed no significant difference from zero for either hand [left: t (36) = 1.88, p = .07, d = 0.309; right: t (36) = 1.11, p = .28, d = 0.182].

Next, the right panel of Fig. 3 illustrates the vertical deviations in the square centering task. For the vertical deviations, the analysis showed neither significant main effect of the response hand [F (1, 36) = 2.30, p = .14, η2p = 0.060; left hand: M = 1.89 mm, SD = 1.95 mm; right hand: M = 1.60 mm, SD = 2.00 mm] nor significant main effect of the numerical magnitude was also observed [F (1, 36) = 0.37, p = .55, η2p = 0.010; smaller number: M = 1.73 mm, SD = 1.92 mm; larger number: M = 1.76 mm, SD = 1.87 mm]. Furthermore, no significant interaction was observed between the response hand and the numerical magnitude [F (1, 36) = 2.21, p = .15, η2p = 0.058] (Fig. 3). In addition, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted on the vertical deviations with the same design as those for the luminance factor. The analysis showed a significant main effect of the response hand [F (1, 36) = 7.98, p < .01, η2p = 0.181; left hand: M = 1.20 mm, SD = 1.85 mm; right hand: M = 0.75 mm, SD = 2.02 mm], and no significant main effect of the luminance was observed [F (1, 36) = 0.09, p = .76, η2p = 0.003; dark: M = 0.96 mm, SD = 1.92 mm; light: M = 0.99 mm, SD = 1.89 mm]. No significant interaction was observed between the response hand and the luminance [F (1, 36) = 0.35, p = .59, η2p = 0.010] (Fig. 2).

In the integrated data across all stimulus types, the deviation from the veridical center showed an upward bias even when participants used either their left or right hand [left: M = 1.66 mm, SD = 1.81 mm; right: M = 1.31 mm, SD = 1.88 mm]. The proportion of participants who exhibited an upward bias with the left and right hand was 86.5% and 70.3%, respectively. A one-sample t-test showed that the deviations with both hands were significantly different from zero [left: t (36) = 5.57, p < .001, d = 0.916; right: t (36) = 4.23, p < .001, d = 0.695].

The above-described analyses on the effect of numerical magnitude and luminance were separately performed. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the coordinates of the subjective center appeared to differ between the stimuli with number strings (i.e., small number and large number stimuli) and those without number strings (i.e., dark and light stimuli). To further examine this difference, the data across the response hand factor and numerical magnitude or luminance factor were merged. Then, paired-sample t-tests were conducted to compare the mean coordinate positions between the stimuli with number strings and those without number strings. The results showed significant differences in both the x-axis and y-axis coordinates [x-axis: t (36) = 4.96, p < .001, d = 0.815; y-axis: t (36) = 3.47, p < .01, d = 0.570]. Taken together, for the square centering task, the stimuli without number strings exhibited deviations with a stronger horizontal component compared to the stimuli with number strings, while the stimuli with number strings exhibited deviations with a stronger vertical component compared to the stimuli without number strings.

The analysis showed that no significant correlation was observed between horizontal deviations in the horizontal centering task and vertical deviations in the vertical centering task [r = − .06, p = .71] (Fig. 5A). In contrast, a significant correlation was found between horizontal deviations in the horizontal and square centering tasks [r = .76, p < .001] (Fig. 5B). Additionally, a paired sample t-test was conducted on horizontal deviations of the horizontal centering task and those of the square centering task, indicating no significant difference [t (36) = 0.92, p = .37, d = 0.151]. Likewise, the correlation analysis showed that a significant correlation was observed between vertical deviations in the vertical and square centering task [r = .79, p < .001] (Fig. 5C). Relationship between response speed and spatial-numerical association

Firstly, a one-way repeated measures ANOVA was performed on the response time with the shape factor (i.e., horizontal, vertical, and square). When the sphericity assumption (Mauchly’s test) was violated, corrections were applied using the Greenhouse-Geisser method81,82. The analysis showed a significant difference in response time depending on the shape [horizontal: M = 1.66 s, SD = 0.43 s; vertical: M = 1.67 s, SD = 0.45 s; square: M = 1.86 s, SD = 0.69 s; F (2, 72) = 6.13, ε = 0.60, p < .05, η2p = 0.146]. Post hoc analysis with Bonferroni correction revealed that the response time for the square stimulus was significantly longer than that for the horizontal stimulus [t (36) = 2.69, p < .05, d = 0.380], and was marginally significantly longer compared to that of the vertical stimulus [t (36) = 2.43, p = .06, d = 0.363]. These results indicate that participants take more time to identify the veridical center of the square (i.e., 2D space) compared to that of the horizontal (and possibly vertical) line (i.e., 1D space).

To investigate whether the effect of numerical magnitude on biases differed depending on response speed, participants were categorized into three groups according to their response speed in each task by applying the criteria of their 33rd and 67th percentiles: slow (n = 12), middle (n = 13), fast (n = 12). Since the ranking of response speed was inconsistent between left-hand and right-hand responses, the categorization was conducted separately for left-hand and right-hand response speed. The difference in numerical magnitude was then calculated by subtracting the deviations for smaller numbers from those for larger numbers. Positive values indicated that the mean subjective midpoint for larger numbers was positioned more rightward or superior compared to that for smaller numbers.

For horizontal deviations in the horizontal centering task, a one-way ANOVA performed on response speed (i.e., slow, middle, and fast) as a between-participants factor showed no significant differences for the left hand [F (2, 34) = 1.82, p = .18, η2p = 0.097] or the right hand [F (2, 34) = 0.48, p = .62, η2p = 0.027]. For vertical deviations in the vertical centering task, the analysis showed no significant difference for the left hand [F (2, 34) = 1.26, p = .30, η2p = 0.069], whereas a significant difference was observed for the right hand [F (2, 34) = 4.01, p < .05, η2p = 0.191]. Post hoc analysis with the Bonferroni method revealed that participants who responded quickly exhibited larger differences of deviations between larger numbers and smaller numbers compared to those who responded slowly [t (34) = 2.78, p < .05, d = 1.133].

For horizontal deviations in the square centering task, response speed did not significantly influence the expression of differences in biases induced by numerical information for the left hand [F (2, 34) = 0.09, p = .92, η2p = 0.005] or the right hand [F (2, 34) = 0.49, p = .62, η2p = 0.028]. Likewise, for the vertical deviations in the square centering task, the analysis showed no significant difference for the left hand [F (2, 34) = 0.06, p = .94, η2p = 0.003] or the right hand [F (2, 34) = 1.68, p = .20, η2p = 0.090].

Additional analysis: influence of the stimulus position and the starting position on biases

In the present study, the target stimulus to be presented was displaced 20 mm from the center of the screen along both the x and y axes in one of the four quadrants (i.e., upper-left, upper-right, lower-left, lower-right). To examine possible modulations induced by the stimulus position, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted on deviations in each task with the left-right factor and the lower-upper factor. For the horizontal deviations in the horizontal centering task, the analysis revealed a significant main effect of the left-right factor [F (1, 36) = 30.50, p < .001, η2p = 0.459; left-position: M = -0.96 mm, SD = 1.52 mm; right-position: M = -0.09 mm, SD = 1.85 mm]. Neither a significant main effect of the lower-upper factor [F (1, 36) = 0.02, p = .89, η2p = 0.001] nor a significant interaction between two factors [F (1, 36) = 2.19, p = .15, η2p = 0.057] was observed. Similarly, for the horizontal deviations in the square centering task, the analysis also revealed a significant main effect of the left-right factor [F (1, 36) = 7.71, p < .01, η2p = 0.176; left-position: M = -0.50 mm, SD = 1.38 mm; right-position: M = -0.22 mm, SD = 1.54 mm]. Neither a significant main effect of the lower-upper factor [F (1, 36) = 0.80, p = .38, η2p = 0.022] nor a significant interaction between two factors [F (1, 36) = 0.16, p = .69, η2p = 0.005] was found. These results showed that leftward biases were greater when the stimulus was presented in the left hemispace from the center of the display compared to the biases when the stimulus was presented in the right hemispace (Fig. 6).

(A) Biases in horizontal dimension based on the position of the stimulus presentation. For both stimulus shapes, leftward biases were greater when the stimuli were presented in the left visual field compared to the right visual field. (B) Biases in horizontal dimension based on the starting position. For both stimuli shapes, leftward biases were greater when participants started moving the mouse cursor from the right side compared to the left side. In contrast, for the vertical deviations in the vertical centering task, the analysis revealed that none of the main effects or the interaction reached significance [lower-upper factor: F (1, 36) = 3.76, p = .06, η2p = 0.095; left-right factor: F (1, 36) = 0.80, p = .38, η2p = 0.022; interaction: F (1, 36) = 0.00, p = .96, η2p = 0.000]. Akin to the vertical centering task, none of the main effects or the interaction showed significant differences for the vertical deviations in the square centering task [lower-upper factor: F (1, 36) = 0.04, p = .85, η2p = 0.001; left-right factor: F (1, 36) = 2.27, p = .14, η2p = 0.059; interaction: F (1, 36) = 0.07, p = .79, η2p = 0.002].

Likewise, the starting position, defined as the mouse position before the presentation of the stimuli, was also assigned to one of four quadrants (i.e., upper-left, upper-right, lower-left, lower-right) relative to the center of the display. The analysis with the same design as that conducted for the stimulus position was then performed. For the horizontal deviations in the horizontal centering task, a significant main effect of the left-right factor was found [F (1, 36) = 45.60, p < .001, η2p = 0.559; left-starting: M = -0.28 mm, SD = 1.68 mm; right-starting: M = -0.77 mm, SD = 1.59 mm]. A significant main effect of the left-right factor was also observed for the horizontal deviations in the square centering task [F (1, 36) = 44.38, p < .001, η2p = 0.552; left-starting: M = -0.11 mm, SD = 1.48 mm; right-starting: M = -0.62 mm, SD = 1.41 mm]. These results revealed that when the starting position was on the right side of the display center, the subjective midpoint exhibited greater leftward bias compared to the bias when participants moved the mouse cursor from the left side (Fig. 6).

Similarly, for the vertical deviations in the vertical centering task, a significant main effect of the lower-upper factor was found [F (1, 36) = 19.49, p < .001, η2p = 0.351; lower-starting: M = 3.10 mm, SD = 2.36 mm; upper-starting: M = 2.62 mm, SD = 2.43 mm]. A significant main effect of the lower-upper factor was also observed for the vertical deviations in the square centering task [F (1, 36) = 41.81, p < .001, η2p = 0.537; lower-starting: M = 1.81 mm, SD = 1.84 mm; upper-starting: M = 1.15 mm, SD = 1.77 mm]. These results revealed that when the starting position was set at a lower position relative to the center of the display, the subjective midpoint exhibited greater upward bias compared to the bias when participants moved the mouse cursor from the upper position. Aside from the above results, no other main effects or interactions showed significant differences in each analysis (Fig. 6).

Discussions

In the present study, neurologically and psychiatrically healthy Japanese participants completed identifying the veridical center of horizontal and vertical stimuli presented on a display monitor by moving and clicking the mouse button. The study also employed a centering task on the square stimuli to examine the influence of numerical magnitude on visuospatial bias in terms of the two-dimensional plane. The results of a comprehensive analysis across all participants demonstrated leftward biases (i.e., pseudoneglect) for horizontal stimuli when participants responded with their left hand, as what have been observed in the previous studies37,38. Likewise, upward biases (i.e., altitudinal bias) for vertical stimuli were also observed when participants responded with either their left or right hand. This upward bias of visuospatial attention is also consistent with previous studies58–60. Notably, the proportion of participants who exhibited an upward bias for vertical stimuli (left hand: 89.2%; right hand: 89.2%) was higher than those who exhibited a leftward bias for horizontal stimuli (left hand: 62.2%; right hand: 56.8%), suggesting that the altitudinal bias is a typical phenomenon even in neurologically and psychiatrically healthy individuals.

Furthermore, in the previous study, the leftward bias was shown to decrease when the aspect ratio of the stimulus approached 100%72. In the present study, although no significant difference between the horizontal and square stimuli was observed concerning the horizontal biases, the upward bias for the square stimuli was smaller than that for the vertical stimuli. Thus, the present study showed a modulation of biases caused by aspect ratio in the vertical dimension. The critical difference between the previous study and our study is underlying whether stimuli with number strings were included. However, in our square centering task, smaller upward biases were observed particularly for the stimuli without number strings (i.e., dark and light stimuli), which were almost similar to those used in the previous study. The difference in the emergence of the aspect ratio effect cannot be fully explained from the perspective of the difference in the stimuli type, thus further research will be necessary to explore the size of the biases that varied depending on the stimuli shape.

The main aim of the present study was to examine the association between numbers and space by applying one or two dimensions of the centering tasks. In this point, our results demonstrated that the deviations for smaller number stimuli were positioned farther to the left relative to the deviations for the larger number stimuli in the horizontal centering task. This finding aligns with previous studies43–46. Previous studies employing the line bisection task have reported that children as young as 5 years old exhibit biases induced by numerical magnitude14. Moreover, other studies reported that even newborns may have a left-to-right spatial representation of numerical magnitude20,21. In the present study, the task did not require participants to make binary responses, and the numerical stimuli were irrelevant to the centering task. Therefore, the representation of an inherent mental number line may have implicitly elicited horizontal spatial biases. Although the differences in biases due to numerical magnitude were found to be less than 1 mm, which are extremely tiny differences in terms of our daily lives, even such small biases are important for understanding the spatial-numerical association as a fundamental characteristic of an automatic implicit processes.

However, contrary to our hypothesis, the biases for smaller numbers were shown to be more upward than those for larger numbers in the vertical centering task, and the effect of numerical magnitude was not observed in either two dimensions of the square stimuli. In terms of vertical spatial-numerical associations, the vertical SNARC effect has typically emerged in line with a bottom-to-top mental number line52–54, even among Japanese individuals31, but a reverse vertical SNARC effect has also been observed83. In particular, the reverse vertical SNARC effect emerged when participants used their hand and foot simultaneously to respond in the parity judgment task, suggesting that response effectors may play a role in this phenomenon83. In our vertical centering task, since the reverse attentional biases induced by numerical magnitude were irrelevant to such effect of the response effectors, other mechanisms may be involved in the observed result contrary to our hypothesis. Another study demonstrated that the spatial-numerical association for the vertical array appeared to be weaker compared to the horizontal array when the manual pointing task to a visual numerical target was applied84. These diverse results may suggest that the visuomotor response to the numerical magnitude is mediated by an unstable spatial mapping to the vertical orientation. Furthermore, such reverse vertical mappings can also be discussed from the perspective of the cultural background of the Japanese speakers, who participated in the present study. A previous study in which Chinese participants performed magnitude comparison employed three types of notations (Arabic numerals, simplified Chinese, and complex Chinese characters) for presenting the numbers in the task33. In Li et al. (2017), the performance of binary response consistently showed a left-to-right spatial representation of numbers in the horizontal dimension, regardless of the notation used. While in the vertical dimension, traditional complex Chinese characters elicited a bottom-to-top spatial representation, whereas reverse vertical mappings were observed for both Arabic numerals and simplified Chinese characters33. A similar small-up response pattern was observed in an experiment where native Chinese speakers in Taiwan performed a parity judgement task using simplified Chinese characters32. Given the influence of culturally specific notation on the spatial-numerical association, it is necessary to examine the spatial-numerical association in Japan as well, by considering the differences among Arabic numerals, hiragana, and kanji (corresponding to simplified Chinese characters). However, the present study employed Arabic numerals, not Japanese-specific notation, which is common to the study of Ito and Hatta (2004)31. Their study demonstrated left-to-right and bottom-to-top spatial representations by using Arabic numerals with the parity judgement task for Japanese speakers. Therefore, the influence of Japanese-specific cultural factors may be minimal in the present study. Additionally, spatial associations related to serial order, which is named the Spatial-Positional Association of Response Codes (SPoARC) effect85, may be involved in the small-up phenomenon observed in the present study. A previous study analyzing the saccade latency revealed that earlier items in a memorized serial order were associated with the left space and later items with the right space in the horizontal axis, whereas earlier items were associated with the upper space and later items with the lower space in the vertical axis86. Given the results of this prior study, the numbers “1” and “2” in the present study, may have activated representations of early serial positions, while “8” and “9” may have activated representations of later positions, resulting in the emergence of the small-up phenomenon in the vertical axis. These findings suggest that the vertical axis has the potential to reflect either bottom-to-top or top-to-bottom spatial directions, depending on the nature of the activated representation. Further experimental examinations will be required to reveal what the “switch” is that toggles the vertical spatial-directional representations.

In the square centering task, different characteristics in the subjective centers occurred depending on whether the number strings were presented in the stimuli. Stimuli with number strings exhibited larger upward biases and smaller leftward biases compared to stimuli without number strings. In the case of the stimuli with number strings, local attention, which is thought to be relevant to the ventral visual stream, may have probably been activated so as to process visually the arrangement of multiple number strings, resulting in the expression of an upward bias58,64. On the other hand, spatial information processing, which may be associated with the dorsal visual stream, was dominantly activated to identify the center of the stimuli without number strings. Such asymmetrical activation of the right hemisphere mediated by the dorsal visual stream possibly leads to attentional bias to the left side49,70,71. Moreover, a greater leftward bias in the line bisection task was observed when the line was removed from the display during the bisection process compared to when the line remained continuously visible during the task87. This finding namely suggests that participants’ reliance on their mental representation leads to greater spatial attentional biases. Indeed, leftward biases can be observed in situations where participants do not rely on visual information87,88. However, our study presented stimuli continuously until the participants clicked the mouse button, thus our task strongly required the perceptual processing of visual information rather than their mental representation. It is possible that the cognitive function for processing numerical information was masked due to the dominant activation for perceptual and visual processing of multiple patterns concerning number strings.

In the present study, response speed did not modulate the effect of numerical magnitude on biases in each task, except for the vertical centering tasks performed with the right hand. In addition, for vertical stimuli bisected using the right hand, participants who responded faster exhibited a larger difference between biases for smaller and larger number stimuli, compared to those who responded more slowly. This result was inconsistent with our assumption. We assumed that the difference between deviations for smaller numbers and larger numbers would be greater for slower responses, as the spatial coding of numbers is presumed to require a longer time duration75. Actually, in the SNARC effect, the compatibility effect also tended to be greater when response speeds were slow than when response speeds were fast2,73,76. However, the time required to complete a line bisection task using mouse operations (1.66–1.86 s) appeared to be longer than that for tasks on the SNARC effect like responding buttons. If response times were over those observed in general SNARC effect studies, the spatial coding of numbers may not be observed because the conscious aspect for accurate movements is predominant over the unconscious influence by the spatial-numerical associations. Moreover, spatial coding of numbers has been interpreted to occur during the motor preparation stage77 or the motor execution stage78. However, in the present study, participants were required to move the mouse cursor towards the subjective center of the stimulus and click the mouse upon stimulus presentation, thereby involving complex movements (reaction time + movement time). Since participants were only instructed not to move the cursor until the stimulus was presented but were not technically restricted from doing so, it is possible that they moved the cursor towards a predicted position in advance. As a result, it is challenging to argue under separating the response time into the time of motor preparation (i.e., reaction time) and motor execution (i.e., movement time). Although response time analysis was expected to provide supplementary evidence for spatial-numerical associations, the findings were inconsistent with those of previous studies. Thus, to clarify and interpret these phenomena, further examination is needed by applying a within-participant design for response speed.

Overall, the results tended to show a larger leftward and upward bias when the task was performed with the left hand. In addition, although biases in the vertical dimension were not modulated depending on the left-right positions of stimulus presentation, the leftward bias in the present study was more pronounced when the stimulus was presented in the left visual field. These findings may imply that biases in both dimensions are attributed to the activation of the right hemisphere. The previous studies showed that activation of the right hemisphere contributes to the emergence of pseudoneglect (i.e., activation-orientation hypothesis)65,66. On the other hand, our findings that left-hand responses elicited stronger upward biases were inconsistent with previous studies. To our knowledge, upward biases have been reported to increase when vertical lines are presented in the right visual field or when participants respond with their right hand59,60. Moreover, the ventral visual pathway, which is involved in processing information in the upper visual field64, may preferentially connect with the left hemisphere on the basis of the findings obtained from the line bisection task in three respective dimensions42,49,70,71,89. The phenomenon found in the present study that the upward bias was stronger when participants responded with their left hand, suggests a likely involvement of the right hemisphere for the emergence of upward bias. This is not consistent with previous studies. One likely explanation for this inconsistent result is that it was difficult for participants to move the mouse cursor with their left hand as they intended because most of the participants were right-handed, resulting in inaccurate operation of the computer mouse and larger biases. As another explanation, rather than a binary distinction between neural mechanisms, such as the ventral visual stream – the left hemisphere or the dorsal visual stream – the right hemisphere, a complementary interaction between these neuroanatomical mechanisms may contribute to the performance of the centering task. Furthermore, although the above-mentioned results appeared to suggest a shared functional mechanism underlying the emergence of leftward and upward bias68, our correlation analysis between horizontal and vertical biases, which yielded low coefficients (r = − .06 or − 0.12), indicated that participants with a strong leftward attentional bias did not necessarily exhibit a strong upward bias. This suggests that, even if the right hemisphere may be involved in biases in both dimensions, the underlying mechanisms for processing horizontal and vertical space are possibly different from each other. Specifically, the involvement of the ventral visual pathway is considered to play a role in the vertical direction, as it is related to attending to the upper visual field. In addition, biases observed in line bisection tasks for the vertical and sagittal dimensions (i.e., altitudinal bias and distal bias) show a significant correlation, suggesting a shared occipitotemporal mechanism with each bias90. Conversely, the system of spatial information processing localized in the right hemisphere is likely to play a role in the expression of leftward bias.

In addition, the starting position, i.e., the direction of hand movement towards stimuli, was also found to influence the expression of biases. Specifically, initiating movement from the left side increased the bias towards the leftward space, while starting from the bottom increased the bias towards the upward space. The observation that horizontal movement direction affects bias is consistent with previous studies using a wooden stick79,80, and may be involved in biomechanical mechanisms. In this study, participants were randomly assigned starting positions from the four quadrants to account for the effects of such movement in advance. When designing line bisection or centering tasks, it is crucial to establish a balanced starting position and accurately record participants’ responses.

The primary goal of the present study was to investigate whether participants’ visuospatial attention was modulated by number strings within the frame of horizontal, vertical, or square stimuli. In conclusion, leftward biases for horizontal stimuli were greater when smaller numbers were presented within the frame of the stimuli compared to when larger numbers were presented. However, the spatial-numerical association, in line with the left-to-right representation of numbers, was not observed in a horizontal dimension of the square stimuli. In contrast, upward biases were smaller when the stimuli with larger numbers were presented within the frame of the stimuli compared to when the stimuli with smaller numbers were presented. Akin to horizontal biases, modulation by numerical magnitude did not occur for the square stimuli. In particular, the subjective center of the square was different depending on whether the stimuli possessed the number strings. The centering for stimuli with the number strings showed biases with a stronger vertical component, whereas stimuli without the number strings showed biases with a stronger horizontal component. This difference observed in bias patterns may reflect the involvement of the ventral visual stream because object-based processing was likely to be activated due to the presentation of number strings. The perceptual dominance of visual processing might have masked the activation of implicit processing for numerical representation. For exploring the interference of the numerical magnitude with the biases in the two-dimensional plane, it should be therefore required to improve the method of stimulus presentation to more effectively activate numerical representations. In particular, the present study aimed to investigate the representational characteristics of numerical magnitude employing Arabic numerals, but differences in the physical size or area of the numerals presented on the computer screen were not controlled. In cases where non-symbolic dots are presented visually, cumulative surface area and occupied area of the stimuli may influence spatial bias rather than the number of dots91. In the present study, the fact that the stimuli of “8” and “9” have higher luminance than those of “1” and “2” (see Methods & Materials section) may also reflect differences in stimulus density. Although with respect to the luminance (dark vs. light stimulus), most analyses did not reach statistical significance, such differences in the physical features of stimuli could confound the interpretation of spatial-numerical associations. In future studies, to address these concerns, it will be required to control the size and area of numbers in accordance with the visual properties of their shapes, for example, by measuring the point of subjective equality (PSE) on the font size/brightness of numerals.

Finally, our task employed two-dimensional stimuli and asked participants to identify the subjective center, which offers a relatively novel approach. At the same time, it is also similar to a study in which birds were required to search for food hidden at the center of a space92. That study, designed for binocular and monocular visual conditions, suggested that search strategies of birds differed depending on whether their left or right eye was used92. Although the present study employed binocular viewing for the centering task, employing monocular conditions in future research may allow us to compare the strength of spatial-numerical associations with each condition and may provide insights into the difference in hemispheric specialization concerning spatial-numerical associations. Furthermore, other findings suggested that numerical magnitude was spatially associated with the horizontal axis, whereas numerical distance corresponds to space in the radial axis93. To advance our understanding of how numerical properties map onto spatial representation and their role, future investigations should examine the relationship between spatial-numerical associations and the underlying mechanisms of visuospatial attention across the horizontal, vertical, and sagittal dimensions.

Methods and materials

Participants

In the present experiment, participants under the age of 40 with no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders and with normal or corrected-to-normal vision on the basis of their self-report were recruited. A meta-analysis revealed that older people are likely to show a rightward attentional bias37, suggesting that the hemispheric lateralization of attentional functions may vary with age. Although another review has shown a leftward bias even in people over 50 years old94, the age of the recruited participants in the present study was restricted because the influence of participants’ age on the bias was outside the scope of the present study. The number of participants was determined through a priori power analysis performed using G*power ver. 3.1.9.795,96. The analysis focusing on the main effect of the numerical magnitude (2 levels) reported that a total of 34 participants would be needed, assuming an effect size of 0.25 and a power of 0.80. The participants were recruited from undergraduate students attending a psychology class, were informed of the experiment in advance, and consented to participate in the experiment.

A total number of 37 participants (24 females; mean age = 18.97 years, SD = 0.90 years) completed the experiment. All participants were native speakers of Japanese. A previous study on the SNARC effect in Japanese has demonstrated that they possess a left-to-right and bottom-to-top mental number line31. According to the Japanese version of the FLANDERS handedness questionnaire97, 33 participants (23 female) were right-handed and 4 participants (1 female) were left-handed (no participants showed mixed-handedness). In the line bisection task, left-handed individuals may exhibit a slightly smaller leftward bias compared to right-handed individuals37. With regard to spatial-numerical associations, however, the influence of handedness is considered to be minimal1. Moreover, a previous study has also examined spatial-numerical associations using the line bisection task with samples that included left-handed participants43. Accordingly, the present study did not exclude left-handed individuals from participation. The participants remained naïve about the hypothesis of the experiment being tested.

Apparatus

The participants, who were tested individually, were seated in front of a table in a quiet and dark room. The stimuli for the present experiment were displayed on a monitor (SAMSUNG, SynscMaster SA700: display size 509.76 × 286.74 mm), positioned at the participant’s eye height, by using PsychoPy ver. 2024.1.4 running on a laptop computer (HP, EliteBook 630 G1098). The viewing distance between the participant’s eye and the monitor was 45 cm, following the previous study72. The participant’s head was held in a fixed position using a chin rest. The participant was instructed to operate the cursor of a computer mouse (Dell, MS116) to identify the center of the presented horizontal, vertical, or two-dimensional stimuli (i.e., square). They were also asked to click a left (or right) mouse button with their right (or left) hand index finger when they identified the center of a given stimulus. The data of participant’s response time, which was measured using a mouse click, from the onset of stimuli presentation to the click, and those of coordinates (i.e., clicked position) were acquired on the laptop computer.

Experimental stimuli

For the horizontal centering task, rectangles measuring 4 mm (0.5° of visual angle) in height and 130 mm (16.4°) in width, surrounded by a white border, were used. For the vertical centering task, rectangles measuring 130 mm (16.4°) in height and 4 mm (0.5°) in width, also surrounded by a white border, were used. The length of the long side in both stimuli was in line with that used in the previous study72. In the present experiment, two rectangles with no strings inside the frame (i.e., light and dark stimulus, as mentioned below) and four rectangles containing strings of Arabic numerals (1, 2, 8, or 9) were created for both horizontal and vertical centering tasks (Fig. 1). The number of Arabic numeral strings inside the border was 33 for both the horizontal and vertical stimuli. A previous study noted that the edges of lines appeared unclear due to the shape of each number44. Therefore, the stimuli used in the present study were framed to ensure that the size of each stimulus remained constant. The number strings were drawn in 9.6-point Aptos font.

For the square centering task, two squares surrounded by a white border without any number strings and four squares filled with strings of numbers within the white border were created (Fig. 1). Each side of the square was 130 mm (16.4°). The number of Arabic numeral strings inside the border was 33 (vertical) × 33 (horizontal). The squares without number strings were prepared in black, matching the background color (i.e., dark stimulus), and in light gray (R, G, B = 75, 75, 75) (i.e., light stimulus). A light square was included for the aim of examining the influence of luminance difference on biases because the squares with number strings appeared to vary in luminance depending on the shape of the numbers. The luminance of the stimulus has also been shown to be encoded in the representational space as one of the magnitude information5,6. The light stimulus with the same color as the square centering tasks was included in the horizontal and vertical centering tasks so that the number of trials was consistent between tasks. The mean luminance of the stimuli was approximately 9.5 cd/m2 (Horizontal stimulus), 11.1 cd/m2 (vertical stimulus), and 18.0 cd/m2 (two-dimensional stimulus): in each two-dimensional stimulus, 16.1 cd/m2 (strings of “1”), 20.7 cd/m2 (strings of “2”), 24.3 cd/m2 (strings of “8”), 23.7 cd/m2 (strings of “9”), 1.9 cd/m2 (dark stimulus), and 21.1 cd/m2 (light stimulus).

Procedure

Participants were seated in front of the monitor and were instructed to click on the perceived center of the stimulus in each task with the manual operation of the computer mouse.

First, in each trial, a red circle with an approximately 14 mm diameter was presented as a starting position (i.e., mouse position), which was displayed 86 mm (10.9° of visual angle) from the veridical center of the screen along both the x and y axes in one of four quadrants (i.e., upper-left, upper-right, lower-left, lower-right). The target stimuli were then presented after 500 ms after participants clicked on the red circle. The target stimulus was presented displaced 20 mm (2.5°) from the true center of the screen along both the x and y axes in one of the four quadrants (i.e., upper-left, upper-right, lower-left, lower-right). Each stimulus was randomly presented 16 times, with four starting positions and four presentation positions. When the perceived center of the target stimulus was clicked by participants, the next red circle was automatically presented again. Participants were instructed to identify the center of a given stimulus as accurately and quickly as possible. They were asked not to move the cursor of the computer mouse from the starting position until the stimulus was presented. The mouse cursor remained visible during the inter-trial interval. In all tasks, participants were required to complete a total of 96 trials (6 stimulus types × 16 times), with breaks provided for every 32 trials. At the 32- or 64-trial point, the instruction to take a break was presented on the monitor, and participants resumed the task at their own pace. The order of the three tasks was counterbalanced among the participants. Furthermore, each task was performed with either left or right hands in consecutive blocks, which order was also counterbalanced. Namely, participants completed a total of 576 trials through 6 blocks.

After completing all tasks, participants were asked to answer the questions written in a questionnaire, which included demographic data (i.e., age and sex) and the Japanese version of the FLANDERS handedness questionnaire97 to identify their hand dominance. For the categorization of handedness, participants with an aggregated score of 5 or more on the questionnaire were regarded as right-handed, and those with a score of -5 or less were regarded as left-handed97.

Statistical analysis

For each trial of each task, the response times, showing the duration from the onset of stimulus presentation to that of the participants’ click on the perceived center, were calculated. The deviations from the veridical center of the stimulus along the x-axis and the y-axis were also calculated using the coordinates of the clicked position registered in the computer via the PsychoPy software. A part of the trials was excluded on the basis of the criteria that the deviations were greater or less than 3MAD from the median, and/or the response times were greater than 3MAD from the median or less than 120 ms. This MAD indicates the median absolute deviation, calculated by multiplying the constant (1.4826) with the median of the differences between each observed value and the median99,100. For horizontal or vertical stimuli, outlier detection was performed as an univariable in the horizontal or vertical direction, respectively. For square, observed coordinates were excluded from the analysis if they were regarded as outliers in either the horizontal or vertical axis.

After excluding the outliers, arithmetic means of clicked position were calculated. A positive value of the deviations indicates that the subjective midpoint was biased towards the right or upper hemispace, while the negative value indicated the opposite directional bias. First, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was performed on horizontal deviations in the horizontal centering task, with the numerical magnitude (i.e., smaller number, larger number) and the response hand (i.e., left, right) as within-participants factors. Smaller numbers included “1” and “2”, and larger numbers included “8” and “9”. In addition, to explore the influence of differences in luminance of the stimuli, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was performed on horizontal deviations, with the luminance (i.e., dark, light) and the response hand (i.e., left, right) as within-participants factors. Next, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was performed on the vertical deviations in the vertical centering task to understand the influences of the numerical magnitude and the luminance factor respectively, with the same design performed on the horizontal deviations. For the square centering task, the same design analysis was also applied to the direct distance from the veridical center, horizontal, and vertical deviations. A scatter plot of coordinates in the square centering task was depicted so as to visualize the deviation of each stimulus type. Given that the coordinates of the subjective center appeared to differ between the stimuli with number strings (i.e., small number and large number stimuli) and those without number strings (i.e., dark and light stimuli), complementary paired t-tests were performed on the integrated deviations data of each stimulus type in horizontal and vertical axes.

Furthermore, an exploratory correlation analysis was performed to clarify the relationship between the deviations across tasks. If the same mechanism underlies attentional biases in horizontal and vertical dimensions, a higher correlation between deviations in both dimensions should be observed. In addition, given the possibility that the deviations in the square centering task may be influenced by the aspect ratio, the degree of the deviations in the horizontal centering task may be different from those in the square centering task. To examine this, a paired sample t-test was conducted to compare the degree of deviations in the same direction across different stimulus shapes. Lastly, to further examine the influence of stimulus position and starting position, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted on deviations in each task, with the left-right factor and the lower-upper factor as within-participants factors. All statistical analyses were performed using JASP101.
