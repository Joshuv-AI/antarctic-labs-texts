Exploring the impact of professional role identity and correlated factors on cultural competence among nurse practitioners
Authors: Wan-Yu Chou, Pei-Ching Liu, I-Ching Hou, Cheng-Chen Chou, Chi-Wen Chen, Geng-Wang Liaw, Ying-Hui Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNurse practitioners (NPs) are internationally acknowledged for their advanced clinical skills and ability to provide cost-effective, high-quality care. Cultural competence, a vital aspect of NP practice, facilitates effective engagement with diverse patient populations. Professional role identity, characterized by the recognition and internalization of the NP role, has been linked to improved clinical competence. However, the interplay between cultural competence and professional role identity in NPs has received limited attention.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 225 NPs and NP candidates from hospitals of different levels in Taiwan. Participants completed self-administered questionnaires measuring cultural competence, professional role identity, demographic characteristics, and professional training backgrounds. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, correlation analyses, and logistic regression in SPSS 24.0 to explore the relationship between cultural competence and professional role identity alongside other influencing factors.ResultsParticipants demonstrated a commendable average cultural competence score. Professional role identity was the most significant factor influencing cultural competence. Higher competence levels were observed in NPs who had completed cultural competence courses or were in earlier career ladder stages. Conversely, lower competence scores were noted among NPs working in pediatric departments.ConclusionThe findings underscore a strong association between professional role identity and cultural competence, suggesting that fostering role identity could enhance cultural competence among NPs. Incorporating cultural competence training and role identity development into NP curricula is crucial to addressing the healthcare needs of diverse populations, particularly as NPs expand their roles in underserved communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-03687-5.Keywords: Cultural competence, Professional role identity, Nurse practitioners

Introduction

Nurse practitioners (NPs) are defined by the International Council of Nurses (ICN) as registered nurses who have developed expert knowledge, advanced decision-making abilities, and clinical competencies for an expanded scope of practice, achieved through additional graduate education, typically at least a master’s degree [1]. Globally, NPs, as one of the most prevalent advanced practice nurses (APNs), are acknowledged for their capacity to deliver high-quality patient care at a reasonable cost. Numerous studies have shown that integrating NPs can reduce hospital length of stay, lower care costs, improve healthcare flow efficiency across hospital departments, and increase patient satisfaction [2, 3]. In Taiwan, most NPs are currently employed in acute care settings [4]. However, consistent with global trends in the development of the NP role, Taiwan is anticipated to shift toward a greater emphasis on primary care, particularly in rural and underserved communities [4, 5]. These populations often encompass diverse ethnic and cultural backgrounds, necessitating a high degree of cultural competence among healthcare providers [6, 7]. To address this evolving need, Taiwan’s Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW) launched a nationally funded curriculum in 2021 to train family nurse practitioners (FNPs) for expanded roles in primary care, with a focus on underserved populations [7]. Embedding cultural competence training within this curriculum is imperative to ensure FNPs are equipped to effectively meet the nuanced healthcare needs of culturally diverse communities. However, current knowledge regarding the cultural competence of NPs, particularly in Taiwan, remains limited [8]. Further research is imperative to assess current cultural competence levels among NPs and inform future training frameworks.

Cultural competence, an ongoing process in which healthcare providers develop the ability to work effectively within the client’s cultural context, is increasingly recognized as essential for NPs [8, 9]. Cultural competence is a critical component of the competencies required for NPs and should be fully integrated into their clinical practice [10]. Leading nursing organizations have emphasized its critical importance, with the American Academy of Nursing (AAN) highlighting its significance and the American Association of Colleges of Nursing (AACN) advocating for its integration across all educational levels in nursing curricula, from bachelor’s to doctoral programs [11, 12]. Given the increasing cultural diversity within healthcare, NPS must be capable of providing culturally competent care to effectively meet the needs of patients from diverse backgrounds [13]. Without sufficient cultural competence, healthcare providers may risk precipitating care crises across clinical practice. Insufficient cultural competence can lead to delayed treatment, misdiagnoses, or even fatal outcomes, underscoring the critical need for effective communication across cultural boundaries [14].

Campinha-Bacote’s model frames cultural competence through four essential components: cultural awareness, cultural knowledge, cultural sensitivity, and cultural skills or cultural care behaviors [9, 15]. Cultural awareness serves as the foundation, involving a process of self-examination in which healthcare providers reflect on their cultural and professional backgrounds. This reflective process fosters an understanding of personal cultural identity and an awareness of the differences between oneself and others, such as variations in values, beliefs, norms, and lifestyles. Enhanced self-awareness reduces the likelihood of projecting personal cultural assumptions onto patients from diverse backgrounds [9]. Building on this, cultural knowledge promotes continual learning about other cultures, including insights into health beliefs, disease prevalence, and culturally relevant treatment practices [16]. Cultural sensitivity follows, emphasizing genuine respect and openness toward cultural diversity, strengthening providers’ ability to connect with patients’ backgrounds, experiences, and values [17]. Lastly, cultural skills or care behaviors center on putting this awareness, knowledge, and sensitivity into action, enabling providers to effectively address cultural differences and barriers during clinical interactions with patients from diverse backgrounds [18].

Professional role identity refers to the attitudes, values, knowledge, beliefs, and skills shared by professional group members directly related to the specific role individuals hold within that profession. It is fundamentally a matter of subjective self-concept, shaped by the way individuals perceive and internalize the work role they adopt [19]. A stronger professional role identity among healthcare providers is associated with greater job satisfaction, higher retention rates, enhanced self-efficacy, and improved quality of care for patients [20, 21]. While previous research suggests that a robust professional identity among NPs positively correlates with clinical competencies, developing this identity poses distinct challenges [5, 22]. Moreover, a well-established professional identity among NPs is also proposed to substantially enhance care competency, improve patient health outcomes, and reduce healthcare disparities [23]. However, other healthcare providers and patients sometimes misunderstand or underestimate the NP’s role and may not fully recognize or acknowledge the scope of NPs’ competencies [24]. Transitioning from registered nurses to NPs involves a complex and evolving professional role identity reconstruction process. As NPs take on responsibilities traditionally held by physicians, such as prescribing medications and diagnosing conditions, they often face skepticism from healthcare colleagues, including physicians and fellow nurses [5, 25].

Cultural competence and professional role identity are developmental constructs that evolve through reflective processes. Campinha-Bacote’s model conceptualizes cultural competence as a dynamic process that begins with cultural awareness and requires ongoing self-reflection, sensitivity, and behavioral change [9]. Meanwhile, professional role identity is increasingly understood as an evolving self-concept that emerges through reflective internalization of professional norms, responsibilities, and expectations [26]. These reflective foundations are central to the development of both constructs and offer a basis for conceptual overlap. Given that cultural competence and professional role identity are developed through ongoing processes of self-reflection and examination, a potential relationship between these constructs may be postulated [9, 26]. However, this connection remains underexplored in the context of NPs. In related fields, Godsey (2011) emphasized the importance of a dual emphasis on both cultural competence and professional identity in the curriculum for speech-language pathology students, concluding that cultivating cultural competence through examining cultural relationships enriched students’ professional role identities [27]. Similarly, Hayward and Li (2014) underscored the value of integrating both cultural competence and professional identity in Doctor of Physical Therapy (DPT) programs, suggesting that a strengthened professional identity could concurrently enhance cultural competence [21].

The formation of professional role identity and the development of cultural competence are essential for NPs to fully leverage their clinical skills in meeting the diverse needs of patients, including those from various ethnic and cultural backgrounds. While cultural competence and professional role identity are essential to enacting effective NP practice, the relationship between these constructs has not been thoroughly explored. Research to date has highlighted the unique ability of NPs to provide culturally competent care and has examined various factors influencing this competence [8, 28, 29]. However, quantitative studies investigating the association between cultural competence and professional role identity among NPs remain limited. Considering the specialized role of NPs, further research is warranted to quantify their current level of cultural competence and to explore how professional identity shapes this competence. To address these gaps, this study aims to elucidate the relationship between cultural competence and professional role identity among NPs in Taiwan, assessing their current competence levels and identifying key influencing factors.

Methods

Research methods, places, and subjects

This study adopted a cross-sectional design. Data were collected using self-administered questionnaires at three hospitals in Taiwan: a medical center in the north, a regional hospital in the east, and a district hospital in the north. Participants meeting the following criteria were included in the study: being a licensed or associate NP who received NP training (at least one of the conditions must be met), willingness to participate in the study, being able to complete the questionnaire independently, and being under 65 years of age. NPs meeting the following criteria were excluded: not receiving NP training or unwilling to participate in the study.

Research instrument

Before administering the questionnaire to the total sample, a series of steps were taken to ensure the instrument’s validity and reliability. First, content validity was assessed through consultation with five experts. The questionnaire was then pilot-tested with a sample of 30 nurse practitioners. The results yielded a sufficient Cronbach’s α value, indicating a satisfactory level of reliability for the research instruments. The participants from the pilot test were subsequently excluded from the main study.

Participants’ background information, including age, gender, nursing education, hospital levels, nursing job tenure, and their professional training background (nursing career ladder, working department, language proficiency, previous encounters with foreign patients, cultural competence, or overseas training/studying) were collected. As outlined below, their cultural competence and professional role identification were assessed using two separate questionnaires.

Cultural competence assessment

The Cultural Competence Assessment Instrument–Chinese Version (CCA-CV) was used in this study, based on its original development and translation as reported in previous studies [15, 17]. The instrument of CCA-CV originally encompassed 25 items. After consulting with five experts, one item was removed due to its vague wording and unclear meaning. The final version contained 24 items and two subscales: Cultural Awareness and Sensitivity (CAS) with ten questions and Cultural Competence Behavior (CCB) with 14 questions. The item-level CVI (I-CVI) and scale-level CVI (S-CVI) of CAS and CCB ranged from 0.93 to 0.95 and 0.78 to 0.81, respectively. Each response is measured on a 7-point Likert scale. For instance, one question assessing the participant’s cultural awareness and sensitivity was: “I believe everyone deserves respect, regardless of their cultural background.” Then, the participant can select a response ranging from “strongly disagree” (scored as 1) to “strongly agree” (scored as 7). The other sample question for assessing the participant’s cultural competence behavior was, “I will avoid stereotyping people from specific groups.” The participant can choose a score for this question ranging from “never” (scored as 1) to “always” (scored as 7). Consistent with the original design and published applications of the instrument, the scores for each subscale were summed, and a total CCA-CV score was calculated by combining the CAS and CCB scores. A higher total score indicates a higher level of cultural competence [15, 17].

In the pilot study, the CCA-CV scale demonstrated strong internal consistency, achieving a Cronbach’s α of 0.93 with 30 participants. Upon administration to a separate, larger sample of 225 participants, Cronbach’s α remained high at 0.89, indicating reliable performance across independent groups. This consistency supports the instrument’s reliability for assessing cultural competence.

Professional role identification questionnaire

The first author developed a Professional Role Identification Questionnaire (PRI) based on the literature [30–32]. The development process was guided by three previously validated instruments that measure professional or interprofessional identity: the Extended Professional Identity Scale [30], the Professional Identity Five-Factor Scale [31], and the Nurses Professional Values Scale-Revised [32]. Drawing upon the theoretical constructs and dimensions proposed in these instruments, such as interprofessional beliefs, professional role modeling, and professional values, a preliminary pool of items was generated and adapted to reflect the nurse practitioner context. These items were reviewed by five experts in the field, resulting in a content validity index of 0.96 at the item level (I-CVI) and 0.81 at the scale level (S-CVI). The final questionnaire comprised 20 items rated on a 6-point Likert scale from “strongly disagree” (scored as 1) to “strongly agree” (scored as 6). One example item is: “Delivering high-quality care is important for a nurse practitioner.” A higher total score indicates a greater degree of professional role identification.

Regarding reliability, the PRI demonstrated a Cronbach’s α of 0.84 in a pilot study with 30 participants. When subsequently administered to an independent sample of 225 participants, the reliability estimate rose to 0.90. The consistently high internal consistency across both samples supports the robustness of the PRI as a measure for assessing professional role identification among nurse practitioners. The full English version of the PRI is provided as a supplementary file to enhance transparency and facilitate peer evaluation.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the institutional review board of a medical center in Taiwan (IRB number: 22MMHIS254e) and the Institutional Review Board of National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University (IRB number: YM111103E).

All procedures involving human participants were conducted following the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committees and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

All research-related information and documents were encoded to protect the participants’ privacy.

Statistical analysis

Before conducting data analysis, the collected data were entered into IBM SPSS Statistics (version 24.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Participants were categorized into low- and high-score groups based on the median cultural competence (CCA-CV) score of 4.79, creating a binary outcome variable (low vs. high CCA-CV score). All statistical analyses were performed with a significance level (α) set at 0.05.

Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize participant characteristics, including frequency distributions, percentages, means, and standard deviations (SDs). Inferential statistics, including independent-sample t-tests, analysis of variance, and Pearson’s correlation analysis, were conducted to examine unadjusted associations between cultural competence (CCA-CV), its subscales (CAS and CCB), professional role identity (PRI), and demographic as well as professional training background variables.

Multivariate analysis was performed using a fitted logistic regression model to determine the variables most significantly associated with cultural competence. All independent variables listed in Table 1 were included as potential predictors. The stepwise forward selection method was applied to develop the most parsimonious model, which was evaluated for goodness-of-fit using the Nagelkerke R² and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit (GOF) test.

Results

Demographic characteristics and professional training background

Two hundred twenty-five nurse practitioners (NPs) were enrolled in the study between July 5, 2022, and December 31, 2022. Most participants were women (216 out of 225, 96%), averaging 41.24 ± 7.18 years. Most NPs had a bachelor’s degree as their highest level of nursing education (178 out of 225, 79.1%). Nearly half of the participants worked in regional hospitals (112 out of 225, 49.8%), and the average job tenure was 17.20 ± 7.49 years.

Regarding professional training background, the most significant proportion of participants belonged to category NP-0 (not yet entered the nursing career ladder system for NPs; 88 out of 225, 39.1%). Many participants practiced in surgical departments (80 out of 225, 35.6%). Regarding language proficiency, 75.6% reported communicating in one to two foreign languages or dialects, while 34.7% indicated prior experience caring for one to three foreign patients.

Additionally, 51.6% of participants stated that they had never taken any courses related to cultural competence, and the vast majority (96.4%) reported no experience with overseas training or studying abroad (Table 1).

Cultural competence

The average cultural competence (CCA-CV) score among participants was 4.79 ± 0.76, with a median of 4.79, ranging from 2.83 to 6.25. The average scores for the subscales were 4.97 ± 0.37 (range: 3.60–5.80) for CAS and 4.66 ± 1.27 (range: 1.43–7.00) for CCB. Significant differences in CCA-CV scores were observed only for prior encounters with foreign patients (p = 0.047) and participation in cultural competence courses (p = 0.014). No significant differences were found in CCA-CV scores based on gender, age, nursing education, hospital level, department, job tenure, career ladder, language proficiency, or experience studying abroad or receiving overseas training.

Participants were categorized into two groups based on their median CCA-CV score of 4.79 to facilitate comparisons, creating a high-score and low-score group. Group comparisons revealed that NPs with longer nursing job tenure were likelier to achieve higher CCA-CV scores (p = 0.04). Similarly, those who had taken cultural competence courses were also more likely to belong to the high-score group (p = 0.03). Additionally, NPs with prior experience caring for foreign patients showed borderline significance when comparing low- and high-score groups (p = 0.056). No significant differences were identified for other demographic characteristics or professional training background variables (Table 2).

Professional role identity

The average professional role identity (PRI) score was 4.87 ± 0.57, with a median of 4.90 and a range from 2.95 to 5.95. Although the result was not statistically significant, a trend suggested that nurse practitioners with longer nursing job tenure tended to have higher PRI scores (p = 0.057). Apart from job tenure, no significant differences in PRI scores were found based on gender, age, nursing education, hospital level, department, career ladder, language proficiency, or prior experience studying abroad or receiving overseas training.

Participants were further categorized into low- and high-score groups based on the median PRI score of 4.90. Although the difference was not statistically significant, a trend was noted regarding encounters with foreign patients between the low- and high-score groups (p = 0.06). No other significant differences were identified between these groups, including demographic characteristics or professional training background variables (Table 2).

Correlation between cultural competence and professional role identity

The correlation between cultural competence (CCA-CV), professional role identity (PRI), and each subscale (CAS and CCB) was evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis. Professional role identity (PRI) exhibited a significant positive correlation with CCA-CV (r = 0.57, p < 0.001). Furthermore, PRI was significantly correlated with both subscales of CCA-CV, with r = 0.19 for the correlation between PRI and CAS (p < 0.01) and r = 0.54 for the correlation between PRI and CCB (p < 0.001) (Table 3).

In addition, significant correlations were observed between the overall CCA-CV score and its subscales. The correlation between CCA-CV and CAS was r = 0.24 (p < 0.01), while the correlation between CCA-CV and CCB was notably stronger (r = 0.98, p < 0.01) (Table 3).

Regression analysis of cultural competence factors for NPs

A logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors significantly influencing cultural competence among nurse practitioners (NPs). This analysis examined the effects of professional role identity (PRI), demographic characteristics, and professional training background on cultural competence, aiming to identify the most explanatory factors. The model demonstrated a good fit, with a Nagelkerke R² value of 0.39 and a Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit (GOF) test result (p = 0.65).

Professional role identity (PRI) emerged as the associated factor of cultural competence, with an odds ratio (OR) of 12.28 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 5.80–26.01, p < 0.001). Participants who had completed courses on cultural competence were significantly more likely to exhibit a higher level of cultural competence (OR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.13–4.01, p = 0.02). Similarly, NPs at the beginning of the nursing career ladder (NP-0) were likelier to demonstrate greater cultural competence than those classified as NP-III or higher (OR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.10–4.14, p = 0.03).

Conversely, NPs working in pediatric departments were significantly less likely to achieve a higher level of cultural competence compared to those in other departments, with an odds ratio of 0.28 (95% CI: 0.08–0.95, p = 0.04) (Table 4).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to comprehensively investigate the relationship between cultural competence and a range of influential factors, with a specific focus on evaluating the role of professional role identity in shaping cultural competence among NPs.

This study aimed to assess the cultural competence of a group of NPs and NP candidates. Among the 225 participants recruited from hospitals across three levels (medical center, regional, and district) in northern and eastern Taiwan, the average CCA-CV score was 4.79 ± 0.76, indicating a commendable level of cultural competence and aligning with previous research [33]. The final regression model identified professional role identity as the most significant factor determining the level of cultural competence, suggesting that NPs with stronger professional role identity demonstrated higher cultural competence. In addition, participants who had taken cultural competence courses or were at the early stage of the nursing career ladder scored higher in cultural competence. Conversely, NPs working in pediatric departments were found to have lower scores on cultural competence compared to those working in other departments.

Though the correlation between professional role identity and cultural competence has received limited attention among NPs, research from other health-related fields indicates a potential linkage between these two constructs [21, 27]. In the nursing domain, self-efficacy has been linked to cultural competence, and it was postulated that there is a positive relationship between cultural competence and self-efficacy [34, 35]. Similarly, self-efficacy has also been recognized as a critical component affecting the development of professional role identity among healthcare professionals [20]. This perspective is further supported by this study’s findings, which demonstrated that professional role identity emerged as the most vital associated factor of cultural competence and showed significant correlations with the overall CCA-CV score and its subscales (CAS and CCB). While self-efficacy was not included as a measured variable in this study, previous literature identifies it as a factor associated with cultural competence and professional role identity. Rather than implying a mediating role, its mention here highlights a possible conceptual intersection. In line with the reflective processes discussed in the Introduction, such as self-reflection and internalization of professional norms, self-efficacy may be among the constructs that help explain how these two domains develop about each other. Future studies may consider incorporating self-efficacy to explore this potential linkage more explicitly.

Consistent with prior research, our findings confirmed that NPs who had taken cultural competence courses demonstrated higher levels of cultural competence [15, 36, 37]. Additionally, previous studies have highlighted an association between career ladder positions and cultural competence [8, 36, 38]. However, in contrast to these studies, our results indicated that NPs at the beginning of the career ladder (NP-0) exhibited higher cultural competence than those in more advanced positions (NP-III or higher). This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the populations studied, as Liang et al. (2014) primarily focused on registered nurses, whereas our study exclusively examined NPs [36].

Regarding departmental differences, there is no consensus in the literature on whether cultural competence varies by work setting. While some studies suggest that cultural competence varies depending on working sites [36], others report no such differences [8]. Differences in the study populations may again explain the inconsistent findings. Liang et al. (2014) focused on registered nurses, whereas Liu et al. (2022) exclusively studied licensed NPs in practice [8, 36]. Our study found that NPs working in pediatric departments had significantly lower cultural competence than those in other departments, suggesting that departmental differences warrant further investigation.

Although previous research has reported a positive correlation between job tenure and cultural competence among NPs [8, 36], our study did not find a significant correlation between seniority and cultural competence in the final regression model. However, our analysis found a significant borderline correlation between nursing job tenure and the CCA-CV score. Furthermore, while previous studies have emphasized the importance of language proficiency and overseas training experience in enhancing cultural competence [39, 40], no significant association was observed in our study. Additionally, no difference in cultural competence was observed between NPs who had never cared for foreign patients and those who had prior encounters with foreign patients, contradicting previous findings [36]. The discrepancies in findings may stem from differences in study populations. Our participants were primarily NPs or NP candidates, whereas Dobrowolska et al. (2020) focused on intensive care nurses, Repo et al. (2017) studied nursing students, and Liang et al. (2014) investigated registered nurses [36, 39, 40]. These differences underscore the importance of further research specifically targeting NPs to better understand the factors influencing cultural competence in this unique population.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design of this study limits the ability to establish causality between influential factors such as professional role identity, prior participation in cultural competence courses, nursing career ladder levels, working departments, and cultural competence. Second, although participants were recruited from hospitals of three varying levels across northern and eastern Taiwan, the generalizability of the findings to other populations or regions may be constrained.

Third, in the absence of a widely recognized and validated questionnaire designed to measure professional role identity among nurse practitioners, this study employed a newly developed PRI questionnaire. While the instrument has not yet undergone complete psychometric validation procedures—such as exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis—its content validity was established through expert review (I-CVI = 0.96; S-CVI = 0.81), and internal consistency was supported by pilot testing (Cronbach’s α = 0.84) and a larger independent sample (α = 0.90). The PRI also demonstrated strong theoretical alignment, as professional role identity measured by this tool emerged as the most significant predictor of cultural competence. Nonetheless, further validation of its construct structure is warranted in future research.

Fourth, while this study followed the original structure and scoring method of the CCA-CV—where the Cultural Awareness and Sensitivity (CAS) and Cultural Competence Behavior (CCB) subscales are summed to yield a total score—we observed a disproportionate contribution of the subscales to the total score in our sample [15, 17, 41]. Specifically, the total CCA-CV score correlated highly with the CCB subscale (r = 0.979). Still, it only weakly correlated with CAS (r = 0.244), and the correlation between CAS and CCB was negligible (r = 0.044). These findings may indicate that the two subscales capture distinct dimensions of cultural competence in this population. Although we retained the original scoring approach to ensure consistency with previous studies, this pattern suggests that future research should consider conducting exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis to evaluate whether combining these subscales into a single score remains psychometrically appropriate in nurse practitioner populations.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical role of professional role identity in shaping cultural competence among nurse practitioners (NPs). The findings reveal a significant association between these constructs, suggesting that strengthening professional role identity could be a strategic avenue for improving cultural competence in clinical practice. Participation in cultural competence courses and early career ladder positions emerged as influential factors, while lower competence levels were observed among NPs in pediatric departments.

The results contribute to the growing evidence supporting the integration of comprehensive cultural competence training into NP education and practice, alongside a focus on fostering professional role identity. As NPs increasingly assume roles in primary care and underserved communities, addressing the complex needs of diverse patient populations requires embedding cultural awareness, knowledge, sensitivity, and skills into NP curricula and the continuous development of professional role identity.

While these findings provide valuable insights, further longitudinal research is necessary to establish causal relationships and explore the dynamic interplay between professional role identity and cultural competence over time. Expanding future studies to include diverse settings and populations will enhance the generalizability of findings and inform evidence-based strategies to promote culturally competent care in nursing practice.
