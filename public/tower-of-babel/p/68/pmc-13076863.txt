Tissue and maturation specific DNA methylation dynamics of gonadotropin genes in chub mackerel (Scomber japonicus) using cost-effective targeted bisulfite sequencing
Authors: Mariel Galotta, Yukiko Ogino, Naoki Nagano, Takehiko Itoh, Michiya Matsuyama, Tapas Chakraborty, Kohei Ohta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFish reproduction is tightly regulated by hormonal pathways, and epigenetic mechanisms may modulate key reproductive genes such as the gonadotropins (GtHs). Determining whether DNA methylation influences GtH expression during maturation is a crucial step before exploring how these regulatory pathways might respond to environmental cues. Chub mackerel (Scomber japonicus) is a key species in fisheries and aquaculture, where improving reproductive performance is a priority. In this study, we used an optimized Targeted bisulfite sequencing (BS-seq) protocol, to investigate methylation patterns in GtH promoters across tissues and age groups. We complemented this analysis with RT-qPCR and functional validation of lhb promoter activity using reporter assays in cultured cells. The modified protocol allowed analysis of 2880 targets, exceeding Sanger sequencing capacity and improving efficiency and cost-effectiveness for larger studies. The DNA methylation patterns exhibited tissue- and maturation-specific dynamics. Fshb promoter methylation was stable in most tissues but varied in the gonad and pituitary: immature gonads had lower methylation, while mature pituitaries showed hypomethylated CpG sites and the lowest overall methylation. In contrast, methylation in the gene body of lhb showed little variation across tissues but was higher in the mature pituitary. In vitro assays identified a silencing regulatory element (222–232 bp) overlapping a putative Sp1 binding site and methylation at this position was low (< 10%). Furthermore, tissue- and maturation-specific variations in DNA methylation enzymes expression (dnmt1, tet1, tet2, tet3) suggest changes linked to reproductive maturation rather than age-related epigenetic drift. This study reveals maturation-related epigenetic regulation of gonadotropins in fish. By optimizing a Targeted BS-seq method, we identified variation in fshb and lhb methylation that appears linked to changes in gene expression during reproductive development, and we found that a regulatory element may influence lhb expression through Sp1 binding. Introduction

Fish are highly sensitive to environmental changes, which can significantly impact their development and sexual characteristics1,2. Research on the field is critical not only for the survival and thriving of natural populations but also for improving the sustainability of marine aquaculture, especially since fish are mostly bred under natural conditions. Furthermore, while marine capture fisheries still account for a major part of total world production, the percentage of overfished stocks (those outside biologically sustainable levels) has increased3, which positions aquaculture as a possible solution for reducing pressure on natural stocks and helping to prevent overfishing. Achieving sustainable aquaculture, however, requires a deeper understanding of fish reproductive maturation and their sensitivity to environmental cues.

One promising approach for understanding and enhancing fish adaptability lies in the field of epigenetics, which explores the complex relationship between environmental factors and gene expression4. Among epigenetic mechanisms, DNA methylation has become a central research focus due to its role in phenotypic adaptability to environmental changes. Its regulatory influence on gene expression is complex: while promoter methylation typically represses transcription, some studies in vertebrates suggest that gene body methylation may have distinct or even opposite effects. For example, in mammals, active gene expression has been observed despite extensive methylation of intragenic CpG islands5,6, emphasizing the context-dependent nature of DNA methylation. One key mechanism by which DNA methylation regulates gene expression is through the inhibition of transcription factor (TF) binding by cytosine (C) methylation. Methylation-sensitive TFs are prevented from binding their motifs and gene activation can be triggered if this epigenetic mark is lost7. However, despite advancements in understanding the role of DNA methylation in gene regulation, research on DNA methylation in commercially valuable marine fish species remains limited.

In fish, DNA methylation plays a crucial role in fine-tuning gene expression during reproductive maturation, as demonstrated in studies about epigenetic regulation of genes like cyp19a and dmrt1 in Japanese flounder8, topmouth culter9, and chub mackerel10, among others. However, less is known about the role of DNA methylation in regulating the expression of two key glycoprotein hormones: the gonadotropins (GtHs), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH). These hormones are synthesized and secreted in the anterior pituitary11and play a central role in regulating gametogenesis and steroid hormone synthesis in the gonads12. While seasonal changes in GtH expression have been explored in teleosts (e.g., chub mackerel13, largemouth bass14), whether DNA methylation is involved in regulating these genes remains unclear. GtHs are therefore promising candidates for studying epigenetic regulation, as analyzing methylation variability within their promoters could help clarify both seasonal expression patterns and the mechanisms underlying reproductive control in fish.

Another factor to consider is that, as interest in DNA methylation patterns grows, the demand for more efficient and cost-effective sequencing methods increases as well. Traditionally, analyzing DNA methylation patterns relied heavily on Sanger sequencing (Sanger-seq), a time-consuming and resource-intensive method. However, with the advent of Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) techniques, there is a newfound opportunity to delve deeper into genetic and epigenetic variations. Yet, while NGS data provide more robust results, whole-genome sequencing remains costly, limiting the number of samples that can be processed. Combining the high-throughput power of NGS with targeted PCR amplification, enables simultaneous processing of multiple samples, reducing time and cost. Vial-Pradel and colleagues introduced a modified Targeted Bisulfite sequencing (BS-seq) method using an NGS platform, incorporating four-nucleotide indexes in primers to increase sample throughput, called SIMON (Simple methods for analyzing DNA methylation by targeted bisulfite next-generation sequencing) using the plant Arabidopsis thaliana as model15.

Building on this approach, this study aims to assess its applicability for processing large sample sizes in a non-model species: the chub mackerel (Scomber japonicus). Studying DNA methylation patterns of GtHs in commercially important fish could contribute to better rearing strategies, boosting productivity and sustainability. Increasing sample sizes not only strengthens the robustness of the dataset but also enables the statistical analysis of a combination of factors, such as sex, age, and maturation status, that may influence observed methylation pattern differences. As data model complexity grows, identifying methylation patterns becomes more statistically demanding, making larger sample sizes essential to maintain statistical power and reliability. Thus, this study has two main objectives: (1) to examine the DNA methylation status of GtH promoters across tissues and maturation stages in chub mackerel, and (2) to optimize a cost-effective Targeted BS-seq protocol based on the method described in15, which has seen limited application to date.

Methods

Fish rearing and sample collection

Fish used in this study were reared in 10 m x 10 m open-sea cages at fish farms in Karatsu Bay, Japan, at a density of approximately 50 fish per cage. They were fed with commercial pelleted feed (Marubeni Nissin, Japan) and routinely monitored for reproductive status. Fish between 5 and 11 months old were sampled at three time points: October 2020 (n = 30), January 2021 (n = 23), and April 2021 (n = 43). October and January represented periods in which fish were predominantly immature, whereas April falls within the reproductive season of chub mackerel and therefore included mature individuals. These sampling points were selected to capture both immature and mature stages, while January served as an intermediate point to assess potential progressive changes in DNA methylation. All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kyushu University (approval number: A23-269-0). Euthanasia procedures were conducted in accordance with Japan’s Act on Welfare and Management of Animals (the Law of Humane Treatment and Management of Animals), the Regulations on Animal Experimentation at Kyushu University, and the ARRIVE guidelines16. Fish were placed in a seawater–ice mixture to induce hypothermia (rapid chilling) and then transported on ice to maintain refrigeration. All fish were confirmed dead upon handling, ensuring no distress or inhumane treatment occurred. Gonad, liver, hypothalamus, pituitary, and brain were stored in DNA/RNA shield (Zymo Research, USA) to be processed later. Additionally, a mid-section of the gonad was fixed in Bouin’s solution for histological analysis and determination of maturation status. Following hematoxylin/eosin staining, the developmental stage of ovaries and testes was evaluated according to the reproductive cycle described for chub mackerel17 (Fig. S1).

Nucleic acid isolation and bisulfite conversion treatment

Genomic DNA and total RNA were isolated from a total of 96 fish, randomly selected from the three separate samplings. The isolation was performed using the Quick-DNA/RNA MagBead kit (Zymo Research, USA) following manufacturer’s instructions, and nucleic acid concentrations were measured with a NanoDrop Lite spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Subsequently, approximately 200 ng of gDNA was bisulfite converted using the EZ-96 DNA Methylation-Gold MagPrep kit (Zymo Research, USA). The bisulfite-converted DNA was then stored until further use.

Primer design and PCR amplification

BS-seq was performed to study the DNA methylation patterns of the lhb and fshb genes. The sequences used were retrieved from the whole genome of chub mackerel available in our laboratory (see ‘Data availability’ section). Primers were designed to target the regulatory region surrounding the start codon of both genes, with amplicons ranging from 220 to 331 bp in length (Table S1), covering a region of approximately 1500 bp. Primer design was primarily focused on including all in silico detected CpGs within the analyzed region. This localized approach is most effective with preliminary methylation data, to ensure choosing epigenetically relevant areas, though this may not be the case for many studies.

To facilitate sample multiplexing and subsequent data analysis, a unique four-nucleotide sequence (hereafter referred to as ‘barcode’) was added to the 5’ end of each primer, acting as an inner index, following the approach described in15. In total, 8 different barcodes were designed and added to the primers out of 24 possible combinations. It is worth noting that some barcode-primer combinations may differentially affect amplification performance. Therefore, these combinations should be tested beforehand. Then, bisulfite-converted DNA was amplified with KOD-Multi & Epi enzyme (Toyobo, Japan). Reaction conditions were as follows: pre-denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 45 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 57 °C for 30 s and extension at 68 °C for 15 s. The annealing temperature slightly changed depending on the target amplified, but it mainly oscillated around 57 °C. PCR products were then purified with the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up kit (Takara Bio, Japan).

Library preparation and NGS sequencing

Prior to library preparation, PCR amplified samples with different barcodes were pooled into a total of 12 tubes, each containing 8 samples. Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA), following manufacturer’s instructions. Since the input material consisted of pre-amplified PCR products, the fragmentation and size selection steps were omitted. AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Agencourt, USA) were used for all cleanup steps, and NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (NEB, USA) were added for indexing. The combination of 8 different barcodes (introduced during the previous PCR amplification step) and 12 Illumina single indexes generates a total of 96 unique combinations, each one corresponding to an individual fish. Library quality was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer before sequencing on a Novaseq platform (paired-end, 2 × 250 bp). This experimental workflow was replicated for each tissue, five times in total.

NGS data processing

Data analysis was carried out using QIAGEN CLC Genomics Workbench (version 20.0.4). The files were already demultiplexed by Illumina indexes. A second round of demultiplexing was conducted, using the primer-in-built barcodes, and allowing mismatches. Next, reads were trimmed and low-quality filtered, applying the following parameters: quality limit 0.05, ambiguous limit of 2, minimum number of 15 nucleotides, and removing homopolymers, broken pairs and adapters (barcodes together with primers). Mapping was then conducted, with the original amplicon sequences as reference. The applied parameters were as follows: match score 1, mismatch cost 2, affine gap cost (insertion open 6, extend 1; deletion open 6, extend 1), length fraction 0.80, similarity fraction 0.80. Global alignment and auto-detection of paired distances were disabled, and non-specific matches were ignored. Finally, methylation calling was performed, and coverage information was retrieved, to continue the analysis in RStudio.

Additionally, power analyses were performed to estimate required sample sizes under different effect sizes and power levels, using the R packages “pwr”18 and “statmod”19.

Gene expression measurement by RT-qPCR

RT-qPCR reactions were carried out to assess the gene expression levels of the GtHs (fshb and lhb), as well as several enzymes related to DNA methylation and maintenance (dnmt1, tet1, tet2, and tet3). Initially, approximately 1 µg of previously isolated total RNA underwent reverse transcription into cDNA utilizing Superscript IV First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen, USA), using oligo-dT primers and following manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed using SSoFast EvaGreen Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA) in a qTOWER3 G touch machine (Analytik jena, Germany). The endogenous reference gene 60 S ribosomal protein L8 (rpl8) was also amplified, for data normalization (primer sequences available in Table S1). All primers were designed to span exon–exon junctions to minimize genomic DNA amplification. Additionally, single-band amplification was confirmed by electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gels to confirm primer efficiency. Samples were run in duplicate and no-template controls were included to check reagent contamination and primer-dimer formation. Cycling conditions were as follows: an initial pre-denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. Gene expression for each gene was calculated using the standard curve method, and only curves that met the required efficiency and fit were included in the analysis.

Lhb vector construction, cell culture and dual luciferase reporter assay

To further investigate regulatory elements and their potential involvement in lhb regulation, a search for transcription factor binding sites (TFBSs) was conducted using the JASPAR database20. Two putative Specificity protein 1 (Sp1) binding sites were selected for deletion, located at positions − 656/− 645 (GTTCTGCCTAC) and 222/232 (GTGGCGCTGT), with nucleotide positions relative to the ATG start codon considered as + 1. Sp1 was prioritized based on a preliminary bibliography search, and because its predicted motifs were overlapping with (or close to) CpG sites of interest, making it a strong candidate for methylation-sensitive regulation.

Specific primers were designed to amplify the proximal promoter and exon1-intron1 region of lhb, encompassing the sequence previously studied for DNA methylation patterns (Table S1) and including an overhang sequence overlapping the pGL3-TK vector. PCR was performed using PrimeSTAR Max DNA Polymerase (Takara Bio, Japan) under the following conditions: 45 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 15 s. The purified amplicon was then inserted into the XhoI and BglII restriction enzyme sites of the pGL3-TK plasmid using the In-Fusion® Snap Assembly kit (Takara Bio, Japan).

Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells were cultured in Ham’s F-12 medium (Wako, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 10,000 cells per well and, the following day, were co-transfected with jetPRIME® (Polyplus, France) using 100 ng of a mix of reporter plasmids: lhb region/pGL3-TK (either promoter or exon1-intron1 region, with and without the introduced deletions) and the internal control pRL-SV40 (Promega, USA), in triplicate. Four hours after transfection, the medium was changed. Cells were harvested 24 h after transfection, and the Dual-Luciferase Reporter (DLR) assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega, USA). Luminescence was measured using the luminometer function of the Thermo Scientific Varioskan® Flash.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio (version 2023.06.1 + 524). Relative methylation levels at each position were calculated as the percentage of Cs over the total read coverage for that position (after the BS treatment only methylated Cs remain as Cs, while unmethylated Cs are converted to uracils and subsequently to thymines during PCR). Since the data did not follow a normal distribution, comparisons were made using the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s test for post-hoc analysis.

Results

Efficiency of data processing

Each tissue dataset was processed using the same workflow. After two rounds of demultiplexing, between 92.04 and 94.33% of the original reads were assigned to a barcode group. The amount of the reads that could not be allocated to any group was relatively low, averaging 7.04% of the total reads. The demultiplexing efficiency for each barcode was consistent, averaging 11.62% of the total reads (Fig. 1A). Based on these results the chosen barcodes were effective, assuming that the original input material for library preparation was similar across samples. Furthermore, after trimming and low-quality filtering, more than 99% of the reads remained. The small percentage of reads removed was mainly due to homopolymers (Fig. 1B). The cleaned reads were then mapped to the reference sequence, resulting in a mapping efficiency ranging from 65.27% to 82.02% (Fig. 1C).

The scalability of the modified Targeted BS-seq protocol is demonstrated by the simultaneous processing and analysis of 2880 sample targets. This level of throughput far exceeds that of conventional Sanger-based bisulfite sequencing, which is typically limited to 10–20 samples per experiment. Power analysis (Fig. S2) indicates that detecting a small effect size (Cohen’s h = 0.2) between two groups with 80% power at a 5% significance level requires a minimum of 393 reads of coverage per CpG site, which would be virtually unattainable with Sanger-based methods.

Fshb DNA methylation pattern dynamics

We studied part of the promoter region of fshb in five different tissues from both immature (October and January samplings) and mature (April sampling) fish. Our analysis revealed that the average DNA methylation was more than 70% in most groups, except for immature gonads (55.94% in October 2020 and 64.05% in January 2021) and mature pituitaries (61.51%).

DNA methylation patterns in most tissues, such as the liver, hypothalamus, and brain, remained relatively stable across different maturation statuses. Interestingly, significant changes in methylation dynamics were observed only in gonad and pituitary, displaying opposite patterns: immature gonads were less methylated compared to the mature group, while pituitary of mature fish had many hypomethylated CpG positions, especially at two specific sites (− 1970 and − 1587) (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, in mature fish, the pituitary was the least methylated tissue among all those studied (Fig. 2B). Fshb was also significantly more expressed in mature fish (Fig. 2C).

Lhb DNA methylation pattern dynamics

Similar analyses were conducted to study DNA methylation patterns in lhb. CpG observations upstream of the START codon were excluded due to low coverage: liver (55), gonad (25), hypothalamus (129), brain (89), and pituitary (59), likely reflecting an amplicon-specific PCR issue. For position-wise pattern comparison, only positions present in all tissues were included.

Intronic DNA methylation patterns were consistent across different tissues with most CpG sites with low levels of methylation. Interestingly, several positions in the distal part of the promoter region (approximately 700 bp upstream of the start codon) were found to be hypermethylated (data not shown). This contrasts with the region immediately upstream and downstream of the START codon, which did not exhibit such high levels of methylation. A trend was observed in most tissues, particularly in the brain, hypothalamus, and liver, where most CpG sites showed lower methylation levels in the mature group compared to the immature fish caught in October. However, this trend was not observed in immature fish caught in January (Fig. 3A). In mature fish, average DNA methylation was highest in the pituitary gland compared to other tissues (Fig. 3B). Similar to fshb, the expression of lhb was highest in mature fish, sampled during the reproductive season (Fig. 3C).

One regulatory element in the lhb gene body exhibits silencing activity

While DNA methylation levels in lhb regulatory region did not exhibit a clear tissue-specific pattern, differences in average DNA methylation levels were observed in mature fish (Fig. 3B). It appears that DNA methylation is not playing a significant role, at least within the gene body and proximal promoter regions analyzed in this study. To further explore whether any regulatory elements within this region could be modulating lhb expression, an in vitro experiment was conducted, introducing two deletions that overlapped with putative binding sites for Sp1. The deletion at -656/-645 did not affect in vitro lhb promoter activation (Fig. 4A). However, the deletion within the gene body at position 222/232 significantly increased luciferase expression (p-value < 0.05, Kruskal-Wallis), when compared to the wild-type (WT) construct. Without that 10 bp sequence, luciferase activity was between 1.22 and 2.40 folds higher, compared to the WT (confidence interval 95%), suggesting a negative regulatory effect on activation (Fig. 4B). On the other hand, the observed DNA methylation level of CpG position 227, within this found regulatory element, was less than 10% in most cases, regardless of maturation status (Fig. 3A). At this point, it is unclear whether small changes in methylation at this position could affect TF binding to the DNA and, consequently, have a silencing effect on lhb expression.

Differential expression patterns of enzymes involved in DNA methylation maintenance

Gene expression of the ten-eleven translocation (TET) enzymes tet1, tet2 and tet3 and the DNA (cytosine-5)-methyltransferase 1 (dnmt1), enzymes responsible for maintaining DNA methylation dynamics was also measured. The expression of these enzymes varied greatly depending on the tissue and maturation status.

Different letters indicate significant differences (Dunn’s test). In the brain, gonad, liver, and hypothalamus, most tet genes expression were lower in mature fish, while dnmt1 expression was highest in mature fish particularly in the gonad and liver. Conversely, dnmt1 levels decreased with maturation in the hypothalamus. In the pituitary, tet2 and tet3 expressions were similar in both immature and mature fish, whereas tet1 expression was slightly lower in the January sampling, which corresponds to the immature (older) group. Dnmt1, on the other hand, remained low overall but showed a slight increase during the same period (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Double-index library preparation optimization and further improvements

Epigenetics has the potential to transform our understanding of phenotype generation and maintenance. By improving predictions of how early environmental exposures affect phenotypes and supporting the selection of traits, it can greatly benefit the aquaculture industry4, hence the importance of optimizing experimental workflows to study epigenetic markers.

In this study, we analyzed partial promoter regions of fshb and lhb across five different tissues in 96 fish, using a modified method of Targeted BS-seq. This resulted in a total of 2880 samples being simultaneously processed and analyzed. The read quality and mapping efficiency were generally sufficient to study the DNA methylation patterns in most samples, although efficiency was slightly lower for the pituitary tissue and for the lhb region. Coverage (number of reads) for each individual sample also varied greatly. We hypothesized that the main issue was not in the library preparation step, as good results were obtained for most fshb sequences, but rather in the PCR amplicon pooling step. For future experiments, rechecking PCR amplification efficiency is recommended, as the addition of four nucleotides at the 5’ end of primers could affect individual efficiencies. Additionally, samples should be pooled in an equimolar manner. It is important not to rely solely on concentration measurements but also to consider amplicon length to ensure a balanced representation of each amplicon in the mix, especially when the number of amplicons and/or the sample size is considerable. Although this step is time-consuming, it is crucial for obtaining high-quality input material for the subsequent library preparation.

In their 2019 article, Vial-Pradel and colleagues compared the SIMON method with Targeted BS Sanger-seq using A. thaliana. They found the results to be reproducible and demonstrated that the SIMON method provides the necessary read coverage to detect small changes in methylation levels more effectively15. In a previous experiment using BS Sanger-seq, we analyzed a similar region in the lhb intron from fish sampled at a different time (October 2019). Briefly comparing the accuracy of both methods, we observed greater data dispersion and less reliable results with the conventional Sanger-seq method. The differences in sample size and the volume of data analyzed significantly impact the accuracy of DNA methylation measurements. Typically, Sanger-seq experiments involve significantly lower read coverage (~ 10 reads), which further emphasizes their limitations compared to NGS methods. Although the samples were not identical and biological variation may contribute to some discrepancies, this comparison highlights the superiority of the modified Targeted BS-seq approach over the less robust read coverage of Sanger-seq.

The modified targeted BS-seq method (SIMON) is time and cost-effective

As mentioned earlier, we implemented the SIMON protocol, a modified version of Targeted BS-seq that incorporates 4-nucleotide barcodes to increase the volume of samples processed15. To date, this method has not been applied beyond the original study. In our experiment, we used only 12 Illumina indexes and 8 barcodes, but it is possible to include more, significantly increasing the number of samples that can be analyzed.

The main hurdle was that PCR amplification and sample pooling were time-consuming and incurred additional costs due to the use of barcoded primers. Unlike traditional BS Sanger-seq, which uses a single primer pair per target, the SIMON method requires one primer pair per barcode. Despite these extra costs, this modified Targeted BS-seq method remains superior and more robust than BS Sanger-seq. It eliminates the extra hours needed for cloning, plasmid purification, and individual sample preparation for sequencing, while also providing higher read coverage. Processing the same number of samples with BS Sanger-seq would be highly labor-intensive and inefficient. Although costs can vary depending on reagents and location, in our laboratory BS Sanger-seq proved substantially more expensive than SIMON, largely due to sequencing and cloning steps. Even without sequencing costs, SIMON remains more economical because of its simpler workflow. Additionally, adding barcodes to primers can be a cost-effective alternative to purchasing Illumina indexes for sample processing. Although designing barcoded primers requires more initial effort, it is still less expensive than acquiring oligo index sets for library preparation.

Importantly, simulations of power analysis showed that approximately 393 reads per CpG position are needed to detect small differences between two groups with 80% statistical power, a level of coverage that would be virtually unachievable using Sanger-seq.

Overall, the modified Targeted BS-seq method proved superior to traditional Sanger-seq in terms of efficiency and cost-effectiveness. It is particularly suitable for handling large sample volumes that exceed the capacity of standard sequencing index kits (e.g., NEBNext® Multiplex Oligos for Illumina) or, as mentioned, it can serve as an affordable alternative in laboratories where NGS library preparation is an infrequent practice, reducing costs by eliminating the need to purchase Illumina indexes.

Gonadotropins DNA methylation patterns varied depending on the tissue and maturation status

While optimizing the SIMON method, we also analyzed and compared DNA methylation patterns of both GtHs in five different tissues of immature and mature chub mackerel. We found that general methylation levels of fshb were significantly lower, and lhb higher in mature pituitaries, compared to the rest of tissues. It is important to note that DNA was extracted from whole tissues, which limits single-cell resolution. This is particularly relevant for heterogeneous tissues such as the pituitary or gonad. The pituitary gland of teleosts contains at least six different endocrine cell types, including gonadotropes, where FSH and LH are synthesized21. DNA methylation patterns probably vary across cell types, so differentially methylated CpG sites could be masked when processing such diverse tissues.

For fshb, general DNA methylation was relatively high (> 70%) and the patterns observed similar for most tissues. However, in mature pituitaries, some CpG positions were differentially lower methylated. Around the analyzed area, many putative TFBSs were observed, including Activating Protein-1 (AP-1), Pituitary Homeobox 1 (PITX) and LIM-Homeodomain 3 and 4 (LHX3/4). The role of these TFs in fshb regulation has been studied before, mainly in mammals. For example, in bovine pituitary, two AP-1 sites located within 200 bp distance of the transcription start site (TSS) are required for fshb expression stimulation by c-Jun and c-Fos22. A PITX1 site proximal to the transcription start site (TSS) was discovered to activate the rat fshb promoter in LβT2 cells. PITX also showed to have a synergistic effect with the gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), leading to further stimulation of fshb expression in GGH3-1’ cells23. Moreover, similar co-transfection in vitro experiments involving the porcine fshb promoter found that both LHX3a and Steroidogenic Factor 1 (SF-1) can activate it. They also studied the nuanced effects of LHX3a on promoter activity and found multiple positive and negative regulatory elements within the upstream promoter region spanning around 5000 bp. Specially at − 1378 bp and − 398 bp, they observed high inducibility by LHX3 and elevated basal promoter activity24. All three studies emphasize the significance of the proximal promoter region for fshb expression regulation. This finding aligns with the considerable number of TFBSs we identified in the proximal region of chub mackerel’s fshb promoter, including motifs for GATA2, LHX3/4, Smad2, PITX1, PITX2, PITX3, and AP-1. Although the overall DNA methylation patterns were similar, the differences observed in the pituitary may be crucial for the tissue- and maturation-specific expression of GtHs, potentially influencing their expression through an effect in TF binding to the DNA.

In the case of lhb, we observed overall low DNA methylation within the gene body across all tissues, with higher methylation levels in CpG sites further upstream, which were not included in the analysis due to lack of quality reads for mature hypothalamus samples. Notably, lhb methylation was highest in mature pituitaries, showing an opposite trend in other tissues and a positive association with gene expression, contrary to the expected link between methylation and gene silencing. This pattern does not align with findings from a comprehensive study on gene features and DNA methylation, which reported a clear inverse correlation between first intron methylation and gene expression across multiple vertebrates25. However, as mentioned in the introduction, DNA methylation is highly context-dependent, with several studies showing that it can be linked to transcriptional activation or have mixed effects depending on its location6,26,27. Although we did not observe clear tissue-specific methylation patterns in the analyzed lhb region, suggesting exon-intron methylation may not drive its differential regulation, our in vitro experiment identified a regulatory element within this region (position 222/232). Deletion of this element led to increased activation, indicating that the exon1-intron1 region of lhb may still modulate gene expression. The elevated methylation levels in mature pituitaries, together with the presence of a negative regulatory element, align with the observed increase in lhb expression in this tissue. This element, which overlaps a putative Sp1 binding site, may have its silencing effect weakened by the higher methylation levels, potentially interfering with Sp1 binding and reducing its influence on gene transcription. In a previous study, two Sp1 sites were identified in the rat lhb promoter and hypothesized to be involved in GnRH-induced lhb expression28. Mutations in these binding sites decreased GnRH responsiveness, hindering promoter activation. Further deletion experiments of other TFBSs in the rat lhb promoter revealed that Sp1, along with SF-1 and Egr-1, contributes to GnRH responsiveness. The disruption of Sp1 binding allows Egr-1 to mediate GnRH responsiveness, suggesting a compensatory relationship between these factors29. Both SF-1 and Egr-1 are present in the intronic region of chub mackerel, downstream of the Sp1 site and partially overlapping (position 416/436). It would be interesting to further analyze if the observed effect in the promoter also replicates in the intronic region.

We did in fact measure DNA methylation at the 227 position (within the deleted TFBS) but no significant differences in methylation levels were observed at this position across different tissues in our study. Methylation levels at this site remained consistently low, with only minor variations, suggesting that its role in regulatory element binding may be subtle or context-dependent. Further investigation is needed to clarify its impact on gene regulation. Even subtle differences in methylation, which may be sufficient to affect regulation, can go undetected without single-cell analysis which could be the case for what we observed in position 227.

The methylation dynamics are regulated by Dnmt1, which maintains methylation patterns during DNA replication, and TET enzymes, which facilitate active demethylation by oxidizing 5-methylcytosine30. When measured, we observed tissue- and maturation-specific variations in the expression of these DNA methylation-related enzymes, suggesting distinct methylation dynamics across tissues. Overall, dnmt1 expression increased while tet expression decreased with maturation, except in the pituitary, where they exhibited consistent expression levels across sampling times. These expression patterns appear to be related to the maturation process rather than general age-related changes, often called “epigenetic drift”, which refers to the gradual buildup of epigenetic marks over time that can affect normal gene regulation31. The relatively small age differences among the fish (ranging from 5 to 11 months, which is not particularly significant for S. japonicus given its average lifespan) suggest that these changes are more likely due to the onset of the reproductive season, as seen in the more pronounced differences in the mature group compared to the immature fish sampled in October and January.

Furthermore, DNA methylation marks in the genome are established through active mechanisms, whereas de-methylation can occur through either active or passive mechanisms32. The decreased dnmt1 expression in immature fish could indicate passive de-methylation and dynamic changes in DNA methylation patterns. In contrast, mature fish may experience higher levels of methylation due to active methylation by Dnmt1, which is reinforced by the low TETs gene expression. However, these enzymes have genome-wide roles, so changes in their expression may not directly account for the specific regulatory differences observed in LH and FSH loci.

Conclusions

In summary, we analyzed tissue-specific DNA methylation patterns and identified differentially methylated CpG sites in mature pituitaries for fshb, while also optimizing a Targeted BS-seq method that proved more efficient, cost-effective, and reliable than traditional BS Sanger-seq. Its higher read coverage, reduced labor, and scalability make it a practical tool for epigenetic research in non-model species and in laboratories with limited access to standard NGS workflows. Additionally, we discovered a regulatory element within the lhb gene body that exhibits silencing effects. We also identified other putative TFBSs, which could serve as a starting point for further investigation into regulatory mechanisms. This is the case for Sp1, whose involvement in lhb regulation was described in two rat studies conducted over two decades ago, yet its role in teleosts remains largely unknown. These findings contribute to the limited information available on the DNA methylation–based regulation of GtHs, particularly in fish. As GtHs are conserved across vertebrates, our results may offer broader insights into the epigenetic control of reproductive hormones in other fish species and even in higher vertebrates. Further research should therefore focus on expanding our understanding in this area through comparative and functional studies across groups.
