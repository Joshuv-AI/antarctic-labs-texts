Effects of Hurricanes and the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) Pandemic on Cervical Cancer Diagnosis in Puerto Rico
Authors: Eduardo J. Santiago-Rodríguez, Tonatiuh Suárez Ramos, Jeslie M. Ramos-Cartagena, Carlos R. Torres-Cintrón, Ashish A. Deshmukh, Karen J. Ortiz-Ortiz, Ana P. Ortiz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Hurricanes and the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic affected the diagnosis of cervical cancer in Puerto Rico.

Puerto Rico, home to nearly 2 million women, has the highest cervical cancer incidence in the United States (11 cases/100,000 women), and the incidence is rising.1,2 Puerto Rico has been affected by major events in recent years. In September 2017, Puerto Rico was hit by two hurricanes, damaging the infrastructure and resulting in thousands of deaths.3,4 Later, during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, Puerto Rico was one of the first U.S. jurisdictions to implement lockdowns to limit the transmission of the virus.5 These major events disrupted the normal functioning of the health care system on the island. As a result, screening rates for cervical cancer decreased sharply.6 In this study, we evaluated the effects of hurricanes and COVID-19 restrictions on cervical cancer diagnosis in Puerto Rico.

METHODS

This study used data from the Puerto Rico Central Cancer Registry, which collects sociodemographic and clinical information on all individuals with cancer diagnoses in Puerto Rico. The number of cervical cancer cases (International Classification of Diseases for Oncology codes C53.0-C53.9; histology codes 9050-9055, 9140, 9590-9993 were excluded) diagnosed between January 2012 and December 2021 was identified and aggregated by month.

Interrupted time series analysis was performed on monthly counts of cervical cancer cases. Four time periods were analyzed: 1) before major events (January 2012–August 2017), 2) after the hurricanes and up to the recovery peak (September 2017–March 2018), 3) after the hurricane recovery peak and before COVID-19 restrictions (April 2018–March 2020), and 4) after the start of COVID-19 restrictions up to the end of study period (April 2020–December 2021). Data were fit using first-order autoregression with the Prais-Winsten method, considering the autocorrelation of data over time.7 The lincom command was used to estimate expected cases and 95% CIs at specific months.8 We estimated changes in cervical cancer cases at key moments, estimated trends in each time period, and calculated the percentage difference between the level changes and the expected count in the absence of the events (counterfactual), using the following formula: (level change/expected cases)×100.9 Analyses were conducted using Stata 18.

All the information evaluated was de-identified, and the University of Puerto Rico Comprehensive Cancer Center IRB classified this study as exempt.

RESULTS

Monthly counts and cervical cancer trends in Puerto Rico from 2012 to 2021 are shown in Figure 1, and estimates of different key moments and periods are presented in Table 1. Before the hurricanes, the trend of cervical cancer diagnoses was relatively stable, with an average of 20 cases (95% CI, 18–22) diagnosed per month. After the hurricanes (end of September 2017), diagnosed cases dropped by 62.1% and reached prehurricane levels in the subsequent 5 months. After the hurricane recovery peak (March 2018), diagnosed cases were 13.7% lower than expected. After the COVID-19 restrictions were implemented (April 2020), only one case was reported. In the subsequent months until the end of 2021, an upward trend of diagnosed cases was observed, but diagnoses did not reach pre–major event levels.

DISCUSSION

Hurricanes and the COVID-19 pandemic affected the diagnosis of cervical cancer in Puerto Rico. Although the number of diagnosed cases returned to prehurricane levels within 5 months, at the end of the study period (ie, 20 months after COVID-19 lockdowns), the number of diagnosed cases did not reach the expected total. This is consistent with previous knowledge that shows that major events, including natural disasters, affect the provision of preventive services and detection of cancer and may result in delayed diagnosis and higher mortality.10

Limitations of this study include the fact that we could not consider fluctuations in the population at risk after the major events, which might have influenced the number of cases observed. We included data for almost 2 years after the onset of the pandemic; future years of data could provide a more complete assessment of the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on the detection and report of cervical cancer cases.

In this study, we documented the immediate decline in the number of diagnosed cervical cancer cases in Puerto Rico after major disruptive events. Of concern, in the years before these events, the incidence of cervical cancer in Puerto Rico showed a constant increase.2 Delays in cancer diagnosis could translate into late-stage disease and affect survival. Future research should consider the implications of delays in detection on patients' courses of treatment and survival, as well as on the health care systems of the island and its preparedness for future major events. Public health–level and system-level preparedness efforts should help guarantee timely diagnosis and treatment of high-grade cervical disease and cervical cancer for women in Puerto Rico.
