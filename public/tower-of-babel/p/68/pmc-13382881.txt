Atropine for the treatment of paediatric sialorrhea: a retrospective case series
Authors: Natalie Sloof, Julie Strychowsky, Agnieszka Dzioba, Breanna A Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis retrospective case series examined the efficacy of sublingual atropine drops for paediatric patients with sialorrhea followed at the multidisciplinary sialorrhea clinic at Children's Hospital, London Health Sciences Centre.MethodsA retrospective review of patients seen for management of sialorrhea at the multidisciplinary sialorrhea clinic between January 1, 2015 and September 30, 2023 was completed. Patient demographics, comorbidities, interventions, frequency and severity of sialorrhea, and adverse effects were recorded. Drooling frequency and severity scale (DFSS) scores were collected to examine the effectiveness of atropine sublingual drops. A descriptive analysis and Wilcox signed-rank tests were conducted to compare pre- and post-treatment DFSS scores.ResultsTwenty-one patients were included in the final analysis. The median DFSS score pretreatment was 4 for frequency and 4 for severity. Post-treatment, the median DFSS score decreased to 3 for both frequency (P = 0.006) and severity (P = 0.003), representing a statistically and clinically significant change.ConclusionAtropine sublingual drops demonstrated efficacy for decreasing both drooling frequency and severity in a paediatric patient population.

Introduction

Sialorrhea is a chronic problem affecting paediatric patients, particularly those with underlying neurodevelopmental disorders. Contributing factors include disorganized tongue movements, reduced oral sensation, incomplete lip closure, inadequate swallowing or dysphagia (1–3). Consequences of sialorrhea include aspiration pneumonia, skin breakdown or irritation, secondary infection and negative impacts on self-esteem and socialization (2,4–6).

Management of sialorrhea is guided by the impact on quality of life and/or the presence of complications (7). Three main management strategies for sialorrhea exist: conservative, medical and surgical (6). Conservative management includes interventions that aim to mitigate the consequences of sialorrhea and include the use of bibs, positioning, suctioning, oral rehabilitation and optimization of oral fluid intake. Medical management relies on either locally or systemically administered anticholinergic agents, given that the physiologic basis of salivation is parasympathetic activation of muscarinic subtype 3 receptors (1). Agents studied and reported in the literature include atropine, benztropine, glycopyrrolate, scopolamine/hyoscine and ipratropium bromide (8–13). When initiating medical management of sialorrhea, one must consider potential adverse anticholinergic effects that include constipation, urinary retention, flushing, tachycardia, blurred vision, irritability, sedation, headache and behavioural changes (14). Surgical and procedural options aim to diminish or redirect salivary flow and include intraglandular botulinum toxin injection or salivary gland ligation (15,16).

The efficacy of various anticholinergic agents in the management of paediatric sialorrhea is not well established, as much of the literature describes adult palliative care populations. There remains a lack of consensus on which pharmaceutical agent is most efficacious in sialorrhea management (14). A systemic review published in 2022 noted that while atropine has been shown to improve sialorrhea in paediatric patients, there is a paucity of data establishing its efficacy and tolerability (14). More recently, studies examining the use of atropine in the paediatric setting have emerged. A retrospective review of the anticholinergic prescribing patterns for paediatric patients with neurodevelopmental disabilities at a children's hospital showed that atropine is a commonly used medication for paediatric sialorrhea, representing the first-line medication pursued in 50% of patients treated at their institution, with varying dosing regimens (17). No data on efficacy or tolerability were collected as a limitation of the study design. A study of hospitalized paediatric patients showed a statistically significant reduction in the teacher drooling scale with short-term sublingual atropine administration, without any side effects reported (9). Despite promising results, the literature remains limited, and more data will help to guide management of chronic sialorrhea. The purpose of this retrospective case series is to capture the efficacy and tolerability of sublingual atropine drops for the treatment of sialorrhea in paediatric patients at our centre.

Methods

Data collection

A retrospective review of paediatric patients followed by the multidisciplinary sialorrhea clinic at Children's Hospital, London Health Sciences Centre between January 1, 2015 and September 30, 2023 was completed. The multidisciplinary sialorrhea clinic cares for paediatric patients who are referred by their primary care provider or subspecialist for assessment and management of sialorrhea. Management recommendations for sialorrhea (including whether to pursue conservative, medical or procedural/surgical therapies) are through shared decision-making with caregivers based on the severity of and complications of sialorrhea, efficacy of previous trials and patient/parent preference. Generally, medical treatments are offered prior to procedural or surgical treatments. Patients were included in this retrospective study if they were initiated on treatment with atropine sublingual drops between January 1, 2015 and September 30, 2023. Patients whose follow-up assessment was not available were excluded. This study received approval from the Western University Health Sciences Research Ethics Board (REB#122157). A waiver of consent was granted.

Data were collected and managed using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) tools. Patient demographics, comorbidities, clinical presentation, previous and concurrent interventions, efficacy (captured by drooling frequency and severity assessment tools) and adverse effects of medication were collected.

The primary outcome was the efficacy of atropine for the treatment of sialorrhea as measured by a change in the drooling frequency and severity scale (DFSS), which is a tool to determine the impact of drooling (Figure 1) (18). The DFSS consists of subjective measures of drooling frequency and severity, with higher scores reflecting more severe outcomes. The DFSS is routinely completed during clinic visits by the parent/caregiver as a part of the patient's ongoing care. These scores were reviewed to compare sialorrhea management pre- and post-treatment.

Drooling frequency and severity scale (DFSS) developed by Thomas-Stonell and Greenberg (18).

Data analysis

Summary statistics, including means, standard deviations, medians, interquartile ranges (IQR) and frequency statistics, were calculated for the appropriate variable types. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were conducted to compare pre- vs. postatropine treatment DFSS scores. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (IBM Corp. Released 2022. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). Statistical significance was set a priori at 0.05. A 1-point difference pre- vs. post-treatment in DFSS scores denoted a clinically significant change.

Results

There were 141 patients 0 to 17 years old seen in the sialorrhea clinic between January 1, 2015 and September 30, 2023. Sixty-six patients were initiated on atropine treatment by our clinic during the study period. Forty-three had documented follow-up visits during the study period. Two patients had not used the atropine at all as prescribed. Of the remaining, 21 patients had complete baseline and post-treatment DFSS and were included in the final analysis.

Patient characteristics

The median age at initial treatment was 10.1 years (IQR, 8.9 years); 47.6% of the patients were male. The most common underlying diagnoses were confirmed chromosomal abnormality (38.1%) and cerebral palsy GMFCS 4 to 5 (33.3%). All patients exhibited developmental delays or speech abnormalities. Nineteen of the twenty-one patients had multiple developmental delays or were diagnosed with global developmental delay (90.5%), one had isolated speech delay and one had Wilson’s disease with associated dysarthria. The most commonly reported negative effects of sialorrhea were social concerns or teasing (38.1%) followed by pneumonia (28.6%), skin breakdown (28.6%) and choking (28.6%). Prior to the atropine trial, 13 patients had no previous treatment for sialorrhea, 4 patients trialed ipratropium bromide, 1 patient trialed scopolamine, 1 patient trialed glycopyrrolate only, 1 patient trialed glycopyrrolate and had a parotid ligation and submandibular gland excision, 1 patient had a bilateral submandibular gland excision only and 1 had a four-duct ligation only. Sialorrhea treatment

During the atropine trial, patients in this study reported using a range of one to two drops one to four times daily via a sublingual or buccal route with a concentration of atropine of 0.5% to 1%. The median dose used by patients was one drop twice daily. The median length of the atropine trial was 42 weeks (IQR 69); 47% (n = 10) of patients continued taking atropine until discharge from the clinic or study conclusion. Of the patients who stopped taking atropine, reasons for discontinuation included lack of perceived efficacy (n = 5), difficulties with availability (n = 1) and occurrence of behaviour concerns (n = 1). Three patients experienced resolution of their symptoms such that no treatment was necessary. Two patients went on to pursue ipratropium bromide; one patient went on to pursue glycopyrrolate, followed by scopolamine, followed by botulinum toxin injection; and five patients pursued botulinum toxin injection only.

Reported side effects of atropine included constipation (n = 2), behavioural concerns (n = 1) and facial flushing (n = 1); 81% (n = 17) of patients reported experiencing no side effects. Two patients described an unpleasant taste.

Efficacy of treatment

At the start of the atropine trial, the median DFSS score was 4 for frequency (range 2 to 4) and 4 for severity (range 3 to 5). There was improvement in the median score to 3 for frequency (range 1 to 4; P = 0.006) and 3 for severity (range 1 to 5; P = 0.003) at post-treatment follow-up. This represents a statistically significant improvement in both drooling frequency and severity (Figure 2). Practically, this reflects a change in frequency from ‘constantly drools’ to ‘frequently drools’ and a change in severity from ‘severe’ to ‘moderate’, which was considered clinically significant.

DISCUSSION

The medical management of sialorrhea in the paediatric population remains challenging due to a paucity of data regarding efficacy and side effects of the various available agents. Barriers such as local availability, cost and provider comfort level with prescribing anticholinergics may also contribute to treating this challenging condition. However, in recent years, there has been more literature published on the use of atropine sublingually for paediatric patient populations. We have been using atropine ophthalmic drops sublingually/bucally for our patients over the last 10 years. This review of our local experience demonstrates a clinically and statistically significant improvement in drooling severity and frequency. A similar reduction in drooling symptoms has been demonstrated in two prospective studies (19,20) and one retrospective study (17) using atropine in paediatric populations. In the study by Scofano Dias et al, paediatric patients with cerebral palsy treated with sublingual atropine showed a reduction in the drooling impact score (used to quantify and longitudinally assess the benefits of interventions for drooling and changes in the impact from drooling) of at least 6 points (19). In the study by Norderyd et al, paediatric patients with disabilities prescribed sublingual atropine for sialorrhea had a statistically significant decrease on the 100 mm visual analogue scale, which is a caregiver's subjective assessment of their child's drooling (20). Although different scales to assess primary outcome were used, the findings in the above studies are consistent with our findings. Our results add to the existing literature to establish atropine as a safe, effective option for paediatric patients with sialorrhea.

There are important limitations of this study to consider. Our sample size of 21 patients is small and from a single centre, therefore limiting generalizability. Due to limitations in the completeness of records and incomplete patient follow-up, there is a risk of attrition bias, which may affect the validity of study findings. Many patients were lost to follow-up from our clinic during the COVID-19 pandemic, which contributes to incomplete data. Additionally, owing to medical complexity, many of our patients are cared for by chronic illness care teams and multiple providers, which may contribute to appointment burden and inadvertent transfer of management of medical issues (managed optimally or otherwise) to other providers, making outcomes difficult to ascertain due to incomplete data. The retrospective nature of the study is subject to limitations in documentation as well. Furthermore, there was no placebo control group for comparison. It is difficult to account for how underlying improvements in oromotor function over time are on account of developmental progress without the use of a placebo control. Fluctuations in comorbid diagnoses that may impact improvement in DFSS scores over time are also difficult to account for in this retrospective design. Lastly, the participants and families may have also exhibited expectation bias, as they were aware of the treatment being provided.

In conclusion, atropine ophthalmic solution administered sublingually or buccally is shown to be efficacious in decreasing both frequency and severity of paediatric sialorrhea, with limited side effects. The data support the use of atropine ophthalmic solution, which is typically widely available, to mitigate sialorrhea and its consequences. Further studies, particularly randomized control trials, should be performed to further explore the effectiveness of atropine for the paediatric population.
