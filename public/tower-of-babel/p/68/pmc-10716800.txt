Type 1 Interferon‐Stimulated Gene Expression and Disease Activity in Pediatric Rheumatic Diseases: No Composite Scores Needed?
Authors: Christina Weiden, Melanie Saers, Tobias Schwarz, Tanja Hinze, Helmut Wittkowski, Christoph Kessel, Katja Masjosthusmann, Michael Mohr, Georg Evers, Sandra Oesingmann‐Weirich, Dirk Foell, Claas H Hinze
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRheumatic diseases are characterized by different patterns of immune overactivation. This study investigated the correlation of whole blood type 1 interferon (IFN) stimulated gene (ISG), IL18, and CXCL9 expression with clinical disease activity in pediatric rheumatic diseases and assessed the required number of ISGs to be included in a composite type 1 IFN score.MethodsWhole blood‐derived RNA and clinical data were collected from 171 mostly pediatric patients with connective tissue diseases (CTDs), systemic autoinflammatory diseases (SAIDs), monogenic interferonopathies (IFNPs) and other inflammatory diseases, and from 38 controls. The expression of six previously established ISGs, IL18, and CXCL9 was assessed by real‐time polymerase chain reaction (471 samples). Individual and composite gene expression was assessed, and correlation and threshold analyses were performed.ResultsCorrelation between ISG expression and clinical disease activity was strongest in CTD, especially in juvenile dermatomyositis (JDM) and IFNP, and modest in patients with SAID. Threshold ISG expression levels for the detection of at least mild clinical disease activity were substantially higher in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus compared with JDM. The correlation of expression levels of limited sets of ISGs and even individual ISGs with clinical disease activity were not inferior to a composite score of six ISGs.ConclusionIn a real‐world cohort, individual ISG expression levels robustly reflected clinical disease activity in CTD and IFNP, especially in JDM, which would simplify such analyses in clinical routine and be more cost‐effective. Threshold levels varied across diseases, potentially reflecting different mechanisms of type 1 IFN overactivation.

INTRODUCTION

Characteristic immune activation patterns are observed in different rheumatic diseases (1). For example, type 1 interferon (IFN) activation is observed in tissues and peripheral blood in viral diseases and aberrantly in monogenic interferonopathies (IFNP) and connective tissue diseases (CTDs). Furthermore, massively increased serum interleukin‐18 (IL‐18) protein levels are observed in several hyperinflammatory conditions, including systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (SJIA)–associated macrophage activation syndrome (MAS), NLR family CARD domain‐containing protein 4 (NLRC4)‐MAS, or X‐linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein deficiency (2). Moreover, IFN‐γ‐induced CXCL9 protein levels are strikingly elevated in patients with primary or secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) and HLH/MAS (3). Type 1 IFN, Toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR‐4) signaling, IL18, IFN‐γ, and CXCL9 are thought to at least partially function as part of a tightly regulated axis. Concerted TLR‐4 and type 1 IFN activation was demonstrated to be required for IL18 gene expression, and IL‐18 in turn is a well‐recognized IFN‐γ‐inducing factor (4, 5).

Gene expression analysis is a robust tool to understand immune activation patterns and can be performed by various methods, including real‐time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR), TaqMan assay, or NanoString (6, 7, 8). It has been demonstrated that a type 1 IFN signature is present in peripheral blood in IFNP and CTD, and less frequently in other diseases (9). Most authors have used composite scores incorporating multiple type 1 IFN–stimulated genes (ISGs) to define overall ISG expression, but it remains unclear how many genes should be reasonably included (6, 7, 10, 11, 12). Several methods have been used to calculate composite IFN scores, such as normalized median, mean, or geometric mean expression, or the sum of Z‐scores (8). Furthermore, in several randomized clinical trials, a type 1 IFN signature was also used as a covariate (13). A task force of the American College of Rheumatology and the European League of Associations for Rheumatology has suggested in their points to consider that the finding of a chronically elevated peripheral blood type 1 IFN signature should raise strong suspicion of IFNPs (14). However, how the measurement of peripheral blood type 1 IFN expression can be integrated into clinical practice is currently unclear. Moreover, whole blood IL18 and CXCL9 gene expression, in contrast to assessment of IL‐18 and CXCL9 protein levels, has not been widely studied.

In this real‐life cohort of well‐characterized patients in two pediatric rheumatology tertiary care centers, we attempt to delineate the correlation of peripheral blood type 1 IFN, IL18, and CXCL9 gene expression and various measures of disease activity in a broad spectrum of patients with pediatric rheumatic diseases.

SIGNIFICANCE & INNOVATIONS

Type 1 interferon (IFN) scores as potential disease activity biomarkers: Peripheral blood type 1 IFN–stimulated gene expression levels, measured through gene expression analysis, closely correlate with disease activity in specific rheumatic diseases. This suggests the potential of type 1 IFN signatures as biomarkers for disease activity assessment, particularly in connective tissue diseases like juvenile dermatomyositis (JDM) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Patients and blood samples

Patients with various rheumatic diseases were seen as part of routine clinical care at two tertiary care pediatric rheumatology centers. Diseases were categorized as either CTD, systemic autoinflammatory disease (SAID), IFNP, or other diseases. Whole blood samples were collected in PAXgene blood RNA tubes. Demographic data, multiple clinical data, laboratory parameters, and treatments were retrieved from the medical records. Furthermore, whole blood was also sampled in 38 pediatric and adult controls without inflammatory disease. The pediatric controls were children who had presented to the pediatric endocrinology unit with concern for short or tall stature but for whom an inflammatory condition had been ruled out. Adult controls were healthy volunteers.

All study subjects or their parents provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the local ethics committee of the Physicians’ chamber Westfalen‐Lippe and the Westphalian Wilhelms University (2015‐670‐f‐s).

Disease activity measures

Global disease activity was assessed by the treating physician or upon chart review by two of the authors (CH, TS). Physician‐rated global disease activity was assessed by a Likert scale (0 to 4) where zero represents no disease activity, 1 minimal disease activity, 2 mild disease activity, 3 moderate disease activity, and four severe disease activity (15). Patient‐ or parent‐rated global disease activity was available in a subgroup of patients. Several disease‐specific disease activity measures were assessed, including the Childhood Myositis Assessment Scale (CMAS) and Manual Muscle Testing (MMT)8 score in juvenile dermatomyositis (JDM) (16). Furthermore, composite disease activity indices were applied where feasible. This included the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) and a modified version of the JDM Activity Index (JDMAI). The conventional JDMAI consists of four parameters (physician global assessment of overall disease activity, parent/patient global assessment of overall well‐being, MMT8/CMAS in deciles, and skin visual analog scale [VAS]) resulting in a score between 0 and 40 (17). As the patient‐ or parent‐rated global disease activity was not collected in all patients, the parameter was removed so that the modified JDMAI results range from 0 to 30.

RNA isolation and RT‐qPCR of six type 1 IFN–stimulated genes, IL18 , and CXCL9

A 2.5 mL whole blood sample was collected in PAXgene tubes (PreAnalytiX). RNA extraction, reverse transcription, processing, storage, and RT‐qPCR of eight target genes (six ISGs [IFI27, IFI44L, IFIT1, ISG15, RSAD2, and SIGLEC1], IL18, and CXCL9) was performed as previously described (4). The gene expression for each of the genes assessed by RT‐qPCR was normalized to the housekeeping genes RPL and GAPDH, and relative gene expression was calculated by the comparative threshold (2−ΔΔCt) method (18). The relative gene expression was then normalized to the same running peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)‐derived control RNA pool derived from three healthy adult donors to achieve further normalization so that the final gene expression value equals the normalized fold change relative to the running control data. In a subset of samples, an additional PAXgene‐derived control pool derived from 10 healthy adult donors was used, and the impact on the readout was assessed.

Type 1 IFN scores

Composite measures, that is, type 1 IFN scores, were examined by using several measures of central tendency. We specifically assessed the median of the normalized six ISG expression values (median‐based IFN score), the geometric mean of the normalized six ISG expression values (geomean‐based IFN score), and the sum of Z‐scores for each individual ISG (= (gene ipatient‐mean gene icontrol)/SD gene icontrol (Z‐score‐based IFN score)).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0 for Mac OS X). Spearman rank correlation analyses were performed to assess the correlation between different variables, given that most variables were not normally distributed. Correlations were labeled as follows: r > 0.8: very strong, 0.6 < r < 0.8: strong, 0.4 < r < 0.6: moderate, 0.2 < r < 0.4: weak, r < 0.2: very weak or none. Depending on measurement scale and distribution, parametric or nonparametric analyses were performed to detect between‐group differences. If three or more groups were compared, either analysis of variance or the Kruskal‐Wallis test was used with the appropriate correction for multiple comparisons. To identify optimal cutoffs for the type 1 IFN scores, receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve analyses were performed by applying type 1 IFN scores and the clinical disease states present in each patient, and the calculation of the Youden index. Combined hierarchical clustering analysis with dendrogram depiction was performed using the R Studio heatmaply package, and correlation coefficients were compared using the R Studio cocor package. Furthermore, hierarchical clustering analysis was also performed using Morpheus (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus).

RESULTS

Patients and samples

The overall cohort included 171 patients of whom 66 (38.6%) had a CTD, 44 (25.7%) a SAID, 8 (4.7%) an IFNP, and 53 (31%) other diseases (specific diagnoses in Supplementary Table 1). Concerning the 471 samples, most samples were collected longitudinally from patients with IFNPs and CTDs with medians of 5 (IQR 1‐11) and 2 (IQR 1‐8) samples per patient, respectively. The clinical disease activity on average was minimal or mild with few samples obtained at moderate or severe disease activity; patients were receiving a variety of therapies at the time of sampling, including glucocorticoids (CTD: 35%, SAID: 35%, IFNP: 68%, other: 29%), conventional DMARDs (CTD: 80%, SAID: 48%, IFNP: 64%, other: 22%), biologic DMARDs (CTD: 1%, SAID: 44%, IFNP: 30%, other: 19%), and targeted synthetic DMARDs (CTD: 4%, SAID: 5%, IFNP: 16%, other: 2%) (Table 1) (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Type 1 IFN–related gene expression

Assessment of ISG expression in healthy controls demonstrated some variability, with normalized expression for the six ISGs ranging from 0.2 to 228.1 (Supplementary Figure 1). Across the patient samples, there was marked variation of ISG expression, most prominent for IFI27 (normalized expression up to 40,000). Applying a cutoff level of mean plus two SDs of healthy control individual gene expression levels, 222 (46.8%), 249 (52.5%), 206 (43.5%), 240 (50.6%), 238 (50.2%), and 219 (46.2%) samples exhibited increased normalized expression levels of IFI27, IFI44L, IFIT1, ISG15, RSAD2, and SIGLEC1, respectively. The correlation of the individual genes’ expression levels across all patients was strong or very strong, with Spearman r ranging from 0.73 to 0.96.

Assessment of composite type 1 IFN scores

Several composite measures to reflect the central tendency of gene expression were assessed. The resulting composite scores, (ie, the median‐based, geomean‐based and Z‐score‐based type 1 IFN scores) correlated highly with each other across the overall patient cohort (Spearman r 0.98‐0.99). We considered upper limits of normal as the mean plus two SDs of the control values. When applying these cutoffs for the median‐based IFN score (6.0), the geomean‐based IFN score (7.7) and the Z‐score‐based IFN score (9.8) overall, 246 (51.9%), 241 (50.8%), and 256 (54%) samples had elevated scores (Figure 1A‐C). For further analyses, we decided to use the geomean‐based IFN score because of its robustness against outliers and the fact that the gene expression values varied on a logarithmic scale.

Distribution of RT‐qPCR‐based type 1 IFN scores and IL18 and CXCL9 gene expression

The distribution of the geomean‐based IFN score was evaluated in the different disease categories. One hundred‐seventy samples (60.7%) within the CTD and IFNP cohorts showed an elevated geomean‐based IFN score. In contrast, in the SAID cohort, only in 21 samples (28.0%) the geomean‐based IFN score was elevated (Figure 1A‐C). When applying thresholds to the IL18 expression, in the CTD, IFNP, and SAID groups, 30 (11%), 4 (10%), and 4 (7%) samples showed an elevated IL18 expression. For CXCL9, in the CTD, IFNP, and SAID groups, 85 (33%), 14 (35%), and 16 (23%) samples, respectively, showed an elevated CXCL9 expression (Figure 1D‐E).

Correlation between clinical disease activity and RT‐qPCR‐based type 1 IFN score and IL18 and CXCL9 expression

Global and disease‐specific disease activity measures were assessed in the different disease categories (Table 2). In the CTD category overall, there was a moderate correlation between disease activity as assessed by Likert scale and the geomean‐based IFN score (r = 0.48). In the IFNP cohort, there was a moderate correlation (r = 0.42) and a weak correlation in the SAID group (r = 0.34). Furthermore, for JDM samples, a modified version of the JDMAI5 was applied; there was a strong correlation between the modified juvenile dermatomyositis activity index5 (JDMAI5) score and the geomean‐based IFN score (r = 0.69). In systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) samples, there was a weak correlation between the SLEDAI scores and the geomean‐based IFN score (r = 0.35). Other disease activity measures were also considered, including the CMAS, MMT8, and skin VAS for patients with JDM. All these individually showed weak to moderate correlation with the geomean‐based IFN score, with coefficients of r = −0.39, r = −0.43 and r = 0.43, respectively. For IL18 and CXCL19 expression, there was only one incidence of at least moderate correlation with disease activity (ie, between CXCL9 expression and MMT score [r = 0.44]).

Correlation between RT‐qPCR‐based type 1 IFN score and IL18 and CXCL9 expression and laboratory parameters

We assessed the correlation of multiple laboratory parameters that were obtained as part of clinical routine with the IFN scores within the different disease categories (Figure 2) (Supplementary Figure 2). For patients with JDM, positive correlations with the IFN score were seen for serum neopterin (r = 0.65), neutrophil percentage (r = 0.43), and IL‐2 receptor (r = 0.36), and negative correlation with lymphocyte percentage (r = −0.47). In SLE, positive correlations were seen for serum neopterin (r = 0.42) and neutrophil percentage (r = 0.39), negative correlation for lymphocyte percentage (r = −0.44), thrombocyte count (r = −0.43), and IgM (r = −0.41). In IFNP, positive correlation was seen for serum neopterin (r = 0.71), IL‐2 receptor (r = 0.60), and neutrophil percentage (r = 0.55) and negative correlation for thrombocyte count (r = −0.66), lymphocyte percentage (r = −0.61), IgA (r = −0.54), serum calprotectin (r = −0.53), hemoglobin (r = −0.55) and leukocyte count (r = −0.54). When considering the correlation between laboratory parameters and IL18 expression, there were less incidences of at least moderate correlation (ie, in patients with SJIA for IL‐2 receptor [r = 0.41], serum neopterin [r = 0.42] and IgG [r = 0.42]). Similarly, for CXCL9 expression, there were few incidences of at least moderate correlation (ie, in patients with SJIA for platelets [r = −0.41], IgM [r = 0.53], GOT [r = 0.48], and LDH [r = 0.44]).

Type 1 IFN score and individual ISG expression in JDM and SLE

Although the IFN score correlated with disease activity measures both in JDM and in SLE, the threshold levels for the discrimination between no/minimal disease activity and mild/moderate/severe disease activity were markedly different. For example, for JDM, the optimal threshold level to accurately define at least mild disease activity, for the geomean‐based IFN score was 6.4 (sensitivity: 89.7%/specificity: 71.7%) (Figure 3A) and for SLE 51.4 (sensitivity: 71.4%/specificity: 62.1%) (Figure 3B), respectively, based on ROC curve analysis and Youden indices. Furthermore, when comparing the performance of individual IFN–stimulated genes’ expression with that of the composite geomean‐based IFN score via ROC curve analyses, it was apparent that there were no substantial differences in the areas under curve when assessing the ability to discriminate any disease activity from no disease activity in JDM (Figure 3C) or SLE (Figure 3D).

Impact of running control origin on type 1 IFN score

Each RT‐qPCR assay was run using the same healthy control PBMC‐derived RNA, derived from three healthy adult donors, and the obtained values were used to normalize the patients’ RNA‐derived values. We subsequently assessed the potential impact of an alternative RNA source for the running control (ie, PAXgene [whole blood]‐derived RNA from 10 healthy volunteers). When comparing the relative gene expression between PBMC‐derived RNA (63 assays) versus PAXgene‐derived RNA (6 assays), it was apparent that there were marked differences on an individual gene level, whereas there were only small differences when looking at composite scores (Supplementary Table 4). For example, although IFI44L and IFIT1 were higher expressed in the PBMC‐derived control, the other genes were higher expressed in the PAXgene‐derived control.

Correlation of limited RT‐qPCR‐based type 1 IFN–stimulated gene sets with disease activity

In order to determine whether a composite 6‐gene type 1 IFN score rather than a more limited set of genes was indeed needed to assess disease activity, we performed correlation analyses of individual type 1 IFN–stimulated genes’ expression and of all possible combinations of the six type 1 IFN–stimulated genes, that is, of 63 (66+65+64+63+62+61 with nr=n!r!n−r!) possible combinations, with clinical disease activity in samples of patients with JDM, SLE and IFNP (Figure 4). This analysis demonstrated that a more limited set of genes, and even individual genes’ expression, correlated numerically better with disease activity than the 6‐gene type 1 IFN score. For example, a combined score reflecting IFI27 and IFI44L expression correlated highly with disease activity in the conditions listed above, whereas, in contrast, other individual genes’ expression, such as RSAD2 and SIGLEC1, correlated to a lesser degree with disease activity.

DISCUSSION

This is the first study to systematically evaluate the use of limited versus composite expression scores in assessing peripheral blood ISG, IL18, and CXCL9 expression to inform about disease activity in pediatric patients with rheumatic diseases. In this observational study of a well‐characterized real‐life cohort, we confirm the previous observation that ISG expression in peripheral blood robustly reflects disease activity in CTDs and IFNPs. This supports the inclusion of type 1 IFN scores into the routine care of patients with CTD, especially with JDM. However, disease‐specific threshold levels may need to be applied. For example, in patients with JDM, a strong association was observed between measures of disease activity and type 1 IFN scores, with scores frequently reaching the healthy control range upon treatment, whereas for patients with SLE, the association was weaker, and IFN scores did not reach the healthy control range consistently. In contrast, we do not observe a use of assessing IL18 and CXCL9 expression in our patient cohort.

Although type 1 IFN gene expression assays currently are not broadly available in commercial laboratories, different methods of assessment of type I IFN–stimulated gene expression are being used on a research basis, including, for example, the assessment of 4, 6, 7, or even 28 ISGs, with different methods to reflect the central tendency. We demonstrate that a much‐reduced set of genes and even individual ISGs would suffice to reliably represent systemic type 1 IFN activation. Because gene expression analyses are cost and labor intensive, a simplified assessment of type 1 IFN activation would improve clinical practicability and cost‐effectiveness.

Although the six ISGs contained in our RT‐PCR panel were indeed strongly regulated in concert, it was apparent that individual ISGs, especially IFI27, were more volatile than other ISGs, which had previously been shown by other investigators (19). Our data suggest that this volatility may also be driven by a predominant expression of IFI27 in granulocytes because expression levels were much higher in whole blood‐derived running controls when compared with PBMC‐derived running controls, even though granulocyte‐specific expression has not been reported previously in gene expression databases (20).

Rice et al had previously reported extensively on the diagnostic performance of the identical six‐gene ISG set in a large cohort of patients (9). In that report, almost 1000 samples were reported from more than 600 patients from multiple disease categories from multiple centers, however, without detailed data on disease activity or other laboratory parameters, and few longitudinal data. The authors demonstrated that an IFN score was an effective tool to define a spectrum of type 1 IFN‐driven diseases and speculated that an IFN score may also be useful in the assessment of treatment efficacy. In our data set, which also contains longitudinal data for a number of patients, we can validate that the type 1 IFN score indeed correlates closely with disease activity and treatment response, especially in CTD, and, most prominently, in JDM. Applying NanoString technology, others have applied a 28‐gene IFN score and demonstrated strong diagnostic accuracy for monogenic IFNP and marked similarities between monogenic IFNP and JDM (6, 19). Subsequently, the presence of IFN signatures has been used to define novel autoinflammatory diseases (21).

In both active JDM and SLE, increased type 1 IFN signaling is present (10, 22, 23, 24, 25). Threshold levels for detecting at least mildly active disease differed between JDM and SLE in our data set with much lower threshold levels in JDM. To our knowledge, this kind of comparative analysis has not been shown previously. This may point toward different roles of type 1 IFN activation in these two diseases, that is, reversible in JDM versus more ingrained in SLE. One possible explanation is that in SLE, type 1 IFN production may be largely driven by plasmacytoid dendritic cells activated via TLR7 and TLR9 by RNA‐associated autoantibodies, which generally do not reflect disease activity (26). This observation may also indicate that normalization of IFN scores into the healthy control range would not be a realistic treatment target in patients with SLE (but might be in JDM), and that IFN scores may be particularly useful in detecting subclinical disease activity in JDM.

In contrast to type 1 IFN‐driven gene expression, IL18 and CXCL9 expression in peripheral blood did not correlate strongly with clinical disease activity. Several factors may play a role here. For instance, IL18/IL‐18 may be predominantly regulated on a protein level with a lesser role of regulation of gene translation in peripheral blood. This is, for example, shown by the fact that although IL‐18 levels in peripheral blood may increase by a factor of 105 in some conditions, such as in PAPA syndrome, MAS, or NLRC4‐MAS (27, 28, 29), IL18 expression varies only by a factor of 10 in our data set. Furthermore, the cellular source of IL‐18 and CXCL9 protein may be outside of the peripheral blood (30) so that peripheral blood gene expression may not be representative.

Several limitations apply to our analysis. First, this is a real‐world cohort of patients with rheumatic diseases in two tertiary care pediatric rheumatology centers, and many samples, especially in the SAID cohort, were obtained, although patients were receiving antirheumatic therapies, and, thus, disease activity on average was absent or mild. Therefore, our data set may underestimate the impact high disease activity may have on target gene expression in some diseases. Second, for some individual diseases, the numbers of patients and samples were low, for example in the IFNP cohort, because this is not a focus of the respective clinical centers. Third, for some diseases, the patients recruited may not be fully representative. For example, our patient with SLE cohort did not include patients with lupus nephritis. Fourth, the noninflammatory pediatric controls were patients evaluated for short or tall stature. These controls do not fully represent “healthy controls”; this is a common issue in many studies because blood samples from healthy children are difficult to access.

Even though our data suggest that a reduced number of ISGs, such as assessing expression of IFI27 and IFI44L, would be sufficient to represent peripheral blood type 1 IFN activation, we believe that further studies are needed to confirm these findings. In any case, because the methods used to assess type 1 IFN signatures in peripheral blood and calculate IFN scores vary substantially between centers, diligence is required when interpreting results.

In conclusion, type 1 IFN signatures in the form of composite type 1 IFN scores or even the expression of individual type 1 ISGs represent reliable reflections of disease activity for diseases with enhanced type 1 IFN signaling. However, disease‐specific threshold levels for the underlying diseases should be considered.
