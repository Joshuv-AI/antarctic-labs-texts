Preterm birth in women with psoriatic arthritis: what are the risks and risk factors? A collaborative cohort study from Sweden, Denmark and Norway
Authors: Anne Emilie Pape Secher, Fredrik Granath, Katarina Remaeus, Bente Glintborg, Ane Lilleøre Rom, Brigitte Michelsen, Merete Lund Hetland, Karin Hellgren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo estimate risk and to identify risk factors for preterm birth in pregnant women with psoriatic arthritis (PsA) in relation to maternal characteristics, treatment and disease activity, both before and during pregnancy.MethodsWe linked clinical rheumatology registers to medical birth registers in Sweden, Denmark and Norway and identified PsA pregnancies and control pregnancies 2006–2021, matched 1:10 on age, parity, country and birth year. Adjusted ORs (aORs) for preterm birth in PsA pregnancies vs control pregnancies were calculated overall and stratified by maternal characteristics, antirheumatic treatment and disease load (9 months before and during pregnancy).ResultsWe observed 54 preterm births among 688 PsA pregnancies (7.8%) versus 312 among 6880 control pregnancies (4.5%); aOR, 95% CI: 1.80, 1.29 to 2.51. The risk estimate was largely unaffected by parity, smoking and body mass index. PsA pregnancies exposed to a combination of biologic and conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) and/or glucocorticoids during pregnancy had a markedly increased risk of preterm birth compared with control pregnancies (4.44, 2.07 to 9.50), whereas exposure to biological DMARD (bDMARD) monotherapy (primarily tumour necrosis factor inhibitors) had not (0.73, 0.22 to 2.42). High disease load before pregnancy was associated with increased risk. The proportion of preterm birth was higher in those stopping bDMARD during the first trimester (21%) opposed to those continuing past the first trimester (10%) based on few events.ConclusionWe identified an 80% increased risk of preterm birth in PsA pregnancies compared with control pregnancies. Antirheumatic combination therapy during and high disease load before pregnancy constituted risk factors. Discontinuing bDMARD treatment in early pregnancy further increased the risk. Our findings support that a subgroup of PsA pregnancies should be considered high-risk pregnancies.

Introduction

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic inflammatory arthritis disease that may affect women of childbearing age.1 2 While rheumatoid arthritis (RA) has been related to several pregnancy complications, including preterm birth, and there is a well-established association between RA disease activity and adverse pregnancy outcomes,38 corresponding data for PsA are scarce.4912 Thus, it is difficult to establish definitive recommendations for the management and treatment of women with PsA in relation to pregnancy.13

Globally, preterm birth is a major contributor to neonatal morbidity and mortality, with well-known short-term effects such as respiratory and feeding problems.14 Studies additionally show higher risks of long-term consequences, such as cardiac dysfunction, obstructive lung disease and learning difficulties.1417 Thus, preventing children from being born preterm is of utmost importance.

Previous studies have suggested a 1.5–2 times increased risk of preterm birth in women with PsA compared with healthy controls, but the correlation to antirheumatic treatment and disease activity remains unclear.91318 Few studies have prospectively investigated the impact of PsA disease severity (eg, disease activity and treatment) on the risk of preterm birth.9

It would be valuable for both the women with PsA and their healthcare team to be able to identify those at increased risk for preterm birth at an early stage, thereby enhancing the likelihood of a successful pregnancy.

Consequently, using clinical data from Swedish, Danish and Norwegian rheumatology quality registers enriched with data from nationwide health registers, we aimed to assess the risk and to identify risk factors for preterm birth in pregnant women with PsA related to maternal characteristics, antirheumatic treatment and disease activity before and during pregnancy.

Methods

Study design and data sources

We conducted a matched cohort study making use of prospectively recorded data from clinical rheumatology registers linked with nationwide health and population registers in Sweden, Denmark and Norway.

The clinical rheumatology registers included the Swedish Rheumatology Quality Register (SRQ),21 22 the Danish Rheumatology Quality Register (DANBIO/DRK)23 and the Norwegian register for antirheumatic drugs (NOR-DMARD, ie, the prospective observational Norwegian multicentre disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) study).24 The health registers included the medical birth registers from all three countries, which provide nationwide information on all pregnancies ending in live birth or stillbirth. The gestational age thresholds for the inclusion of stillbirths were ≥12+0 weeks in Norway and ≥22+0 weeks in Denmark and Sweden (in Sweden, ≥28+0 weeks before 1 July 2008).2527 For Sweden and Denmark, we additionally included data from the national patient registers,28 29 the prescribed drug registers30 31 and the educational and total population registers.32 33 The personal identification number assigned to all citizens in each country allowed for linkage between the different registers. For details on each register, see online supplemental table S1a).

Study population

From SRQ, DANBIO/DRK and NOR-DMARD, we assembled data on all women with a diagnosis of PsA. The clinical rheumatology registers capture longitudinal information on PsA disease activity and treatment, with data entered by the rheumatologist at each visit. All diagnoses are rheumatologist-based (online supplemental table S1b).23

By linking to the medical birth registers, we identified all singleton pregnancies that resulted in a delivery after the diagnosis of PsA of the women with delivery dates from 1 July 2007 to 31 December 2021 in Sweden, from 1 January 2006 to 31 December 2018 in Denmark and from 1 January 2012 to 31 December 2021 in Norway. For Sweden and Denmark, we only included pregnancies of women registered in the clinical rheumatology register at least 6 months before pregnancy to collect prepregnancy disease activity and DMARD treatment data. For Norway, due to the data structure of NOR-DMARD, we accepted all women registered in NOR-DMARD at the start of pregnancy.

For Denmark and Norway, we identified singleton control pregnancies from the general population in the medical birth register, matched 1:10 on maternal age, parity and calendar year of childbirth (figure 1). In Sweden, singleton control pregnancies were identified from a pre-existing linkage of comparators to individuals with chronic inflammatory arthritis, enriched with pregnancy data from the Swedish medical birth register (online supplemental table S1c).22

Exposure

The exposure of interest was PsA before pregnancy, stratified on maternal characteristics (ie, body mass index (BMI), parity and smoking), type of antirheumatic treatment and level of disease activity and functional status. Treatment, disease activity and functional status were assessed in two periods: ‘pre-pregnancy’, defined as the 9 months preceding the start of the pregnancy, and ‘during pregnancy’, which refers to the period from the start of pregnancy to delivery. The start of pregnancy was calculated based on gestational age and delivery date as registered in the medical birth registers (online supplemental table S1b).

Antirheumatic treatment

We obtained information on antirheumatic treatment exposure in the prepregnancy period and during pregnancy for all women with PsA. Treatment was defined as any exposure to conventional synthetic (cs)DMARD, oral glucocorticoids and biological DMARD (bDMARD), including tumour necrosis factor inhibitor (TNFi) and non-TNFi during the two aforementioned time periods (anatomical therapeutic chemical (ATC) and treatment codes enclosed in online supplemental table S1b). We further divided treatment exposure into four strata: (1) no treatment (2) treatment with csDMARD and/or glucocorticoids only, (3) monotherapy with bDMARD and (4) combination therapy with bDMARD plus csDMARD and/or glucocorticoids (‘bDMARD combination’).

Treatment data were gathered from the clinical rheumatology registers and the prescribed drug registers. When unambiguous start and stop dates for treatment were recorded in the clinical rheumatology registers without obvious discrepancies to other sources (eg, methotrexate registered as used throughout pregnancy despite this being known to be withdrawn before a pregnancy34 35), this information was considered valid. In cases where the start and stop dates in the clinical rheumatology registers were unclear, we relied on dosage and supply data from the prescribed drug register in Sweden, the prescribed drug register and national patient register (which includes bDMARD information) in Denmark and the medical birth register in Norway (online supplemental table S1b).

PsA disease activity

From the clinical rheumatology registers, we obtained information on functional status and disease activity in the prepregnancy period and during pregnancy. We collected data on Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ, low <1, high ≥1), C-reactive protein (CRP, low <10 mg/L, high ≥10 mg/L), patient global-Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score (low <50 mm, high ≥50 mm) and Disease Activity Score in 28 joints-CRP adjusted (DAS28-CRP, low <3.2, moderate/high ≥3.2). To avoid influence by pregnancy on the patient scoring, we calculated DAS28-CRP without the patient global-VAS score.36 We also excluded HAQ and patient global-VAS scores registered in the third trimester as they are likely affected during late pregnancy.36

We established a composite measure of disease load based on clinically meaningful cut-offs to account for multiple dimensions of disease activity in a simplified and pragmatic way. Disease load was defined as high if there was any registration of HAQ ≥1, CRP ≥10 mg/L, patient global-VAS score ≥50 mm and/or DAS28-CRP ≥3.2 prepregnancy or during pregnancy, respectively. If not, it was defined as low. Disease load was classified as ‘missing’ only if none of the four measures were registered.

Of note, in Scandinavia, there are no specific, agreed guidelines in rheumatology for monitoring pregnant women with PsA. Thus, disease activity measures were collected as part of routine care. Estimates of maternal disease activity and functional status were mainly based on one registration per period (ie, prepregnancy and during pregnancy) since most patients visit their rheumatologist only once a year when their disease is stable. If multiple registrations existed in the given period, the highest value was used.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was preterm birth overall, defined as giving birth before gestational week (gw) 37+0. The outcome was further subcategorised into (a) spontaneous or induced preterm birth and (b) very or moderate preterm birth defined as gw 22+0 to 31+6 and 32+0 to 36+6, respectively.

Covariates

From the medical birth register, we identified maternal age, parity, calendar year of delivery and information on smoking and measured BMI in early pregnancy (at first antenatal visit about gestational week 6–12). From the educational register, we collected information on the mother’s highest educational level at the time of delivery as proxy for socioeconomic status. As a proxy of rheumatological monitoring prepregnancy and during pregnancy, we extracted whether the women had any registered visits prepregnancy and during pregnancy in the clinical rheumatology register. For comparison purposes, we also extracted selected maternal comorbidity conditions before pregnancy (diabetes mellitus, arterial hypertension, renal disease and inflammatory bowel disease), see online supplemental table S1b).

Statistical analyses

We present descriptive characteristics as counts and percentages. We used logistic regression models to calculate crude and adjusted ORs (aORs, hereafter ‘risk’) with corresponding 95% CI for preterm birth in PsA versus control pregnancies. This was performed for preterm birth overall, spontaneous, moderate and very preterm birth. For all the analyses, the matched control pregnancies constituted the reference group.

Based on a priori knowledge, we adjusted for potential confounders, that is, BMI, smoking, educational level at the time of delivery, parity, maternal age and calendar year of delivery.2 3 5 10 11 37 We refrained from adjusting for diabetes and pre-eclampsia since we considered these factors potential mediators and including them could result in overadjustment. We also adjusted for country of delivery (Sweden/Denmark/Norway) and stratified by type of antirheumatic treatment, disease load and whether there was a rheumatological visit (yes/no), prepregnancy and during pregnancy. Due to limitations in statistical power, we were unable to conduct more detailed stratification beyond these two time periods.

In a separate analysis, we assessed the risk of preterm birth depending on whether antirheumatic treatment was continued or discontinued during pregnancy in a pairwise comparison to the control pregnancies.

To assess the impact of maternal characteristics, we estimated risks of all preterm births stratified by (a) parity at delivery (primiparous/multiparous), (b) smoking status in early pregnancy (yes/no) and (c) BMI group in early pregnancy (<25/25–30/>30 kg/m2). As a sensitivity analysis to test the robustness of our results, we repeated the main analyses using spontaneous preterm births as the outcome rather than all preterm births because spontaneous preterm birth constitutes a harder endpoint compared with preterm birth overall since the latter may be a result of various external factors.

As a sensitivity analysis, we also applied the generalized estimating equation (GEE) method to the main analysis to account for the potential clustering of pregnancies from the same mother in the PsA cohort. Norway was not included in this analysis, as maternal ID data were not available.

We additionally tested effect modification on the multiplicative scale between PsA (yes/no) and the risk factors parity, smoking status and BMI group by appropriate interaction terms. We calculated absolute risk and predicted absolute excess risks due to PsA in different combinations of the above-listed risk factors, estimated from the multiplicative model.

We present risks for preterm birth overall both on pooled data and by country, whereas the stratified analyses are presented on pooled data for all countries together. As a sensitivity analysis, we tested the homogeneity of the effect of PsA on preterm births across countries in the main analysis using an interaction term. Due to the small number of pregnancies from Norway, we also conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding Norway.

All analyses were performed in SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, USA).

Results

We identified a total of 688 PsA pregnancies in Sweden (n=373, 54.2%), Denmark (n=256, 37.2%) and Norway (n=59, 8.7%) and 6880 matched control pregnancies. Pregnant women with PsA were more likely to be obese, less educated and more often smokers, compared with the controls. They also had a higher prevalence of hypertension but not diabetes mellitus (table 1).

Overall, 7.8% of the PsA pregnancies resulted in preterm birth (54 events) compared with 4.5% in the controls (312 events), yielding an aOR of 1.80 (95% CI 1.29 to 2.51) (figure 2).

The proportion of preterm birth was slightly higher in Sweden (8.6%, aOR 2.07, 1.33 to 3.21) and Norway (8.4%, aOR 2.11, 0.68 to 6.57) compared with Denmark (6.6%, aOR 1.43, 0.81 to 2.51). However, the estimate for Norway was based on only five events. We also tested the homogeneity of the effect of PsA across countries and found no significant difference (p=0.58), nor between Denmark and Sweden specifically (p=0.31).

Excluding Norwegian data, the aOR for Sweden and Denmark was 1.78 (1.26 to 2.52), similar to the OR for all three countries.

The estimate for Sweden and Denmark was unaffected when applying GEE to account for potential clustering of pregnancies (aOR 1.80, 1.25 to 2.59).

The pooled risk estimates were largely similar when assessing spontaneous preterm birth (figure 2). Likewise, the risk estimates for moderate and very preterm birth were largely similar. Of note, approximately 85% of the events were moderate preterm births, whereas very preterm births only constituted eight cases out of the total 54 preterm births (figure 2).

We observed no significant interaction between PsA and parity, smoking status and BMI group, and the ORs of preterm birth did not differ substantially in the different categories of these risk factors (figure 3 and online supplemental table S2). To show how the multiplicative effect of PsA translated to absolute risks depending on these risk factors, we calculated the absolute excess risk of preterm birth due to PsA for all combinations of these risk factors. This showed absolute excess risks due to PsA spanning from about 2 percentage points for multiparous non-smokers with a BMI <30 kg/m2 to 7 percentage points in primiparous smokers with BMI >30 kg/m2 (online supplemental table S3).

Pre-pregnancy risk factors

When stratifying on treatment strategy in the prepregnancy period, we observed an approximately doubled risk of preterm birth both in PsA pregnancies exposed to bDMARD monotherapy (aOR 2.23, 1.05 to 4.73) and bDMARD combination therapy (aOR 2.13, 1.04 to 4.35) compared with the matched controls (figure 4). For those without treatment in the prepregnancy period, we found an aOR of 1.57 (0.90 to 2.71), and for those only on csDMARD/glucocorticoid therapy prepregnancy, the aOR was 1.68 (0.84 to 3.37). However, the CIs for all four treatment groups were overlapping. Narrowing the analysis to spontaneous preterm as the outcome did not substantially change the results (online supplemental table S4).

Approximately half of the women with PsA had a registered visit in the clinical rheumatology registers in the prepregnancy period. Consequently, we had registrations of disease load in 46% of the PsA pregnancies in this period. Among those with available data, we observed an increased proportion and risk of preterm birth in women with high disease load prepregnancy (12.7%, aOR 2.66, 1.56 to 4.56) compared with their matched controls. The corresponding proportion among those with low disease load prepregnancy was 3.0% (aOR 0.78, 0.28 to 2.20, figure 4).

Risk factors during pregnancy

The proportion of preterm birth was 16.4% in the PsA pregnancies exposed to bDMARD combination therapy during pregnancy, resulting in an aOR of 4.44 (2.07 to 9.50) compared with their matched controls (figure 5). By contrast, PsA pregnancies exposed to bDMARD as monotherapy had a proportion of preterm birth of 3.1% resulting in an aOR of 0.73 (0.22 to 2.42). For exposure to csDMARD/glucocorticoid monotherapy during pregnancy, the aOR was 1.26 (0.56 to 2.86). The aOR for the no treatment group was largely unchanged from the corresponding prepregnancy estimate (1.87, 1.19 to 2.95). Restricting the analysis to spontaneous preterm did not substantially change the results (online supplemental table S5).

We had registrations of disease load in 31% of the PsA pregnancies during pregnancy, and among those, we noted a similar risk of preterm birth among PsA registered with high disease load (aOR 1.97, 0.85 to 4.56) and low disease load (aOR 1.82, 0.83 to 4.01). There was no clear signal of a different risk of preterm birth whether the patient had a visit or not in the rheumatology registry during pregnancy, although the point estimate of risk (aOR 1.97, 1.29 to 3.00) was higher among those without as compared with those with a visit (aOR 1.58, 0.93 to 2.70)(figure 5).

In an additional analysis stratifying on treatment strategy (continuing treatment/discontinuing treatment), we observed a proportion of preterm birth of 20.9% among women with PsA treated with bDMARD combination therapy prepregnancy but discontinuing during the first trimester (nine events of 43), resulting in an aOR of 7.24 (2.75 to 19.0) compared with the matched controls (3.7%, 16 events of 430). For women treated with bDMARD combination therapy prepregnancy that continued past the first trimester, the proportion of preterm birth was 10.0% (three events of 30) compared with 5.7% in the controls (17 events of 300), yielding an aOR of 2.11 (0.57 to 7.90).

Discussion

In this large Scandinavian cohort study, we made several key observations: (1) the overall risk of preterm birth among women with PsA was nearly twice that of women in the general population. (2) Treatment with bDMARD in combination with csDMARD and/or oral glucocorticoids during pregnancy was associated with significantly higher risk, whereas monotherapy with bDMARD was not. (3) There were signals of an increased risk associated with high PsA disease load before (but not during) pregnancy. (4) For women treated with bDMARD treatment at the start of pregnancy, the proportion of preterm birth was markedly higher in those discontinuing bDMARD during the first trimester versus those continuing bDMARD past the first trimester, although numbers were low.

Our finding of an almost doubled overall risk of preterm birth in PsA is in line with previous studies. A recent systematic review including a meta-analysis20 based on four studies from the USA,18 Canada,9 Sweden11 and Denmark/Sweden10 found a pooled OR of 1.48 (1.24 to 1.78) of preterm birth in PsA. Of note, the studies from the USA and Canada reported preterm birth rates in the control groups twice as high as in the Danish background population, limiting generalisability to a Scandinavian population.9 18 The Scandinavian studies by Remaeus et al and Bröms et al, having partially overlapping cohorts with ours, both identified PsA from the national patient registers.10 12 Using rheumatologist-based PsA diagnosis from the clinical rheumatology registers enhances the diagnostic validity of our study but may also have led to a selection towards women with more severe PsA, characterised by a higher prevalence of bDMARD treatments or more comorbidities. This could explain the observed variation in preterm birth rates in PsA across the three countries, with a somewhat higher proportion observed in Sweden and Norway since SRQ, to some extent, and NOR-DMARD, exclusively, include women with PsA receiving bDMARDs, whereas DANBIO includes women with PsA regardless of treatment.

We observed that women with PsA were more often smokers and had higher BMI in early pregnancy compared with their controls. This is not surprising, given the well-established association between PsA and metabolic syndrome, including hypertension and obesity.37 Apart from adjusting for BMI and smoking, we were also able to match on parity, which is a potential confounder since women with PsA more frequently experience primiparity due to infertility but also challenges and personal beliefs associated with their arthritis condition.38 Noticeably, when stratifying for parity, smoking and BMI, we found largely similar risks among all groups. Hence, our results strongly suggest that BMI, smoking and parity could not solely account for the increased risk of preterm birth observed in PsA and that the absolute excess risk attributable to PsA increases somewhat proportionally with a greater burden of risk factors. Taking account of multiple pregnancies from the same women by applying GEE to the main analysis did not affect the result. Other risk factors for preterm birth, such as diabetes mellitus and pre-eclampsia, are also very closely linked to PsA, but may act as mediators. Hence, adjusting for these factors could potentially lead to over-adjustment.

During pregnancy, the risk of preterm birth was strongly driven by the group receiving more intensive treatment with combination therapy, including bDMARDs. This is in line with Remaeus et al, who reported an aOR of 4.49 (2.60 to 7.79) for PsA pregnancies exposed to any type of bDMARD (both monotherapy and combination therapy) compared with general population.12 Reassuringly, when we evaluated the PsA pregnancies only exposed to bDMARD as monotherapy, we found no increased risk, which supports that the bDMARD per se did not cause the increased risk of preterm birth. For comparison, in the only other study attempting to assess the effect of treatment, Smith et al found no increased risk for those receiving bDMARDs compared with those who did not. However, the robustness of their findings is limited by the small size of the comparison group—only 16 out of 100 women in their PsA cohort were not receiving bDMARDs.

Data on the impact of disease activity and functional status on preterm birth in PsA have, to our knowledge, only been addressed in one previous study, Smith et al, who reported an increased risk of preterm birth in women with PsA with HAQ >0.5 in gw 32 compared with PsA women with HAQ <0.5 (adjusted risk ratio 3.82, 1.51 to 9.67).9 A similar trend was not found in our study since the ORs for both low and high disease loads during pregnancy compared with the controls were highly overlapping. However, our results highlight the importance of maintaining low disease activity when planning for pregnancy, since we observed a substantially higher proportion of preterm birth among women with high disease load in the 9 months preceding pregnancy. Nonetheless, disentangling the impact of disease activity on the risk of preterm birth was challenging due to a significant number of PsA pregnancies without information on disease activity and functional status.

Assessing the timing of treatment exposure, we found a markedly higher proportion of preterm birth among those who discontinued bDMARD treatment during the first trimester, compared with those continuing past the first trimester, although the number of events was low in both groups. A possible explanation is that discontinuing bDMARDs in women with PsA may trigger flares during pregnancy, thereby increasing the risk of preterm birth. More prospectively collected data on disease activity and treatment are needed to explore this in detail.

The strengths of our study include the large study size enhanced by prospectively and independently collected clinical and robust nationwide health data. We also had the possibility to identify control pregnancies from the general population, placing our results into a relevant context. Our register-based design eliminates the risk of recall bias, and by integrating multiple data sources, we were able to obtain the most accurate treatment information available.

The study also has some limitations. Our study is based on data from the medical birth registers, which are uniquely comprehensive national registers, covering all deliveries in the countries. However, they do not include pregnancies ending in early losses. While this limits our ability to assess total pregnancy risk, it remains clinically relevant to quantify the risk of preterm delivery among women with PsA whose pregnancies progress to delivery. Also, most preterm births in our study were moderate to late, which is likely to have a different pathophysiology than early losses, as early preterm birth is typically driven by severe inflammation or placental dysfunction, while moderate preterm birth often results from milder mechanical or maternal factors.39 We believe that despite this limitation, our study offers insights for clinical counselling in women with PsA.

Additionally, although we adjusted for several important confounders, potential residual confounding cannot be excluded due to the observational design of the study. Furthermore, as stated, we only had data on disease activity in half of the women before pregnancy and less than half of the women during pregnancy, which limited our ability to adjust for disease activity measurements. We handled these missing data by including a separate missing category, which allowed us to retain all pregnancies in the model and to show whether patients without recorded disease activity had different risks than others. Of note, this strategy may introduce bias if data are not missing completely at random; for example, reflecting lower healthcare engagement or differences in clinical monitoring, which could affect outcomes. We did not find any difference in the risk of preterm birth between those with and without data on disease load, supporting that we did not introduce major bias applying this strategy. However, we lacked sufficient timing information to allow for reliable imputation based on treatment patterns or other clinical variables.

The missingness on disease activity also raises questions about the monitoring practices during routine care of PsA in pregnancy. However, it is reassuring that no significant risk difference was observed between those who had a visit and those who had not.

In conclusion, we found a nearly doubled risk of preterm birth in women with PsA compared with the general population, which was largely unaffected by BMI, parity and smoking. Intensive antirheumatic treatment during pregnancy appeared to increase the risk, whereas bDMARD monotherapy did not, suggesting that disease severity rather than treatment was a risk factor. Alongside our observation that discontinuing bDMARD in the first trimester was associated with high preterm birth rates, this may support clinicians and reassure patients to continue bDMARD during pregnancy, if needed. Taken together, our findings emphasise the importance of monitoring women with high PsA disease burden and additional risk factors before and during pregnancy.
