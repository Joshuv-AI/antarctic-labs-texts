Comparative analysis of horsetail and lavender-derived nanovesicles in wound healing and antioxidant defense
Authors: Selin Aşık, Aybüke Okay, Başar Karaca, Burak Derkuş, Cemil Can Eylem, Emirhan Nemutlu, Emine Sümer Aras, İlker Büyük
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (pEVs) have emerged as promising natural biomaterials due to their safety profile, high biocompatibility, and rich bioactive cargo. In particular, pEVs have attracted interest because of their roles in intercellular communication. This study aimed to isolate and comprehensively characterize pEVs derived from Equisetum arvense (H-EVs) and Lavandula angustifolia (L-EVs), and to evaluate their morphological features, protein content, and biological functions, with a specific focus on wound healing, senescence, oxidative stress modulation, and antimicrobial activity.ResultsH-EVs and L-EVs displayed spherical morphology, high stability, narrow size distribution, and substantial protein content as confirmed by TEM and DLS analyses. In vitro assays in human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) and keratinocytes (HaCaT) demonstrated that both pEV types were non-cytotoxic and significantly enhanced cellular metabolic activity. Scratch wound assays revealed markedly accelerated wound closure, driven by a combination of cell migration and proliferation. Senescence analyses showed reduced β-galactosidase activity and improved cellular morphology following with pEV treatment. Antioxidant assessments indicated increased total antioxidant capacity, decreased oxidant load, and a reduced oxidative stress index. Gene expression analysis by qPCR confirmed upregulation of COL1A1, COL1A2, and FGF, supporting extracellular matrix production and tissue repair. Additionally, both H-EVs and L-EVs exhibited notable antibacterial and antifungal activities.ConclusionsH-EVs and L-EVs effectively alleviate oxidative stress and cellular senescence while promoting wound healing through enhanced collagen synthesis, cell migration, and proliferation. Their non-cytotoxic nature, combined with antioxidant, anti-senescent, and antimicrobial properties, underscores their versatility and therapeutic potential. These findings highlight that pEVs as safe and effective biotherapeutic candidates, supporting their future clinical translation in dermatology and regenerative medicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-026-01146-w.

Background

Exosomes first attracted scientific attention in 1983, when Johnstone, Pan, and colleagues reported their release from sheep reticulocytes during in vitro culture [1]. These nanoscale extracellular vesicles, typically 30–150 nm in diameter, are formed through endocytosis, fusion, and exocytosis [2]. They transport proteins, lipids, DNA, and non-coding RNAs, functioning as key mediators of intercellular communication. Exosomes have been isolated from various mammalian sources, including stem, cancer, and immune cells, as well as body fluids such as blood and urine [3–5]. Their high molecular transport capacity and biocompatibility enable them to deliver bioactive molecules and enhance therapeutic efficacy by transporting miRNAs, DNA, proteins, and drugs [6–10]. However, mammalian-derived exosomes exhibit several limitations. Some act as waste disposal vesicles and may transport tumor-derived or infectious materials [11–13]. They can modulate immune responses unpredictably and are difficult to produce at scale due to complex purification and high costs, restricting clinical translation. These challenges have prompted the search for alternative, safer, and more sustainable nanocarriers.

In this context, plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (pEVs) have emerged as a promising alternative [14]. Isolated from diverse plant species, they exhibit anti-inflammatory, antiviral, antifibrotic, and antitumor effects comparable to mammalian exosomes, while avoiding immunogenicity and scalability issues. The Food and Agriculture Organization estimates that over 50,000 plant species worldwide possess therapeutic potential [15], offering a vast reservoir for pEV production. These vesicles can be readily isolated from edible sources such as grapes, ginger, and grapefruit [16–18] and participate in both intercellular and interspecies communication. Physiological and pharmacological studies have shown that pEVs promote wound healing, antioxidant defense, and tissue regeneration, while exhibiting strong safety and biocompatibility [19–22]. They can cross biological barriers, including the blood–brain barrier, to alleviate inflammation, target tumors, and modulate gut microbiota [22–25].

Unlike synthetic nanocarriers based on metals or polymers, pEVs’ natural origin ensures superior safety and minimal toxicity [22, 26]. Plants have evolved robust antioxidant systems to counteract oxidative stress caused by UV radiation, drought, and pathogens. These include enzymatic antioxidants—superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPX)—and non-enzymatic molecules such as vitamin C, phenolics, and carotenoids [27–29] have long been utilized to prevent oxidative stress–related skin aging and chronic wounds [30, 31]. pEVs can enhance the bioavailability of these compounds, delivering them efficiently to dermal and epidermal cells. Through their bioactive cargo, they reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS), prevent oxidative damage, and promote collagen synthesis and tissue repair [32, 33].

Oxidative stress arises when the balance between ROS generation and antioxidant defenses is disrupted, leading to DNA, protein, and lipid damage that accelerates inflammation, aging, and tissue degeneration [34–36]. Environmental stress and microbial factors further increase ROS production, which triggers pro-inflammatory gene expression and impairs wound closure [37, 38]. Skin aging is influenced by both intrinsic (genetic, hormonal) and extrinsic (UV, visible light, ozone) factors [39–41]. Among these, UV-induced ROS accumulation is a major driver of photoaging, resulting in fibroblast loss, decreased collagen synthesis, and matrix degradation via AP-1 activation and MMP upregulation [41–43]. Collectively, evidence indicates that pEVs can reduce oxidative stress, improve dermal cell function, and accelerate wound repair.

In this study, pEVs were isolated from Equisetum arvense (horsetail) and Lavandula angustifolia (lavender), two medicinal plants with well-documented dermatological relevance. Horsetail, rich in silica, flavonoids, and phenolics, exhibits antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, stimulating fibroblast collagen synthesis and promoting wound healing [44, 45]. It also inhibits tyrosinase and MMP enzymes, preventing hyperpigmentation and extracellular matrix degradation [46]. Lavender, an aromatic member of the Lamiaceae family, produces essential oils abundant in linalool and linalyl acetate—compounds with antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and wound-healing activities [47, 48]. Lavender oil maintains epidermal integrity and activates TGF-β signaling, increasing type I and III collagen expression and fibroblast proliferation [49, 50]. With their combined phytochemical richness and bioactivity, horsetail and lavender serve as safe and potent candidates for developing advanced dermatological formulations targeting oxidative stress, photoaging, and wound repair [51, 52].

In conclusion, the isolated pEVs were systematically evaluated in human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) and human epidermal keratinocytes (HaCaTs) with respect to wound healing, cellular senescence, antioxidant capacity, and oxidative stress parameters, thereby comprehensively revealing their biological activities.

Results

Morphology and characterization of pEVs

Horsetail-derived exosome-like vesicles (H-EVs) and lavender-derived exosome-like vesicles (L-EVs), isolated through stepwise centrifugation followed by ultracentrifugation, were morphologically and dimensionally characterized (Fig. 1A). Morphological analyses were carried out using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM), and both types of vesicles were found to exhibit a spherical structure (Fig. 1B). Repeated measurements in our study determined the protein concentrations of H-EV and L-EV samples to be approximately 13,200 µg/mL (13.2 mg/mL) (Fig. 1C). These values are significantly higher than the protein concentrations of pEVs reported in the literature. The high protein concentrations obtained in our study indicate that the applied isolation protocol achieved efficient vesicle purification and that the isolates carry rich content in terms of biological functionality. The size distribution was evaluated by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS). In the H-EV fraction (Fig. 1D), analyses performed with a Nanosizer revealed two distinct size populations. The dominant population was measured at 161.8 nm with a concentration of approximately 3.74 × 10⁸ particles/mL, while the second population measured 430.6 nm with a concentration of 6.73 × 10⁶ particles/mL. These findings indicate that H-EVs are predominantly concentrated within the nanometric scale (100–200 nm), although larger-sized particles or possible aggregate structures were also detected (Supplementary Fig. 1). In the L-EV fraction (Fig. 1E), the dominant size population was determined to be 173.5 nm, with a particle concentration of 3.01 × 10⁹ particles/mL. The volumetric distribution curve revealed a single prominent peak in the analyzed sample. In a recent study, the average size of lavender-derived exosome-like nanoparticles was reported as 160.1 nm [53].

The low PDI values (~ 0.1) obtained for the isolated pEV samples indicate that the vesicle populations generally exhibit a narrow size range and a relatively uniform distribution. While the L-EV samples displayed a high degree of homogeneity, the low-intensity secondary peak observed in the H-EV samples (Fig. 1D) may reflect the presence of a small number of vesicle aggregates or microscopic plant debris, likely due to the high sensitivity of the DLS technique to larger particles. Nevertheless, the overall low PDI values confirm that the isolation procedure yielded vesicle populations with sufficient purity and a controllable size distribution suitable for downstream biological experiments.

These findings demonstrate that the DLS analysis of the lavender sample supports the presence of monodisperse and biologically active nanovesicles. This result further confirmed that L-EVs exhibit high homogeneity without showing extensive aggregation or multiple peak formation (Supplementary Fig. 1). Our results demonstrate that H-EVs and L-EVs possess high purity, structural stability, and narrow size distribution, features that make them suitable for potential biological applications.

Identification of bioactive contents of pEVs by GC–MS-based metabolomic profiling

The metabolomic contents of exosome-like vesicles play a critical role in understanding the mechanisms underlying their biological activities. In this study, the comparative analysis of the metabolite profiles of H-EV and L-EV fractions enabled the identification of molecular components particularly associated with wound healing and cellular aging processes (Fig. 2). Our findings further revealed that H-EVs were relatively enriched in tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates, including citrate, malate, and fumarate. These intermediates not only sustain carbon flux into oxidative phosphorylation but also serve signaling functions that regulate cellular orientation and proliferative capacity. Therefore, their elevated levels are biologically significant in the context of tissue repair, where energy demand is markedly increased [54, 55]. Although the precise mechanisms underlying the functional delivery of these intermediates to recipient cells remain to be fully elucidated, the literature supports that plant-derived nanovesicles (pEVs) are capable of protecting and transporting such small molecules into recipient cells [56]. In addition to the TCA cycle components identified in our GC–MS analysis, metabolites such as trehalose and glycerol-1-phosphate provide important insights into the biological potential of the vesicular cargo. In particular, sugar molecules such as trehalose and various organic acids have been reported to be encapsulated within vesicle membranes, which protects them from enzymatic degradation in the extracellular environment [57]. This protective encapsulation may help explain the strong correlation observed between the metabolic profile and the biological effects identified in our study. For instance, considering the stress-protective properties of trehalose and the roles of TCA cycle intermediates in cellular energy metabolism, the vesicle-mediated transport of these molecules may be directly associated with the wound healing and antioxidant effects observed in the context of tissue repair, where cellular energy demand is markedly increased [58, 59]. However, it is also possible that some of these metabolites may be associated with the vesicle surface, allowing them to exert their effects through surface interactions as well. Therefore, further mechanistic studies are required to clearly distinguish direct causality from correlation regarding the roles of these components in recipient cells.

Metabolic profiles of pEVs from E. arvense and L. angustifolia

In the H-EV fraction, the marked increase in the levels of D-glucose, trehalose, mannose, and sucrose in terms of carbohydrate metabolites indicates an enhanced shift toward glycolysis and, in particular, the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). The PPP has been clearly demonstrated in the literature to contribute to nucleotide biosynthesis through ribose-5-phosphate production and to the maintenance of redox homeostasis and antioxidant defense through NADPH generation [60]. This metabolic framework functions as a critical biochemical axis in maintaining tissue integrity, damage response, and regenerative processes. Of note, D-(+) trehalose, which was expressed in the heatmap, was reported by Jin et al. (2023) to stimulate ATG5- and ATG7-mediated autophagy in keratinocytes, thereby enhancing cellular migration and significantly accelerating wound closure under both in vitro and in vivo conditions [59].

This finding supports the potential contribution of trehalose enrichment in the H-EV metabolome to wound healing. On the other hand, mannose is one of the fundamental substrates of protein N-glycosylation and directly influences collagen folding, secretion, and fibril organization. These processes play a central role in extracellular matrix remodeling and the sustainability of tissue regeneration [61]. In particular, stearic acid detected in the H-EV fraction is classified among long-chain saturated fatty acids and contributes to maintaining structural stability and sustaining epidermal barrier functions within the ceramide–cholesterol–free fatty acid ternary matrix of the stratum corneum. In contrast, oleic acid, a monounsaturated fatty acid, is distinguished by its ability to increase membrane fluidity and permeability. In the heatmap, α-tocopherol and caffeic acid, which were particularly enriched in L-EVs, are thought to have potentially contributed to the wound healing process through their strong antioxidant properties. α-Tocopherol, the main form of vitamin E, neutralizes peroxyl radicals and reactive oxygen species, thereby reducing lipid peroxidation and preserving the integrity of cell membranes. Similarly, caffeic acid, a phenolic compound, is known to reduce lipid peroxidation through its free radical-scavenging and anti-inflammatory effects, to decrease the activity of myeloperoxidase as an inflammatory marker, and to accelerate tissue repair by increasing collagen-like polymer synthesis in fibroblast-like cells. In a study conducted the effects of caffeic acid on wound healing were investigated in mice with skin incisions. Following topical application, caffeic acid was found to reduce lipid peroxidation through its strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, decrease myeloperoxidase activity, and increase collagen-like polymer synthesis in fibroblast-like cells. As a result of these effects, tissue repair was accelerated, and the wound closure process was significantly improved [62]. In addition, glycerol-1-phosphate (biologically sn-glycerol-3-phosphate), which was highly enriched in L-EVs, is an essential precursor of glycerolipid biosynthesis in keratinocytes. For L. angustifolia, the literature has primarily focused on volatile compounds, with linalool and linalyl acetate reported as the dominant constituents [63, 64]. In our study, however, phenolic acids (caffeic acid), vitamins (α-tocopherol), organic acids, and long-chain lipids (palmitic and stearic acids) were identified. These results highlight that L. angustifolia possesses not only aromatic compounds but also a broader metabolomic diversity, thereby providing an important contribution to the literature. This comprehensive metabolomic analysis reveals that the H-EV and L-EV fractions present distinct yet complementary biochemical axes related to energy metabolism, antioxidant defense, and tissue repair, thereby suggesting potential synergistic contributions to wound healing and aging processes. Supplementary Fig. 2 summarizes the compounds identified through GC–MS analysis by categorizing them according to their chemical classes and reporting the number of detected compounds within each group (Suplementary Fig. 2).

Evaluation of the bioactive potential of pEVs by EC₅₀ (half maximal effective concentration) analysis

In the next phase of the study, the effects of pEVs on human skin cells were investigated. In this context, the potential effects of pEVs tested on HDF and HaCaT cell lines on cell physiology were examined. These cell types represent the dermis and epidermis, the main structural components of human skin. In vitro, pEVs were applied to cells at concentrations of 1, 10, 50, 100, 200, and 500 µg/mL for 24 and 72 h. Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay, which measures the activity of dehydrogenase enzymes reflecting cellular metabolic activity [65]. This method provided valuable data regarding the effects of the applied extracts on cell health and potential toxicity. The results obtained in this study revealed that both H-EVs and L-EVs exhibited a non-cytotoxic profile on HaCaT keratinocytes and HDF dermal fibroblasts during both 24-hour and 72-hour incubation periods (Fig. 3). At all tested concentrations (1, 10, 50, 100, 200, and 500 µg/mL), cell viability values remained above 90%. Notably, at 24 h of exposure, the highest cell viability was recorded at 100 µg/mL concentration for both exosome groups. These findings suggest that H-EVs and L-EVs not only lack cytotoxicity but may also enhance cellular metabolic activity and viability under certain conditions. Consequently, both types of exosomes were found to be well tolerated by keratinocytes and dermal fibroblasts under both short- and long-term exposures, presenting a high potential for biocompatibility.

Effects of pEV applications on wound closure activity

To evaluate cell wound closure activity, which plays a critical role in wound healing, an in vitro scratch-healing model was established in HaCaT and HDF cells. Subsequently, H-EVs, L-EVs, and S-EVs were applied at three different concentrations: EC₅₀ dose, half-dose (½ EC₅₀), and double-dose (2× EC₅₀). No pEVs were applied to the control group. Wound closure activity and wound closure rates in the scratch assay were quantitatively assessed using ImageJ software which has been widely applied for wound healing analysis in cell culture models [66, 67].

When the effects of H-EVs on wound healing in HaCaT cells were examined (Fig. 4A), the closure rate in the control group remained at 45.01% at 48 h. In contrast, all H-EV-treated dose groups achieved complete closure (100%) at 48 h. At 24 h, closure rates were measured as 17.75% at ½ dose, 18.75% at 1 dose, and 23.61% at 2 doses, indicating that dose escalation slightly accelerated wound closure activity during the first 24 h. Microscopic images demonstrated that H-EVs markedly enhanced wound closure activity, resulting in complete closure of the wound area, particularly at 48 h (Supplementary Fig. 3).

In lavender-derived L-EV applications, wound closure was significantly accelerated compared with the control group (Fig. 4B). While closure in the control was 42.44% at 48 h, all L-EV dose groups achieved complete closure (100%) by the same time point. At 24 h, closure rates were 33.34%, 41.18%, and 46.47% at ½ dose, 1 dose, and 2 doses, respectively. Notably, high-dose L-EV application resulted in the greatest enhancement of wound closure activity and wound area reduction within 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 3).

S-EVs, obtained from the combination of H-EVs and L-EVs, achieved the most rapid wound closure in HaCaT cells (Fig. 4C). In the control group, closure at 48 h was limited to 48.31%, whereas in the ½ dose and 1 dose applications, closure reached 100% by 48 h. At 2 doses, closure was recorded at 88% at 48 h. At 24 h, closure rates were determined as 41.75% at ½ dose, 41.68% at 1 dose, and 28.32% at 2 doses, indicating that the medium-dose application most efficiently enhanced wound closure activity during the early phase (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Wound-healing analyses in HaCaT cells revealed that both H-EV and L-EV applications individually increased keratinocyte migration and proliferation significantly, with L-EVs producing a higher closure rate in the early phase. S-EV application, particularly at the medium dose, accelerated wound closure activitysynergistically and achieved high closure rates within a shorter period. These findings demonstrate that plant-derived EVs, either individually or in combination, possess strong therapeutic potential in keratinocyte-based wound-healing models.

When the effects of horsetail-derived H-EVs on wound healing in HDF cells were evaluated (Fig. 4D), closure in the control group was limited to 49.58% at 48 h. In contrast, all H-EV-treated dose groups achieved complete closure (100%) at 48 h. At 24 h, closure rates were 35.48% at ½ dose, 31.27% at 1 dose, and 43.75% at 2 doses, with the highest closure observed in the 2-dose group (Supplementary Fig. 4). Microscopic images confirmed that H-EVs, particularly at 2 doses, significantly accelerated wound closure activity. These findings indicate that H-EVs accelerate the wound closure process by enhancing fibroblast wound closure activity in a dose-dependent manner.

The wound-healing effects of lavender-derived L-EVs in HDF cells were also clearly evident (Fig. 4E). In the control group, closure at 48 h was 33.33%, whereas in the 1-dose and 2-dose L-EV groups, complete closure (100%) was achieved by 48 h. At 24 h, closure rates were 25% at ½ dose, 37.71% at 1 dose, and 35.86% at 2 doses. Notably, the 1-dose and 2-dose applications provided significant increases in closure rates compared with the control before 48 h (Supplementary Fig. 4). Image analyses confirmed that L-EV applications accelerated fibroblast wound closure activity and filled the wound gap within a shorter time.

S-EVs, obtained by combining H-EVs and L-EVs, produced the most potent wound-healing response in HDF cells (Fig. 4F). In the control group, the closure rate at 48 h was 57.32%, whereas the 2-dose S-EV group achieved complete closure (100%) at 48 h, and the 1-dose group achieved 92%. At 24 h, closure rates were 30% at ½ dose, 44.60% at 1 dose, and 51.08% at 2 doses. Particularly in the 2-dose S-EV group, both rapid closure at 24 h and complete healing at 48 h were observed, demonstrating that the synergistic effect enhanced cell migration and proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 4). H-EV, L-EV, and S-EV applications significantly accelerated wound healing in HDF cells, with the S-EV formulation producing the highest closure percentage within 24 h and highlighting the biological advantage of the synergistic effect.

When the results from HDF and HaCaT cells are considered together, all EV types (H-EVs, L-EVs, and S-EVs) accelerated wound closure in both cell lines. In HDF cells, both H-EV and L-EV applications achieved 100% closure at 48 h, while in HaCaT cells, this level was reached particularly with L-EVs and H-EVs, whereas in S-EVs, closure at high dose was limited to 88%. Early-phase (24-hour) data indicated that closure rates were generally higher in HDF cells compared with HaCaT cells, with S-EVs showing a pronounced synergistic effect particularly in HDFs.

Characterization of cellular senescence via β-galactosidase activity

In this study, an in vitro senescence model was established in HaCaT and HDF cells to investigate the effects of pEVs on cellular aging. Cells were induced into a senescent phenotype using β-galactosidase application, and SA-β-gal staining was performed to assess the senescent state. The color changes observed in cell cultures after treatment served as the primary evaluation criterion for visually determining the degree of senescence.

Phenotypic data obtained from SA-β-gal staining revealed that pEV application exerted senescence-attenuating effects in both HaCaT and HDF cells. In the control groups, senescence-associated morphological flattening and intense blue staining were commonly observed. In contrast, groups treated with pEVs, particularly at 1 × EC₅₀ and 2 × EC₅₀ doses, exhibited a notable reduction in blue staining intensity and marked improvement in cell morphology.

In HaCaT cells, H-EV treatment at high dose replaced the enlarged cytoplasmic structures, indicative of senescence, with more compact and spindle-shaped cells. L-EVs reduced staining intensity, with morphological improvements becoming more pronounced at medium and high doses. Combination treatments showed effects similar to or stronger than single treatments across both parameters (Fig. 5A).

A similar trend was observed in HDF cells, where the intense blue staining and irregular morphology seen in the control group were reduced in a dose-dependent manner following pEV treatment; cells displayed more regular, elongated, and healthy morphological features (Fig. 5B). These observations demonstrate that pEVs can reduce the proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells at the phenotypic level and partially reverse senescence-associated morphological changes. Relative SA-β-galactosidase activity data showed that both H-EVs and L-EVs significantly suppressed senescence-associated biochemical activity in HaCaT (A) and HDF (B) cell lines. In the control groups, the normalized β-galactosidase activity reference value was set at 1.0-fold, whereas pEV application decreased this value in all dose groups.

In HaCaT cells, H-EVs, particularly at 2 × EC₅₀, reduced β-galactosidase activity to minimal levels, generating a strong anti-senescence response. L-EVs also decreased activity in a dose-dependent manner, with marked suppression observed at high dose. S-EVs exhibited a stronger inhibitory effect compared with single treatments in both cell types. A similar pattern was observed in HDF cells, where all pEV applications reduced β-galactosidase activity compared with the control group, with high-dose combination treatment providing the lowest activity levels. These findings demonstrate that pEVs derived from both plant species inhibit the senescence biomarker SA-β-gal activity in a dose-dependent manner and significantly attenuate the biochemical indicators of cellular aging.

Figure 5 also illustrates the effects of plant-derived extracellular vesicles (pEVs) on cellular senescence markers in both keratinocytes (HaCaT) and dermal fibroblasts (HDF). In HaCaT cells (Fig. 5C), a similar decrease was observed; however, reductions in β-Gal activity were more limited compared with fibroblasts. This reduction, observed even at low doses, highlights the potential of pEV treatments to suppress senescence in dermal fibroblasts. This suggests that the anti-senescence effects of pEVs may vary depending on cell type and may be more pronounced in dermal fibroblasts.

Antioxidant capacity of pEVs and modulation of oxidative stress

In the literature, plant-derived exosome like nanovesicles have been shown to support tissue repair by influencing intracellular mechanisms and gene expression. Specifically, wheat- and grapefruit-derived pEVs have been reported to enhance collagen synthesis, accelerate fibroblast proliferation and migration to the injured site, and support structures contributing to neovascularization. In line with these studies, it has been demonstrated that the natural antioxidant components of pEVs reduce cell damage associated with skin aging by decreasing intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and stimulating collagen production [68–71]. Similarly, in our study, pEVs were found to exhibit potential for counteracting senescence and promoting wound healing. Based on this background, the antioxidant capacities of H-EVs and L-EVs and their effects on oxidative stress were evaluated.

Plant-derived exosomes possess the potential to reduce oxidative damage by supporting cellular antioxidant defense systems through their bioactive components. Investigating the effects of pEV application on oxidant levels in cellular senescence models is important to elucidate their functions in redox homeostasis and aging-related mechanisms. Accordingly, total oxidant status (TOS) measurements were performed to evaluate how pEV treatment modulates oxidative load under senescent conditions. In HDF cells (Fig. 6A), total oxidant status under senescent conditions was markedly increased compared with the control group. pEV treatment significantly reduced this increase across all plant-derived and synergistic groups (p < 0.0001). A similar trend was observed in HaCaT cells (Fig. 6B), where oxidant levels elevated by senescence were significantly decreased following pEV application. In both cell types, H-EVs were particularly effective in suppressing the increase in oxidant levels.

In HDF cells (Fig. 6C), the total antioxidant status under senescent conditions was significantly reduced compared with the control group. pEV treatment markedly reversed this decline in all plant-derived and synergistic groups, with values exceeding control levels (p < 0.0001). A similar trend was observed in HaCaT cells (Fig. 6D), where pEV treatment significantly increased antioxidant capacity that had been reduced by senescence. In both cell types, H-EVs and synergistic combinations stood out as the most effective in enhancing antioxidant capacity.

Oxidative Stress Index (OSI) is an important biochemical marker used to determine the extent of oxidative stress in cells or tissues. This index is obtained by evaluating the ratio between total oxidant status (TOS) and total antioxidant status (TAS), thereby quantitatively reflecting disturbances in cellular redox balance. High OSI values indicate an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant defense mechanisms, leading to increased oxidative damage. Therefore, OSI measurements serve as a critical tool for understanding the role of oxidative stress in processes such as aging, chronic inflammation, degenerative diseases, and wound healing. In particular, examining how natural biological vectors such as plant-derived exosomes influence OSI provides valuable insights into their therapeutic potential [72, 73].

In line with this, both H-EVs and L-EVs significantly suppressed senescence-induced increases in oxidative stress in both HDF and HaCaT cells (Fig. 6E and F). Whether applied individually or synergistically, pEVs from both plant species markedly reduced the oxidative stress index, thereby contributing to the maintenance of redox balance. These results suggest that pEVs isolated from different plant species may play a crucial role in managing oxidative stress during cellular aging processes and in preserving tissue health.

Transcriptional effects of pEVs at the cellular level

qPCR analyses demonstrated that H-EVs and L-EVs, both individually and particularly under synergistic conditions, significantly modulated genetic programs associated with wound healing in HaCaT and HDF cells (Fig. 7). Overall, our findings demonstrate that pEVs modulate regenerative processes in both epidermal (Fig. 7A) and dermal (Fig. 7B) cells in a multifaceted manner. While collagen synthesis and FGF expression were enhanced, the regulation of MMPs and IL-6 occurred in a way that aligned with the physiological stages of wound healing. Notably, synergistic applications elicited stronger biological responses compared to single treatments.

Antimicrobial activity of H-EVs and L-EVs

Previous studies have reported that pEVs carry numerous proteins with antifungal, antimicrobial, and hydrolytic activities, representing key components of the innate immune system of plants and contributing to cell wall organization [74–76]. In line with this, the antimicrobial and antifungal effects of H-EVs and L-EVs were investigated. Our findings revealed that the antimicrobial effects of H-EVs varied depending on the microorganism tested. Against Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 (Fig. 8A), a significant reduction in bacterial growth was observed only at the highest concentration (10⁷ particles/mL) at 12 h, whereas no inhibition was detected at 6–24 h. This suggests that H-EVs may exert a limited and transient effect against Gram-negative bacteria. In contrast, a stronger and sustained antimicrobial effect was observed against Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 43,300 (Fig. 8B). At 6 h, significant inhibition was detected only at 10⁷ particles/mL, but by 12 h, growth inhibition became evident across all concentrations tested (10⁵, 10⁶, and 10⁷ particles/mL). This suppressive effect persisted at 24 h, demonstrating a stronger and sustained antimicrobial activity. These results indicate that H-EVs exhibit a stronger and longer-lasting inhibitory effect against Gram-positive bacteria. No significant inhibition was observed against Candida albicans ATCC 10,231 (Fig. 8C), indicating that H-EVs do not possess antifungal potential. Overall, these results demonstrate that the antimicrobial activity of H-EVs is microorganism-dependent, showing particularly strong and sustained inhibition against S. aureus, while exhibiting limited or negligible effects against P. aeruginosa and C. albicans.

The antimicrobial and antifungal activities of L-EVs were also evaluated, and the effects were found to vary depending on the microorganism tested. On Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 (Fig. 8D), no significant changes were observed within the first 6 h. However, at 12 h, significant bacterial growth inhibition was detected at concentrations of 10⁶ and 10⁷ particles/mL, and by 24 h, this inhibition became statistically significant at all tested concentrations. These findings indicate that the antimicrobial activity of lavender-derived EVs strengthens over time, becoming more pronounced after 24 h. In contrast, for Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 43,300 (Fig. 8E), no significant differences were observed at any concentration during the 6-, 12-, and 24-hour time points. This indicates that L-EVs are ineffective against Gram-positive bacteria. Similarly, for Candida albicans ATCC 10,231 (Fig. 8F), no significant inhibition was observed compared with the control group after 24 and 48 h of incubation. These results suggest that lavender EVs do not exert antifungal effects. Overall, L-EVs exhibited a time-dependent antimicrobial effect against the Gram-negative bacterium P. aeruginosa, but showed no activity against the Gram-positive bacterium (S. aureus) or yeast (C. albicans). This suggests that L-EVs may possess selective antimicrobial potential, being particularly effective against Gram-negative bacteria. The antimicrobial effect of EVs observed under the tested conditions was interpreted as bacteriostatic, since bacterial growth resumed after prolonged incubation, as indicated by increased OD values in the treatment groups at the additional 24 h time point.

Discussion

The morphology and characterization of Equisetum arvense pEVs were analyzed, and the observations were consistent with bimodal size distributions reported for plant-derived nanovesicles, such as those from Raphanus sativus [10]. Similarly, broccoli (Brassica oleracea var. italica)-derived nanoparticles exhibited two separate size distributions at ~ 18 nm and ~ 118 nm [77]. In addition, exosome-like nanoparticles derived from ginseng (Panax ginseng) were reported to have an average size of ~ 105.8 nm; however, depending on the measurement mode, the presence of larger particle populations has also been described [15]. Furthermore, comprehensive reviews on plant-derived extracellular vesicles have highlighted the occurrence of two or more vesicle populations in several plant species, including apple (Malus domestica), maize (Zea mays), and blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum) [56]. These findings suggest that the double peak observed in our E. arvense sample (Fig. 1D) may represent smaller exosome-like nanovesicles together with larger microvesicle or aggregate subpopulations. The secondary peak detected in our H-EV sample most likely reflects minor aggregates, which can arise due to the high sensitivity of dynamic light scattering (DLS) to larger particles. In contrast, Lavandula angustifolia samples exhibited a homogeneous single-peak profile (Fig. 1E), indicating stronger colloidal stability and greater resistance to aggregation. Considering that particle size distributions ranging from 50 to 500 nm have been widely reported in the literature, the 430 nm peak observed in this study may also fall within the biologically acceptable range. However, further validation using complementary techniques such as, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and fractionation approaches would be necessary to confirm this interpretation [56].

Although the surface charge (zeta potential), which plays a critical role in vesicle colloidal stability and electrostatic interactions with cellular membranes, was not directly measured in this study, the DLS data still provide valuable insight into the physicochemical properties of the isolated pEVs. Previous reports have indicated that plant-derived nanovesicles generally exhibit negative zeta potentials, typically ranging from approximately − 15 to − 30 mV, primarily due to the presence of acidic lipids and membrane-associated proteins on their surfaces. This negative surface charge is thought to facilitate interactions with mammalian cell membranes. Consequently, the strong regenerative responses observed in both experimental groups further support that the isolated pEVs possess functional surface properties that enable effective interactions with biological membranes [78, 79]. Overall, the DLS results obtained in this study are consistent with previous reports demonstrating the presence of multiple vesicle size populations in plant-derived vesicles, such as those isolated from grapefruit and tomato [80].

The protein concentrations of H-EV and L-EV samples were determined to be approximately 13,200 µg/mL (13.2 mg/mL), which is higher than many protein concentrations previously reported for plant-derived vesicles. For example, Emmanuela et al. reported a protein concentration of 275.38 µg/mL for pEVs isolated from Solanum nigrum fruits using the BCA assay [81]. Similarly, papaya-derived pEVs were reported to have protein concentrations ranging from 1486 to 1761 µg/mL when measured by the BCA method [82]. Such variations can be attributed to differences in the plant source as well as the diversity of isolation and protein quantification methods used [82]. The relatively high protein concentration obtained in our study is consistent with previous reports describing high vesicle yields from plant tissues. Plant tissues possess complex cell walls and high metabolic activity, which may facilitate vesicle release and protein recovery during isolation procedures [83]. For instance, Ju et al. (2013) obtained protein yields of approximately 300–400 mg per 1 kg of starting material from grapes, while Zhang et al. (2016) reported comparable yields from ginger tissue using ultracentrifugation-based isolation methods. The total protein amount obtained from 20 g of starting material in our study is comparable to these pioneering studies in terms of yield per gram of tissue [16, 18]. In the present study, protein concentrations were determined using the Qubit fluorescence assay, which is less affected by contaminants such as free amino acids compared to colorimetric methods. Although nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) or density-gradient purification was not performed, the Qubit assay enabled reliable protein quantification of the isolated vesicles. Based on these measurements, the isolation yield was calculated as 0.46 mg/g (mg protein per g fresh tissue), producing 500 µL of pEV stock with 13.2 mg/mL protein from 20 g of fresh tissue. Furthermore, the low polydispersity index (PDI = 0.1) obtained from DLS analysis indicates a highly homogeneous vesicle population. The small secondary peak (~ 450 nm) observed in H-EV samples likely reflects minor aggregation caused by the intensity-weighted measurement principle of DLS and does not significantly compromise the purity of the dominant vesicle population. Consistent with our approach, Bokka et al. (2020) compared BCA and Qubit methods for protein quantification in tomato-derived vesicles and reported that the Qubit assay provided more reliable measurements. Consequently, only Qubit-based results were used in their analyses [84].

Metabolomic investigations of E. arvense reported in the literature have primarily identified volatile oils containing hexahydrofarnesyl acetone, cis-geranyl acetone, thymol, and trans-phytol [85], along with flavonoids and phytosterols [86]. In contrast, our study detected several non-volatile metabolites, including 5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide, α-tocopherol, organic acids, and sugars, providing additional insight into the metabolic diversity of this species. Among the detected metabolites, oleic acid has been reported to modulate membrane properties and enhance skin penetration at low or moderate concentrations, whereas higher concentrations may disrupt epidermal barrier integrity, indicating dose-dependent biological effects [87, 88]. α-Tocopherol has been widely associated with wound healing due to its antioxidant activity and its ability to promote keratinocyte and fibroblast proliferation [38]. For example, Bonferoni et al. demonstrated that α-tocopherol incorporated into chitosan oleate nanoemulsions improved antioxidant activity and bioavailability in wound healing models, leading to increased keratinocyte and fibroblast proliferation and reduced oxidative stress [89]. Similarly, Inti et al. (2025) reported that topical α-tocopherol derived from red palm oil accelerated wound healing in Wistar rats by reducing inflammation and increasing collagen density [90]. In combination with caffeic acid, α-tocopherol may further strengthen antioxidant defenses and support cellular proliferation, inflammation control, and extracellular matrix remodeling. Topical glycerol is also known to accelerate epidermal barrier repair; therefore, the elevated glycerol-1-phosphate levels detected in L-EV samples may indicate a phenotype associated with improved barrier integrity and moisture balance [91]. Although numerous plant extracts and vesicles have been reported to support wound healing through mechanisms such as enhanced cell proliferation, migration, collagen synthesis, and anti-inflammatory activity, E. arvense is particularly notable due to its silica and flavonoid content. In diabetic rat models, topical ointments containing 5% and 10% horsetail extract increased wound closure rates to 99.71% and 99.93%, respectively, while also enhancing dermal and epidermal regeneration and granulation tissue formation [92]. A 3% horsetail ointment similarly accelerated episiotomy wound healing and reduced pain levels [93] and horsetail extracts have been shown to increase fibroblast collagen synthesis in diabetic wounds [94]. Consistent with these findings, plant-derived extracellular vesicles from various species have also been reported to promote wound healing. Vesicles from Allium sativum regulate intercellular communication and reduce inflammation [95], while Zingiber officinale vesicles promote wound repair through bioactive compounds such as 6-gingerol and 6-shogaol [96]. Vesicles derived from Malus domestica increase fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis [97] whereas citrus-derived vesicles enhance fibroblast proliferation and exhibit anti-inflammatory effects [98]. Plant-derived nanovesicles have also been reviewed as promising therapeutic agents for chronic wound healing [32]. For instance, Dendrobium officinale vesicles enhanced endothelial tube formation in HUVEC cells, demonstrating pro-angiogenic activity [99] Collectively, these findings indicate that plant vesicles can mitigate oxidative endothelial dysfunction and promote tissue repair responses. In a study conducted by Kim, Choi et al. (2021), the antioxidant effects of small extracellular vesicles (A-EVs) obtained from Aloe vera peels were investigated, and in an in vitro wound model, A-EVs were found to enhance the migratory capacity of HaCaT and HDF cells. In another study conducted the following year, extracellular vesicles isolated from Aloe saponaria (AS-EVs) were investigated for their potential use as a therapeutic approach in chronic wound healing [100, 101]. The study demonstrated that AS-EVs supported proliferation and migration of HDF cells and enhanced tube formation in human umbilical vein endothelial cells, thereby stimulating angiogenesis, a critical step in effective wound healing. Additional studies further support the regenerative potential of plant-derived EVs: vesicles from Aloe vera enhanced the migration of HaCaT and HDF cells in wound models, while Aloe saponaria vesicles promoted fibroblast proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis in endothelial cells [100, 102]. In our wound-healing experiments, closure rates were primarily evaluated within the first 24 h. Since dermal fibroblasts typically have a doubling time of 20–24 h, wound closure during this early period largely reflects cell migration rather than proliferation. Because proliferation inhibitors such as mitomycin C were not used and observations extended to 48 h, the observed wound closure likely represents a combined effect of both migration and proliferation. Previous studies have similarly reported that vesicles derived from medicinal plants can stimulate both processes simultaneously in wound models [8, 100]. Accordingly, our results were interpreted as evidence of overall wound-healing potential rather than a strictly migration-specific effect. The wound closure observed after H-EV and L-EV treatment clearly demonstrates the regenerative potential of plant-derived nanovesicles.

Lavandula angustifolia, for example, possesses strong anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties due to compounds such as linalool and linalyl acetate. Nanoemulsion formulations containing lavender essential oil have been shown to accelerate deep skin wound healing, and exosome-like nanoparticles derived from L. angustifolia protect against UVB-induced skin aging [53]. Oxidative stress is a critical factor in impaired angiogenesis and delayed wound healing, particularly under hyperglycemic conditions [103, 104]. In this context, ginger-derived vesicles have demonstrated strong anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities in vivo [105] and vesicles from related species have promoted diabetic wound healing by upregulating the pro-migratory factor follistatin-like 1 [106]. Grapefruit-derived vesicles have been reported to exhibit no cytotoxic effects on peripheral blood mononuclear cells [107] while papaya vesicles have shown high cell viability across multiple concentrations [82]. In another study conducted by a different research group, the protective role of exosomes isolated from the iris plant (Iris-exosomes) against oxidative stress-induced cellular aging was investigated. Human epidermal keratinocytes (nHEKs) exposed to H₂O₂ exhibited a markedly increased proportion of senescent cells, whereas groups pre-treated with Iris-exosomes showed a significant, dose-dependent reduction in this proportion [102]. Moreover, Panax ginseng-derived vesicles reduced SA-β-gal activity and reversed senescent morphology in UVB-irradiated melanocytes, indicating strong anti-senescence effects [108], and Iris-derived exosomes decreased ROS production and p21 expression in keratinocytes under oxidative stress, suggesting a protective role against cellular aging [109]. Furthermore, expression of the cell cycle inhibitor gene p21 was decreased following Iris-exosome treatment. These results demonstrate that Iris-exosomes possess the capacity to reduce intracellular ROS production and attenuate associated aging processes. Additional evidence supporting the antioxidant activity of plant vesicles comes from studies on lemon-derived nanovesicles, which significantly reduced ROS levels in fibroblasts under UVB- and H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress. This effect was associated with activation of the AhR/Nrf2 signaling pathway and was confirmed in zebrafish models [77]. Similarly, Aloe vera-derived vesicles increased antioxidant enzyme activity and activated Nrf2-mediated defense mechanisms in HaCaT cells exposed to oxidative stress [100]. In our study, cellular senescence was evaluated using SA-β-gal activity, the most widely used phenotypic biomarker for senescent cells. The significant decrease in SA-β-gal-positive cells and improvement in cell morphology strongly suggest that pEVs suppress senescent phenotypes. However, the lack of confirmation of cellular senescence with additional molecular markers such as p16, p21, or DNA damage markers is considered a limitation of the study [10, 109–113]. Although cellular uptake mechanisms were not directly visualized using fluorescent labeling in this study, the observed anti-senescence and redox-modulating effects suggest that pEVs may deliver bioactive cargo such as microRNAs or polyphenols through endocytic pathways or activate intracellular signaling through membrane receptors [83]. Several researchers have proposed that combinations of plant-derived vesicles may exert systemic protective effects by correcting redox imbalance and reprogramming tissue microenvironments [33]. Plant-derived vesicle mixtures obtained from grape, blood orange, papaya, pomegranate, and mandarin have been reported to restore mitochondrial homeostasis, reduce oxidative stress, and enhance the expression of wound-healing-related proteins such as vimentin and MMP-9 in human dermal fibroblasts [112, 114]. A subsequent study further demonstrated that this pEV mixture corrected H₂O₂-induced redox imbalance and contained natural antioxidants including citric acid, ascorbic acid, glutathione, catalase, and SOD, supporting improved cellular redox balance and wound healing responses [115]. Plant-derived extracellular vesicles are increasingly recognized not only as carriers of antioxidant molecules but also as regulators of intracellular signaling pathways. Several studies have shown that these vesicles activate the Nrf2 signaling pathway, leading to the transcriptional upregulation of antioxidant defense genes such as HO-1, SOD, and CAT in mammalian skin cells [116–119]. Activation of this pathway enhances cellular antioxidant capacity and promotes redox homeostasis. Accordingly, the elevated TAS levels observed in the present study may reflect an adaptive cellular response induced by pEV-mediated priming of the endogenous antioxidant system. Consistently, vesicles derived from red mandarin have also demonstrated strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties attributed to their phenolic and flavonoid content [98].

Our qPCR results further support these findings. Upregulation of COL1A1 and COL1A2 indicated increased collagen synthesis, particularly in fibroblasts, which are responsible for extracellular matrix production. Similar effects have been reported in kale-derived vesicles enriched in glucoraphanin, which increased type I collagen expression through microRNA-mediated suppression of Smad7. In addition, significant upregulation of FGF suggested that pEVs enhance growth factor signaling that promotes cell proliferation and migration, consistent with previous studies on plant-derived vesicles [120, 121]. The increase in antioxidant capacity, reduction in oxidant load, and decrease in the oxidative stress index indicated that pEVs contribute to the rebalancing of redox homeostasis and to protective mechanisms against cellular damage. Furthermore, qPCR results showed that the upregulation of COL1A1/COL1A2 and FGF genes stimulated ECM synthesis and proliferative processes, while the regulation of MMP and IL-6 contributed to the balanced remodeling of wound healing.

The antimicrobial activity of EVs under the tested conditions appears to be predominantly bacteriostatic rather than bactericidal, as bacterial growth resumed after prolonged incubation, evidenced by the increased OD values observed in the treatment groups at the additional 24-hour time point. Antimicrobial analyses also support the therapeutic potential of the investigated plants. E. arvense essential oil inhibits pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli [85], while L. angustifolia essential oil exhibits activity against S. aureus, E. coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Candida albicans, including MRSA and VRE strains [122]. Previous studies on the parent plants also support the possibility that vesicle preparations from these sources may have antimicrobial potential. For example, Equisetum arvense essential oil has been reported to inhibit both Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli [85], while Lavandula angustifolia essential oil showed broad inhibitory activity against pathogens including S. aureus, E. coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Candida albicans, with notable efficacy against MRSA and VRE strains [123]. Although essential oils and plant-derived vesicles are compositionally distinct and should not be considered functionally equivalent, these findings suggest that both plants contain bioactive components with antimicrobial relevance. This interpretation is further supported by recent studies on plant-derived nanovesicles. Saroj et al. (2024) reported that mint-derived extracellular vesicles incorporated into an oxidized alginate/chitosan hydrogel significantly inhibited M. luteus and E. coli and promoted wound healing in vivo, achieving 99% wound closure within 10 days and reducing inflammatory responses [124]. Similarly, dandelion-derived extracellular vesicle-like nanoparticles were shown to bind S. aureus exotoxins and protect host cells from toxin-mediated damage, resulting in complete detoxification in vivo [125]. In this context, our findings are consistent with growing evidence that plant-derived vesicular systems may contribute to antimicrobial and wound-supportive responses. However, under our experimental conditions, the observed effect was interpreted as primarily bacteriostatic rather than bactericidal, since bacterial growth resumed after prolonged incubation. Therefore, the present findings should be considered preliminary in vitro evidence of growth-inhibitory activity, and further studies are needed to define the active cargo, mechanism of action, and clinically relevant topical dose range. A limitation of this experiment is that direct interaction between the isolated plant-derived vesicles and bacterial cells was not evaluated using fluorescence labeling or microscopy. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the observed bacteriostatic effect is mediated by direct vesicle-bacterial cell association, membrane interaction, cargo transfer, or indirect extracellular mechanisms. Another limitation of this study is that the antimicrobial activity of pEVs was evaluated only against planktonic bacterial cultures. Because biofilm-forming microorganisms more accurately represent infected wound environments and often exhibit increased tolerance to antimicrobial agents, future studies should investigate the effects of pEVs on wound-relevant biofilm-producing strains.

Overall, this study demonstrates that pEVs not only mitigate the detrimental effects of oxidative stress and senescence but also accelerate tissue regeneration, collagen synthesis, and wound closure, thereby representing multifunctional biotherapeutic candidates. Notably, among the treatments applied, H-EVs exhibited significant effects, which varied in a species-specific and time-dependent manner. The obtained results further indicated that these vesicles possess not only antimicrobial activity but also potential as biotherapeutic candidates in suppressing infection and supporting host responses. Collectively, the data strongly support the notion that pEVs represent safe, biocompatible, and effective natural nanovesicle platforms with high translational potential to be advanced into clinical research for applications in both dermatology and regenerative medicine.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles isolated from Lavandula angustifolia and Equisetum arvense possess significant biological functions associated with wound healing and skin regeneration. Comprehensive physicochemical characterization revealed that both H-EVs and L-EVs exhibit a stable spherical morphology with a narrow size distribution and high protein content, supporting their applicability as bioactive nanocarriers. However, the absence of molecular marker analyses, such as PEN1, TET8, or HSP70, represents a limitation of the present study, as the use of these proteins could further strengthen the confirmation of the vesicular origin of the isolated particles. In addition, the uptake mechanisms of pEVs by target cells were not directly investigated in this study, which can also be considered an important limitation. Elucidating these mechanisms and confirming the cellular internalization pathways of the vesicles will therefore represent an important objective for future studies.

In vitro investigations demonstrated that these vesicles are non-cytotoxic and enhance cellular metabolic activity in keratinocytes and dermal fibroblasts. Functional assays further showed that pEVs significantly accelerate wound healing and cell migration, with synergistic effects observed in combination treatments. By increasing antioxidant capacity and reducing oxidant load, pEV administration markedly decreased oxidative stress and cellular senescence, highlighting their protective role against stress-induced cellular damage. In this study, cellular senescence was evaluated using senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity, which is widely recognized as a phenotypic biomarker of senescent cells rather than a direct inducer of the senescence process. The observed reduction in SA-β-gal-positive cells, together with the improvement in cellular morphology, suggests that pEV treatment may attenuate senescence-associated phenotypes. Nevertheless, the absence of additional molecular validation using established senescence markers such as p16, p21, or DNA damage-associated markers constitutes another limitation of the present study.

While maintaining the balanced regulation of MMP and IL-6, which is essential for controlled tissue remodeling, gene expression analysis revealed increased levels of key wound-healing markers, including COL1A1, COL1A2, and FGF. Furthermore, the observed antifungal and antibacterial properties indicate that pEVs may contribute to the prevention of infections during the wound-healing process. This antimicrobial activity of EVs observed under the experimental conditions is more consistent with a bacteriostatic effect than a bactericidal one, as reflected by the increased OD values detected in the treatment groups at the additional 24-hour measurement. Although the in vitro nature of this study represents a limitation in terms of validating the findings in more complex biological systems, the comprehensive data obtained here highlight the therapeutic potential of pEVs and provide an essential foundation for our planned in vivo wound model studies as the next step. Overall, our results support the development and clinical application of pEVs derived from Equisetum arvense and Lavandula angustifolia as adaptable, safe, and effective biotherapeutic options in dermatology and regenerative medicine.

Methods

Plant material selection

The plant materials used in this study were obtained from the Garden of Medicinal and Aromatic Plants, which is affiliated with the Department of Traditional, Complementary, and Integrative Medicine under the Institute of Public Health at Ankara Yıldırım Beyazıt University (Ankara, Turkiye). For experimental applications, two different medicinal plant species were selected as sources of exosome-like nanoparticles: Equisetum arvense L. and Lavandula angustifolia. The plants were freshly harvested, and in the present study, the flower parts of both species were selected for use. During the sterilization step, the plant samples were washed, disinfected with 70% ethanol, and then rinsed with autoclaved double-distilled water (ddH₂O) to remove residual ethanol. The prepared plant materials were subsequently stored for use in the planned analyses.

Isolation of pEVs

Following sterilization, the plants were homogenized in PBS (Phosphate-Buffered Saline). The obtained suspension was cleared of cellular debris through stepwise centrifugation (1000–10,000 rpm) and stored at − 20 °C. Isolation of exosome-like nanoparticles was carried out using an Ultracentrifuge CP100NX (Eppendorf / Himac) at 34,000 rpm (~ 134,000 × g) and 4 °C through sequential ultracentrifugation steps. Following the first ultracentrifugation step, the resulting pellet was resuspended in fresh PBS to remove impurities and subjected to a washing step under the same speed and temperature conditions. The resulting supernatant was filtered through 0.22 μm pore size filters to remove large vesicles and residues. The isolated pEVs were stored in PBS in single-use aliquots at -80 °C, which is considered the gold standard for exosome-like vesicles in the literature. This method was meticulously applied to avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles that could compromise the structural integrity of the vesicles.

Determination of protein content

The protein content of the isolated pEVs was determined using the Qubit™ Protein Assay Kit (Catalog No: Q33211) with the Invitrogen™ Qubit™ 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Three standards were prepared for the analysis, and measurements were carried out based on this standard curve. The samples were diluted at a 1:1 ratio with RIPA (Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay) buffer, added to the reaction mixture, and incubated for 15 min in the dark. After measurement, the final protein concentrations were calculated by taking the dilution factor into account.

Examination of exosome morphology and size by transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The morphology and size distribution of the exosome-like nanoparticles were examined using TEM (FEI, Tecnai G2 S-TWIN, USA). The samples were adsorbed onto carbon-coated Formvar-supported copper grids and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, negative staining was performed with 1% uranyl acetate, and after drying, imaging was carried out under the TEM.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis

The size distribution and polydispersity index (PDI) of the exosome-like nanoparticles were determined using the dynamic light scattering method. For measurement, the samples were diluted at a ratio of 1:10 with DPBS and analyzed using a Nanosizer (Malvern Panalytical, UK) instrument.

GC-MS analysis and data processing

For metabolomic analyses, metabolites isolated using the methanol–water–chloroform system were analyzed by Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS) after derivatization. The derivatization process was carried out using N-methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (MSTFA) and N-tert-butyldimethylsilyl-N-methyltrifluoroacetamide (MTBSTFA) agents. The spectra obtained from GC-MS were processed using MetaboAnalyst software. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to identify differences between groups, and heat maps were generated to illustrate the contributions of the metabolites.

In vitro cell experiments

Cell culture conditions

To evaluate the biological effects of exosomes, human keratinocyte (HaCaT) and human dermal fibroblast (HDF) cell lines were used. HaCaT cells were cultured in RPMI (Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 Medium) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), while HDF cells were cultured in DMEM (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium) medium supplemented with 10% FBS. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Cell passaging

The cells were monitored morphologically at regular intervals under an inverted microscope, and the culture media were refreshed every 2–3 days.

Cell counting

Cell viability was assessed using the trypan blue exclusion method, and viable cell density was calculated with the TC20™ Automated Cell Counter (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc. Hercules, CA, USA). Exponentially growing cells were seeded into 96-well cell culture plates (Corning Inc. Corning, NY, USA) at a density of 1.0 × 10⁵ cells per well and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

pEV application to cells

To determine the optimal dose and incubation time for exosome treatment, preliminary experiments were conducted at concentrations of 1, 10, 50, 100, 200, and 500 µg/mL with incubation periods of 24 and 72 h. Cell viability was evaluated using the MTT assay (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide). This method is based on the ability of metabolically active cells to reduce MTT to insoluble formazan, and the absorbance measured at 590 nm is proportional to the number of viable cells (Supino, 1995). All experiments were performed in three technical and three biological replicates. Cell viability data were expressed as percentages (%) relative to the corresponding untreated control groups. For each independent experimental set, the mean value of the control group was defined as the baseline reference point (100%). All individual data points, including control replicates, were normalized to this mean value. In the graphical representation, control groups function as a fixed unit reference to illustrate the relative effects and biological responses induced by pEV treatments. Accordingly, the error bars (SD/SEM) presented reflect the relative variation observed in the treatment groups compared with this baseline level, whereas the control bars were designed to represent the normalized reference point for each experimental condition.

The synergistic group (S-EVs) was prepared by mixing H-EV and L-EV stocks at a 1:1 ratio based on total protein content. During dose preparation, the calculations were performed so that equal amounts of protein (µg) from each vesicle type were included, resulting in a homogeneous mixture. This approach ensured that both plant sources contributed equally to the system when evaluating potential synergistic effects.

Wound healing (Scratch) assay

The wound healing potential of plant-derived exosome-like vesicles (pEVs) was evaluated using an in vitro scratch assay in human keratinocyte (HaCaT) and dermal fibroblast (HDF) cell lines. The experiments were designed as three independent biological replicates (n = 3) performed at non-consecutive time points to ensure biological reproducibility and statistical reliability. Within each biological replicate, images were acquired from at least three different fields of view (FOV) per treatment group using an inverted microscope (e.g. Olympus IX71).

To ensure methodological standardization, the scratch area created at the beginning of the experiment (t = 0) was measured individually for each well and defined as 100% open wound area. This normalization strategy minimized the impact of micrometer-level variations in scratch width (inter-well variability) that may occur during manual scratch generation, thereby preventing potential bias in the statistical outcomes. The obtained data were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA), and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD).

The wound closure observed during the experimental period (0–48 h) was interpreted as the combined effect of cellular migration and proliferation induced by pEV treatment. Consistent with previous reports in the literature, a 48-hour incubation period is generally considered sufficient to capture both chemotactic responses and mitotic activity in scratch assays. Accordingly, this timeframe allows for a comprehensive evaluation of the regenerative potential of pEVs.

Detection of senescent cells based on β-galactosidase activity

In this study, cellular senescence was induced based on a replicative senescence model derived from the limited replicative capacity of HaCaT and HDF cell lines. Cells were cultured through serial passaging under in vitro conditions until approaching the Hayflick limit, and the development of the senescent phenotype—characterized by cell cycle arrest—was monitored through morphological observations, including cellular enlargement and vacuolization.

In the experiments, cells within the P5–P8 passage range were defined as the “young control” group, whereas cells that had undergone extensive serial passaging and approached the end of their replicative capacity (> P35 for HaCaT and > P20 for HDF) and displayed morphological features consistent with senescence were used as the “aged” model. To confirm the senescent state, senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity—one of the most widely recognized biomarkers of cellular senescence that exhibits enzymatic activity at pH 6.0—was assessed.

Twenty-four hours after cell seeding, plant-derived extracellular vesicles (L-EV, H-EV, and S-EV) were applied to evaluate their potential anti-senescence effects, and the cells were incubated with the vesicles for 72 h. At the end of this incubation period, the MCE Cell Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (HY-K1089) protocol was performed. Cells were fixed with the provided fixation solution for 10–15 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with an X-Gal-containing staining solution at 37 °C overnight in a CO₂-free environment to prevent pH changes.

Senescence induction and the potential protective effects of pEVs were quantitatively evaluated by counting the number of blue-stained cells under a microscope and analyzing the associated morphological changes.

Total antioxidant status (TAS) and total oxidant status (TOS) analyses

HDF and HaCaT cells were seeded into T25 culture flasks at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per flask. When the cells reached approximately 90% confluence, they were treated with H-EV, L-EV, or S-EV (synergistic group). Following 24 and 48 h of incubation, the cells were detached using trypsin and the cell lysates were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. After removal of the supernatant, the cell pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of cold PBS. The Total Antioxidant Status (TAS) was determined using an ABTS-based colorimetric assay kit (E-BC-K801-M, Elabscience, USA). A standard curve was generated using dilutions ranging from 0 to 2 mmol/L, and absorbance changes were recorded at 660 nm. The Total Oxidant Status (TOS) was measured using a colorimetric kit (E-BC-K802-M, Elabscience, USA) with H₂O₂ standards (0–100 µmol/L), and absorbance was recorded at 590 nm. For both assays, TAS and TOS concentrations of the samples were calculated using linear regression equations (y = ax + b) derived from the corresponding standard curves.

To eliminate variations related to cell density and sample volume, all TAS and TOS values were normalized to the total protein concentration (mg protein/mL) of each cell lysate. Protein quantification was performed using the Qubit Protein Assay method. Antibacterial and antifungal activity

P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and C. albicans strains were activated on MHA plates and adjusted to McFarland standards. EV concentrations of 10⁵–10⁷ vesicles/mL were tested. Cultures were incubated (bacteria: 37 °C/24 h; fungus: 35 °C/48 h), and OD₆₀₀ values were measured at specified time points. Antimicrobial activity was determined by OD reduction. Data (mean ± SD) were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s test.

Gene expression analyses

RNA Isolation and cDNA Synthesis

Total RNA was isolated using the FavorPrep™ Tissue Total RNA Mini Kit (FAVORGEN, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The purity and concentration of RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop Lite spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA), and a 260/280 absorbance ratio between 1.9 and 2.0 was considered the success criterion for isolation. RNA integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, the isolated RNA samples were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Primer design

Primers for the target genes (Col 1A1, Col 1A2, FGF, MMP, IL-6) were designed using the Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) PrimerQuest Tool online software. Gene-specific regions were selected, and GC content was optimized during the design process. Amplicon lengths and potential secondary structures (hairpins, dimerization) were checked using IDT algorithms (using PrimerQuest Tool online software). The designed primer sequences were validated for specificity through the NCBI Primer-BLAST database and were commercially synthesized (Supplementary Fig. 5).

qPCR analysis

cDNA samples were amplified with gene-specific primers using SYBR Green I dye (Bio-Rad, USA). qPCR reactions were performed on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA), and gene expression levels were quantitatively evaluated.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software LLC, Boston, MA, USA). All experiments were carried out with at least three biological replicates, and the data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between groups in cell culture experiments were evaluated using the ANOVA. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For real-time PCR analyses, normalization of gene expression levels was performed using. To determine the relative expression levels of the target genes, the 2⁻ΔΔCT (Livak) method [125] was employed. To eliminate potential variations in mRNA quantity among experimental groups and to standardize the data, Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was selected as the internal control (housekeeping gene). All gene expression data were first normalized to GAPDH and subsequently to the control group. The results were expressed as fold change relative to the control group.
