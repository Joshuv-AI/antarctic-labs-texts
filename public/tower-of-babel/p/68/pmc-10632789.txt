Deacetylation of chitin oligomers by Fusarium graminearum polysaccharide deacetylase suppresses plant immunity
Authors: Su Hu, Minxia Jin, Yangjie Xu, Qin Wu, Qiantao Jiang, Jian Ma, Yazhou Zhang, Pengfei Qi, Guoyue Chen, Yunfeng Jiang, Youliang Zheng, Yuming Wei, Qiang Xu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractChitin is a long‐chain polymer of β‐1,4‐linked N‐acetylglucosamine that forms rigid microfibrils to maintain the hyphal form and protect it from host attacks. Chitin oligomers are first recognized by the plant receptors in the apoplast region, priming the plant's immune system. Here, seven polysaccharide deacetylases (PDAs) were identified and their activities on chitin substrates were investigated via systematic characterization of the PDA family from Fusarium graminearum. Among these PDAs, FgPDA5 was identified as an important virulence factor and was specifically expressed during pathogenesis. ΔFgpda5 compromised the pathogen's ability to infect wheat. The polysaccharide deacetylase structure of FgPDA5 is essential for the pathogenicity of F. graminearum. FgPDA5 formed a homodimer and accumulated in the plant apoplast. In addition, FgPDA5 showed a high affinity toward chitin substrates. FgPDA5‐mediated deacetylation of chitin oligomers prevented activation of plant defence responses. Overall, our results identify FgPDA5 as a polysaccharide deacetylase that can prevent chitin‐triggered host immunity in plant apoplast through deacetylation of chitin oligomers.Keywords: chitin oligomers, filamentous fungi, Fusarium graminearum, plant immunity, polysaccharide deacetylases

INTRODUCTION

Wheat (Triticum aestivum) is one of the key food crops worldwide, and in recent years, pathogenic infections have seriously undercut its global production. Fusarium head blight (FHB), caused by Fusarium graminearum, is one of the most important diseases affecting wheat and barley worldwide (Bai & Shaner, 2004; Santiago et al., 2007). Epidemics of FHB cause losses of billions of dollars as a result of reduction in yield and flour quality and contamination with trichothecene mycotoxin deoxynivalenol (DON), which seriously threatens the health of humans and animals (Audenaert et al., 2014; Boenisch & Schäfer, 2011; De Walle et al., 2010). In the past decade, certain key genes responsible for pathogen virulence have been identified in F. graminearum, including a non‐ribosomal octapeptide, fusaoctaxin A, which is a virulence factor crucial for cell‐to‐cell invasion in wheat heads and coleoptiles (Jia et al., 2019). Numerous proteins secreted by F. graminearum are involved in pathogen virulence, including lipase FGL1, orphan protein Osp24 and effector Fg02685 (Jiang et al., 2020; Voigt et al., 2005; Xu et al., 2022). However, the molecular mechanism underlying direct suppression of host basal defence responses by pathogen‐secreted proteins remains unclear.

In the co‐evolution of plants and pathogens, the cell wall is the first battleground in the struggle between plants and pathogens. As a main component of the fungal cell wall, chitin is a long‐chain polymer of β‐1,4‐linked N‐acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) that forms rigid microfibrils to protect the cell wall from hydrolysis and maintains hyphal integrity, ion exchange and osmotic pressure in fungi (Free, 2013; Lenardon et al., 2010). However, plant chitinases secreted by the host first degrade the fungal cell wall and inhibit colonization (Jones et al., 2014; Pham et al., 2019). For example, expressing the chitinase gene EuCHIT2 from Eucommia ulmoides in tobacco increased host resistance to Erysiphe cichoracearum (Dong et al., 2017). In transgenic wheat, overexpressing endochitinase Ech42 also substantially restricted the mycelial growth in wheat spikes between glume and palea, and reduced mycotoxin content, ultimately increasing FHB resistance (Cheng et al., 2015). Moreover, chitin oligosaccharides released by chitinases are further perceived as elicitors that activate plant immunity via plant surface‐localized receptor recognition, known as pathogen‐associated molecular pattern (PAMP)‐triggered immunity (PTI) (Bozsoki et al., 2017).

As countermeasure, evidence indicates that fungal pathogen delivery of effectors for the suppression of plant immunity is an effective strategy. Effectors, such as PEC1666, PEC1961 and PEC2158 from powdery mildew fungus Podosphaera xanthii, exhibit chitinase activity and chitin‐binding ability to degrade chitin oligomers but cannot degrade chitin from fungal cell walls, counteracting chitin‐triggered immunity (Martínez‐Cruz et al., 2021). Chitinases are well‐known fungal pathogenicity factors that are primarily involved in the reshaping of cell walls during growth and further dissociation of chitin fragments into chitin oligomers that cannot be recognized by the host receptors (Tian et al., 2022). A functional chitinase MoChi1 from Magnaporthe oryzae suppresses chitin‐triggered plant immunity by either degrading chitin oligomers or preventing chitin fragments from being recognized by plants (Yang et al., 2019). In addition, pathogens can also deliver polysaccharide deacetylases (PDAs), such as VdPDA1 from Verticillium dahliae and Pst_13661 from Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (Gao et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2020), to deacetylate and inactivate elicitor‐active chitin oligomers. Through this, chitin‐triggered immunity is prevented and virulence is promoted. However, the chitin deacetylases (CDAs) of M. oryzae neither affect its pathogenicity nor regulate the activation of PTI, but instead mediate the adhesion of germlings to artificial hydrophobic surfaces (Geoghegan & Gurr, 2016). In comparison with other fungal PDAs, our knowledge regarding the function of PDAs in F. graminearum is limited.

In this study, PDAs in F. graminearum were evaluated. Seven genes were retrieved from the F. graminearum genome and their roles in pathogenicity were analysed in wheat spikes. The deletion of FgPDA5 reduced the fungal growth in infected wheat heads and disease development. FgPDA5 formed intermolecular homodimers. We also demonstrated that the enzymatic activity of FgPDA5 was required for its virulence. FgPDA5 showed high affinity toward chitin and fungal hyphae, and FgPDA5 also removed acetyl moieties from chitohexaose with six GlcNAc moieties (A6) in vitro. Deacetylation of chitin oligomers by FgPDA5 failed to activate an autocatalytic defence response. We speculate that FgPDA5 may have positive roles in modifying fungal chitin to avoid plant recognition and the defence response. This study could provide a novel strategy to interfere with hyphal modification in order to develop durable disease‐resistant breeding material.

RESULTS

Identification of the polysaccharide deacetylases secreted by F. graminearum

To evaluate the effect of polysaccharide deacetylase activity on the pathogenicity of F. graminearum, an InterPro search for proteins with a nodulation protein B from Rhizobium (NodB) homology domain (IPR002509) initially identified seven proteins designated FgPDA1 to FgPDA7 in F. graminearum. Only FgPDA2, FgPDA4 and FgPDA7 possessed a chitin‐binding domain, and unlike other PDA proteins, FgPDA4 had two polysaccharide deacetylase domains (Table S1). Except for FgPDA6, all putative FgPDA proteins had a predicted signal peptide (SP) (Table S1). Interestingly, none of the putative F. graminearum PDA proteins contained the GPI anchor (predicted with PredGPI) (Table S1). Phylogenetic analysis of fungal PDAs revealed that the six proteins were distributed in different clusters, with only FgPDA5 protein aggregating with the Fusarium species group, suggesting its conservation in Fusarium species (Figure S1a). The amino acid identity among the seven putative PDAs was low and ranged from 15.6% to 43.8% (Figure S1b). All PDA proteins harboured the five motifs characteristic of the catalytic site of PDAs; motif I, motif II and motif III existed in the polysaccharide deacetylase domain. Interestingly, the conserved active site residues (aspartic acid) within motif IV of FgPDA6 and FgPDA7 were replaced by glycine and tyrosine, respectively (Figure S1b).

Functional analysis of FgPDA genes in F. graminearum

To detect the function of FgPDAs under various stress conditions, six FgPDA‐knockout mutants were generated in which the target genes were replaced with the hygromycin‐resistance gene (Figure S2). However, ΔFgpda1 mutants could not be obtained after numerous attempts, thus the function of FgPDA1 was not studied in depth. We analysed the tolerance of ΔFgpda knockout mutants to cell wall, hyperosmotic and oxidative stresses. No differences in the hyphal growth rate and colony morphology were observed on complete medium (CM) after the knockout of FgPDA2, FgPDA3, FgPDA4, FgPDA5, FgPDA6 or FgPDA7 (Figure S3a,b). On CM with 1 M NaCl, only ΔFgpda2 and ΔFgpda5 mutants had significantly reduced growth rate (Figure S3b). In the presence of 0.05% H2O2, all of these mutants except for ΔFgPDA7 significantly reduced growth compared with the wild type (WT). We also assayed the effects of Congo red treatment that mimics cell wall stress. In the presence of 0.5 mM Congo red, colony growth was significantly stunted in the ΔFgpda2 and ΔFgpda5 mutants (Figure S3b).

To explore the pathogenicity during F. graminearum–wheat interaction, we evaluated the transcript level of FgPDAs from the conidia, vegetative hyphae and several early infection stages of the wheat head using reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR). As shown in Figure S2, FgPDA1, FgPDA2, FgPDA3 and FgPDA5 were highly induced in the Fusarium early infection stages (Figure S4). The transcript levels of FgPDA1 and FgPDA5 at 12 h postinoculation (hpi) showed 60‐ and 30‐fold changes, respectively, compared with the germinating conidia (Figure S4). However, FgPDA4 and FgPDA7 had higher expression levels at vegetative hyphal stages, and the expression level of FgPDA6 showed approximately a 5‐fold change at 120 hpi compared with the control conidia (Figure S4).

To further examine the roles of FgPDAs in the virulence of F. graminearum, conidial suspensions of the six FgPDA‐knockout mutants were inoculated in the wheat head. Only ΔFgpda2 and ΔFgpda5 significantly reduced pathogenicity in wheat spikes (Figures 1a and S5a). In repeated infection assays, the disease index of the ΔFgpda2 and ΔFgpda5 deletion mutants was reduced by 70% and 80%, respectively, compared with that of the WT (Figures 1b and S5b). To evaluate the development of F. graminearum, the fungal biomass was also determined. Consistent with the disease index results, less fungal biomass was obtained in wheat heads inoculated with ΔFgpda2 and ΔFgpda5 (Figures 1c and S5c). In the inoculated wheat spikelet, the transcription levels of FgTRI5, the marker gene of DON production, was similar between the WT and ΔFgpda mutants, indicating that differences in virulence were not caused by differences in DON production in the inoculated spikelet (Figure 1d). Thus, the ΔFgpda2 and ΔFgpda5 mutants are vital for the full virulence of F. graminearum during plant infection.

Because the ΔFgpda5 mutant reduced pathogenicity most significantly and it was also more sensitive to various stresses, we selected it for further investigation. During infection of wheat coleoptiles, the ΔFgpda5 mutant caused smaller lesions compared with those inoculated with the WT (Figure S6a). In repeated experiments, phenotypic statistics showed smaller lesions in wheat inoculated with the ΔFgpda5 mutant than in coleoptiles inoculated with the WT and its complementary strain (Figure S6b). To further verify the synergism among the PDAs in pathogenesis, we knocked out FgPDA5 in the ΔFgpda2, ΔFgpda3, ΔFgpda4, ΔFgpda6 and ΔFgpda7 mutants and generated several double mutants. The virulence of all double mutants was significantly reduced compared with that of the single mutants (Figure S7a,b). In addition, we also observed the hyphal growth of these double mutants and no differences in the hyphal growth and colony morphology were observed on CM (Figure S7c). To exclude the expression of another PDA family member to compensate for ΔFgpda mutation, we measured the expression of FgPDA1, FgPDA2, FgPDA4, FgPDA6 and FgPDA7 in the WT, ΔFgpda3 and ΔFgpda5 mutants. The transcript levels of FgPDA1, FgPDA2, FgPDA4, FgPDA6 and FgPDA7 were not significantly different in ΔFgpda3 compared with those in ΔFgpda5 mutants and the WT strain (Figure S8). These results demonstrate that FgPDA5 contributes to F. graminearum virulence during wheat head blight infection.

Enzymatic activity of the seven putative PDA proteins

As chitin is an essential component of fungal cell walls and chitin deacetylases can convert chitin to chitosan, we next examined the chitin deacetylase activity of the FgPDAs. Purified FgPDAs:GFP (green fluorescent protein) were incubated with 1 mM A6 (chitohexaose with six GlcNAc moieties) for 30 min at 37°C in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), followed by determination of products using matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (MALDI‐TOF‐MS). FgPDA1 partially converted A6 to A4D2 and A2D4 as the major reaction products (where A is N‐acetyl‐d‐glucosamine, Dd is d‐glucosamine and the number is the amount of N‐acetyl‐d‐glucosamine or d‐glucosamine), while FgPDA2 mainly produced monodeacetylated hexamers (Figure S9). FgPDA3 and FgPDA4 mainly produced A5D1 and A4D2 after incubation for 30 min (Figure S9). FgPDA5 converted different intermediate products, including A4D2, A1D5 and A2D4 (Figure 2a). To detect the effect of the PDA‐typical metal‐binding amino acids on enzymatic activity, constructs of FgPDA5 with these amino acids changed to glycine (G) were generated and named FgPDA5m (Figure S10a). Low amounts of deacetylated intermediate products, including A5D1 and A4D2, were found after 30 min of incubation with FgPDA5m and A6, indicating the deacetylase activity of FgPDA5m was significantly reduced compared with that of the WT FgPDA5 (Figure 2a). Notably, FgPDA6 and FgPDA7 converted approximately one‐sixth of the substrate into the monodeacetylated product and one‐fifth into the double deacetylated product, respectively (Figure S9).

FgPDA5 is capable of binding chitin and deacetylating chitin oligomers

Because the FgPDA5 deletion mutant was most significantly reduced in virulence and FgPDA5 had enzymatic activity on A6, we selected it for further characterization and explored the effects of FgPDA5 on chitin. Purified FgPDA5:His was incubated with several polysaccharides to test its affinity toward chitin. The protein FgPDA5:His showed high affinity toward chitin, cellulose and chitosan (Figure 2b). The negative control GFP could not bind to chitin, cellulose or chitosan (Figure 2b). We used the vegetative hyphae of the ΔFgpda5 mutant to precipitate the FgPDA5 protein and to further confirm its affinity to the fungal cell wall. As expected, the FgPDA5 protein was precipitated from total proteins, but the control could not precipitate the protein (Figure 2b). We examined the thermodynamics of FgPDA5–chitin oligomer binding using isothermal titration calorimetry. As shown in Figure 2c, when chitohexaose with six GlcNAc moieties (A6) was injected into cells containing GFP, the heat of integration released per titration did not vary significantly, indicating that there was no specific binding. When chitin oligomers were injected into the reaction pool containing FgPDA5, considerable heat changes were observed (Figure 2c). The binding constant (K d) values of A6 were 19.8 ± 2.63 and 106 ± 35 μM for FgPDA5 and FgPDA5m protein, respectively, indicating that both FgPDA5 and FgPDA5m had high affinity to chitin oligomers (Figure 2d). We also tested whether FgPDA5 could protect F. graminearum hyphae from hydrolysis by chitinase. The crude vegetative hyphae of the ΔFgpda5 and WT strain were collected and incubated with FgPDA5 at 25°C for 2 h and then treated with chitinases for 24 h. Neither FgPDA5 nor GFP control could protect the fungal hyphae against chitinases (Figure S10b,c).

The SP of FgPDA5 is required for its secretion and apoplast localization

To test the function of the typical 24‐amino acid SP of FgPDA5 in yeast, FgPDA5SP (the signal peptide of FgPDA5) and Avr1bSP (the signal peptide of Phytophthora effector Avr1b) were introduced into pSuc2t7M13ori and transformed into YTK12 yeast strain lacking a secreted invertase (Hassing et al., 2019; Jacobs et al., 1997; Oh et al., 2009). Transformants were streaked on the complete minimal medium lacking tryptophan (CMD–W) and YPRAA plates, indicating that the empty vector pSUC2T7M13ori and its recombinant vector were introduced into the yeast strain. Similar to the positive control Avr1bSP, FgPDA5SP grew on the YPRAA plates, which support the growth of yeast secreting invertase (Figure 3a). The enzyme activity of secreted invertase from the transformant containing Avr1bSP and FgPDA5SP was also detected using a 2,3,5‐triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) assay (Figure 3b). On the contrary, the negative control failed to produce the insoluble red‐coloured 1,3,5‐triphenyl formazan (TPF).

To predict the localization of PDAs in the plant, we analysed the FgPDAs with ApoplastP software; all except for FgPDA6 were predicted to be apoplastic proteins (Sperschneider et al., 2018). The full‐length FgPDA5 and FgPDA5ΔSP (without signal peptide) were fused with GFP and transiently expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves to detect the presence of FgPDA5 in the apoplast after NaCl‐induced plasmolysis and to further explore the localization of FgPDA5 in plant cells. In the resulting cells expressing FgPDA5:GFP, GFP signals were observed in the apoplast after plasmolysis (Figure 3c). In cells expressing GFP alone and FgPDA5ΔSP:GFP as the control, GFP signals were observed in the cytoplasm and nucleus (Figure 3c). Other FgPDAs were predicted as apoplast‐targeting proteins that are secreted into the plant apoplast when transiently expressed in Ni. benthamiana (Figure S11). However, FgPDA6 harboured a putative transmembrane region and was localized into the plasma membrane (Figure S11).

To validate the apoplastic location of FgPDA5 targeted by its native SP, a full‐length FgPDA5‐GFP construct driven by the CaMV 35S promoter was generated and transformed into F. graminearum. Apoplastic fluid of wheat coleoptiles treated with F. graminearum expressing FgPDA5‐GFP was extracted and examined by western blotting. Only FgPDA5‐GFP could be successfully detected in apoplastic fluid, whereas GFP alone could not be detected (Figure 3d), indicating the apoplastic location of full‐length FgPDA5 during the wheat–F. graminearum interaction.

FgPDA5 forms a homodimer in vivo

Due to the homodimer formation of another chitin deacetylase in P. striiformis (Xu et al., 2020), we hypothesized that FgPDA5 might function as a homodimer in the apoplast. We cotransformed pBD‐FgPDA5 and pAD‐FgPDA5 using yeast two‐hybrid systems to test the hypothesis. Similar to the positive control, the transformed strains with pBD‐FgPDA5 and pAD‐FgPDA5 grew well on SD (−Leu/−Trp) and SD (−Leu/−Trp/−His/−Ade/X‐α‐gal) media, but the negative control strains with pBD‐FgPDA5 and pAD did not grow (Figure 4a). We looked for homodimer formation of FgPDA5 using co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assays in plants. The FgPDA5:HA tag and FgPDA5:GFP constructs were transiently co‐expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana. FgPDA5:HA was pulled down by the FgPDA5:GFP protein using western blotting with anti‐HA antibody when the total protein was exposed on the GFP‐trap beads (Figure 4b). However, the FgPDA5:HA protein was not pulled from the total proteins by the GFP‐trap beads with GFP control. The interaction was further confirmed via bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) in N. benthamiana (Figure 4c). The coding sequence of FgPDA5 was cloned into pSPYNE(R)173 and pSPYCE(M), and then co‐expressed in N. benthamiana via an Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation system (Waadt et al., 2008). The fluorescence signals of interaction were observed throughout the cell compared with the negative control (Figure 4c). Thus, the results show that FgPDA5 forms a homodimer in vivo.

Deacetylation of chitin oligomers fails to activate plant defence

Chitin oligomers, such as A6, act as elicitors that activate the plant immune system with well‐known receptors for chitin recognition (Cord‐Landwehr et al., 2016). To evaluate the effect of the substrate and product of FgPDA5 on plant defence, the product deacetylation of A6 (D6) was obtained after an overnight treatment of A6 with FgPDA5 protein. Plant leaves were treated with A6 and the product of FgPDA5 to test its ability to induce plant defence. We found that, similar to chitin treatment, which is known to induce plant defence, A6 induces a reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst (Figure 5a). Deacetylation of A6 by FgPDA5 failed to induce ROS accumulation (Figure 5a), indicating the importance of the N‐acetyl moieties in A6 for the priming of plant defence. Further evidence about the role of FgPDA5 in the activation of plant defence was acquired by measuring the expression levels of defence‐related genes and the phosphorylation of mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK). MAPK phosphorylation levels in leaves treated with A6 were found to be similar to those in leaves treated with chitin (Figure 5b), whereas A6 treated with FgPDA5 failed to induce the phosphorylation of MAPK. Consistent with these results, the defence‐related genes NbPR1 and NbPR2 showed lower expression levels after deacetylated A6 infiltration in repeated experiments than in leaves treated with chitin and A6 (Figure 5c). These results indicate that the deacetylation of A6 by FgPDA5 fails to activate plant defence.

The polysaccharide deacetylase function of FgPDA5 is necessary for F. graminearum pathogenicity

To further test whether PDA5 and PDA5m could complement the ΔFgpda5 mutation, these constructs were generated and then transformed into ΔFgpda5 mutant strains for complementation. We randomly selected two independent transformants of ΔFgpda5/FgPDA5 and ΔFgpda5/FgPDA5m, and detected the copies number inserted in genome. The complementary strains exhibited the presence of two or three copies of FgPDA5 or FgPDA5m (Figure S2h). When inoculated on the wheat head, the resulting ΔFgpda5/FgPDA5 transformants restored the normal virulence. However, the ΔFgpda5/FgPDA5m complementation strains were similar to the ΔFgpda5 mutant in terms of virulence (Figure 6a). In repeated infection assays, the disease index of the ΔFgpda5/FgPDA5m strain was reduced by 80% compared with that of the ΔFgpda5/FgPDA5 strain (Figure 6b). In repeated experiments, the ΔFgpda5/FgPDA5m transformants showed a longer lesion on wheat coleoptiles compared with those inoculated with the ΔFgpda5/FgPDA5 strain (Figure 6c,d), indicating that the ΔFgpda5/FgPDA5m strain failed to restore fungal virulence in wheat. However, the ΔFgpda5/FgPDA5 strain showed restored fungal virulence. These results indicate that the polysaccharide deacetylase function is required for F. graminearum pathogenicity.

FgPDA5 suppresses A6‐induced plant immunity

To test whether FgPDA5 could suppress chitin‐induced plant immunity, FgPDA5‐GFP, FgPDA5m‐GFP and GFP alone were first transiently expressed in N. benthamiana; 10 μM A6, acting as an elicitor to activate plant immunity, was used to treat leaves at 48 h after inoculation with Agrobacterium. Aniline blue staining showed fewer callose deposits in N. benthamiana leaves expressing FgPDA5‐GFP than in leaves expressing GFP alone and FgPDA5m:GFP at 24 h after infiltration with A6, indicating that FgPDA5:GFP could suppress A6‐induced callose accumulation. However, FgPDA5m:GFP and GFP alone could not suppress A6‐induced callose accumulation (Figure 7a). In repeated experiments, FgPDA5:GFP significantly suppressed numbers of callose foci, but FgPDA5m:GFP could not suppress it (Figure 7b). The marker genes of plant immunity in leaves treated with chitin were also assessed. The transcript levels of PR1, PR2 were threefold lower, and WRKY12 fourfold lower, in leaves expressing FgPDA5:GFP than in the control plants expressing GFP, respectively, but in leaves expressing FgPDA5m:GFP the expression of defence genes was not significantly different compared with the control (Figure 7c). These results show that FgPDA5 suppresses chitin‐induced plant immunity.

DISCUSSION

As the first major battleground between plants and pathogens, fungus–host recognition is an important part of a plant's innate immunity in the apoplastic space. Chitin and flg22 have been reported to be recognized by the plant's receptors in the apoplast region for priming of the plant's immune system, like chitin elicitor receptor kinase (CERK1) and Flagellin‐sensitive 2 (Chinchilla et al., 2007; Miya et al., 2007). Chitin deacetylases are a conserved family of enzymes in fungi, insects and bacteria, and remove acetyl moieties from chitin and convert chitin to chitosan (Baker et al., 2011; Gorzelanny et al., 2010; Hembach et al., 2020). In the present study, we analysed the functions of seven FgPDAs from F. graminearum. Except for FgPDA6, the FgPDAs were predicted to be apoplast‐targeting proteins that are secreted into the plant apoplast when transiently expressed in N. benthamiana (Figure S8). However, FgPDA6 harboured a putative transmembrane region (of about 25 amino acids) at the N‐terminus and was localized into the plasma membrane in N. benthamiana, indicating that FgPDA6 may still exhibit enzyme activity due to the position of the the conserved PDA domain in the apoplast according to the transmembrane predictor. None of the FgPDAs had a loop region between motifs III and IV, unlike Ustilago maydis UmCDA7 that displayed an insert of 61 amino acids, which was also detected in Cda1 and Cda8 of the silk worm Bombyx mori (Rizzi et al., 2021). All PDA proteins contained the five motifs characteristic for the catalytic site of CDAs and the conserved active site residues, which are typically present in most fungal CDAs.

FgPDA5 is the most conserved member of Fusarium PDAs, and its homologous protein, VdPDA1 from Verticillium dahliae, also showed chitin oligosaccharide deacetylation as the major virulence strategy in cotton plants (Gao et al., 2019). The ΔFgpda1 mutants could not be obtained after numerous attempts. FgPDA1 is conserved in ascomycete fungi (Fusarium and Colletotrichum), with high amino acid sequence similarities of 69%–74% (Figure S1). Similar observations were reported in Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici; a previous study failed to obtain the deletion mutants of the fungal acetyltransferase FolArd1 (Li et al., 2022). Of the single mutants, only strains lacking FgPDA2 or FgPDA5 exhibited reduced Fusarium pathogenicity. Based on the different levels of their enzyme activities and gene expression, we speculated that these FgPDAs might exhibit functional differentiation. For example, FgPDA2 and FgPDA5 were highly induced in the early Fusarium infection stages, while FgPDA4 and FgPDA7 showed higher expression levels at vegetative hyphal stages. Chitosan not only assists the fungal pathogen in evading the plant immune system but also modulates fungal resistance under stress conditions (Geoghegan & Gurr, 2016). In U. maydis, only the strain lacking CDA7 exhibited reduced virulence, but all six CDAs showed enzyme activity (Rizzi et al., 2021). However, UmCDA2 and UmCDA4 proteins are responsible for the accumulation of chitosan in budding cells. The chitin and chitosan composition of the fungal cell wall changes during fungal growth and infection stages (Rizzi et al., 2021). The conversion of chitin to chitosan was observed in Epichloë festucae when hyphae switched from free‐living to endophytic growth during infection of Lolium perenne. However, chitin becomes predominant in the fungal cell wall as fungal hyphae transition from endophytic to epiphytic growth (Noorifar et al., 2021). After infection of plants with rust fungi, the chitin polymers of cell walls become de‐N‐acetylated to chitosan (El Gueddari et al., 2002).

Increasing evidence suggests that chitin is also recognized as a PAMP that can activate the plant defence response, unless it is masked, modified or sequestered to prevent interaction with plant chitin receptors (Boller & Felix, 2009; Boutrot & Zipfel, 2017; Cao et al., 2014; Zipfel & Oldroyd, 2017). Successful pathogens overcome host immunity by avoiding immune signalling transduction from the apoplast to cytoplasm, such as by Ecp6 of Cladosporium fulvum and MoSlp1 of M. oryzae (de Jonge et al., 2010; Mentlak et al., 2012; Sánchez‐Vallet et al., 2013). These fungal effectors, containing the LysM domain, can mask and modify chitin oligomers to perturb the host perception. The secreted LysM protein 1 (Slp1) binds the chitin oligosaccharides, competing against the host chitin elicitor‐binding protein (CEBiP), to avoid activating plant immunity (Mentlak et al., 2012). In this study, FgPDA5 also showed a high affinity for chitin binding, including cellulose and hyphal binding. However, FgPDA5 was incapable of preventing chitin degradation by plant chitinases in vivo. A previous study showed that fungal effectors, such as Cladosporium fulvum Avr4, could protect fungi against plant chitinases by binding the chitin component in the cell wall (Ha et al., 2006). Importantly, FgPDA proteins, as polysaccharide deacetylases, showed different levels of enzyme activity on chitin substrates. Moreover, FgPDA5 could not deacetylate insoluble colloid chitin when it was incubated with the fungal hyphae. Different FgPDA proteins showed varied levels of enzyme activity due to substrate diversity. However, FgPDA6 and FgPDA7 showed low enzymatic activities due to the replacement of conserved active site residues (aspartic acid) within the motif IV of them with glycine and tyrosine. Interestingly, the deacetylase activity of FgPDA5m was significantly reduced when the PDA‐typical metal‐binding amino acids were replaced in PDA motifs I and II, which is consistent with the enzyme activity of VdPDA1m (Gao et al., 2019). In U. maydis, UmCDA7 was inactive on the tetramer but exhibited increased activity on the hexamer (Rizzi et al., 2021). Some pathogens facilitate virulence through deacetylation of chitin oligomers whose acetyl group contributes to plant receptor perception (Bozsoki et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2012). Previous reports showed that A8 (chitooctaose with eight acetyl moieties), A7, A6 and A5 could be removed acetyl moieties, and the ROS response elicited by A5 was much lower than that elicited by A8 and A6 (Gao et al., 2019). FgPDA5 could deacetylate acetyl moieties from A6 to A2D4 or A1D5, which failed to activate plant immunity, indicating that this could be a potential new strategy for preventing chitin‐triggered host immunity by fungal pathogens. A previous study showed that the presence of chitosan oligomers with at least five acetyl groups also results in an association between LYK5 and CERK1 and inducing plant immunity (Gao et al., 2019). Therefore, the elicitor activity of the monodeacetylated product (A5D1) by FgPDA2 was not evaluated in this study. We speculate that FgPDA5 acts as a polysaccharide deacetylase to deacetylate chitin oligosaccharides to chitosan and prevent chitin‐triggered plant immunity.

FHB, which is predominantly caused by F. graminearum, is one of the most devastating diseases worldwide. However, how to develop new approaches to control FHB and improve wheat grain yield, quality and safety has been an extensive and pressing question. We assessed the function of FgPDAs in virulence as well as activities on chitin substrates. Thus, this study not only provides FgPDA proteins as promising potential new candidates for fungicide targets, but also contributes to an improved understanding of crop disease management, thereby aiding the development of sustainable disease‐resistant breeding materials.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Biological materials and culture conditions

Wheat cultivars SM482 and N. benthamiana were grown in the growth chamber at 22–25°C under a 16 h light/8 h dark cycle. F. graminearum PH‐1 was grown on potato dextrose agar (PDA) at 25°C. Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 or AGL1 and Escherichia coli DH5a were cultured on Luria–Bertani medium at 28°C and 37°C, respectively. The yeast strain YTK12, AH109 was cultured on yeast nitrogen base (YNB) medium at 30°C.

Sequence analysis and transcript level detection

An InterPro search for proteins with a NodB homology domain (IPR002509) initially identified FgPDAs in F. graminearum by basic local alignment search tools (BLAST). The open reading frames of FgPDAs were amplified via PCR using specific primers. The SP was predicted using SignalP v. 4.0 for FgPDA sequence analysis. The sublocalization was predicted by ApoplastP (Sperschneider et al., 2018). Analysis of protein domains was based on the SMART website (https://smart.embl.de/smart/). Protein sequences of FgPDAs were obtained from the Ensembl Fungi database (http://fungi.ensembl.org/index.html) and these sequence alignments were established by the MULTALIN website with default settings (http://multalin.toulouse.inra.fr/multalin/multalin.html). In a phylogenetic tree of fungal PDAs, PDA homologues were identified by BLAST from the NCBI protein database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Polygenetic relationships were inferred with the neighbour‐joining method using the software MEGA 5.0 with default settings, and the sequences of Valsa mali were used as an outgroup. For the expression profile of FgPDA genes, the samples of wheat heads infected with F. graminearum at 12, 24, 48, 72, 96 and 120 h postinoculation (hpi), F. graminearum conidia from 5‐day‐old carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) cultures and vegetative hyphae from 4‐day‐old PDA cultures were harvested for RNA extraction using a MiniBEST RNA Extraction Kit (TaKaRa) following manufacturer's instructions. Genomic DNA was extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method for fungal biomass (Healey et al., 2014). TaEF‐1α for wheat, FgCHS5 for F. graminearum and NbActin for N. benthamiana were used as the reference genes for RT‐qPCR using CFX Manager v. 3.1 (Bio‐Rad) under the corresponding conditions.

Protein expression in N. benthamiana

For localization in N. benthamiana, Agrobacterium cells carrying FgPDA5:GFP, FgPDA5ΔSP:GFP and GFP alone were treated with AS buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM 2‐(N‐morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid (MES), 10 μM acetosyringone, pH 5.6) and strains carrying recombinant vectors were diluted to optical density (OD600 nm) of 0.5 and were transiently expressed in leaves from 3–4‐week‐old N. benthamiana plants using a 1‐mL syringe. The epidermis of leaves was treated with 70 mM NaCl for 20 min after 48 hpi and observed using confocal microscopy. For chitin oligomer‐induced plant immunity, Agrobacterium containing FgPDA5:GFP, FgPDA5m:GFP and GFP alone was infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves at an OD600 of 0.6. Then A6 was infiltrated at 48 hpi with Agrobacterium. Samples were harvested at 12 hpi with A6 to detect callose deposits and the expression of defence genes. All the primers used in this study are listed in Table S2.

Transient expression of proteins in yeast

The SP of FgPDA5 was cloned into the pSUC2T7M13ori vector, which carried the sucrose invertase gene (SUC2) without the start codon ATG and the SP sequence. It was then transformed into yeast YTK12 (Hassing et al., 2019; Jacobs et al., 1997; Oh et al., 2009). Transformant strains were screened on CMD–W plates (0.67% YNB, 0.1% glucose, 2% sucrose, 0.075% tryptophan dropout supplement and 2% agar) and selective YPRAA plates (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 2% raffinose, 2 μg/mL antimycin A, 2% agar). YTK12 strains with empty vector pSUC2 or pSUC2‐Avr1bSP were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. The enzymatic activity was tested by reducing TTC to red TPF. For the yeast two‐hybrid assay, the coding sequence of FgPDA5 was inserted into the bait vector pGBKT7 and the prey vector pGADT7, and the recombinant vectors were co‐transferred into yeast strain AH109. Transformant strains were screened on the corresponding medium (SD/−Leu/−Trp/−His and SD/−Leu/−Trp/−His/−Ade).

Detection of deacetylase enzyme activity

The enzyme activities were evaluated via the qualitative analysis of deacetylated products using MALDI‐TOF‐MS on an AB Sciex 5800 MALDI‐TOF mass spectrometer as described previously (Gao et al., 2019). In brief, purified FgPDA proteins (50 μM) were incubated with 1 mM A6 (cat. no. SCN06; BZ OLIGO BIOTECH) for 30 min at 37°C in 50 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 8.0) and then heated at 100°C for 10 min. Enzymolysis products (1 μL) were mixed with 1 μL of 2,5‐dihydroxybenzoic acid solution (20 mg/mL in 1:1 acetonitrile/water containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid). The mixture was then spotted on the target plate with 1 μL of 5‐chloro‐2‐mercaptobenothiazole (10 mg/mL) and dried, followed by MALDI‐TOF MS analysis. Mass spectra were obtained in the positive reflection mode.

ROS measurement and MAPK assay

To detect ROS, 4‐week‐old N. benthamiana leaves of 0.5 cm2 were collected into a 96‐well plate with 200 μL of double‐deionized water and maintained overnight to eliminate physical damage by ROS. Leaf fragments were then treated with 200 μL of reaction solution containing 25 μg/mL horseradish peroxidase (HRP), 37.5 μg/mL luminol and 1 μM of chitin oligomers (A6) or 5 μg/mL chitin (cat. no. C8181; Solarbio). Luminescence was detected using a multiscan spectrum (Varioskan LUX) at 562 nm for 60 min (Song et al., 2021). Each data point was from six replicates, and these experiments were repeated three times. To detect the phosphorylation of MAPK, total proteins were extracted from 2 g of leaves with chitin oligomer treatment using a plant phosphorylated protein extraction kit (cat. no. HR0011; Baiaolaibo). The phosphorylated MPK3 and MPK6 proteins in leaves were detected by western blot with α‐pERK1/2 antibody (cat. no. AF1891; Beyotime). Protein loading was detected by Coomassie brilliant blue R‐250 (Beyotime).

Protein expression, purification and analysis

The FgPDA5ΔSP sequence was inserted into the pET‐28a vector and transformed into Escherichia coli BL21. The crude proteins of FgPDA5ΔSP tagged with His tag were purified by Ni‐chelating affinity chromatography (cat. no. 17531802; General Electric Company). The concentration of Fg02685 protein was tested using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein concentration assay kit (P0012; Beyotime). For detection of the affinity to chitin, about 0.1 μg of recombinant protein FgPDA5 and FgPDA5m was incubated with chitin beads, cellulose (cat. no. 310697; Sigma‐Aldrich), chitosan (cat. no. 417963; Sigma‐Aldrich) and hyphae from the WT strain at 4°C for 4 h with 5 mL of incubation buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl). The supernatant (200 μL) was preserved for western blot detection. After 4 h of incubation, the compounds were then precipitated by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 5 min and washed three times with 500 μL of incubation buffer. The final precipitation with 200 μL of incubation buffer was detected using a western blot with an anti‐His antibody (cat. no. AF2873; Beyotime).

Fungal cell wall protection assay

To test whether FgPDA5 could protect F. graminearum hyphae from hydrolysis by chitinase, chitinase protection assay was performed as described previously (Sánchez‐Vallet et al., 2020). Approximately 104 conidiospores of ∆Fgpda5 mutants and WT strain were incubated overnight at room temperature in 100 μL of CMC medium in a 96‐well microtitre plate. Subsequently, FgPDA5 protein was added at a final concentration of 50 μM. After 2 h of incubation, 50 μL of chitinase solution (50 ng/μL) was added and the microtitre plate was incubated at room temperature for 24 h. Fungal growth was visualized under a bright field by a BX‐51 fluorescence microscope (Olympus).

BiFC assay

The coding sequence of FgPDA5 (without signal peptide) was ligated with the yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) gene in the vectors pSATN‐nEYFP and pSATN‐cEYFP (Table S3). The fusion constructs were transferred into A. tumefaciens GV3101, independently. The Agrobacterium strains were infiltrated into 4‐week‐old N. benthamiana leaves at an OD600 of 0.5. Two days after inoculation, pictures were captured by laser confocal microscopy with a 488 nm laser (Stellaris Sted & Stellaris; Lycra).

Co‐IP assay

A. tumefaciens GV3101 strains carrying the FgPDA5:GFP (pCAMBIA2300) and FgPDA5:HA (pICH86988) constructs were co‐infiltrated into 4‐week‐old N. benthamiana leaves. The inoculated leaves were harvested at 48 hpi and total proteins were extracted at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred into a fresh tube and incubated with GFP‐trap beads at 4°C for 2 h. The bound beads were then washed three times with 500 μL of extraction buffer according to the manufacturer's instructions. Proteins bound to the GFP‐trap beads were detected using western blots with the corresponding mouse anti‐GFP antibody (cat. no. AF0159; Beyotime Biotechnology) and anti‐HA antibody (cat. no. AF2858; Beyotime Biotechnology).

Isothermal titration calorimetry assay

The binding affinities of FgPDA5 with chitin oligomer were obtained using an ITC200 microcalorimeter (Microcal LLC) at 25°C (Guo et al., 2018). All samples used in the isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) assay were dialysed in the reaction buffer (200 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.0). Protein concentration was determined using absorbance spectroscopy at 280 nm. Chitin oligomer (800 μM) was injected into the stirred calorimeter cells containing FgPDA5 or FgPDA5m (1000 mM). All ITC data were analysed using Origin software (v. 7.0, OriginLab).

Transformation of F. graminearum and pathogenicity assays

The downstream and upstream DNA sequences of FgPDAs were constructed into Prf‐HU2 vectors for F. graminearum transformation. A. tumefaciens carrying Prf‐HU2:FgPDAs were co‐cultured with F. graminearum, and the positive strains were selected as described previously (Maier et al., 2005). For complementation assays, the entire FgPDA5 gene with its native promoter was inserted into the pFL2 vector and then transformed into A. tumefaciens and co‐cultured with ∆Fgpda5 mutants (Zhou et al., 2011). The ∆Fgpda transformants were detected using PCR for phenotype complementation (Table S2). For apoplastic protein extraction, the apoplastic fluid from wheat coleoptiles inoculated with the strain expressing FgPDA5‐GFP was extracted by the infiltration–centrifugation method as described (O'Leary et al., 2014). For pathogenicity assays of F. graminearum (Jonkers et al., 2012), conidia were harvested from 5‐day‐old CMC cultures and resuspended to 105 spores/mL in sterile distilled water. Ten microlitres of conidial suspension was inoculated on the fifth wheat spikelet, and inoculated wheat heads were sealed with plastic wrap for 48 h to retain moisture (Ding et al., 2009). The phenotype of wheat head blight was observed at 14 dpi for the disease index measurement. For the detection of fungal biomass, total genomic DNA was extracted from infected wheat heads at 5 dpi and tested by qPCR assays. TaEF‐1α and FgCHS5 genes were used to normalize the DNA level of wheat and F. graminearum, respectively (Li et al., 2018). For wheat coleoptiles inoculated with F. graminearum (Zhang et al., 2012), seedlings of SM482 were cultivated at 22°C under a 16 h light:8 h dark cycle. About 10 μL of spore suspension (106/mL) were inoculated on wheat coleoptiles. The disease lesion was photographed 7 days after treatment with F. graminearum.
