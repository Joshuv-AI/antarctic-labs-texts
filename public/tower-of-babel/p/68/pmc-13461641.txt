Litigation trends within rheumatology: a retrospective analysis of claims data from England for the Getting It Right First Time programme
Authors: Robert D Sandler, William K Gray, Nick Dunkley, John T Machin, Luke Martin, Flora McErlane, Lesley J Kay, Tim W R Briggs, Peter C Lanyon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo examine rheumatology-related litigation claims over a 5-year period to describe trends and identify learning opportunities.MethodsData were extracted from NHS Resolution for all claims within rheumatology or claims referencing ‘giant cell arteritis (GCA)’ or ‘vasculitis’ within any specialty over 5 years (2018/19–2022/23). Variables for each claim included cause, injury, costs and claim status.ResultsWe identified 152 rheumatology claims (total cost £28.2 million). The most frequent injuries were unnecessary pain [n = 70 (46%)], fatality [n = 14 (9%)] and psychiatric/psychological damage [n = 13 (9%)]. Where specified, 37 claims (24%) involved inflammatory arthritis and 22 (14%) related to rare autoimmune rheumatic diseases (RAIRDs), such as vasculitis. Only 23 of the 152 claims were closed by the end of the study period, with just 4 being settled in favour of the claimant. Of these four, three involved soft tissue steroid injections. All 14 fatality cases with specified underlying conditions were RAIRDs. Diagnostic issues accounted for 38% of claims and treatment issues for 30%. Six claims exceeded £1 million. Forty-four GCA-related claims (total cost £13.1 million) were identified, mostly in ophthalmology or emergency medicine rather than rheumatology, representing a doubling compared with the previous 5-year period. Sixteen vasculitis-related claims (£3.4 million) were identified.ConclusionsHigh-cost, high-harm claims persist in rheumatology, with most remaining open for several years, creating personal, financial and professional strain. GCA-related claims are increasing and occur mostly outside rheumatology, highlighting the need for pan-speciality awareness agreed-upon care pathways. RAIRD-related claims seemed overrepresented in cases of fatality, highlighting the importance of timely suspicion, close monitoring and careful management.

Introduction

In England, the annual cost of clinical negligence claims against National Health Service (NHS) providers has more than doubled over the past decade, increasing from £1.561 billion in the financial year 2015/16 to £3.144 billion in 2024/25. In the latter year alone, >13 000 clinical negligence claims were settled [1]. The frequency and financial burden of litigation are comparatively high in the UK, partly reflecting the characteristics of the NHS as a universal healthcare system with no upper limit on compensation payments and a comprehensive reporting framework [1]. In the 2025 NHS 10-Year Health Plan, the UK government committed to reviewing clinical negligence processes and emphasised the importance of systematic learning from claims to improve patient safety and reduce costs [1, 2].

Within rheumatology, data presented in the 2021 Rheumatology Getting It Right First Time (GIRFT) program national report indicated that between 2013/14 and 2017/18, the annual cost of rheumatology litigation claims ranged from £4.3 million to £15.8 million [3]. Rheumatologists frequently manage patients with rare autoimmune rheumatic diseases (RAIRDs) that can affect multiple organ systems. Consequently, some litigation claims related to these conditions may be attributed to other specialties. The 2021 GIRFT report also identified additional litigation associated specifically with GCA, amounting to £6.6 million in costs over the same 5-year period. One of the report’s principal recommendations was to mitigate the burden of litigation through service improvement and enhanced clinical governance.

Building on this prior work, we aimed to examine trends in NHS litigation claims in rheumatology, and in GCA and vasculitis across all specialities, for the subsequent 5 years (2018/19–2022/23). Specifically, we sought to describe changes in the number, cost and nature of claims and to identify potential areas for improvement and recommendations to reduce future litigation risk arising from preventable harm.

Methods

Study design

This was an exploratory, retrospective analysis of routine administrative data.

Ethics

This study did not directly involve NHS patients as study subjects and so ethical approval and consent were not required.

Data extraction and inclusion criteria

Data were obtained from the NHS Resolution claims database. NHS Resolution is an arm’s-length body of the UK Department of Health and Social Care, responsible for managing litigation claims on behalf of NHS care providers in England through its Clinical Negligence Scheme for Trusts (CNST) [4]. The CNST is a membership-based indemnity scheme that manages and funds clinical negligence claims on behalf of NHS Trusts and other approved providers in England, covering incidents occurring after 1 April 1995.

Data were extracted through three separate searches covering the financial years 2018/19–2022/23. Each search extracted all notified claims, including open and closed claims, meeting one of the following criteria, which were reviewed as three separate analyses: the recorded specialty was ‘rheumatology’; the free-text claim description included the terms ‘giant cell arteritis’ or ‘GCA’, for any recorded specialty; and the free-text claim description included the term ‘vasculitis’, for any recorded specialty.

We focused on litigation relating to vasculitis, including specifically GCA, as these conditions are among the most severe rheumatic diseases, carrying high early mortality and risk of preventable harm. Because their management often involves multiple specialties beyond rheumatology, we examined NHS Resolution data across all specialties to capture the full scope of claims related to these rare conditions.

For each claim, the extracted variables included claim status, clinical incident leading to the claim (brief, free-text), NHS Resolution notification dates, cause of the claim, up to three alleged injuries related to the claim (e.g. unnecessary pain, fatality), up to three alleged causes for the claim (e.g. failure/delay diagnosis, inappropriate treatment) and costs. For closed claims, costs included defence, damages, claimant and total costs. For open claims, the estimated total claims cost was used, as recorded in NHS Resolution.

All claims were independently reviewed by two authors (R.D.S. and P.C.L.). Where sufficient detail was available, each claim was categorised according to the primary cause, e.g. diagnostic error, treatment error, communication failure, consent-related issue or other. In addition, where the type of rheumatological condition could be identified from the free-text data, this was recorded (e.g. inflammatory arthritis or RAIRD).

Statistical methods

All data were extracted onto and stored in secure NHS England server space. Data analysis was largely descriptive and performed using Excel (version 2508; Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) and RStudio (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All three analyses were descriptively compared with NHS Resolution data from 2013/14 to 2017/18, extracted using the same search terms and methodology as the Rheumatology GIRFT national report [3].

Results

Rheumatology specialty claims

Between 2018/19 and 2022/23, 152 litigation claims in which rheumatology was the recorded specialty were identified, spanning 75 NHS care providers. The most frequently reported injuries were unnecessary pain [n = 70 (46%)], fatality [n = 14 (9%)], psychiatric/psychological damage [n = 13 (9%)] and fracture [n = 10 (7%)]. Thirty-seven claims (24%) related to inflammatory arthritis, while 22 (14%) were associated with RAIRDs. The underlying rheumatological conditions for the remaining claims were osteoporosis [n = 13 (9%)], fractures excluding osteoporosis-related [n = 9 (6%)], spinal injuries (e.g. cauda equina syndrome) [n = 7 (5%)], non-specific infections [n = 5 (3%)], carpal tunnel syndrome [n = 2 (1%)] and single cases related to complex regional pain syndrome and hypermobile Ehlers–Danlos syndrome. For the remaining 55 (36%) claims the underlying condition was unspecified.

Among the 14 fatalities, all 4 cases in which the rheumatological diagnosis was specified involved RAIRD. Three fatalities were related to infection. Ten of the 152 claims concerned complications following soft-tissue steroid injection and 6 related to visual impairment attributed to hydroxychloroquine use.

GCA-related claims in all specialities

Between 2018/19 and 2022/23, 44 litigation claims were identified across 33 NHS providers in which ‘giant cell arteritis’ or ‘GCA’ appeared in the free-text claim details. The most common injury was blindness or visual problems [n = 40 (91%)] and most claims related to delayed or failed diagnosis [n = 36 (82%)].

The total cost of GCA-related claims was £13.1 million, with individual claim values ranging from £500 to £1.8 million. The most frequent recorded specialties were ophthalmology (n = 22) and emergency medicine (n = 8). Rheumatology, general medicine and geriatric medicine each recorded three claims, with single cases in neurology, respiratory, gynaecology, urology and dermatology.

Most claims were closed [29/44 (66%)], with 10/29 (34%) being settled in favour of the Trust (i.e. negligence was not demonstrated). The aggregated costs of claims based on their status is summarised in Table 4.

Vasculitis-related claims in all specialities

Between 2018/19 and 2022/23, 16 litigation claims were identified across 14 NHS providers in which ‘vasculitis’ appeared in the free-text claim details. Six claims (38%) involved a fatality and five (31%) involved renal damage or failure. Other reported injuries included amputation, cosmetic disfigurement, unnecessary pain and psychological harm.

Eleven claims (69%) were primarily related to diagnostic issues, four (25%) to treatment and one to a failure to act on an abnormal blood test result. Of note, no claims were related to complications of overtreatment or overdiagnosis. The total cost of vasculitis-related claims was £3.4 million, with individual claim values ranging from £1685 to £1.6 million. The single very-high-value claim (£1.6 million) was already captured within the rheumatology specialty dataset, as rheumatology was the recorded specialty.

Discussion

We examined NHS litigation data in which rheumatology was the recorded speciality as well as claims relating to GCA and vasculitis across all specialities. Compared with the preceding 5-year period (2013/14–2017/18), there was a modest reduction in both the number and total value of rheumatology-related claims during 2018/19–2022/23. In contrast, the number and total value of claims referencing GCA approximately doubled, while claims relating to vasculitis remained broadly similar across both periods, allowing for a single very-high-value open case. The majority of GCA- and vasculitis-related claims were recorded under specialties other than rheumatology, most commonly ophthalmology and emergency medicine.

The reduction in both the number and total value of litigation claims in which rheumatology was the recorded specialty was 152 vs 184 claims, with total costs of £28.2 million vs £46.1 million, respectively. Diagnostic issues have now overtaken treatment-related issues as the most frequent basis for claims. However, it should be noted that the 2013/14–2017/18 data were reported in less detail than the current dataset, and likely included ‘incident’ status claims that were excluded from this analysis. Only four claims during 2018/19–2022/23 related to consent, representing a notable decrease from 12 in the preceding period.

The reduction in claims is consistent with that reported in other specialities, such as orthopaedic and general surgery [1]. While it was beyond the scope of this study to explore the specific reasons for this, the GIRFT program introduced a national approach to learning from litigation claims in 2018, and it is encouraging that rheumatology appears to be following this reducing trend.

A notable difference compared with the 2013/14–2017/18 data was that the most common underlying cause of litigation in the present period related to diagnostic issues rather than treatment errors. This finding is plausible given the inherent diagnostic complexity of many rheumatological conditions. A recent single-centre Belgian study explored diagnostic errors over a 10-year period by a single rheumatologist, emphasising how misdiagnosis can be mitigated through more rigorous adherence to validated disease activity measures and diagnostic criteria [6]. Similarly, a Japanese study analysing litigation claims from a Tokyo-based insurance firm over a 10-year period reported that diagnostic errors accounted for half of all claims in their rheumatology cohort [7]. Prabhu et al. [8], in their review of rheumatology-related litigation over a 31-year period in the USA, also identified diagnostic and treatment failures as the leading causes of claims.

In our dataset, a minority of claims [23/152 (15%)] against rheumatology were notified and closed within the 5-year period. This is much lower than reported proportions of closed cases and settlement in favour of claimants across other specialities using similar NHS Resolution data and methodology, such as otorhinolaryngology, emergency medicine and ophthalmology, where the majority of litigation cases notified over a 5-year period were reported to be closed [9–11]. This finding can also be contextualised against data published by the NHS Litigation Authority, the precursor to NHS Resolution. Between 2000 and 2010, 190 rheumatology-related claims were recorded, 89% of which were closed, with total costs amounting to £7.7 million. Diagnostic error was again the most frequent cause, and one quarter of all claims were related to the spine [12]. In contrast, in the present dataset, only 8 of 152 cases involved spinal issues (excluding osteoporotic fractures) and only a minority of cases were closed.

The lower proportion of closure in our study period serves to indicate that litigation processes in rheumatology can be active for long periods of time and the financial and personal burden of open litigation may be felt for many years [13, 14]. It is unsurprising that 82% of closed claims were settled in favour of the Trust. This may reflect that, when documentation is clear, claims can often be defended quickly and at relatively low cost. In contrast, when there is a likelihood of admission that a breach of duty may have occurred and contributed to harm, the process of assessing damages becomes more complex and prolonged, leading to higher legal costs and greater damages. This is an important finding, as prolonged medical litigation can be distressing for patients and families, potentially exacerbating the trauma associated with the original incident. There can be many motivations behind pursuing litigation, such as financial compensation, a desire for an apology, or the hope to prevent recurrence [15].

The rates of closure and settlement for vasculitis and GCA-related claims, most of which occurred outside of rheumatology, were higher and more aligned with those reported in other specialities. In the case of visual loss resulting from undiagnosed or untreated GCA or fatality resulting from untreated vasculitis, the demonstration of harm may be more straightforward than other cases in rheumatology, leading to the higher closure rate within the study period.

The fact that three of the four claims that were settled in favour of the claimant arose from procedural complications, in these cases steroid injections, gives pause to rheumatologists to ensure that appropriate consent is given and the recommendation for injections is a shared decision with patients. While it was beyond the scope of this study, our findings may suggest that the demonstration of negligence is more straightforward when a claim relates to a procedural complication rather than the allegation of delays or failures in diagnosis and treatment decisions. It may be that claims of this nature are also easier to settle within the 5-year time period of the study.

The identification of several very-high-value claims (cost >£1 million) within our cohort is noteworthy. Such cases are known to account for more than two-thirds of total clinical negligence costs to the NHS [1]. Although high-value claims are typically associated with procedural injuries in acute settings, such as obstetrics and surgical specialities, it is important to recognise that these claims also occur in less procedure-based specialties, such as rheumatology.

This work has some limitations. First, litigation data represent a single way of learning from harm. It is likely that there are many other instances of harm or error that never get to the point of litigation, therefore we do not consider learning from litigation as the only way to learn from harm. Second, the data only include cases where NHS Resolution was notified about litigation during the 5-year period 2018/19–2022/23. Therefore, for cases that may take ≥5 years to close, depending on how far into the study period notification occurred, may introduce bias into the claim settlement rate. A free-text word search for GCA and vasculitis within claim details was considered the most comprehensive method to identify relevant claims. However, we note that it may not identify all claims, as diagnosis is not always provided in the claim details. Additionally, the data available for this analysis was exclusively from the NHS Resolution Clinical Negligence Scheme for Trusts, in keeping with the GIRFT remit. Therefore, claims raised against primary care providers were not identified. Finally, NHS Resolution was not primarily established for research purposes and claim details are entered by claims handlers rather than clinical staff and are often brief, as highlighted by other studies using these data [16]. Where claims have not been closed, costs are estimated. However, these data provide unique insights into the nature of litigation across rheumatology, GCA and vasculitis, shining a spotlight on an area of practice where harm occurs and clinician anxiety is prevalent.

Conclusions

There has been a modest reduction in both the number and overall cost of litigation claims in rheumatology. However, our key finding relates to the length of time claims in rheumatology remain open compared with procedural-based specialities. It is important to learn from litigation to reduce the harm that may precede such claims and future work will look to identify and share learning from providers who have experienced litigation. Fatalities related to RAIRDs highlight the need for greater clinician awareness, effective peer support networks and timely diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making. The increasing proportion of claims linked to diagnostic issues indicates the scope for improvement in early recognition and clinical assessment.

In contrast, litigation relating to GCA has doubled in both frequency and cost. As litigation data likely underestimate the true burden of harm, strengthening early diagnostic processes, ensuring rapid initiation of treatment and fostering cross-specialty collaboration are essential. Enhancing awareness among all healthcare professionals who may encounter people with symptoms of GCA, supported by clear and accessible care pathways, could meaningfully reduce patient harm and the overall burden of litigation.
