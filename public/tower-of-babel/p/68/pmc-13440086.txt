Caffeine expectancy, explicit and implicit memory performance among female nursing students: a placebo-controlled quasi-experimental study
Authors: Eman A Shokr, Hasan Abualruz, Mohammad Faisal Al Ali, Loai M Zabin, Rania Maher Alhalawany, Shereen Roushdy Hashem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFor nursing students, memory performance is important for medication learning, clinical reasoning, patient safety, and skill acquisition. This study examined whether caffeine expectancy was associated with explicit and implicit memory performance during morning and afternoon testing sessions.MethodsA convenience sample of 74 female nursing students participated. Participants were classified into high- and low-expectancy groups based on CaffEQ scores and were tested either in the morning (8 AM) or afternoon (4 PM). Explicit and implicit memory were assessed using standardized memory tasks. Data were analyzed using a 2 × 2 factorial ANOVA.ResultsParticipants had a mean age of 21.05 ± 0.97 years. Explicit and implicit memory scores showed only minor descriptive differences across expectancy groups and testing sessions. Two-way factorial ANOVA revealed no significant main effects of caffeine expectancy or time of day on explicit or implicit memory performance, and no significant interaction between caffeine expectancy and time of day was found. Overall, memory performance did not differ significantly across study conditions, and all effects remained nonsignificant under the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of p < .025.ConclusionCaffeine expectancy was not significantly associated with explicit or implicit memory performance under the present study conditions. Given the limited sample size, absence of chronotype assessment, and relatively weak expectancy manipulation, these null findings should be interpreted cautiously.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-05045-5.Keywords: Caffeine, Memory, Recall, Placebo effect, Circadian rhythm, Students, Nursing

Introduction

For students at universities all around the world, taking caffeine has become a common practice associated with their learning activities. It has been estimated that over 80% of the world’s adult population uses caffeine on a regular basis, mostly via coffee and tea products [1]. Many university students consume caffeine to maintain alertness during studying and examinations [1, 2]. This issue is particularly relevant for nursing students, whose academic and clinical training relies heavily on memory, clinical reasoning, medication learning, and patient safety competencies.

Memory is a fundamental component of nursing education because students must retain and apply large volumes of theoretical and clinical information across diverse healthcare settings. Effective memory performance supports clinical reasoning, clinical judgment, medication administration accuracy, and patient safety, all of which are essential competencies in nursing practice. Previous nursing education research has highlighted that successful clinical decision-making depends on the integration of memory, knowledge retrieval, and reasoning processes, whereas difficulties in information recall may adversely affect academic achievement and increase the risk of clinical errors [3, 4].

Caffeine is a widely consumed stimulant that enhances alertness through adenosine receptor blockade [5]. Although its effects on alertness are well established, evidence regarding memory performance remains inconsistent and appears to depend on factors such as dosage, timing, task characteristics, and individual differences [6–9].

Timing is also crucial. The human body does not maintain a constant level of alertness throughout the day. Instead, cognitive efficiency varies with the circadian system, an internal biological clock that regulates sleep-wake cycles, hormone patterns, and neural arousal over 24 h [10, 11]. For many university students, cognitive performance may vary across the day because of circadian influences and individual chronotype differences. Memory and other cognitive functions may be less efficient during early morning hours than during later periods of the day [12]. Sherman et al. [13] found that caffeine produced notable memory gains in young adults during their time of day but had minimal effects during later testing sessions, which is a central observation for the present study.

It is the most common CNS stimulant used around the globe [14] via consumption of coffee, tea, energy drinks, and other items containing caffeine. Caffeine expectancy in this study represents the independent variable, which means individuals’ beliefs or expectations about the effects of caffeine consumption [15]. According to studies, individuals’ expectancies regarding the consumption of caffeine can impact their experience of the effects of caffeine consumption and the resulting performance outcomes, irrespective of the direct effects of caffeine [16].

Two types of memory performance serve as the dependent variables in this study. Explicit memory is the conscious, intentional recall of information such as events, names, and experiences that a person can verbalize, often measured using free recall tests. Implicit memory refers to memory that affects behavior without conscious awareness, where prior exposure influences current performance even without deliberate recollection [17].

Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in expectancy theory and circadian arousal theory. Expectancy theory proposes that what people believe about the anticipated effects of a substance can shape their experiences and performance even without the substance itself [18]. The CaffEQ has been shown to reliably measure caffeine-related expectations and their link to perceived stimulant effects [15]. Caffeine expectancy has been shown to affect perceived alertness and performance independently of caffeine’s actual physiological impact [16, 19]. Because explicit memory relies on attention control and arousal for effective retrieval [17], it may benefit more from expectancy-driven increases in perceived alertness than implicit memory, which operates more automatically.

Circadian arousal theory maintains that cognitive performance fluctuates throughout the day according to biological rhythms. The synchrony effect literature shows that cognitive outcomes are strongly influenced by the alignment between an individual’s chronotype and the time of testing [20]. Integrating these frameworks, this study proposes that caffeine expectancy may enhance memory performance through increased perceived alertness, while time of day may moderate this relationship by shifting baseline arousal levels.

Empirical evidence

An increasing number of studies suggest that caffeine expectancy can affect cognitive abilities even without caffeine itself. For instance [21], found that people who expected to have taken caffeine performed better in cognitive tasks compared to others who were not under the influence of caffeine, implying that there was a placebo effect. Similarly [22], indicated that expectance-related behaviors were influenced by psychological processes, where the performance gain was attributed to the person’s belief and not because of any drug effects.

Fewer studies have examined caffeine expectancy in relation to implicit memory. Specifically, implicit memory functions unconsciously most of the time, hence making it less vulnerable to the effects of expectancy-induced changes in alertness [23]. observed that placebo effects of caffeine were usually more pronounced in tasks involving executive functioning. As for the effects of time of day [13], found that the efficiency of cognitive performance was usually low in morning or afternoon testing sessions, especially early mornings. According to [24], individual circadian preferences made a difference in cognitive performance at various times of day.

Research gap and aims

Despite this body of research, important gaps remain. No study has tested a model where caffeine expectancy affects both explicit and implicit memory simultaneously within a controlled experimental design that also accounts for the time of testing. The nursing university student population has rarely been studied in this context, despite their high caffeine use. This study addresses these gaps by examining the influence of caffeine expectancy on both memory types during morning and afternoon testing sessions.

Hypotheses

H1

Participants with higher caffeine expectancy will demonstrate significantly better explicit memory performance than participants with lower caffeine expectancy.

H2

Participants with higher caffeine expectancy will demonstrate significantly better implicit memory performance than participants with lower caffeine expectancy.

H3

Participants tested during the afternoon session will demonstrate significantly better explicit and implicit memory performance than participants tested during the morning session.

H4

The positive effect of caffeine expectancy on explicit and implicit memory performance will be greater during the morning session than during the afternoon session.

Operational definitions

Caffeine expectancy served as the independent grouping variable in this study and was operationalized as participants’ measured beliefs about the expected effects of caffeine consumption. All participants received decaffeinated coffee and were informed that the beverage might or might not contain caffeine. Caffeine expectancy was assessed before beverage consumption using the CaffEQ, and participants were classified into high- or low-expectancy groups using a median split procedure. Therefore, the study compared measured caffeine expectancy groups rather than a strongly experimentally induced expectancy condition.

Explicit memory, the first dependent variable, refers to conscious and intentional recall of previously studied information and is measured by the total number of words correctly recalled from a word list. Implicit memory, the second dependent variable, refers to the unconscious influence of prior exposure on performance and is measured by the number of word-stem completions that match previously studied words. Time of day was operationalized as the testing session during which participants completed the study procedures, either the morning session (8 AM) or the afternoon session (4 PM). No chronotype measure was collected; therefore, these sessions should not be interpreted as representing individually optimal or non-optimal periods.

Methods

Aim of the study: This study aimed to examine the influence of caffeine expectancy on explicit memory, measured using a word recall test, and implicit memory, measured using a word-stem completion task, among female nursing students tested during morning and afternoon sessions.

Study design

This study used a placebo-controlled, single-blind, quasi-experimental between-subjects design. All participants received decaffeinated coffee while being informed that the beverage might or might not contain caffeine. Caffeine expectancy was measured using the CaffEQ and then categorized into high and low expectancy groups using the sample median. A placebo-controlled design was chosen because the goal was to examine the psychological impact of believing one has consumed caffeine, rather than the actual physiological effects of the substance. All participants received a decaffeinated beverage while being told that it might or might not contain caffeine. Participants were unaware that all beverages were decaffeinated and were informed that the beverage might or might not contain caffeine. However, the research team was aware of the beverage content. Therefore, the study is more accurately described as single-blind rather than double-blind.

The study followed a 2 × 2 between-subjects factorial design. Participants were placed into one of four conditions depending on their caffeine expectancy level (High or Low) and the time of day they were tested (Morning at 8 AM or Afternoon at 4 PM). Caffeine expectancy was treated as a between-subjects factor determined by a median split of CaffEQ scores. Time of day served as the second between-subjects factor, reflecting the participant’s testing session (morning or afternoon). The two outcome variables were explicit memory performance using a word recall test and implicit memory performance using a word-stem completion task.

Study setting

Data collection took place at the Faculty of Nursing, Menoufia University, Egypt. Testing sessions were scheduled at 8:00 AM and 4:00 PM to ensure participants were assessed during either the morning (8 AM) or afternoon (4 PM) testing session. All participants completed the tasks in quiet, controlled rooms to create a consistent environment and reduce distractions. Data collection was carried out between February and April 2025.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was calculated to ensure statistical significance and representativeness of the students in the Faculty of Nursing, Menoufia University, Egypt. A sample size of 64 has been calculated according to the data from the research conducted by [25], which mentioned that approximately 61.4% of the Egyptian population consumes at least one caffeinated beverage daily. Black tea is the most commonly consumed source of caffeine, followed by instant coffee and cola. While caffeine is widely used, about 36.6% of these consumers exceed the recommended safe limits. Using the following equation: n = (z2×p × q)/D2 at power 80% and CI 95%, in order to examine the same result and detect significant differences. Therefore, the sample size was estimated to be 64 students in total. However, considering the possibility of a 20% dropout, the sample size was increased to 74 students to maintain the statistical power of the study in case of dropout.

Although this prevalence-based approach was used to estimate the required sample size, it was not specifically designed for factorial ANOVA memory outcomes. Therefore, the study may have had limited statistical power to detect small interaction effects, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Participants

Participants were recruited through convenience sampling from undergraduate students enrolled at the Faculty of Nursing, Menoufia University, Egypt. The final sample included 74 female undergraduate nursing students (N = 74). This sampling method was chosen because of the practical demands of the study, which required participants to attend in-person sessions at specific times and to abstain from caffeine for at least 12 h before testing.

Inclusion criteria required participants to provide signed informed consent, be currently enrolled as undergraduate students between the ages of 18 and 25, enrolled at the faculty of nursing, be available for either the 8:00 AM or 4:00 PM session, agree to abstain from caffeine-containing products and nicotine for at least 12 h before the testing session, with compliance assessed through participant self-report and verbal confirmation prior to participation, and have no known physical or psychological conditions or caffeine allergy.

Participants were excluded if they did not fulfill the inclusion criteria, were taking medications that could interact with caffeine or affect memory, or reported a cardiovascular condition (which may be contraindicated with the use of caffeine) or caffeine allergy, or were not available to continue with the study, First-year students and male students were excluded from the study because they had limited exposure to the academic and clinical demands characteristic of nursing education, which may influence caffeine use patterns and cognitive performance. Male students were excluded to maintain a more homogeneous sample and reduce potential variability related to sex differences in caffeine metabolism, sleep patterns, and cognitive functioning. Restricting the sample to female students also helped control for biological and behavioral factors that may influence caffeine expectancy and memory performance, thereby improving the internal consistency of the study.

Tools for data collection

Data collection was performed using four primary tools.

Tool (1): A structured interview questionnaire, which was developed by researchers after a widespread literature review [26], to collect background information, including age, gender, year of study, average sleep hours per night, regularity of sleep patterns, daily coffee consumption, and any known cardiovascular or health conditions. This information was used to describe the sample and identify potential confounding variables [27]. The questionnaire consisted of demographic and lifestyle items and was used for descriptive purposes only; therefore, no total score was calculated.

Tool (2): Caffeine Expectancy Questionnaire (CaffEQ): Caffeine expectancy was measured using the CaffEQ [28], a 47-item self-report scale assessing beliefs about the psychological and physiological effects of caffeine. Each item is rated on a six-point Likert scale from 1 (Very Unlikely) to 6 (Very Likely), with higher scores reflecting stronger caffeine-related expectations. Total scores range from 47 to 282. The scale includes seven subscales covering Dependency and Withdrawal, Vigor and Alertness, Suppression of Appetite, Mood and Social Support, Physical Performance, Anxiety and Adverse Physical Reactions, and Disruption of Sleep. The CaffEQ has demonstrated good internal consistency (α = 0.89) and established construct validity [28]. Participants were divided into High and Low Expectancy groups using a median split of total scores.

Tool (3): Explicit Memory Task: Word Recall Test: Explicit memory was measured using an adapted version of the Word Recall Test based on [29]. Participants were shown a total list of 60 words, presented in sets of 15 words, with each word displayed on the screen for two seconds. After a brief distractor task, they were asked to write down as many words as they could remember. The explicit memory score was the total number of words correctly recalled. This task measures conscious and intentional memory retrieval and has been widely used as a validated measure of explicit memory in experimental cognitive research. Previous studies have demonstrated its suitability for assessing conscious recall processes and memory performance under different experimental conditions [29, 30]. Scores ranged from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating better explicit memory performance.

Tool (4): Implicit Memory Task: Word-Stem Completion: Implicit memory was measured using an adapted version of the Word-Stem Completion Task [29]. Participants were given the first three to four letters of each word and asked to complete each stem with the first word that came to mind from a total list of 60 words, 15 words presented at a time on the screen every two seconds. The implicit memory score was the number of completions that matched words from the previously studied list. This task captures unconscious priming effects from prior exposure and is one of the most frequently used paradigms for assessing implicit memory in cognitive psychology. Previous research has demonstrated the validity of word-stem completion tasks as indicators of unconscious memory processes and priming effects [29, 31]. Both tasks follow the structure and measurement principles of the original instruments, supporting their validity for the present study. Scores ranged from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating stronger implicit memory performance.

Ethical considerations

This study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines for approval of an ethics committee opinion. Ethical approval for this research was gained from the Ethical and Research Committee of the Faculty of Nursing, Menoufia University (Approval number: ERCNMA 1000/4/1/193/26). The dean of the faculty of nursing granted permission to conduct this study. Written consent was obtained from each student who was willing to participate after the study purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits had been explained. Because the study involved partial disclosure regarding beverage content, the ethics committee approved the deception procedure. Participants were fully debriefed immediately after completing the memory tasks and were informed that all beverages were decaffeinated. They were also given the opportunity to ask questions and withdraw their data after debriefing. The participants who agreed to participate were assured of confidentiality and anonymity. They were informed about their right to withdraw from the study at any time without giving a reason.

Validity and reliability of the tools

All study instruments, including the adapted Explicit Memory Task and Implicit Memory Task, underwent forward–backward translation procedures and were reviewed for face and content validity by a panel of five experts from the psychiatric nursing department. The expert panel evaluated the clarity, cultural appropriateness, and relevance of the translated materials for the target population, and necessary modifications were made before finalizing the instruments. In addition, the adapted memory tasks were pilot-tested before the main study to assess comprehensibility and feasibility among nursing students. The tools were considered valid based on expert evaluation.

The tools were evaluated for reliability using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. Reliability coefficients were α = 0.86 for the Caffeine Expectancy Questionnaire (Tool 2), α = 0.88 for the Explicit Memory Task (Tool 3), and α = 0.89 for the Implicit Memory Task (Tool 4), indicating acceptable internal consistency.

Pilot study

A pilot study was conducted on 8 students (10% of the calculated sample size) to examine the feasibility, clarity, and applicability of the study tools. The subjects in the pilot study were selected from the faculty of nursing and were not included in the main sampling frame. The pilot was intended to determine the time required to administer the questionnaire and uncover confusion in the items. There were some slight changes in wording and sequence of some of the questions for clarity, based on feedback. The pilot study revealed that the study tools were clear, understandable, and applicable.

Statistical analysis

Because the study examined the effects of two independent variables (caffeine expectancy: high vs. low; and time of day: morning vs. afternoon) on two continuous memory outcomes, a two-way between-subjects factorial ANOVA was considered the most appropriate analytical approach. This method allowed the evaluation of both main effects and the interaction effect between the two study factors.

The data were analyzed using Python software version 3.12 along with pingouin and scipy statistics packages, yielding identical results to SPSS. Descriptive statistics such as means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages were calculated for a summary of participant and baseline variables. Before conducting inferential statistics, normality was tested within cells using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test for homogeneity of variance. The assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were satisfactorily met for all analyses (all p > .05). Detailed assumption-testing results are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Because two separate ANOVAs were conducted for the two memory outcomes (explicit and implicit memory), a Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of p < .025 was applied to control for familywise Type I error.

A 2 (Expectancy: High vs. Low) x 2 (Time of Day: Morning vs. Afternoon) between-subjects factorial ANOVA was conducted separately for explicit and implicit memory. The analysis inspected the main effect of expectancy, the main effect of time of day, and the Expectancy x Time of Day interaction. Effect sizes were reported using partial eta squared (np2), interpreted as negligible below 0.01, small from 0.01 to 0.05, medium from 0.06 to 0.13, and large at 0.14 or above [32]. Post-hoc independent samples t-tests with Bonferroni correction were planned if any ANOVA effects reached significance. CaffEQ scores were split using the sample median to classify participants into High and Low Expectancy groups [28].

The manuscript was reviewed to ensure completeness and transparency in the reporting of study design, methods, analyses, and findings. The completed TREND checklist has been provided in Supplementary File 1.

For explicit memory, the ANOVA found no significant main effect of caffeine expectancy group, F (1, 70) = 0.198, p = .658, ηp² = 0.003. The main effect of time of day was also non-significant, F (1, 70) = 0.198, p = .658, ηp² = 0.003. The Expectancy x Time of Day interaction was not significant, F (1, 70) = 1.383, p = .244, ηp² = 0.019. Effect sizes for all three effects were negligible to small, indicating that neither caffeine expectancy nor time of day had a meaningful influence on the number of words correctly recalled.

Implicit memory

For implicit memory, the ANOVA similarly yielded no significant main effect of caffeine expectancy group, F (1, 70) = 0.035, p = .852, ηp² = 0.001. The main effect of time of day was non-significant, F (1, 70) = 0.719, p = .399, ηp² = 0.010. The interaction effect was also non-significant, F (1, 70) = 2.205, p = .142, ηp² = 0.031. Although the interaction produced the largest effect size across all tests, it remained within the small range and did not reach the 0.05 threshold.

Post-hoc comparisons

Because none of the ANOVA effects reached statistical significance for either dependent variable, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were not conducted. Running post-hoc tests in the absence of a significant omnibus ANOVA would increase the risk of a Type I error without theoretical justification. Accordingly, no post-hoc analyses were performed. Because two separate ANOVAs were conducted for explicit and implicit memory outcomes, a Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of p < .025 was applied. All reported p-values remained non-significant under the adjusted criterion, and therefore the interpretation of the results was unchanged.

Summary of findings

Overall, the two-way between-subjects ANOVA analyses showed that caffeine expectancy did not significantly affect either explicit or implicit memory performance. Neither the main effect of expectancy group, the main effect of time of day, nor their interaction reached significance for explicit memory (all p > .24) or implicit memory (all p > .14). Because two separate ANOVAs were conducted, a Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of p < .025 was applied, and all effects remained nonsignificant under this adjusted criterion. Effect sizes were consistently negligible to small, with the Expectancy × Time of Day interaction for implicit memory producing the largest value (ηp² = 0.031). These findings indicate that the study hypotheses were not supported, and the possible reasons for these results are discussed in the Discussion section.

Discussion

Overview

This study set out to examine whether caffeine expectancy would meaningfully influence explicit and implicit memory performance among university students tested at two testing times, morning and afternoon, and whether this relationship would be moderated by testing time. Based on expectancy theory and prior research, the study hypothesized that students with higher caffeine expectancy would demonstrate better memory performance across morning and afternoon testing sessions. The findings did not support this hypothesis. The results of two separate two-way between-subjects ANOVAs revealed that neither caffeine expectancy, time of day, nor their interaction significantly affected either memory outcome. The following sections discuss these findings in detail and address their broader implications.

The effect of caffeine expectancy on memory performance

However, the key finding of this study was that caffeine expectancy did not significantly affect either explicit or implicit memory recall. This conclusion seems strange against the background of the theoretical grounds of the study. According to expectancy theory, one’s belief concerning the impact of a certain substance can affect his/her cognitive/behavioral performance in the same way as real physical effects [18]. Several studies have supported this in the context of caffeine, showing that participants who believed they had consumed caffeine performed better on attention tasks [33, 34]. The present study extended this to memory, but the expectancy manipulation did not produce a similar benefit.

One possible reason for this null result lies in the type of task itself. While attention and reaction time are relatively easier to activate by psychological states of arousal, memory tasks depend on complicated neural processes that cannot be as easily activated by mere belief [9, 30]. Another aspect to take into consideration is the ambiguity of the manipulation of expectancy. In studies where expectancy was found to produce a significant effect, the manipulation was strong in the sense that participants were told that they had consumed caffeine [35].

Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence that caffeine expectancy has no effect on memory. Rather, methodological factors such as the relatively weak expectancy manipulation, the absence of an active caffeine condition, and limited statistical power may have contributed to the null findings.

The effect of time of day on memory performance

The second main result was that the time of day had no significant effect on memory performance. This result seems to be slightly contradictory to previous findings, indicating that cognitive performance changes depending on circadian rhythms and chronotype of an individual [10, 11]. Prior studies indicate that memory performance among students examined outside the specific testing session is lower [13, 24]. The lack of a time-of-day effect can be explained by the fact that the difference between 8 AM and 4 PM may not have been sufficient to capture meaningful variations in cognitive performance. In addition, because chronotype was not assessed, it was not possible to determine whether the testing sessions corresponded to participants’ individual circadian preferences [11].

The interaction between caffeine expectancy and time of day

Despite the fact that there was no statistically significant interaction between Expectancy and Time of Day for any of the outcomes, the Expectancy by Time-of-Day interaction yielded the highest effect size of all the ANOVAs, especially for implicit memory (ηp² = 0.031). The descriptive means showed a crossover pattern: for both explicit and implicit memory, the High Expectancy group scored higher on the task in the morning compared to the afternoon, whereas the Low Expectancy group had the reverse pattern. Although the descriptive means showed a crossover pattern, the interaction effects were not statistically significant. Therefore, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence for expectancy theory. Although similar patterns have been reported in some previous studies, the absence of a statistically significant interaction in the present study prevents firm conclusions regarding a moderating effect of testing time on expectancy-related memory performance [13].

Explicit memory versus implicit memory

One of the significant aspects of the current research was the analysis of two types of memory. According to [30], as well as [31], explicit and implicit memory use different mechanisms and have different sensitivities towards such factors as arousal and attention. In the current study, both types of memory did not show any statistically significant differences, although the observed interaction effect size was numerically larger for implicit memory than for explicit memory. This fact implies that implicit memory could be more sensitive to the indirect effects of beliefs and arousal, which is something unexpected.

Implications

Scientific implications

Scientifically, this paper adds to the body of knowledge concerning placebo and expectancy effects within cognitive psychology. Though many papers have demonstrated that caffeine expectancy enhances attention and alertness [33, 34], the present results show that this does not hold true with respect to memory, at least within the scope of the present experiments. Given the methodological limitations of the present study, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence that memory is inherently less responsive to expectancy effects. Further research is needed before theoretical conclusions can be drawn. Future studies should use validated chronotype measures and wider testing windows to more accurately assess time-of-day effects.

Practical implications

In terms of application, the findings have clear implications for learners, educators, and health practitioners. The lack of an expectancy effect on memory implies that students who depend on the expectancy to perform better through caffeine consumption during early morning and late-night learning sessions, the present findings did not provide evidence that caffeine expectancy improved memory performance under the conditions examined in this study.

This is especially important considering the increased use of caffeine among university students based on such expectations [1, 2, 36]. Health and education policies must focus on developing evidence-based approaches like ensuring adequate sleep, scheduling learning sessions according to the body clock, and using efficient methods of studying. Clinicians dealing with university students must understand how expectations influence caffeine consumption to devise appropriate psychoeducational interventions against the problem.

Limitations

The use of a female-only sample may also limit the generalizability of the findings to male nursing students. Despite the fact that the experiment was well-designed and performed, some limitations should be taken into account when discussing the results obtained. First of all, it should be mentioned that there is a limitation connected with the sample used for the research. In fact, all participants involved in the experiment were female undergraduate students who were conveniently selected, which implies that the findings cannot be generalized. For example, they cannot be applied to male students or older adults. Moreover, the absence of male participants implies that the effect of caffeine expectancy on memory in males could not be explored.

The second limitation relates to the expectancy manipulation procedure. Participants were informed that the beverage might or might not contain caffeine rather than being explicitly told that it contained caffeine. This probabilistic framing may have weakened the expectancy induction and reduced the likelihood of detecting expectancy-related effects. In addition, no formal manipulation check was administered to verify whether participants believed that they had consumed caffeine or experienced increased alertness following beverage consumption. Consequently, the effectiveness of the expectancy manipulation could not be directly confirmed and should be considered when interpreting the null findings.

Another limitation is the absence of an active caffeine control group. Because all participants received decaffeinated coffee, the study could not distinguish expectancy effects from the pharmacological effects of caffeine. Future studies should include both placebo and active caffeine conditions to better disentangle these mechanisms.

The third limitation is related to the operationalization of the time of day. The assignment of participants to sessions was done on the basis of availability and not using a standardized measure like the MEQ and MCTQ. Without using a chronotype measure, it cannot be ruled out that some of the participants had their test sessions conducted during morning or afternoon testing sessions for them.

The fourth limitation pertains to statistical power. With 74 participants distributed across four conditions, there were between 17 and 20 participants in each cell. This number may be adequate to detect main effects, but smaller numbers are necessary to detect interaction effects. Non-significant findings could thus simply mean lack of statistical power rather than lack of an effect. The fifth limitation is the use of adapted versions of memory tasks that were not entirely standardized and validated locally. Neither the Word Recall Test nor the Word-Stem Completion Task had been validated separately using Arabic-speaking individuals.

Engagement with memory tests may also depend on language and culture-specific issues.

In addition, the sample size was originally estimated using a prevalence-based formula rather than an effect-size approach tailored to memory outcomes and factorial ANOVA designs, which should be considered when interpreting the findings.

An outlier analysis was conducted, and no extreme values were detected. Therefore, the variability observed in memory performance is unlikely to be driven by anomalous observations.

Recommendations for future research

Future studies should employ stronger and more explicit expectancy manipulations and incorporate formal manipulation checks immediately after beverage consumption to verify the success of expectancy induction. Based on the identified limitations, the following are suggestions for future research that could be pursued. The first suggestion is that future research should employ a sample that is more diverse in terms of gender, age, chronotype, and cultural differences. The inclusion of males in the study will fill an important gap in the existing literature on caffeine expectancy and memory among nursing students. The second suggestion is that there should be a more potent and clear manipulation of caffeine expectancy.

The use of chronotype measures like the MEQ or MCTQ is highly encouraged to ensure that testing sessions are aligned with each participant’s chronotype and circadian preferences, thereby increasing ecological validity in terms of the time-of-day manipulation. Future studies could also benefit from a much greater number of participants, with power analyses showing that a minimum of 120–150 participants would be required to capture the small to medium interactions seen in the present study. Crossover designs, whereby the same participants take part in both morning and afternoon sessions, will also aid in detecting time-of-day effects.

Finally, developing validated Arabic word recall and word-stem completion materials appropriate for university students would strengthen the reliability and cultural relevance of the measurement. Researchers might also consider expanding cognitive outcomes beyond word-based memory tasks to include working memory, prospective memory, or real-world academic performance measures, providing a more comprehensive picture of how caffeine expectancy interacts with circadian variation across different aspects of student cognition.

Conclusion

The present study did not find significant effects of caffeine expectancy, testing session, or their interaction on explicit or implicit memory performance. However, these null findings should be interpreted cautiously in light of several methodological limitations, including the relatively weak expectancy manipulation, the absence of chronotype assessment, the lack of an active caffeine condition, and limited statistical power for detecting interaction effects. Consequently, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence against expectancy effects on memory. Future studies employing stronger expectancy manipulations, chronotype measures, active caffeine control conditions, and larger samples are needed to clarify these relationships.
