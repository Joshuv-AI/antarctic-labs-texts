Physicochemical characterization of a recently developed resin-based calcium silicate cement compared to established pulp capping materials
Authors: Kemal Furkan Güdül, Gülşah Tonga, Hüseyin Hatirli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to compare the physical and chemical properties of TheraBase, a newly introduced resin-based calcium silicate cement, with TheraCal LC, Biodentine, and Bio MTA+ under in vitro conditions. Disc-shaped specimens were prepared from each material. Compressive strength was tested using a universal testing machine, radiopacity was assessed through digital radiographic analysis, and water sorption and solubility were determined based on weight changes after immersion. pH and calcium ion release were measured at multiple time points. Data were analyzed using ANOVA and post hoc tests (p < 0.05). TheraBase exhibited the highest compressive strength, whereas Bio MTA+ showed the lowest values. Biodentine showed the highest solubility, whereas TheraCal LC had the lowest. TheraBase presented the lowest values for water sorption, pH, and calcium ion release (p < 0.05). TheraBase demonstrated favorable properties, including high compressive strength, low solubility, and adequate radiopacity, suggesting its potential for clinical use in pulp capping. However, its low calcium ion release may limit its regenerative potential.Keywords: Calcium ion release, Compressive strength, pH, Radiopacity, Solubility, Water sorptionSubject terms: Materials science, Medical research

Introduction

Vital pulp therapy (VPT) aims to preserve and maintain the vitality of the pulp tissue of a tooth damaged by trauma, caries, or iatrogenic causes. In vital teeth, the proximity of deep carious lesions or trauma to the pulp chamber, as well as direct pulp exposure, forms the clinical basis for VPT. Vital pulp therapy is an effective caries management approach that aims to preserve pulp vitality by creating a biologically favorable environment and preventing bacterial contamination1.

Indirect and direct pulp capping are among the key VPT protocols employed in the management of deep carious lesions and cavity preparations. Indirect pulp capping is a treatment procedure in which a thin layer of caries-free dentin is left over the pulp chamber, and this is sealed with a biomaterial. Direct pulp capping is a treatment modality used in vital teeth with mechanical or traumatic pulp exposures, provided that the tooth is radiographically healthy and pulpal bleeding can be adequately controlled1. The success of both indirect and direct pulp capping treatments depends largely on the biocompatibility of the materials used and their ability to prevent bacterial microleakage. Ideal pulp capping materials should promote dentinogenesis, effectively prevent bacterial penetration into the pulp tissue, and exhibit minimal cytotoxicity to maintain pulp vitality. For this purpose, calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) has long been regarded as the gold standard material in pulp capping treatments due to its antibacterial properties and ability to stimulate hard tissue formation. Calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) protects the pulp by stimulating dentin bridge formation and promoting tertiary dentinogenesis2. However, it has notable drawbacks, including poor sealing ability to dentin, gradual dissolution over time, and the formation of tunneling defects within the reparative dentin bridge3. Due to the limitations of calcium hydroxide, researchers have sought alternative materials. Currently, calcium silicate-based (CSB) cements, known for their superior regenerative and restorative properties, are widely preferred as alternatives to Ca(OH)2 in vital pulp therapy4.

CSB materials have gained increasing importance in dentistry since the 1990s1. Mineral Trioxide Aggregate (MTA) was the first CSB pulp capping material introduced to the market. It demonstrates superior dentinogenic potential by promoting reparative dentin formation more effectively than calcium hydroxide5,6. However, MTA has notable drawbacks, including low water solubility, a prolonged setting time, and a relatively high cost7. Over time, various CSB materials such as Biodentine (Septodont, Saint-Maur-des-Fossés, France) and TheraCal LC (Bisco Inc., Schaumburg, IL, USA) have been developed. These materials were developed to enhance the physical and mechanical properties of MTA while preserving its biological advantages and have been successfully used in clinical practice. Although Biodentine exhibits radiopacity values higher than dentin, its radiopacity has been reported to be relatively lower compared with some other calcium silicate–based materials, which has been described as a potential limitation8. In contrast, TheraCal LC has been shown to exhibit lower calcium (Ca²⁺) ion release compared with Biodentine9. These limitations have prompted the development of new-generation materials. TheraBase (Bisco Inc., Schaumburg, IL, USA), introduced as a recently developed CSB material, offers an innovative resin-based formulation. This newly developed material features a dual-curing mechanism and self-adhesive properties, facilitating ease of application. Additionally, TheraBase releases calcium (Ca⁺2) and fluoride (F⁻) ions and chemically bonds to the tooth structure. According to the manufacturer’s information, TheraBase is described as a radiopaque material that allows for clear evaluation on radiographs10.

The physicochemical properties of CSB materials are of critical importance in clinical practice, as they directly influence pulp response. Although numerous studies have compared the physical and chemical properties of different CSB materials11,12, the number of investigations focusing on TheraBase remains limited13,14. Moreover, to date, no studies have evaluated the compressive strength or radiopacity of TheraBase. As the present study provides the first data on these properties, it is expected to make a significant contribution to the existing body of literature.

In light of this information, the present study aimed to evaluate and compare the physicochemical properties, compressive strength, radiopacity, solubility, water sorption, pH, and Ca²⁺ release of TheraBase with those of other CSB materials, namely Bio MTA+ (Cerkamed Medical Company, Poland), Biodentine, and TheraCal LC. The null hypothesis tested was that there would be no significant differences in the physicochemical properties among the tested pulp capping materials.

Methods

A total of 84 disk-shaped samples were prepared in four groups for the compressive strength, radioopacity, water absorption, and solubility tests, with 95% test confidence (1-α), 95% test power (1-β), and an effect size of f = 0.66. For pH and calcium ion release tests, a total of 16 disk-shaped samples were prepared in four groups with 95% test confidence (1-α), 95% test power (1-β), and an effect size of f = 2.48215.

Sample preparation

Aluminum and teflon molds of different diameters and thicknesses were fabricated, and the CSB materials were prepared and placed into the molds according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transparent matrix strips and glass plates were pressed onto the top surfaces of the molds to obtain flat specimen surfaces. Excess material was removed with a cement spatula. The specimens were then removed from the molds and allowed to set according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Table 1).

Compressive strength analysis

To evaluate the compressive strength, samples were prepared using teflon molds with a diameter of 4 mm and a thickness of 6 mm (n = 7)7. The specimens that completed the initial hardening were removed from the molds. Then, the prepared specimens were kept in a 37 °C incubator for 24 h in a dry environment7. A universal testing machine (Shimadzu AGS-10kNXD, Shimadzu Co, Japan) was used to measure the compressive strength. The device was positioned to create a load on the long axis of the specimens. The load was applied at a head speed of 1.0 mm/min until a fracture occurred. The software recorded the maximum load value causing the specimens to fracture in Newtons. Compressive strength was calculated by the following formula7:

k = 4 F/ πd2

k = Compressive strength (Megapascal)

F = Maximum load force before fracture (Newton)

d = Average diameter of the sample (Millimetre)

Radiopacity analysis

To evaluate the radiopacity, the samples were prepared using aluminum molds with a diameter of 5 mm and a thickness of 1 mm (n = 7)16. The samples were prepared as previously described and that completed the initial hardening were removed from the aluminum molds. Then, the prepared specimens were kept in an oven at 37 °C for 48 h16. To obtain samples of enamel and dentin, newly extracted human third molar teeth were used. A precision cutting device (Micracut 152, Metkon, Bursa, Turkey) was used to prepare samples from extracted teeth. The crowns of the teeth were sectioned horizontally using a precision saw to obtain enamel and dentin samples approximately 1 mm thick. The step-wedge prepared by superimposing 12 aluminum sheets of 99,5% purity, each 1 mm thick, was used as a reference to evaluate the relationship between the radiopacity of the samples and the radiopacity of the aluminum.

The materials, tooth sections, and aluminum step-wedge were placed on a number 4 phosphor plate. The dental X-ray machine (Kodak 2100 intraoral X-ray, NY, USA) was set to 60 kVp and 7 mA. The focal spot-object distance was 30 cm and the exposure time was 0,32 s17. The image obtained after irradiation was scanned using a phosphor plate scanner (Vistascan, Dürr Dental, Germany), and images were transferred to Image J (National Institutes of Health, Maryland, USA) to measure the mean grey value (MGV) of the materials and aluminum step-wedge on the digital radiographs. Each sample was measured 3 times using the ImageJ program, and these measurements were averaged (Fig. 1). The MGVs of the materials and tooth sections were converted into millimeter aluminum (mm Al) units using the following formula16:

Determination of MGV using image J software.

+mm Al below material’s MGV

A: MGV of the material - the MGV of the aluminum step-wedge increment immediately below the material’s MGV.

B: MGV of the aluminum step-wedge increment immediately above the material’s MGV - MGV of the aluminum step-wedge increment immediately below the material’s MGV.

1: 1 mm increments of the aluminum step-wedge.

Water sorption and solubility analysis

To evaluate water sorption and solubility, samples were prepared using teflon molds with a diameter of 8 mm and a thickness of 2 mm (n = 7)18. The prepared samples were kept in an incubator at 37 °C for 24 h14. Evaluation of water sorption of the samples was performed on the 1st, 7th, and 30th days after placement in the tubes19. Solubility evaluation was performed on the 30th day after the samples were placed in the tubes. All samples were weighed three times with a precision of 0,01 g on a precision measuring balance (Precisa BJ 410 C, Dietikon, Switzerland). The weight measurements for each sample were averaged and recorded (I). The samples were then placed in sealed tubes containing 10 mL distilled water and placed in an oven at 37 °C. For water sorption evaluation, the initial dry mass (D) of each specimen was recorded after setting. The specimens were then stored in sealed containers containing distilled water at 37 °C. On days 1st, 7th, and 30th the specimens were removed from the water, gently blotted with absorbent paper to remove surface moisture, and immediately weighed to obtain the wet mass (M₁, M₇, and M₃₀, respectively). After weighing, the specimens were returned to the same containers for continued water storage. The specimens were not fully dried after the 1-day and 7-day measurements. Complete drying was performed only after the 30 days by placing the specimens in a desiccator containing calcium chloride at 37 °C for 24 h, after which the final dry mass (D₃₀) was recorded for solubility calculation18:

Water sorption: (Mt – D) / D × 100

(Mt= represents the wet mass at the respective time point (day 1, 7, or 30))

Solubility: (I-D) / D × 100

pH analysis

To evaluate pH, the samples were placed in aluminum molds with a diameter of 5 mm and a thickness of 1 mm (n = 2)20. The samples placed in the molds were kept in an incubator at 37 °C for 24 h21. Each sample was then placed in a separate tube containing 10 mL of distilled water. The samples were stored in an incubator at 37 °C during the test period. Distilled water was collected and replaced at 3 h, 24 h, and at the end of the 3rd, 7th, 14th, and 28th day after the samples were placed in the tubes22. pH was measured using a pH meter (SevenEasy™, Mettler Toledo, USA) calibrated with pH = 10.1, 7.01, and 4.01 solutions in advance at the specified measurement periods. During the measurement process, the electrode was washed with distilled water between each measurement and dried with a sponge. The pH value was recorded as the pH value after fixing the value appearing on the display of the pH meter. All measurements were repeated three times and the average pH value was taken for each measurement.

Calcium ion release analysis

To evaluate Ca+ 2 release, the samples were placed in aluminum molds with a diameter of 5 mm and a thickness of 1 mm (n = 2)20. The samples placed in the molds were kept in an incubator at 37 °C for 24 h21. Each sample was then placed in a separate tube containing 10 mL of distilled water. The samples were stored in an incubator at 37 °C during the test period. Distilled water was collected and replaced at the 3rd hour, 24th hour, and at the end of the 3rd, 7th, 14th, and 28th day after the samples were placed in the tubes22. Calcium ion release was measured by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) (Thermo Scientific™ iCAP™ 7000 Series, USA). Measurements were performed at the specified test periods. All measurements were repeated three times and averaged.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data obtained from solubility, compressive strength, and radiopacity tests were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test. For time-dependent comparisons of water sorption, pH, and Ca²⁺ release, two-way repeated measures ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc correction was applied. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

As a result of this study, the null hypothesis stating that “there would be no significant differences in the physicochemical properties among the tested pulp capping materials” was rejected, as significant differences were observed among the tested materials.

Compressive strength is a critical property influencing the clinical performance of dental materials. According to the ISO 9917-1 standard, the recommended minimum compressive strength for dental cements is 50 MPa23. In the present study, TheraBase, TheraCal LC, and Biodentine satisfied this requirement, whereas Bio MTA+ exhibited lower values. A possible explanation for the reduced compressive strength of Bio MTA+ compared with the other tested materials may be related to its unique formulation. MTA-based materials are commonly prepared with a relatively high water-to-powder ratio, which can result in the formation of increased porosity within the material during the setting reaction24. Another contributing factor may be the manual mixing of the powder and liquid components of Bio MTA+. In contrast, calcium silicate-based materials containing resin or those mixed using an encapsulated system tend to achieve a more homogeneous consistency, promoting improved integration of the components during setting and, consequently, greater durability25.

The highest compressive strength was observed in the TheraBase group. Several factors may account for this finding. One possible explanation is the presence of titanium dioxide (TiO₂) in TheraBase. Tahir et al.26 incorporated 3% and 5% TiO₂ nanoparticles into glass ionomer materials and evaluated their mechanical properties. Their results demonstrated that the groups containing 3% and 5% TiO₂ nanoparticles exhibited significantly higher compressive strength compared with the group without TiO₂. Another potential factor is the relatively high glass filler content of TheraBase (50–75%). Fillers enhance the mechanical performance of restorative materials and improve their resistance to occlusal forces, thereby contributing to their long-term clinical durability.

A review of the literature reveals numerous studies that have investigated the compressive strength of Biodentine, TheraCal LC, and various MTA-based materials25,27,28. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has specifically evaluated the compressive strength of TheraBase. Gasperi et al.28 conducted a comparative analysis of MTA Angelus, TheraCal LC, and Dycal, reporting that the TheraCal LC group exhibited significantly higher compressive strength than the other materials. The findings from these previous studies are consistent with the results of the present investigation.

Appropriate radiopacity is essential for the radiographic detection of restorative materials and their clinical applicability. According to ISO 4049, restorative materials applied to coronal tooth tissue should demonstrate radiopacity equal to or greater than that of pure aluminum of the same thickness. Moreover, ISO 4049 specifies that the radiopacity of restorative materials must be at least equivalent to that of dentin of the same thickness29. All materials tested in the present study met this requirement. Elements such as strontium (Sr), barium (Ba), zirconium (Zr), and ytterbium (Yb) are commonly incorporated into restorative materials to enhance radiopacity, which increases proportionally with the atomic number of these elements. In line with this, the presence of heavy metal oxides with relatively higher ratios or atomic numbers specifically Bi₂O₃ and ZrO₂ in Bio MTA + may explain its superior radiopacity30. Beyond the type and amount of radiopacifying agents, the organic-to-inorganic ratio of the material can also influence radiopacity. Resin-based materials, which contain higher proportions of organic components, may exhibit lower radiopacity, whereas mineral-based materials such as Bio MTA + and Biodentine, characterized by a predominance of inorganic content, demonstrate higher radiopacity31,32.

Numerous studies have evaluated the radiopacity of Biodentine, TheraCal LC, and various MTA-based materials32–34. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has specifically investigated the radiopacity of TheraBase. Nevertheless, the findings reported for similar materials are consistent with the results of the present study33–35.

The TheraBase material used in this study contains ytterbium trifluoride as its radiopacifying agent. The evaluation revealed that the TheraBase group exhibited higher radiopacity than dentin and a value comparable to enamel. Previous studies have reported that ytterbium trifluoride provides greater radiopacity than both enamel and dentin35,36. These findings are partially consistent with the results of the present study. The observed differences may be attributed to variations in the proportion of ytterbium trifluoride incorporated into the materials.

The solubility of restorative materials is a critical factor influencing their clinical durability and sealing ability. In the present study, the highest solubility was observed in the Biodentine group, whereas the TheraCal LC group exhibited the lowest solubility after the 30-day evaluation. Both TheraBase and Bio MTA+ also demonstrated solubility values below the ISO threshold. This outcome may be related to the types and proportions of calcium silicate the main component of these materials as well as the presence of additives, fillers, binders, and polymers, all of which may influence solubility37,38. The elevated solubility of Biodentine can be attributed to the addition of water-soluble polycarboxylate to the powder formulation, which improves the workability of the cement mixture. Although this additive allows for a reduced water/powder ratio, the hydrophilic and surfactant nature of water-soluble polycarboxylate may contribute to the increased solubility observed in Biodentine39,40. In contrast, the low solubility of resin-based materials such as TheraCal LC and TheraBase can be explained by the presence of hydrophobic monomers (particularly Bis-GMA and Bis-EMA) in their composition18,37. Bis-GMA, a monomer present in both materials, has limited water solubility and undergoes phase separation in the presence of water41. This characteristic may decrease water permeability and thereby contribute to lower solubility. To the best of our knowledge, only one study has investigated the solubility of TheraBase, and its findings were consistent with those of the present study14. However, no study was found specifically addressing the solubility of Bio MTA+. The present results indicated that the solubility of Bio MTA + was comparable to that reported for other MTA-based materials in the literature27,37.

In the present study, all materials exhibited an increase in water sorption over the 28 days. Among them, TheraCal LC demonstrated the highest water sorption, whereas TheraBase showed the lowest. This difference may be attributed to variations in the monomer composition of the materials. Bis-EMA, a monomer present in TheraBase, is more hydrophobic than Bis-GMA owing to the absence of hydroxyl (–OH) groups, which may account for the reduced water sorption observed in TheraBase42. Furthermore, the hydrophobic characteristics of the polymer matrix and filler phase in TheraBase may enhance its stability by limiting water diffusion.

An alkaline environment plays a crucial role in supporting the healing and mineralization of pulp tissue. Therefore, monitoring pH changes is essential when evaluating the antimicrobial efficacy of calcium CSB materials, as pH strongly influences both microbial growth and biocompatibility43. In the present study, the Biodentine group exhibited the highest pH values across all time periods. The sustained alkalinity of Biodentine can be attributed to its high proportion of calcium silicate particles, which react with water to release Ca²⁺, OH⁻, and silicate ions, thereby producing a highly alkaline environment44. In addition, the composition of Bio MTA+ includes multiple metal oxides. However, the alkalinity of CSB materials primarily results from the formation of calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂) during the hydration of tricalcium silicate45. Accordingly, the elevated pH values observed in the Bio MTA+ group can be attributed to the hydration reactions of calcium silicate phases, with the presence of metal oxides potentially influencing the hydration process indirectly. In contrast, the TheraCal LC and TheraBase groups consistently demonstrated significantly lower pH values at all test intervals. This reduction may be associated with the resin matrix, which restricts ion migration and limits hydration of the calcium silicate components46. Furthermore, the low solubility of these materials may reduce their alkalization potential by inhibiting the formation of Ca(OH)₂.

In the literature, the results of previous studies evaluating the pH of Biodentine, TheraCal LC, and various MTA-based materials are in agreement with the findings of the present study14,15,43. However, to the best of our knowledge, only one study has investigated the pH of TheraBase14, whereas no study has specifically evaluated the pH of Bio MTA+.

Calcium ion (Ca2+) release plays a crucial role in pulp tissue healing and dentin bridge formation. This ion promotes cell differentiation and supports hard tissue mineralization by regulating biomarkers such as osteopontin and BMP-240. The long-term Ca2+ release capacity of bioactive materials is particularly valuable, as it provides sustained support for these biological processes. According to the findings of the present study, the Biodentine group exhibited the highest Ca2+ release, followed by Bio MTA+, TheraBase, and TheraCal LC, respectively. This ranking is consistent with previous studies reporting that resin-modified materials release lower levels of Ca2+18,40,47. The elevated Ca²⁺ release observed in Biodentine can be attributed to its high tricalcium silicate content (80.1%) in the powder formulation, in combination with a CaCl₂-containing liquid that acts as an accelerator and water-reducing agent. As a result, Biodentine contains a relatively high concentration of calcium-releasing sources47. Another contributing factor is its porous, highly hydrophilic structure, which facilitates hydration and promotes the formation of substantial amounts of calcium silicate hydrate and calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂)47.

The low Ca²⁺ release observed in the TheraBase and TheraCal LC groups can be attributed to both their lower Portland cement content (10–30% and 50%, respectively) and the restrictive effect of their resin matrices on ion release46. TheraBase and TheraCal LC are therefore more appropriately described as resin-modified calcium silicate–based cements, as their resin matrix partially limits hydration while preserving calcium silicate–derived bioactivity. Camilleri et al.48 reported that tricalcium silicate, a major component of Portland cement, constitutes approximately 80.1% of Biodentine’s composition. This suggests that TheraBase and TheraCal LC contain comparatively fewer Ca²⁺ sources than Biodentine. Consequently, the reduced Ca²⁺ release in these groups relative to Biodentine may primarily be explained by compositional differences.

Numerous studies have investigated the Ca²⁺ release of Biodentine, TheraCal LC, and various MTA-based materials14,15,21. However, to the best of our knowledge, only one study has evaluated the Ca²⁺ release of TheraBase, and its findings were consistent with those of the present study14, whereas no studies have yet assessed Bio MTA+. The results of the present investigation revealed that Bio MTA+ demonstrated outcomes largely consistent with those reported for other MTA-based materials in the literature15,18,49, while some studies have reported differing results44,50. Such variability may be attributed to differences in porosity and particle size among MTA-based formulations.

Although pronounced numerical differences were observed among the tested materials in terms of pH and calcium ion release, not all comparisons reached statistical significance at every measurement time point. This finding may be explained by the extremely large effect size observed in the statistical analysis (f = 2.482), indicating substantial variability both within and between groups. Such variability, particularly in time-dependent measurements, can reduce statistical sensitivity despite marked numerical trends.

This study has certain limitations. As it was conducted under in vitro conditions, the complex dynamics of the intraoral environment could not be fully replicated. Therefore, the actual physical and chemical behavior of these materials should be further validated through well-designed clinical studies. In addition to the in vitro nature of the study, another limitation is the absence of material characterization analyses and biological evaluations such as antibacterial activity or cytotoxicity tests. These analyses require different experimental infrastructures and methodological approaches and were therefore beyond the predefined scope of the present study, which focused on physicochemical properties relevant to clinical performance. Future studies combining physicochemical assessment with material characterization and biological analyses are needed to further elucidate the behavior of these materials.

Based on these findings, TheraBase appears to be suitable for clinical use. Its high compressive strength, combined with low water sorption and solubility, may enhance durability and support its application as a long-lasting cement. Furthermore, its ability to maintain an alkaline pH may contribute to antibacterial properties, which may be advantageous in clinical settings. Additionally, TheraBase demonstrated radiopacity values comparable to Biodentine and higher than dentin, fulfilling ISO requirements and supporting its radiographic detectability. However, the relatively low Ca²⁺ release of TheraBase could limit its potential healing effects on dental tissues.
