Evolutionary trade-offs under stochastic water stress shape xylem vulnerability to drought
Authors: Ross M. Deans, Patrick Meir, Roderick C. Dewar, Maurizio Mencuccini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificancePlant productivity and survival under drought are fundamentally shaped by the ability of the water conduction pathway (xylem) to avoid damage caused by water stress. We show that observed xylem vulnerability to water stress is consistent with natural selection that optimizes trade-offs among photosynthetic gains, the costs of possessing xylem that can withstand water stress, and the likelihood of experiencing water stress. Crucially, our theory predicts the likelihood of complete loss of water conduction and shows how it depends on climate variability and a metric of xylem safety, the hydraulic safety index. The work highlights the importance of water stress stochasticity in shaping xylem vulnerability to water stress and the risk of deadly damage now and under future climates.

The ability of the plant to maintain an unbroken water connection from root to leaf via the water conduction pathway (xylem) is fundamental to plant productivity and survival. This connection can be disrupted and eventually severed at sufficiently high water stress through the formation of gas bubbles (embolisms), which block water transport in affected conduits, leading to reduced gas exchange for photosynthesis, reduced growth, desiccation of meristems, and eventual death (1–4). As global carbon and water cycle models increasingly include the effects of declining xylem function with water stress (5, 6), there is a pressing need to improve predictions of hydraulic traits and the risk of complete loss of xylem function (hydraulic failure) under a given environment, and to predict how both may shift under a changing climate (3, 7–10).

Xylem vulnerability curves describe how the functional ability for xylem to conduct water (measured here as hydraulic conductance per leaf area, K, mmol m−2 s−1 MPa−1) declines with increasing water stress (measured as xylem water tension or water potential, a negative pressure that becomes more negative at higher stresses, Ψ, MPa). Species adapted to greater water stress under natural conditions (measured as the strongest xylem tension or the minimum water potential experienced, often over a year, Ψmin) often possess xylem that is more resistant to embolism (e.g., a more negative water potential at which conductance declines by 50%, P50, MPa) (11). The difference between the maximum water stress and the median embolism resistance of xylem, the hydraulic safety margin (HSM50 = Ψmin – P50, MPa), varies across species and locations, and is a common metric of hydraulic risk and the potential for drought-induced mortality (7, 11–13). Smaller safety margins have been observed in angiosperms than gymnosperms and have been explained previously by differences in xylem anatomy, and by proposing that angiosperms are better able to refill embolized xylem (11, 14). However, xylem refilling in woody plants is rare and requires very specific conditions (1, 15–17). Hydraulic function can recover through new xylem growth (18, 19), implying that the cost of xylem replacement, and differences in costs driven by different anatomy, may be central to the adaptive selection of xylem vulnerability.

Why are xylem vulnerability curves shaped the way they are and how are they adapted to environmental conditions? Previous theories of xylem vulnerability to water stress have been heavily anatomical, reflecting the physics of embolism formation and propagation (20, 21), and the structural requirements to withstand implosion from water tension in xylem, even if these tensions are rarely met (22). Embolisms can propagate from one conduit to another via small pits that also permit water flow, with smaller pit membrane radii withstanding higher tensions before embolisms propagate but also restricting water flow (23). Models of embolism propagation through a xylem network highlight the important roles that pit anatomy and xylem connectivity have on xylem vulnerability to water stress and have successfully reproduced observed vulnerability curves (24–30), including the safety–efficiency trade-off between maximum conductance/conductivity and xylem embolism resistance observed in stems (31) and leaves (32), driven by differing connectivity between conduits. Optimization models based on evolutionary assumptions hypothesize an adaptive link to the environment by suggesting that the observed xylem anatomy is the one that maximizes some fitness goal such as net carbon gain (33), or optimizes the trade-off between hydraulic efficiency and safety (14). Despite successes, anatomically based approaches require detailed structural data, while the translation of conduit dimensions to vulnerability curves using physical theory is uncertain (34). Recent optimization studies have also been limited by assuming a parametrized form for the vulnerability curve (14), neglecting the diversity of embolism resistance within xylem that gives rise to the vulnerability curve (35), and thus losing critical information such as the water potentials of first xylem embolism and complete hydraulic failure.

The stochastic nature of the environment introduces an inherent randomness to water stress from an expected distribution that is based on the regional hydrology (36–39), but this stochasticity has been largely ignored in explaining xylem vulnerability to water stress. Recent datasets of sufficient duration have enabled us to start probing the stochasticity of naturally occurring water stress (40). Xylem in woody species can withstand the most likely water stress scenarios, sustaining negligible embolism within a typical growing season (41, 42). However, this does not necessarily indicate a plant’s ability to withstand rare events, such as severe drought (43, 44). Could considering Ψmin as a random variable help explain adaptive xylem vulnerability to water stress, and predict the likelihood of hydraulic failure and death now and under future water stress regimes (10, 45, 46)?

Here, we develop an optimization approach to predict xylem vulnerability curves based on maximizing long-term average plant fitness, for when maximum water stress stochastically follows a hydrologically driven probability distribution (Fig. 1). We use net carbon gain as a proxy for fitness, defined as the difference between carbon gains from photosynthesis and carbon costs of building new xylem needed for water conduction (33, 47, 48). We assume that the vulnerability curve is derived from individual elements of hydraulic conductance that become embolized at different water potentials, the cumulative sum of elements defining the vulnerability curve. Construction and replacement of conductive xylem entails a carbon cost, with the unit cost of hydraulic conductance increasing with increasing embolism resistance within a species (33), but the way the cost increases could vary across species; the total carbon cost being the sum over all elements. The minimum water potential during the dry season is a random variable drawn from a specified probability distribution (dependent on local climate and plant water use traits), which irreversibly embolizes susceptible xylem elements, reducing water conduction and photosynthetic carbon gain upon water stress release. Embolisms are only removed by complete replacement of xylem at the natural end of the xylem’s functional lifespan, after incurring drought legacy effects on carbon gain. Both total carbon gain and cost depend on the vulnerability curve, with embolism resistance entailing a trade-off between photosynthetic benefits and higher construction costs. The optimal vulnerability curve maximizes the long-term average net carbon gain; thus, we consider trait optimization over evolutionary timescales to imply adaptation to many decades of a stationary water stress probability distribution. Photosynthesis and water transport are scaled per leaf area, which is intuitive for fluxes of carbon and water, naturally integrates differences in plant growth between good and bad years, and avoids assumptions on how xylem cross-section is partitioned among different cell types.

We explore the extent to which xylem hydraulic safety, safety–efficiency trade-offs, water-use efficiency, when the onset and complete loss of xylem function occur, and the likelihood of hydraulic failure can be explained as emergent properties of optimal xylem vulnerability to water stress. We also ask: what factors can explain differences in hydraulic safety between angiosperms and gymnosperms, and how does the likelihood of hydraulic failure depend on natural water stress?

Results

The optimal xylem vulnerability theory predicts S-shaped vulnerability curves composed of three parts (Fig. 1v). At the most negative water potentials (region I), there is no investment in highly embolism resistant xylem because the carbon cost of doing so is greater than the photosynthetic return. There is also no investment in highly vulnerable xylem at the least negative water potentials because it is highly likely that more severe water stress will be experienced, and so these conduits would be rarely functional (region III). In between, optimal investment in xylem that is lost at each water potential occurs when the marginal photosynthetic benefit of xylem, weighted by the likelihood of experiencing that water stress, equals the marginal unit cost increase of building that xylem (region II; Eq. 7; see Methods). The crossover between region I and II defines the point of hydraulic failure (Ψfailure), while the crossover between regions II and III defines the point of first embolism (Ψembolism). The point of first embolism occurs when the relative change in water stress likelihood equals the relative change in the marginal xylem cost (Eq. 8; see SI Appendix). This means that, if there is a most likely water stress, then Ψembolism is less negative, more negative, or equal to the most likely Ψmin, depending on whether xylem costs accelerate, decelerate, or rise linearly at more negative water potentials, respectively. Therefore, native embolism under the most-likely water stress experienced over the xylem lifespan depends on how the cost of building embolism-resistant xylem increases at more negative water potentials.

We used our theory, together with models for photosynthetic carbon gain, the likelihood of water stress severity and the unit cost of xylem of a given embolism resistance, to predict how optimal vulnerability curves depend on plant traits and environmental factors (SI Appendix, Figs. S4 and S5). Emergent optimal vulnerability curves fell into three characteristic classes depending on how the vulnerability curve width and maximum optimally shifted with a lower P50 (Fig. 2). Class 1, coinciding with lower xylem costs (cost parameters c1 and c2, see Methods) and longer photosynthetic seasons (l), exhibited broader vulnerability curves (Ψwidth) and higher ability to transport water per photosynthetic carbon gain (Kmax/Amax), reflecting carbon acquisition benefits (Fig. 2A). Class 2 also had broader curves but had lower water transport capacities and reflected water supply/demand trade-offs (for example, drier modal water stress (ΨM), less frequent rainfall (r), drier air (Δw), and larger difference in water tension across the plant (ΔΨ, MPa, indicative of water loss per ability to transport water) (Fig. 2B). Class 3 exhibited negligible changes in width or maximum capacity and occurred with longer lived xylem (S), reflecting the increased risk of dry conditions over multiple years but with no trade-off in the carbon/water unit costs (Fig. 2C). Consequently, greater embolism resistance commonly co-occurred with embolism spread over a broader range of water potentials, because the point of hydraulic failure typically shifted more than the point of first embolism. These divergent classes highlight the at times contrasting effects that plant traits and the environment can have on adaptive xylem vulnerability and help explain the diversity observed across species.

We investigated the effects of changing levels of site aridity by varying the modal maximum water stress and investigated how optimal embolism resistance, vulnerability curve width, maximum capacity, intrinsic water-use efficiency (iWUE, indicative of photosynthetic carbon gain per water use, μmol mol−1), and probability of hydraulic failure within the xylem lifetime [pfailure(S)] covaried (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Drier conditions coincided with greater median embolism resistance, wider vulnerability curves, smaller maximum xylem capacities and higher minimum water-use efficiencies, in almost all cases (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Interestingly, plants adapted to drier conditions had larger hydraulic safety margins but were also predicted to have a greater probability of hydraulic failure (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6).

We also varied plant traits and environmental conditions at fixed water stress regimes to see their effect on optimal plant traits (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Effects could again be summarized into classes like those for vulnerability curves (Table 1). For the same modal stress, higher xylem cost increases, and a shorter favorable season (Class 1) were all associated with less embolism resistance, narrower vulnerability curves, smaller maximum xylem capacities, higher minimum water-use efficiencies and increased risk of hydraulic failure. Likewise, larger differences in water tension across the plant and a moister atmosphere (Class 2) drove greater embolism resistance, wider vulnerability curves, smaller maximum xylem capacities, lower minimum water-use efficiencies and decreased risk of hydraulic failure. However, the effect of rainfall frequency bifurcated from the other water balance drivers. Longer-lived xylem (Class 3) was again associated with greater embolism resistance but no effect on other optimal traits.

We compared our general predictions against observations from the Xylem Functional Traits (XFT) dataset (11). Without further knowledge of water stress stochasticity in each species, we assumed that observed Ψmin was the best estimate for the modal water stress (ΨM). We also assumed that water stresses where xylem function is reduced by 12% and 88% (P12, and P88, respectively) were the best estimates for the point of first embolism (Ψembolism) and the point of hydraulic failure (Ψfailure), respectively. Observed trends were similar to predictions where data was available (P50 vs. Ψmin angiosperms: r = 0.59, P = 1.29 × 10−85, n = 883; gymnosperms: r = 0.52, P = 4.1 × 10−12, n = 153; Fig. 3 A–D; Ψwidth = Ψembolism – Ψfailure vs. Ψmin angiosperms: r = −0.56, P = 1.2 × 10−36, n = 431; Fig. 3 E–H; iWUE vs. Ψmin angiosperms: r = −0.64, P = 2.8 × 10−7, n = 52; gymnosperms: r = −0.68, P = 2.0 × 10−3, n = 18; Fig. 3 M–P). Larger hydraulic safety margins for the same water stress in our model were predominantly driven by decelerating xylem costs (Fig. 3B) or increasing xylem longevity (Fig. 3D). No relationship between Ψwidth and Ψmin was observed in gymnosperms, potentially consistent with gymnosperms having longer-lived xylem.

We also used the XFT dataset and our theory to test whether the cost of making more embolism resistant xylem appeared to accelerate or not, based on whether the first embolism (P12) occurred before or after the most likely water stress (Ψmin) (Fig. 4). Angiosperm observations were consistent with the cost of embolism resistant xylem accelerating at greater water stresses (Ψmin - P12 < 0, t = −13.9, P = 1.3 × 10−37, n = 480), while gymnosperm observations could be explained by decelerating costs at greater water stress or building xylem to be functional for multiple years (Ψmin - P12 > 0, t = 5.7, P = 5.6 × 10−8, n = 118).

The water potential where 12% of xylem function is lost (P12) was used as a proxy for the point of first embolism, while the observed minimum water potential (Ψmin) was used as a proxy for the most likely water stress.

We tested the prediction that longer lived xylem requires greater embolism resistance for the same water stress for stems and leaves by matching stem and leaf hydraulic data from the XFT dataset with the number of sapwood rings calculated from the BAAD database (49, 50) and leaf longevity data from the GLOPNET dataset (51). Species overlap across datasets was small (11 and 12 species, respectively) and no relationship between xylem longevity and relative hydraulic safety was found (SI Appendix, Fig. S7).

We simulated the effect of a rapidly drying climate by taking optimal vulnerability curves and their associated water stress probability distributions and predicted how the likelihood of hydraulic failure over the xylem lifespan changed if the modal maximum water stress shifted drier by 0.5 MPa, without adaptation to the new water stress regime (nonadapted) (Fig. 5). The probability of hydraulic failure was always higher without adaptation than with adaptation, as expected. For most plant traits, changes in the trait value, for the same water stress, that increased the risk of hydraulic failure in the adapted case also increased the risk in the not adapted case (e.g., the water tension difference across the plant, indicative of water loss per ability to transport water, Fig. 5A). In contrast, longer-lived xylem was associated with lower pfailure when adapted to conditions but corresponded to the higher pfailure when not adapted, predicting that plants with longer-lived xylem are particularly at risk of increased hydraulic failure over the xylem lifespan if conditions become drier faster than adaptation occurs (Fig. 5B).

The predicted higher risk of hydraulic failure in species adapted to drier conditions, despite larger safety margins, revealed that the hydraulic safety margin is not the most natural metric for assessing hydraulic risk. Our stochastic water stress theory shows that, to a first approximation, the risk of hydraulic failure should depend on both the hydraulic safety margin scaled by the modal water stress, and rainfall variability. This is because the spread of likely water stresses, particularly the drier tail, gets broader under more arid climates (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), so the point of hydraulic failure also needs to shift drier to maintain the same likelihood of failure. Discussion

Optimal xylem vulnerability curves emerge from the trade-offs among the photosynthetic benefit of possessing functional xylem, the cost of building embolism-resistant xylem, and the likelihood of water stress severity. Our theory provides a powerful framework, based on long-term plant adaptation, that unifies water stress, xylem vulnerability to water stress, water-use, and hydraulic efficiencies, and the probability of hydraulic failure. It predicts how plant traits and environment affect these variables, and the optimal level of hydraulic adaptation to long-term changes in water stress.

The formal specification of maximum water stress as a nonfixed value (11), but instead a random variable (40) whose distribution depends in part on the environment, is a substantive advance on previous formulations of this problem. Physiologically critical water stresses of first embolism and hydraulic failure are explicitly defined by building xylem from a spectrum of embolism resistance, which is more informative than the use of parameterized vulnerability curves. S-shaped vulnerability curves emerge from the spectrum of xylem embolism resistance, rather than from a function determining average behavior (14). The water stress where embolism first occurs is predicted to be close to the most likely maximum water stress over the xylem lifetime but is modified by how xylem costs scale with embolism resistance and is consistent with typically minimal native embolism in woody plants (41, 42). The predicted water stress where all xylem function is lost and the probability distribution of maximum water stress together naturally define a probability of hydraulic failure. The resulting vulnerability curve is a delicate balance of competing influences that can have opposing effects, explaining some of the surprising diversity in xylem vulnerability seen across vascular plants.

Species experiencing greater maximum water stress under arid climates possess xylem that is both more resistant to embolism (11) and embolizes over a broader range of water stresses (52). Our theory predicts that drier maximum water stress leads to greater embolism resistance in xylem when xylem damage is costly and not rapidly reversible. Under drier conditions, broader vulnerability curves emerge because both the water potential difference across the plant during favorable conditions and the modal midday water potential are relatively conserved across species (40, 53), so that a more negative modal Ψmin is associated with a broader spread of Ψmin (Eq. 16 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). This contrasts with conduit network models, which relate embolism resistance and spread through conduit connectivity (24, 25, 28). Variation in the observed P50 vs. Ψmin relationship can also emerge from differences in plant traits and environment, and from the sampling of the varying Ψmin (40).

How do different hydraulic safety margins emerge in Nature, and why might gymnosperms appear hydraulically safer than angiosperms (11)? Our theory suggests that the largest drivers of hydraulic safety were how xylem cost scaled with embolism resistance, and xylem longevity, suggesting major roles for xylem anatomy and life history, respectively. Gymnosperms are regarded as slower and more conservative in their growth than angiosperms (54), and may need greater embolism resistance because of the cumulative chance of experiencing extreme water stress with longer lived xylem (14), linking xylem vulnerability with the broader fast–slow trait continuum (55). The lack of a relationship between water stress severity and vulnerability curve width in gymnosperms supports this. However, we did not find a relationship between observed xylem longevity and relative hydraulic safety in the limited data available (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), which could reflect declining function of leaves and xylem as they age so that more plant function is carried by younger tissues (50, 56). It could also reflect hydraulic safety being more important for the organism lifespan or for time to reproductive maturity than tissue lifespan, or insufficient data. Angiosperms did exhibit embolism before the maximum stress observed, but this was not the case for gymnosperms. Our theory suggests then that xylem costs for more embolism resistant xylem are accelerating in angiosperms, which is consistent with rapid cost increases to avoid embolism propagation through pits while maintaining the same water transport capacity (57). At less severe xylem tensions, angiosperm vessels may provide cheaper water transport per unit construction cost than tracheids found in gymnosperms because of a larger cross-sectional area relative to wall thickness (22), but their construction becomes rapidly more costly at greater xylem tensions because of the wall reinforcing required to avoid buckling (22, 33, 58, 59). The more specialized torus-margo pit structure in gymnosperms could allow greater marginal embolism resistance gains for the same marginal carbon investments and conductance penalties than the equivalent change in angiosperm pit structure (60). Differences in xylem network segregation and complexity are known to impact xylem vulnerability to water stress (24, 26, 29, 30) and will alter the perceived cost of embolism resistant xylem. Our model did not include refilling, but the ability to refill embolized xylem was not required to reproduce diverse trajectories of hydraulic safety in the model that reflect the data (11, 14). Xylem embolism under freeze-thaw conditions can also drive the necessity of embolism resistant xylem independently of water stress (61) but is not covered by our model.

Our theory has the powerful ability to link water-use and hydraulic efficiency with xylem vulnerability because of the intrinsic link between hydraulic supply and photosynthetic gas exchange. Our model provides a xylem-based interpretation of long-term water-use efficiency (36, 62), correctly predicting that plants adapted to drier conditions should have higher intrinsic water-use efficiency. Our theory predicts that a safety–efficiency trade-off should emerge at the leaf or plant scale, once the effect of photosynthetic scaling is removed, although our model cannot provide insights at the conduit (14) or sapwood level (31, 57, 63). The safety–efficiency trade-off emerges because of the increased cost of embolism-resistant xylem for the same amount of hydraulic conductance, but it will vary with plant traits and the environment. Including photosynthetic scaling and other environmental and trait effects could further enhance our understanding of xylem safety–efficiency trade-offs (32).

An explicit point of hydraulic failure and its associated probability is defined by our theory. This provides an avenue for predicting the likelihood of drought-induced mortality both now and under future climates. Plants adapted to drier water stresses are predicted to be at a greater risk of hydraulic failure in their natural environment, even as hydraulic safety margin increases, because the likelihood of severe water stress becomes greater under drier modal conditions (although rainfall variability also impacts this spread) but optimal xylem embolism resistance does not fully compensate. This may seem counterintuitive, but it summarizes the common ecological understanding that water stress is a strong selective pressure when water is scarce, whereas competition for other resources, such as light, are more dominant when water is plentiful. It should not be confused with artificial drought experiments, where mesic species are at greater risk of hydraulic failure than drought adapted species when exposed to the same water stress. Interestingly, longer-lived xylem is disproportionately more vulnerable to hydraulic failure during the xylem lifespan than shorter-lived xylem, if climate shifts to a more arid state faster than acclimation. Long lived xylem carries greater risk in a drying climate because the chance of experiencing dry conditions increases with the time of exposure. Higher risk of hydraulic failure in drought-adapted species is supported empirically, as are higher increases in mortality under changing environments in species with wood anatomies with slow xylem turnover rates (64). Rapid xylem turnover might then be evolutionarily advantageous under changing environments, even if the shift is wetter, because it builds a lower cumulative risk when shifting to drier conditions; and avoids the growth penalty of investment in more-embolism-resistant xylem that long-lived xylem necessitates. Our hydraulic safety index should represent hydraulic risk better than the common hydraulic safety margin and helps resolve why previously unexplained divergent risks of mortality with hydraulic safety margin have been observed (7, 13), depending on whether changes in embolism resistance or Ψmin dominate, or whether rainfall variability differs across sites.

Important questions emerging from our theory include: Does longer lifespan entail greater hydraulic safety? Does the point of first embolism occur before or after the most likely water stress? Does hydraulic safety index and rainfall variability predict hydraulic failure risk? We tested some of the wealth of predictions emergent from the model using available data, but further tests require long-term studies of plant water stress under natural conditions to better characterize the stochastic nature of maximum water stress, coupled with precise measurement of xylem vulnerability to water stress. More measurements of xylem functional longevity and time to plant maturity are needed in species that already possess hydraulic data to better test the impact of lifespan on hydraulic safety. Long-term rainfall data, which is readily available (65), may be a useful proxy for the statistical spread of water stress at locations where xylem embolism resistance have been measured and could provide a direct test of the theory. Datasets of drought induced mortality (66) could also provide a test of the likelihood of hydraulic failure in conjunction with embolism resistance data.

Our theory is both general and relatively simple, but it has limitations. We phrased our theory in terms of maximum water stress because it integrates several plant and environmental drivers, and data are available. However, plants influence maximum water stress and its impact on productivity, through different controls of water loss (both stomatal and canopy loss), investment in water storage tissues, rooting depth, and vegetative timing. The theory does not directly distinguish between different strategies (67, 68) and their trade-offs between carbon gain during water stress and embolism resistance (69, 70), but could be extended and improved by including dynamic water loss over the short- and long-term (48, 71–73), and coupling this to plant water storages and available groundwater (36, 39, 73). For tractability, we also ignored hydraulic segmentation (74, 75), xylem network complexity (24, 26), within-plant variability in embolism resistance (76, 77), and contributions that reversible extraxylary components may play in hydraulic conductance (78, 79), all of which can protect parts of the plant from the effects of more severe water stress and alter overall xylem costs, although there are limited data across species. Dynamic changes can also trigger rapid hydraulic failure (28, 52, 80–82), while mortality may occur before all xylem conductance is lost (45, 46). Drought stress timing is also important in determining productivity, while we also prescribed xylem longevity but it could be under selection from water stress (55). Finally, neither xylem costs nor probability distributions of minimum water potential are well known and require much better characterization, although theoretical approaches provide useful insights (14, 33, 83, 84). Despite these limitations, the theory provides a powerful framework for explaining observed trends in xylem embolism resistance in terms of optimal adaptation to stochastic water stress and provides predictions of xylem vulnerability and the likelihood of hydraulic failure both now and under future climates.

Methods

Theory.

The aim was to find the optimal way that the xylem ability to conduct water (hydraulic conductance per leaf area, K, mmol m−2 s−1 MPa−1) declines with increasing water stress (more negative water potential, Ψ), i.e., the vulnerability curve K(Ψ), that maximized the expected long-term net carbon gain. We assumed there was a carbon cost of building xylem, after which the plant experienced a maximum water stress (Ψmin), from a probability distribution, that caused embolism in all xylem elements susceptible to the water stress. The reduction in K caused a reduction in the photosynthetic carbon gain possible when conditions improve. Net carbon gain was the difference between the photosynthetic carbon gain and the carbon costs of building new xylem (Cx, μmol m−2 s−1). The wet-dry cycle continued over many seasonal cycles, with embolisms only removed with xylem replacement at the end of its lifespan (S), so that the best estimate of long-term net carbon gain was the expected value (G, μmol m−2 s−1). The probability density function for the minimum water potential over S seasons [f(Ψmin)] was used as a conservative approximation for legacy effects across multiple years when xylem lifespan (e.g., leaf longevity or multiple sapwood rings) was greater than 1 y. Ψmin was the only environmental variable considered to change across seasons.

Hydraulic conductance was assumed to consist of an ensemble of parallel conducting elements, each embolizing at a certain water potential (Ψe). K(Ψ) was the cumulative sum of all elements that embolize at water potentials more negative than Ψ (that is, all unembolized elements); while all elements for which Ψe > Ψ do not contribute to K, so that[3]KΨ=∫-∞ΨdKΨedΨedΨe=∫-∞ΨK′ΨedΨe,

where the “prime” notation has been used as a shorthand to denote the derivative.

We assumed a carbon cost of making xylem and replacing it at the end of its lifespan (Cx, μmol m−2 s−1), expressed as a rate so that construction costs were amortized over the lifespan of the xylem:[4]Cx=∫0-∞c(Ψe)K′ΨedΨe,

where c(Ψe) is the carbon cost per unit hydraulic conductance. For stems, we assumed that xylem longevity was the natural transition from sapwood to heartwood.

Xylem consists of several cell types, including those used for water conduction, and carbohydrate and water storage. Cell type proportions vary across species and between early and late wood, and correlates with climate and plant traits, including vulnerability to water stress (85–88). We assumed carbon costs represent only xylem involved in water conduction; carbon for other xylem tissue derives from the net carbon gain pool. Sapwood space filling implies a trade-off in the proportion of cell types per sapwood area (63). Our model avoids this by scaling hydraulic conductance per leaf area, not per sapwood area, so trade-offs are ameliorated by varying sapwood production for the same leaf area. The cost of tyloses to block xylem at the end of its lifespan are included in xylem costs.

We assumed the mean rate of photosynthetic carbon gain over the xylem lifespan (As, μmol m−2 s−1) depended on K, and K was irreversibly reduced because of embolism until xylem was replaced, the largest suppression occurring at the minimum water potential experienced over the xylem lifetime (Ψmin). Because Ψmin was assumed to vary randomly, the expected rate of carbon gain from photosynthesis (Ā, μmol m−2 s−1) was the appropriate measure for long-term photosynthetic carbon gain:[5]A¯=∫-∞0AsKΨminfΨmindΨmin,

General Solution for K.

Optimal K was found by maximizing G using calculus of variations and must satisfy two additional constraints that have physical meaning for the plant: 1) K ≥ 0, because hydraulic conductance cannot be negative, and defined the water potential of hydraulic failure; 2) K’ ≥ 0, because embolism increases at more negative water potentials, and defined the water potential where embolism first occurs.

When constraints are satisfied, optimal K at each Ψ occurs when the marginal photosynthetic benefit of hydraulic conductance, weighted by the likelihood of experiencing that water stress, equals the marginal unit cost increase of building xylem. When the unconstrained solution violated K ≥ 0 it was replaced by K = 0, and when K’ ≥ 0 was violated the unconstrained solution was replaced by the constant value of K at which K’ = 0 (89).

The least negative water potential at which Eq. 7 predicts K = 0 identifies the point of hydraulic failure (Ψfailure), while the most negative water potential at which dK/dΨ = 0 identifies the point of first embolism (Ψembolism). If f is a peaked distribution, so that there is a most likely Ψmin (where f’ = 0), then Ψembolism is greater than, less than, or equal to the most likely Ψmin, depending on whether construction costs accelerate (c’’ > 0), decelerate (c’’ < 0), or rise linearly (c’’ = 0) at more negative water potentials, respectively. A more negative ΨM (from either a longer, drier season, or more anisohydric traits) produces a broader spread distribution with smaller maximum f, for the same relative rainfall regime, because the tail of the distribution is approximately exponential with characteristic water potential of ΨM/ln r (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Similarly, minimum water potential is relatively tighter when soaking rainfalls are more frequent, for the same ΨM (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). S seasons of active xylem shifts the modal minimum water potential experienced over that time by a factor related to ln S/ln r. However, the tail of the distribution remains unaltered, so that the distribution is (within the truncation constant) transposed to drier water potentials (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). The distribution is consistent with observations in Martínez-Vilalta et al. (40).

The probability of hydraulic failure in 1 y [pfailure(1), Eq. 1] emerged from Eqs. 14 and 15 by ignoring the truncation constant and using P88 as an estimate for the point of hydraulic failure, with the new hydraulic safety index (HSI88) defined by Eq. 2. In our theory, ρ = ln r and so depends on rainfall statistics.

Cost of Xylem.

We expressed the cost of conducting xylem as a rate, amortizing construction costs over the xylem lifespan. How xylem costs vary with longevity is not well known but is required because we considered the effect of xylem lifespans on optimal xylem vulnerability to water stress. Greater longevity requires greater construction costs in leaves, but less than proportionally more (51), but the direct relationship between construction costs and longevity is obscured by construction for photosynthetic capacity (90). We assumed that it costs proportionally more to build long-lived xylem than it does to produce short-lived xylem (91, 92).

How the cost per unit K changes with water potential of embolism (c’) is also unknown. We used an exponential equation because it can be parameterized to give both concave up and concave down forms, and it complemented the exponential tail of the probability distribution:[17]-c′Ψ=c1e-c2Ψ,

where c1 is the cost slope at zero water potential and c2 describes how quickly costs relatively increase (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Differences in cost arising from different xylem anatomy at the pit level are represented through the c2 parameter, while c1 will also be impacted by this and by different investments in xylem relative to leaf area. Differences in xylem network connectivity will also appear as a different cost scaling, even though the model is designed with no transverse connectivity.

Model Testing.

Model simulations were performed using Python. Optimal vulnerability curves were calculated from the minimum water potential probability density function (Eq. 16) and xylem cost function (Eq. 17). The unconstrained optimal vulnerability curve (Eq. 9) was first calculated over a sufficiently large water potential range (−12.0 to −ΔΨ MPa for example vulnerability curves, −30.0 to −0.5 MPa for comparisons with data) in steps of 0.01 MPa. The constrained vulnerability curve was then calculated stepwise from more negative to less negative values by 1) checking whether the next point of the vulnerability curve increased in value (k’ ≥ 0 constraint) and setting the new value to the previous value if this was not the case, and 2) if the previous constraint was satisfied by the unconstrained solution, checking whether the unconstrained solution was positive and setting the new value of the vulnerability curve to zero if not. The water potentials for hydraulic failure (Ψfailure), first embolism (Ψembolism), and loss of half-maximum conductance (P50) were all found by simple searches for when the constrained vulnerability curve first became positive, when the unconstrained vulnerability curve first decreased in value, and when the constrained vulnerability curve was first larger than half its maximum value, respectively. Ψwidth was calculated from Eq. 13. Optimal vulnerability curves normalized for photosynthesis (K/Amax) were calculated from Eqs. 9 and 10. Maximum K/Amax (Kmax/Amax) was found directly from the optimal vulnerability curve. Minimum intrinsic water use efficiency (iWUEmin) was calculated from Eq. 12 using kmax from the constrained vulnerability curve. Probability of hydraulic failure [pfailure(S)] was calculated from Eqs. 14 and 15.

Example vulnerability curves were calculated to demonstrate the impact of plant traits and environmental conditions on the shape of resulting vulnerability curves. Traits and environmental conditions varied were c1, c2, ΔΨ, ΨM, S, r, Δw, and l. Parameters were set at a default value unless the parameter was actively being varied. Default and varying parameter values used in the simulation are shown in SI Appendix, Table S1.

Simulated trajectories of P50, Pdiff, Kmax/Amax, iWUE, and pfailure with changing modal minimum water potential were calculated for the same parameters as the example vulnerability curves (except for ΨM), to compare the effect of each against observed species trends. Trajectories were calculated for ΨM between −8.0 and −0.5 MPa in steps of 0.5 MPa. Parameters were set at a default value unless the parameter was actively being varied. Default and varying parameter values used in the simulation are shown in SI Appendix, Table S1.

Using the optimal trajectories above, we compared the scenario of complete adaption to the water potential distribution with a scenario of climate shifting to a drier state faster than adaptation could occur, the latter being where ΨM of the distribution became drier by 0.5 MPa but the traits still being those optimal for the original ΨM. Optimal Ψfailure from the above trajectories for ΔΨ and S were used to calculate pfailure(S) for when ΨM became drier by 0.5 MPa without optimal adaptation. pfailure(S) was calculated from Eq. 14 and the shifted cumulative density function from Eq. 15, with the new ΨM set at ΨM – 0.5 MPa. ΔΨ and S were chosen as they represent different water balance and life history strategies, respectively.

Hydraulic Data Comparison.

Model-data comparisons used hydraulic trait data from an updated version of the global Xylem Functional Traits dataset (11). Hydraulic traits for stem measurements of plant hydraulics were used because they represented the most abundant measurements. iWUE was calculated from δ13C values (93), assuming ambient CO2 concentration of 400 μmol mol−1. Empirical Ψmin was assumed to best correspond to the modal Ψmin because many studies only collected data for one dry season, while P12 and P88 were assumed to best correspond with Ψembolism and Ψfailure, respectively. Correlations between observed plant hydraulic traits were assessed by Pearson r value using the function pearsonr in the scipy stats package of Python. Whether Ψmin – P12 was less than or greater than 0 in angiosperms and gymnosperms, respectively, was assessed by one-sided one-sample t tests using the function ttest_1samp in the scipy stats package of Python. Hydraulic safety index (HSI88) was calculated using Eq. 2.

Comparisons between xylem longevity and hydraulic safety at the stem level were done using sapwood area, diameter at breast height (dbh) and tree age data from the BAAD database (49) to calculate the number of sapwood rings (50) as a measure for xylem longevity. The number of sapwood rings was calculated from sapwood area by assuming constant ring width across both sapwood and heartwood for the age of the stem. The number of sapwood rings increases with tree size and age, so we correlated the number of sapwood rings against dbh for each species and calculated the number of sapwood rings at a dbh of 20 cm. We then compared this against P50/Ψmin for stem data from the XFT dataset where possible, using species means for P50 and Ψmin.

Comparisons between leaf longevity and hydraulic safety used species mean leaf or petiole P50 and Ψmin data from the XFT dataset to calculate P50/Ψmin and matched this with species mean data for leaf longevity from the GLOPNET dataset (51).
