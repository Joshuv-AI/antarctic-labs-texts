Management of retroperitoneal perforated descending duodenal diverticulum using de pezzer catheter drainage combined with nutritional jejunostomy: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Limin Guo, Shuk Ying WONG, Dongdong Zhang, Lei Guo, Simin Chen, Peng Guo
Published: 2025

Introduction

Gastrointestinal diverticula are outpouchings of the gastrointestinal wall and can occur anywhere in the gastrointestinal tract. Duodenal diverticulum (DD) is the second most prevalent form of gastrointestinal diverticula, following colonic diverticula [1, 2]. The prevalence of DD can be as high as 22% [3]. Extraluminal DD are asymptomatic in 95% of cases and are typically discovered incidentally during diagnostic investigations for other conditions [4].

Duodenal diverticula can be either congenital or acquired, with the latter further classified as primary or secondary types and can also be either extraduodenal (most common) or on occasion intraduodenal. Primary diverticula are always found distal to the first part of the duodenum, consisting only of mucosal and serosal layers, and are believed to be pulsion diverticula. In contrast, secondary diverticula include all layers of the duodenal wall and are thought to result from traction as the result of peptic ulcer disease. Primary diverticula can be single or multiple (20%), with 95% occurring on the concave border of the second and third parts of the duodenum. Typically, they are usually asymptomatic and require surgery only when complications arise [5]. Perforation of DD is a rare but potentially fatal occurrence [1]. The diagnosis of the retroperitoneal duodenal perforation is particularly challenging for surgeons and radiologists, owing to the hidden location of the retroperitoneal space, delayed clinical presentation, and obscure findings during clinical examination. The rarity of such cases means there are no established guidelines for managing perforated DD [6]. Additionally, a perforated posterior DD presents a significant surgical challenge due to delayed diagnosis and the associated risk of developing a duodenal fistula after primary repair. In the existing literature, there are a limited number of cases involving simple surgical drainage for DD perforation, resulting in subsequent complications such as duodenal fistula [19, 21, 23]. We could not find any documented cases of the use of a De Pezzer catheter in the literature for a perforated DD, but these catheters have been used for perforations elsewhere in the gastrointestinal tract [26–28]. We present a case of posterior perforation of descending DD, diagnosed via computed tomography and successfully treated with operative placement of periduodenal drainage catheters and intramural decompression and drainage of the duodenal lumen using a De Pezzer catheter and nasogastric tube, combined with nutritional jejunostomy. This case has been reported in line with the SCARE Criteria (include citation) [7].

Case report

A 78-year-old Chinese male patient presented with epigastric pain after excessive eating the day before admission, accompanied by nausea but no vomiting or fever. The patient had a history of hypertension and had undergone surgery for a right renal cyst. On May 26, 2021, the patient presented to the emergency department of our hospital and a plain abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan incidentally revealed a DD at the corresponding anatomical site. There was no history of smoking, alcohol consumption, or abnormal health conditions in first-degree relatives. After admission on June 12, 2024, relevant examinations were completed, revealing that hemoglobin was 97 g/L. Tumor markers CEA, AFP, CA19-9, and CA72-4 were all within normal ranges. Body temperature measured 37.1 ℃, pulse rate was recorded at 94 beats per minute, respiration rate was observed to be 21 breaths per minute, and blood pressure reading showed 137/67 mm Hg. Physical examination revealed a soft abdomen with tenderness in the epigastric region and mild rebound pain. No palpable mass was detected in the abdominal area. A contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis revealed extraluminal air in the retroperitoneal duodenum. Additionally, a fluid density shadow was observed in the abdominal cavity (Fig. 1).

The patient is an elderly individual presenting with signs of abdominal irritation. CT scan reveals the presence of free gas within the abdominal cavity. Based on the clinical findings, the diagnosis is digestive tract perforation. In light of this, surgical exploration is deemed necessary. The patient was placed in a supine position and intubated under general anesthesia. Laparoscopic exploration revealed no evident empyema or effusion, with a small quantity of pus observed beneath the liver. Laparoscopic dissection of the descending duodenum using the Kocher maneuver exposed the posterior wall and mesentery, revealing a localized encapsulated abscess cavity containing a fecalith. The fecalith had a diameter of 5 mm, while the perforation cavity measured 8 mm in diameter. We converted to a laparotomy by the challenges encountered during laparoscopy. A mushroom-type (De Pezzer) catheter was inserted into the duodenal lumen through the site of perforation in the duodenal wall, accompanied by two additional drainage tubes for drainage purposes. The patient underwent successful surgical intervention and was discharged on the 12th post-operative day without any complications. Enteral nutrition support was initiated on the third postoperative day, after the patient experienced the first flatus and defecation. Upper gastrointestinal radiography revealed an unobstructed gastrointestinal tract with no evidence of digestive fluid leakage. Consequently, upon discharge, the gastric tube had been removed, the drainage fluid from De Pezzer catheter was less than 200 ml/day, and a continuous decrease in drainage fluid was observed from external counterpart drainage tubes. A repeat upper gastrointestinal radiography was scheduled for follow-up in the three months after surgery. The inflammatory indicators showed a gradual decline (Fig. 5). There is no leakage of digestive fluid, the two counterpart drainage tubes were removed gradually, followed by the withdrawal of De Pezzer catheter. Discussion

The duodenum serves as the common outlet for secretions from the stomach, pancreas, bile, and duodenal mucosa. These digestive fluids can interact with each other in the duodenum, potentially causing detrimental effects on wounds, complicating the management of duodenal injuries. Perforation of DD is a relatively complex acute abdomen complication in clinical practice, with a low incidence and limited experience [8]. Current medical textbooks lack detailed descriptions of this condition, making preoperative diagnosis challenging and resulting in a lack of a standardized treatment approach during surgery and slow postoperative recovery. Consequently, duodenal posterior wall perforation has a high mortality rate, necessitating careful attention to its diagnosis, surgical approach selection, and perioperative management.

Pain is the most common symptom and has been described as sharp, severe, gnawing, or cramping. It is usually localized in the epigastrium or right upper quadrant and may radiate to the back [9]. Over time, retroperitoneal effusion and gas can extend to the right anterior pararenal space and right iliac fossa, resulting in “shifting” right lower abdominal pain that may be misdiagnosed as an acute appendicitis. In rare cases of large perforations, a significant amount of air may pass down to the scrotum, resulting in pneumoscrotum or scrotal abscess [10]. The findings on a standing abdominal radiograph are typically nonspecific, whereas an abdominal CT scan can reveal the presence of retroperitoneal gas, as observed in this patient’s abdominal CT examination, accompanied by inflammatory changes. Therefore, for selected patients with acute abdominal pain, routine abdominal CT examinations should be performed along with a careful history and physical examination to improve diagnostic accuracy. Additionally, Sergio and colleagues summarized sonographic findings of duodenal perforation [11]. They found a strong echo area with an acoustic shadow between the liver and right kidney, but no surrounding area around the right kidney (referred to as “veiled right kidney sign”), which also supports the diagnosis of retroperitoneal pneumatosis. Conservative treatment cannot be chosen solely based on the mild and localized nature of the patient’s peritonitis. This patient exhibited no apparent signs of peritoneal irritation syndrome and maintained stable vital signs, while a localized abscess formation was observed intraoperatively, resulting in the need for a complex operative treatment and delayed postoperative recovery.

Non-operative management can be considered for certain well-selected patients with stable vital signs and prompt diagnosis. Surgical intervention is indicated if conservative management fails or if perforation leads to generalized peritonitis or poorly-tolerated sepsis [4]. Due to the unique nature of duodenal perforation, selecting the appropriate surgical methods remains challenging [12]. For anterior wall perforations, primary repair, limited resection, jejunal serosal patching, enteric drainage with a jejunal loop or Roux limb of jejunum are usually considered. For large perforations, a similar use of a De Pezzer or Malecot catheter can be beneficial externally control the perforation. In rare cases a true duodenal diversion/diverticulization may be necessary. However, for posterior wall perforations, a comprehensive assessment of factors such as location and size of the perforation, surrounding tissue conditions, and the patient’s overall health is necessary to determine the most appropriate surgical approach. This may include endoscopic drainage, diverticulectomy, duodenal diversion, or gastro-enteric anastomosis for each individual case [3, 13–16]. Farné et al. [17] recently reviewed the available literature and now reported 210 cases of DD perforation. Referring to a systematic review conducted by Knapp and colleagues [18] in 2022, which encompassed nearly a decade of research, we present comprehensive treatment information for seven patients diagnosed with DD perforation who underwent damage control techniques involving drainage and irrigation (Table 2).

A Kocher maneuver is necessary to gain access to the diverticulum on the posterior wall of the duodenum. Complete kocherization (mobilization) of the duodenum must be mandatory, even in the presence of obvious injury on its anterior wall [24]. As a result of the thin wall of the duodenum, and limited healing capacity compared to the stomach and small intestine, severe peridiverticular inflammation and fragility are common concerns. Additionally, digestive juices in the duodenum further deteriorate the wound. The exposure to a significant amount of gastric juice, bile, and pancreatic juice increases pressure within the duodenum. These factors significantly contribute to an elevated risk of leakage following the repair of a DD perforation. Therefore, surgical intervention for posterior DD perforations should not only focus on repairing the wound but also emphasize proper drainage and decompression of both periduodenal and intestinal cavities in order to minimize occurrences of intestinal fistula. The perforation of DD has a large diameter and was accompanied by severe local inflammation. Consequently, conventional drainage or local excision is not deemed appropriate.

De Pezzer catheter, with its enlarged mushroom head, can play a role in preventing prolapse and sealing the stump of the perforation. For better drainage, the nipple of the catheter is partially cut and, if necessary, its side holes are enlarged [26]. In our center’s clinical practice, it has been utilized for procedures such as gastrostomy, drainage of duodenal stump leakage, and transanal rectal decompression. Meanwhile, it has also been documented in the literature as a method for anorectal abscesses drainage [27]– [28]. During the operation, a De Pezzer catheter and a nasogastric tube were inserted into the duodenal cavity, while two external counterpart drainage tubes were placed to ensure optimal and unobstructed surgical area drainage. Additionally, a jejunal feeding tube was introduced for enteral nutrition support. Early postoperative enteral nutrition treatment can effectively maintain the stability of the intestinal internal environment and protect the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier [25]. We utilize an amino acid-based enteral nutrition formula due to the absence of essential digestive enzymes. This formulation offers a pre-digested alternative compared to more conventional enteral feeding solutions, which require further enzymatic breakdown for nutrient absorption. The placement of counterpart drainage tubes serves two purposes: firstly, it allows for irrigation in case of leakage around the De Pezzer catheter or inadequate drainage of surrounding abscess cavities; secondly, the gradual reduction of drainage fluid in the counterpart drainage tubes, along with the absence of leakage observed during contrast enterography, indicates unobstructed and sufficient drainage.

In this case, daily abdominal cavity irrigation was performed using 3000 mL of sterile normal saline. The actual irrigation and drainage volumes were recorded on a daily basis to ensure effective outflow of the irrigation fluid. The patient’s vital signs and abdominal symptoms were closely monitored throughout the treatment period. The patient was discharged with the drainage tube in place once the following discharge criteria were met: stable body temperature, resumption of oral intake, normalization of inflammatory markers, clear drainage fluid, and a daily drainage volume of less than 20 mL. Subsequently, after removal of the counterpart drainage tubes, the De Pezzer catheter can be withdrawn. This multifunctional approach simultaneously facilitates decompression, efficient drainage, and promotes wound healing.

Although this case report presents valuable insights into surgical management approaches and clinical outcomes, it is subject to the following limitations: First, the sample size is limited in generalizability. As the report focuses on a single 78-year-old male patient, it cannot be extrapolated to represent broader patient populations, such as females, younger individuals, or those with different comorbidities. Second, the absence of a control group and long-term follow-up data restricts the ability to evaluate the efficacy and complication rates in comparison to alternative treatment modalities, such as simple suture repair or conservative management. Furthermore, no postoperative assessments regarding intestinal fistula recurrence, nutritional status, or quality of life were included. The report only notes the absence of complications at the time of discharge. Third, the conclusions drawn from a solitary successful case are insufficient to establish a robust level of evidence, and the proposed approach may not be applicable to all clinical scenarios, such as asymptomatic small perforations. Future studies should aim to include multi-center case series, provide long-term follow-up data, clarify surgical indications and postoperative protocols, and incorporate comparative analyses with other treatment strategies. In conclusion, while this report outlines a technically feasible approach, further research is necessary to validate its applicability across diverse clinical settings.

Conclusion

Compared with ordinary drainage tubes, the De Pezzer catheter placed in the duodenal cavity can be properly fixed and fully drain a large amount of fluid in the duodenum. The combined utilization of De Pezzer catheter, nasogastric tube, and extra-duodenal counterpart drainage, along with enteral nutrition support via a jejunal feeding tube, adhered to the principles of damage control and yielded favorable clinical outcomes.
