Metabolic and renal factors associated with plasma phosphorylated tau analytes used to detect Alzheimer's disease pathology
Authors: Kao Lee Yang, Yue Ma, Gilda Ennis, Erin Jonaitis, Ramiro Eduardo Rea Reyes, Sterling C. Johnson, Nicholas J. Ashton, Kaj Blennow, Henrik Zetterberg, Barbara B Bendlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe examined the extent to which insulin resistance (IR), type 2 diabetes mellitus, obesity, and kidney function are associated with plasma phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) concentrations (p‐tau181, p‐tau217, p‐tau231) in the same cohort and the contribution of these factors on amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) concordance and longitudinal p‐tau levels.MethodsWe analyzed data from 287 participants using partial Spearman rho, Mann–Whitney U, receiver operating characteristic analysis, and linear mixed models.ResultsConcordance between plasma p‐tau217 and amyloid PET was not influenced by IR or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Accounting for eGFR improved concordance of p‐tau181 and p‐tau231 with amyloid PET. Age, binarized waist‐to‐hip ratio, and amyloid status were associated with all longitudinal p‐tau concentrations, and eGFR associated with longitudinal p‐tau231.DiscussionMetabolic factors may influence the interpretation of longitudinal plasma p‐tau concentrations and kidney function may influence amyloid PET concordance for p‐tau181 and p‐tau231, potentially necessitating analyte‐specific adjustments or considerations in clinical interpretation.

INTRODUCTION

Plasma measurements of phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) at amino acid residues 181, 217, and 231 are among the leading p‐tau biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease (AD) as they are strongly concordant with amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) assessments, 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 and can be used to support AD dementia diagnosis. 1 , 2 , 4 , 5 However, conditions such as insulin resistance (IR), type 2 diabetes mellites (T2DM), obesity, and kidney function may impact plasma levels by altering tau phosphorylation, 6 , 7 , 8 disrupting blood–brain barrier integrity, 9 or impairing protein clearance, 10 , 11 and may impact the interpretation of plasma‐based biomarkers. Indeed, prior studies have shown that renal function affects the interpretation of plasma p‐tau181 and p‐tau217. 5 , 11 , 12 , 13 IR has been linked to altered plasma biomarker profiles 14 and T2DM has been associated with plasma amyloid and total tau. 15 While obesity has mixed associations across plasma biomarkers, 15 it has been shown to impact plasma p‐tau217 cutoff optimization. 12 Less is known about whether these factors differ systematically across leading p‐tau analytes. As such, an analysis of analyte performance within the same cohort could elucidate whether physiological influence on interpretation is shared among leading p‐tau analytes.

Here we sought to address this gap and determine the extent to which IR, T2DM, obesity, and kidney function are associated with plasma p‐tau (181, 217, and 231) biomarker concentrations, and determine the extent to which accounting for these potential confounders improves concordance with amyloid PET. For a subset of individuals with serial plasma measurements, we also investigated the relationships of these metabolic and renal factors with the mean level of p‐tau analytes. We hypothesized that IR, T2DM, obesity, and kidney function would be associated with plasma p‐tau analytes and that accounting for these would improve the concordance between plasma p‐tau and brain amyloid burden as measured with PET.

METHODS

Participants

This analysis drew from a subsample of participants enrolled in the Wisconsin Registry for Alzheimer's Prevention (WRAP) study 16 with available serial plasma data for all p‐tau analytes: p‐tau181, p‐tau217, and p‐tau231 (n = 425). Of these participants, 5 were excluded for missing insulin and glucose data (to calculate IR values), and 133 were excluded for having an amyloid PET scan that was acquired > 2.5 years from the plasma acquisition for a final sample size of 287 participants. All study procedures were approved by the University of Wisconsin–Madison's Institutional Review Board. Written consent was obtained from all participants.

Plasma and amyloid PET acquisition

All participants underwent a blood draw after 8 to 12 hours of fasting. Plasma p‐tau181 and p‐tau231 concentrations were measured using single molecule array (Simoa) assays developed at the University of Gothenburg on HD‐X instruments (Quanterix). 1 , 2 Plasma p‐tau217 was measured using the ALZPath pTau217 Simoa assay on Quanterix HD‐X as previously described. 4 Brain amyloid was quantified through [C‐11]Pittsburgh compound B (PiB) PET scans. Pet scans were processed using an in‐house pipeline and global PiB distribution volume ratio (DVR; Logan graphical analysis, cerebellar gray matter reference region) was derived by averaging across eight bilateral regions of interest. Participants were dichotomized into amyloid beta (Αβ) positive versus negative groups based on a previously derived in‐house cutoff of PiB DVR > 1.19. 17

Metabolic and renal measurements

All blood measurements were collected and analyzed at the University of Wisconsin Hospital and Clinics Laboratory (Madison, WI). Fasting blood insulin and glucose measurements were assayed using standardized methods and have been described previously. 18 Briefly, serum insulin was assayed on an ADVIA Centaur XP Immunoassay System (Siemens Corporation) using a chemiluminescent immunoassay. Plasma glucose was assayed on the Siemens Dimension Vista System (Siemens Corporation) using a hexokinase method as part of an overall metabolic panel. 18 Finally, plasma creatinine was measured using an enzymatic colorimetric assay on an Abbott Alinity platform (Abbott Diagnostics).

IR

IR was assessed using the updated homeostatic model assessment of IR (HOMA2‐IR) and z scored. HOMA2‐IR 19 is an improved version of the original HOMA‐IR 20 metric because it accounts for peripheral insulin secretion variations that may be influenced by hepatic function. 21

Diabetes status

Diabetes status (positive or negative) at all visits was determined using fasting blood glucose ≥ 126 (mg/dL) and/or medication usage to treat T2DM (e.g., Metformin). The HOMA model was derived from normoglycemic and mildly diet‐treated (not medication‐treated) individuals with T2DM. 20 Given that T2DM was defined with medication usage, IR was not calculated for individuals with T2DM at initial visit or at follow‐up visits when individuals reported developing T2DM to avoid potential confounding.

Obesity

Body mass index (BMI) has been commonly used as a metric of obesity. 22 However, the Lancet Diabetes and Endocrinology Commission on Clinical Obesity published guidelines in 2025 stating that BMI use be restricted to population screening purposes and advised that central measures, such as waist‐to‐hip ratio (WHR), be used to accurately assess excess adiposity (Rubino et al. 23 ). As such, for this study, we calculated both general (BMI) and central (WHR) obesity measurements. We generated binarized classifications for BMI and WHR based on established cutoffs. BMI was classified as obese (≥ 30 kg/m2) or non‐obese (< 30 kg/m2), and WHR was classified as high (men: > 0.90, women: > 0.85) or normal (men: ≤ 0.90, women: ≤ 0.85). 23

Kidney function

Kidney function was assessed using the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), derived from serum creatinine levels (mg/dL), according to the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease—Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD‐EPI) equation. 24 We computed the eGFR for the subset of individuals in this study that had creatinine values at the time of plasma data collection (n = 73). For the subset with eGFR data, 37 individuals met the criteria for mildly decreased kidney function (eGFR 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m2). However, because no individual met the criteria for chronic kidney disease (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2), 24 we used eGFR analytically to indicate normal to mildly decreased kidney function on a continuous scale with lower eGFR values indicating worse kidney function and higher values indicating better kidney function. 24

Measurement schedule

Plasma p‐tau analytes were the primary outcome measures with 142 individuals having a single measurement, while others had up to four measurements available. For those with longitudinal data, plasma measurements were acquired 3.43 ± 0.77 years apart (mean ± standard deviation [SD]), with the shortest time interval being 1.78 years and the longest interval being 6.78 years. Given that not every available plasma p‐tau datapoint had a corresponding PET scan and/or available glucose and insulin data, this spacing in years between plasma data measurements does not reflect the fixed 2‐year WRAP schedule. 16 All HOMA2‐IR, WHR, BMI, assessments for T2DM, and creatinine measurements were acquired at the time of plasma measurement, except for amyloid PET for which we used the scan acquired closest to the plasma measurement (mean ± SD time interval: 0.81 ± 0.77 years).

Statistical analysis

Cross‐sectional analysis

Cross‐sectional analyses were performed using data from the time of the first plasma measurement. To evaluate the relationship between p‐tau analyte levels among binarized variables (diabetes status, BMI, and WHR), we first assessed normality of data distribution and homogeneity of variances. A Shapiro–Wilk test showed that all three p‐tau analytes (p‐tau181, p‐tau217, and p‐tau231) significantly deviated from normality, indicating violation of the normality assumption for parametric tests. Next, a Levene test showed that all p‐tau analytes met the assumption for equal variances, except for p‐tau217 among the BMI groups. Given these results, we compared p‐tau analytes across binarized groups using the non‐parametric Mann–Whitney U test to assess median differences in p‐tau concentration between groups. For continuous variables HOMA2‐IR and eGFR, we used partial Spearman rho correlations to test the strength and direction of relationships with p‐tau analytes at baseline measurements adjusting for age. Any factor that indicated a weak or non‐existent relationship (P > 0.05) in the prior described analyses was excluded from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.

We next performed ROC analysis to evaluate how well each plasma p‐tau biomarker can correctly classify brain amyloid PET positivity when accounting for metabolic and renal factors. To perform ROC analysis, we first conducted logistic regression models predicting Αβ positive or negative (Αβ±) PET status not accounting for IR or eGFR significantly related to p‐tau analytes (Model 1), accounting for IR (Model 2), or eGFR (Model 3). Model 1 included p‐tau (181, 217, or 231), age at plasma acquisition, and sex as predictors to classify Αβ ± PET. Model 2 included all Model 1 predictors and IR. Model 3 included all Model 1 predictors and eGFR. Predicted probabilities of being amyloid PET positive were obtained from these models. These estimates were then used as a continuous predictor to derive area under the ROC curve (AUC) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to assess model discrimination.

2.5.2 Longitudinal analysis

To examine the longitudinal association between metabolic and renal factors and plasma p‐tau measurements, we fitted three linear mixed‐effects models—one for each of the p‐tau analytes (p‐tau181, p‐tau217, and p‐tau231)—that included random intercepts and fixed effects for age (mean‐centered), sex, amyloid positivity status, z scored HOMA2‐IR, and binarized WHR. As a sensitivity analysis, we also ran all primary models using z scored WHR as a continuous variable. We did not include T2DM in these models as we were underpowered to reliably detect an effect of T2DM given the small number of individuals who had the disease. We selected WHR over BMI as the primary obesity measure as prior studies have shown this metric to have greater relevance to dementia risk and cognitive impairment. 25 , 26 , 27 Given the limited number of people with eGFR data, we first performed model selection on a subset with complete data (N = 70) using Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) to select the best model for each p‐tau analyte. For p‐tau181, models without eGFR (AIC = 212.93, BIC = 231.89) performed better than models with eGFR (AIC = 214.19, BIC = 235.51). Similarly, p‐tau217 models without eGFR (AIC = −25.50, BIC = −6.54) performed better than models with eGFR (AIC = −18.83, BIC = 2.50). However, the model with eGFR (AIC = 432.50, BIC = 453.83) showed improved fit versus the model without eGFR (AIC = 435.52, BIC = 454.48) for p‐tau231, supporting its inclusion as a predictor for this analyte despite the smaller sample. Based on these model testing results, we used the full sample with longitudinal data to examine p‐tau217 and p‐tau181 as outcomes (N = 272), and the smaller sample for p‐tau231 (N = 70). Results were considered significant at unadjusted P value < 0.05.

RESULTS

Participants in this study were mostly female (67.6%), White (95.8%), and cognitively unimpaired (97.2%). Of these participants, 50 (17.4%) individuals were amyloid PET positive at the time of the first plasma sample, and 37.6% were carriers of at least one apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele. Moreover, 33.1% met obesity criteria based on BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 and 47.8% were classified as high WHR (men: > 0.90, women: > 0.85). Among individuals classified as having high BMI, 59.7% were also classified as having high WHR. Cohen κ indicates a slight, though significant, overlap between BMI and WHR classifications (κ = 0.14, P < 0.05; 95% CI = 0.03, 0.14). Twelve individuals (4.2%) had T2DM at baseline, while six developed T2DM in subsequent visits (see Table 1 for participant characteristics). Mann–Whitney U results indicated that plasma p‐tau analyte concentrations did not differ based on diabetes status or obesity status measured by binarized BMI or WHR at baseline. Partial Spearman rho analysis showed that all p‐tau analytes were significantly correlated, exhibiting large effect sizes (magnitude of the correlations 28 ). Plasma p‐tau181 had the largest effect size with both p‐tau231 (Spearman ρ = 0.63; 95% CI = 0.56, 0.70) and p‐tau217 (ρ = 0.63; 95% CI = 0.54, 0.70) followed by plasma p‐tau217 and p‐tau231 (ρ = 0.54; 95% CI = 0.45, 0.62). HOMA2‐IR and p‐tau181 showed a small but significant effect size (ρ = 0.12, P < .05; 95% CI = 0.01, 0.24). Finally, eGFR had significant correlations with plasma p‐tau181 (ρ = −0.26; 95% CI = −0.46, −0.03) and plasma p‐tau231 (ρ = −0.35; 95% CI = −0.53, −0.12), but not plasma p‐tau217(Figure 1).

ROC analysis demonstrated that all plasma p‐tau analytes were strong classifiers of amyloid positivity. Plasma p‐tau231 and p‐tau181 had very similar AUCs (p‐tau231: AUC = 0.78 [95% CI = 0.07, 0.84]; p‐tau181: AUC = 0.79 [95% CI = 0.74, 0.85]), whereas p‐tau217 had a larger AUC of 0.91 (95% CI = 0.86, 0.97). Our results indicated that accounting for IR did not improve AUCs for any of the p‐tau analytes. Finally, accounting for eGFR improved accuracy for both p‐tau181 and p‐tau231, but not p‐tau217 (Figure 2).

Linear mixed models showed that older age significantly predicted mean level of longitudinal p‐tau181 (β = 0.04, P < 0.001; 95% CI = 0.02, 0.05), p‐tau217 (β = 0.01, P < 0.001; 95% CI = 0.01, 0.01), and p‐tau231 (β = 0.20, P < 0.05; 95% CI = 0.05, 0.34). Amyloid positivity status was also associated with mean level of longitudinal p‐tau181 (β = 0.66, P < 0.001; 95% CI = 0.42, 0.89), p‐tau217 (β = 0.40, P < 0.001; 95% CI = 0.33, 0.46), and p‐tau231 (β = 4.39, P < 0.05; 95% CI = 1.43, 7.36). For all p‐tau analytes, high WHR was a significant negative predictor (see Table 2). When WHR was modeled continuously, the relationship with p‐tau181 remained significant (β = −0.15, P < 0.05; 95% CI = −0.27, −0.02), the effect with p‐tau217 was marginal (β = −0.03, P = 0.08; 95% CI = −0.07, 0.00), and there was no longer an association with p‐tau231. The direction of all relationships remained in the negative direction, but there was variation in magnitude and significance (see Table S1 in supporting information). Finally, the relationship between eGFR and mean level of longitudinal p‐tau231 was significant (β = −1.01, P < 0.05; 95% CI = −1.88, −0.13) suggesting that lower kidney function was associated with higher levels of p‐tau231. No other predictors reached significance.

DISCUSSION

This study examined the association between metabolic (IR, T2DM, BMI, and WHR) and renal factors (eGFR) and plasma p‐tau biomarkers (p‐tau181, p‐tau217, and p‐tau231) in a head‐to‐head sense, allowing for a controlled evaluation of relative diagnostic performance under consistent biological conditions. We observed that IR was correlated with p‐tau181, and eGFR was correlated with both p‐tau181 and p‐tau231 after adjusting for age. ROC analyses indicated that accounting for IR did not alter concordance of any plasma p‐tau analyte with amyloid PET, and accounting for eGFR improved concordance with amyloid PET for p‐tau231 and p‐tau181 but not p‐tau217. Inclusion of IR and eGFR in the ROC analysis did not affect plasma p‐tau217 concordance with amyloid PET. These findings suggest that p‐tau217 is likely not impacted by IR or kidney function and largely aligns with prior studies indicating that p‐tau217 may be a more sensitive peripheral marker of AD‐related amyloid pathology, particularly in preclinical and prodromal phases. 4 , 29 Our longitudinal analysis indicated that age, binarized WHR, and amyloid status were all significant predictors of mean level of all plasma p‐tau analytes, and that lower eGFR associated with higher plasma p‐tau231. Together, these results indicate that metabolic and renal factors may contribute to plasma p‐tau variability in an analyte‐specific manner.

It is not clear why plasma p‐tau181 and p‐tau231, but not p‐tau217, were impacted by IR and kidney function in this cohort. One possible explanation is that p‐tau analytes may be differentially affected based on the types of prevalent comorbid conditions. 30 , 31 Our preclinical cohort was largely healthy with low prevalence of diagnosed health conditions such as T2DM and kidney disease. One study conducted in a population with higher occurrence of metabolic and renal diseases indicated that decreased renal function was associated with both higher plasma p‐tau181 and p‐tau217, with more pronounced effects for p‐tau181, and this effect was largely mitigated by usage of a p‐tau217/Aβ42 ratio. 30 Conversely, Olvera‐Rojas et al. 31 found that diabetes and cardiovascular conditions attenuated the concordance of plasma p‐tau217 and p‐tau217/Aβ42 with amyloid PET. Together, our findings and findings from these studies suggest that plasma p‐tau analyte interpretations should consider the comorbid conditions relevant to the target population.

Baseline HOMA2‐IR correlated with p‐tau181 after adjusting for age, although accounting for IR did not improve the concordance between p‐tau181 and amyloid PET. It is unclear in our study if the relationship between IR and p‐tau181 reflects an important biological relationship relevant to dementia risk, or whether IR is operating as a confound. IR can be broadly defined as the inability of tissues in the body to respond to insulin secretion 32 and is a key feature of hyperinsulinemia (sustained excess blood insulin levels 33 ) and T2DM. 34 IR has been associated with increased risk for AD dementia 35 and insulin signaling pathways can influence AD‐relevant enzymes, including glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK‐3β), which phosphorylates tau. Hyperinsulinemia can exacerbate the activity of GSK‐3β, leading to increased tau phosphorylation and aggregation. We have previously shown that IR is associated with early brain changes in asymptomatic individuals at increased risk for AD, including brain glucose metabolism, 36 amyloid deposition, 37 and altered brain myelin content; 38 however, IR was not associated with tau PET in the entorhinal cortex. 39 Although accounting for IR did not improve AUC for any plasma p‐tau analyte, it is advisable to consider its correlation with p‐tau181 when interpreting this analyte.

We found that high WHR was associated with lower mean level of longitudinal plasma p‐tau concentrations for all analytes, and that when modeled continuously, the direction of relationships remained unchanged although the magnitude and significance varied (see Table S1). Our results suggest an effect of WHR on plasma p‐tau concentrations in a manner that aligns with prior reports that BMI can influence plasma biomarker levels. 15 , 40 We do not suggest interchangeable interpretation for BMI and WHR, as each metric captures different dimensions of adiposity. 23 However, considering that BMI levels have been shown to influence plasma total tau 15 and negatively correlated with plasma p‐tau217 in one study, 40 there may be a broader role for adiposity‐related physiology in shaping plasma p‐tau concentrations. Finally, obese individuals are shown to have greater overall total blood volume (e.g., Cepeda‐Lopez et al. 41 ) and the lower plasma concentrations of p‐tau may be due simply to this effect, although more investigations are needed to understand this phenomenon.

In a small subsample of participants with available eGFR data, our analysis showed that lower eGFR was associated with higher plasma p‐tau231 concentrations after adjusting for age, sex, WHR, and amyloid status. Moreover, accounting for eGFR resulted in improved AUCs for both p‐tau181 and p‐tau231. These findings suggest that even within the range of mildly decreased to normal kidney function, renal clearance may have an impact on plasma p‐tau concentrations. This contrasts with Arslan et al., 42 who reported no independent associations between eGFR and plasma p‐tau181, p‐tau231, or p‐tau217 after adjusting for age, sex, and amyloid status in a sample from the Translational Biomarkers in Aging and Dementia (TRIAD) cohort. Possible explanations for this discrepancy include differences in health status or the age distribution as the TRIAD cohort included older participants. 42 Our results altogether reinforce the importance of considering kidney function, even in the absence of chronic kidney disease (CKD), when using p‐tau biomarkers as an indicator for amyloid pathology, especially for p‐tau181 and p‐tau231. This is consistent with prior studies indicating populations with CKD display higher levels of plasma p‐tau concentrations, possibly due to impaired renal clearance. 11 , 13 , 43 For example, analyses from the Mayo Clinic Study on Aging found that plasma p‐tau181 and p‐tau217 concentrations were higher in individuals with a history of CKD, myocardial infarction, and stroke when adjusting for age, sex, and amyloid PET, 13 and amyloid PET‐negative individuals with CKD displayed higher concentrations of plasma p‐tau217. 43 Moreover, a study following a sample of participants with CKD before and after kidney transplantation showed that, prior to transplantation, plasma p‐tau181 levels were elevated in CKD patients relative to controls. After kidney transplantation, plasma p‐tau181 levels dropped by ≈ 71% at 12 weeks, suggesting that elevated p‐tau181 in CKD likely reflects renal dysfunction as opposed to AD pathology. 11

Sauvé et al. 44 recently proposed that brain tau fragments are cleared into the bloodstream via tanycyte‐mediated clearance, but the mechanism for how these fragments are later peripherally cleared is not fully known. Murine studies indicate that tau travels from the brain to blood by way of central nervous system–derived exosomes, that this transport may be impacted by neurodegenerative disease etiology, 45 and that the kidney and liver may be responsible for peripheral tau clearance. 46 As such, conditions that alter peripheral metabolism or organ function may affect the level of tau fragments measured in plasma. These factors should be carefully considered especially when interpreting plasma p‐tau concentrations.

Limitations

A few limitations should be noted. First, the participants in this study were mostly White, highly educated, and relatively healthy, limiting generalizability of our sample to the larger population. Our correlation and ROC analysis are cross‐sectional in nature, and causation cannot be inferred from these analyses. Future studies are also needed to determine whether metabolic and renal factors are acting synergistically, independently, or in an overlapping manner on plasma p‐tau levels. In addition, this study used non–low molecular weight assay designs, and low molecular weight tau assays may perform differently (e.g., Janelidze et al. 47 ). As such, we cannot determine whether the health factors we investigated will impact low molecular weight tau assays in similar ways. Our longitudinal analysis highlighted important associations among age, WHR, amyloid status, and eGFR with p‐tau231, although this analysis was conducted with a limited subset, which could influence robustness of the results. Finally, additional studies are needed to determine how brain‐derived tau is cleared in the periphery, given that diseases that impact clearance could affect measurements of brain‐derived proteins.
