From plant oxidative stress to food safety: a versatile fluorescent probe for H2O2 imaging in plant roots, living cells, and residual detection in milk
Authors: Ying-Long Fu, Xin-Yue Liu, Hou-Han Chen, Yao-Wei Hu, Xiang-Wei Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is an important signaling molecule in plants under stress, and its level can be stimulated by abiotic stress and oxidative stress, which will seriously affect plant growth and development. Additionally, the presence of excessive residual H2O2 in food can pose significant health risks to humans, because intake of H2O2 can lead to serious pathological conditions. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a simple and efficient method to detect H2O2 in both plants and food. In this paper, we designed a fluorescence probe NBP, which has the advantages of high selectivity, low detection limit (80 nM) and long emission wavelength (648 nm). The imaging effect of exogenous H2O2 was realized in the roots of Platycodon grandiflorum. By exploring the interplay between H2O2, plant metals, and drought stress, we can observe the up-regulation of H2O2 in the roots of Platycodon grandiflorum under adverse conditions, and the root 3D imaging study could be realized. Then we combined the fluorescence probe with a smartphone, which enables on-site detection of residual H2O2 in various milk samples. In addition, we investigated the fluorescence imaging of endogenous and exogenous H2O2 in living cells using NBP. Therefore, this study provides a new way to assess the oxidative stress risk of Platycodon grandiflorum roots under abiotic stress, which is expected to improve plant production and has broad application prospects in food sample detection.

Introduction

During the whole life cycle, plants are constantly subjected to various abiotic stresses in the ecological environment, including metals, drought, low or high temperature, and floods, etc., which will seriously damage plant tissues and hinder plant growth.1–4 Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), as an important reactive oxygen species (ROS) involved in a variety of biological processes, has become a key signaling molecule regulating stress response and is considered to be an indicator of acute stress.5,6 Oxidative stress is a common response of plants to abiotic stress.7 ROS produced by moderate oxidative stress can improve the adaptability of plants to the environment and enhance the antioxidant capacity.8,9 However, excessive ROS produced by severe oxidative stress will reduce the survival rate of plants.10 For example, abiotic stress such as salt can cause oxidative damage through the formation of ROS, and continuous salt stress treatment can inhibit the growth of centipedegrass leaves, thus affecting the development of centipedegrass.11 Excessive mercury can inhibit the activity of aquaporins and reduce the water absorption capacity of plants.12 Excessive chromium can inhibit water transport and photosynthesis, inhibit seed germination, and affect plant growth.13Platycodon grandiflorum, a plant possessing homologous characteristics of both medicine and food, presents a more grave situation. Platycodon grandiflorum contains certain nutritional and medicinal values, and has a good health care effect on human body.14–16 The combination of Platycodon grandiflorum and lily can treat pneumonia, which has great research value in disease prevention and clinical application. When excessive H2O2 is produced in Platycodon grandiflorum, people will ingest the body through the food chain, causing serious harm to the skin, digestive tract and respiratory tract.

In addition, H2O2 is also an antifungal agent, which is widely used in food disinfection and sterilization. Excessive H2O2 residues in food can cause human body the occurrence of various diseases, such as cerebral stroke,17 depression18 and gas embolism,19etc. According to the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the residual amount of H2O2 in dairy products cannot exceed 0.5 mg L−1.20–22 In the milk industry, excessive H2O2 is harmful to the nutritional value of milk. For example, too much H2O2 can destroy the nutrient component folic acid in milk, and its degradation will reduce the nutritional value of milk.23 H2O2 residue can accelerate the spoilage process of milk, resulting in poor taste and a shortened shelf life of milk. In order to ensure the safety of milk Therefore, it is necessary to develop a simple, efficient and visual method to monitor H2O2 to improve plant stress resistance and on-spot quantitative detection of H2O2 levels in food.

So far, there are many analytical methods for detecting H2O2, such as high-performance liquid chromatography, colorimetry (HPLC), mass spectrometry, spectrophotometry, electron spin resonance and electrochemical method.24–28 However, these methods all have certain limitations, such as long response time, poor specificity, high cost, and inability to achieve on-site detection.29,30 In contrast, fluorescent probe monitoring is considered as a promising method for the determination of H2O2, which has the advantages of high selectivity, simple operation, low cost and real-time in situ imaging.31–34 In recent years, a number of fluorescent probes have been reported to monitor the changes of H2O2 levels in cells and mice, but studies to monitor H2O2 in plant and food samples have not been widely used. In addition, some fluorescent probes have long emission wavelength characteristics and certain tissue penetration, which realize in situ imaging of plant samples instead of limited to the determination of H2O2 levels in plants. For example, Li et al. developed a near-infrared fluorescence probe DRP-B to perform in situ imaging of H2O2 in cabbage roots under abiotic stress.3 Zhou et al. developed a fluorescent probe YFE-1 to perform in situ imaging of rice roots under cadmium ion stress to detect H2O2 levels.35 Therefore, it is urgent to develop a fluorescent probe with excellent selectivity and sensitivity to achieve real-time, dynamic imaging and accurate detection of H2O2 in real samples. It can accurately identify H2O2 molecules in complex biological environments, and its fluorescence response is more sensitive to capture the change of H2O2 concentration.

Experimental

Materials and instruments

All chemicals and reagents can be used directly in experiments without further purification. HepG2 Cells were purchased from Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). 8-Hydroxyzulonidin-9-formaldehyde, 6-hydroxy-1-tetrahydronaphthalone, glacial acetic acid, perchloric acid, pinacol 4-(hydroxymethyl) phenylborate, triphosgene and triethylamine are purchased from Anergy or Micrel. 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AM 600 MHz and 151 MHz NMR spectrometer using deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide as solvent. High resolution mass spectrometry was performed using the Alliance E2695/SQD2 TOF 1260–6224 mass spectrometer. UV-visible absorption and fluorescence spectra were measured using a Tsushima UV-2401 PC spectrophotometer and an RF-5301 PC fluorescence spectrophotometer. Plant root fluorescence imaging experiments were performed using an Olympus FV1000 laser confocal microscope. Photographs were taken using an iphone 13 and RGB analysis was performed using the cell phone software ColorAssist.

Spectral analysis

The stock solution of NBP (10 µM) was obtained by dissolving NBP in DMSO. All analytes and H2O2 solutions were prepared with deionized water to form a 10 mM aqueous solution for storage. All analytes were tested in a PBS buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4, 40% acetonitrile). Various analytes were added to the probe NBP and the fluorescence intensity was recorded at 648 nm after reaction at room temperature for 20 min. The excitation wavelength for all spectral tests was 560 nm.

Cell culture and fluorescence imaging

HepG2 cells were seeded into a 24-well plate and incubated at 37 °C under an atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 95% air for 24 h. In the exogenous imaging group, cells were treated with H2O2 (50 and 100 µM) for 30 min and then NBP (10 µM) for 30 min. In the endogenous imaging group, the cells were divided into two groups. The first group was treated with PMA (1 µg mL−1) for 30 min and then NBP (10 µM) for 30 min. The second group was treated with PMA (1 µg mL−1) for 30 min, NAC (1 mM) for 30 min, and then with NBP (10 µM) for 30 min. All cells were subjected to fluorescence imaging analysis using a laser scanning confocal microscope.

Cultivation of Chinese herbal medicine Platycodon grandiflorum

Platycodon grandiflorum was purchased from Bozhou Qingyi Chinese Herbal Medicine Technology Co. The seeds were soaked in deionized water for 24 h, then evenly spread on filter paper moistened with deionized water, and incubated in Petri dishes for 2–3 weeks at room temperature. After the root system of the seeds grew to 2–3 cm, the better growth state of Platycodon grandiflorum roots were selected for fluorescence imaging experiments.

Fluorescence imaging of exogenous H2O2 in Platycodon grandiflorum

Platycodon grandiflorum seedlings were immersed in H2O2 solution (0, 50, 100, and 150 µM) and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. When the incubation was completed, the H2O2 solution was poured out and washed three times with deionized water, and then immersed in NBP (10 µM) for 30 min, and washed three times with deionized water. Exogenous H2O2 fluorescence imaging was performed on the seedlings of Platycodon grandiflorum in each group.

Evaluation of oxidative stress in Platycodon grandiflorum roots under metal and drought stresses

The stem roots of Platycodon grandiflorum were cultured in four heavy metal ions, Cd2+ (100 µM), Cu2+ (100 µM), Al3+ (100 µM) and Hg2+ (100 µM) for 24 h, and washed with deionized water three times. The roots were incubated in NBP (10 µM) for 30 min, cleaned with deionized water three times, and fluorescence imaging was performed. Then, after the roots were incubated in 5% polyethylene glycol (PEG 6000) solution for different times (0, 6, 12, 18, 24 h), the roots were cleaned in deionized water for three times, immersed in NBP (10 µM) for 30 min, and cleaned with deionized water for three times for root fluorescence imaging.36

Determination of H2O2 in milk samples

Whole milk, skim milk and organic milk are purchased from Anhui University of Chinese Medicine supermarket. Combine 25 mL of each of the three milk samples in a 100 mL volumetric bottle, followed by adding zinc acetate and potassium ferricyanide solution as clarifying agents in order to precipitate the proteins in milk. Subsequently, dilute the mixture to 100 mL with deionized water. The mixture was centrifuged (1000 min−1) for 10 min, and a sample supernatant was mixed with acetonitrile at a ratio of 6/4 as a test sample.37

Results and discussion

Spectral response of NBP to H2O2

In order to investigate the ability of probe NBP in detecting H2O2, the UV-vis absorption and fluorescence spectra of probe NBP were obtained in PBS buffer containing 40% acetonitrile (pH 7.4) without and with different concentrations of H2O2. As shown in Fig. 1a, probe NBP has an obvious absorption peak at 560 nm. After reacting with H2O2 (0–460 µM), the absorption peak redshifts to 605 nm, and the solution changes from purple to blue (Fig. 1a inset). At the same time, when the excitation wavelength is 560 nm, the fluorescence of probe NBP at 648 nm is gradually enhanced with the increase of H2O2 concentration, and the fluorescence color changes from weak to red (Fig. 1b), fluorescence quantum yield (Φf) from 0.07 to 0.36 (Cresol violet Φf = 0.54 (ref. 38) as a reference). Accordingly, in the range of 0–460 µM, the fluorescence intensity of NBP showed a good linear relationship with H2O2 (R2 = 0.9824) (Fig. 1c), and the detection limit was 80 nM (3 δ/k). Furthermore, the response time of probe NBP to H2O2 was assessed (Fig. 1d), revealing that the fluorescence intensity reached a state of saturation following 20 min reaction with H2O2.

In view of the good detection performance of probe NBP for H2O2, we conducted further investigation into the photostability and pH characteristics of probe NBP. As shown in Fig. S1, with the passage of time (0–60 min), the fluorescence intensity of NBP and NBP-OH was relatively stable, and no obvious fluorescence changes were observed. This shows that NBP and NBP-OH have good light stability and can be used in detection and imaging experiments for a long time. As illustrated in Fig. S2, the graph depicts the fluorescence intensity of probe NBP both before and after its reaction with H2O2 at various pH levels. At a pH range of 2–6, the fluorescence intensity before and after the H2O2 reaction is negligible. Upon the introduction of H2O2, the fluorescence intensity of NBP solution gradually increases within a pH range of 6–8, reaching its peak at a pH value of 8, and then rapidly decreasing within a pH range of 8–11. Within a pH range of 6–11, the fluorescence intensity of the probe exhibits slow increments. The results show that NBP is suitable for detection in physiological environment.

Reaction mechanism

The probe NBP uses benzopyran salt as fluorophore and 4-(hydroxymethyl) benzeneboronic acid pinacol ester as the reaction site, and its red fluorescence signal is weak. In this study, anthocyanin derivatives were selected as fluorescent parent nuclei and combined with arylboronic acid ester as the specific recognition site to construct fluorescence probe NBP successfully. It is worth emphasizing that the red fluorescence signal of probe NBP in its unreacted state with H2O2 showed a low intensity. To verify the reaction mechanism of probe NBP with H2O2, we performed HRMS tracking analysis of NBP and NBP + H2O2. The results show that the molecular mass of [NBP]+ was m/z 604.3494, which corresponds to the theoretical molecular mass of m/z 604.2865 (Fig. S9). After reacting with H2O2, a new peak appears at m/z 344.1680, which is consistent with the theoretical molecular mass of the sensing product NBP-OH at m/z 344.1645 (Fig. S10).

Then, we further explored the changes in UV and fluorescence of the probe NBP before and after its reaction with H2O2 through spectrometry. As shown in Fig. S3, the maximum absorption peak of probe NBP was 560 nm, and after reacting with H2O2, the absorption peak was redshifted to 605 nm, which was the same as the absorption peak of NBP-OH (605 nm). Fluorescence spectrum is also supported the NBP after reaction with H2O2 produce the sensing mechanism of NBP-OH. All theoretical calculations were carried out by using the Gaussian 09 program. Geometry optimizations for molecules were carried out using density functional theory (DFT) at the level of B3LYP/6-31+G(d,p). Then, solvent effects of water were performed by the polarizable continuum model (PCM). Density functional theory (DFT) calculations show that HOMO–LUMO energy gap values for NBP and NBP-OH are 2.74 eV and 2.73 eV in the ground state (S0), respectively (Fig. 2). The decrease of the energy gap value of the reaction product indicates that the absorption band in the corresponding absorption spectrum is red-shifted, which is consistent with the results of the above absorption spectra.

Selectivity and anti-interference capability of NBP

Specificity and anti-interference are important parameters for evaluating the performance of fluorescent probes in detection. To investigate the specificity of probe NBP to H2O2, specificity experiments were performed by detecting changes in fluorescence intensity in the presence of cations, anions, and biothiols. As shown in Fig. 3a, probe NBP has a strong fluorescence increase effect on H2O2, and the reaction with other analytes does not cause significant changes in fluorescence intensity. In the anti-interference experiment, when the interfering compound and H2O2 exist at the same time, the interference of the interfering compound on the probe NBP detection of H2O2 is small. These interferents had little interference with the detection of H2O2 by probe NBP and showed obvious fluorescence enhancement (Fig. 3b). These results indicate that the probe NBP has good specificity and anti-interference ability for the detection of H2O2.

Cell imaging

The prerequisite for achieving cellular visualization is low cytotoxicity. Therefore, we assessed the cytotoxicity of NBP at various concentrations (0–20 µM) using the CCK-8 method. After incubating HepG2 cells with NBP for 24 h, over 85% of the cells remained viable (Fig. S4), indicating that NBP exhibits low toxicity and can be applied for fluorescence imaging of live cells.

Next, we investigated the imaging capability of NBP for exogenous H2O2 levels in HepG2 cells. As shown in Fig. 4, after induction with PMA and subsequent treatment with NBP, a clear red fluorescence was observed. In contrast, cells treated with NBP alone exhibited almost no fluorescence. To further explore the application of NBP in detecting endogenous H2O2, we pretreated cells with N-acetyl-l-cysteine (NAC, a common inhibitor of H2O2), which resulted in a significant and negligible reduction in intracellular fluorescence. Additionally, by exogenously adding different concentrations of H2O2 (50 and 100 µM), we observed a gradual increase in fluorescence intensity as the H2O2 concentration increased. The results indicate that NBP can be used for imaging both exogenous and endogenous H2O2 in live cells. Given the effectiveness of NBP in imaging and semi-quantitative analysis of H2O2 in biological samples, we further applied it to monitor and image H2O2 in Platycodon grandiflorum roots.

Fluorescence imaging of exogenous H2O2 in Platycodon grandiflorum roots

Platycodon grandiflorum, a member of the Campanulaceae family, is a significant medicinal and food plant in East Asia with potential therapeutic effects.39 As an important signal molecule, H2O2 can regulate the growth of Platycodon grandiflorum. The determination of H2O2 level under stress by fluorescence imaging is of great significance for evaluating the risk of oxidative stress and improving the stress resistance and productivity of Platycodon grandiflorum. Therefore, before studying the performance of probe NBP in imaging H2O2 in plants, we analyzed the toxicity of probe NBP by observing the overall morphological changes of Platycodon grandiflorum seedlings. As shown in Fig. 5a, the morphology of seedlings treated with probe NBP (0–30 µM) was very similar to that of the control group. When the concentration of probe NBP was below 20 µM, the average root length of seedlings incubated with probe NBP remained essentially the same (Fig. 5b). These results indicate that probe NBP can be safely used for plant root staining and fluorescence imaging, with low toxicity to the whole plant.

Photographs of Platycodon grandiflorum seedlings (a) and average root length of seedlings (b) after incubation with different concentrations of probe NBP (0–30 µM) for 3 days at room temperature.

Inspired by the above experimental results, we further utilized NBP to perform fluorescence imaging of exogenous H2O2 in the roots of Platycodon grandiflorum. This approach enhanced our capacity to visualize and investigate crucial signaling molecules that regulate plant stress response during plant growth. We first incubated seedling roots with different concentrations of H2O2 (0, 50, 100 and 150 µM) for 30 min, followed by incubation with NBP (10 µM) for 30 min. As shown in Fig. 6a, the red channel fluorescence gradually increased with the continuous increase of H2O2 concentration, while the red fluorescence of seedling roots without H2O2 treatment was very weak. At the same time, 3D imaging was performed on d3 group to record the fluorescence intensity of seedling roots at different depths (Fig. 6b). These findings suggest that NBP is suitable for fluorescence imaging of exogenous H2O2 in plant specimens, and exhibits a degree of tissue penetration.

Evaluation of Platycodon grandiflorum roots under metal and drought stress

Platycodon grandiflorum, as a plant homologous to medicine and food, is susceptible to contamination by various metals in the environment during its growth process. This contamination can hinder the development of the plant's roots and its ability to absorb nutrients. Furthermore, metal ions can gradually accumulate in the human body through the food chain, posing a serious threat to human health. To study the changes in Platycodon grandiflorum under metal stress, we selected four metal ions, Cd2+, Cu2+, Al3+ and Hg2+. As shown in Fig. 7a, no obvious fluorescence appeared in the control group after being treated with NBP (10 µM) for 30 min. After adding each metal ion Cd2+, Cu2+, Al3+ and Hg2+ 100 µM and stressing them for 24 h, they were incubated with probe NBP (10 µM) for 30 min, and obvious fluorescence signals were observed. These results showed that an oxidative stress effect occurred in the roots of Platycodon grandiflorum under the stimulation of metal ions, resulting in an overall upregulation of H2O2 levels. At the same time, we performed 3D imaging of roots after metal ion stress in each group, and we were able to observe clear fluorescence imaging of seedling roots at various depths (Fig. 7b and S5).

Studies have found that plants can also be disturbed by abiotic drought stress during their development, resulting in reduced crop yields and seriously affecting the production quality and quality of crops. Therefore, we simulated the drought stress model to study the change trend of H2O2 level in the roots of Platycodon grandiflorum. Under the same conditions as metal stress on the roots of Platycodon grandiflorum, different drought stress treatment times were adopted, and the results were shown in Fig. 8a. Various drought-stress treatment times were applied using the same conditions employed to stress Platycodon grandiflorum roots with metals, and the results are shown in Fig. 8a. With the extension of drought stress treatment time, the fluorescence signal of red channel was enhanced gradually. This important finding indicates that the level of H2O2 is up-regulated with the response of plants to drought stress, which further reflects the degree of oxidative stress under this condition. Finally, we performed 3D imaging of experimental group e3 with the strongest red fluorescence signal after treatment under drought stress conditions, which could obtain fluorescence imaging of Platycodon grandiflorum roots at different depths (Fig. 8b).

Visual detection of H2O2 in real milk samples

Encouraged by the successful application of actual plant root samples, we further investigated the ability of probe NBP to detect H2O2 in milk samples. We selected three milk samples (whole milk, skim milk, and organic milk), pretreated the milk samples according to the methods described in the experimental section, and kept them for future use. In order to further investigate the ability of probe NBP to visually detect H2O2 in other practical samples, we selected different types of milk (whole milk, skim milk, and organic milk) and obtained milk samples according to the treatment methods in the experimental section. As shown in Fig. 9a, when probe NBP (10 µM) was added to the sample, the red fluorescence of the solution was very weak under the irradiation of 365 nm UVlight, and the red fluorescence of the solution was gradually enhanced with the increase of exogenous H2O2 concentration. The RGB value of fluorescence of each group of milk samples was analyzed by using mobile phone software ColorAssist (Fig. 9b and c), and it was found that there was a good linear relationship between the R value of each milk and the concentration of H2O2 (R2 = 0.9848, 0.9246 and 0.9693) (Fig. 9d). Then, we further performed routine recovery experiments on three types of milk to evaluate the accuracy of probe NBP for quantitative detection of H2O2 in milk. We performed routine recovery experiments to further evaluate the accuracy of probe NBP in the detection of H2O2 in actual food samples. The samples containing the probe NBP were added with 30, 60 and 90 µM H2O2, and the fluorescence intensity changes were recorded at 648 nm wavelength, three times for each concentration gradient. As shown in Table 1, the recoveries of all three types of milk ranged from 88.61% to 103.44%, with relative standard deviations (RSD) ranging from 2.10% to 4.67%. The above results demonstrate that the NBP probe is capable of visually and quantitatively detecting residual H2O2 in a variety of milk samples. Furthermore, it exhibits a high recovery rate, acceptable accuracy, and practical applicability in real food samples.

Conclusions

Overall, our designed probe BZD has good spectroscopic performance, and it can be an important tool for detecting changes in NO levels in cells, zebrafish, and plant roots. In summary, we developed and synthesized a fluorescent probe NBP for accurate visual recognition and fluorescence imaging of H2O2 in food samples and living cells. Based on the excellent spectral performance (for example, short reaction time, specific selectivity and high sensitivity, strong photostability) of probe NBP in detecting H2O2 and the advantages of fluorescence emission probe with certain tissue penetration, NBP was used to detect exogenous H2O2 in different concentrations of Platycodon grandiflorum. More importantly, this study demonstrated that H2O2 levels in Platycodon grandiflorum roots increased when they were subjected to metal or drought stress, further confirming the up-regulation of H2O2 in plant resistance to common abiotic stresses. In addition, on-site detection of H2O2 in milk samples can beaccomplished through the utilization of a smartphone platform and the color change can be visually observed without the need for additional equipment. The findings of this study have the potential to facilitate further investigations into H2O2 resistance in various plant species, thereby enhancing both crop yield and safety. This has significant implications within the realm of food analysis, making it an invaluable contribution to the field.
