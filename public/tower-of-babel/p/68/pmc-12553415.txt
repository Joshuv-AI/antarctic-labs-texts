Cerebral Small‐Vessel Disease Markers on Magnetic Resonance Imaging as Predictors of Recurrent Vascular Events and Death in Ischemic Stroke: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Muhammad Nurhakim Hairus S, Adithya Srinivasan, Shemin Ayesha Shaik Mohideen, Neil Chen Yi Lun MacAlevey, Mark Y. Chan, Aloysius S. T. Leow, Xin Zhou, Gregory Y. H. Lip, Benjamin Y. Q. Tan, Leonard L. L. Yeo, Yao Neng Teo, Yao Hao Teo, Ching‐Hui Sia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCerebral small‐vessel disease encompasses a spectrum of magnetic resonance imaging features reflecting microvascular pathology in ischemic stroke. Among these, white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) and cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) are the most widely studied. While both are well established as risk factors for cognitive decline in patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS), their prognostic value for vascular events and death after AIS remains unclear. This study aims to evaluate WMH and CMB as predictors of recurrent stroke, cardiovascular death, and all‐cause death in patients with AIS.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed and Embase for longitudinal studies from July 1977 to March 2024 reporting adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) or odds ratios for these outcomes after AIS. Pooled estimates were generated using random‐effects models, and heterogeneity was explored using meta‐regression and prediction intervals.ResultsFifty studies (n=44 140) were included. WMHs were a significant predictor of stroke recurrence (HR,1.72 [95% CI, 1.39–2.13]) and all‐cause death (HR, 1.58 [95% CI, 1.11–2.23]). Similarly, CMBs were associated with stroke recurrence (HR, 1.88 [95% CI, 1.52–2.33]), with lobar and mixed types conferring higher risk than deep CMBs, and predicted cardiovascular death (HR, 1.45 [95% CI, 1.2–1.75]). For stroke recurrence, risk estimates increased in parallel with WMH and CMB burden.ConclusionsThis meta‐analysis supports WMHs and CMBs as independent predictors of stroke recurrence and death in AIS. Burden and distribution further refine prognostic accuracy and inform poststroke risk assessment.

Cerebral small‐vessel disease (CSVD) is a chronic, progressive disorder of arterioles, capillaries, and small veins supplying white matter and deep structures but consisting of different clinical and radiological phenotypes. 1 Nonetheless, their pathophysiology is similar and attributed to myriad mechanisms including arteriosclerosis, impaired cerebral blood flow autoregulation, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammation. 2 , 3 , 4 These mechanisms alluding to microvascular dysfunction are postulated as systemic pathological processes, 3 implicated in coronary 5 and renal microvascular disease. 6 In community‐dwelling older adults, CSVD has been associated with an increased risk of vascular outcomes including incident strokes, 7 gait impairment, 8 cognitive decline, 9 and depression. 10 In broad terms, CSVD includes white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) and cerebral microbleeds (CMBs).

WMHs are defined as patchy or confluent hyperintensities on T2‐weighted or fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without cavitation. 11 They represent the most common incidental CSVD finding 12 and are thought to arise from chronic hypoxia–ischemia due to stenosis of the thalamostriate and perforating arteries. 13 WMH prevalence and progression are associated with risk factors including advanced age, 12 , 14 hypertension, 15 , 16 smoking, 16 , 17 diabetes, and baseline lesion volume. 16 In patients with ischemic stroke (IS), WMH severity serves as a prognosticator for outcomes in acute and subacute phases of stroke, 18 , 19 including that of current treatment strategies such as mechanical thrombectomy 20 and intravenous thrombolysis. 19 , 21

CMBs are identified as hypointensities on paramagnetic‐sensitive MRI sequences such as T2*‐weighted gradient recalled echocardiography or susceptibility weighted imaging. 11 They reflect perivascular collections of hemosiderin deposits, hence serving as a marker for hemorrhage‐prone vascular changes. As with WMHs, CMBs demonstrate an age‐associated increase in prevalence, with cardiovascular risk factors and other baseline CSVD features more strongly associated with deep CMBs than lobar CMBs. 22 This corresponds with the underlying pathophysiology behind the distribution of bleeds, with deep CMBs reflecting hypertensive arteriopathy, while lobar CMBs reflect cerebral amyloid angiopathy 23 (CAA). In patients with ischemic cerebrovascular events (including transient ischemic attack), CMB presence and severity portend poor outcomes including higher risk of stroke recurrence.

While WMHs and CMBs are recognized markers of cognitive decline, their role in predicting cardiovascular outcomes, particularly in poststroke patients, is less well‐defined. Previous systematic reviews and meta‐analyses 24 , 25 , 26 have explored the association of WMHs and CMBs with stroke recurrence and death, but comprehensive analysis regarding their impact on cardiovascular death is lacking. Our study aims to address this gap by providing an updated analysis of recent literature, specifically focusing on patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS).

The objectives of this study were to evaluate the impact of WMHs and CMBs on 3 primary outcomes in patients with AIS: recurrent stroke, comprising IS and intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), cardiovascular death, and all‐cause death. Additionally, we aimed to examine whether a higher burden of WMH or CMB is associated with an increased risk of these outcomes, and if the distribution of CMB influences these risks.

METHODS

We registered the study protocol on the International Prospective Register for Systematic Reviews, on August 27, 2023 (CRD42023450007). The reporting of the systematic review and meta‐analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis guidelines. The data, analytic methods (including statistical code), and materials used to conduct this research are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request for the purposes of reproducing the results.

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

We conducted a comprehensive literature search using PubMed and Embase, from July 1977 to May 2024, for studies investigating the impact of WMHs and CMBs on the prognosis of IS patients. The search strategy is detailed in (Table S1).

We included published full‐text articles from longitudinal studies that studied the association between MRI‐defined WMHs or CMBs and recurrent stroke, cardiovascular death, and all‐cause death after AIS, providing hazard ratios (HRs) or odds ratios (ORs) and their corresponding 95% CIs. Case reports, case studies, and randomized trials were excluded, along with studies that recruited patients with cerebral inflammatory (eg, multiple sclerosis), neurodegenerative (eg, Huntington disease), or inherited monogenic cerebral small vessel (eg, cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy). Studies recruiting only patients with hemorrhagic stroke or transient ischemic attack were also excluded.

Title and abstract screen and duplicate article removal were performed independently, with conflicts identified using Rayyan. Full‐text review was performed independently, with at least 2 reviewers for each article.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was performed by all reviewers using a prespecified data collection template (Table S2). Data extracted include the following: study characteristics, which comprised the name of the first author, publication year, sample size, and follow‐up duration in years; demographic characteristics, which consisted of the country of study, mean or median age, sex, and prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors; and data related to MRI assessment, which included MRI sequences used for detection of CSVD, methods of assessing WMH (eg, Fazekas scale) and CMB, as well as approaches to WMH and CMB analysis.

Statistical Analysis

For WMHs, our analysis consisted of 3 categories: (1) presence versus absence; (2) extensive WMHs, which we defined by a Fazekas score ≥2, versus mild WMHs, defined by a Fazekas score <2; as well as (3) severe WMHs, which we defined by a Fazekas score ≥3, versus nonsevere WMHs, defined by a Fazekas score <3. For studies reporting subgroup‐specific estimates within a single cohort (eg, stratified by CMB location or WMH severity), we first derived an aggregated estimate using a fixed‐effect meta‐analysis. This step served to unify intrastudy subgroup estimates under the assumption of a shared underlying effect within a single population: a reasonable approximation of an individual participant data analysis adjusting for subgroup.

We then pooled these study‐level estimates across studies using a random‐effects model, anticipating heterogeneity arising from differences in study design, populations, imaging protocols, and outcome definitions. We used the Restricted Maximum Likelihood estimator to calculate between‐study variance (τ2), applied Knapp–Hartung adjustments to reflect uncertainty, and computed prediction intervals (PIs) to estimate the likely range of true effects in future studies. Between‐study heterogeneity was quantified using the I 2 statistic.

The choice of a random‐effects model aligns with the Cochrane Handbook, which recommends this approach when pooling studies with differing characteristics and unmeasured sources of variability. However, we acknowledge that the estimation of between‐study variance may be unstable when few studies are available.

In our analysis of WMH impact on recurrent stroke, we noted heterogeneity in the analysis of WMH. Most studies used the Fazekas scale in analyzing extensive and severe WMH, while 2 studies 27 , 28 used the Age‐Related White Matter Changes (ARWMC) scale, 29 1 study 30 used the Deep Space White Matter Hyperintensity (DSWMH) scale, 1 study used the 4‐point ordinal scale, and 1 study 31 used the van Swieten Scale.

The Fazekas 32 scale was noted to be similar to both the ARWMC scale 27 , 28 and the DSWMH 30 scale, allowing us to include Hert et al, Wilson et al, and Kashima et al. Hert et al compared groups with no or focal WMH (ARWMC score 0–1) to those with confluent WMH (ARWMC score 2–3). Wilson et al also defined severe WMH as an ARWMC score ≥2. We considered an ARWMC score ≥2 to be equivalent to a Fazekas score ≥2 for the analysis of extensive WMH. Kashima et al also states that DSWMH and Fazekas grades were similar, with DSWMH grade ≥3 being comparable to a Fazekas score ≥3. The 4‐point ordinal scale assessment of WMH is also similar to the methodology of Fazekas, and the van Swieten Scale was found to have a high rank correlation 33 with the Fazekas scale (γ=0.96), which allowed us to include Kissela et al and Arba et al in our analysis of severe versus nonsevere WMH.

For CMB, our analysis consisted of 3 categories: (1) presence versus absence, (2) burden (CMB=1, CMB=2–4, and CMB ≥5), and (3) distribution (strictly lobar, strictly deep, and mixed). CMB are categorized as lobar when they involve the cortical gray matter and adjacent subcortical or periventricular white matter, and deep when they affect the basal ganglia, thalamus, brainstem, or cerebellum. 11

As we anticipated considerable between‐study heterogeneity, a random‐effects model was used to pool HRs for recurrent stroke, cardiovascular death, and all‐cause death for each category. We performed a similar set of analyses for ORs of the same outcomes. The Restricted Maximum Likelihood estimator was used to calculate the between‐study variance (τ2), while heterogeneity was quantified using the I 2 statistic and PIs. PIs provide an estimate of the expected range of true effects in future settings, thereby offering a more informative metric of heterogeneity beyond I 2 alone. Knapp–Hartung adjustments were applied to account for uncertainty in τ2 and to calculate robust CIs around the pooled effect estimates. Publication bias was assessed via visual inspection of funnel plot symmetry and Egger's regression test. To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, univariable meta‐regression analyses were conducted when substantial between‐study variability was observed. Random effects models were implemented using the metareg() function from the R meta package (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), which accounts for residual between‐study heterogeneity not otherwise explained by study‐level covariates. This approach was appropriate given the clinical and methodological diversity across studies. Univariable models were selected due to the limited number of studies contributing to each covariate to reduce the risk of overfitting and preserve statistical validity.

Study‐level covariates were selected a priori based on biological plausibility and availability across studies. These included mean age, mean follow‐up duration, baseline prevalence of comorbidities, history of ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack, smoking status, mean National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score on admission, and use of antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy on discharge. For each covariate, meta‐regression estimated the extent to which variation in the covariate explained between‐study differences in effect size. A significant regression coefficient indicated a modifying effect of that variable on the association between WMH burden and the outcome of interest. Bubble plots were generated to visually represent the covariate‐effect size relationship, with bubble size proportional to the inverse variance of the effect estimate (Figure S1–S3). All analyses were performed using RStudio version 4.3.2. 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39

RESULTS

Our search retrieved 1696 results after removal of 431 duplicates. After title and abstract screening, 100 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 40 studies met our inclusion criteria and were included in the meta‐analysis. Additionally, we identified 10 relevant studies through backward citation searching. In total, 50 studies were included in our final analysis. Thirty‐eight studies were for recurrent stroke, 5 were for cardiovascular death, and 7 were for all‐cause death. The study selection process is detailed in Figure 1. The included studies are summarized in (Tables S3 and S4).

Study Characteristics

We included 50 cohort studies published between 2003 and 2024 with a total of 44 140 patients (median, 459; minimum, 75; maximum, 9522). Two studies 40 , 41 used duplicate cohorts as the same author published separate papers for deep and lobar CMBs, respectively. The mean age of patients was 68 years. During follow‐up, 5018 patients had a recurrent stroke and 2705 died. Additional study characteristics are recorded in Tables S3 and S4.

WMH and Recurrent Stroke

In patients with AIS, the presence of WMH was associated with an increased risk of recurrent stroke (HR, 1.72 [95% CI, 1.39–2.13]; PI, 1.25–2.38; k=6; N=5945; E=969; Figure S4). A sensitivity analysis (Table S5) was performed as 1 study 42 was found to be influential. Extensive WMHs increased the risk of recurrent stroke (HR, 1.87 [95% CI, 1.33–2.64]; PI, 0.826–4.24; k=10; N=12 275; E=844; Figure S5). As 1 study 43 was found to be influential, a sensitivity analysis (Table S6) was performed. The results of both analyses remained statistically significant. Two studies 30 , 44 evaluated the impact of severe WMH on the risk of recurrent stroke. Severe WMH more than quadrupled the risk of recurrent stroke (HR, 4.22 [95% CI, 3.69–4.84]; k=2; N=465; E=61; Figure S6). The presence versus absence analysis for WMHs demonstrated low heterogeneity (I 2=10%) and a narrow PI >1, suggesting a consistent and reproducible positive association in future studies. In contrast, the extensive versus mild WMH analysis demonstrated substantial heterogeneity (I 2=57.9%) and a wide PI (0.826–4.24) that crossed the null value, indicating variability in true effect sizes and potential for a trivial effect in future studies. Hence, potential confounding factors were investigated independently using meta‐regression for the extensive versus mild WMH analysis (Table 1).

WMH and Cardiovascular Death

Cardiovascular death is defined by death caused by cardiovascular events, such as myocardial infarction, and stroke, both IS and hemorrhagic stroke. Current literature on the association between WMHs and cardiovascular death is sparse. At the time of writing, only 4 studies examined this relationship. Jeong et al 53 and Song et al 54 evaluated the impact of severe WMHs on the risk of cardiovascular death, while Jeon et al 55 and Oksala et al 56 evaluated the impact of extensive WMHs on the odds of cardiovascular death. Severe and extensive WMHs were associated with a statistically insignificant increase in risk (HR, 2.63 [95% CI, 0.988–7]; k=2; N=1331; E=38; Figure S11) and odds of cardiovascular death (OR, 1.77 [95% CI, 0.0421–74]; k=2; N=652; E=154; Figure S12), respectively.

WMHs and All‐Cause Death

In patients with AIS, the presence of WMHs was associated with an increased risk of all‐cause death (HR, 1.58 [95% CI, 1.11–2.23]; PI, 1.03–2.41; k=4; N=4354; E=896; Figure S13). Extensive WMHs also increased the risk of all‐cause death but at a lower magnitude (HR, 1.49 [95% CI, 1.09–2.04]; PI=0.845–2.62; k=5; N=2957; E=339; Figure S14). Two studies 43 , 57 were found to be influential, so a sensitivity analysis (Table S10) was performed. However, the result became statistically insignificant, suggesting that the overall effect size was driven by these studies, and the robustness of the association between extensive WMHs and all‐cause death decreased with their exclusion. Severe WMHs increased the risk of all‐cause death to a degree similar to that of extensive WMH (HR, 1.48 [95% CI, 1.06–2.05]; PI=0.409–5.34; k=3; N=1869; E=504; Figure S15). While the presence versus absence analysis reflected substantial heterogeneity (I 2=54%), it posed a narrow PI (1.03–2.41) that remained >1, suggesting a reproducible positive association with odds of all‐cause death (ACD) across future studies. In contrast, the extensive versus mild analysis demonstrated low heterogeneity (I 2=27%) with a wider PI (0.845–2.62) that crossed the null value, indicating variability in effect sizes and the possibility of a negligible effect in future settings. Similarly for the severe versus mild analysis, there was a markedly wide PI (0.409–5.34) despite low heterogeneity, likely due to imprecision in estimation of true effect size from a smaller number of studies (k=3). As between‐study heterogeneity was found to be substantial in the presence versus absence analysis (I 2=53.5% [95% CI, 0%–84.6%]), potential confounding factors were investigated independently using meta‐regression (Table 3).

One study 58 evaluated the odds of all‐cause death in patients with severe WMH. We further calculated ORs for 3 studies 44 , 53 , 59 to increase the statistical power of our analysis, which revealed that WMHs more than tripled the odds of all‐cause death (OR, 3.74 [95% CI, 2.7–5.18]; PI, 1.97–7.11; k=4; N=3150; E=723, Figure S16). A sensitivity analysis (Table S11) was performed, as 1 study 59 was found to be influential, with the result remaining statistically significant (OR, 2.92 [95% CI, 2.14–4]; PI, 1.97–7.11). The analysis for severe WMH demonstrated a strong and consistent association with all‐cause death (OR, 3.74; PI, 1.97–7.11) with no observed statistical heterogeneity (I 2=0%). The PI, while entirely >1, indicates variability in the magnitude of effect across studies, suggesting potential clinical heterogeneity despite the absence of statistical heterogeneity. Hence, the actual strength of the effect may differ across patient populations and study settings.

CMB and Recurrent Stroke

In patients with AIS, the presence of CMB increased the risk of recurrent stroke (HR, 1.85 [95% CI, 1.52–2.26]; PI, 1.19–2.88; k=16; N=12 178, E=1156; Figure S17). There was potential publication bias, evidenced by funnel plot asymmetry (Figure S18) and Egger's regression test yielding P<0.05 (intercept, 1.5 [95% CI, 0.3–2.69]; P=0.0275). A sensitivity analysis (Table S12) was performed, as two studies 60 , 61 were found to be outliers, with the result remaining statistically significant (HR, 1.89 [95% CI, 1.68–2.12]; PI, 1.63–2.18). The analysis reflected moderate heterogeneity (I 2=39%) with a narrow PI entirely >1, indicating a reproducible positive association in future studies.

The risk of recurrent stroke increases in parallel with CMB burden, rising nearly 3‐fold in patients with ≥5 CMB (HR, 2.88 [95% CI, 2.07–4.01]; PI, 1.5–5.53; k=4; N=6237, E=550; Figures S19–S21). For patients with ≥5 CMBs, heterogeneity was negligible (I 2=0%), and the PI remained entirely >1, indicating a robust and reproducible association with recurrent stroke in future studies. When stratified by CMB location, the risk of recurrent stroke was higher in patients with strictly lobar (HR, 1.91 [95% CI, 1.19–3.06]; PI, 0.838–4.35; k=4; N=4398; E=354; Figure S22) and mixed CMBs (HR, 1.95 [95% CI, 1.25–3.02]; PI, 0.78–4.88; k=4; N=4398; E=354; Figure S23) compared with the overall pooled estimate for any CMB. In contrast, strictly deep CMBs were associated with a lower but still significant risk (HR, 1.72 [95% CI, 1.03–2.87]; k=2; N=2909; E=123; Figure S24). For CMB location, the heterogeneity across subgroups was uniformly low (I 2=0%). However, PIs for strictly lobar (0.838–4.35) and mixed CMBs (0.78–4.88) were wide and crossed the null value, indicating greater uncertainty in the magnitude and potential for null effects in future studies. This likely reflects the limited number of included studies (k=4) and events, reducing precision.

Five studies 40 , 41 , 46 , 47 , 49 reported ORs as effect sizes, and 2 studies 40 , 41 had duplicate cohorts, as described above. We derived an aggregated effect size for the duplicate cohort by performing a fixed‐effect meta‐analysis before the random‐effects meta‐analysis. CMB presence increased the odds of recurrent stroke (OR, 1.86 [95% CI, 1.61–2.15]; PI, 1.53–2.25; k=17; N=9309; E=808; Figure S25). Like the HR analysis, there was no observed statistical heterogeneity (I 2=0%) and a narrow PI (1.53–2.25) entirely >1, demonstrating a strong and reproducible positive association in future studies.

CMBs and Cardiovascular Death

In patients with AIS, the presence of CMB increased the risk of cardiovascular death (HR, 1.47 [95% CI, 1.21–1.77]; PI, 1.07–2; k=6; N=5177; E=328; Figure S26). Only 1 study 55 examined the effect of CMB on the odds of cardiovascular death. We further calculated ORs for 5 studies 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 , 66 to increase the statistical power of our analysis. The presence of CMB was associated with an increased odds of cardiovascular death (OR, 1.69 [95% CI, 1.2–2.38]; PI, 1.1–2.58; k=6; N=3872; E=244; Figure S27). Across both HR and OR analyses, there was no observed statistical heterogeneity (I 2=0%) in either model. PIs for both estimates remained entirely >1 (HR PI, 1.07–2.00; OR PI, 1.10–2.58), indicating a robust and generalizable association across studies.

Higher CMB burden corresponded to a statistically insignificant, progressive increase in risk of cardiovascular death (Figures S28 through S30). Heterogeneity wise, all analyses showed wide PIs but low‐moderate heterogeneity. The PIs for all subgroups crossed the null value, including ≥5 CMBs (PI, 0.42–15.70), despite a statistically significant pooled HR. This suggests that the association between CMB burden and cardiovascular death lacks precision and consistency across studies, likely due to a limited number of included studies. When stratified by CMB location, the risk of cardiovascular death is higher in patients with mixed CMBs (HR, 1.61 [95% CI, 1.01–2.69]; k=2; N=3098; E=216; Figure S31) compared with the overall pooled estimate for any CMB. Strictly lobar CMBs were associated with a statistically insignificant increase in cardiovascular death risk (HR, 1.72 [95% CI, 0.06–46.3]; k=2; N=3098; E=216; Figure S32). At the time of writing, there were no studies examining the relationship between strictly deep CMBs and cardiovascular death.

CMBs and All‐Cause Death

In patients with AIS, the presence of CMBs was associated with a slight increase in risk of all‐cause death (HR, 1.18 [95% CI, 1.02–1.36]; PI=1.01–1.38; k=11; N=6815; E=990; Figure S33). There was no observed statistical heterogeneity (I 2=0%) and a narrow PI (1.01–1.38) entirely >1, demonstrating a strong and reproducible positive association in future studies. An increase in CMB burden was linked to a progressive but statistically insignificant increase in risk of all‐cause death (Figures S34–S36). At the time of writing, only 1 study 67 has examined the relationship between CMB distribution and all‐cause death.

DISCUSSION

In this systematic review and meta‐analysis (SRMA) of 50 studies, comprising a total of 44 140 patients with mean follow‐up periods ranging from 0.247 to 8.3 years, we demonstrate the robust association between CSVD markers, specifically WMHs and CMBs, and cardiovascular outcomes in patients with AIS.

Both WMHs and CMBs significantly predicted recurrent stroke in patients with AIS, consistent with findings of previous meta‐analyses, 24 , 25 , 26 with increasing severity correlating with higher risk. WMHs and CMBs reflect the cumulative effect of chronic cerebral small‐vessel pathology, including endothelial dysfunction, 68 blood–brain barrier disruption, 69 and subsequent chronic hypoperfusion. 70 The presence of these markers correlates with an increased risk for vascular events, underscoring their role as not just diagnostic markers, but also predictors of clinical trajectory in patients with AIS. The association between WMHs and recurrent stroke could be attributed to the ongoing vascular infarcts that predispose patients to further ischemic events, while the link between CMBs and recurrent stroke could reflect a compromised vascular integrity conducive to both ischemic and hemorrhagic sequelae.

Our analysis also revealed variability in the risk of recurrent stroke when stratified by CMB distribution, the risk being higher in patients with strictly lobar CMBs than those with strictly deep CMBs. This difference may be attributable to distinctions in the small‐vessel pathology underpinning each subtype, with strictly lobar CMBs being related to CAA and strictly deep CMBs linked to hypertensive arteriopathy. 71 Patients with mixed CMBs are at highest risk of stroke recurrence, concordant with the findings of a previous SRMA. 72 This could be related to the potentiation of risk resulting from the progression of lobar CMBs, as mixed CMBs often reflect concomitant amyloid and hypertension‐related vessel pathology. Our findings align with prior evidence suggesting that these pathologies synergistically accelerate lobar CMB progression, 73 which we found to be associated with a higher risk of stroke recurrence.

Our meta‐analysis also explored the implications of WMHs and CMBs on cardiovascular and all‐cause death in patients with AIS. WMHs were a significant predictor of all‐cause death but not cardiovascular death, while CMBs were a significant predictor of cardiovascular death but only modestly associated with all‐cause death. Interestingly, the risk of all‐cause death remained relatively consistent across varying degrees of WMH severity, with HRs clustering around 1.5 to 1.58 for WMHs of any severity, 1.49 for extensive WMHs, and 1.48 for severe WMHs. WMHs are a crucial indicator of CSVD, reflecting structural damage to the brain's microvascular network. The severity of WMHs, particularly at higher Fazekas scores, reflects impaired blood flow, reduced vascular reserve in the white matter, and the fragility of subcortical arterioles, all heightening susceptibility to ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes. However, the relatively consistent HRs for all‐cause death, regardless of WMH severity, may point to the multifactorial nature of death in this population. 74 In addition to the WMH burden, systemic vascular risk factors, comorbid conditions, and the effects of aging likely contribute to overall mortality risk. Unlike the risk of recurrent stroke, which is closely tied to the progression of small‐vessel disease and increasing WMH severity, the impact of WMHs on all‐cause death may be less directly attributable to their pathophysiological effects and more influenced by broader health factors.

Strengths and Limitations

This SRMA demonstrates several key strengths that enhances the reliability and validity of the findings. First, we obtained a large sample size from multiple cohorts spanning different countries, which increases the generalizability and external validity of our results. The inclusion of diverse populations from various geographic regions helps to ensure that the findings are broadly applicable across different health care settings. By pooling data from numerous studies, our meta‐analysis integrates a wide range of patient demographics and clinical characteristics, allowing for a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of WMHs and CMBs on clinical outcomes in patients with AIS.

The statistical methods applied in this meta‐analysis are robust and appropriate for addressing the study objectives. The use of random‐effects models accounts for between‐study heterogeneity, providing more generalized and reliable pooled estimates. Additionally, the application of the Restricted Maximum Likelihood estimator and Knapp–Hartung adjustments adds to the robustness of the analysis, ensuring accurate estimation of the CIs. While I 2 provides a relative estimate of heterogeneity, it is limited in capturing the absolute dispersion of true effects. By supplementing our analysis with PIs, we provide a more clinically interpretable measure of heterogeneity. We also explored potential sources of between‐study heterogeneity through meta‐regression analyses. The detailed sensitivity analyses performed to assess the robustness of the findings further enhance the reliability of the results. By identifying and removing outliers and influential studies, the meta‐analysis ensures that the overall conclusions are not unduly influenced by individual studies with extreme results. Furthermore, publication bias was low overall, appearing only in our analysis of CMBs and risk of recurrent stroke.

However, we do acknowledge several limitations. First, our study is limited by the heterogeneity in WMH assessment methods across the included studies. While most used the Fazekas scale, others used alternative scoring systems such as the ARWMC, DSWMH, and van Swieten scales. Although these scales are well validated and exhibit strong correlations, minor differences in scoring criteria mean that direct comparisons may not be entirely perfect. Nonetheless, the robust correlations between these methods support the overall validity of our findings.

Our analysis was also limited by scarce current literature examining the relationship between WMHs or CMBs and certain outcomes of interest, namely, cardiovascular and all‐cause death. For instance, only 2 studies investigated the impact of severe WMHs on the risk of recurrent stroke. The paucity of studies in this category represents a key limitation, as the small number of studies restricts the generalizability of these findings. Another significant limitation was the lack of complete data on key covariates, such as antithrombotic use, in most studies. This gap is particularly critical given that stroke recurrence was our primary outcome, and antithrombotic therapy is a major determinant of recurrence. The exclusion of these studies may have affected the comprehensiveness of our meta‐regression analyses.

Most importantly, we did not explore the risks of WMHs and CMBs for individual stroke subtypes, such as IS and ICH, and instead analyzed their association with recurrent stroke as a combined outcome. Evaluating risk of CMBs on relative risks of IS and ICH is important in guiding initiation and duration of antithrombotic therapy. Recent SRMAs have produced conflicting results in outcomes of patients with AIS with CMBs on anticoagulation. Charidimou et al, 75 in a meta‐analysis involving 1552 patients across 8 centers, illustrated that baseline CMB presence, particularly ≥5 CMBs, was associated with higher future ICH risk but no association with recurrent IS. Contrarily, Wilson et al, 24 in a pooled cohort analysis of 20 322 patients, reported a substantially higher absolute risk of IS regardless of CMB burden, distribution, and type of antithrombotic treatment. Studies assessing ICH risk in patients on oral anticoagulants 76 and antiplatelets 77 report a significantly higher risk of future ICH. Interestingly, 1 study 78 evaluating antiplatelet treatment outcomes in 2 large cohorts of patients with AIS, reported a time‐dependent risk for patients with higher CMB burden. In patients with ≥5 CMBs, the risk of ICH increased steeply and matched that of IS after the first year following the index IS. While a lobar distribution of CMBs is believed to portend a higher hemorrhagic stroke risk due to possible association with CAA, recent SRMAs 24 , 72 have demonstrated that mixed and deep CMBs confer a higher ICH risk even when accounting for antithrombotic use. First, this may be due to low diagnostic accuracy of strictly lobar pattern of CMBs in predicting CAA in patients without incident ICH or dementia. 79 Second, the lobar microbleed topography needs to be considered, as strictly intracortical/juxtacortical CMBs are more strongly associated with CAA than subcortical ones. 80 Evidently, more studies are required to assess not only the influence of CMB burden and distribution on ICH risk relative to recurrent IS, but also if there is a temporal change in risks. There is also an ethnic correlation between CMB and ICH risk. A previous SRMA 81 elucidated that the association of CMB with ICH was significant for Asian cohorts (1915 patients; OR, 10.43) but borderline and of lower magnitude for Western cohorts (885 patients; OR, 3.87). Hence, it is also prudent to conduct and stratify future analysis by ethnicity, given a higher prevalence of CMB 82 and an inherent predisposition of Asians to ICH. 83 , 84

Future Directions

Future research should focus on larger, well‐designed cohort studies to better elucidate the impact of WMHs and CMBs on the clinical trajectory of patients with AIS, particularly pertaining to cardiovascular death. Investigating additional potential confounders and their interactions with WMHs and CMBs would also prove to be beneficial. Moreover, developing targeted therapeutic strategies for patients with significant WMHs and CMBs may help mitigate the risk of adverse outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

This systematic review and meta‐analysis affirms the prognostic significance of WMHs and CMBs in patients with AIS. The presence and severity of these MRI markers are associated with increased risks of recurrent stroke. Future studies should focus on thoroughly exploring the relationship between CSVD and death in diverse populations, enabling a more statistically robust analysis.
