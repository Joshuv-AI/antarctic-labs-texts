The effect of hyper-induced SOS response on evolution in Escherichia coli
Authors: Ruoxuan Zhao, Lars Jelsbak, Yufei Zhao, John Elmerdahl Olsen, Line Elnif Thomsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe SOS response, a conserved bacterial DNA repair system, plays a critical role in adaptation under stress, by inducing spontaneous mutations. This study compared the evolutionary dynamics of Escherichia coli and a derivative of this strain with a constitutively activated SOS response (SOS*), focusing on growth phenotype, competitive fitness, genome evolution, and metabolism over 90 days of evolution.ResultsLaboratory evolution revealed that both wild-type and SOS* populations increased in growth rates, reduced average cell lengths, improved competitive fitness, and expanded their catabolic substrate utilization over time. However, SOS* strains, which revealed initial reduced growth rate, fitness and carbon-utilization, adapted at a faster rate than the wild-type, and obtained levels comparable to the wild-type. Whole-genome sequencing identified higher mutation rates and distinct mutation patterns in the SOS* populations compared to the wild-type populations, particularly an increase in non-synonymous substitutions, suggesting positive selection for adaptive traits. Mutational analyses revealed convergence in regulatory and metabolic pathways in both wild-type and SOS* groups. However, SOS* accumulated a higher number of mutations in regulatory and metabolic genes, consistent with increased phenotypic flexibility during adaptation. Despite this, their fitness and carbon source utilization abilities did not surpass those of the evolved wild-types.ConclusionOur results indicate that while SOS-mediated mutagenesis enhances genetic diversity and accelerates adaptation, its long-term impact on adaptive evolution may be limited under non-selective environments due to the sustained SOS activation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04877-0.Keywords: SOS response, SOS mutagenesis, Adaptive evolution, Metabolic regulation

Background

Hypermutability is characterized by an elevated rate of spontaneous mutations caused by DNA replication errors, defects in error correction mechanisms, or other genetic and environmental disruptions. Mutations provide the ultimate source of heritable variation on which natural selection acts, although most mutations are deleterious rather than beneficial [1]. In changing environments, strong selection for advantageous mutations can favor the evolution of bacteria with higher mutation rates [2]. In E. coli, hypermutators comprise approximately 0.1 % of the natural population [3], with a higher incidence observed in some clinical isolates [4–6]. Generally, mismatch repair (MMR) and SOS response are the two primary mechanisms responsible for bacterial hypermutation, thus providing a diverse repertoire of genetic variants, some of which confer survival advantages under specific selective pressures [7]. While the MMR system has been extensively studied [8, 9], the SOS response has been characterized mainly for its vital role in bacterial survival under stress, and its contribution to environmental adaptation warrant further investigation [10, 11].

DNA damage poses a ubiquitous threat to genome integrity, and bacteria have developed specialized and conserved pathways to repair or tolerate it [12]. The SOS response is such a conserved and tightly regulated bacterial DNA repair system first described in E. coli. It is characterized by DNA replication arrest and inhibition of cell division during the repair process [13, 14]. To date, more than 48 genes involved in DNA protection, repair, replication, mutagenesis, and metabolism have been identified as part of the SOS response [15, 16]. Under normal conditions, these genes are repressed by the transcriptional regulator LexA, maintaining the SOS response at a basal level. However, upon DNA damage, the generated single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) activates the RecA protein, which forms RecA nucleofilaments that promote LexA auto-cleavage, thereby triggering the SOS response [17]. While external factors such as UV irradiation, antibiotic treatment, oxidative stress, environmental stress, and horizontal gene transfer can induce the SOS response, the ultimate trigger is the formation of ssDNA [18]. Once activated, the SOS response repair damaged DNA through one or more pathways, including homologous recombination (HR), nucleotide excision repair (NER), and translesion synthesis (TLS) [18]. Among these, the TLS pathway, involving DNA polymerase V (UmuD’2C) and DNA polymerase IV (DinB), replicates damaged DNA in a mutagenic manner, contributing to hypermutability and genomic plasticity.

Laboratory evolution experiments are methods to study bacterial adaptation to specific environmental conditions [19]. Previous evolution experiments have demonstrated that E. coli can evolve mutator phenotypes [20, 21]. Barrick et al. reported that a hypermutator carrying a mutT mutation became established between 20,000 and 30,000 generations, followed by subsequent reductions in the mutation rate along two branches of the phylogenetic tree that partially compensated for the hypermutability [22, 23]. In the current study, we aimed to understand the role of hypermutability induced by the SOS response in bacterial evolution. To avoid using SOS-inducing compounds, we used an E. coli strain with a constitutively induced SOS response, inherently conferring hypermutability, to investigate bacterial physiology and genome dynamics during 90 days of continuous passaging in laboratory media. Evolutionary trade-offs associated with bacterial growth, morphology, competitive fitness, metabolism, and genetic plasticity were identified, providing a deeper understanding of the role of the SOS response in microbial evolution.

Methods

Growth analysis in LB and morphology investigation

Overnight cultures of E. coli MG1655 (WT), its derivative strain SOS*, and the complemented SOS*_C [24] were diluted to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.05 in 10 mL LB or Davis minimal broth (pH 7+/- 0.2) (Sigma-Aldrich, Denmark), supplemented with 1000 µg/mL D-glucose (DM1000) medium and incubated at 37℃ with shaking at 180 rpm. Three biological replicates were performed for each strain. Growth curves were plotted and analyzed using Prism10 software.

For growth rate determination, fresh cultures of ancestral and adapted strains were adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard and then diluted 2-fold in DM1000 medium. A volume of 200 µL of each diluted culture was dispensed into individual wells of a 96-well microtiter plate, with three technical triplicates per strain. OD600 was measured every 20 min for 24 h using a Bioscreen system (Bioscreen, Finland) at 37℃ with medium shaking. Growth curves were plotted and the growth rates (µ) were determined from the steepest portion of the exponential growth phase, as µ = (ΔlnOD600)/Δt, where t is the time in hour and OD600 is the value determined at time t. Doubling times (Dt) was calculated based on the growth rate: Dt = ln2/µ. Bacteria at exponential growth phase were collected and observed under a phase-contrast microscope, and the bacterial length was measured using ImageJ software (version 1.54j).

Laboratory evolution experiment

A single colony of WT and the SOS* strain [24], was used as ancestral strains for the evolution experiment. At the outset, each strain was propagated in three independent tubes, labeled P1, P2, and P3. Evolution experiments were conducted by daily serial transfer in 10 mL of Davis minimal broth (pH 7+/- 0.2), supplemented with 25 µg/mL D-glucose (DM25), incubated at 37℃ with 180 rpm shaking. At each daily passage, approximately 1–2 × 105 cells were transferred into fresh medium based on viable plate counts. Evolution was continued for 90 days. Samples from each population were collected, and samples from three time points, day 15, 45, 90, corresponding to 0, ~ 100, 330, and 711 generations respectively, were used for further analysis.

Realtime quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

RNA extraction and reverse transcription were performed as previously described with three biological replications [24]. The genes sulA (primers 5’-ATCAGCCCATGATGACGCAA-3’ and 5’-CCCAGATGCCTGAACCCATT-3’) and recN (primers 5’-CACGGCGCGTAAAATGGAAA-3’ and 5’-ACCTGAGTTGATTCGCCGAG-3’) were used to monitor bacterial SOS levels, with gapA (primers 5’-ACTGACTGGTATGGCGTTCC-3’ and 5’-GTTGCAGCTTTTTCCAGACG-3’) serving as the reference gene.

Relative fitness assays

Relative fitness was assessed by competing each population against an E. coli MG1655-derived arabinose-negative strain EE90, which was selected in a MG1655 transposon library as a mutant which formed red colonies on a tetrazolium and arabinose (TA) agar plate (this work). The stability of the arabinose-negative phenotype of EE90 was confirmed after 10 days of propagation before the competition assays were conducted. Overnight cultures were diluted 10,000-fold in DM25 medium, after which 50 µL of each competitor was mixed and transferred into 10 mL of DM25 medium for co-culture. Competition was conducted over 24 h at 37 °C with shaking at 120 rpm. Colony-forming units (CFUs) per mL were determined by serial dilution and plating on TA agar plate at t = 0 and 24 h. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) and mutation identification

The ancestral WT and SOS*, and populations after 45 and 90 days of continuous passage, as well as three colonies from each population isolated on DM100 agar plates after 45 and 90 days, were collected and sequenced. Genomic DNA was extracted using Maxwell RSC cultured Cells DNA kit (Promega, USA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. DNA integrity was evaluated by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel, and DNA quantity was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A DNA library was then prepared using the Flex DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Paired-end sequencing performed on an Illumina MiSeq platform using a Nextera XT v3 kit, with a read length of 2 × 300 bp. The quality of raw reads were analyzed using FastQC v0.12.0 [25], and low-quality reads and adapters were removed in the Qiagen CLC Genomics workbench v24.0.2. Candidate mutations of each evolved population and colonies, including base substitutions and small/large indels, were identified by comparing their genome with the corresponding ancestor using Breseq pipeline v0.38.0 [26]. Given the ~ 30× mean sequencing depth, this approach robustly detected fixed and high-frequency mutations (variant allele frequency, VAF, ≥ 10–20%) in the population, while variants at lower frequencies (VAF < 10%) may have remained undetected. The ratio between nonsynonymous and synonymous substitution rate (dN/dS) was calculated using R studio software (version 2023.12.1 + 402).

Mutation rate estimation

Fluctuation tests, a standard method for experimentally estimating spontaneous mutation rates based on the emergence of phenotypic resistance, were performed as previously described in two ancestral strains [27]. Briefly, 20 tubes were prepared, each containing 1 mL of DM250 medium, inoculated with 1 ⋅ 104 cells/mL. After incubation at 37 °C with 120 rpm agitation for 24 h, 50 µL aliquots from six randomly selected tubes were plated on LB agar plates to determine the final cell number (Nt). The entire culture from each tube was plated on LB agar supplemented with 50 µg/mL rifampicin to determine the number of rifampicin-resistant cells. The estimated number of mutations per tube (m) was calculated from the number of resistant colonies using the maximum likelihood method (http://shinyflan.its.manchester.ac.uk/). The mutation rate (µ) was calculated as m divided by Nt and multiplied by the volume of each tube. The mutation rate (µ) based on WGS was calculated as: µ = M/G⋅N, where M is the total number of observed mutations identified using gdtools from the breseq pipeline [26], G is the genome size in base pairs, N is the total number of generations.

Carbon source utilization analysis

Bacterial D-glucose utilization ability was manually measured in DM1000. Phenotype MicroArray PM1 plate (Biolog, Hayward, CA) was used to analyze a wider spectrum of carbon utilizations. The two ancestral strains and WT-P2 and SOS*-P2 from 90 days, were streaked onto LB agar plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Cells were then swabbed from the plates and suspended in IF-0 GN Base inoculation fluid (Biolog, USA) to a density corresponding to 85% transmittance, as measured by the Biolog turbidimeter. The cell suspensions were then supplemented with an appropriate volume of Biolog redox dye mix D (100⋅) (Biolog, USA). Growth data were analyzed, and the carbon-source utilization capacity of each strain was calculated and normalized using OmniLog OL_FM/Kin software v1.20.02, then visualized by using ChiPlot v2.6.1 [28]. Metabolic pathway analysis of the different carbon sources used was analyzed and visualized using MetaboAnalyst v6.0 [29].

Bioinformatics analysis

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted in Python 3.7 (statsmodels), with fitness as the dependent variable, cell length and doubling time as independent variables. For phylogenetic analysis, sequences of ancestral and adapted colonies were aligned, and a maximum-likelihood phylogeny was inferred under the TIM + F nucleotide substitution model with 1000 bootstrap replicates using IQ-tree v2.3.6 [30]. Further mutation statistical analysis was performed using gdtools from the breseq pipeline [26]. Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) analysis of day 90 samples was conducted using STRING v12.0 [31] and visualized in Cytoscape v3.10 [32]. Cluster of Orthologous Groups (COGs) analysis was performed using EggNOG-mapper v2.1.13 with default parameters [33] and the results were visualized using ChiPlot v2.6.1 [28].

Results

Evolution experiment

To investigate the impact of the SOS response on evolutionary dynamics in E. coli, we performed an evolution experiment with a previously described hyper-induced SOS mutant with constitutively increased levels of SOS gene expression (SOS*), and its corresponding wild-type (WT) [24]. Samples of both whole population and of single colonies at 0, 15, 45, and 90 days, were collected to monitor bacterial evolution trajectories (List of isolates can be found in Table S1).

Increased growth rate and shorter cells are favored during evolution

Relative growth rates of three independent populations (P1, P2 and P3) in the WT and SOS* groups were determined at the chosen time points. At day 0, the hyper-induced SOS response significantly impaired bacterial growth (p < 0.0001), with the growth rate of the ancestral SOS* strain (Ac_SOS*) being 0.6 times that of ancestral WT (Ac_WT) (Fig. 1A). Complementation with lexA in the Ac_SOS* (strain SOS*_C) restored bacterial growth to the level comparable to Ac_WT (Figure S1). In the adapted populations, both WT and SOS* groups showed significant increases (p < 0.001) in growth rates after 15 days compared to their respective ancestors (Fig. 1A). WT populations increased from an initial relative growth rate of 1.0 to ~ 1.4, 1.4 and 1.2 respectively. The SOS* populations exhibited a more pronounced increase, from an initial of 0.6 to ~ 1.2, 1.2 and 1.1, respectively. This enhanced growth performance was largely sustained throughout the experiment for both groups, with only minor fluctuations observed from day 15 to day 90 (Fig. 1A).

In parallel, significant changes in cell morphology were observed in both WT and SOS* populations (Fig. 1B). At day 0, Ac_WT cells were short and relatively uniform (1.9 ± 0.4 μm), whereas Ac_SOS* cells were significantly longer than Ac_WT (p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 1.01) and displayed pronounced filamentation-associated heterogeneity, with cell lengths ranging from 1.1 to 40.8 μm. Complementation with lexA in Ac_SOS* (SOS*_C) reduced the filamentation (Fig. 1B). Consistent with previous lexA complementation studies, the hyper-induced SOS phenotype of SOS* was caused by SNPs in the lexA gene [24], confirming that the observed increase in cell length was associated with this response. After 90 days of evolution, both WT and SOS* populations exhibited reduced cell length relative to their ancestral states. Among SOS populations, SOS*-P2 showed the most pronounced reduction with mean cell length decreasing to 1.7 ± 0.3 μm. Overall, the evolved WT populations (WT-L123; 1.7 ± 0.3 μm) remained significantly shorter (p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 0.87) than the evolved SOS* populations (SOS*-L123; 2.0 ± 0.5 μm). Despite the apparent evolution towards similarity to the WT phenotypes, analysis of SOS gene expression revealed that the evolved SOS* populations maintained the hyper-induced SOS response (Figure S2).

Filament length and fitness are directly associated during growth under SOS induction

Relative fitness (W) was determined through competition assays against the arabinose-negative strain EE90 (Fig. 2A). At day 0, Ac_WT showed a baseline relative fitness of 1.0, while the Ac_SOS* exhibited significantly (p = 0.0003) lower fitness of ~ 0.5 (p = 0.0003). By day 45, both WT and SOS* populations showed significant fitness improvement compared to their corresponding progenitors (with p = 0.008 and p = 0.001, respectively), both reaching an average fitness of 1.2. Notably, SOS*-P2 achieved the highest fitness of 1.3 after 45 days, effectively compensating for its initial disadvantage. By day 90, WT populations continued to improve, with fitness levels increasing to ~ 1.3–1.6. In contrast, no further significant increases were detected in the SOS* populations, showing further limited gains beyond day 45. While SOS*-P2 reached a fitness of 1.5, SOS*-P1 and SOS*-P3 remained at ~ 1.2, reflecting a slower fitness trajectory during this phase of evolution. Strong relationships between filament length, doubling time (Dt), and fitness were observed in the SOS* group (Figure S3). However, multiple linear regression analysis revealed that doubling time was more directly associated with changes in fitness, while filament length was more likely to influence fitness indirectly, with a much smaller coefficient of 0.06 (p > 0.05) (Fig. 2B).

SOS response accelerates mutation rates and drives genetic divergence

Mutations contribute to a pool of variation essential for adaptive change. Mutation rates for the two ancestral strains were assessed using the fluctuation test. Ac_SOS* exhibited an approximately 3-fold higher mutation rate (~ 7.1 × 10⁻⁹ bp−1gen− 1) compared to Ac_WT (~ 2.4 × 10⁻⁹ bp−1gen− 1) (Fig. 3A). WGS of colonies (labeled as A, B and C) (Table S1) from each evolved population revealed that across all populations in both groups, higher mutation rates were observed during the first 45 days compared to the subsequent 45 days (Fig. 3A). While the mutation rate in SOS*-P1 decreased to a level comparable to that of the WT populations, the hyper-induced SOS response resulted in significantly higher mutation rates and a greater accumulation of mutations in the SOS* group at both day 45 and day 90 in the two other populations compared to WT populations during the same period (p < 0.05).

Despite sharing a common genetic background and the same environmental conditions, the mutational landscapes diverged between the evolved populations within each group. In the phylogenetic tree, the WT and SOS* populations exhibited distinct separation, with colonies from the three independent populations (P1, P2, P3) within each group clustering together while displaying inter-population variation (Fig. 3B). Notably, the SOS* populations exhibited longer branching patterns compared to the WT populations, with WT colonies accumulating 2–4 mutation events and SOS* colonies accumulating 2–7 mutation events per colony by day 90.

To investigate mutational patterns, mutations in each colony from both groups were analyzed. At day 0, 25 mutations spanning 10 genes and 3 intergenic regions were identified across the three WT populations (Table S2). In contrast, 37 mutations were identified across the three SOS* populations at day 90, spanning 16 genes and 3 intergenic regions (Table S2). Mutational analysis revealed that base substitutions were the primary mutation type induced by the SOS response, as evidenced by approximately twice as many base substitutions in the SOS* group compared to the WT group (Table 1). In the WT group, the number of non-synonymous substitutions was comparable to intergenic substitutions. However, in the SOS* group, most base substitutions were non-synonymous and nonsense mutations that potentially could influence protein structure and function. To evaluate the strength and type of natural selection on genes, the ratio between rates of non-synonymous and synonymous substitutions (dN/dS) was calculated [34]. Non-synonymous substitution rates were higher than synonymous substitution rates in both WT and SOS* groups (Table 1), indicating positive selection. However, the relative dN/dS ratio in the SOS* group was approximately twice as high as that in the WT group, suggesting a stronger positive selection pressure and a higher proportion of adaptive mutations in the SOS* populations.

Cluster Orthologous Groups (COGs) analysis revealed that genes in the “information storage and processing” category of genes were the most affected in both the WT group (n = 15) and the SOS* group (n = 25), with the rpoB gene being commonly mutated (Fig. 3C). Mutations related to metabolism were also prevalent, with n = 9 in the WT group and n = 13 in the SOS* groups. Notably, the “cellular process and signaling” category of genes was substantially impacted in the SOS* group (n = 17), including mutations in the cell division inhibition gene sulA and the outer membrane gene ompC, likely reflecting an adaptive response to mitigate the burden imposed by the hyper-induced SOS response (Fig. 3C and Table S2).

SOS response enhances regulatory and metabolic mutations for adaptive advantage

Population sequencing revealed that in SOS*-P2, mutations in the yeaG gene accounted for 26% of the population at day 45 but vanished by day 90, while mutations in ycgB (88% to 100%), ptsP (14% to 100%), and hemN (10% to 61%) expanded during the same period (list of all mutations can be found in Table S3.1 and S3.2). In the WT-P2 at day 45, only a single mutation site in the rpoB gene was detected at 100% frequency, but after an additional 45 days, 33 distinct mutations in the rpoB emerged, each with varying frequencies, highlighting ongoing genetic diversification. When comparing WT day 45 to day 90 populations, more mutations were found at day 90, however, most of the mutations obtained after 45 days were lost at day 90, with only 2 mutations in each population remaining. In the SOS* populations, up to 11 mutations which occurred in samples from day 45 were still present after 90 days. This suggests that more beneficial mutations occur in the hyper-induced SOS* during initial growth. Furthermore, the sequencing revealed that only three genes (ycgB, sapB and rpoC) were mutated in more than one WT population. An almost similar pattern was seen for the SOS* populations where yoeF mutations were found in 6 to 18.2% in all three day 45 populations, but after 90 days, they were lost in SOS*-P3. pykF, gltP and rpoB mutations occurred in two of the SOS* populations.

By day 90, Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network analysis further underscored functional or physical connections among mutations within each group, revealing distinct evolutionary trajectories influenced by the WT or SOS* ancestral background (Fig. 4). In the WT group, the mutational network appeared more dispersed, with numerous small clusters targeting diverse pathways, including amino acid metabolism, central carbon metabolism, and stress response pathways (Fig. 4A). In contrast, the SOS* group exhibited a more centralized network with tightly connected clusters, particularly around pgi and zwf (Fig. 4B). Neither of the original mutations in the ancestral SOS* strain was affected by the evolution experiment.

Hyper induced SOS response is associated with changes in metabolic capability

Several mutations in metabolic genes were identified, indicating bacterial adaptation during growth in DM25. Growth analysis of Ac_WT and Ac_SOS* showed comparable growth in rich medium, but Ac_SOS* exhibited significantly impaired growth in D-glucose containing minimal medium (Figure S1). Based on this, we further analyzed the growth capabilities of Ac_SOS* over a wider range of carbon sources on the PM1 Plate using the Biolog assay. Reduced growth of Ac_SOS* compared to Ac_WT was observed when utilizing various other carbohydrates, carboxylic acids, and amino acids (Fig. 5). Enrichment analysis of the top 20 compounds, according to the differences in growth between the two, revealed enrichment of core carbohydrates, amino acids, and carbon fixation pathways (Figure S5).

After 90 days, the evolved WT exhibited moderate improvements in utilizing various carbon sources, whereas the evolved SOS* displayed more pronounced changes (Fig. 5). Notably, the utilization of glycolytic intermediates associated with glucose metabolism significantly increased in both groups, with the evolved SOS* showing the largest improvements. Furthermore, in both evolved strains, mutations lead to increased utilization of carbon sources, not associated directly with glucose utilization. Because of the larger changes in the evolved SOS* populations, the comparison between the evolved SOS* and WT strains showed much smaller variations in carbon utilization, compared to the comparison of the two ancestral strains.

Discussion

The SOS response has been shown to be associated with increased mutation rates and has been implicated in bacterial adaptability [35–37]. In the current study, we examined the impact of SOS-mutagenesis independently of SOS-inducing compounds, by performing an evolutionary experiment on a hyper-induced SOS response mutant and its corresponding WT. Throughout evolution, SOS* populations maintained higher mutation rates than WT populations, although mutation rates declined in both groups over time. Analysis of mutational spectra revealed a distinct distribution of mutation types in SOS*. In particular, SOS* populations showed a pronounced enrichment of base-substitution mutations relative to WT populations, consistent with the known activity of the SOS-induced DNA Polymerase V [38]. These substitutions were predominantly non-synonymous and were accompanied by the emergence of nonsense mutations, resulting in a substantially elevated relative dN/dS ratio in the SOS* populations compared to the WT populations. In addition, large deletions were observed exclusively in the SOS* populations, whereas mobile element insertions were rare, suggesting that the altered mutational spectrum reflects increased replication stress and error-prone DNA repair under constitutive SOS induction, rather than elevated transposition activity.

As populations become better adapted, the selective advantage of high mutation rates diminishes, and variants with reduced mutation rates can be favored because they are less likely to accumulate additional deleterious mutations [22]. This is consistent with our observations where an apparent reduction in mutation accumulation was observed during the last 45 days of evolution. Previous long-term evolution experiments have described sustained adaptation that proceeds at progressively slower rates consistent with a power-law relationship over more than 50,000 generations. Furthermore, long-term evolution experiments also revealed step-like fitness trajectories, consistent with selective sweeps of beneficial mutations [39, 40]. In this study, fitness trajectory of the SOS* strain is not exclusively consistent with the early evolution patterns previously observed for other high-mutator background [22]. However, SOS* populations start from lower initial fitness but exhibit faster adaptation, likely owing to a larger supply of accessible beneficial mutations. Therefore, the fitness gains observed in the SOS* populations likely reflect the combined effects of lower initial fitness and the accessibility of beneficial mutations, rather than being attributable solely to an increased mutation supply.

The ancestral SOS* exhibited filamentation due to the hyper-induced SOS response. However, during the evolutionary experiment, bacteria became shorter in both groups, although the evolved SOS* remained longer than evolved WT after 90 days. The Growth Law states that bacterial size is influenced by the interplay between central carbon metabolism, cell cycle progression, and biosynthetic capacity [41]. For many rod-shaped bacteria, mean cell size scales exponentially with nutrient-determined growth rate [42]. Notably, in contrast to prior evolution experiments, where cell size increased with fitness [39], WT cells in the current study became shorter after 90 days of evolution. This reduction in size likely reflects an optimization of the surface-area-to-volume ratio to enhance resource uptake efficiency under nutrient-limited conditions during the early stages of adaptation. Although SOS-induced filaments consist of chains of connected bacterial cells post-division [43], we observed a similar exponential relationship between filament length and growth rate. Shorter filament lengths in the SOS* populations indicated adaptation to sulA overexpression. SOS*_P2 displayed the shortest filaments among the evolved SOS* populations, which could be caused by the acquisition of a sulA mutation (Q47*). Furthermore, two mutations in rpoB were identified in the evolved SOS*_P2 (P520L) and SOS*_P3 (D516G). In many E. coli evolution experiments, rpoB mutations arise frequently and are associated with adaptive changes that can alleviate metabolic bottlenecks to growth [44–47]. These mutations may indirectly modulate cell division and stress responses, thereby alleviating filamentation and reducing cell length via unrevealed mechanisms.

Adaptive evolution under constant conditions has been associated with changes in metabolic capacity, including losses of unused functions. Early work suggested such losses reflected antagonistic pleiotropy and/or mutation accumulation [21]. However, subsequent work by Leiby and Marx showed that several key observations, including the frequent early improvement of alternative substrate performance during adaptation, strong lineage-specific heterogeneity, and pronounced effects in mutator lines, are inconsistent with a trade-off–based origin of specialization and are more parsimoniously explained by mutation accumulation under relaxed selection [48]. Consistent with this framework, we observed that both WT and SOS* populations displayed broadened substrate utilization after 90 days of evolution. Such early gains are consistent with beneficial pleiotropy arising during adaptation to a constant environment, but we do not preclude the emergence of metabolic specialization over longer evolutionary timescales. Notably, the ancestral SOS* strain began from a globally constrained state, characterized by impaired utilization of diverse carbon sources compared to WT. Under such conditions, selection is more likely to favor mutations that alleviate global physiological constraints rather than affecting a single metabolic pathway, leading to pleiotropy improvements across multiple substrates. Mutations in SOS* populations were organized into fewer clusters than in WT populations, resulting in a mutational network in which genetic changes were concentrated within several small, functionally interconnected sets of metabolic genes. Together, these results indicate that, over the short evolutionary timescale examined here, SOS-mediated mutagenesis reshapes adaptive trajectories by biasing the spectrum and accessibility of beneficial mutations toward metabolic targets with pleiotropic effects, thereby promoting early metabolic generalization rather than rapid specialization.

Conclusion

While SOS-mediated mutagenesis enhances genetic variation and facilitates rapid early adaptation from a low-fitness ancestral state, it does not result in superior fitness or broader metabolic capacity over the experimental timescale. Our results show that SOS activation alters the trajectory of adaptation by biasing beneficial mutations toward pleiotropic metabolic targets, promoting early metabolic generalization. These findings provided a deeper understanding of how bacteria cope with trade-offs associated with the SOS response and provide valuable insights into the evolutionary dynamics of the stress response and their impact on bacterial survival in challenging environments.
