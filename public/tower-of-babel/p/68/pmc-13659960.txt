Clinical characteristics and risk factors of cytomegalovirus infection in patients with good syndrome
Authors: Qi Chen, Ruxuan Chen, Yujie Shi, Shiyang Wang, Zhiyi Li, Yupei Zhang, Chi Shao, Hui Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGood Syndrome (GS) is a rare immunodeficiency disorder classically defined by the triad of thymoma, hypogammaglobulinemia, and recurrent infections. Cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection is the most frequently reported viral infection in patients with GS. This study aimed to characterize the clinical features and immunological alterations in patients with GS complicated by CMV infection.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of GS patients with or without CMV infection at Peking Union Medical College Hospital from January 2015 to December 2024. Logistic regression and COX proportional hazards regression model were employed to identify potential risk factors for CMV infection and prognostic factors of overall survival among GS patients.ResultsAmong the 27 enrolled patients, 15 developed CMV infection: 1 presented with CMV pneumonia, 6 was clinically diagnosed with CMV pneumonia, 6 with isolated CMV DNAemia, 1 with CMV colitis, and 1 with CMV retinitis. Peripheral blood CD4 + T-cell counts were significantly lower in the CMV group than non-CMV group (156 [116–210] vs. 374 [200–443] cells/µL; P < 0.05). The CMV group exhibited a higher rate of glucocorticoid (GC) use, greater cumulative GC exposure prior to diagnosis, and higher frequencies of respiratory pathogen isolation and fungal infection compared with the non-CMV group. Multivariable logistic regression identified lower serum albumin (P = 0.012; OR, 0.743; 95% CI: 0.58–0.938) and lower serum IgG (P = 0.041; OR, 0.565; 95% CI: 0.327–0.978) as potential risk factors for CMV infection in GS patients.ConclusionGS patients with CMV infection exhibited lower lymphocyte counts, higher glucocorticoid administration rate and increased susceptibility to respiratory and opportunistic fungal infections. During active CMV infection, these patients consistently displayed CD8+ T-cell lymphocytosis. Hypoalbuminemia and low serum IgG were associated with an increased risk of CMV infection. However, it is important to emphasize that the factors identified in our study apply to the broad definition of CMV infection and may not specifically predict end-organ disease.Keywords: Good Syndrome, Immunodeficiency, Cytomegalovirus infection, Thymoma, Retrospective study

Background

The thymoma-associated immunodeficiency syndrome, commonly known as Good Syndrome (GS), is a rare form of combined immunodeficiency first described by Robert Good in 1954 [1]. The condition is characterized by the presence of thymoma, hypogammaglobulinemia, a reduction or absence of peripheral blood B lymphocytes, and decreased levels of CD4 + T cells. Thymoma, a relatively rare anterior mediastinal tumor, arises from thymic epithelial cells. Patients with thymoma frequently present with comorbid autoimmune diseases or immunodeficiency [2]. Prognostic risk factors for patients with GS as reported in the current literature include thymoma status, cellular immunodeficiency, sinopulmonary infections, central nervous system infections, and bloodstream infections [3]. The clinical manifestations of GS include thymoma, infections due to immunodeficiency, autoimmune disorders, and/or secondary malignant neoplasms. Patients with GS demonstrate an increased susceptibility to various opportunistic pathogens, with CMV infection being the most frequently reported [4, 5]. CMV exhibits a seroprevalence of up to 60% in developed countries and over 90% in developing countries [6]. It establishes lifelong latency following primary infection and can reactivate to cause severe end-organ disease (EOD) in immunocompromised hosts [7]. A systematic review of 162 GS patients identified CMV infection as the most prevalent viral pathogen in this population [3]. However, clinical characteristics, disease trajectory, and prognostic factors of CMV infection in GS remain poorly characterized. To address this gap, we conducted a comparative analysis of clinical features between GS patients with and without CMV infection; further, we identified risk factors for CMV infection, longitudinally assessed hematological and immunological dynamics during CMV infection, and explored predictors of adverse outcomes in GS-CMV patients.

Methods

Study design and patients

This study employed a retrospective cohort design, enrolling all patients diagnosed with GS admitted to Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) between January 2015 and December 2024, with or without confirmed CMV infection. Inclusion criteria required: (1) a clinical diagnosis of Good Syndrome; (2) histopathologically confirmed thymoma (via surgical resection or biopsy); and (3) exclusion of other primary or secondary immunodeficiency disorders. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) absence of histopathological confirmation of thymoma; and (2) incomplete medical records. The research received approval from the institutional ethical review board of PUMCH (approval number: K5596) in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All analyses were conducted using de-identified, routinely collected electronic health record data. Written informed consent was waived, as the study involved minimal risk and fully satisfied the waiver criteria stipulated by the PUMCH institutional ethical review board.

Definitions

To date, no internationally validated diagnostic criteria for GS have been proposed. The minimal diagnostic requirement is the concurrent presence of thymoma and hypogammaglobulinemia. Other supplementary criteria are referred to the research published by Kabir A et al. in 2024 [8]. CMV infection and CMV disease were defined according to the 2021 guideline of American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) and 2024 consensus of the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) [9, 10].

CMV infection

Detection of CMV DNA, antigens, or viable virus in blood, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), gastrointestinal tissue, or other clinical specimens. Quantitative CMV DNA testing was performed using real-time PCR (detection limit: <500 copies/mL), as validated by the Department of Laboratory Medicine, Peking Union Medical College Hospital.

CMV pneumonia

New pulmonary infiltrates on imaging accompanied by hypoxia, tachypnea, or dyspnea plus CMV detection in BALF or lung tissue. Clinical diagnosis may be assigned when patients present with respiratory symptoms, plasma CMV DNA positivity, exclusion of alternative etiologies, and effective response to antiviral therapy.

CMV colitis

Presence of gastrointestinal symptoms, endoscopically or surgically identified mucosal lesions, and histopathologic or molecular confirmation of CMV in colonic tissue.

CMV retinitis

Classic fundoscopic findings, such as peripheral retinal necrosis with granular borders, hemorrhage, and vasculitis, which confirmed by an ophthalmologist experienced in CMV retinitis, plus detection of CMV DNA in aqueous humor.

CMV infection and disease diagnoses were independently confirmed by two physicians and discrepancies were resolved by consensus review with a third senior physician. Patients were then divided into the CMV infection group and the non-CMV infection group according to the above criteria. Laboratory test data were collected at the time of diagnosis of GS complicated by CMV infection, prior to the initiation of antiviral therapy and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) administration. Following diagnosis, laboratory test data were collected again at monthly follow-up visits. Corticosteroid and immunosuppressants exposure was defined as receipt of oral or intravenous corticosteroid/immunosuppressants therapy within one month before disease onset. Active CMV infection was defined as the persistence of clinical symptoms along with plasma CMV DNA levels > 500 copies/mL. The criteria for CMV clearance were defined as resolution of clinical symptoms and plasma CMV DNA levels below 500 copies/mL. A concentration of 500 copies/mL represents the lower limit of quantification for laboratory-based CMV-DNA testing. Patients underwent plasma CMV DNA testing at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months after discharge.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0. Continuous variables are presented as mean±standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]). Comparisons between groups regarding quantitative variables were carried out using the t test or rank sum test, and categorical variables were compared using Fisher’ s exact test or chi-square test. Binary logistic regression identified predictors of CMV infection and variables that demonstrated both clinical importance and statistical association (p < 0.20) in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate regression model [11]. The events-per-variable ratio in the logistic multivariable regression model was 7.5. Firth’s penalized likelihood method, implemented via the logistf package in R, was employed to adjust the binary logistic regression analysis. All 27 patients had documented vital status and last follow-up date, and no patients were excluded from the survival analysis due to loss to follow-up. Patients alive at the end of follow-up were described as censored at their last documented contact. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the date of GS diagnosis to death from any cause or the most recent contact. Survival curves were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared via the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate the association of prognostic factors. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value below 0.05.

Results

General characteristics of GS patients with or without CMV infection

All 27 enrolled patients underwent CMV testing. Fifteen patients were diagnosed with CMV infection; of these, 14 had detectable plasma CMV DNA. Demographic features, clinical characteristics, and laboratory indicators of the two groups were summarized in Table 1. No significant between-group differences were observed in mean age at GS onset, sex distribution, or GS duration. Lymphocyte counts and serum IgG levels were numerically lower in the GS-CMV group, though differences did not reach statistical significance. Peripheral blood CD4 + T-cell counts were significantly reduced in the GS-CMV group compared with the GS-non-CMV group (Z = − 2.147, P = 0.03). Prior corticosteroid use was more prevalent (46.7% vs. 8.3%; P = 0.04) and the median pre-infection prednisone-equivalent dose was higher (Z =-2.058, P = 0.04) in the GS-CMV group. Among all patients, four received immunosuppressive agents during GS: three for pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) received cyclosporine (125 mg twice daily), and one for rheumatoid arthritis received leflunomide plus methotrexate. Notably, all four developed CMV infection. ICU admission and all-cause mortality rates were numerically elevated in the CMV-infected group, but differences were not statistically significant.

Concurrent pathogens and CT imaging of GS patients with or without CMV infection

Among the 15 GS patients with CMV infection, one had CMV DNA detected in BALF, one in both aqueous humor and plasma, and one was diagnosed with CMV colitis by colonoscopic biopsy; the remaining 12 had CMV DNAemia. Among the 12 patients with detected CMV DNAemia, 6 were clinically diagnosed with CMV pneumonia according to the diagnostic criteria specified previously. Pathogen profiles in CMV versus non-CMV groups are summarized in Table 2. Respiratory pathogens were more frequently isolated in the CMV group, whereas gastrointestinal pathogen diversity was higher in the non-CMV group. Notably, the incidence of invasive fungal infection was higher in the CMV group. Regarding candidal infections, the majority of cases were confirmed oral candidiasis, with a small number of cases being candidal vaginitis. Among the four patients with pleural effusion in GS-CMV group, one presented with postoperative changes following thymoma resection, two had pleural metastasis from thymoma, and one had suspected co-infection with other pathogens. Additionally, four patients exhibited diffuse pulmonary ground-glass opacities in GS-CMV group, a common radiological manifestation of CMV pneumonia. These patients had a history of recurrent pulmonary infections, which may have contributed to the development of bronchiectatic lesions.

Peripheral blood cells and immunoglobulins among GS-CMV patients

We analyzed hematological parameters, lymphocyte subsets, and immunoglobulin levels in GS patients with or without CMV infection. Figure 1 shows the mean longitudinal trends of these indicators. All patients received simultaneous diagnoses of GS and CMV infection. Within three months of diagnosis, patients in the GS-CMV group showed mild declines in white blood cell, lymphocyte, and platelet counts (Fig. 1a and c). During this period, a more pronounced lymphocytopenia was observed in the GS-CMV group. Following CMV clearance, these parameters trended toward recovery; however, long-term trajectories remain undetermined. Red blood cell counts and hemoglobin levels remained relatively stable after CMV infection (Fig. 1d and e). As most patients (13/15) initiated immunoglobulin replacement therapy post-diagnosis, mean IgG levels rose during the first two months and then gradually declined. In contrast, IgA and IgM levels showed progressive declines (Fig. 1f and h).

CD4 + T-cell counts showed no significant change in the two groups and persisted below the lower reference limit (561–1137 cells/µL) (Fig. 1i). CD8 + T-cell counts increased significantly during the first month and remained above the upper reference limit (404–754 cells/µL) through month 3 (Fig. 1j). CD4 + T-cell percentages fluctuated but remained consistently below the reference lower limit (30.0–46.0%) (Fig. 1k). The proportion of CD8-positive T cells in both groups exceeded the upper limit of the normal reference range (19.2–33.6%), with a higher proportion observed in the GS-CMV group than in the GS-non-CMV group (Fig. 1l). Given that B-cell counts remained near zero in most patients over prolonged follow-up, B-cell dynamics were omitted from the figure.

Treatment, prognosis and risk factors of GS patients with CMV infection

The median follow-up times for the GS-CMV group and the GS-non-CMV group were 272 (54, 956) days and 217 (70, 375) days, respectively, with no statistically significant difference between them (Z = -0.488, P = 0.63). Among the 15 GS patients with confirmed CMV infection, the median duration of active CMV infection was 60 days (interquartile range [IQR]: 41–107 days). Fourteen patients received antiviral therapy (ganciclovir or foscarnet) with a mean treatment duration of 22.0 ± 9.7 days. Another patient, due to mild systemic symptoms and a low CMV viral load, received only supportive care and intravenous IVIG without antiviral agents. All 15 patients had bacterial or fungal co-infections, confirmed by culture, qPCR, or histopathology. Fourteen received antibiotics and/or antifungals; 13 received IVIG. Two declined IVIG due to cost or personal preference, with documented informed refusal. During the follow-up period of this study, no patient experienced a secondary CMV infection after clearance. Additionally, 6 patients achieved CMV clearance, while 6 patients continued to have persistent chronic CMV DNAemia.

A 3-month attributable mortality analysis of the GS-CMV group identified three deaths: one due to respiratory failure secondary to CMV pneumonia, one from concurrent CMV and Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, and one from intestinal infection caused by CMV and other pathogens. Twelve patients showed clinical improvement. Subsequently, an all-cause mortality analysis as of December 31, 2024, revealed 7 deaths in the GS-CMV group, including 4 after CMV clearance, attributed to thymoma metastases (n = 2) and unidentified pathogen infections (n = 2). In the GS-non-CMV group, 4 deaths occurred from severe infections with unidentified pathogens. Survival curves (Fig. 2) indicated a non-significantly higher mortality rate in the GS-CMV group (46.7% vs. 33.3%, p = 0.506) and a non-significantly longer mean survival time [272 (54–956) days vs. 217 (70–375) days, p = 0.63]. The mild survival advantage observed in the intermediate phase of CMV-infected patients may be attributed to heightened clinical vigilance and optimized therapeutic interventions prompted by their compromised immune status. However, due to sample size limitations, this finding should be interpreted with caution.

Binary logistic regression was used to perform univariate and multivariate analyses of potential associated factors for CMV infection in 27 patients with GS. Potential risk factors identified in the univariate analysis are summarized in Table 3. Multivariable analysis confirmed two potential associated factors: serum albumin (P = 0.012, OR = 0.743, 95% CI: 0.588–0.938) and blood IgG (P = 0.041, OR = 0.565, 95% CI: 0.327–0.978). Given an EPV of 7.5, we used the logistf package in R to perform Firth’s penalized likelihood method for correcting binary logistic regression analysis. The derived P-values and confidence intervals were consistent with those obtained from conventional logistic regression, indicating the model’ s relative stability. However, due to the limited sample size, this conclusion remains hypothetical and necessitates validation through studies with larger sample sizes. However, multivariate analysis revealed no statistically significant predictors. Due to the limited sample size, the model results are presented only as exploratory analysis, highlighting its limitations.

Discussion

This study investigated the clinical characteristics and prognosis of Good Syndrome (GS) patients with cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection. Changes in complete blood count and lymphocyte subsets were analyzed following diagnosis of GS with CMV. To our knowledge, this represents the first retrospective cohort study focusing on GS patients complicated by CMV infection, underscoring its novelty and contribution to the field, and we hope the findings provide valuable insights for managing CMV infection in this population.

CMV infection is frequently observed in GS patients, with CMV-DNAemia representing the primary clinical manifestation, followed by CMV pneumonia and colitis. Rare cases may involve nephritis, encephalitis, or retinitis (with/without optic nerve involvement) [3, 12]. The natural course often features recurrent episodes, which may progress to viremia; a subset of patients subsequently develops end-organ disease (EOD) [13]. In studies of solid organ transplant (SOT) and hematopoietic stem cell transplant (SCT) recipients, EOD incidence shows a non-linear relationship with viral load, rarely occurring before high-level viremia [6]. EOD pathogenesis involves inadequate immune control permitting high-titer viral replication, leading to cytolysis and tissue damage through multiple mechanisms. Low viral loads may not support completion of the full replication cycle, yet can trigger CMV antigen expression, potentially inducing immunopathological targeting of host cells [14]. In our study, a significant proportion of patients presented with CMV-DNAemia, and its clinical significance may differ from that of EOD. However, owing to the limited sample size of patients in this study, subgroup analysis was not conducted. Combining these patients into a single CMV cohort introduces inherent limitations to the study.

CMV engages in a complex interplay with the human immune system. Primary and latent CMV infections are controlled by host immune mechanisms, including type I/II interferons (IFNs), natural killer (NK) cells, CD8 + T cells, and CD4 + T cells [15]. The functional competence of CD4 + and CD8 + T lymphocytes is associated with controlling CMV. In SCT recipients, robust reconstitution of these T-cell populations correlates with effective containment of CMV infection [16]. Previous studies have reported significant CD4 + T cell depletion in most GS patients, while CD8 + T cell counts demonstrate a progressive decline over time. Nevertheless, CMV infection is associated with a transient increase in peripheral blood CD8 + T cell counts [8, 17, 18], which aligns with our observations. Furthermore, CMV can reactivate from latency in immunocompromised individuals experiencing systemic hyperinflammation [15]. The virus encodes an interleukin-10 (IL-10) homolog, cmvIL-10, which suppresses proinflammatory cytokine production, modulates host immune responses, promotes persistent viral replication, and facilitates the establishment of chronic infection [15].

We conducted a detailed analysis of peripheral blood cell counts and immunoglobulin levels in 27 GS patients with or without CMV infection within the first six months post-diagnosis. Following CMV infection, these patients exhibited a decrease in white blood cells, lymphocytes, and platelet counts. The duration of this decline closely aligned with the course of CMV infection, likely resulting from viral suppression and antiviral treatment. However, due to the inherent limitations of this retrospective study, long-term hematological data were unavailable. At three months post-diagnosis of GS-CMV, mean serum IgG levels showed an increasing trend, possibly attributable to the administration of IVIG. Subsequently, serum IgG levels began to decline gradually, while IgA and IgM levels continued to decrease steadily, indicating a return to an immunodeficient state. These findings highlight the importance of regular IVIG supplementation in the GS patients.

Previous studies have consistently reported progressive pancytopenia over time in patients with GS [8]. To explain these findings, the same research group proposed in a subsequent study that anti-cytokine autoantibodies (e.g., anti-GM-CSF antibodies) in GS patients may disrupt the bone marrow microenvironment, impair B lymphopoiesis, and ultimately lead to progressive bone marrow failure [19]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated a profound deficiency of pre-B cells in the bone marrow of GS patients, with differentiation arrest occurring at the post-pre-B cell stage [20]. In another study examining B cell expression and distribution in thymoma tissues from GS patients, B cell numbers were markedly reduced, and the expression levels of CD19, CD20, and PAX5 were significantly lower compared to control tissues [21]. Future investigations are needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying B lymphopenia and bone marrow dysplasia in GS. In our study, peripheral blood B cells were consistently undetectable in most GS patients. Nevertheless, detectable levels of immunoglobulins were still present. Concerning this discrepancy, it has been suggested that within the hematopoietic system of these patients, there may exist a subset of cells capable of low-level antibody production, such as CD19-negative plasma cells in the bone marrow or innate B1 lineage cells in peripheral tissues [22]. Further research on these antibody-producing cells could enhance our understanding of the pathophysiology of GS [23].

Hypoalbuminaemia and glucocorticoid exposure preceded CMV infection in our patients. Low serum albumin and IgG levels are predictive risk factors for CMV infection in GS patients. Therefore, we recommend regular CMV surveillance in GS patients with hypoalbuminemia or low IgG levels. In clinical practice, albumin supplementation and immunoglobulin replacement therapy represent targeted strategies to address these deficiencies. Albumin supplementation may exert a supportive effect on the overall disease status of GS by improving the body’s nutritional condition and enhancing the defensive capacity of the immune system. Accordingly, maintaining these parameters within normal ranges should be prioritized in the management of GS. Prior to CMV infection, patients in our cohort demonstrated both higher glucocorticoid (GC) exposure rates and greater prednisone-equivalent exposure doses compared to non-CMV group. Notably, all patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy subsequently developed CMV infection, highlighting the critical need for vigilant monitoring when using glucocorticoids or other immunosuppressants in immunodeficient populations.

The treatment strategies for GS-CMV patients primarily comprise antiviral therapy, management of bacterial or fungal co-infections, supportive care, and immunoglobulin supplementation [24]. Routine monitoring of plasma CMV viral load and prompt initiation of antiviral therapy are critical for the management of CMV infection and the prevention of EOD [25]. Currently, FDA-approved antiviral agents for CMV include foscarnet, ganciclovir, valganciclovir, cidofovir, letermovir, and maribavir [26]. Furthermore, CMV-neutralizing antibodies have showed efficacy in phase 2 clinical trials [27]. A substantial number of SOT recipients have exhibited favorable clinical and virological responses to CMV-specific T-cell therapy [28, 29].

The primary limitations of this study include the following: First, GS patients with concurrent CMV infection were predominantly hospitalized due to severe disease, introducing potential selection bias that limits the generalizability of their clinical and immunophenotypic features to the broader GS population. Second, the retrospective design and limited sample size precluded systematic collection of longitudinal laboratory data specifically serial measurements of key immune parameters and thus hindered robust assessment of temporal dynamics. Therefore, the results derived from this study should be interpreted with caution.

This study aimed to characterize the clinical features and immunological alterations in patients with GS complicated by CMV infection. Furthermore, it sought to identify potential risk factors of CMV infection in this population. CMV infection is highly prevalent among immunocompromised individuals and frequently leads to life-threatening complications [30]. Future research should prioritize in-depth investigation of the immunopathogenic mechanisms underlying GS, including the potential role of autoantibodies against cytokines in driving autoimmune dysregulation [31]. Simultaneously, it is imperative to perform genetic susceptibility analyses in patients with GS. Specifically, this includes targeted sequencing of established disease-associated genes such as transmembrane activator and CAML interactor (TACI) and B cell-activating factor receptor (BAFF-R) to uncover the genetic basis of GS [32, 33]. These integrated approaches will advance our understanding of the etiopathogenesis of this rare primary immunodeficiency.

Conclusions

GS patients with concurrent CMV infection had lower lymphocyte counts and higher risks of respiratory tract and opportunistic fungal infections. During active CMV infection, they consistently developed CD8 + T-cell lymphocytosis. Hypoalbuminemia, low serum IgG, and glucocorticoid administration may be associated with increased risk of CMV infection in GS patients. However, it is important to emphasize that the factors identified in our study apply to the broad definition of CMV infection and may not specifically predict end-organ disease. Future studies need broader detection methods to define the immunopathological phenotypes of GS-CMV patients. This will clarify disease mechanisms and improve therapeutic strategies for this rare immunodeficiency.
