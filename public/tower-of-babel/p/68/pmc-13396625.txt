From infection to cholangiocarcinoma: Why opisthorchiids break these rules
Authors: Petr Heneberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHelminth infections are among the most prevalent causes of chronic inflammation in humans, but only a small subset of these infections is causally linked to cancer. This discrepancy challenges the long-standing assumption that chronic inflammation is intrinsically carcinogenic and suggests that additional constraints govern inflammation-driven tumorigenesis in parasitic diseases.ObjectivesThis review aims to explain why most helminths fail to induce malignancy despite long-term inflammatory host responses, and why opisthorchiid liver trematodes represent a rare and informative exception. We seek to integrate primary experimental, epidemiological, and pathological evidence into a unified conceptual settings applicable to helminth-associated cancers.Key findingsWe introduce the concept of a “pro-oncogenic inflammation threshold,” proposing that carcinogenesis emerges only when multiple dimensions converge simultaneously: sustained inflammatory intensity and quality, prolonged exposure, genotoxic stress, tissue-specific vulnerability, and permissive environmental cofactors. Most helminths remain below this threshold because of evolutionary selection that favors host survival, the absence of direct genotoxins, and effective immunoregulatory mechanisms. In contrast, opisthorchiids exceed this threshold through chronic mechanical injury to bile ducts, secretion of genotoxic and mitogenic metabolites, synergy with dietary nitrosamines, perturbation of the biliary microbiome, and failure of reparative homeostasis.ConclusionsHelminth-induced cancers represent a neglected category of tropical disease that remains poorly integrated into global oncology strategies because of disciplinary fragmentation and a mutation-centric cancer paradigm. Setting helminth-associated carcinogenesis in a One Health context highlights actionable prevention opportunities at the human–animal–environment interface. Chronic helminth-induced inflammation is therefore a conditional, rather than universal, driver of cancer.Keywords: Carcinogenesis, Cholangiocarcinoma, Infection-associated cancer, Opisthorchis, Neglected tropical disease, Zoonosis

The helminth–cancer paradox

Helminth infections frequently persist for years or decades and are typically accompanied by chronic inflammation and tissue remodeling [1], [2]. However, only a small subset of helminths are causally linked to human cancer. Infection with Opisthorchis viverrini (Fig. 1), Clonorchis sinensis, or Schistosoma haematobium is classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1). The first two are associated with cholangiocarcinoma, whereas S. haematobium infection is associated with squamous cell carcinoma of the urinary bladder [3], [4]. In endemic regions of Southeast Asia, high prevalence of O. viverrini infection coincides with some of the highest global incidences of cholangiocarcinoma [5], [6]. Comparable associations have been documented for C. sinensis in China and the Republic of Korea [7], [8]. In Africa and the Middle East, long-standing urogenital schistosomiasis caused by S. haematobium is associated with bladder cancer, which is supported by epidemiological and histopathological evidence [9], [10]. By contrast, infection with Schistosoma japonicum is classified as possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B), whereas infection with Opisthorchis felineus remains not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity to humans (Group 3).

These well-documented carcinogenic helminth species stand in contrast to the vast majority of helminth infections, which are not associated with increased cancer risk despite inducing chronic inflammatory responses [1], [11]. This discrepancy challenges the assumption that chronic inflammation is inherently carcinogenic, an assumption that is largely derived from nonparasitic conditions such as chronic viral hepatitis, inflammatory bowel disease, or Helicobacter pylori–associated gastritis [12], [13], [14], [15]. Helminth infections, therefore, constitute a natural model system in which long-term inflammation frequently fails to progress to malignancy.

In this review, we aim to explain why chronic inflammation induced by most helminths does not lead to cancer and why opisthorchiid liver trematodes represent a reproducible exception. We propose that helminth-associated carcinogenesis requires the convergence of multiple conditions that collectively exceed a critical biological limit, conceptualized here as a pro-oncogenic inflammation threshold. These settings emphasize that inflammation alone is insufficient and must intersect with specific tissue contexts, parasite-derived genotoxic or mitogenic factors, prolonged exposure, and permissive environmental cofactors. The following sections examine the mechanisms that typically constrain carcinogenesis in helminth infections, features that allow opisthorchiids to overcome these constraints, and the implications for understanding helminth-induced cancer as a neglected One Health problem. This One Health perspective is essential because the carcinogenic pathway is not confined to the infected human host. It is maintained by freshwater snail and fish intermediate hosts, reservoir animals, food-preparation practices, aquaculture and sanitation conditions, dietary nitrosamine exposure, and environmental contaminants that can converge on the same biliary epithelium. Literature selection

To prepare this mechanistic narrative review, we identified relevant literature through searches of PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, supplemented by backward and forward citation tracking from key articles. Searches combined terms related to helminth-associated carcinogenesis, liver trematodes, schistosomiasis, cholangiocarcinoma, bladder cancer, chronic inflammation, genotoxicity, nitrosamines, microbiome, and One Health. Search terms included “Opisthorchis viverrini cholangiocarcinoma”, “Clonorchis sinensis cholangiocarcinoma”, “Opisthorchis felineus biliary intraepithelial neoplasia”, “opisthorchiid carcinogenesis”, “helminth inflammation cancer”, “Schistosoma haematobium bladder cancer”, “liver trematode nitrosamine”, “liver trematode microbiome”, “helminth secretome proteomics”, “granulin liver trematode”, “biliary fibrosis liver fluke”, and “foodborne trematode One Health”. Priority was given to original experimental studies, human epidemiological studies, pathology studies, mechanistic cell or animal studies, and primary biomarker studies. Reviews, monographs, and agency documents were used for background, classification, or historical context, and to identify additional original sources. Statements based on species-specific or model-specific evidence were treated as such and were not generalized to all helminths unless supported by additional comparative evidence.

Chronic inflammation does not equal carcinogenesis

Host immune regulation and tissue repair are dominant anticarcinogenic constraints in most helminth infections. Many chronic helminth infections induce immune environments characterized by Th2 polarization, regulatory cytokine output, and alternatively activated macrophages [[22], [23], [24]]. This profile contrasts with the Th1- or Th17-dominated inflammation associated with carcinogenesis in nonparasitic diseases [25]. Parasite-derived molecules actively suppress host inflammatory pathways, preventing excessive tissue damage that would compromise host survival and parasite transmission [26].

Regulatory T cells that expand during chronic helminth infection produce IL-10 and TGF-β, thereby dampening effector responses [27]. Experimental depletion of these cells leads to severe immunopathology, demonstrating their functional necessity [23]. Reduced activation of neutrophils and macrophages limits oxidative DNA damage in the surrounding tissues [28].

Helminth infections also activate wound-healing programs rather than sustained proliferative signaling. Alternatively activated macrophages promote fibrosis and structural repair without driving epithelial hyperplasia [[24], [29]]. In tissues with rapid epithelial turnover, such as the intestine, damaged cells are replaced before mutations accumulate [30].

Comparative pathology illustrates these constraints. Schistosoma mansoni and S. japonicum induce intense hepatic inflammation and fibrosis, but no consistent causal link to hepatocellular carcinoma has been established despite long-standing infection [[31], [32]]. Migratory helminths cause acute, spatially dispersed tissue injury that resolves after larval passage without sustained epithelial proliferation or dysplasia [[33], [34], [35]]. These features collectively limit oncogenic risk.

Across several helminth taxa, excretory-secretory products are dominated by molecules involved in host immune modulation, antioxidant defense, proteolysis, and tissue interaction rather than by well-characterized direct mitogens or DNA-reactive genotoxins. Original proteomic studies of Fasciola hepatica, Heligmosomoides polygyrus, and Trichuris muris identified protease inhibitors, antioxidant enzymes, venom allergen-like proteins, cathepsins, extracellular vesicle-associated proteins, and other host-interacting molecules consistent with immune deviation and tissue repair [[36], [37], [38], [39]]. Only a limited number of helminth species, most clearly the carcinogenic liver flukes O. viverrini and C. sinensis, are known to produce growth factor-like molecules or excretory-secretory products that can directly stimulate host epithelial or cholangiocarcinoma-cell proliferation. Ov-GRN-1 from O. viverrini is present in excretory-secretory products and promotes host-cell proliferation [40], whereas suppression or functional characterization of Ov-GRN-1 supports its role in biliary epithelial proliferation and wound-healing-like responses [[41], [42]]. Clonorchis sinensis granulin and C. sinensis excretory-secretory products have also been reported to promote proliferation or malignant phenotypes in hepatobiliary cell models [[43], [44], [45]]. Comparable functional evidence is not yet available for all opisthorchiids, including O. felineus, suggesting that species differences in carcinogenic potential may depend partly on the presence, abundance, and activity of parasite-derived mitogenic factors.

Why most helminths do not induce cancer

Chronic helminth infections can persist for years or decades, but for most human helminths no reproducible causal association with cancer has been established [[3], [10], [46]] (Table 2). This absence of an established association should not always be interpreted as strong negative epidemiological evidence, because long-term cancer surveillance is limited for many helminth infections. Nevertheless, several chronic helminth infections induce prolonged inflammation and immune remodeling, with fibrosis or granulomatous pathology in selected tissue-invasive infections [[1], [2], [31], [32], [47]]. These observations suggest that chronic inflammation alone is generally insufficient and that additional conditions are required for malignant transformation.

Experimental infections and human studies have demonstrated that most helminths induce Th2-polarized and regulatory immune responses, characterized by the production of IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, and IL-10, as well as the expansion of regulatory T cells and the activation of alternative macrophages [[24], [26], [27]]. These programs suppress tissue-damaging Th1 and Th17 responses that are strongly linked to DNA damage and carcinogenesis in other chronic inflammatory conditions [13]. In murine models of colitis-associated cancer, sustained IFN-γ and IL-17 signaling promotes tumor initiation, whereas helminth-induced immune deviation reduces epithelial dysplasia and mutational burden [[62], [63]].

The tissue context may further constrain oncogenic risk. Many intestinal helminths inhabit the lumen or interact with mucosal surfaces characterized by continuous stem cell–driven epithelial renewal. Intestinal helminths differ substantially in their relationship with this epithelium. Adult A. lumbricoides are primarily luminal, Trichuris spp. occupy epithelial syncytial tunnels, and hookworms attach to and injure the mucosa [[64], [65]]. Rapid epithelial replacement could limit the persistence of some damaged differentiated cells, but its contribution to the low apparent carcinogenicity of intestinal helminths remains untested. In contrast, chronic injury in the bile ducts or bladder urothelium may expose locally proliferating epithelial cells repeatedly to inflammatory and genotoxic stress [[66], [67]].

The patterns of tissue injury also differ among helminths. Many species migrate through tissues transiently during their larval stages, causing acute but self-limiting injuries that resolve once the parasites relocate or mature [68]. Studies of Toxocara canis and Strongyloides stercoralis have demonstrated focal inflammation without sustained epithelial proliferation or dysplasia [[34], [69]]. Repeated migratory injury rarely produces the persistent microtrauma characteristic of adult parasites anchored within confined epithelial ducts.

A further limitation is the absence of direct genotoxic mechanisms. Most helminth species secrete immunomodulatory proteins, protease inhibitors, and metabolic enzymes rather than DNA-reactive compounds [[70], [71], [72]]. Direct genotoxicity testing of noncarcinogenic helminth secretomes is limited, and the results are mixed, with several helminth antigens or infections capable of inducing DNA damage markers in host cells, even in the absence of a consistent cancer association [[73], [74]]. Unlike oncogenic viruses, helminths have no replication strategy that requires integration into host genomes, and helminth carcinogenesis has not been linked to insertional mutagenesis or to viral-like oncoproteins that directly bind p53 or RB [75]. Helminth infections commonly induce regulatory circuits, including IL-10-dependent pathways, that limit inflammatory pathology and can constrain iNOS-linked nitrative chemistry in vivo [[76], [77]].

Environmental cofactors further distinguish carcinogenic from noncarcinogenic infections. In opisthorchiasis and clonorchiasis, dietary nitrosamines and bile acid metabolites amplify parasite-induced DNA damage [78]. Documented experimental multihit models that combine defined exogenous carcinogens with helminth infection to yield high tumor penetrance are best established for opisthorchiids in the bile-duct niche [78]. Cancer endpoints are not tracked in soil-transmitted helminth control programs, limiting inference about potential long-term effects on cancer incidence. In contrast, praziquantel-mediated clearance of O. viverrini may reduce hepatobiliary inflammation, although advanced periductal fibrosis persists in a substantial proportion of treated patients [79]. From a One Health perspective, these differences mean that carcinogenic risk depends not only on parasite species but also on the ecological and dietary setting in which infection occurs. Liver helminth transmission links human infection to freshwater snails, cyprinid fish, animal reservoirs, aquaculture practices, and local food traditions, whereas coexposure to nitrosamines or aquatic pollutants can add genotoxic pressure to the same chronically injured tissue.

What is unique about opisthorchiids (and a few other exceptions)

Carcinogenic and cancer-associated opisthorchiid liver trematodes, including O. viverrini, C. sinensis, and O. felineus, share a broadly similar life cycle involving freshwater snails, fish intermediate hosts, and fish-eating definitive hosts, and adult worms reside for extended periods in intrahepatic or extrahepatic bile ducts. Opisthorchis viverrini and C. sinensis have the strongest human epidemiological links to cholangiocarcinoma, but animal models show that O. felineus can induce related hepatobiliary pathology. Histopathology in opisthorchiid-infected hamsters and human liver specimens revealed epithelial abrasion, focal ulceration, hyperplasia, ductal remodeling, oxidative hepatobiliary injury, and, in O. felineus-infected hamsters, biliary intraepithelial neoplasia [[59], [80], [81], [82], [83], [84]]. This pattern differs from that of helminths, which cause transient injury during tissue migration, in which lesions are spatially dispersed and resolve after parasite relocation [85]. Repetitive microtrauma and chronic inflammation in a fixed ductal niche can sustain regenerative signaling and increase the risk of replication-associated errors in the same epithelial lineage over time [86]. Thus, the distinction among these opisthorchiids is not the basic bile-duct life cycle or the capacity to induce chronic hepatobiliary injury, but the strength of human epidemiological evidence, the availability of experimental carcinogenesis data, and the degree to which parasite-derived metabolites, cocarcinogen exposure, microbiome changes, and host ecology have been characterized.

The bile duct epithelium has biological properties that can increase susceptibility to chronic injury. Cholangiocytes have low baseline turnover, with proliferation concentrated in states of injury, such as cholestasis or obstruction [83]. In hamster opisthorchiasis models, infection induces a ductular reaction and persistent cholangiocyte proliferation, supported by increased Ki-67 and PCNA expression in bile ducts [[76], [87]]. This shift from low baseline turnover to long-term injury-driven proliferation increases the time-integrated number of cell divisions in cholangiocytes, which increases the probability that DNA lesions become fixed as mutations [[76], [87], [88]]. Intestinal epithelia differ because of their rapid baseline turnover and frequent shedding, which can dilute damaged cells and limit clonal persistence under many conditions [88].

The biliary lumen also exposes cholangiocytes to bile acids that can act as chemical stressors. Hydrophobic bile acids induce oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and DNA damage responses in biliary epithelial cells [[89], [90]]. In opisthorchiasis and clonorchiasis, bile stasis can occur because of the presence of parasite bodies, eggs, and periductal fibrosis, resulting in prolonged retention of bile acids and other biliary constituents in contact with the epithelium [87]. Infected hamsters show increased lipid peroxidation and oxidative DNA lesions, including 8-oxo-deoxyguanosine, in biliary tissues [76]. Inflammatory cells recruited to chronically injured ducts contribute reactive nitrogen and oxygen species, further increasing oxidative and nitrative stress in the ductal microenvironment [76]. This combination of ductal confinement, prolonged exposure to retained bile constituents, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and sustained injury-driven proliferation may create a permissive setting for malignant evolution during long-term infection.

Opisthorchiids also produce excretory–secretory products with activities that extend beyond immune modulation (Table S1). Proteomic analyses of O. viverrini secretions have revealed bioactive molecules that are involved in host signaling, tissue remodeling, and redox biology, which is consistent with direct host–parasite biochemical crosstalk in the biliary lumen [91]. A potentially important feature of opisthorchiid infection is the presence of infection-associated sterol-, oxysterol-, and estrogen-like metabolites in infected hosts. Some of these compounds, or their downstream products, may undergo redox cycling to DNA-reactive quinone intermediates [[59], [92]]. Related metabolites induce oxidative DNA damage in vitro, including base oxidation and formation of abasic sites in mammalian cells [93]. In O. felineus-infected hamsters, biliary intraepithelial neoplasia was accompanied by oxysterol-like compounds interpreted as putatively parasite-derived, together with parasite-associated oxysterol metabolites conjugated with DNA bases [59]. These findings support exposure of host biliary tissues to infection-associated metabolites, but do not establish that all detected compounds were synthesized exclusively by the parasite rather than generated through parasite–host metabolism. Infection models have also shown increased oxidative, nitrative, and genotoxic damage markers in hepatobiliary tissues during chronic O. viverrini, O. felineus, and C. sinensis infection [[84], [91], [94], [95]]. Covalent DNA adducts have been reported in cholangiocytes from infected hamsters, with adduct patterns consistent with quinone-related chemistry [96]. Collectively, these findings support a possible mechanistic contribution of infection-associated and putatively parasite-derived metabolites to host DNA damage. In O. felineus-infected hamsters, biliary intraepithelial neoplasia was accompanied by cholesterol-derived, oxysterol-like parasite metabolites and parasite-specific oxysterol metabolites conjugated with DNA bases [59]. Direct DNA-adduct-forming chemistry provides a potential bridge between chronic injury and mutagenesis in opisthorchiid infection.

Environmental and ecological cofactors amplify these processes in endemic regions. Nitrosamine-related exposure should be divided into exogenous intake of preformed N-nitroso compounds and endogenous nitrosation within the host. Preformed nitrosamines have been detected in fermented fish and other preserved foods consumed in high-risk areas, whereas urinary N-nitrosoproline and related markers indicate that endogenous nitrosation can also occur in exposed populations [[97], [98], [99]]. In experimental hamster models, low-dose N-nitrosodimethylamine synergizes with O. viverrini infection to produce cholangiocarcinoma at a higher frequency than either exposure alone does, linking a defined chemical carcinogen to parasite-driven injury and proliferation [[100], [101]]. A comparable coexposure model has been reported for O. felineus: Syrian hamsters infected with O. felineus and administered N-nitrosodimethylamine developed cholangiocarcinoma, extending the multihit concept beyond O. viverrini and supporting the view that low-dose carcinogen exposure can cooperate with opisthorchiid infection in biliary carcinogenesis [60]. This supports a multihit model, in which parasite-driven epithelial injury and nitrosated chemical stress act together. In humans, however, the direct contribution of nitrosamines is more difficult to quantify because fermented fish consumption, raw-fish exposure, infection intensity, reinfection, other fermented foods, socioeconomic factors, and environmental contaminants are correlated within endemic communities. Human case–control data are consistent with an interaction between opisthorchiid infection and nitrosamine-related dietary exposure in terms of cholangiocarcinoma risk [48]. Biliary and intestinal microbiome changes represent another potential amplifier. Altered biliary bacterial composition, including enrichment of taxa with inflammatory and nitrate-reducing potential, has been reported in liver trematode-associated cholangiocarcinoma [[102], [103]]. Hamster infection experiments have shown that bacterial translocation to the biliary tree and activation of epithelial pathways responsive to bacterial products can contribute to local generation of reactive nitrogen species and changes in bile acid metabolism [103]. In addition, a comparative hamster model showed that O. viverrini, C. sinensis, and O. felineus affect the mammalian host microbiome in a species-specific manner, indicating that liver-fluke-associated microbiome perturbation should not be treated as a uniform process across opisthorchiids [104]. Environmental chemical contamination may represent an additional coexposure in some endemic freshwater systems. Field studies have detected organochlorine pesticide residues in fish and other aquatic organisms from affected regions [[105], [106], [107]]. However, direct evidence that these contaminants interact with opisthorchiid infection to increase biliary dysplasia or cholangiocarcinoma risk is currently lacking. Their potential contribution to opisthorchiid-associated carcinogenesis should therefore be regarded as a One Health research question rather than an established component of the causal pathway. Thus, opisthorchiid-associated cholangiocarcinogenesis can be viewed as a One Health process shaped by parasite exposure, food systems, animal reservoirs, aquatic ecology, microbial communities, and established dietary cocarcinogens. The contribution of other environmental chemical coexposures remains insufficiently defined. Comparisons within Opisthorchiidae help define what is specific to carcinogenic opisthorchiids. Metorchis bilis and related Metorchis species share a broadly similar life cycle involving snails and fish, and they can inhabit the biliary system in their definitive hosts. However, they have not been linked to cholangiocarcinoma [[111], [112]]. Original experimental pathology studies show that Metorchis infections can induce hepatobiliary inflammation, bile-duct epithelial hyperplasia, connective-tissue proliferation, and fibrosis, but these studies did not report biliary intraepithelial neoplasia or cholangiocarcinoma [[55], [113]]. Differences in secretory biology have also been proposed by comparative proteomics and functional studies, as a parasite-derived granulin-like mitogen has been characterized in O. viverrini and linked to cholangiocyte proliferative signaling. Available functional evidence for parasite-derived granulin-like mitogenic activity comes from O. viverrini, where Ov-GRN-1 has been linked to cholangiocyte proliferative signaling [[40], [41], [42]]. Comparable functional studies of Metorchis excretory-secretory products are lacking. These contrasts support a model in which opisthorchiid carcinogenesis reflects a specific alignment of long-term ductal residence, concentrated mechanical injury, parasite-derived genotoxic and mitogenic inputs, and consistent environmental cocarcinogens, rather than a generic property of biliary helminths. The “pro-oncogenic inflammation threshold” concept

The pro-oncogenic inflammation threshold concept formalizes a recurring observation in helminth biology: chronic inflammation is common, but cancer causation is rare. The model proposes that carcinogenesis emerges only after multiple dimensions converge beyond a critical limit within the same tissue niche and timeframe. These dimensions include inflammatory intensity, inflammatory quality, exposure duration, genotoxic inputs, and tissue context vulnerability. The model aligns with primary evidence from nonparasitic inflammation-associated cancers, in which risk depends on factors beyond the mere presence of chronic inflammation, including the combined effects of duration, mutagenic stress, and tissue regenerative dynamics [[13], [114], [115], [116]]. In population studies, helminthiases provide a natural comparative system because many infections generate long-term inflammation without measurable cancer signals, suggesting that most helminthiases remain below a functional carcinogenic threshold [[1], [47]].

The inflammatory intensity captures the magnitude and persistence of local inflammatory cell infiltrates and inflammatory mediators within the target niche. In opisthorchiasis, persistent periductal infiltrates and elevated IL-6, TNF-α, and TGF-β have been documented in human infection and experimental models [[91], [94], [95]], supporting prolonged inflammatory and profibrotic signaling in the biliary tract. In other helminth infections, the spatial distribution and persistence of inflammation vary according to parasite tissue niche and life cycle [117]. Inflammatory quality captures the balance between regulatory or repair-dominant programs and cytotoxic, oxidative, or nitrosative programs. Many helminths induce Th2 and regulatory patterns that promote repair, as an alternative to macrophage activation and fibrosis, which can limit tissue-destructive effector responses [26]. In opisthorchiasis, primary studies support a mixed profile in the biliary tract with concurrent regulatory signals and persistent activation of neutrophils and macrophages that generate oxidative stress, providing a route to higher mutagenic chemistry in situ [118].

The duration of exposure is not interchangeable with intensity. This dimension should also include cumulative exposure history, because the biologically relevant dose is determined not only by the number of worms present at sampling but also by repeated acquisition, persistence, treatment, clearance, and reinfection over time. This is particularly relevant in endemic communities, where infection may begin early in life, whereas cholangiocarcinoma typically becomes clinically apparent only after a long lag period in adulthood. Among treated villagers with O. viverrini infection, the 12-month cumulative reinfection rate was 10.9%, and previous praziquantel use was independently associated with reinfection (adjusted OR 2.04, 95% CI 1.13–3.69) [119]. In a hospital-based matched case–control study, repeated praziquantel use was also associated with cholangiocarcinoma risk, with each additional treatment associated with a 23% increase in the odds of disease [120]. This association may reflect recurrent recognized infection, reinfection, or persistent raw-fish consumption, although praziquantel use itself cannot distinguish among these explanations. Epidemiological data from endemic regions are consistent with increasing cholangiocarcinoma risk after prolonged cumulative exposure. Experimental hamster models likewise show that premalignant and malignant biliary lesions develop after prolonged infection and can be used to model long-term exposure in humans [[49], [78]]. Many helminths fail to maintain a stable residence in a single epithelial lumen for comparable durations, limiting cumulative damage to a single susceptible cell pool. Genotoxic inputs represent direct drivers of DNA damage beyond general inflammation. In opisthorchiasis, oxidative and nitrosative DNA damage markers, as well as DNA adducts, have been reported in infected hosts. Additionally, parasite-derived estrogen-like metabolites and oxysterols have been identified as probable contributors to redox cycling and adduct formation [[121], [122]]. The tissue context specifies how the same inflammatory stimulus can yield different long-term outcomes. The biliary epithelium normally has low cellular turnover, but cholestasis, obstruction, and chronic infection induce sustained injury-driven cholangiocyte proliferation and ductular reactions [[123], [124]] . Repeated proliferation in an inflammatory and genotoxic environment may increase the opportunity for damaged epithelial clones to persist and expand. Comparable persistent proliferative responses in key epithelial progenitor compartments are not documented for many noncarcinogenic helminth infection sites, such as the intestinal mucosa or skeletal muscle. Under the threshold model, these five dimensions interact, and no single dimension is sufficient in isolation.

The same settings explain why most helminths remain below the proposed threshold. Chronic gastrointestinal nematode infection models, such as those of Heligmosomoides polygyrus or Trichuris muris, exhibit sustained immune activation and epithelial remodeling [[125], [126]]. Nevertheless, a reproducible neoplastic phenotype has not been established in these infection models. Helminth secretomes from many noncarcinogenic species contain immunomodulatory proteins and glycans, and in vitro exposure studies typically detect immune and barrier modulation rather than robust DNA strand break signatures in epithelial cells [[127], [128], [129]]. Helminths do not integrate genetic material into host genomes, and comparative helminth genomics does not support virus-like insertional mutagenesis as a general mechanism [[130], [131]]. Immunoregulatory circuits further limit mutagenic inflammation; experimental depletion of regulatory pathways increases pathology but does not reliably generate a cancer phenotype in standard helminth infection models, which is consistent with regulation acting as a brake on escalation rather than a direct oncogenic driver [[132], [133], [134]]. Evolutionary constraints align with these findings. Parasites dependent on long-lived hosts face selection against excessive pathology that reduces host survival and transmission opportunities, promoting immune tolerance and tissue preservation [[135], [136]].

Opisthorchiids cross the threshold through convergence across dimensions in one anatomical niche. Adult O. viverrini and C. sinensis persist in bile ducts through prolonged contact with cholangiocytes, resulting in continuous mechanical irritation and compensatory proliferation [[20], [137], [138]]. Primary metabolomic and mechanistic studies have identified parasite-associated oxysterols and catechol estrogen-like metabolites with the potential to form DNA adducts, accompanied by host-tissue evidence of oxidative DNA damage in infected settings [[76], [94], [139]]. Dietary nitrosamines serve as exogenous genotoxic amplifiers in endemic regions, and hamster experiments have demonstrated a substantial acceleration of cholangiocarcinoma with combined infection and nitrosamine exposure [[100], [101]]. Biliary dysbiosis and enrichment of inflammatory taxa, including Helicobacter spp., have been reported in patient bile samples. Experimental data support microbial translocation and inflammatory activation in the biliary niche, providing an additional route for reactive nitrogen species and tissue stress [[140], [141]]. Parasite-derived mitogens such as granulin-like factors stimulate cholangiocyte proliferation and migration in vitro and biliary hyperplasia in vivo, coupling DNA damage to sustained proliferation [142]. Persistent IL-6, TGF-β, and STAT3 activation is characteristic of biliary tissue in opisthorchiasis, linking infection to pathways repeatedly implicated in inflammation-associated epithelial survival and proliferation [[143], [144]]. Under the threshold concept, these components combine into a multihit trajectory that is rarely assembled by other helminths in a single tissue niche [102]. Several threshold components are generated outside the human host: repeated infection reflects transmission through aquatic food webs and reservoir hosts, whereas genotoxic amplification reflects dietary and environmental exposures that are unevenly distributed across endemic landscapes. Testable predictions of the pro-oncogenic inflammation threshold model.

This review proposes that helminth-associated carcinogenesis occurs only after a critical convergence of inflammatory, genotoxic, and tissue-specific factors. The threshold model generates several testable predictions that can be empirically evaluated.First, individuals with a comparable duration of infection but differing exposure to environmental genotoxins should exhibit markedly different cancer risks. In opisthorchiasis, dietary nitrosamine intake or biliary bacterial composition should be used to stratify cholangiocarcinoma incidence among hosts with similar infections. This prediction is supported by regional epidemiological heterogeneity in Thailand and Laos, where parasite prevalence is similar but cancer incidence differs substantially.Second, parasite burden alone should be a poor predictor of cancer risk unless it is combined with markers of epithelial proliferation and DNA damage. Quantitative egg counts or fluke numbers are expected to correlate weakly with malignancy unless accompanied by increased γH2AX, 8-oxo-dG, or sustained cholangiocyte Ki-67 labeling.Third, interruption of any single axis of the threshold should reduce cancer risk without necessarily clearing infection. Antioxidant interventions, bile acid modulation, or microbiome alteration are predicted to lower oncogenic progression even in persistently infected hosts.Fourth, helminths that share a phylogeny but lack specific threshold components should fail to induce cancer. Closely related trematodes, such as Metorchis bilis, should not trigger malignant transformation despite chronic inflammation unless experimental conditions artificially introduce genotoxic cofactors.The model predicts that cancer risk emerges nonlinearly, with sharp inflection points rather than gradual increases as exposure accumulates. Longitudinal cohort studies should reveal threshold-like behavior rather than dose–response linearity.

Helminth-induced cancer as a neglected infection-associated cancer (one health perspective)

Helminth-induced cancers should be treated as neglected infection-associated cancers and as One Health diseases, as their prevention depends on coordinated control of human infections, animal reservoirs, intermediate hosts, freshwater ecosystems, food safety, and environmental coexposures. Nevertheless, helminth-associated cancers remain poorly integrated into global oncology strategies despite causal evidence for specific parasite–cancer links and preventability signals from parasite control programs [[80], [145], [146]]. One reason is the dominance of mutation-centric priorities in cancer policy and research prioritization, which emphasize molecular classification, driver mutations, and tumor-intrinsic features that are most actionable in high-resource settings [[147], [148]]. Cancers driven by chronic infection, tissue injury, and environmental exposure patterns receive less strategic focus, despite established attributable fractions for several infection-associated cancers [46]. Helminth-related cancers also lack direct human-to-human transmission, reducing perceived urgency relative to viral carcinogens with epidemic propagation potential and scalable vaccine solutions [149]. Helminths require intermediate hosts and environmental reservoirs, producing geographically stable transmission patterns that are socially entrenched rather than rapidly expanding, thereby reducing visibility in crisis-driven prioritization [[150], [151]]. This distinction is important for public health practice; O. viverrini and C. sinensis cannot be managed solely as ordinary helminth infections, because their most severe outcome is not immediate morbidity but long-latency cholangiocarcinoma. Conventional control endpoints, such as reduced egg counts, reduced infection prevalence, or temporary clearance after praziquantel treatment, do not fully capture cancer prevention. In endemic communities, the relevant prevention target is cumulative biliary risk, which reflects repeated infection, reinfection, persistent raw-fish consumption, animal reservoirs, snail and fish intermediate hosts, sanitation, food-safety practices, and coexposures that promote genotoxic stress.

Helminth-attributable cancers disproportionately affect rural and socioeconomically marginalized populations with limited access to imaging, pathology, and etiologic diagnostics [[152], [153], [154]]. In opisthorchiasis hotspots in northeastern Thailand and the neighboring Mekong regions, cholangiocarcinoma incidence is concentrated in subsistence fishing communities with high exposure to O. viverrini, aligning cancer risk with environmental, dietary, and economic constraints that reduce early detection and registry completeness [[155], [156]]. With respect to Schistosoma haematobium-associated squamous bladder cancer, regional series from Africa and the Middle East document persistent egg-associated pathology in tumors and advanced presentation patterns, which is consistent with upstream infection control gaps and downstream diagnostic limitations [[149], [152]]. Registry structures rarely encode infectious etiology for tumors beyond a small set of widely recognized viral drivers and pathology reporting. These registries commonly classify cholangiocarcinoma or bladder carcinoma without consistent documentation of a history of parasite exposure or parasite-associated lesions [156].

Clinical invisibility compounds this. In endemic settings, cholangiocarcinoma often enters tertiary pathways late, and Thai clinical cohorts report narrow therapeutic windows after presentation. In a tertiary referral cohort, the interval from first hospital contact to definitive diagnosis and treatment was approximately one month, with reported pretreatment mortality consistent with advanced disease at entry [157]. In an ERCP palliation series, the average duration of symptoms before diagnosis was a few months, and most patients were not candidates for curative resection at presentation [158]. Outside endemic regions, patient-reported diagnostic intervals can be substantially longer, which is consistent with a lack of risk-based triage for rare hepatobiliary malignancies in low-exposure clinical environments [159]. With respect to schistosomiasis-associated bladder cancer, some regional cohorts show high proportions of muscle-invasive disease at diagnosis, providing a quantitative indicator of late capture even when interval timing is not recorded [[9], [160]].

Disciplinary fragmentation sustains the gap. Oncology programs prioritize tumor treatment and molecular diagnostics, whereas parasitology and infectious disease programs prioritize prevalence reduction and short-term morbidity endpoints [[9], [157]]. NTD control success is often measured in terms of reduced egg burden or infection prevalence, rather than cancer outcomes occurring decades later. Thus, the longitudinal linkage between parasite control and cancer registries remains limited [[9], [160]]. The veterinary and environmental sectors add another boundary. Many helminths circulate through animal reservoirs, and transmission intensity depends on aquatic ecology, snail hosts, intermediate fish hosts, and food systems; however, these determinants often fall outside the remit of oncology services and even outside human clinical and public health structures [[161], [162]]. Molecular epidemiology revealed genetic identity among trematodes in humans, reservoir animals, and fish, indicating that continuous zoonotic transmission cycles necessitate multisector interventions for sustained control [153]. These observations directly link cancer prevention to veterinary parasitology, aquatic ecology, food systems, and environmental management, rather than to oncology or human chemotherapy alone. For this reason, O. viverrini-associated cholangiocarcinoma prevention requires cross-sector collaboration beyond traditional infection control. Health services are needed for diagnosis, praziquantel treatment, ultrasound-based detection of advanced hepatobiliary pathology, and cancer referral. Veterinary and environmental sectors are needed to reduce reservoir-host egg contamination, monitor snail and fish intermediate hosts, improve sanitation around freshwater systems, and reduce environmental coexposures. Food-safety and community-education programs are needed to modify high-risk preparation of cyprinid fish without disregarding local dietary traditions.

A One Health frame aligns the causal chain with actionable prevention. Primary ecological and epidemiological studies link liver trematode transmission to aquaculture practices, freshwater snail ecology, flood cycles, and food preparation traditions [[163], [164], [165]]. Behavioral intervention trials have demonstrated that praziquantel chemotherapy combined with dietary education reduces reinfection more effectively than chemotherapy alone does, which is consistent with the concept of prevention leverage at the food system interface [166]. Field studies have indicated that integrated reservoir-host deworming can reduce environmental egg load and downstream human infection pressure in operational settings, providing a route for primary prevention upstream of cancer care [167]. Environmental contamination introduces a second One Health axis, as carcinogenic exposures, including nitrosamine sources and aquatic pollutants, can cooccur with parasite transmission zones, aligning parasite-driven epithelial injury with exogenous genotoxic pressure in the same population [97]. The preventive implication is that parasite treatment alone may not fully mitigate cancer risk after prolonged exposure and established biliary pathology, and thus, combined strategies are needed that address infection, reinfection drivers, food safety, and environmental exposures [[97], [160]]. The nitrosamine component illustrates why prevention cannot be limited to chemotherapy. Exogenous exposure can be reduced through safer preparation and storage of fermented fish and meat products, avoidance of raw or insufficiently fermented cyprinid fish, and reduction of nitrite- and nitrate-rich preservation conditions that favor N-nitroso compound formation. Endogenous nitrosation requires a complementary approach, including control of chronic infection and biliary inflammation, reduction of reinfection pressure, improved sanitation, and dietary practices that limit nitrosation chemistry. Once cholangiocarcinoma or schistosomiasis-associated bladder cancer develops, outcomes remain poor, reinforcing prevention as the dominant opportunity for population impact [[9], [159]]. Implications and future directions

The threshold model suggests that the cancer risk associated with helminth infection is stratifiable and depends on factors such as infection intensity, duration, tissue context, and coexposures [[168], [169]]. Community-based case–control and cross-sectional studies in northeastern Thailand consistently report higher cholangiocarcinoma risk and more advanced hepatobiliary pathology among individuals with more intense O. viverrini infection intensity (egg counts) and with evidence of long-standing or repeated infection, as assessed by prior egg positivity, screening history, or cumulative exposure proxies [[168], [169]]. Age should therefore not be treated only as a demographic covariate, but also as an imperfect proxy for cumulative exposure opportunity, repeated reinfection, and the long latency required for chronic biliary injury to progress toward malignant transformation. In this context, repeated praziquantel use can provide an additional, although imperfect, marker of repeated infection and persistent exposure behavior, rather than evidence for a direct carcinogenic effect of the drug. Dose–response patterns between C. sinensis infection intensity and biliary tract pathology have also been reported in China and Korea [[170], [171]]. These observations support risk modeling based on measurable parameters rather than parasite presence alone. Available diagnostic and risk-stratification tools relevant to identifying individuals approaching or exceeding a pro-oncogenic inflammation threshold are summarized in Table S4. In One Health terms, such risk models should combine individual infection intensity with reinfection history, dietary practices, local fish and snail transmission ecology, reservoir-host pressure, and environmental coexposures, because these factors jointly determine cumulative biliary risk.

Biomarkers aligned with threshold dimensions have been reported (Table 5). Oxidative DNA damage markers such as 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine are elevated in bile duct tissues and urine from patients with opisthorchiasis combined with advanced periductal fibrosis [172]. Experimental studies in O. viverrini-infected hamsters have additionally documented biliary DNA damage and adduct-related markers [118]. Circulating mediators, including IL-6 and TGF-β1, are correlated with the severity of fibrosis and proliferative biliary pathology in infected cohorts [[169], [173]]. Case–control data link dietary nitrosamine exposure to an increased risk of cholangiocarcinoma in the presence of opisthorchiasis infection [99]. Biliary microbiome studies report enrichment of nitrate-reducing and bile acid–transforming taxa in advanced opisthorchiasis-associated disease, consistent with endogenous carcinogen-generating mechanisms [102].

Prevention needs to extend beyond anthelmintic treatment. Praziquantel clears infection, but follow-up studies report a persistent risk among individuals with established periductal fibrosis [172]. In O. viverrini-infected hamsters, praziquantel treatment reduced iNOS expression and infection-associated oxidative DNA damage in the biliary tract [118]. Population studies have associated reduced consumption of raw or fermented freshwater fish with a lower prevalence of opisthorchiasis and a reduced incidence of biliary disease [183]. Hamster experiments demonstrated that nitrosamines potentiate parasite-associated cholangiocarcinogenesis, supporting a reduction in exposure as a component of cancer prevention [95]. Reservoir control and aquaculture interventions can reduce reinfection by lowering environmental egg contamination and interrupting transmission through intermediate hosts [[165], [184]]. Nitrosamine prevention should therefore be treated as a food-safety and inflammation-control problem. In endemic settings, the most practical target is not the complete removal of culturally important fermented foods, but the reduction of high-risk preparation practices, the prevention of infection from raw or undercooked fish, the interruption of reinfection, and the monitoring of nitrite, nitrate, and N-nitroso compound formation in fermented products.

Research gaps

Despite decades of epidemiological and experimental work, several critical gaps remain that limit a comprehensive understanding of helminth-associated carcinogenesis. Longitudinal human cohort studies that integrate age at first exposure, repeated egg positivity, reinfection after praziquantel treatment, cumulative parasitological burden, raw-fish consumption, inflammatory markers, genotoxic damage, periductal fibrosis, and cancer outcomes over time are lacking. Most available evidence relies on cross-sectional associations or retrospective case–control designs, which constrain causal inference and threshold modeling. Direct comparative studies between carcinogenic and noncarcinogenic helminths that share phylogenetic proximity or ecological niches remain rare. The contrast between opisthorchiids and closely related noncarcinogenic trematodes illustrates the potential of such comparisons; however, systematic, multiomics analyses across species are still lacking. The molecular identity and quantitative contribution of parasite-derived genotoxic compounds require further clarification. Although estrogen-like metabolites, oxysterols, and reactive oxygen species have been detected in opisthorchiid infections, their dose–response relationships, tissue distribution, and interactions with host detoxification pathways remain insufficiently defined. The relative contribution of exogenous nitrosamine intake and endogenous nitrosation remains insufficiently resolved in human opisthorchiasis-associated cholangiocarcinoma. Future studies should combine food-chemistry measurements, urinary or tissue biomarkers of nitrosation, infection intensity, reinfection history, inflammatory markers, dietary data, and environmental contaminant profiles in the same longitudinal cohorts. The role of the biliary and urinary microbiome in modulating genotoxic stress and epithelial proliferation is only beginning to be explored, with few studies integrating microbial, metabolic, and host genomic data in the same system. From translational and public health perspectives, there is a significant gap in etiological attribution within cancer surveillance systems. Helminth-associated cancers are rarely classified as infection attributable in cancer registries, which limits the estimation of burden, risk stratification, and prioritization in global oncology agendas. A related One Health gap is the limited linkage among cancer registries, parasitological surveillance, veterinary reservoir data, freshwater ecological monitoring, food-safety records, and environmental contaminant measurements. Mouse models frequently fail to reproduce helminth-associated carcinogenesis. Biological and methodological reasons for their failure are summarized in Box 2. Bridging these gaps will require coordinated efforts that combine evolutionary biology, mechanistic parasitology, cancer biology, and One Health–oriented epidemiology.

Why mouse models often fail to recapitulate helminth-associated cancer.

Experimental mouse models have been indispensable for studying helminth immunobiology. However, they frequently fail to reproduce the infection–associated carcinogenesis observed in humans. Several biological and methodological factors explain this limitation.First, mice rarely sustain the decades-long infections typical of human opisthorchiasis or schistosomiasis. Adult flukes survive for years in humans but persist for shorter periods in rodents, limiting cumulative epithelial injury and reducing the probability of crossing an oncogenic threshold.Second, murine biliary and urothelial epithelia differ fundamentally from their human counterparts in terms of turnover kinetics, stem cell organization, and bile acid composition. Mouse cholangiocytes display more rapid regeneration and distinct bile acid pools, which alter responses to chronic injury and oxidative stress.Third, standard laboratory mice lack the environmental and dietary cofactors critical for genotoxic synergy. Nitrosamine exposure, traditional fermented fish consumption, and complex biliary microbiomes are absent unless artificially introduced. Without these elements, inflammation remains largely reparative rather than mutagenic.Fourth, parasite–host coevolution is mismatched. Rodents are not natural definitive hosts for Opisthorchis viverrini or Clonorchis sinensis. Parasite excretory–secretory products may fail to engage the same signaling pathways or epithelial receptors as in humans.The failure of mouse models may reflect true biological robustness rather than experimental inadequacy. If cancer requires a rare convergence of factors, most short-term or simplified systems will systematically underestimate the risk.

Conclusions

In most host–parasite relationships of helminths, long-term coexistence is maintained through immunomodulation, limited genotoxicity, and avoidance of irreversible host damage. Cancer develops only in exceptional contexts in which multiple constraints break down simultaneously. Opisthorchiid liver trematodes exemplify such an exception. Their unique anatomical localization in the bile ducts, prolonged mechanical irritation, secretion of proliferative and genotoxic metabolites, and synergy with environmental and microbial cofactors create a biological convergence that is not replicated by most other helminths. These features place opisthorchiid infections above a critical threshold at which chronic inflammation transitions into carcinogenesis. Helminth-induced cancers represent a genuine One Health problem. They arise from inseparable interactions among parasite biology, human behavior, animal reservoirs, and environmental exposures. Addressing them effectively will require moving beyond isolated infection control or late cancer treatment toward integrated prevention, early risk identification, food-safety interventions, reservoir-host management, sanitation, and reduction of environmental coexposures in endemic freshwater systems.
