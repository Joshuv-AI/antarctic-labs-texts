Biofunctionalized magnetic nanoparticles incorporated MoS2 nanocomposite for enhanced n-butanol sensing at room temperature
Authors: Ruchika Thayil, Saidi Reddy Parne
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractN-butanol is well known to be a flammable and harmful liquid that is a potential threat to human health and property. Therefore, it is important to monitor the concentration of n-butanol in the surroundings. The need for highly efficient toxic gas detection is urgent and has been driving the research on gas sensors for practical applications. Molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) has been attracting significant interest for gas detection at room temperature. Herein, we report biofunctionalized magnetic nanoparticles incorporated MoS2 for sensing n-butanol. The biosynthesized magnetite nanoparticles (CT-Fe3O4) were synthesized by the addition of Cinnamomum Tamala (CT) leaf extract, and subsequently, the nanocomposite was synthesized using a hydrothermal method. Highly sensitive sensors based on MoS2-CT-Fe3O4 were fabricated and tested for sensing different concentrations of n-butanol. The nanocomposites showed a good sensing performance (Δ R/Rair %) of 72% towards 20 ppm of n-butanol, indicating the potential use of MoS2-CT-Fe3O4 nanocomposite for sensing n-butanol at room temperature.Keywords: Biosynthesis, Gas sensing, Nanocomposite, MoS2, N-butanolSubject terms: Sensors and biosensors, Pollution remediation

Introduction

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are those compounds that evaporate quickly at ambient temperature 1. In addition to being harmful to the environment, it also severely impacts human health and property 2. Among them, n-butanol is one of the vital solvents in organic chemistry laboratories and is also widely used as an extracting agent and raw material in industries and laboratories. Exposure to n-butanol vapors can cause several symptoms, like headache, drowsiness, dizziness, dermatitis, and also irritation of the nose, eyes, and throat 3. Furthermore, n-butanol is highly inflammable. Furthermore, an air mixture that contains 1.45–11.25% n-butanol (by volume) can explode at a temperature greater than 35 ºC. Its combustion product contains carbon oxides, such as CO and CO2, that are suffocating gasses 4. Therefore, efficient sensors for monitoring n-butanol for industries and laboratories are essential 5. The demand for monitoring room temperature has intensified due to the growing necessity for processes that are friendly to the environment 6.

Recent research has highlighted that two-dimensional (2D) materials transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) are capable of detecting toxic gases at low and room temperatures 7–11. Their effectiveness in gas detection is largely due to their unique properties, including a single-atom thickness, extensive surface areas, semiconductor characteristics, edge-located dangling bonds, and flexible basal planes, making them promising candidates for gas sensing technologies, especially for room temperature identification of toxic gases 12–16. Traditional sensors, which often need significant power for gas sensing, are not well-suited for application in flexible electronic devices due to their energy demands. In contrast, sensors based on these novel 2D materials offer a solution that could significantly enhance convenience and pave the way for innovative applications.

Over recent years, TMDs have gained significant attention for their gas-sensing abilities due to their ability to operate at lower temperatures, including ambient conditions 17. These 2D materials are noted for their remarkable structural as well as physical characteristics, such as large surface areas, durability, porosity, and layered assembly 18–22. Among the various TMDs, molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) is known for its exceptional stability. The incorporation of TMDs into chemical sensor designs has grown, driven by their distinctive characteristics, notably their large surface areas and semiconductor properties. Research focusing on MoS2 as a gas sensor has notably contributed to advancing the field beyond traditional metal oxide materials, offering new possibilities for detection technologies 23,24.

Current studies reveal that materials based on MoS2 have been effectively applied in sensing various gases at ambient temperatures, including ammonia (NH3), carbon oxides, hydrogen (H2), nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide (SO2), as well as organic compounds such as toluene, benzene, methanol, ethanol, and formaldehyde 24, 25. Despite this wide range of detectable gases, there are a few reports concerning the ability of MoS2-based sensors to detect n-butanol.

Choi et al. reported MoS2 nanostructures synthesized by employing a solvothermal method, which showed a response of 7.8% towards n-butanol at 130 °C 26. Recently, Liu et al. synthesized CdS/MoS2 composite via a hydrothermal process, which led to a response (Ra/Rg) of around 85 towards 100 ppm butanol at a temperature of 230 °C 27. The reports indicate low response and high-temperature requirements. Moreover, there are no reports that show that MoS2-based nanostructures successfully enhanced n-butanol sensing performance at room temperature.

Bio-synthesized materials are gaining popularity due to their sustainability, biodegradability, and reduced carbon footprint, making them eco-friendly alternatives to traditional materials. Advances in biotechnology have enabled the creation of materials with tailored properties, offering superior performance for specific applications. Moreover, they are often non-toxic, align with increasing environmental regulations, and meet the growing consumer demand for green products. They also support economic benefits by reducing dependency on imported raw materials and enhancing local production capabilities. Using leaf extracts to synthesize nanoparticles offers a green, cost-effective, and sustainable approach, which makes use of plant compounds to reduce metal ions into nanoparticles with diverse applications in medicine, electronics, and environmental remediation.

Cinnamomum Tamala (CT) leaf is a well-known Indian spice called “Indian bay leaf” or “tejpatta” and has been well-known for its antibacterial properties 28. The CT leaf extract (aqueous) is composed of eugenol (as a main ingredient) and kaempferol in addition to many flavonoids, which include different coordinating groups like carbonyl and hydroxyl; hence, this extract has been used as a surface modifier and also as a reducing agent for reducing metal ions in nanoparticle synthesis 29.

In this work, we report biofunctionalized magnetic nanoparticles incorporated MoS2 for room temperature n-butanol sensing. The biosynthesized magnetite nanoparticles (CT-Fe3O4) were synthesized by the addition of CT leaf extract, and subsequently, the nanocomposite was synthesized using a hydrothermal method. The gas sensors based on MoS2-CT-Fe3O4 were used to sense various concentrations of n-butanol. The nanocomposites showed good sensing performance (Δ R/Rair %) of around 72% for 20 ppm n-butanol, indicating the potential use of MoS2-CT-Fe3O4 nanocomposite for sensing n-butanol at room temperature.

Experimental techniques

All chemicals used were analytical grade, obtained from Sisco Research Laboratories, and used without further purification. The CT leaves were collected from a home-grown tree located in Nuvem-Goa, India.

Preparation of CT extract

The procedure includes preparing the CT extract from fresh CT leaves. These leaves were meticulously washed and sun-dried until complete dehydration was achieved. The leaves were then ground to a fine powder. A precise quantity of this powder of 5 g was mixed with 200 mL of deionized water (D.I water), and the solution was maintained at a temperature of 80 °C for 2 h. After heating, the mixture was filtered with Whatman filter paper No. 1 to obtain the liquid extract, which was then stored under refrigeration for future applications.

Synthesis of CT-Fe3O4

The synthesis of the CT-Fe3O4 was initiated with the addition of 20 mL of the prepared CT extract to 230 mL of D.I. water. Subsequently, 3.407 g of ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4.7H2O) was incorporated into the solution. The mixture was then stirred at a speed of 870 rpm, which formed a light green solution. To regulate the solution’s pH to 10, a solution of 6 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was incrementally added while constant stirring for an additional 10 min, leading to the formation of a black precipitate. This precipitate was extensively washed with D.I. water and subsequently air-dried overnight.

Synthesis of CT-MoS2-Fe3O4

For the synthesis of CT-MoS2-Fe3O4 nanocomposite, 250 mg of Fe3O4 nanoparticles were added into 60 mL of D.I. water, followed by a 15-min sonication to ensure uniform dispersion. To this solution, 5 g of sodium molybdate dihydrate (Na2MoO4.2H2O) was added, which was then subjected to another 15 min of sonication. Subsequently, 4 g of thioacetamide was introduced into the solution, followed by a further 15 min of sonication at room temperature. The resultant mixture was transferred into an autoclave and maintained at a temperature of 200 °C for a duration of 24 h, facilitating the formation of the CT-MoS2-Fe3O4 nanocomposite. Characterizations

UV–Vis absorbance spectra were recorded using a Shimadzu UV-2401 PC UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The Raman spectra have been obtained using the LabRAM HR Evolution Raman system using a 532 nm laser source. Powder XRD pattern was obtained in the angle range of 10°–70°, using CuKα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images and Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) spectra were acquired using a Carle Zeiss EVO-18 system. An atomic force microscope (Bruker AFM Probes-TESPA-300, USA) was used to find the surface features of the samples. The gas testing experimental setup consisted of a gas detection chamber and a Keithley (6517 B, USA) connected to a computer.

Gas sensing study

Chemiresistors are characterized by several beneficial properties like low detection limits, ease of manufacture, cost-effectiveness, and a straightforward principle of operation 41–43. Additionally, they require only a minimal amount of sensing material, which significantly reduces expenses 44. They operate by making use of their ability to convert environmental exposure into changes in electrical resistance. For enhancing the efficiency of MoS2 gas detectors, strategies like the introduction of sulfur vacancies, designing morphologies, decorating with noble metals, irradiation using light, doping techniques, and composite formation have been frequently applied. The structure’s morphology significantly contributes to enhancing the sensor’s sensitivity by increasing the availability of reactive sites 45, which is vital for gas detection. Thus, the gas-sensing efficiency is largely influenced by the chemical and physical properties that arise from the material’s structural design.

In the realm of gas detection, the development of MoS2-based composites has proven to be a successful approach to improving gas sensing characteristics. Compared to pure MoS2, the construction of nanocomposites of MoS2, incorporating both binary and ternary configurations, has demonstrated superior gas detection capabilities 44. This enhancement is attributed to the combined strengths of each material component, along with the heterojunction formation of p–p, n–n, and n–p types. These junctions facilitate a more efficient electron transfer at the material interface and increase the interfacial barrier due to variations in the Fermi level 46. Consequently, these composites notably boost the sensor’s responsiveness, making them an efficient method for modification 47.

For the construction of the sensor element, both MoS2 and MoS2-CT-Fe3O4 were applied onto glass substrates. To ensure adequate electrical conductivity, silver paste was applied on the glass substrate and subsequently heated using a controlled oven at 70 °C for 20 min to establish reliable electrical connections. The sensors thus prepared were tested for sensing n-butanol in a static environment, as shown in the experimental setup shown in Fig. 5.

Gas sensing characteristics

The properties of semiconductors used for sensing rely on reactions of the semiconductor with gasses. These reactions cause a variation in the resistance of the semiconductor. Oxygen in the atmosphere gets adsorbed on the sensor surface and traps the electrons from the sensor’s conduction band 44. Several reactions could take place when the required concentration of gasses is present. It may either react with previously extracted oxygen or with the surface of the semiconductor material. In an n-type semiconductor, there is an increase in resistivity due to electrons being transferred into the conduction band by the oxidizing gas molecules, and there is a decrease in resistivity due to the capture of electrons from the conduction band upon exposure to a reducing gas 47. The opposite behavior is observed in semiconductors of p-type, where in response to the same gas, the resistivity changes are reversed 47. The nanocomposite sensor synthesized exhibit a p-type semiconductor response when detecting n-butanol, as illustrated in the experimental results shown in Fig. 6.

(a) Measurements of sensing (b) Response % vs Concentration (c) Response and Recover times.

The gas sensing performance was evaluated at varying concentrations of n-butanol vapors, which is 5, 10, 15, and 20 ppm. The performance was evaluated by estimating the response, which was calculated using,

Response (%) = Rg-RaRa×100%

wherein Ra is the resistance value under air while Rg is the resistance value under gas. The sensitivity of the sensor to n-butanol gas concentration was found to enhance as the concentration levels of n-butanol increased. The time required for a sensor to achieve 90% of its maximum resistance upon exposure to the gas is defined as the response time. Conversely, the recovery time refers to the duration needed for the sensor to revert to 10% of its maximum resistance after the elimination of the target gas from its environment 48. The sample MoS2-CT-Fe3O4 demonstrated good sensing, with sensing response (ΔR/Rair%) for 20 ppm of n-butanol around 72%, while pure MoS2 showed no response towards n-butanol sensing at room temperature.

Achieving full sensor recovery at ambient temperature can sometimes pose a challenge 49. However, our sensor demonstrated complete recovery, which can be attributed to its adsorption capabilities and surface functionalization along with unique morphology. The occurrence of incomplete recovery can often be linked to how strongly gas molecules adhere to the MoS2 surface; a strong bond can slow the process of these molecules detaching, thereby extending the recovery period. The way in which MoS2 interacts with gas molecules can be significantly altered through functionalization, a process crucial for enhancing the sensor’s sensitivity and speed of recovery. Additionally, the availability of a large surface area provides an abundance of active sites for interaction. Nonetheless, the selection of specific functional groups and their precise concentrations requires fine-tuning to achieve optimal performance. The response/recovery times of the sensor were found to be around 121 s and 156 s, respectively. The sensors were found to be stable for up to 80 days at room temperature.

The SEM images show flower-like MoS2 with rod-like Fe3O4, which gives rise to an increase in surface roughness. The high RMS roughness of 209.2 nm and mean roughness of 162.5 nm were obtained from AFM measurements, as seen in Fig. 4. An increasing surface roughness leads to an increase in surface area, which facilitates gas molecules’ adsorption 50. This characteristic is essential for detecting gases. The development of MoS2 composite for gas detection has markedly improved these properties. Compared to pure MoS2, nanocomposites of MoS2 have demonstrated superior gas detection efficiencies.

This improvement is attributed to the unique properties of each constituent material, coupled with the formation of heterojunctions of p–p, n–n and n–p types. These heterojunctions facilitate the directional transfer of electrons at the material interfaces, thereby increasing the interface barrier due to variations in the Fermi level 45. Consequently, these composites significantly enhanced the sensitivity for n-butanol sensing and were found to be ineffective in sensing NH3, xylene, benzene, acetone, and ethanol. Creating MoS2 composites is, therefore a highly effective way to enhance gas-sensing properties. Additionally, the improved sensing performance can be attributed to the unique hierarchical heterostructure and multiple synergistic effects.

This effectively illustrates the efficiency of nanocomposite structures in overcoming the challenges associated with the low gas sensing response of MoS2, thereby improving the overall sensing performance. The results show the potential of MoS2-based nanocomposites for detecting n-butanol gas, which opens new avenues for advancements in gas sensors that can be useful for industrial and environmental monitoring. Mechanism of gas sensing

The mechanism of gas sensing in air is accompanied by the capture of the free electrons from the conduction band of MoS2. As a result, the oxygen molecules would form surface oxygen ions which is negatively charged 49.

Conclusion

In this work, we report biofunctionalized magnetic nanoparticles incorporated MoS2 for room temperature n-butanol sensing. The biosynthesized magnetite nanoparticles were synthesized by adding CT leaf extract, and subsequently, the nanocomposite was synthesized using hydrothermal method. Highly sensitive sensors based on MoS2-CT-Fe3O4 were fabricated and applied sensing at different n-butanol gas concentrations. The nanocomposites showed good sensing performance (Δ R/Rair %) for 20 ppm n-butanol around 72%, indicating the potential use of MoS2-CT-Fe3O4 nanocomposite for sensing n-butanol at room temperature. This highlights the effectiveness of nanocomposite formations in improving the inherently weak gas detection capability of MoS2, thereby boosting its overall performance in sensing n-butanol. The research indicates that nanocomposites incorporating MoS2 have significant potential in detecting gases, particularly n-butanol, for gas detection technologies for environmental monitoring and industrial uses.
