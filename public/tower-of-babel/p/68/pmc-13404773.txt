The Level and Clinical Significance of Red Blood Cell Distribution Width in Patients With Congenital Heart Disease Associated With Pulmonary Hypertension
Authors: Xiaoqin luo, Xianliang Xiong, Jiang Li, Jun Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrevious studies have shown that red blood cell distribution width (RDW) is an independent risk factor for the prognosis of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and is also associated with cardiovascular events in patients with congenital heart disease (CHD). However, its role in patients with CHD‐associated pulmonary hypertension (PAH‐CHD) has not been reported. This study aimed to investigate the level and clinical significance of RDW in PAH‐CHD patients. A total of 129 patients with CHD confirmed by right heart catheterization (RHC) were enrolled, including 109 PAH‐CHD patients and 20 CHD patients without PAH. Cardiac ultrasound, blood biochemistry, and RHC hemodynamic data were collected and analyzed. The RDW level in the PAH‐CHD group was significantly higher than that in the non‐PAH‐CHD group and the normal control group (p < 0.05). Using a cut‐off value of 13.25% for RDW, the sensitivity and specificity for predicting severe PAH‐CHD were 72.2% and 77.9%, respectively, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.798 (p < 0.001). RDW was significantly correlated with PAH risk stratification indicators (r = 0.54, p < 0.001). In conclusion, RDW levels are significantly elevated in patients with severe PAH‐CHD, and a cut‐off value of 13.25% demonstrates high predictive value for severe PAH‐CHD. Therefore, RDW level could serve as an economical and practical indicator for assessing the condition and prognosis of PAH‐CHD patients.

Introduction

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a common pulmonary vascular disease, characterized by progressive elevation of pulmonary arterial pressure, which often leads to right heart failure and even death [1]. The most common cause of PAH in China is CHD, with 5%–10% of adult CHD patients developing PAH [2, 3, 4]. Patients with PAH‐CHD generally have a better prognosis, as early‐stage CHD patients can often be treated surgically; however, for patients who are not diagnosed and treated timely, long‐term pulmonary circulation volume or pressure overload can damage pulmonary vascular endothelial function and lead to vascular remodeling, potentially progressing to Eisenmenger syndrome (ES), with disease progression resulting in right heart failure and even death [4, 5]. Therefore, the prognosis of PAH‐CHD depends on early detection and treatment of PAH.

RDW is currently a routine test item in automatic hematology analyzers and an indicator for measuring the heterogeneity of the volume of circulating red blood cells. In the past, RDW was often used to distinguish the types of anemia [6]. In recent years, more and more studies have shown that RDW is closely related to the occurrence and prognosis of cardiovascular events, including heart failure, acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary embolism, atrial fibrillation, etc [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. In addition, studies have shown that RDW is closely related to the risk of all‐cause mortality and heart failure hospitalization in adult patients with CHD, and RDW can be used as a risk stratification indicator for adult CHD patients [13, 14]. Besides, RDW is also of great significance in the population of patients with pulmonary hypertension (PH) [15, 16]. For example, elevated RDW is associated with the risk of death in PH patients, as well as with the risk of developing chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) after acute pulmonary embolism (PE) [17, 18, 19]. Recently, a study by Zhao et al. showed that RDW can identify PH in patients with systemic sclerosis (SSc) [20].

The above studies indicate that the RDW level is of great significance in the occurrence, development, and prognosis of PH. The purpose of this study is to explore the relationship between RDW level and PAH condition assessment and risk stratification in patients with PAH‐CHD, and to evaluate the feasibility and application value of RDW as a biomarker for PAH‐CHD patients.

Methods

Research Object

This study is a single‐center retrospective study. A total of 129 patients with CHD confirmed by RHC were collected from The Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University from June 2016 to June 2018. The diagnostic criteria for PAH are: mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) ≥ 20 mmHg (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa) measured by RHC at rest, pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) ≤ 15 mmHg, and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) > 3 Wood Units. Patients with other known causes of elevated RDW, including hematological disorders, active malignancy, or end‐stage renal disease requiring dialysis, were excluded. However, patients with mild renal impairment, subclinical iron deficiency, or chronic low‐grade inflammation were not systematically excluded, which may have contributed to RDW elevation independent of PAH‐CHD.

For the purpose of the study, basic information such as gender, age, height, and weight of all subjects was recorded; and each group took 3 mL of fasting antecubital vein blood on the next morning after admission to the hospital in an EDTA anticoagulant tube and sent it to the laboratory. Various blood routine indexes were detected by using the Sysmex XN10 hematology analyzer, the biochemical indicators such as aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total bile acid (TBA), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), creatinine (Cr), and uric acid (UA) were determined using Hitachi 7600‐020 automatic biochemistry analyzer; N‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) was determined using Roche E411 electrochemiluminescence immunoassay analyzer.

Echocardiography

In this study, a GE Vivid7 full‐digital color Doppler ultrasound was used to measure and record the following parameters in the left ventricular long‐axis view, left ventricular short‐axis view, and apical four‐chamber view: left atrial systolic diameter (LAS), right atrial systolic diameter (RAS), left ventricular end‐diastolic diameter (LVEDd), right ventricular end‐diastolic diameter (RVD), aortic diameter (AO), main pulmonary artery diameter (PA), systolic tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRV), and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF).

Right Heart Catheterisation

All patients underwent RHC via the right femoral vein at the Interventional Catheterization Centre of The Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. In addition, all CHD patients underwent puncture of the right femoral vein under local anesthesia, with a 6 F vascular sheath inserted. Heparin (50 U/kg) was intravenously injected for anticoagulation. An MP catheter was advanced through the sheath to the superior vena cava, inferior vena cava, right atrium, right ventricle, and pulmonary artery in sequence to measure the pressure and blood oxygen saturation of each chamber. When an abnormal channel was present, the catheter was inserted into the abnormal channel, and selective angiography was performed if necessary to confirm the diagnosis. Routine puncture of the right femoral artery was conducted to measure femoral artery oxygen saturation, and based on the measurement results, hemodynamic parameters such as cardiac output (CO), pulmonary blood flow (Qp), systemic blood flow (Qs), shunt flow, and total pulmonary resistance (PVR) were calculated using the Fick method.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis of data was performed using SPSS 22.0 statistical software. Enumeration data were expressed as frequencies. Measurement data conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (± SD), and those not conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as median (1/4–3/4 quantile). Normality test was conducted using exploratory analysis or one‐sample K‐S goodness‐of‐fit test. The comparison of enumeration data was performed using the chi‐square test for row × column data. For the comparison of means among multiple samples of measurement data with a normal distribution, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. Before comparing the means within and between groups, a homogeneity of variance test was conducted. Under the premise of homogeneous variance, the LSD test was used for multiple comparisons; under the premise of heterogeneous variance, Dunnett's T3 test was used. For the comparison of means between paired samples of measurement data with a normal distribution, the paired samples t‐test was used. Measurement data not conforming to a normal distribution were first transformed to conform to a normal distribution; if they still did not confirm to a normal distribution after transformation, non‐parametric tests for multiple independent samples were used. Pearson correlation analysis was used for correlation analysis. All tests were two‐tailed, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. ROC curves were used to evaluate the diagnostic cut‐off value, sensitivity, and specificity of RDW levels for different degrees of PH. To address concerns regarding statistical power in the smaller subgroups, we additionally performed pre‐specified pairwise Welch's t‐tests with reporting of mean differences, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), Cohen's d effect sizes and post‐hoc achieved power, and a sensitivity analysis combining the mild and moderate PAH‐CHD subgroups; power and required sample size calculations were performed using a two‐sided alpha of 0.05.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Enrolled Patients

129 eligible cases of patients with CHD were enrolled in this study, and divided into three groups based on pulmonary artery pressure in patients with CHD: the PAH‐CHD group (Group A, 109 cases), the nPAH‐CHD group (Group B, congenital heart disease without PAH, 20 cases), and the normal control group (Group C, 20 cases). Group A was further subdivided according to pulmonary artery pressure into mild PAH‐CHD (Group A1, 20 mmHg ≤ mPAH < 35 mmHg, 19 cases), moderate PAH‐CHD (Group A2, 35 mmHg ≤ mPAH < 45 mmHg, 18 cases), and severe PAH‐CHD (Group A3, mPAH ≥ 45 mmHg, 72 cases).

In this study, among the general data of the subjects in each group, there were no statistically significant differences in gender, age, and height between the groups (p > 0.05). However, the weight and BMI of the PAH‐CHD group (Group A) were lower than those of the normal control group (Group C), with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05). Among them, there were no statistically significant differences between the mild PAH‐CHD group (Group A1), moderate PAH‐CHD group (Group A2), and the normal control group (Group C) (p > 0.05). But the weight and BMI of the severe PAH‐CHD group (Group A3) were statistically different from those of the nPAH‐CHD group (Group B), normal control group (Group C), mild PAH‐CHD group (Group A1), and moderate PAH‐CHD group (Group A2)(Table 1).

RDW Levels and ROC Curve Analysis

Comparing of complete blood cell count(CBC) results, there were no statistically significant differences in MCV, MCH, and MCHC across the groups. The RBC, Hb, and HCT in the PAH‐CHD group (Group A) were significantly higher than those in the nPAH‐CHD group (Group B), with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05). Further comparisons revealed that only the severe PAH‐CHD group (Group A3) exhibited statistically significant differences in RBC, Hb, and HCT when compared with the nPAH‐CHD group (Group B) (p < 0.05). In addition, the levels of RBC, Hb, and HCT in the severe PAH‐CHD group (Group A3) also showed statistically significant differences when compared with the normal control group (Group C), mild PAH‐CHD group (Group A1), and moderate PAH‐CHD group (Group A2) (all p < 0.05). At the same time, the study found that the RDW in the PAH‐CHD group (Group A) was significantly higher than that in the nPAH‐CHD group (Group B) and the normal control group (Group C) (p < 0.05). Further analysis revealed statistically significant differences in RDW levels between the severe PAH‐CHD group (Group A3) and the normal control group (Group C), nPAH‐CHD group (Group B), mild PAH‐CHD group (Group A1), and moderate PAH‐CHD group (Group A2) (p < 0.05). However, there were no statistically significant differences in RDW levels between the mild PAH‐CHD group (Group A1), moderate PAH‐CHD group (Group A2), normal control group (Group C), and nPAH‐CHD group (Group B) (Table 4). To further evaluate whether the absence of an RDW difference in the milder subgroups reflected a true null finding or insufficient statistical power, we performed pre‐specified pairwise Welch's t‐tests as a sensitivity analysis. This re‐analysis showed that the moderate PAH‐CHD group (Group A2) had a significantly higher RDW level than both the normal control group (mean difference 0.72%, 95% CI 0.26–1.18; t = 3.19, p = 0.004; Cohen's d = 1.06; achieved power 88.8%) and the nPAH‐CHD group (mean difference 0.62%, 95% CI 0.16–1.08; t = 2.78, p = 0.010; Cohen's d = 0.93; achieved power 79.1%). When the mild and moderate subgroups were combined (n = 37), RDW remained significantly higher than that of the normal control group (mean difference 0.41%, 95% CI 0.07–0.75; p = 0.020; Cohen's d = 0.60). In contrast, the mild PAH‐CHD group (Group A1) did not differ from the control or nPAH‐CHD group (p = 0.52 and p = 0.95, respectively); however, the observed effect size was small (Cohen's d = 0.21) and the achieved power was only 9.8%, indicating that this null result should be interpreted with caution and may in part reflect a Type II error owing to the limited size of the mild subgroup (n = 19) (Table 5).

The Influence of Serum RDW Levels on Clinical Indicators

To further clarify the direct relationship between RDW and risk stratification in patients with PAH‐CHD, we performed risk stratification on 109 PAH‐CHD patients in accordance with simplified risk stratification criteria. The risk stratification results showed that among the 109 PAH‐CHD patients, 56 were classified as low‐risk, 46 as intermediate‐risk, and seven as high‐risk. The RDW levels in the low‐risk, intermediate‐risk, and high‐risk groups were 13.3 ± 1.06%, 13.76 ± 1.15%, and 19.36 ± 3.46%, respectively. One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed that the RDW level in the high‐risk group was significantly higher than that in the low‐risk group and intermediate‐risk group, with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05). However, there was no statistically significant difference in RDW level between the low‐risk group and the intermediate‐risk group. A further correlation analysis between RDW and risk stratification showed a correlation coefficient of r = 0.54 (p < 0.001), suggesting that RDW is correlated with risk stratification (Figure 2).

Discussion

This study was a single‐center retrospective study. We observed the RDW levels in patients with PAH‐CHD, and the results showed that RDW levels have certain clinical significance for the assessment of disease severity and prognosis in PAH‐CHD patients.

We found that the RDW levels in PAH‐CHD patients were significantly higher than those in nPAH‐CHD patients and healthy individuals. Notably, the increase in RDW levels only occurred in patients with severe PAH‐CHD, while no significant change in RDW levels was observed in patients with mild‐to‐moderate PAH‐CHD. Furthermore, when using the ROC curve to evaluate the accuracy of RDW in predicting severe PAH‐CHD, it was found that when RDW was set at a cutoff value of 13.25%, it exhibited high predictive value for severe PAH‐CHD. The results also revealed that RDW levels had a good correlation with indicators used to measure the disease severity of PAH‐CHD patients, such as SPAP, mPAP, PVR, and Qp/Qs. Specifically, the higher the RDW level, the more severe the patient's condition. In addition, the correlation analysis between RDW levels and PAH risk stratification indicators showed that RDW levels were positively correlated with WHO functional class and NT‐proBNP, and negatively correlated with right heart CI and SvO2. This indicates that the higher the RDW level, the worse the prognosis of PAH‐CHD patients. It should be noted, however, that this study relied on univariate correlation analyses and did not perform multivariate logistic regression; therefore, we cannot establish RDW as an independent predictor of disease severity after adjusting for established confounders such as NT‐proBNP, 6‐min walk distance (6MWD), and WHO functional class. Furthermore, no head‐to‐head ROC comparison between RDW and these established biomarkers was conducted, and it remains unclear whether RDW provides incremental predictive value beyond currently used risk stratification tools.

In this study, subgroup analysis of PAH‐CHD revealed that there was no significant increase in RDW in patients with mild‐to‐moderate PAH‐CHD, a significant elevation in RDW levels was only observed in the severe PAH‐CHD subgroup. This pattern is similar to the changes in other indicators across PAH subgroups, including indicators related to systemic status, such as body weight, SaO2, and AST; CBC indicators, such as RBC, Hb, and MCV. Therefore, we propose that during the disease progression of PAH‐CHD patients, RDW is more likely a “phenomenon” that emerges when the disease progresses to a certain stage‐one associated with the deterioration of general systemic condition and abnormal erythropoiesis‐rather than an “essence” that drives the progression of PAH‐CHD. Notably, this phenomenon is only fully manifested in patients with severe PAH‐CHD. In addition, since the PAH‐CHD group in this study included a relatively large number of patients with severe PAH‐CHD, the RDW levels in the PAH‐CHD group were higher than those in the normal control group and nPAH‐CHD group. However, RDW is more consistent with clinical reality and exhibits higher accuracy when used as an indicator for predicting severe PAH‐CHD, rather than for predicting PAH‐CHD in general. It should be noted, however, that the pairwise Welch's t‐test sensitivity analysis demonstrated that RDW was in fact significantly elevated in the moderate PAH‐CHD subgroup compared with both control and nPAH‐CHD groups, indicating that RDW elevation does not arise abruptly only at the severe stage but follows a graded, dose–response pattern that becomes statistically detectable from the moderate stage onward and is most pronounced in severe disease. The apparent flat RDW level in the mild subgroup should therefore be interpreted with caution: with only 19 patients in the mild group and 20 controls, the analysis had merely 9.8% achieved power to detect the observed small effect, so a true but modest RDW elevation in mild PAH‐CHD cannot be excluded and would require a substantially larger cohort to confirm or refute.

The prognosis of PAH‐CHD is generally favorable. Most patients with CHD who present with left‐to‐right shunting in the early stage can be treated successfully with surgery. However, if the condition is not detected and treated surgically in a timely manner, disease progression may lead to right‐to‐left shunting or even ES at this stage, patients mostly lose the opportunity for surgical treatment, and the prognosis is poor. The 3‐year survival rate of ES patients is only 77%, with a mean life expectancy of (32.5 ± 16) years. Therefore, the prognosis of PAH‐CHD depends on the progression of PAH and the results of risk stratification. The 6th WSPH recommends using a simplified risk stratification scale to assess patients’ risk and predict their long‐term prognosis by evaluating key clinical indicators in the baseline state before treatment [21]. Specifically: High‐risk patients have an estimated 1‐year mortality rate of > 10%; Intermediate‐risk patients have an estimated 1‐year mortality rate of 5%–10%; Low‐risk patients have an estimated 1‐year mortality rate of < 5%. Since most PAH‐CHD patients have a better prognosis compared to patients with other types of PAH‐especially those who are eligible for surgery in the early stage, as most of them can be treated successfully with surgery‐this study did not conduct an analysis of endpoint events (such as mortality or heart failure hospitalization rate) in all PAH‐CHD patients. However, in the correlation analysis between RDW and risk stratification indicators, we found that RDW had a strong correlation with WHO functional class, NT‐proBNP, right heart CI, and SvO2. An increase in RDW indicates a poor prognosis. By comparing RDW levels across different risk stratification groups, we found that the RDW level in the high‐risk group was significantly higher than that in the low‐risk and intermediate‐risk groups‐this further validates the relationship between RDW, risk stratification, and prognosis. Previous studies have shown that elevated RDW levels indicate poor prognosis in patients with PAH caused by mixed factors, IPAH, CHD‐related ES, and adult CHD. The conclusions of our study are consistent with those of previous research.

Elevated RDW is commonly observed in conditions such as hemoglobin synthesis disorders, increased red blood cell destruction, and shortened red blood cell lifespan. It is often associated with the body's pathophysiological states, such as malnutrition (deficiency of iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid), bone marrow suppression, reduced erythropoietin synthesis, renal impairment, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammatory reactions [22, 23]. However, the specific reasons for the elevation of RDW in patients with PAH‐CHD remain unclear, and it is currently considered to be related to the following factors.

Current studies suggest that impaired iron activation, reduced erythropoietin synthesis, and deficiency of hematopoietic raw materials are direct factors causing elevated RDW levels [23]. Existing studies have shown that in patients with chronic heart failure, the reduction of hematopoietic raw materials leads to a decrease in hemoglobin, which is related to poor prognosis [24, 25]. In patients with IPAH, it has also been found that serum iron, ferritin, and transferrin saturation are significantly decreased, erythropoietin is compensatorily increased, and RDW levels are significantly elevated. The abnormal increase of the iron regulatory hormone hepcidin, which impairs intestinal iron absorption, may be the main mechanism [26]. In ES patients, approximately 35% are complicated with iron deficiency, and iron deficiency is an independent risk factor for all‐cause mortality [27], while iron replacement therapy can improve exercise tolerance and quality of life in ES patients [28]. Unfortunately, this retrospective study did not detect indicators directly reflecting iron metabolism, nor did it measure the levels of folate and vitamin B12 in PAH‐CHD patients, thus failing to further explore whether PAH‐CHD patients have nutritional deficiencies.

However, the data of this study show that PAH‐CHD patients do not have obvious anemia; on the contrary, the levels of RBC, Hb, and HCT in the severe PAH‐CHD group are significantly higher than those in the mild‐to‐moderate PAH‐CHD group, and the levels of MCV, MCH, and MCHC do not decrease significantly. Similar results were observed in Baggen et al.‘s study on RDW levels in CHD patients [14]. These phenomena suggest that absolute deficiency of hematopoietic raw materials and anemia play a limited role in PAH‐CHD patients. It is worth noting that in PAH‐CHD patients, especially in severe PAH‐CHD, most patients have chronic and persistent hypoxia due to the existence of bidirectional shunts or pulmonary‐systemic shunts. In this study, it can also be found that SaO2 and mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO2) are significantly decreased in patients with severe PAH‐CHD, while the levels of RBC, Hb, and HCT are correspondingly increased, and RDW is significantly negatively correlated with SaO2 and SvO2, with correlation coefficients of −0.651 (p < 0.001) and −0.351 (p < 0.001) respectively. Therefore, we speculate that the elevation of RDW may be related to the aggravation of hypoxia. Under hypoxic conditions, the increase of erythropoietin leads to a compensatory increase in red blood cells. Whether there are other factors such as relative deficiency of hematopoietic raw materials that cause elevated RDW levels in this process requires further research.

Oxidative stress and inflammatory responses are known factors that can affect the lifespan of red blood cells. Existing studies have shown that oxidative stress can promote the occurrence of hemolytic anemia, shorten the lifespan of red blood cells, increase the proportion of young red blood cells, and thus lead to an increase in RDW [29]. Oxidative stress can also increase the heterogeneity of red blood cell volume by affecting the maturation process and life cycle of red blood cells [30, 31, 32]. Chronic inflammation is closely related to ineffective erythropoiesis. Inflammatory factors such as TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 can reduce the sensitivity of bone marrow erythroid progenitor cells to erythropoietin, inhibit the production and maturation of red blood cells, and result in elevated RDW levels [33, 34]. It has also been found in studies that RDW is significantly positively correlated with inflammatory factors such as C‐reactive protein [35], erythrocyte sedimentation rate [36], IL‐6 [23], and soluble tumor necrosis factor receptor [22], suggesting that inflammatory response is an important factor causing elevated RDW. However, due to the lack of data on inflammatory factors in the subjects of this study, it is impossible to further verify the relationship between RDW and inflammatory factors. Other factors such as free cholesterol [37], thrombosis [38], activation of the neuroendocrine system [39], and impairment of liver and kidney functions [36, 40] may also be related to changes in RDW levels [41].

It is worth noting how the findings of the present study relate to and differ from prior research. Previous studies have demonstrated that elevated RDW predicts adverse outcomes in patients with PAH of mixed etiology, idiopathic PAH (IPAH), and ES [17, 18, 19]. However, those studies primarily focused on overall mortality or clinical deterioration as endpoints, and did not specifically examine the relationship between RDW and PAH severity grading within the PAH‐CHD subgroup. In contrast, the present study specifically investigated PAH‐CHD as a distinct etiological subgroup and stratified patients by PAH severity (mild, moderate, and severe). A key novel finding is that RDW elevation was confined to patients with severe PAH‐CHD, with no significant increase observed in mild‐to‐moderate PAH‐CHD—a pattern not previously described in the literature. This suggests that RDW may reflect a threshold effect associated with advanced disease burden rather than a linear marker of PAH progression. Additionally, while Baggen et al. reported the prognostic value of RDW in adult CHD patients broadly, their cohort was not restricted to PAH‐CHD, and the specific contribution of PH severity to RDW elevation was not examined. The present study therefore adds specificity to the existing literature by demonstrating that within the PAH‐CHD population, RDW is most informative as an indicator of severe disease.

In conclusion, elevated RDW may be a phenomenon related to multiple functional abnormalities in the body of PAH‐CHD patients. This indicator can reflect the overall functional state of the body to a certain extent, but its specific mechanism still needs further research.

Study Limitation

This study is a single‐center retrospective study with a relatively small total sample size, so there may be biases in data analysis. Indicators such as iron metabolism, folic acid, vitamin B12, and inflammatory factors are missing, and further exploration into the causes and mechanisms of elevated RDW in PAH‐CHD patients is lacking. In addition, the absence of formal exclusion criteria for conditions known to elevate RDW—such as iron deficiency anemia, active inflammatory states, and significant renal insufficiency—limits the ability to attribute RDW elevation specifically to PAH‐CHD pathophysiology. Furthermore, this study did not collect clinical endpoint data such as mortality or hospitalization rates; therefore, the direct prognostic value of RDW cannot be established from the current data, and prospective studies with long‐term follow‐up are needed to validate its prognostic utility. Additionally, multivariate logistic regression analysis and comparative ROC analysis with established biomarkers (e.g., NT‐proBNP, 6MWD) were not performed, precluding conclusions about the independent predictive value of RDW or its superiority over existing indicators; future studies should address these analytical gaps. In particular, the mild PAH‐CHD subgroup (n = 19) and the normal control group (n = 20) were modest in size, and a post‐hoc power analysis indicated that the comparison between these two groups had only 9.8% statistical power to detect the observed effect (Cohen's d = 0.21); approximately 358 participants per group would be required to achieve 80% power at this effect size. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant RDW difference between the mild PAH‐CHD subgroup and the control or nPAH‐CHD groups should not be interpreted as evidence of true equivalence, and a small but real RDW elevation in mild PAH‐CHD cannot be ruled out (Type II error). Adequately powered, multicentre prospective studies are warranted to clarify whether RDW changes occur in early‐stage PAH‐CHD.

Conclusion

RDW is significantly elevated in patients with severe PAH‐CHD, and when RDW is 13.25%, it has high predictive value for severe PAH‐CHD. Therefore, RDW can serve as a practical, economical, and easily accessible indicator for evaluating disease severity and risk stratification in PAH‐CHD patients, which may carry prognostic implications warranting further investigation in prospective studies with clinical endpoints.
