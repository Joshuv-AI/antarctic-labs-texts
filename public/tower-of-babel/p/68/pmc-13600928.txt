Effect of muscle fatigue on cycling asymmetry during a constant-power test
Authors: Shahram Rasoulian, Seyed Hamidreza Heidary, Reza Ahmadi, Samira Fazeli Veisari, Walter Herzog, Amin Komeili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsymmetry in cycling may reduce mechanical efficiency and increase the risk of overuse injuries, particularly as fatigue develops. While asymmetry is often assessed using global power output, analyzing directional force components may reveal biomechanical patterns more relevant to performance and injury. In this study, we investigated the effect of progressive fatigue on bilateral asymmetry in power and pedal force components during a submaximal constant-power cycling test. Twenty healthy recreational participants performed prolonged cycling at 70% of their individual peak power until exhaustion while three-dimensional pedal forces were recorded using an instrumented ergometer. Asymmetry was quantified using the Normalized Symmetry Index (NSI) for power and for tangential, radial, and mediolateral force components, and evaluated at five time points representing progressive fatigue. Fatigue did not significantly alter mean relative interlimb asymmetry in power output or pedal force components as quantified by NSI, nor did it significantly change mean absolute interlimb force or power differences. However, cycle-to-cycle variability of the asymmetry measures and absolute interlimb differences changed across time, suggesting that fatigue influenced variability in limb coordination rather than producing a systematic shift in mean left–right force sharing. Asymmetry also differed significantly between force directions, with mediolateral forces showing the largest NSI range (−10% to 8%) compared to radial (−3% to 6%) and tangential (−7% to 3%) forces. These findings suggest that, in healthy individuals performing seated constant-power cycling, fatigue-related changes may be expressed more strongly through variability and direction-specific force behavior than through systematic changes in mean relative or absolute interlimb asymmetry.Keywords: Cycling, Asymmetry, Pedal force, Power, FatigueSubject terms: Engineering, Health care, Physiology

Introduction

Fatigue is broadly defined as a sensation of tiredness accompanied by a decline in muscular performance and function, often acting as a safety mechanism to prevent injury or physiological overload during exercise1–4. From a performance perspective, human fatigue has been defined as performance decline due to the inability to continue a motor task at the required intensity5. In cycling, fatigue manifests as reduced power output, decreased endurance, and diminished efficiency, ultimately limiting performance6–8. Muscle fatigue in cycling is a complex phenomenon influenced by a myriad of interconnected factors spanning physiological, biomechanical, and environmental domains. For instance, a cyclist may experience fatigue due to glycogen depletion during prolonged cycling, resulting in reduced energy availability for muscle contractions9. Similarly, elevated core body temperature in hot conditions can impair central nervous system activity, leading to reduced neural drive and compromised performance10. Mechanical factors, such as cycling position and ergonomics, significantly contribute to fatigue. For example, an improper saddle height or handlebar position can lead to excessive strain on muscles and joints, while poor pedaling technique may cause inefficient power transfer, thereby increasing muscular effort demands over time11–13. Fatigue can be measured using several indices. In high-intensity intermittent exercise, a common fatigue index is the percentage decline in power output across repeated sprints14. In resistance exercise, fatigue resistance is often operationalized as the task duration or maximum number of repetitions performed at a given load until volitional exhaustion15. While a comprehensive analysis of fatigue development lies beyond the scope of this study, fatigue was operationalized as time-to-exhaustion during steady submaximal-intensity cycling16,17 and its association with interlimb asymmetry was examined.

Cycling asymmetry, defined as unequal contributions of the lower limbs throughout the pedal cycle, may reflect interlimb differences in muscle activation patterns, motor recruitment, joint loading, or force transmission between the lower limbs18,19. From a biomechanical and clinical perspective, cycling asymmetry is relevant because cycling is a highly repetitive task; at a cadence of 80–90 rpm, a cyclist performs approximately 4800–5400 pedal cycles per hour. Therefore, even relatively small interlimb differences in loading or force application may become meaningful over prolonged exercise exposure. Previous work has shown that non-traumatic cycling injuries commonly involve regions such as the knee and lower back, and overuse injuries are frequently reported in professional cyclists20,21. In addition, pedal-force asymmetry has been linked to cycling performance, with previous studies suggesting that greater effective-force asymmetry may, in some contexts, be associated with better time-trial performance rather than simply representing impaired function22. Accordingly, cycling asymmetry should not be interpreted only as a deficit but as a potentially task-dependent neuromuscular strategy. Therefore, understanding how asymmetry in lower-limb function evolves with the onset and progression of fatigue is important for interpreting bilateral coordination, evaluating mechanical loading, and identifying fatigue-related changes in cycling technique.

In addition to metabolic, cardiovascular, and mechanical contributors to fatigue, recent cycling studies have shown that exercise-induced fatigue alters muscle-tendon unit properties. For example, repeated sprint cycling was reported to alter quadriceps muscle-tendon unit characteristics, including changes in muscle thickness, pennation angle, fascicle length, and stiffness, with more pronounced alterations after repeated sprint cycling at optimal cadence compared with a lower cadence23. Fatigue-induced alterations have also been reported in the patellar tendon of elite sprint track cyclists, including changes in tendon stiffness, thickness, cross-sectional area, and echogenicity following sprint cycling efforts24. Such changes in muscle-tendon behavior may influence force transmission, joint loading, and neuromuscular coordination, thereby providing a plausible mechanism through which fatigue could affect interlimb asymmetry during cycling. However, whether these fatigue-related muscle-tendon alterations are reflected in measurable changes in bilateral pedal force asymmetry during prolonged constant-power cycling remains unclear.

Exercise-induced fatigue has been suggested as a contributor to asymmetry development25, yet findings on lower-limb asymmetry during fatiguing cycling tasks remain inconclusive. For example, Stefanov et al.26 examined asymmetry across cycling bouts at three intensities (35%, 55%, and 85% of heart rate reserve) performed consecutively, but it is unclear whether the observed changes reflected exercise intensity per se or the cumulative fatigue from repeated trials. Moreover, their pedal force analysis was limited to five data points per cycle, potentially overlooking important fluctuations in asymmetry throughout the pedal stroke. Other investigations into the intensity-asymmetry relationship have been similarly inconsistent: some reported decreased asymmetry at higher intensities26–28, whereas others found no significant effects29–31. This inconsistency in the relationship between fatigue and asymmetry extends beyond cycling, appearing in activities such as running. For example, one study reported no change in asymmetry between rested and fatigued states32, whereas another found that fatigue increased asymmetry33,34. Taken together, these inconsistencies highlight the importance of protocol design (e.g., task failure vs. fixed intensity) and measurement resolution when assessing asymmetry. This relationship has not been thoroughly examined in cycling. Identifying and addressing lower-limb asymmetry as a result of fatigue development, especially at metabolically relevant intensities, enables athletes to improve efficiency, delay fatigue onset, and enhance performance while minimizing injury risk35,36.

Past research has relied on various methods to measure asymmetries, such as simple difference calculations of biomechanical parameters, symmetry indices (SI), and ratios of dominant to non-dominant leg performance37–39. However, these conventional indices can be sensitive to measurement conditions and may yield inflated or misleading values, particularly when analyzing discrete events or variables near zero40,41. To address these limitations, modified approaches like the Normalized Symmetry Index (NSI)42 and waveform analysis methods43,44, including cross-correlation coefficients (CCC)45, have been introduced. These methods allow for a more robust, continuous, and time-normalized assessment of asymmetry throughout the entire pedaling cycle, capturing subtle differences in force application patterns rather than relying solely on discrete peak values42–45.

Previous studies investigating the relationship between fatigue, exercise intensity, and interlimb asymmetry in cycling often reported inconsistent findings. In some studies, it has been suggested that asymmetry changes with increasing workload or fatigue, whereas other studies reported stable bilateral force or power distribution across exercise conditions. These discrepancies may be partly explained by differences in experimental design, including protocols that varied exercise intensity across trials, making it difficult to separate fatigue-related effects from workload- or intensity-dependent effects. In addition, many previous analyses relied on global power output, discrete summary values, or limited sampling points within the pedal cycle, which may not adequately capture the continuous and direction-specific nature of force application during cycling.

These limitations highlight gaps in current knowledge. First, it remains unclear whether progressive fatigue alters relative interlimb asymmetry during constant-power cycling. Second, the extent to which fatigue affects asymmetry differently across tangential, radial, and mediolateral pedal force components remains insufficiently understood. Third, although muscle-tendon alterations and neuromuscular adaptations may occur during fatiguing cycling, it is not clear whether these changes are reflected in cycle-resolved asymmetry metrics. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of asymmetry that accounts for both temporal progression and three-dimensional force direction is needed to better understand bilateral coordination during fatiguing cycling.

The present study addresses these gaps by combining a constant-power cycling protocol performed to task failure with continuous three-dimensional pedal force measurements and a cycle-based Normalized Symmetry Index. This approach allowed us to evaluate whether fatigue-related changes in relative asymmetry occur over normalized exercise time and whether asymmetry depends on the biomechanical direction of pedal force application.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine whether progressive fatigue during constant-power cycling alters relative interlimb asymmetry in mechanical output, and whether this response depends on the direction of pedal force application. Specifically, we assessed asymmetry in power output and in tangential, radial, and mediolateral pedal force components using a cycle-based Normalized Symmetry Index across five normalized time points during a constant-power test performed to task failure. Based on previous evidence suggesting that fatigue can alter neuromuscular coordination, muscle activation, and lower-limb force production patterns19,25,46, we hypothesized that progressive fatigue would alter relative interlimb asymmetry in power output and pedal force components. We further hypothesized that asymmetry would differ between force components, with mediolateral forces showing greater asymmetry than tangential and radial forces because mediolateral force application may be more closely related to lateral stabilization, postural control, and non-propulsive force regulation during cycling44,47,48.

Materials and methods

Experimental setup

A custom-configured stationary ergometer (Echelon Connect EX-5, Echelon Fitness Multimedia, LLC, Chattanooga, TN, USA) was instrumented with saddle, handlebars, and pedals (ICS-MB, Mountain-BMX, Shimano SPD, Sakai, Japan), featuring integrated 3D force sensors (SENSIX, Poitiers, France)49. These sensors recorded handlebar, saddle and pedal forces at a sampling rate of 250 Hz. Crank angle was measured with a rotary encoder (RLS-MR076G060A120N00) and used for transforming pedal forces into the crank reference frame. For the pedal-crank system, radial force was defined as the component acting along the crank arm, tangential force as the component perpendicular to the crank arm in the plane of rotation, and mediolateral force as the component perpendicular to the crank plane. For the saddle and handlebar, force components were resolved into anterior-posterior, tangential, and mediolateral directions within the sagittal and frontal planes (Fig. 1a). Standard calibration procedures, as outlined by the manufacturer, were followed to ensure accurate force measurements. In addition, the rate of perceived exertion (RPE; Borg 6–20 scale50) was obtained verbally at the predefined time points, and heart rate (HR) and oxygen uptake (VO2) were measured using a COSMED metabolic system (COSMED S.R.L., Rome, Italy). Surface electromyography (EMG) was recorded from the rectus femoris (RF) using a wireless Trigno EMG system (Delsys Inc., Natick, MA, USA) sampled at 2.2 kHz and acquired with Trigno Discover software (v1.7.0)51.

Participants and test protocol

Twenty healthy recreational cyclists whose demographics and average test characteristics are summarized in Table 1 volunteered to participate in this study. All participants were free of locomotor or cardiopulmonary pathologies that could interfere with their ability to complete the test or influence the results. Leg dominance was determined using a self-report “kick-a-ball” question, identifying 18 right-dominant and 2 left-dominant individuals52. The study was approved by the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (REB #23-1803; approved July 16, 2024), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to testing. Participants were fitted to a stationary ergometer with the saddle height standardized to 109% of their inseam length (measured from the bottom bracket to the saddle top)53. The handlebar position was adjusted individually for comfort and proper posture. Participants were provided with cycling shoes in various sizes to ensure a comfortable and secure fit. Cleats were positioned to align with the fat pad of the first metatarsal head on each foot. Positioning was further verified using customized cardboard insoles to ensure identical orientation and alignment across both shoes, minimizing variability in pedal interface and promoting consistency in force application measurements.

Data preprocessing

Linear bias correction was applied to the idle pedal force data before and after each test session to measure potential drift in the force sensor. This drift was eliminated by subtracting the calculated drift from the raw data. Pedal force vectors were transformed from the local pedal frame to the crank reference frame using the recorded crank angle (encoder) data. This transformation allowed for the computation of crank torque and, consequently, instantaneous power output.

EMG signals from the RF muscle were high-pass filtered at 30 Hz, detrended, full-wave rectified, and low-pass filtered at 10 Hz (zero-lag Butterworth filters) to obtain linear envelopes54. Signals were downsampled to 250 Hz to synchronize with the force data. To facilitate comparisons across participants, EMG amplitudes were normalized to a task-based peak derived from the pre-test at maximal power output, defined as the mean of the top 10% of EMG amplitudes for each muscle19,55.

In the data cleaning stage, the 3-minute warm-up period was excluded from all sessions. Additionally, the first 10 s of each test were removed to ensure that participants had reached the set power output. The end of the test period was defined as the final time point preceding task failure, as described in Participants and Test Protocol Section.

Each session’s data were segmented into individual pedaling cycles using a custom labeling function, where the start of each cycle was defined as the top dead center (TDC) of the left pedal. Due to variability in test duration among participants, time was normalized with respect to cycling progress, calculated as the ratio of elapsed time to total session duration (% of total time). To facilitate comparison across individuals and time, five discrete time points were defined at 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of cycling progress, labeled as Initial, Early, Midpoint, Late, and Final phases, respectively (see Table 2). For each of these points, a 4% window of the session duration was extracted, and the complete pedaling cycles within this window were used to calculate the minimum, maximum, and mean values at each time point.

An NSI value of 0% indicates perfect symmetry. Positive NSI values reflect greater force or power output from the dominant limb, while negative values indicate greater contribution from the non-dominant limb40. For force components with direction-dependent signs (particularly radial and mediolateral), all forces were expressed in a crank-referenced coordinate system attached to the left pedal. In this convention, when the right pedal reaches the same crank position as the left, the corresponding force components for the right limb have the opposite sign. This convention, independent of limb dominance, ensured consistent directional interpretation across limbs.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted to examine the effects of fatigue development (time) on asymmetry measures and physiological and neuromuscular responses, as well as to compare asymmetry across force directions. All analyses were performed using Python (v3.13.1), and statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

For outcomes assessed across time only, including power asymmetry and physiological and neuromuscular responses (RPE, HR, VO2, and RF EMG), normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When the normality assumption was satisfied, a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used to examine changes across the five time points (Initial, Early, Midpoint, Late, Final)61–63. When normality was violated, the non-parametric Friedman test was applied64.

To assess directional differences in asymmetry, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted with time (Initial, Early, Midpoint, Late, Final) and force component (tangential, radial, mediolateral) as within-subject factors. This analysis evaluated the main effects of time and force component, as well as their interaction65. When normality assumptions were not met, separate Friedman tests were conducted to examine changes across time for each force component and differences across force components at each time point64.

When a significant omnibus effect was detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni-adjusted tests for parametric analyses or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with adjusted p-values for non-parametric analyses61,63.

Absolute interlimb differences and cycle-to-cycle CV values were analyzed across the five time points using the same normality-dependent statistical framework described above.

Positive power values began slightly before and extended beyond the push-down (power) phase, spanning approximately 340° to 200° crank angles (Fig. 2), reflecting a sustained power phase44. Negative power values, shown in gray for clarity, occurred during the pull-up (recovery) phase (approximately 200° to 340°), when the leg’s weight opposed the motion. NSI values, plotted in the central subplot, generally peaked during the power phase. Although a decrease in the NSI was observed over time points, suggesting a possible reduction in asymmetry with fatigue development, this trend was not statistically significant. A one-way repeated-measures ANOVA on mean NSIpower values at each time point showed no significant effect of fatigue on NSI (p = 0.11) with a medium effect size (ηp² = 0.10). Similarly, a repeated-measures ANOVA on minimum NSIpower values showed no significant differences (p = 0.65) with a small effect size (ηp² = 0.03). Consistent with these results, a Friedman test on maximum NSIpower values showed no significant differences (p = 0.44) with a very small effect size (Kendall’s W = 0.05). Thus, mean relative asymmetry in power was not significantly altered across time points, indicating that mean bilateral power sharing remained statistically stable throughout the test.

The absence of a significant time effect for NSI does not necessarily indicate that each limb maintained a consistent force profile. Rather, mean mediolateral forces at the saddle, handlebar, and pedal increased progressively over the course of cycling (Fig. 4). Inter-individual variability also increased significantly as fatigue developed, as reflected by greater standard deviations at the five time points. Significant omnibus effects of time were observed for saddle variability (p < 0.01; ηp² = 0.28), handlebar variability (p < 0.01; ηp² = 0.39), and pedal variability (p < 0.01; ηp² = 0.35). Post hoc comparisons indicated that variability at Midpoint, Late, and Final was significantly greater than at Initial and Early for the saddle (all padj ≤ 0.02), handlebar (all padj ≤ 0.01), and pedal (all padj ≤ 0.01), with no significant differences among the later time points. Together, these findings indicate a marked increase in mediolateral force magnitudes at lower- and upper-body segments and inter-individual variability during the latter stages of the task, followed by stabilization as fatigue progressed.

Cycle-to-cycle CV was calculated to assess within-subject variability of the raw mechanical variables and derived asymmetry indices. For power and force components, mechanical signals showed relatively low cycle-to-cycle variability, with time-point mean CVs ranging from 5.9% to 12.5%; when pooled across participants, time points, and power and force components, the full individual-participant CV range was 2.7% to 14.4%. In contrast, NSI showed higher variability, with time-point mean CVs ranging from 13.4% to 18.1%; when pooled across participants, time points, and NSI power and force components, the full individual-participant CV range was 4.3% to 23.8%. The CV of NSI changed significantly across time for power and all force components (all p < 0.01). Effect sizes were small for the Friedman-tested variables, including NSIpower (Kendall’s W = 0.23) and NSItan (Kendall’s W = 0.19), and large for the ANOVA-tested force components, including NSIrad (ηp² = 0.80) and NSImed (ηp² = 0.58). Therefore, fatigue influenced cycle-to-cycle variability of the asymmetry index, although mean NSI values did not show a significant time effect.

The complementary absolute difference analysis showed no significant effect of time on mean absolute interlimb differences for power and force components (all p > 0.05). Across time points, mean absolute interlimb differences ranged from 22.5 to 23.6 W for power and from 16.1 to 22.6 N for force components. The corresponding full individual-participant ranges were 13.1 to 32.5 W for power and 12.5 to 31.3 N for force components. However, the CV of absolute interlimb differences changed significantly across time for power and all force components (all p < 0.02). Effect sizes were large for absolute interlimb-difference power CV (ηp² = 0.19) and Frad CV (Kendall’s W = 0.65), and small for Fmed CV (Kendall’s W = 0.20) and Ftan CV (Kendall’s W = 0.14). When pooled across participants, time points, and power and force components, time-point mean CVs ranged from 8.2% to 16.6%, with a full individual-participant CV range of 4.2% to 20.5%. These findings indicate that fatigue did not produce a systematic change in mean absolute left–right force or power difference but did affect the cycle-to-cycle variability of that difference.

Physiological and neuromuscular responses

RPE increased progressively as fatigue developed across the five time points (Fig. 5a; p < 0.01; Kendall’s W = 0.98). Post hoc analysis indicated that RPE was significantly higher at each time point relative to Initial, including a significant increase from Late to Final (all padj ≤ 0.03), reflecting continued perceptual strain near task failure. Heart rate increased progressively as fatigue developed (Fig. 5b; p < 0.01; ηp² = 0.95), with post hoc comparisons showing significant elevations from Initial to all subsequent time points (all padj < 0.01). Changes between Late and Final were comparatively smaller, suggesting an approach toward a physiological ceiling. Oxygen uptake (VO2) increased as fatigue developed (Fig. 5c; p < 0.01; ηp² = 0.75). Post hoc analyses revealed significant increases from Initial to Early and Midpoint (all padj ≤ 0.02), with no further significant increases thereafter, indicating a plateau in aerobic demand during the latter stages of the task. RF EMG amplitude increased as fatigue developed in both the dominant (p < 0.01; ηp² = 0.38) and non-dominant limbs (p < 0.01; ηp² = 0.40; Fig. 5d). Post hoc comparisons demonstrated significantly greater EMG amplitude at Midpoint, Late, and Final compared with Initial and Early (all padj ≤ 0.02), with no significant differences among the later time points, indicating an increase in neuromuscular activation followed by stabilization.

(a) Rate of perceived exertion (RPE; Borg 6–20 scale). (b) Heart rate (HR). (c) Oxygen uptake (VO2). (d) Rectus femoris (RF) electromyography (EMG) amplitude, expressed as RMS, shown separately for the dominant (D) and non-dominant (ND) limbs.

Discussion

Overall findings

In this study, we investigated the influence of fatigue development on bilateral cycling asymmetry during a constant-power test, specifically examining power output and three-dimensional pedal force components. The primary finding was that fatigue did not significantly alter mean relative interlimb asymmetry in power output or pedal force components when asymmetry was quantified using NSI during a constant-power pedaling protocol performed at 70 rpm and at an intensity corresponding to 70% of each participant’s peak power until task failure. However, this finding should be interpreted specifically as the absence of a systematic shift in relative left–right force sharing, rather than as evidence that all aspects of force production or cycling mechanics remained unchanged. In contrast, asymmetry differed significantly between force directions, with mediolateral forces showing the largest NSI range compared with radial and tangential forces. In addition, mediolateral force magnitude and variability increased at the pedal, saddle, and handlebar as fatigue developed, suggesting that fatigue influenced stabilization strategies and inter-individual movement variability more strongly than mean relative interlimb asymmetry.

Fatigue development during the constant-power test

The development of fatigue in cycling can involve both peripheral and central mechanisms, and the relative contribution of each depends on exercise intensity and duration. High-intensity, short efforts tend to evoke greater peripheral fatigue, whereas low-intensity, long efforts are more associated with central fatigue. Hsu et al.66 defined low- and high-intensity cycling workloads in healthy recreational cyclists as approximately 70 W and 140 W, respectively, corresponding to 25% and 50% of maximal voluntary contraction. Similarly, Thomas et al.67 used a constant-power protocol in well-trained male cyclists at ~76% of each participant’s peak power from a step-incremental test, with an average workload of 305 ± 23 W. Our protocol followed a similar principle: participants cycled at 70% of their individual peak power as determined in a step-incremental test, corresponding to an average workload of 161.0 ± 35.8 W and an average test duration of 15.7 ± 9.3 min (Table 1), which is within the range reported in the literature for constant-load tasks of similar intensity to volitional exhaustion. Physiological responses during the constant-power test further support the development of fatigue. RPE reflected progressively increasing perceived effort, reaching near-maximal levels by the end of the task (Fig. 5a). Concordant increases in heart rate and oxygen uptake (Fig. 5b, c) indicate rising global metabolic and cardiovascular demands, with heart rate continuing to increase throughout the test and oxygen uptake plateauing during the latter stages. Heart rate values at the final stage approached age-predicted maximal levels (calculated as 220 minus age in years)66,68,69, which are known to slightly overestimate true maximal heart rate during cycling but still provide a reasonable upper bound for exertional intensity. In parallel, rectus femoris EMG amplitude increased in both limbs (Fig. 5d), indicating greater neuromuscular activation from the mid to late stages of the task to maintain the constant external workload. These responses are consistent with the development of fatigue-related reductions in force-generating capacity of the working musculature. In addition, the workload selection (70% of individual peak power), test duration (~16 min), and consistent upward trends in RPE, VO2, heart rate, and RF EMG indicate that participants developed substantial fatigue during the constant-power task. Therefore, the absence of a significant time effect on mean NSI should not be interpreted as an absence of fatigue but rather as evidence that mean relative interlimb asymmetry remained stable despite clear physiological and neuromuscular signs of fatigue development. What remains less clear from the present data is the relative contribution of peripheral versus central mechanisms to this fatigue development. Although the overall intensity and EMG behavior suggest increasing local muscular demand, the protocol duration was long enough that central factors could also have contributed to task failure. Distinguishing between these mechanisms would require measures such as potentiated twitch force70,71 or markers of neuromuscular excitability72–74, which were beyond the scope of this study.

Power asymmetry

Mean relative power asymmetry did not change significantly across the five fatigue-related time points. Although a small visual reduction in power NSI was observed toward the end of the test, statistical analysis did not indicate a significant time effect. This finding suggests that participants maintained relatively stable mean left–right power sharing during seated constant-power cycling. Previous cycling studies reported limited and stable asymmetry in healthy individuals during controlled ergometer cycling, which is consistent with the present results44,56. In addition, it has been reported previously that there is no significant change in NSI for lower-limb EMG responses during fatiguing cycling, which aligns with the stable NSI pattern observed for power output in the present study19. A likely explanation is that maintaining a fixed external power output required continuous bilateral regulation of mechanical output, while the seated posture and cleated pedals constrained large compensatory shifts between limbs.

The overall NSI range of approximately ±10% across power and force components is consistent with values reported in previous cycling studies44,56, reinforcing the interpretation that cycling is predominantly symmetric when external workload, cadence, posture, and pedal interface are controlled. The use of cleated pedals and the restriction to seated cycling likely helped constrain lower-limb mechanics and may have limited the emergence of larger interlimb differences during fatigue development.

The present findings should also be interpreted within the broader fatigue-asymmetry literature, which suggests that fatigue-related changes in interlimb asymmetry are task-dependent and may differ across populations, movement tasks, and measured outcomes. Previous studies have reported fatigue-related changes in between-limb asymmetry during single-leg jump tests, functional performance tasks, dynamic balance assessments, and judo-specific lower-limb tasks75–77. However, fatigue does not always affect all biomechanical outcomes similarly. For example, running after cycling has been shown to induce interlimb differences in muscle activation without corresponding changes in kinetic or kinematic variables78. This task- and variable-specific behavior is consistent with the present results: fatigue did not produce a systematic change in mean relative interlimb asymmetry in power or pedal force components, but fatigue-related changes were evident in mediolateral force magnitude, inter-individual variability, and cycle-to-cycle variability.

Direction-specific pedal force asymmetry

Although mean NSI did not change significantly over time, asymmetry differed significantly across force directions. Mediolateral force showed the largest NSI range (−10% to 8%), whereas tangential (−7% to 3%) and radial (−3% to 6%) force components showed smaller relative asymmetry ranges. This indicates that the biomechanical direction of force application had a stronger influence on asymmetry than fatigue progression itself. The smaller asymmetry in tangential force may reflect the direct role of this component in crank propulsion, which likely requires tighter bilateral regulation to maintain the prescribed external power output. Radial force, although not directly propulsive, is mechanically constrained by the crank-pedal system and showed a relatively smaller asymmetry range. In contrast, mediolateral force is less directly constrained by the requirement to maintain power and may reflect lateral stabilization, trunk control, hip-knee alignment, or individual pedaling strategies. Therefore, the larger mediolateral NSI suggests that non-propulsive force directions may be more sensitive to individual coordination strategies than the primary propulsive component.

Mediolateral force magnitude, stabilization, and variability

The increased mediolateral force magnitude and variability observed at the pedal, saddle, and handlebar provide additional evidence that fatigue affected cycling mechanics even though mean relative interlimb asymmetry remained statistically unchanged. Greater mediolateral forces during the later stages of cycling may reflect increased side-to-side loading at the body-bike interfaces, increased reliance on upper-body stabilization, or less consistent regulation of mediolateral force application as fatigue developed. Previous studies have shown that cycling fatigue can alter lower-limb and trunk motion and reduce pedaling efficiency46,47. Therefore, the mediolateral force response observed in the present study may represent a fatigue-related stabilization or force-regulation strategy within the constraints of stationary cycling, rather than a systematic change in left–right force sharing. This directional sensitivity emphasizes the value of decomposing pedal forces into individual components when assessing cycling performance, fatigue responses, or movement imbalances48.

Cycle-to-cycle variability and absolute interlimb differences

The cycle-to-cycle variability and absolute interlimb difference analyses provide a more nuanced interpretation of the NSI findings by distinguishing changes in mean asymmetry from changes in within-subject variability across cycles. The raw power and force signals showed relatively low cycle-to-cycle variability, supporting the consistency of the mechanical recordings within the analyzed windows. In contrast, NSI showed higher CV values than the raw mechanical variables, which is expected because it is calculated from the relative difference between dominant and non-dominant limb signals; small cycle-to-cycle fluctuations in either limb can therefore be amplified in the resulting asymmetry value. Importantly, although NSI CV changed across time, mean NSI did not change significantly.

Similarly, the complementary absolute interlimb difference analysis showed that mean absolute left–right differences did not change significantly across time, indicating that the NSI analysis did not mask a systematic fatigue-related increase in absolute force or power difference. However, the CV of absolute interlimb differences changed significantly, indicating that fatigue influenced the cycle-to-cycle variability of left–right differences without changing their mean magnitude. This distinction is important because the lack of a significant fatigue effect on mean NSI does not imply that fatigue had no effect on the biomechanics of cycling. Rather, the increased mediolateral force magnitude and inter-individual variability observed at the pedal, saddle, and handlebar suggest that participants adopted more variable mediolateral force-regulation strategies as fatigue developed within the constraints of stationary cycling. These mediolateral findings should also be interpreted with consideration that mediolateral forces were smaller in magnitude than the primary in-plane force components, so relative changes may appear more pronounced. Therefore, the present results indicate that seated constant-power cycling preserved mean relative interlimb asymmetry and mean absolute left–right force and power differences, while fatigue-related changes were more evident in cycle-to-cycle variability and in mediolateral force regulation.

Radial force interpretation

The radial force does not contribute directly to propulsion and has been considered by some to be mechanically inefficient79,80, but it has also been shown to be biomechanically necessary for optimal cycling performance81,82. Its magnitude was comparable to the tangential force during the push-down phase, suggesting that radial force should not be interpreted only as wasted force. Possibly, strategies aimed at optimizing radial versus tangential pedal forces may help enhance overall cycling efficiency. Although simulations showed that reducing radial force is proportional to reducing tangential force81, higher efficiency may be obtained at the cost of lowering maximum power output.

Limitations and future directions

A limitation of this work is that muscle fatigue was not directly quantified. We studied asymmetry in interlimb mechanical performance as a consequence of skeletal muscle fatigue, but did not determine whether fatigue was associated with impaired actin-myosin interaction83–86 or reduced muscle fiber excitability87–89. At volitional exhaustion, user-reported RPE, along with the physiological responses shown in Fig. 5, indicated that fatigue was sufficient to influence cycling mechanics and variability, although it did not produce a systematic change in mean relative interlimb asymmetry or mean absolute left–right force and power differences. Although RPE, EMG, and VO2 profiles were consistent with high-intensity cycling fatigue protocols, objective confirmation would require biomarkers such as metabolic by-products (e.g., blood lactate), changes in muscle excitability (e.g., M-wave properties), or indices of reactive oxygen species. Future studies could examine whether higher fatigue levels, variable cadences, or unconstrained foot positioning lead to greater asymmetry, and whether mediolateral variability serves as an early indicator of technique changes or injury risk. In addition, exploring different populations such as older adults or injured athletes, as well as real-world cycling scenarios including outdoor riding, sprinting, or uphill conditions, would broaden the applicability of these findings. Finally, integrating wearable sensors with AI-based systems for real-time monitoring of asymmetry could enhance the translational impact of this research and support early detection of maladaptive technique.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the impact of fatigue on cycling asymmetry using power and three-dimensional force-component data collected throughout a constant-power test. Although visual trends suggested minor fluctuations in asymmetry over time, no significant effects of fatigue on mean relative power or force asymmetry were observed across the five time points when asymmetry was quantified using NSI. Similarly, no significant fatigue-related changes were detected within individual force components, including tangential, radial, and mediolateral forces, indicating that mean bilateral force-sharing patterns were largely preserved under conditions leading to fatigue and task failure. Significant differences in asymmetry were observed between force directions, underscoring the importance of analyzing directional force components rather than relying solely on global symmetry metrics.

Together, these findings suggest that, during seated constant-power cycling with cleated pedals, fatigue did not produce a systematic disruption of mean relative interlimb asymmetry in healthy recreational cyclists. The complementary absolute interlimb difference analysis further indicated that fatigue did not significantly alter mean absolute left–right force or power differences, supporting the NSI-based finding that mean relative interlimb asymmetry remained statistically unchanged. However, the cycle-to-cycle CV analyses indicated that fatigue influenced the variability of the asymmetry index and the variability of absolute interlimb differences. Therefore, fatigue-related changes in cycling mechanics may be expressed more strongly through mediolateral force behavior and cycle-to-cycle variability than through systematic changes in mean relative or absolute interlimb asymmetry. These results may not generalize to out-of-the-saddle cycling or to conditions involving variable cadence and power, where asymmetry patterns may be less stable. Direction-specific asymmetry measures therefore remain a valuable tool for performance optimization, injury risk assessment, and biomechanical evaluation.
