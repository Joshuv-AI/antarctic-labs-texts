Stoichiometry‐Controlled Cobalt Sulfide‐Based Hole Transport Layers for Perovskite Solar Cells
Authors: Bonkee Koo, Wooyeon Kim, Young Kim, Xiangyu Cao, Jaewon Jang, Min Jae Ko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMost hole transport layers (HTLs) used in perovskite solar cells (PSCs) require extrinsic doping to enhance conductivity and modify energy levels. However, such doping often induces structural disorder, compositional inhomogeneity, and band edge distortion owing to dopant segregation, reducing hole mobility and interfacial charge trapping. Herein, CoxSy with controlled stoichiometry is reported as a dopant‐free inorganic HTL. Three phase‐pure compositions, namely, CoS, Co4S3, and Co9S8, are synthesized using a hot‐injection method by controlling the injection temperature of a sulfur‐oleylamine precursor. Each stoichiometrically defined CoxSy HTL possessed distinct valence band positions, enabling systematic control of band alignment with that of the perovskite layer. PSCs containing these HTLs exhibited power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of up to 18.65% and open‐circuit voltages of up to 1.09 V. To further enhance hole transport and charge collection efficiency, a bilayer HTL composed of CoxSy and 2,2′,7,7′‐tetrakis[N,N‐di(4‐methoxyphenyl)amino]‐9,9′‐spirobifluorene (spiro‐OMeTAD) is introduced. The PSC containing a Co4S3/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer HTL exhibited a PCE of 24.41%. Moreover, the thermal and operational stabilities of the PSCs containing the CoxSy HTLs are better than those of the PSCs employing conventional spiro‐OMeTAD‐only HTLs. This strategy can expand the utility of previously underutilized nonstoichiometric materials as functional HTLs in high‐photovoltaic‐performance PSCs.

Introduction

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have achieved power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of up to 27%, positioning them as leading candidates for next‐generation photovoltaic technologies.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] This progress can be attributed to concurrent advancements in perovskite absorber materials and the development of charge transporting materials (CTMs), which facilitate efficient charge extraction.[ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] Among CTMs, hole transporting materials (HTMs) are crucial for enhancing device efficiency and stability.[ 8 , 9 ] An ideal HTM requires appropriate energy‐level alignment with the perovskite layer, high hole mobility, and excellent chemical stability. Accordingly, extensive research has been conducted on developing diverse organic and inorganic HTMs tailored for application in high‐performance PSCs.

Most HTMs, whether organic or inorganic, typically rely on extrinsic doping to achieve the required conductivity and energy‐level alignment with the perovskite layer. Organic HTMs such as 2,2′,7,7′‐tetrakis[N,N‐di(4‐methoxyphenyl)amino]‐9,9′‐spirobifluorene (spiro‐OMeTAD), poly[bis(4‐phenyl)(2,4,6‐trimethylphenyl)amine] (PTAA), and poly(3‐hexylthiophene) (P3HT) are widely adopted owing to their processability and favorable valence band maximum (VBM) levels.[ 10 , 11 ] However, the inherently low conductivities of these HTMs necessitate the use of dopants such as Li bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (Li‐TFSI) and 4‐tert‐butylpyridine (tBP) to enhance hole transport.[ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ] Doping increases conductivity via oxidation but induces pinhole formation and moisture penetration during deposition, thereby degrading the stability of PSCs and the corresponding PCEs.[ 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 ] Inorganic HTMs such as NiOx, CuSCN, and CuI offer improved chemical compatibility with perovskites and interfacial stability.[ 23 , 24 , 25 ] Inorganic HTMs exhibit greater resistance to humidity, thermal stress, and ultraviolet (UV) exposure than their organic counterparts. Low material cost, availability of inexpensive precursors, and facile synthesis routes render inorganic HTMs suitable candidates for mass production. However, a large VBM offset between the HTM and the perovskite layer leads to poor energy‐level alignment, thereby reducing the open‐circuit voltage (VOC). Extrinsic doping is often required to improve hole density and adjust band alignment.[ 26 , 27 ] Nevertheless, dopants tend to segregate at grain boundaries and interfaces, yielding compositional inhomogeneity and localized band edge distortion, thereby lowering hole mobility.[ 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 ] Furthermore, mobile dopant species can diffuse into the perovskite layer and react with halide species and vacancies, inducing interfacial charge trapping and accelerating performance degradation under continuous operation.[ 32 , 33 ]

Recently, Co‐based materials such as CoOx, owing to their high conductivities and favorable energy levels for charge carrier extraction, have been explored for application as hole‐transporting layers (HTLs) in PSCs.[ 34 , 35 , 36 ] CoOx‐based PSCs have demonstrated superior stability with respect to conventional NiOx‐based devices under harsh conditions, including thermal and UV stress. However, Co oxides typically exhibit nonstoichiometric compositions owing to the difficulty in precisely controlling their oxygen contents, causing their optical and electronic properties to vary. By contrast, relatively improved stoichiometric control is possible with transition metal chalcogenides. For instance, FeS2 and MoS2 have been investigated as potential HTLs for PSCs because the sulfur‐based compositions enable more precise stoichiometric control than metal oxides provide.[ 37 , 38 ] Nevertheless, these materials have yielded limited device performance, possibly attributable to unfavorable energy‐level alignment with the perovskite absorber, thereby reducing VOC. CoxSy presents a promising alternative owing to its low cost, high conductivity, and favorable band alignment with perovskite absorbers. However, their intrinsic non‐stoichiometry continues to present challenges in terms of phase control and electronic structure uniformity.[ 37 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 ] Notably, the VBMs of CoxSy are close to that of a perovskite absorber, potentially reducing VOC losses. To harness these advantages, we devised a stoichiometrically controlled CoxSy system by adjusting the Co‐to‐S ratio, enabling tailored VBM alignment without requiring extrinsic doping. This approach enables rational energy‐level engineering for obtaining high‐VOC PSCs.[ 47 , 48 ]

Herein, using a hot‐injection method, we synthesized three stoichiometrically controlled CoxSy phases, namely, CoS, Co4S3, and Co9S8. Each phase was selectively obtained by adjusting the injection temperature of a sulfur‐oleylamine (S‐OLA) precursor, which modulated the Co‐to‐S ratio without requiring post‐treatment. The VBM levels of CoS, Co4S3, and Co9S8 were measured via ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) to be −5.25, −5.37, and −5.31 eV, respectively. The VBM positions of the stoichiometrically tuned CoxSy were aligned closer to that of the perovskite layer, increasing the VOC. PSCs containing CoS, Co4S3, and Co9S8 HTLs exhibited VOC values of 0.99, 1.09, and 1.04 V and PCEs of 16.02%, 18.65%, and 17.02%, respectively. Additionally, we sequentially deposited CoxSy and spiro‐OMeTAD. The PSC containing spiro‐OMeTAD alone, fabricated using the same process, exhibited a PCE of 22.53% (VOC = 1.145 V). By contrast, the PSCs containing bilayer HTLs of CoS/spiro‐OMeTAD, Co4S3/spiro‐OMeTAD, and Co9S/spiro‐OMeTAD achieved PCEs of 22.8% (1.150 V), 24.41% (1.191 V), and 23.88% (1.167 V), respectively. Furthermore, these PSCs exhibited improved long‐term stability against external stimuli such as moisture, heat, and continuous illumination. This enhanced performance was achieved by designing a bilayer HTL structure that integrates stoichiometrically controlled CoxSy, enabling both high PCE and stability.

Results and Discussion

Phase‐Controlled Synthesis of CoxSy

CoxSy materials often possess nonstoichiometric compositions and exhibit temperature‐dependent phase formation, yielding intermediate or mixed phases. In conventional methods, H2S gas is flowed over Co precursor films, and the resulting phase is determined by temperature and sulfur activity.[ 45 , 49 ] However, stoichiometric control in these gas‐phase routes typically depends on the regulation of gas flow using mass flow controllers.[ 50 , 51 ] Such gas‐phase syntheses are vulnerable to local fluctuations in sulfur partial pressure and offer limited control over sulfur incorporation kinetics.[ 52 ] These limitations often yield nonstoichiometric or mixed‐phase products.

To address these issues, we employed a hot‐injection method using an S‐OLA precursor. This solution‐phase route allows the precursor ratios to be directly controlled, and sulfur activity can be tuned by adjusting the injection temperature and precursor composition.[ 53 ] A prepared S‐OLA solution was injected into a hot Co precursor solution at controlled temperatures, enabling reproducible and phase‐pure synthesis of metal chalcogenides with tailored stoichiometry. Under these conditions, thermally activated sulfur species, such as H2S and thioamides, are formed in situ.[ 54 ] These intermediates act as transient sulfur donors and influence Co redox reactions during nucleation. By controlling the injection temperature, we regulated the reactivity of the sulfur precursor and guided phase formation. The synthesis procedure is depicted in Figure 1a.

To determine the crystallographic identities of the synthesized CoxSy compositions, X‐ray diffraction (XRD) was conducted on the samples prepared using different injection temperatures of the S–OLA precursor (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The XRD pattern obtained at 230 °C exhibited diffraction peaks corresponding exclusively to CoS, indicating phase‐pure formation. At 250 °C, diffraction peaks corresponding to CoS and Co4S3 were observed, suggesting the presence of overlapping crystallization windows of the two phases. At 270 °C, only peaks associated with Co4S3 were detected, indicating successful isolation of the Co4S3 phase. On further increasing the temperature to 285 °C, peaks corresponding to both Co4S3 and Co9S8 appeared. At 300 °C, only the peaks corresponding to Co9S8 were present, confirming the formation of phase‐pure Co9S8 under this condition. These results demonstrate that individual CoxSy phases can be selectively synthesized by tuning the injection temperature. Based on these results, we selected 230, 270, and 300 °C as representative injection temperatures for synthesizing phase‐pure CoS, Co4S3, and Co9S8, respectively. The XRD patterns shown in Figure 1b confirmed that the products synthesized at the selected temperatures were phase‐pure, with no discernible peaks corresponding to secondary or impurity phases. The obtained diffraction peaks matched well with the reference patterns from the International Centre for Diffraction Data (ICDD) database corresponding to CoS (ICCD card No.03‐065‐8977), Co4S3 (ICCD card No. 01‐086‐2273), and Co9S8 (ICCD card No. 00‐002‐1458). The crystallographic refinement parameters of each sample are listed in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

Each CoxSy phase was obtained via an independent reaction using a freshly prepared S‐OLA solution at a controlled temperature. The differences in phase identity, as determined from the assigned XRD patterns, cannot be attributed to thermal transformation within a single reaction system. Therefore, the crystallization of distinct phases at each synthesis temperature was possibly influenced by variations in sulfur reactivity, precursor decomposition rate, and nucleation kinetics. The formation of CoS at low temperatures and Co4S3 and Co9S8 at high temperatures aligns with previous reports on temperature‐dependent phase evolution in CoxSy systems.[ 52 , 55 ] Although high temperatures typically yield Co‐rich phases, Co9S8, formed at 300 °C, contained a higher sulfur ratio than Co4S3, formed at 270 °C. This deviation indicates that Co4S3 is a compositionally distinct intermediate phase that does not follow a monotonic trend of sulfur depletion with increasing temperature. Such non‐linear behavior suggests a unique structural or electronic reorganization that cannot be sufficiently explained using stoichiometry alone.

To determine whether differences in chemical bonding states are responsible for the phase selectivity of the CoxSy compositions at varying crystallization temperatures, we conducted X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) focused on the Co 2p region. The Co 2p signals obtained from each CoxSy were deconvoluted into two sub‐spectra corresponding to Co3+ and Co2+, with peak centers located at 793.7 and 796.9 eV, respectively (Figure 1c–e).[ 56 , 57 , 58 ] The spectrum of the CoS sample synthesized at 230 °C was dominated by Co2+ features, and strong satellite peaks were observed near 785.2 and 802.8 eV, which are characteristic signals of Co2+ originating from strong multiplet splitting and electron correlation effects.[ 59 ] The intensities of the satellite peaks of Co4S3 synthesized at 270 °C were relatively low, indicating reduced Co2+ contribution and an increased number of Co3+. This trend was also observed in the spectra of the Co9S8 phase synthesized at 300 °C, in which the satellite features were considerably suppressed. The progressive decrease in satellite peak intensity from CoS to Co4S3 and Co9S8 indicates a gradual transition in the Co–S bonding character from ionic to increasingly covalent.

Based on the integrated experimental results, we propose that the phase selectivity observed in the CoxSy nanocrystals originates from variations in the chemical bonding characteristics of Co, influenced by synthesis temperature. CoS crystallizes under sulfur‐rich conditions at 230 °C, whereas Co4S3 emerges at 270 °C as a Co‐rich intermediate phase, driven by the loss of volatile sulfur‐containing species generated during precursor decomposition.[ 54 ] At 300 °C, a further reduction in satellite peak intensity in the XPS spectra indicates enhanced covalency in Co–S bonding, correlating with the formation of a structurally distinct Co9S8 phase.[ 52 , 59 ] This non‐linear sequence of phase formation cannot be explained solely by the Co‐to‐S ratio or phase identity. A comprehensive understanding requires concurrent consideration of chemical bonding character and crystallographic structure. This interpretation, constructed by integrating the obtained experimental data with known reaction mechanisms, represents a new perspective on stoichiometry‐controlled crystallization in chalcogenide systems.

Furthermore, we performed high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR‐TEM) to examine the morphologies, particle sizes, and phase identities of the CoxSy nanocrystals (Figure 1f–h). The obtained images displayed well‐dispersed nanocrystals with minimal aggregation and average diameters of ≈10, 12, and 14 nm for CoS, Co4S3, and Co9S8, respectively (Figure S2, Supporting Information). In addition, clear lattice fringes were observed, with interplanar spacings of 0.15 nm for the (100) plane of CoS, 0.15 nm for the (100) plane of Co4S3, and 0.16 nm for the (311) plane of Co9S8, respectively. These measured spacings are consistent with the crystal structures obtained via XRD analysis. Selected area electron diffraction patterns further confirmed the phase identity of each sample by exhibiting diffraction features that matched the corresponding XRD data (Figure S3, Supporting Information).

Thus, we synthesized three distinct CoxSy nanocrystals by varying the injection temperature of the S‐OLA precursor. This temperature‐controlled synthesis regulated the Co‐to‐S ratio and adjusted the Co oxidation state, selectively forming phase‐pure CoxSy phases. The crystallographic phases, chemical compositions, and bonding states were confirmed via XRD, XPS, and HR‐TEM results. These results indicate a stoichiometry‐controlled approach for simultaneously tuning the phase identity and chemical bonding characteristics of chalcogenide nanocrystals.

Photovoltaic Performance of the PSCs with Stoichiometrically Controlled CoxSy HTLs

We used stoichiometrically controlled CoxSy as the HTLs in PSCs and evaluated the photovoltaic performance of these devices. Devices were fabricated with the structure of indium tin oxide (ITO)/SnO2/FAPbI3/CoxSy/Au (where FAPbI3 represents formamidinium lead iodide) structure. The thicknesses of each layer in the CoxSy‐based PSCs were identified using cross‐section scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Figure 2a, the measured thicknesses of the SnO2 electron transport layer were ≈20 nm, the perovskite layer was 690 ± 55 nm, the CoxSy HTL was 150 ± 27 nm, and Au electrode was 80 nm. Figure 2b schematically illustrates the energy‐level alignment of the CoxSy HTLs‐based PSCs, as determined via UPS measurements conducted on CoxSy nanocrystals deposited on ITO substrates (Figure S4, Supporting Information). The VBM positions of CoxSy were located progressively closer to that of the perovskite layer across the CoS, Co9S8, and Co4S3. The VBM values of CoS, Co9S8, and Co4S3 were −5.25, −5.31, and −5.37 eV, respectively, and that of the perovskite layer was −5.50 eV. The VBM positions of these CoxSy HTLs varied depending on their compositions, and the difference in the VBM relative to that of the perovskite would result in a difference in the VOC. Additionally, we evaluated the hole mobility of each CoxSy film using Hall effect measurements. The measured hole conductivities of CoS, Co9S8, and Co4S3 were 8.64 × 10−2, 8.89 × 10−2, and 9.11 × 10−2 cm2 V−1·s, respectively. These values are approximately two orders of magnitude higher than that of pristine spiro‐OMeTAD (2.92 × 10−5 cm2 V−1·s), indicating the superior hole transport property of the CoxSy films. In addition, we analyzed the surface morphology of the CoxSy‐based HTL using atomic force microscopy. Figure S5 (Supporting Information) reveals that the films with the three compositions exhibited similarly uniform surfaces, with root mean square (Rq) roughness values of ≈23.4, 24.9, and 24.6 nm for CoS, Co9S8, and Co4S3, respectively.

EPMA elemental maps of the d) perovskite/Co4S3/Au and e) perovskite/Co4S3/PMMA/Au device cross‐sections. Au, Co, and Pb are represented using red, green, and blue pixels, respectively. f) J–V curves of the PSCs containing CoxSy HTLs and an additional PMMA layer measured under simulated AM 1.5 G solar irradiation.

To assess the effects of these band positions on photovoltaic performance, we measured the photovoltaic parameters of the devices containing each CoxSy film as the HTL (Figure 2c and Table S2, Supporting Information). The device containing CoS yielded a PCE of 16.02%, VOC of 0.99 V, short‐circuit current density (JSC) of 22.08 mA cm−2, and fill factor (FF) of 73.05%. The Co9S8‐based device yielded a PCE of 17.02%, VOC of 1.04 V, JSC of 22.31 mA cm−2, and FF of 73.58%. The highest performance was observed for Co4S3, yielding a PCE of 18.65%, VOC of 1.09 V, JSC of 22.53 mA cm−2, and FF of 76.20%. The gradual increase in VOC can be associated with the downward shift of the VBM position, indicating that the stoichiometric tuning of CoxSy aligns this VBM closer to the VBM of the perovskite layer, thereby increasing VOC.

To investigate the origin of the PCE losses, we conducted electron probe microanalysis (EPMA) to examine the cross‐sectional elemental distributions within the PSCs. Figure 2d presents the elemental maps of a device with the perovskite/Co4S3/Au configuration, and Au, Co, and Pb are represented as red, green, and blue signals, respectively. The EPMA images revealed the presence of an indistinct boundary between the HTL and the perovskite and high‐density Au signals within the perovskite region. These results indicate that the Au electrode migrated through the CoxSy HTL and reached the perovskite layer, inducing interfacial degradation and nonradiative recombination.[ 60 , 61 ] To prevent the migration of electrode elements, an ≈2‐nm‐thick poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) layer was introduced over the CoxSy HTL. The elemental maps of the modified device (Figure 2e) confirmed that the PMMA layer effectively blocked Au migration, as evidenced by the separation of the Au and Pb signals.

To assess the influence of the bilayer HTL structure on device performance, we measured the photovoltaic parameters of the PSCs containing CoxSy/PMMA bilayer HTLs (Figure 2f and Table S3, Supporting Information). The CoS/PMMA‐based device exhibited a PCE of 22.07%, VOC of 1.12 V, JSC of 22.07 mA cm−2, and FF of 79.89%. The Co9S8/PMMA device exhibited a PCE of 22.36%, VOC of 1.15 V, JSC of 22.36 mA cm−2, and FF of 78.74%. The Co4S3/PMMA device achieved the highest PCE of 20.48%, VOC of 1.18 V, JSC of 22.37 mA cm−2, and FF of 77.94%. The JSC values remained comparable to those of the corresponding devices without PMMA; however, the VOC and FF improved substantially. This improvement can be attributed to the PMMA top layer, which possibly forms a physical barrier inhibiting Au migration from the electrode and suppressing nonradiative recombination.

Charge Extraction and JSC Enhancement Enabled by the CoxSy‐based Bilayer HTLs

Although the PMMA layer improved VOC by preventing Au electrode migration, its insulating properties may have hindered hole transfer across the interface. To overcome this issue, we introduced spiro‐OMeTAD, instead of PMMA, atop the CoxSy layer. The VBM of spiro‐OMeTAD is positioned at an appropriate level to facilitate hole extraction from the VBMs of CoxSy. Moreover, a uniform organic top layer can facilitate hole transport to the electrode and act as a physical barrier, preventing Au migration.

To examine the interfacial properties of PSCs containing CoxSy/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer HTLs, we conducted electrochemical impedance spectroscopy. Electrochemical parameters were extracted by fitting the impedance data using the Z‐view software (Figure S6, Supporting Information). The series resistance (RS) and recombination resistance (Rrec) of the devices were derived from Nyquist plots. The lower RS values of the CoxSy‐based devices compared to the spiro‐OMeTAD‐only device indicate their superior charge transport capability.[ 62 ] The Rrec value increased in the order of the CoS‐, Co9S8‐, and Co4S3‐based PSCs. The larger Rrec value observed in the Co4S3‐based device indicates a lower interfacial recombination probability compared to the other devices.[ 63 , 64 , 65 ] Further studies are needed to clarify the origin of the observed differences in Rrec across the CoxSy compositions.

We further performed steady‐state photoluminescence (PL) measurements to examine the effect of the bilayer configuration. Figure 3a shows the PL spectra of the perovskite films with different CoxSy/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer HTLs. The pristine perovskite film exhibited strong emission centered at ≈800 nm under 463 nm excitation. All the CoxSy/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer films exhibited more pronounced PL quenching than the spiro‐OMeTAD‐only film, indicating efficient charge extraction from the perovskite layer to the bilayer HTLs. However, given the larger VBM offset between spiro‐OMeTAD and the perovskite compared to the CoxSy compositions, hole extraction into spiro‐OMeTAD may be more efficient than into CoxSy. Figure S7 (Supporting Information) confirms that the perovskite/CoxSy films display less pronounced PL quenching than the perovskite/spiro‐OMeTAD film, indicating less efficient hole extraction in the absence of the bilayer configuration. Therefore, the enhanced hole extraction observed in the CoxSy/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer configuration may be attributed to additional factors such as the high hole conductivity of CoxSy and the VBM offset between CoxSy and spiro‐OMeTAD.[ 66 , 67 , 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 ]

To evaluate the interfacial hole extraction characteristics of each HTL configuration, we measured the time‐resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) decays of the perovskite films with various HTLs deposited on top (Figure 3b). The TRPL data were fitted using a bi‐exponential decay model, from which three parameters were extracted, namely, the fast decay time (τ1), the slow decay time (τ2), and the average decay time (τAVG). Specifically, the fast component τ1 corresponds to the timescale of charge transfer from the perovskite to the HTL, whereas the slow component τ2 reflects radiative recombination of charge carriers within the perovskite layer. These parameters are listed in Table S4 (Supporting Information). We compared the average decay times of the CoxSy/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer HTLs to evaluate their hole extraction performances. The Co4S3‐based bilayer HTL yielded the shortest τAVG of 48.4 ns, followed by the Co9S8/spiro‐OMeTAD (61.9 ns) and CoS/spiro‐OMeTAD (108.9 ns) bilayers. These results imply that the Co4S3/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer configuration offers the most appropriate energy level to facilitate hole extraction from the perovskite layer. However, this order does not fully correspond to the trend expected from the energy levels, which suggests that additional factors may have influenced the overall extraction dynamics. HTMs with high hole mobility can reduce interfacial charge accumulation, thereby promoting more efficient charge transfer.[ 66 , 67 , 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 ] Given the intrinsically high hole mobility of CoxSy, the enhanced extraction observed in CoxSy‐based HTLs may be attributed to reduced interfacial charge accumulation and accelerated hole transfer.

To further understand the individual roles of each layer in the bilayer HTL, we compared the hole extraction dynamics of the Co4S3/PMMA and Co4S3/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayers with that of a single Co4S3 HTL (Figure 3c). The perovskite film with a single Co4S3 HTL yielded a τAVG of 153.6 ns. When a PMMA layer was deposited on the Co4S3 HTL, the τAVG value changed slightly to 150.4 ns, indicating that the insulating PMMA layer had little effect on charge transfer. By contrast, when spiro‐OMeTAD was deposited on top of Co4S3 to form a bilayer HTL, all three lifetime parameters decreased considerably. The average decay time was reduced to 48.4 ns, shorter than the τAVG of 117.4 ns observed in the case of spiro‐OMeTAD alone. These results indicate that the bilayer structure enhanced hole extraction and suppressed interfacial recombination.

We prepared PSCs containing CoxSy/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer HTLs and evaluated the photovoltaic characteristics of these devices, which shared the same architecture as the CoxSy‐only PSCs, except for the spiro‐OMeTAD layers deposited atop the CoxSy HTLs. To confirm the reproducibility of device fabrication, we measured the thickness of each layer using cross‐sectional SEM (Figure 3b). The perovskite, CoxSy, and spiro‐OMeTAD layers had average thicknesses of 690 ± 38, 150 ± 32, and 223 ± 22 nm, respectively. The bilayer HTL was well formed, and the overall device architecture remained identical to that of the CoxSy‐HTL‐based PSCs, except for the spiro‐OMeTAD layer. The J–V characteristics obtained under AM 1.5 G illumination are presented in Figure 3c and Table S5 (Supporting Information). The reference device containing spiro‐OMeTAD alone exhibited a PCE of 22.53%, VOC of 1.145 V, JSC of 23.89 mA cm−2, and FF of 82.39%. All bilayer HTL‐based PSCs exhibited improved photovoltaic performances compared to the reference device. The device containing the CoS/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer HTL achieved a PCE of 22.80%, VOC of 1.150 V, JSC of 24.16 mA cm−2, and FF of 82.03%. The Co9S8‐based device yielded a PCE of 23.88%, VOC of 1.167 V, JSC of 24.22 mA cm−2, and FF of 84.46%. Notably, the Co4S3/spiro‐OMeTAD device achieved the highest PCE of 24.41%, VOC of 1.191 V, JSC of 24.37 mA cm−2, and FF of 84.07%, exhibiting negligible hysteresis (Figure S8, Supporting Information). The PCE enhancement in the bilayer devices was mainly attributed to increases in JSC and FF.

To further examine the origin of JSC enhancement, we obtained the incident‐photon‐to‐current conversion efficiency (IPCE) spectra of the PSCs (Figure 3d). The integrated JSC values from the IPCE are in good agreement with those obtained from the J–V measurements. Notably, the IPCE enhancements of the PSCs containing CoxSy/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer HTLs were observed in the long wavelength regions. The enhanced long‐wavelength intensity indicates that holes generated within the perovskite layer (near the HTL) were efficiently collected at the electrode,[ 73 ] owing to the high intrinsic hole conductivity of CoxSy. The degree of IPCE enhancement is consistent with the hole conductivities in the order of CoS, Co9S8, and Co4S3. Furthermore, the CoxSy with the spiro‐OMeTAD prevented Au migration, increasing the FF and JSC.

Enhanced Long‐term Stability of the PSCs based on the CoxSy/spiro‐OMeTAD Bilayer Architecture

We monitored the long‐term stabilities of the unencapsulated PSCs incorporating CoxSy/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer HTLs under continuous exposure to a temperature of 65°°C and 65% relative humidity (RH) for 1000 h (Figure 4a). The PSCs containing bilayer HTLs retained over 92% of their initial PCEs after 1000 h, whereas the PCE of the PSC with spiro‐OMeTAD alone degraded to ≈60%. This degradation can be attributed to the hygroscopic nature of spiro‐OMeTAD, which facilitates moisture penetration and accelerates perovskite decomposition.[ 37 ] By contrast, the stability of the bilayer configuration was high because the CoxSy layer acted as a separate and continuous interface below the spiro‐OMeTAD layer. This structure limited moisture access and prevented direct exposure of the perovskite to humidity. Although the CoxSy‐only device degraded rapidly owing to Au electrode migration, the bilayer structure effectively suppressed such instability and enabled long‐term device operation.

a) Long‐term stability of the unencapsulated PSCs containing CoxSy/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer HTLs (red, blue, and green) and spiro‐OMeTAD (black) under controlled conditions (65 ± 1°°C, 65 ± 5% RH). Changes in the PCEs of the PSCs employing different HTLs during thermal aging at 85 °C under an b) N2 atmosphere and c) 25% RH. d) MPPT performances of the Co4S3/spiro‐OMeTAD and spiro‐OMeTAD‐based devices under continuous illumination.

We further investigated the thermal stability under both inert and ambient conditions. The PSCs containing the Co4S3, Co4S3/PMMA bilayer, and Co4S3/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer HTLs were aged at 85 °C for 100 h in either an N2 atmosphere or ambient conditions. In the N2 atmosphere (Figures 4b,c), the device containing the Co4S3 HTL retained 52.2% of its initial PCE, whereas the PCE of the device containing spiro‐OMeTAD degraded further to 35.8%. By contrast, the Co4S3/PMMA bilayer HTL and Co4S3/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer devices maintained 91.5% and 90.2% of their initial PCEs, respectively. The negligible difference between the two bilayers indicates that the top HTL suppressed Au migration and mitigated degradation. Under humid conditions, the performance loss of all the devices accelerated. The spiro‐OMeTAD device retained only 10.6% of its initial PCE, and the PCE of the Co4S3 device degraded further. However, both bilayer devices maintained high stability, confirming the protective role of the top layer under combined heat and moisture stress. To assess the structural changes of the CoxSy HTLs, we conducted XRD analysis after 10, 50, and 100 h of thermal aging at 85 °C (Figure S9, Supporting Information). All the CoxSy compositions maintained their initial crystalline phases. These results confirm that CoxSy remained stable under thermal stress, and the improved device stability can primarily be attributed to the thermal resistance of the top HTL deposited on the CoxSy HTL. To evaluate degradation behavior at the film level, we visually examined the CoxSy HTLs exposed to thermal (85 °C, 25% RH) and humid (65 °C, 65% RH) conditions for 100 h (Figures S10,S11, Supporting Information). No changes were observed, indicating that CoxSy‐deposited films remained stable even under harsh conditions.

Moreover, we evaluated the operational stability of the PSCs under continuous 1‐sun illumination under maximum power point tracking (MPPT) conditions. Figure 4d reveals that the PSC containing the Co4S3/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer HTL retained 96.6% of its initial PCE, decreasing slightly from 24.14% to 23.34% after 100 h. By contrast, the device containing spiro‐OMeTAD alone retained 91.2% of its PCE, decreasing from 22.27% to 20.31% over the same period. These results confirm that the bilayer HTL configuration enhances operational stability by suppressing degradation.

Conclusion

We proposed stoichiometrically controlled CoxSy, a new class of p‐type HTLs, for application in PSCs. The temperature‐controlled hot‐injection method was used to synthesize three distinct CoxSy compositions as phase‐pure nanocrystals without post‐treatment. These HTLs exhibited VBM positions that were well aligned with those of the perovskite layer, thereby enhancing the VOC of the PSCs. Additionally, we introduced a bilayer HTL structure by depositing spiro‐OMeTAD atop the CoxSy layer. The best‐performing PSC, containing the Co4S3/spiro‐OMeTAD bilayer HTL, achieved a PCE of 24.41%, exceeding that of the device containing spiro‐OMeTAD alone.

In addition to the photovoltaic performance, we elucidated the influence of interfacial energy‐level alignment on charge extraction. PL and TRPL analyses revealed that Spiro‐OMeTAD promotes hole extraction in CoxSy and suppresses recombination at the HTL/perovskite interface. These findings confirm that the enhanced photovoltaic performance originated from stoichiometrically controlled band‐alignment‐based design, instead of empirical optimization. Furthermore, the bilayer configurations improved stability under various thermal and environmental conditions. The unencapsulated PSCs retained 92% of their initial PCE after 1000 h under 65 °C and 65% RH, whereas the PCE of the spiro‐OMeTAD‐only device substantially degraded. Under MPPT tracking with 1‐sun illumination, the Co4S3/spiro‐OMeTAD device maintained 96.6% of its initial PCE after 100 h. Under thermal stress at 85 °C and ambient humidity, the device retained over 90% of its PCE. These results demonstrate that stoichiometric engineering of transition metal sulfides yields electronically and structurally stable HTLs for application in high‐efficiency PSCs. The design principles established in this study can be extended to other bilayer configurations, providing a generalizable strategy for interfacial energy‐level tuning for emerging optoelectronic devices.

Experimental Section

Materials

CoCl2 · 6H2O (98%), S (powder, 99.98%), oleic acid (OA, 90%), n‐hexane (anhydrous, 95%), n‐octane (extra pure, ≥99%), HI (57 wt.% in water), 2‐methoxyethanol (2ME, anhydrous, 99.8%), 2‐propanol (IPA, anhydrous, 99.5%), chlorobenzene (CB, anhydrous, 99.8%), N,N‐dimethylformamide (DMF, anhydrous, 99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, >99.5%),), methylammonium chloride (MACl, 98%), PMMA, acetonitrile (ACN, anhydrous, 99.8%), tBP (98%), and Li‐TFSI were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Moreover, 1‐octadecene (ODE, 90%), SnO2 (15% in H2O colloidal dispersion), and formamidine acetate salt (99%) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. Ethanol (absolute, 99.9%), methyl acetate (MeOAc, ≥99.5%), and diethyl ether (extra pure grade) were purchased from Duksan. Additionally, spiro‐OMeTAD and tris(2‐(1H‐pyrazol‐1‐yl)‐4‐tert‐butylpyridine)‐cobalt(III) tris(bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (FK209) were purchased from Lumtec. Oleylamine (OLA, 50%) and PbI2 (99.9985%) were purchased from TCI.

Synthesis of the CoxSy Nanocrystals

CoCl2 · 6H2O (0.4 mmol) was added to a 250 mL three‐neck flask containing OA (35 mmol), OLA (35 mmol), and ODE (140 mmol). The mixture was degassed and heated under an Ar atmosphere at 110 °C for 1 h. Separately, a sulfur precursor solution was prepared by dissolving elemental sulfur (2 mmol) in OLA (5 mmol) under an Ar atmosphere. Once the Co‐containing solution reached the target injection temperature, the sulfur solution was swiftly injected under vigorous stirring. A rapid color change from cobalt blue to black indicated the formation of CoxSy nanocrystals. After conducting the reaction for 1 h, the product was purified by washing with ethanol and a mixed solvent of ethanol and CB (molar ratio of CB = 0.05). Residual side products were removed via centrifugation at 7000 rpm for 15 min. The purified nanocrystals were subsequently redispersed in n‐hexane (10 mL) and centrifuged again under the same conditions to remove aggregates. For film preparation, n‐hexane was removed under vacuum, and the dried CoxSy nanocrystals were redispersed in n‐octane at a concentration of ≈100 mg mL−1.

Synthesis of the Perovskite Precursor

FAI and FAPbI3 powder were synthesized via methods reported previously.[ 6 ] Briefly, 25 g formamidine acetate was mixed with 50 mL HI in a 500 mL round‐bottomed flask under vigorous stirring first. A powder with a light‐yellow color was synthesized after evaporating the solvent at 80°°C for 1 h under vacuum conditions. The powder was redissolved in ethanol and precipitated using diethyl ether. This procedure was repeated thrice until a white powder was obtained, which was recrystallized from a mixed ethanol and diethyl ether solution (1:1 v/v) in a refrigerator. The final white powder was collected using a glass filter and dried at 60°°C for 24 h. FAPbI3 powder was synthesized by mixing the synthesized FAI (0.8 m) with PbI2 (1:1 molar ratio) in 2ME under stirring. The yellow mixed solution was heated and stirred vigorously at 120°°C and subsequently recrystallized using the retrograde method. The resulting black powder was filtered using a glass filter and baked at 150°°C for 30 min.

Fabrication of the PSCs

Patterned ITO (8 Ω/□) substrates were cleaned by sequential sonication in detergent water, deionized water, and IPA for 10 min each. To produce SnO2 electron transport layers, SnO2 nanoparticles (NPs) were diluted in deionized water and IPA (SnO2 NPs:H2O:IPA = 1:4:2). The solution was dropped onto UV‐ozone‐treated transparent ITO substrates and spin‐coated at a speed of 3000 rpm for 30 s and subsequently annealed at 150°°C for 30 min. The perovskite layer was spin‐coated onto the UV‐ozone‐treated SnO2 film at 8000 rpm for 50 s, and 1 mL of diethyl ether was dropped after 10 s during spin‐coating. The deposited film was annealed on a hotplate at 150°°C for 15 min and 100°°C for 1 h. The perovskite precursor solution was prepared as follows: 778 mg of the synthesized FAPbI3 powder and 23 mg of MACl were dissolved in 0.5 mL DMF/DMSO (4:1). To fabricate the CoxSy films via a solid‐state ligand exchange process, a solution of CoxSy nanocrystals in n‐octane (≈100 mg mL−1) was spin‐coated onto the perovskite layer at 1000 rpm for 20 s. The resulting film was immersed in MeOAc and spin‐dried. The film was subsequently soaked and rinsed with MeOAc, followed by spin‐drying. The PMMA layer was prepared by dissolving PMMA in CB at a 1 mg mL−1 concentration and spin‐coating at 4000 rpm for 30 s. The spiro‐OMeTAD layer was deposited by spin‐coating at 4000 rpm for 30 s. The spiro‐OMeTAD solution was prepared as follows: spiro‐OMeTAD (90 mg mL−1 in CB) was doped with 39 µL tBP, 23 µL Li‐TFSI (520 mg mL−1 in ACN), and 5 µL FK209 (180 mg mL−1 in ACN). Finally, the Au electrode with a thickness of 80 nm was deposited using a thermal evaporator.

Characterization

High‐resolution XRD (SmartLab, Rigaku) using 1.541 Å Cu Kα radiation was conducted to characterize the crystal structure of CoxSy. The morphologies of the CoxSy nanocrystals and their films were analyzed using a transmission electron microscope (JEM2100F, JEOL). XPS and UPS were conducted using a scanning XPS microprobe (PHI 5000 Versa Probe, Ulvac‐PHI) with an Al Kα monochromator (1486.6 eV) for XPS and HeI (21.2 eV) for UPS. The PSCs were analyzed using an extremely high‐resolution field‐emission scanning electron microscope (Verios G4 UC, FEI). The elemental distributions of the CoxSy‐based devices were investigated using an electron probe microanalyzer (JXA‐8500F, JEOL). Under dry conditions (20 ± 5°°C, below 25% RH), the J−V characteristics of the devices were obtained using a Keithley 2400 source under an Xe‐lamp‐based solar simulator (Newport 91160s, AAA class). An Si solar cell, calibrated by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory, was employed to adjust the light intensity to the AM 1.5 G 1‐sun condition (100 mW cm−2). The voltage sweep rate was 20 mV with a delay of 20 ms for the reverse sweeping direction from 1.20 to −0.05 V. The devices were characterized using a SUS304 aperture with an area of 0.09 cm2. IPCEs were obtained using a specific IPCE measurement system (PV Measurement, Inc.). The operational stability was evaluated using the maximum power point (MPP) tracking characteristics of devices. These were measured using a Keithley 2400 source under a Xenon‐lamp‐based solar simulator with calibrated light intensity of 100 mW cm−2. The surface roughness of CoxSy films was measured using AFM (XE‐100; Park Systems). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed using an electrochemical workstation (SP‐300, BioLogic) under dark conditions. These measurements were conducted over a frequency range of 10 Hz to 1 MHz with an AC perturbation amplitude of 10 mV. The impedance spectra were analyzed using Z view modeling software. The steady‐state PL and TRPL spectra were recorded using a Fluoromax‐4 (iHR320, HORIBA Scientific) spectrometer. The excitation wavelength was 463 nm, and the TRPL measurements were conducted at the maximum PL (≈800 nm) of the perovskite film.
