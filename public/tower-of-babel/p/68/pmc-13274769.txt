Interocular Comparison of Refractive Development in Children With Unilateral Traumatic Optic Neuropathy
Authors: Lirong Liao, Yang Gao, Xiaojie Feng, Jinmiao Li, Ling Jin, Rong Lu, Xiaohu Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo characterize interocular refractive development in children with unilateral traumatic optic neuropathy (TON) and evaluate the effect of visual loss severity on myopia progression.MethodsThis retrospective, longitudinal, self-controlled cohort included 21 children (18 boys) aged 3 to 15 years with unilateral TON, each having ≥2 cycloplegic spherical equivalent refractions (SERs) at least 6 months apart. Both eyes were analyzed, and TON eyes were stratified by baseline best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) into no light perception (NLP) and better than NLP. Longitudinal SER trajectories were examined using linear mixed-effects models with subject-specific random intercepts and time slopes. Model 1 included Eye (TON versus fellow), Time (years since injury), Eye × Time, and baselineSER; model 2 additionally incorporated BCVA and interactions of Time with BCVA and baselineSER.ResultsMean age at injury was 11.29 ± 3.20 years, and mean follow-up was 2.94 ± 1.66 years. The Eye × Time coefficient was 0.250 D/y (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.170–0.330; P < 0.001), indicating significantly slower myopic progression in TON eyes. The Time × BCVA effect (0.195 D/y; 95% CI, 0.005–0.384; P = 0.044) indicated a flatter (less negative) myopic trajectory in NLP eyes.ConclusionsIn pediatric unilateral TON, the injured eye shows markedly slowed and, in severe cases, nearly arrested myopic progression compared with the fellow eye, supporting a key modulatory role of postretinal visual input in refractive development.Translational RelevanceUnilateral TON provides a human model linking optic nerve integrity to refractive development and highlights the fellow eye as a high-risk target for myopia monitoring and control.

Introduction

Myopia is a complex, multifactorial disease, and its underlying mechanisms remain a major focus of vision research. Current evidence suggests that refractive development is a visually guided process: abnormal visual input can disrupt emmetropization, alter ocular growth, and ultimately drive the onset and progression of myopia.1–3 In animal studies, form deprivation myopia (FDM) and lens-induced myopia (LIM) are two classical and representative models. In FDM, eyelid suturing or opaque occluders prevent the formation of a clear retinal image and reliably induce axial myopia.4,5 This model mimics clinical situations such as congenital cataract or severe congenital ptosis, in which affected children often show marked myopic shifts and axial elongation.2,6–8 In LIM, additional negative lenses are placed in front of the eye so that images are focused behind the retina, leading to compensatory eye elongation and myopia. This process more closely resembles the development of myopia in most children. In both models, abnormal visual signals trigger changes in eye growth, but the pathogenesis of the two types of myopia models is different.1,9

The optic nerve is the key pathway between the retina and the brain. Structural or functional abnormalities of the optic nerve can directly affect visual signal transmission and may influence refractive development. Experimental work has used optic nerve section (ONS)10–12 or optic nerve crush (ONC)13 to interrupt optic nerve signaling. Multiple studies have shown that robust FDM can still be induced in animals after ONS, suggesting that the mechanisms underlying FDM are mediated primarily within the eye.10,14–17 In contrast, LIM is partially suppressed in chick models,11,14,16 and studies on the effects of ONS on LIM in mammals have not yet been conducted. In addition, even in the absence of form deprivation or defocus, eyes with isolated ONS frequently exhibit slower axial elongation and a shift toward hyperopia.10,11,16 These findings indicate that intact postretinal visual pathways may modulate visually guided eye growth, although the exact contribution of optic nerve signaling remains uncertain.

Human data also suggest a link between optic nerve abnormalities and refractive error.18–25 Optic nerve hypoplasia (ONH) is a complex developmental disorder that is frequently associated with mutations in genes such as HESX1 and SOX2, as well as systemic conditions including pituitary dysfunction and growth hormone deficiency.26–30 Several cross-sectional studies have reported a high prevalence of marked hyperopia and astigmatism in patients with ONH.19–23,25 However, because ONH is often accompanied by genetic, endocrine, and neurodevelopmental abnormalities, it is difficult to isolate the specific impact of optic nerve dysfunction on refractive outcomes.

Traumatic optic neuropathy (TON) offers a way to address this problem. In TON, mechanical trauma damages the optic nerve and disrupts eye–brain visual signal transmission, usually in otherwise healthy children and without systemic disease. Unilateral TON in particular provides a natural model: the injured eye has severe visual loss, while the fellow eye retains normal vision, and both eyes share a similar prenatal and early postnatal environment. This configuration is well suited for a self-controlled comparison to examine how interruption of visual signal transmission affects subsequent refractive development.

Therefore, this retrospective study analyzed long-term binocular refractive changes in children who developed unilateral TON before the age of 15 years. The fellow eye served as an internal control to compare refractive trajectories between the injured and fellow eyes and to assess how the severity of visual loss in the TON eye influences refractive development.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This was a retrospective, longitudinal, self-controlled study conducted at Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Sun Yat-Sen University. Children diagnosed with unilateral TON between January 2018 and December 2022 were identified through the hospital electronic medical record (EMR) system.

Potential cases were identified in the EMR system and then verified by chart review. Inclusion criteria were (1) a clear diagnosis of unilateral TON, (2) age at injury between 3 and 15 years, and (3) at least two cycloplegic refractions for both eyes with a minimum interval of 6 months between the first (baseline) and last visit.

TON was diagnosed based on documented orbital or craniofacial trauma with subsequent visual function decline, together with ophthalmic findings consistent with optic nerve injury (a positive relative afferent pupillary defect); computed tomography (CT)/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) evidence and/or abnormal visual evoked potentials were considered supportive when available in the EMR.

Exclusion criteria were (1) preexisting anisometropia at baseline, defined as an interocular difference in spherical equivalent refraction (SER) ≥2.00 diopters (D); (2) concomitant ocular conditions that could affect refractive development or refraction measurements, such as globe rupture, media opacity, or retinal detachment; (3) systemic diseases known to affect ocular development; (4) any history of myopia control treatment (e.g., orthokeratology, atropine, or other antimyopia interventions) during follow-up; and (5) any history of intraocular surgery.

Ethical Approval

The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center (2022KYPJ202). Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants. This was an observational study, and no experimental interventions were applied.

Data Collection and Examinations

Demographic information (age, gender), etiology of injury, and ocular examination findings were retrieved. For each participant, the first measurement obtained within 6 months after injury was defined as the baseline. Follow-up examinations were scheduled at least 6 months after baseline. Follow-up duration was calculated as the interval (in years) between the baseline and the final visit.

Cycloplegic Refraction Protocol

Bilateral cycloplegic refractions were measured using an automated refractor (KR-8800; Topcon, Tokyo, Japan) by experienced optometrists after standard cycloplegia. Cycloplegia was induced using three drops of 1% tropicamide administered at 5-minute intervals prior to autorefraction. After an additional 15 minutes, pupil light reflex and pupil diameter were assessed to confirm adequate cycloplegia. Successful dilation was defined as a pupil diameter ≥6 mm and/or an absent pupillary light reflex. If cycloplegia was deemed insufficient, one additional drop was instilled and reassessment was performed 15 minutes later.

Refractive error was expressed as SER, calculated as sphere + 0.5 × cylinder. ChangeSER was defined as the difference in cycloplegic SER between the last and first eligible visits (SER_last − SER_first). ΔSER was defined as the visit-specific change in SER relative to the first eligible visit.

Best-Corrected Visual Acuity and Severity Stratification

Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) refers to monocular distance best-corrected visual acuity measured at 5 m using a standard logarithmic visual acuity chart under routine clinical conditions and recorded as Snellen fractions. When chart acuity could not be quantified, vision was recorded as counting fingers (CF), hand motion (HM), light perception (LP), or no light perception (NLP), as documented. For subgroup analyses, TON eyes were stratified by baseline BCVA into an NLP group versus a better-than-NLP (above-NLP) group; the above-NLP group included any documented acuity better than NLP (LP/HM/CF or chart-measured acuity).

Axial Length Measurement

Because this was a retrospective chart review, axial length (AL) was not part of the routine examination protocol for children with unilateral TON and was therefore available only in a subset of participants. AL was measured using noncontact optical biometry (IOLMaster 500, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany, or Lenstar LS 900, Haag-Streit, Koeniz, Switzerland). For any given visit, both eyes of a child were measured on the same device. Because different biometers were used across subjects and visits, we did not analyze absolute AL values or between-subject differences. Instead, we performed an exploratory analysis of within-subject interocular differences at a single follow-up time point ≥1 year after injury, defined as AL_DIFF = ALTON − ALFellow.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Crude intereye differences in SER change between baseline and final visit were compared using paired tests. Normality of paired differences (TON − Fellow) was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test to determine whether to apply a paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Additionally, intergroup comparisons between BCVA subgroups (above-NLP versus NLP) in terms of ChangeSER were performed using independent samples t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, based on normality of the data.

Longitudinal refractive development was analyzed with linear mixed-effects models (LMMs), with SER as the dependent variable and subject-specific random intercepts and time slopes. Time since injury was expressed in years and double-centered: for each child, a subject-mean centered variable (Time_c) was used for random slopes, and a grand-mean centered variable (Time_gc) was used for fixed effects. For each eye, baselineSER was defined as the cycloplegic SER measured within 6 months after injury and closest to the injury date; this value was used as a covariate for both eyes in the LMMs.

Let i index subjects and j index visits. SERij denotes the spherical equivalent refraction for subject i at time j. Eyeij was coded as 1 for the TON eye and 0 for the fellow eye. BCVAi was coded 1 for NLP and 0 for above-NLP.

The primary model (model 1) evaluated whether refractive change over time differed between TON and fellow eyes while adjusting for baseline refractive status. The model was specified as SERij=β0+β1Time_gcij+β2Eyeij+β3Eyeij×Time_gcij+β4BaselineSERi+b0i+b1iTime_cij+ɛijwhere b0i and b1i are the subject-specific random intercept and random time slope, modeled with an uncorrelated structure ( Time _c∥ Subject ), and εij is the residual error. The key parameter β3(Eye × Time) was interpreted as the intereye difference in the annual SER slope (D/y); a positive value indicates a less negative time slope (slower myopic shift) in TON eyes relative to fellow eyes. Candidate covariates (age at injury, gender, age at examination, and BaselineSER) were evaluated in alternative models fitted with maximum likelihood; only BaselineSER notably improved model fit (Δ akaike information criterion (AIC) −48.2 versus. base model; likelihood ratio test [LRT] P ≈ 1 × 10⁻¹²) and was retained in model 1.

To assess effect modification by the severity of visual loss in the TON eye, model 1 was extended to include BCVA stratum and its interaction with time. The final model (model 2) was specified as SERij=β0+β1Time_gcij+β2Eyeij+β3Eyeij×Time_gcij+β4BCVAi+β5Time_gcij×BCVAi+β6BaselineSERij+β7Time_gcij×BaselineSERij+b0i+b1iTime_cij+ɛijNested models were compared using LRTs under maximum likelihood. Relative to the base model including Eye × Time and BaselineSER, adding the Time × BCVA(TON) interaction significantly improved fit (χ² = 8.49, df = 2, P = 0.014; lower AIC), whereas adding a higher-order Eye × Time × BCVA(TON) interaction did not (χ² = 4.61, df = 2, P = 0.100), and that term was omitted. Adding the Time × BaselineSER interaction further improved fit (LRT, P = 0.033), so it was retained in model 2. Final parameter estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained under restricted maximum likelihood with Satterthwaite's approximation for degrees of freedom (lmerTest). Model assumptions (linearity, homoscedasticity, and normality of residuals and random effects) were checked using standard diagnostic plots.

In the subset with AL data at ≥1 year after injury, interocular AL differences (AL_DIFF = ALTON − ALFellow) were summarized descriptively without formal hypothesis testing, given the small sample size and retrospective, opportunistic nature of AL measurements.

All tests were two-sided with a significance level of 0.05. Data processing, analysis, and visualization were performed using STATA version 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) or R (available at https://www.r-project.org/).

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Participants

A total of 21 children (18 boys, 3 girls) with unilateral TON were included in the study. The average age at the time of injury was 11.29 ± 3.20 years. The mean time from injury to the first examination was 0.09 ± 0.12 years, and the average follow-up duration was 2.94 ± 1.66 years. Patients attended an average of two visits (range, two to four visits). Regarding injury causes, the most common cause was ground-level fall (7/21, 33.3%), followed by motor vehicle accident (6/21, 28.6%), blunt impact (4/21, 19.0%), and fall from height (4/21, 19.0%). Crude Longitudinal Patterns of Refractive Change

Figure 1 illustrates the longitudinal change in SER relative to eye-specific baseline (ΔSER) as a function of time since injury. Across 21 children, 47 follow-up visits per eye were available. In the crude plots (Fig. 1A), fellow eyes showed a clear linear myopic shift over time, with most points falling below zero and a steep negative regression slope. In contrast, TON eyes clustered around 0 D with a nearly flat slope, consistent with markedly slower myopic progression.

Discussion

In this self-controlled cohort of children with unilateral TON, the injured eyes showed markedly slower myopic progression than their fellow eyes, with the greatest attenuation observed in NLP eyes. Over the follow-up period, refractive change in TON eyes was essentially minimal (ChangeSER = −0.17 ± 0.67 D), whereas fellow eyes exhibited a clear myopic shift (ChangeSER = −0.86 ± 1.25 D). When stratified by posttraumatic BCVA, TON eyes in the NLP subgroup showed almost arrested refractive change, some drifting slightly toward hyperopia, while TON eyes with residual vision (above-NLP) fell between NLP eyes and fellow eyes. These findings suggest that intact postretinal visual signaling may play an important modulatory role in refractive development and myopia progression during childhood.

The linear mixed-effects models provided a more refined quantification of these patterns. In model 1, after accounting for baselineSER in each eye, the main effect of Time was negative, indicating an overall trend toward myopia over the observation period in the cohort as a whole. At the same time, the Eye × Time interaction was approximately +0.25 D/y, implying that, relative to the fellow eyes, the TON eyes became about 0.25 D less myopic per year. In other words, against the background of the typical school-age myopic shift, refractive development in the injured eye was substantially decelerated and in some cases effectively halted. In model 2, BCVA stratification showed that NLP TON eyes had the flattest (least negative) refractive trajectory over time, whereas above-NLP TON eyes exhibited a less attenuated trajectory, approaching that of the fellow eyes. In addition, the Time × BaselineSER term showed that eyes with more hyperopic baseline refraction progressed more slowly, an effect that was explicitly adjusted for in the model and therefore unlikely to confound the observed Eye differences.

Because of obvious ethical constraints, most prior work on the effect of optic nerve injury on refractive development has relied on animal models. Experimental paradigms have typically fallen into three categories: isolated ONS or ONC, form deprivation combined with ONS (FDM + ONS), and LIM + ONS. In models with isolated ONS, without additional extreme visual perturbation, injured eyes generally tend to become relatively more hyperopic with shorter axial length compared with their fellow eyes.10,11,16 This pattern is particularly well documented in chick models. These observations are directionally consistent with our finding that TON eyes exhibit attenuated or arrested myopic progression and, in some cases, mild hyperopic drift. Together, such data support the view that normal optic nerve structure and function are important for physiological refractive development. By contrast, in a guinea pig model, ONS eyes showed a transient myopic shift with increased vitreous chamber depth, which returned to normal within approximately 1 month, suggesting species-specific differences in the response to ONS.15,17

On this background, investigators have further applied marked abnormal visual stimuli after ONS to probe the role of optic nerve signaling in experimental myopia. Across several species, including chicks,10,14,16 rhesus macaques,31 and guinea pigs,15,17 ONS does not prevent the development of FDM, nor does it abolish refractive recovery after removal of the diffuser. These results suggest that FDM is mediated predominantly by local retina–sclera mechanisms, with comparatively limited dependence on an intact optic nerve. However, in multiple chick studies, LIM induction is attenuated to varying degrees in ONS eyes. For example, Wildsoet and Wallman11 found that chicks wearing −15 D lenses after ONS developed substantially less myopia in the lesioned eye, with median refraction shifting to mild hyperopia (approximately +2.1 D) instead of high myopia (approximately −8.6 D) in the control eye, indicating significant loss of compensatory response to hyperopic defocus. Another chick study reported only a modest reduction in LIM (induced refractive error −9.21 D in ONS eyes vs. −11.58 D in control eyes).16 These attenuated responses to imposed hyperopic defocus imply that the eye's ability to decode and respond to defocus signals is at least partly dependent on intact optic nerve transmission. Given that LIM is thought to better approximate the pattern of visual experience associated with common human myopia, the animal data are relevant to the present findings. The markedly slowed myopic progression in TON eyes observed here may similarly reflect impaired detection and relay of hyperopic defocus signals after optic nerve damage, although this remains a speculative mechanism that requires confirmation in prospective and experimental studies.

To date, there have been no systematic longitudinal studies of refractive development in children with TON. Related clinical evidence comes primarily from cross-sectional studies of ONH. Several cohorts have consistently demonstrated a high prevalence of hyperopia in ONH.19,21–23 For instance, reports indicate that approximately 40% of patients with ONH are hyperopic compared with about 17% of controls, and in another study, hyperopia (22.6%) and high hyperopia (16.9%) were more common than myopia (15.1%) among patients with ONH.22,23 This hyperopic tendency is directionally similar to the attenuated myopic progression and occasional hyperopic drift observed in TON eyes in our study. However, the refractive profile among patients with ONH is highly heterogeneous, with a substantial proportion also developing myopia.19–21,23,24 Such heterogeneity likely reflects the complex nature of ONH, which often coexists with genetic mutations, pituitary dysfunction, and systemic developmental disorders, all of which may influence ocular growth via multiple pathways and were not fully controlled or stratified in prior work. In contrast, TON represents an acquired, acute, and predominantly focal insult affecting the optic nerve and its projections. In this study, additional confounders such as globe-penetrating trauma, media opacities, and major systemic disease were carefully excluded, providing a relatively clean human model to examine the impact of postretinal signal loss on refractive trajectories.

AL measurements were available in only a subset of children at 1 year or more after injury, but in most of these cases, the interocular AL difference (TON − fellow) was negative, indicating that the TON eye was slightly shorter than the fellow eye at follow-up. This pattern is consistent with the refractive findings and aligns with reports from ONS animal models in which axial elongation is suppressed in injured eyes. Nonetheless, given the limited subsample, heterogeneous time points, and use of different instruments across visits, the axial data in this study should be regarded as exploratory and interpreted cautiously.

Building on our findings and prior experimental and clinical evidence, we propose a hypothesis. The markedly flatter SER trajectory observed in TON eyes, particularly in NLP cases, is consistent with the possibility that severely reduced postretinal visual input attenuates visually guided ocular development during childhood. However, in this retrospective study, longitudinal ocular biometry was not routinely available and treatment exposures varied across patients; therefore, we cannot exclude confounding from optical component changes or treatment-related factors. This hypothesis should be evaluated in future prospective studies with standardized longitudinal biometry and detailed treatment documentation.

From a clinical standpoint, unilateral TON creates a distinctly asymmetric scenario for refractive management in children. The injured eye typically shows minimal or no myopic progression, but its visual function is severely compromised. In contrast, the fellow eye exhibited a substantially greater myopic shift than the TON eye during follow-up and may therefore warrant closer surveillance and timely myopia control when indicated.

This study has several strengths. The unilateral nature of TON allowed each child's fellow eye to serve as an internal control, substantially reducing confounding by interindividual genetic and environmental differences and enabling a within-subject longitudinal comparison of refractive trajectories. All refractive measurements were obtained under cycloplegia, minimizing the influence of accommodative fluctuations and improving the reliability of SER estimates. Furthermore, although AL data were available only for a subset of participants and at nonstandardized time points, the fact that both eyes were measured with the same instrument at each visit enhances the robustness of interocular AL difference estimates, which provide structural context for the refractive findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively strict eligibility criteria resulted in a modest sample size. Second, the available AL data were collected at heterogeneous time points using different biometers across participants, so axial findings should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Third, we cannot rule out the possibility of transient injuries, such as commotio retinae, which may not have been consistently documented but could have occurred in some participants. Finally, we lacked standardized measurements of anterior segment ocular parameters (e.g., corneal or lens-related changes posttrauma) and behavioral data, and there was variability in postinjury treatments across participants. Consequently, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded.

In this study, cycloplegic refraction was performed using 1% tropicamide, a cycloplegic agent with a relatively favorable adverse effect profile, rather than stronger agents such as cyclopentolate or atropine. This choice may leave some residual accommodation, particularly at younger ages, and therefore represents an important limitation of this study. It should also be noted that the adequacy of cycloplegia can be influenced by factors such as age, iris pigmentation, and the timing of refraction after instillation.32 The American Academy of Ophthalmology Pediatric Eye Evaluations PPP provides an age-stratified cycloplegia table: 1% tropicamide for ≥12 years and 1% cyclopentolate or 1% atropine for 1 to 12 years.32 Adding to the evidence base, Sarayar et al.33 conducted a crossover study in brown-irised children aged 9 to 12 years using residual accommodative amplitude (AA) as the primary endpoint and reported no significant differences in residual AA between 1% tropicamide and 1% cyclopentolate at 20, 30, and 40 minutes after instillation, with the cycloplegic effect appearing to plateau by 20 minutes. In addition, evidence from randomized trials in school-aged children, including children with darker irises, suggests that cycloplegic refraction outcomes obtained with tropicamide are generally comparable to those obtained with cyclopentolate.34,35

In summary, in childhood unilateral TON, refractive development in the injured eye diverges from the usual myopic course, showing substantial deceleration, most evident in NLP eyes, whereas the fellow eye continues along a conventional myopic trajectory. These observations are directionally consistent with experimental optic nerve injury models and clinical reports from optic nerve hypoplasia, and they suggest that postretinal pathway integrity may modulate visually guided refractive development. Clinically, unilateral TON often results in profound, permanent visual impairment in the injured eye, making the fellow eye the primary determinant of long-term functional vision. Therefore, the fellow eye warrants long-term surveillance and timely myopia control when indicated to preserve overall visual quality.
