One-step synthesis of a Tb-PET MOF for quantitative visual sensing of Anthrax biomarker
Authors: Can Wang, Peng Hang, Zhe Zhang, Jie Chen, Lili Jiang, Xiaobo Yu, Jianping Li, Liang He, Dai Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNovel strategies for the sensitive and rapid detection of the anthrax biomarker (2,6-pyridinedicarboxylic acid, DPA) are critical for ensuring food safety and preventing the disease. In this study, a novel fluorescent metal-organic framework (MOF) was fabricated via a directly from waste polyethylene terephthalate (PET) plastic. The sensor exhibited excellent pH stability (pH = 2–10), ultrafast response (within 5 s), a low detection limit (0.15 μM), and strong anti-interference performance. Spectroscopic and DFT studies confirmed that the fluorescence enhancement originates from DPA chelation with Tb3+, where DPA acts as an efficient energy-transfer antenna. Building on this mechanism, we demonstrated the immediate visual detection of DPA in tap water, tea, and milk using the MOF solution under UV light. Furthermore, quantitative analysis of DPA in milk was performed via a smartphone-based assay, thereby establishing a rapid, accurate, and practical method for on-site detection.Keywords: Anthrax biomarker, Fluorescent metal-organic framework, Plastic recycling, Density functional theory, Smartphone visual sensing

Introduction

Anthrax is an acute zoonotic infectious disease caused by Bacillus anthracis, primarily affecting livestock but also transmissible to humans through contact with contaminated animal carcasses or uninspected meat products (Cetinkaya et al., 2023; Qu et al., 2019). The high pathogenicity and environmental resilience of Bacillus anthracis classify it as a potential biowarfare agent (Bao et al., 2021). Consequently; 2;6-pyridinedicarboxylic acid (DPA); a characteristic and abundant biomarker specific to its spores; has become a key target for early anthrax detection (Carlson et al., 2019). Various analytical methods have been developed for DPA detection; including electrochemical analysis; high-performance liquid chromatography; and fluorescence techniques (Redan et al., 2022; Wang, Bi, et al., 2024). Among them, fluorescence sensing has garnered particular interest due to its simplicity, high sensitivity, and rapid response (Gou et al., 2024; Xu, Li, Chen, et al., 2024).

Meanwhile, the global consumption of plastic products continues to rise, especially for polyethylene terephthalate (PET), which is widely used in beverage bottles, food containers, and textiles due to its excellent physical and chemical properties (Wang, Li, et al., 2024). Each year; approximately 70 million metric tons of PET are produced worldwide (Diao et al., 2023). However; large volumes of discarded PET are difficult to degrade naturally and gradually break down into microplastics; which have been widely detected in water bodies; soil; and even drinking water; and can accumulate in organisms through the food chain; posing ecological and health risks (Karamat et al., 2024). The worsening plastic pollution problem has driven the search for efficient and environmentally friendly recycling and reuse strategies. Among various recycling methods; chemical recycling has attracted growing attention for its ability to depolymerize plastic waste into high-value chemicals or functional materials. Compared to incineration or physical recycling; chemical recycling offers greater sustainability (Chinglenthoiba et al., 2024; Efimov et al., 2023). However, current technologies often require harsh conditions such as strong acids or bases, high temperatures, and high pressures, resulting in low efficiency and poor environmental compatibility (Zhang et al., 2026). Therefore, developing mild, efficient, and green PET upcycling approaches remains a significant challenge.

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), constructed from metal ions or clusters and organic ligands, feature high surface areas, tunable structures, and diverse functionalities, making them highly promising for applications in catalysis, gas separation, and sensing (Cai, Li, et al., 2025; Cai, Wu, et al., 2025). Among them, lanthanide-based MOFs (Ln-MOFs) are considered ideal fluorescence sensing materials due to their excellent photophysical properties, including long fluorescence lifetimes and large Stokes shifts (Luo et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024). MOFs based on terephthalic acid (BDC) as the organic ligand have been extensively studied, with typical structures such as UiO-66(Zr) (Zhou et al., 2019); MIL-101(Fe) (Li et al., 2019); and MOF-5 (Cheng et al., 2024) widely reported. Notably; the PET backbone contains approximately 85% BDC units; offering intrinsic potential for resource-efficient MOF synthesis (Song et al., 2021).

Herein, we report a highly stable fluorescent MOF (Tb-PET), fabricated in one step from waste PET and Tb3+, which functions as an effective turn-up sensor for DPA detection. The synthetic and sensing approach is outlined in Scheme 1. Tb-PET delivered exceptional performance in sensitivity, stability, selectivity, and resistance to interference. This material was integrated into a smartphone-assisted portable platform, enabling rapid and visual DPA detection. Furthermore, spectroscopic characterization and density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to elucidate the microscopic mechanism behind the fluorescence enhancement. The approach thus establishes a dual-purpose strategy: achieving high-value plastic waste upcycling while providing a practical tool for on-site emergency bio-monitoring and anthrax prevention.

Experiments

Materials

Waste polyethylene terephthalate (PET) bottles were collected from domestic waste and used as the raw material. Terbium chloride hexahydrate (TbCl3·6H2O), 2,6-pyridinedicarboxylic acid (DPA), and carrageenan were obtained from Energy Chemical. N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and ethanol (>99.5%) were purchased from Tianjin Damao. All chemicals and solvents were of analytical grade and used without further purification.

Preparation of Tb-PET

Tb-PET was prepared by a one-pot solvothermal method, employing small pieces (approximately 3 mm) of waste PET bottles directly as the organic ligand source. TbCl3·6H2O (0.5 mM, 187 mg) and PET fragments (0.9 mM, 173 mg) were added to a mixed solvent of 20 mL DMF and 10 mL H2O. After the mixture was stirred thoroughly, the mixture was transferred into a 100 mL Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 160 °C for 12 h. After cooling to room temperature, the colorless transparent crystals were collected by centrifugation (4000 rpm, 5 min) and washed three times with DMF and ethanol. Finally, the product was dried at 70 °C overnight. Yield: 181 mg (95.5% based on TbCl3·6H2O). Particularly, scaling up the PET loading five-fold without increasing Tb3+ yielded no detectable PET residues, suggesting that this approach offers a highly efficient route for PET depolymerization.

Preparation of Tb-PET-based fluorescent composite

First, 2 mg of finely ground Tb-PET was dispersed in 50 mL of deionized water and ultrasonicated for 10 min. The resulting suspension was then heated in a water bath at 80 °C, followed by the addition of 1200 mg of carrageenan with continuous stirring until fully dissolved. Subsequently, 1 mL of the homogeneous mixture was evenly cast onto a circular mold and dried in an oven at 50 °C for 3 h to obtain the Tb-PET fluorescent film.

Characterization

More detailed information about X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS), Fluorescence lifetime decay curves, Mott–Schottky plots experiments, AQE, DFT calculations and other characterization are provided in supporting information.

Fluorescence detection of DPA

3 mg of the prepared Tb-PET was dispersed in 10 mL of deionized water and then ultrasonicated for 10 min to obtain a uniform suspension (0.3 mg/mL). As followed, 0.2 mL of the above suspension was added to 1.8 mL of deionized water, and then different volumes of DPA were added to adjust the DPA concentration in the suspension (0–60 μM). Emission spectra of the Tb-PET suspension (λex = 274 nm) were recorded following each DPA addition until the fluorescence intensity stabilized. To evaluate anti-interference capability and selectivity, we introduced various potential interferents into the Tb-PET suspension, including amino acids (L-arginine, L-histidine, D-arginine, Glycine), common ions (Ca2+, K+, Na+, Cl−, NO3−, CO32−), and structurally similar compounds including 2,5-pyridinedicarboxylic acid (PDC), 2-aminoterephthalic acid (2-ATA), terephthalic acid (TPA), pyrimidine-4,6-dicarboxylic acid (PDCA), and 3,5-pyrazinedicarboxylic acid (PDA). The emission spectrum was then recorded immediately under 274 nm excitation. All tests were repeated three times to ensure the accuracy of the experiment.

Determination of DPA in practical samples

Recovery tests for DPA were performed in tap water, tea, and milk samples to evaluate the practical application of the Tb-PET sensor. The samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane. The resulting filtrates were spiked with varying concentrations of DPA and analyzed using the Tb-PET sensor, following the same procedure established for DPA detection.

Result and discussion

Characterization of Tb-PET

As shown in Fig. 1a, the PXRD pattern of waste derived Tb-PET MOF, with its characteristic peaks at 8.2°, 10.7°, and 18.9° matching the simulated pattern (CCDC: 820774), confirms the successful synthesis of a pure-phase and high crystalline material (Wang et al., 2022). Tb-PET showed robust stability in H2O, EtOH, and DPA solutions, maintaining PXRD patterns consistent with the simulations without decompose (Fig. 1a). To evaluate the chemical stability of Tb-PET, samples were immersed in solutions with pH value of 2–10 by using HCl and NaOH. The samples were allowed to stand for a duration of 24 h. Subsequently, the soaked samples were collected for PXRD tests. As depicted in Fig. 1b, the PXRD patterns clearly demonstrated that Tb-PET maintained its crystalline structure in solutions with a pH range from 2 to 10. This phenomenon revealed the excellent stability of Tb-PET, making it suitable for detection in complicated aqueous environments.

FT-IR spectroscopy was employed to investigate the structural evolution from PET to Tb-PET (Fig. 1c). The surface textural properties were further investigated through nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms at 77 K, as shown in Fig. S1a. The thermal stability of the synthesized waste derived Tb-PET MOF had been evaluated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). As shown in Fig. 1d, the TGA curve revealed two distinct stages of weight loss. The initial weight loss of 4.23% observed up to 414.17 °C corresponds to the removal of residual water and dimethylformamide (DMF) solvents from the framework. The second stage, with a weight loss of approximately 33.66%, occurs between 414.17 °C and 660.74 °C and was mainly due to the collapse of the Tb-PET framework at elevated temperatures (Chen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023). At 800 °C, the remaining mass was 59.6%, corresponding to the residual metal oxides.

Elemental mapping images (Fig. S2) confirmed the presence of C, O, and Tb, with mass percentages of 31.44%, 29.50%, and 39.06%, respectively (Table S2) (Wu, Wang, et al., 2025). The EDS spectrum further showed the homogeneous distribution of these elements throughout the Tb-PET structure, confirming the successful synthesis (Fig. S3).

Luminescent properties of Tb-PET

The luminescent properties of Tb-PET were investigated by fluorescence spectroscopy. Upon excitation at 274 nm, Tb-PET exhibited five characteristic emission peaks corresponding to the transitions of Tb3+: 5D4 → 7F6 (489 nm), 5D4 → 7F5 (545 nm), 5D4 → 7F4 (585 nm), and 5D4 → 7F3 (621 nm), as shown in Figure 2a (Chen, Li, Tu, & Zhang, 2024; Wang et al., 2023). The 5D4 → 7F5 transition, which was highly sensitive to the chemical environment around Tb3+, presented the most intense emission peak at 545 nm (Xu, Li, Jing, et al., 2024).

Tb-PET (a) UV–vis absorption, FL excitation, and FL emission spectra; (b) Fluorescence spectra recorded under excitation from 254 to 294 nm; (c) pH-dependent fluorescence stability; (d) Temperature-dependent fluorescence stability.

To determine the optimal excitation wavelength for DPA detection, fluorescence spectra of Tb-PET suspensions were recorded under excitation wavelengths ranging from 254 to 294 nm. As shown in Fig. 2b, the highest emission intensity was obtained at 274 nm, which was chosen for subsequent experiments (Wang et al., 2020). The effects of pH and temperature were systematically investigated. As shown in Fig. 2c; Tb-PET retained stable fluorescence intensity in a pH range of 3–9 after 24 h of immersion; suggesting minimal influence from pH changes (Qie et al., 2024). Temperature-dependent studies from 10 to 40 °C (Fig. 2d) revealed negligible influence on fluorescence intensity; indicating that detection experiments can be reliably conducted at room temperature (Yu, Liu, et al., 2025).

Sensing of DPA

Given the increasing awareness of the threat posed by Bacillus anthracis, the potential application of Tb-PET for anthrax biomarker detection was further investigated. The fluorescence response of Tb-PET was examined in the presence of various 20 μM substances, such as amino acids (L-arginine, L-histidine, D-arginine, glycine), common ions (Ca2+, K+, Na+, Cl−, NO3−, CO32−), and structurally similar compounds (2,6-pyridinedicarboxylic acid, 2,5-pyridinedicarboxylic acid, 2-aminoterephthalic acid, terephthalic acid, pyrimidine-4,6-dicarboxylic acid) (Wang et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2024). Among these substances, only 2,6-pyridinedicarboxylic acid (DPA) induced a significant fluorescence enhancement (Fig. 3a), demonstrating the high selectivity of Tb-PET as a luminescent probe toward DPA. The response time of the probe was subsequently investigated by monitoring the fluorescence intensity at 545 nm over time after 40 μM DPA addition. As shown in Fig. 3b, the fluorescence signal reached its peak within 5 s, indicating rapid response characteristics and excellent real-time detection capability (Wei et al., 2025).

(a) Fluorescence responses of Tb-PET toward different analytes; (b) The fluorescence intensity of Tb-PET within 3 min after 40 μM DPA addition; (c) FL spectra of Tb-PET after adding 0–60 μM DPA; (d) Correlation curves between I/I0 and DPA concentrations (λex = 274 nm, 0.03 mg/mL Tb-PET deionized water suspension 2 mL).

With the gradual increase in DPA concentration, the fluorescence intensity of Tb-PET at 545 nm increased steadily over the range of 0–60 μM (Fig. 3c). Notably, at 60 μM, the emission intensity was more than five times higher than that of the blank, indicating a significant fluorescence enhancement. Within the linear range of 0–30 μM (Fig. 3d), a good linear relationship was observed between the fluorescence intensity ratio (I/I0) and DPA concentration (R2 = 0.999). The limit of detection (LOD) was calculated to be 0.15 μM based on the equation LOD = 3σ/k, where σ is the standard deviation of ten blank measurements and k is the slope of the calibration curve. This LOD is much lower than the DPA threshold concentration (60 μM) associated with anthrax spore infection (Zhang et al., 2026). The corresponding CIE chromaticity coordinates exhibited a clear and concentration-dependent shift, specifically from (0.2146, 0.3177) at 0 μM to (0.2615, 0.5883) at 60 μM (Fig. S4). Under 254 nm UV illumination, the fluorescence color of the Tb-PET suspension gradually changed from dull green to bright green with 0–60 μM DPA addition (Fig. S5), enabling rapid and intuitive visual identification. These results collectively demonstrate the platform's utility for quantitative visual detection. A comparison of the analytical performance between this study and other reported methods is presented in Table S3. The proposed method demonstrates a low limit of detection (LOD) and a wide linear range, endowing it with the capability for effective trace DPA detection and showing significant potential for the early diagnosis of anthrax infections.

To evaluate the practical applicability of the probe, interference resistance and photostability were assessed. Even in the presence of interferents at 20-fold concentrations relative to DPA (Fig. S6), the fluorescence response remained unaffected, indicating excellent anti-interference performance (Li et al., 2021). In addition to the excellent operational stability; where the fluorescence intensity remained constant for at least 40 min post-DPA addition (Fig. S7a); we further investigated the long-term storage stability of the Tb-PET sensor. As depicted in Fig. S7b; the sensor retained its initial fluorescence intensity with minimal deviation over a period of 4 weeks (monitored at weekly intervals); confirming its robustness for long-term application (Wang et al., 2025). After six consecutive detection cycles, the fluorescence intensity remained nearly unchanged, indicating excellent stability and reusability of the probe (Fig. S8).

Determination of DPA in real samples

To further evaluate the quantitative detection capability of the Tb-PET sensor in practical samples, spiking recovery experiments were performed. Different concentrations of DPA (10, 30, and 60 μM) were added to the pretreated samples. The fluorescence emission intensity at 545 nm was recorded under identical conditions, and the corresponding DPA concentrations were determined according to the established calibration curve. As shown in Table S4, the recoveries of DPA in tap water ranged from 96.1% to 100.8%, with relative standard deviations (RSD) below 1.36%; in tea, recoveries ranged from 98.8% to 101.3%, with RSDs less than 1.36%; and in milk, recoveries ranged from 98.8% to 108.1%, with RSDs below 1.26%. These results indicate that Tb-PET is a reliable and efficient fluorescent probe for detecting DPA in complex aqueous samples.

Sensing mechanism of Tb-PET and DPA

The structural integrity of Tb-PET was maintained upon DPA interaction, as evidenced by the PXRD patterns in Fig. 1a, where both treated and untreated samples match the simulation without showing free DPA peaks. This observation effectively ruled out structural alteration as the origin of the fluorescence enhancement.

To further investigate the sensing mechanism of Tb-PET, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to study the interaction between Tb-PET and DPA. The survey spectrum of Tb-PET confirms the presence of Tb, C, and O elements (Fig. 4a). As shown in Fig. 4b, the Tb 3d spectrum exhibits shifts in the Tb 3d3/2 peak from 1280.1 eV to 1279.3 eV and the Tb 3d5/2 peak from 1244.4 eV to 1244.7 eV. This decrease in binding energy indicates an increased electron density around the Tb3+ centers, which was attributed to coordination with the carboxylate oxygen atoms of DPA (Wang, Wu, et al., 2024). To investigate the interaction mechanism between Tb-PET and DPA, Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was performed. As shown in Fig. 5a, the characteristic absorption peaks of free DPA at 2620.7 and 2546.3 cm−1, assigned to the O—H stretching vibration of the carboxyl group, were significantly weakened or even disappeared upon incorporation into Tb-PET. Concurrently, the strong peak at 1703.7 cm−1, corresponding to the carbonyl (C=O) stretching vibration of DPA, vanished in the Tb-PET/DPA complex. Instead, a new absorption band emerged at 1553.9 cm−1, which can be attributed to the C—O stretching vibration of the carboxylate (-COO) group formed after coordination (Zhang et al., 2018). These consistent spectral changes clearly indicate a substantial alteration in the chemical environment of the DPA carboxyl group, confirming that its oxygen atoms coordinate to the Tb3+ ions and thus verifying the successful binding between DPA and Tb-PET. In addtion, EDS analysis supported the idea of DPA binding to Tb-PET, as evidenced by the appearance of nitrogen (Fig. S9). This result was attributed to the successful adsorption or coordination of the nitrogen-containing DPA molecule, further supporting a chelation or binding interaction.

Subsequently, the UV–vis spectra of Tb-PET before and after the addition of DPA were examined, as shown in Fig. 5b. Upon the introduction of DPA, the emission peak of Tb-PET exhibited a red shift from 308 nm to 318 nm, indicating an interaction between Tb-PET and DPA (Zhang et al., 2024). In addition; the UV–vis absorption spectrum of DPA partially overlaps with the excitation spectrum of Tb-PET; suggesting the possibility of energy resonance between DPA and Tb3+ centers (Chi et al., 2024).

A schematic of the energy transfer process in the Tb-PET system is presented in Fig. 5c, illustrating the proposed mechanism among PET, Tb3+, and DPA. Specifically, the lowest triplet energy level (T1) of the BDC ligand (21,527 cm−1) was mismatched with the 5D4 energy level of Tb3+ (20,430 cm−1), resulting in an energy gap of 1097 cm−1 that does not satisfy Latva's rule and thus hinders effective sensitization (Yu, Deng, et al., 2025). However; upon introduction of DPA; its T1 energy (25;510 cm−1) aligned well with the 5D4 (20;430 cm−1) of Tb3+. This match enabled efficient ligand-to-metal energy transfer; where DPA acted as an energy donor; leading to the characteristic green fluorescence enhancement (Fig. 3c and Fig. 5c) (Wang, Xu, et al., 2024d). On the other hand; DPA had a more vital coordination ability with Tb3+. This coordination interaction enhanced the local coordination environment and rigidity around Tb3+ ions; effectively suppressing non-radiative relaxation pathways and thus significantly amplifying the fluorescence emission intensity. This effect was directly corroborated by fluorescence lifetime analysis. As shown in Fig. 5d; the lifetime of Tb3+ under 274 nm excitation increased from 1.2099 ms to 1.2233 ms upon DPA addition; consistent with a reduction in non-radiative decay and an enhancement in radiative emission (Wang, Xu, et al., 2024d).

To confirm energy transfer mechanisms, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels of Tb-PET were determined experimentally by Mott–Schottky plots and solid-state UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 6a, the LUMO potential of Tb-PET was measured by Mott–Schottky plots to be −0.692 V versus the Ag/AgCl reference electrode. The LUMO position with respect to vacuum level (ELUMOυac = −4.31 eV) was calculated from the equation ELUMOυac = EAg/AgCl + Eref + ESHEvac, where Eref is the work function of the spectrometer used for the measurements (+0.2 eV) and ESHEvac is the energy of the SHE with respect to the vacuum level of the electron with the value of −4.8 eV (Le Huec et al., 2024).

(a) Mott-Schottky plot of Tb-PET; (b) Band gap energy of Tb-PET derived from the Kubelka-Munk function; (c) Graphical representation of ground and excited states of Tb-PET, BDC and DPA.

As shown in Fig. 6b, tauc plots derived from UV − vis DRS data yielded optical band gaps (Eg) of 3.71 eV for Tb-PET. Thus, the HOMO position with respect to vacuum level (EHOMOυac) was calculated as −8.02 eV from the equation EHOMOυac = ELUMOυac − Eg. DFT calculations of BDC, DPA, and Tb-PET were also conducted using the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) and the Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE) method to investigate the absorption processes of each component (Wu, Guo, et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). The DFT calculation results are summarized in Table S5, and the molecular structures along with the corresponding frontier molecular orbitals are shown in Fig. S9. As shown in Fig. 6c and Fig. S9, the electron density in Tb-PET shifted upon photoexcitation, moving from localization around Tb3+ to delocalization over the organic ligand. Additionally, electron transfer from the LUMO level (−3.23 eV) of DPA to the LUMO level (−4.31 eV) of Tb-PET further promotes energy transfer to Tb3+, resulting in amplified Tb3+ fluorescence (Chen, Li, Dou, et al., 2024). This electronic perturbation correlated directly with the observed fluorescence enhancement and provided conclusive evidence that the sensing mechanism was driven by the direct coordination between the Tb3+ center and DPA molecules; thereby facilitating efficient sensitization of Tb3+ luminescence (Guo et al., 2025).

Smartphone-assisted visual sensing of DPA

To enable rapid and visual detection of DPA, a smartphone-based fluorescent sensing platform was developed using Tb-PET carrageenan film as the fluorescent probe (Fig. 7). Under 254 nm UV irradiation, the fluorescence color of the Tb-PET film changed from pale green to bright green with increasing DPA concentrations from 0 to 60 μM, allowing clear visual differentiation by the naked eye. Fluorescence images were captured using a smartphone app, and the RGB values were extracted with the aid of a color recognition application. The ratio of green to blue channel intensity (G/B) was selected as the quantitative parameter (He et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2024). The experimental results showed a good linear correlation between G/B values and DPA concentration (G/B = 0.0138C + 0.967, R2 = 0.995), with a calculated detection limit of 9.13 μM. Although the carrageenan film exhibits a slightly higher LOD compared to the Tb-PET powder (0.15 μM), its value lies in its portability for on-site detection with an LOD well below the typical DPA concentration (60 μM) associated with Bacillus anthracis spores (Tang et al., 2025). This indicates that Tb-PET enables highly sensitive and quantitative detection of DPA. In addition; the Tb-PET carrageenan film is characterized by moderate mechanical stability; evidenced by an elongation at break of 14.5% (Fig. S12); and good hydrophilicity; with a water contact angle of 55° (Fig. S13) (Liu et al., 2026). EDS elemental mapping (Fig. S14) further confirmed the uniform distribution of Tb-PET within the carrageenan matrix in the composite containing 2 mg of Tb-PET and 1200 mg of carrageenan (approximately 0.17 wt%). The method not onlyoffers advantages such as ease of operation; rapid response; and high sensitivity; but also integrates smart devices to achieve digital and visual analysis of fluorescence signals; providing an efficient strategy for on-site DPA detection (Wang, Xu, et al., 2024e).

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a Tb-PET-based fluorescence sensing platform for rapid and sensitive detection of the anthrax biomarker DPA. This system relies on chelation-induced fluorescence enhancement and the antenna effect between Tb3+ and DPA molecules. The sensor exhibited excellent pH stability (pH = 2–10), ultrafast response (with in 5 s), a low detection limit (0.15 μM), and strong anti-interference performance, even in the presence of interferents at 20-fold higher concentrations. Additionally, a smartphone-assisted visual detection method was developed, enabling portable and real-time DPA detection. This work develops an eco-friendly sensor from recycled PET, and in doing so, transforms a sustainable material strategy into a functional tool for rapid, on-site DPA detection, demonstrating considerable potential for practical deployment. Nevertheless, future efforts will focus on precisely quantifying depolymerization yields and employing ratiometric fluorescence strategies to further lower the detection limit, ensuring even greater accuracy for next-generation applications.
