Pollen-like trichomes may reward pollinators of Cypripedium wardii (Orchidaceae)
Authors: Chen-Chen Zheng, Zong-Xin Ren, Shi-Qi Li, Wu-Fan Zhang, Andre Rodrigo Rech, Yun-Dong Gao, Guo-Qiang Wang, Bo Xu, Xin-Fen Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe investigated the function of the white trichome tufts on and within labellum and the pollination mechanism of Cypripedium wardii. We conducted field observations on a large population over four years analyzing its floral epidermis, breeding system, and trichome consumption by its visitors. Flowers bear two tufts of white, clustered, moniliform, trichomes located on the infolded entrance to the labellum, and a third tuft is located on the floor of the labellum. These trichomes are lipid-rich and are collected by bees and consumed by hoverflies. Although self-compatible, these flowers require pollen vectors for fruit set. Trichomes of C. wardii provide a pollen surrogate potentially rewarding polylectic/polyphagic pollinators. This results into an unusually high fruit set (76%–82%) contrasting with a lower-level of fruit set in other deceptive orchids. Our findings provide new insights on the gradation between reward and deception in the evolution of orchid flowers.

Introduction

Darwin disagreed that there are “deceptive blossoms.” He insisted that most insect-pollinated flowers, including orchids, offered rewards to recruit pollinators.1 In fact, it is estimated that 3.7% of known angiosperm species, especially 30%–40% of orchids deceive their pollinators.2,3 Orchids thus provide the most common and dependable models to understand the evolution of pollination-by-deceit.3,4,5 Moreover, Darwin suggested that pollinating insects were rewarded while they foraged on labellum sculptures, hairs, or trichomes in the Lady’s Slipper orchids.1 These structures may act as potential rewards for pollinators;6 however, current knowledge is insufficient to determine whether such rewards are commonly exploited by pollinators in this group of orchids dominated by food-deceptive flowers.

In this study, we investigated the function of the white trichome tufts on and within labellum and the pollination mechanism of Cypripedium wardii Rolfe. We conducted field observations on a large population over four years analyzing its floral epidermis, breeding system, and trichome consumption by its visitors. We found that flowers bear two tufts of white, clustered, moniliform, trichomes located on the infolded entrance to the labellum (Figures 1A–1C), and a third tuft is located on the floor of the labellum (under the stigma; Figure 1C). These trichomes are lipid-rich (Figures 1D–1F), and they are collected by bees and consumed by hoverflies (Figure 2). When these insects forage on the trichomes, they actively climb into the labellum sac, and then escape via one of the two rear exits carrying pollen smears on their thoraces, by doing so, they act as pollen vectors. Although self-compatible, these flowers require pollen vectors for fruit set. Trichomes of C. wardii provide a pollen surrogate potentially rewarding polylectic/polyphagic pollinators. This results into an unusually high fruit set (76%–82%) contrasting with a lower-level of fruit set in other deceptive orchids. Results

Breeding system

We conducted hand-manipulated cross- and self-pollination to test the breeding system of C. wardii. The results showed that bagged flowers set no fruit. Hand-manipulated cross-pollinated flowers show a 96% rate of flowers turning into fruits in 2019 and 100% in 2020. Hand-manipulated self-pollinated flowers showed a similar high rate of fruit set of 89% in 2019 and 96% in 2020 (Table 1). Natural rates of fruit set in flowers that were not bagged and exposed to resident insects was 82% in 2019 and 76% in 2020. In 2019, there was no significant difference of fruit sets among cross, self, and natural pollination treatments (χ2 = 3.499, p = 0.174; χ2 test), while in 2020, a significant difference was detected for three treatments (χ2 = 13.485, p = 0.001) due to a low fruit set in 2020. Pollen vectors

In the genus Cypripedium, flowers attract appropriately sized pollinators that become trapped temporarily within the saccate labellum (Figure 1A). These insects are forced to contact reproductive organs in order to escape and disperse pollen smears to subsequent flowers.7 In our observation, we found that solitary bees and hoverflies visited flowers of C. wardii during the daytime. We recorded 277 insect visits, of which 192 were by bees and 87 by hoverflies, over 172 h of diurnal observations (Figure S1, Table S2). Over four years, we collected 98 specimens of bee and hoverfly dominated by females while they visited C. wardii flowers, representing seven bee species in the families Apidae and Halictidae (e.g., Figures 2B and 2C and Video S1), as well as five species in the fly family Syrphidae (Figures 2E–2G, Video S2, and Table 2). Specifically, we recorded 25 visitors wearing fresh pollen smears after exiting the flower while 12 insects arriving at the orchid flowers already wore old, eroded smears indicating previous visits to flowers of the same species (e.g., Figures 2C and 2G). These flower visitors always attempted to forage first on the trichome tufts distributed on the outer, enfolding margins of the labellum (Figures 1A–1C). Upon actively climbing into the labellum sac, they continued to forage on the tuft located directly on the floor under the stigma (Figure 1C). Bees were also observed flying directly to the central tuft on the labellum floor (e.g., Figure 2C). Bees were observed scraping rim and central tufts with their forelegs and/or biting trichomes with their mandibles (e.g., Videos S3 and S4). Hoverflies also flew to the enfolded rim of the central opening, and landed and extended their proboscides onto either of the paired tufts (e.g., Videos S5, S6, and S7, and Figure 2D). Some then entered the labellum to probe the floor tuft (e.g., Video S8). We recorded insects approaching the flowers, entering the labellum sac through the central opening and escaping via one of the two rear exits 35 times (32 bees and 3 flies; Table S2). We also observed five bees escaping the labellum by flying or climbing out of the sac via the central opening through which they entered. Three small bees entered the labellum via one of the two rear exits. Some visitors were observed visiting the same flower more than once and/or visiting a second flower after escaping from the first (e.g., Videos S1 and S10). Bees and hoverflies with pollen smears on their thoraces rested on nearby leaves (Figure 2H). Bee and hoverfly species that visited C. wardii were also recorded in other co-blooming flowers. Plant species that shared floral visitors with C. wardii included the white with pink-centered flowers of Leptodermis potaninii Batalin (Rubiaceae), the blue Gentiana rubicunda Franch (Gentianaceae; Figure 2I), and the yellow Phtheirospermum tenuisectum Bur. et Franch (Orobanchaceae). Moreover, we never found eggs of hoverflies on or in the flowers of C. wardii or any of the co-blooming species.

Morphology and contents of the white trichomes

Observations and dissections found no evidence of nectar secretion in fresh flowers of C. wardii. SEM and light microscopy images showed that the white trichome tufts on the labellum (Figures 1A and 1C) consisted of many multicellular but moniliform hairs (Figures 1D–1F) corresponding to the previous descriptions of pseudopollen in other orchid species.8,9,10,11 Under light microscopy, trichome cells contained large vacuoles (Figure 1E). These cells tested positively for lipids (Figure 1F) but not for protein or starch.

Trichomes on and within insect bodies

We examined pollen loads and gut content of 12 bees (randomly selected 1–4 specimens for each species) and five hoverflies collected on C. wardii. We found that bee scopae (Halictidae) or corbiculae (Apidae) of all bee specimens contained the pollen grains of co-blooming plants (e.g., Gentiana rubicunda and Leptodermis potaninii) mixed with cells derived from trichomes of C. wardii (e.g., Figures 3A–3C). While the frequencies of pollen morphotypes on different bees differed, the presence of trichome cells remained consistent (Figure S2, Table S3). There were 21.5 ± 19.8 (mean ± SD, N = 12) trichome cells within an area of 100 × 100 μm on the hind leg of bees. We also observed 1–3 trichome fragments on mouthparts (e.g., Figure 3D) in an area of 100 × 100 μm for four specimens of Lasioglossum sinicum, but we did not find trichome fragments in the guts of all bee specimens. We did not find trichome cells on the bodies of hoverflies, but these trichome cells were found in the guts of all five, dissected specimens accompanied by true pollen exines (e.g., Figure 3E). Visitor responses to the removal of trichomes on the outer surface of the labellum

Over a period of 30 h of observation, we did observe bees to visit flowers deprived of their white trichomes on the outer surface of the labellum (Figure 1A, 2B and 2C, and Table S4). Bees visiting shaved flowers simply flew directly into the labellum sac, and then they collected trichomes actively from the inner tuft located under the stigma (e.g., Videos S9 and S10). We observed eight continuous bee visits to flowers lacking the two external trichome tufts (e.g., Video S10). They re-visited the same shaved flower and/or they visited a second flower of C. wardii after escaping from the shaved flower. In total, we observed 49 bees and 10 hoverflies visiting shaved flowers, among them 32 bees entering labella (Table S4). There were no obvious differences between bee-visiting frequencies to flowers with (1.77 times per hour, N = 53) and without (1.63 visits per hour, N = 49) trichome tufts on the outer surface of the labellum in 2020.

Discussion

The role of trichomes

Despite their self-compatibility, flowers of C. wardii are pollinator dependent. They may reward insects from three families in two orders, with lipid-rich pseudopollen requiring active collection by bees and eating by flies. As trichome cells of C. wardii contain at least one class of nutrients, they could be edible for flies; however, we should be careful to say it is also edible for bees, since we did not find trichome fragments in the bee guts. Meanwhile, it is most likely that bees feed what they collected to larvae and we do not know the consequences of feeding bee larvae with a diet of pseudopollens. When the foraging behavior of the insects is combined with analyses of their pollen loads and gut content, we support Darwin’s original hypothesis that some Cypripedium species offer rewards, although not the Cypripedium species Darwin examined during his lifetime.1 Darwin believed that insects could be attracted to trichomes “secreting little drops of slightly viscid fluid.”1 The pollination mechanism in this species is not indicative of classic pollination by deceit as defined by Johnson and Schiestl;3 however, we also cannot exclude the possibility of deceptive pollination in this system, since nutrients from trichome cells are not comparable with pollen grains of other angiosperms. If it is the case, this should be a case of pollen mimicry. Furthermore, it is not the generalized model of food deceit observed in most Cypripedium species.12 Importantly, Jiang et al.6 reported that trichome tufts on the surface of exposed labellum lobes of C. subtropicum, as observed by S.C. Chen & K.Y. Lang, were fed on by hoverflies. They showed that hoverflies pollinated C. subtropicum as a consequence of feeding on the external trichome tufts prior to falling into the saccate labellum. They interpreted volatiles in the orchid’s scent and the presence of orchid trichomes in the tongues of flies as evidence that the flower mimics aphid colonies, on which female hoverflies oviposit and drink honeydew secretions of aphids, the prey of hoverfly’s larvae. In contrast, in C. wardii, trichomes are not just on the outer of labellum but also on the labellum floor; accordingly, bees and hoverflies feed on trichomes and actively climb into labellum. It seems that flowers do not exploit generalist insects by other deception as we did not find other behaviors, i.e., egg laying of flies in the flower. This represents a novel trapping pollination mechanism involving potential rewarded trichomes acting as attractant and trap device that traps pollinator and then guides their navigation internally to achieve reproductive success through precise pollen placement.

Among animal-pollinated plants with rewarding flowers, nectar and pollen are the most common resources collected by bees and consumed directly by hoverflies,13 but only a few orchid species offer edible, granular pseudopollen.4 Furthermore, when pollen is the sole floral reward, plants always face a dilemma regarding its allocation for reproduction versus its loss due to its collection and consumption. Lunau et al.14,15 refers to this trade-off as the pollen dilemma resulting in the evolution of multiple strategies. Partial solutions include a division of labor in heterantherous flowers,10 automimicry,16 generalized food deception, and full pollen/anther mimesis.14 The last of these evolutionary trends has been described repeatedly in the Orchidaceae,11,16,17 but that does not mean that these mimetic structures always lack nutrition. The flowers of some species in the monandrous genera Dendrobium, Maxillaria sl, Mycaranthes, Polystachya, and C. subtropicum in the diandrous genus Cypripedium produce pollen surrogates.6,9,10,11,18 Now we add one more species in Cypripedium that produces pollen surrogates. These pollen surrogates are usually composed of multicellular moniliform trichomes offering different proportions of nutritious cells. This kind of new potential reward is often referred to as pseudopollen or farina.6,9,10,11,18 Most reports of orchid pseudopollen focus only on its structure, cell contents, and development e.g., Davies et al.18 There are some field observations showing that visiting bees or syrphids (hoverflies) collect/eat pollen-like tissue on the labellum.19,20,21,22,23,24 Some studies propose that pseudopollen is not nutritious and a false reward.6,9,10 Others suggest the opposite and refer to these structures as edible trichomes.9,18,22

Our field observations and breeding system experiments show that C. wardii, unlike most other reported Chinese Cypripedium species, e.g., C. fargesii, Ren et al.11 and C. lichiangense, Zheng et al.,25 is not pollinator-limited.26 The possible reward offered by C. wardii appears sufficient to attract pollinators and ensure higher levels of fruit set. Trichome cells in C. wardii contribute to its fruit set just as floral nectar secretions in other orchid species.5 The trichome tufts locations on and in the labellum are intrinsic to insect activity that when the outer tufts are removed it causes a decline in visitations by one of the two sets of pollinators.

The role of olfactory cues

None of the volatiles identified in the scent of C. wardii are unique to this orchid species since they are manufactured by flowers belonging to a broad range of seed plants.27 The variable presence of alpha-pinene is unexpected. While it appears in trace amounts in other orchid scents, including the cypripedioid genus Paphiopedilum, it is most commonly associated with the leaves and cones of conifers. In contrast, the domination of benzaldehyde and benzyl alcohol comprises a broader range of flowers pollinated by an equally broad range of pollen-eating/collecting insects.28,29,30,31,32

While there is no evidence that there is a generalist floral odor for pollen,33,34 the lipophilous pollen coats on the exines of many animal-pollinated species contain benzaldehyde and/or benzyl alcohol.30,35,36,37,38 Our results strongly suggest that these two compounds combine as an olfactory cue attracting pollen-eating/collecting foragers to the trichomes surrogates. Therefore, the pollination strategy of C. wardii appears transitional between deception and true reward. This mode of pollination is also found in its allied species, C. subtropicum offering trichomes while attracting hoverflies via aphid mimicry using visual and chemical signals.6

More pseudopollen within Cypripedium

Darwin first proposed edible trichomes lining the interior of the labellum of C. parviflorum Salisb. (syn. C. calceolus L.).1 To our knowledge, this hypothesis was not well tested by plant histologists (but see Jiang et al.6). Edens-Meier et al.39,40 also found plant hairs on the bodies and in the pollen-collecting hind legs of bees collected on two varieties of C. parviflorum and between the claws of bee pollinators of C. kentuckiense, but they were not secretory or oil containing hammer glands (Edens-Meier, unpublished data). In C. subtropicum, trichome tufts are only found on the surface of exposed labellum lobes, and there is no trichome tufts in the labellum; hoverflies feed on these trichomes, then fall down into the labellum.6

Cypripedium wardii is a sister species of C. subtropicum according to the phylogeny of Liao et al.41 The potential rewarding function of hairs based on their nutrients6,23 as we found in C. wardii may be more extensive within the genus in China and abroad than anticipated. Bees in the genus Lasioglossum species (Halictidae) performed similar foraging behaviors on flowers of C. plectrochilum Franch., which has trichomes around the infolded margins of its labellum.42 For bees, lipids, such as sterol,43 are crucial resources for their development, survival, and reproduction,44,45 and pollen, as usual, is their main resource for these nutrients.46 Likewise, lipid-containing pollen remains the essential food for pregnant hoverflies.47,48 Future research into this topic must ask whether certain orchid species are either programmed to develop pseudopollen without nutrients or a pseudopollen with nutrients.

Conclusions

Our study confirms Darwin’s hypothesis that epidermal vestiture in some Cypripedium species provide pollinators with rewards in exchange for pollen dispersal and deposition. By testing Darwin’s hypothesis, we find that some taxa in Cypripedium solve the reproductive dilemma of pollenivory through the production of previously unknown pollen-like floral surrogates. As this pseudopollen is nutritious, its presence in the flowers of C. wardii may be regarded as a case of potential rewarding system, and not deceptive pollination like most other species in the genus. This finding supports that the evolution of pseudopollen in plants varies from pure deception to true reward. This study also highlights that how little we know about floral rewards and pollination systems even in a well-studied plant group. These plants, previously unknown to provide floral resources, now could be viewed as important in the maintenance of different pollinator groups.

Limitations of the study

There are two limitations in this study. First, we cannot exclude the possibility of deceptive pollination, i.e., pollen mimicry in this system. Future research should investigate the reflectance color patterns of flowers to test if it is similar to classical pollen-offering flowers and how it is perceived in bee and fly visions. Second, we do not know what the consequences of feeding bee larvae a diet of pseudopollen are. In the absence of experimentation to test if nutrients from the trichomes can be metabolized and benefit the pollinator, it remains an open question.

Experimental model and subject details

Cypripedium wardii is an endangered species endemic to the Hengduan Mountains of southwestern China.50 This is a perennial herb with terrestrial habit and each flowering plant produces 1 flower/scape, rarely 2–3 flowers. The flowers are small, white or creamy-white with reddish-purple spots inside and outside the labellum and on the outside around the rim of its central entrance (Figures 1A and 1B). The labellum wears a pair of white, trichome tufts along the rim (Figures 1A–1C), a trait not described by Chen & Cribb.50

Field observatioin and experiments were conducted in a site near Heba village, Kangding City, Sichuan Province, southwestern China. A large population of C. wardii with more than 800 flowering plants was found in an area of 15,540 m2. This site was on a limestone mountain with a secondary forest of deciduous broad-leaved trees and conifers at an elevation of 2,200–2,300 m. There were 64 co-flowering species (Table S1). The dominant co-flowering species under the canopy were Berberis wilsoniae Hemsl., Calanthe davidii Franch., Campylotropis polyantha (Franch.) Schindl., Cypripedium lichiangense S.C. Chen & P.J. Cribb, Cotoneaster horizontalis Decne, Gentiana rubicunda Franch, and Leptodermis potaninii Batalin. Vouchers of co-occurring plants were deposited in the Herbarium of the Chengdu Institute of Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (CDBI), Chengdu, China. Over four years (2019-2022) of observations, flowers of Cypripedium wardii bloomed from early June to mid-July. Typically, a single flower opened its bud and the labellum collapsed after a period of 7 ± 3 (Mean ± SD, n = 158) days.

Method details

Breeding system experiments

To determine whether pollinators were necessary for C. wardii to fruit, we performed hand pollination experiments in 2019 and 2020. In 2019, we marked flowers (n = 333), and subdivided these flowers into three treatment categories before the buds opened. (1) self-pollinated flowers (n = 19), (2) cross-pollinated flowers (n = 23), and (3) naturally pollinated (insect-mediated) flowers (n = 291). We removed the labellum of self-pollinated flowers using a razor blade and hand-pollinated them with the pollen from their own anthers. For the cross-pollinated flowers we also removed the labellum and hand-pollinated them with the adhesive pollen from flowers located > five meters away. Only one flower on each plant received one of the three treatments. We checked the fruit set from each treatment in mid-October.

In 2020, we repeated the same breeding system experiments as in 2019, but we also added a bagged control treatment (n = 38) to determine the frequency of self-pollination (autogamy). For each bagged control, flower buds were covered and tied using organza bags until the end of their flowering periods. Fruits of treated flowers were counted in mid-October. We compared fruit set among breeding system treatments for each year. Since flowers of bagged control produced no fruits, we excluded this treatment in the comparison. We compared the difference of fruit sets of self-, cross-pollination and natural pollination using the Chi-Squared (χ2) test by SPSS Pro software.49 If significant differences were detected, we also used χ2 tests to do pair-wise post hoc differences between any two treatments.

Pollinator observation

Floral visitors were observed over four flowering seasons (2019 -2022), on sunny days, between 9:00–18:00 in 2019, 2021 and 2022 and from 10:00–16:00 in 2020. We waited for nocturnal visitors over three nights from 19:00–24:00 in 2020 without success and discontinued the procedure. In total, we observed flowers for a total of 187 hours (172 h daytime + 15 h night). We recorded the behaviour of floral visitors, including their approach or flight followed by their landing, entering, and escape from the flower.27 To identify floral visitors, we first captured them with an insect net and euthanized them with fumes of ethyl acetate. We also recorded whether the pollinators visited other co-flowering species before/after visiting the flowers of C. wardii. Insect specimens were sent to entomologists for identification, and deposited in the Herbarium of the Chengdu Institute of Biology in 2019 (see above), and in the State Key Laboratory of Systematic and Evolutionary Botany, Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China, in 2021 and 2022.

Analysis of floral odors

A headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) method was used to collect volatile odours outdoors in 2022. A flower of C. wardii was enclosed in a polyethylene terephthalate oven bag (25 × 38 cm, Wrapok). A thermally cleaned 85 μm carboxen/polydimethylsiloxane (CAR/PDMS) SPME fiber (57334-U; Supelco Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was then inserted into the bag for one hour, then the sample was injected into the GC-MS system. The air control was collected from an empty oven bag placed in close proximity to the flower. In total, we sampled three flowers from three plants growing more than five meters apart. Following Zhang et al.,51 the volatiles were analyzed on an Agilent 7890B Gas Chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a HP-5MS GC column (Agilent Technologies) coupled to a 5975C mass spectrometry (MS) detector. The temperature of the GC injector was set to 280 °C and the fiber was thermally desorbed for 1 min. The oven temperature was programmed to 40°C for 2 min, and heated to 300°C at 12 °C/min for 3 min. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow of 1 mL/min in the column. The electron energy was 70 eV at 230°C. Kovàts indices were calculated for the separated components relative to a C7-C30 n-alkane mixture (49451-U; Supelco Sigma-Aldrich). Compounds were analyzed by comparing their spectra with those recorded in the NIST library (NIST17), and confirmed by reported linear retention index (Kovàts) values in the literature. We also conducted dynamic headspace adsorption techniques with adsorbent tubes containing a thin layer of activated charcoal (CLSA, 1.5 mg, Granicher and Quartero) to collect floral volatile odor in 2022 (see Brodmann et al.52 and Zhang53).

Analysis of morphology and contents of the white trichomes

To examine floral architecture and epidermal ornamentation, we collected flowers of C. wardii and placed them in 70% alcohol: acetic acid: formaldehyde (8:1:1). Using these preserved specimens, we observed the distribution of trichomes under a light microscope (Olympus BX43F, Olympus, Japan). We also observed trichomes under a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Phenom Pro, Netherlands) following dehydration with graded ethanol–isoamyl acetate, followed by plating samples with gold palladium and observing them at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV.11

To test whether trichome cells contained nutrients we conducted histochemical tests for proteins, lipids, and starches, following Davies et al.8 and Dafni et al.54 This required additional collections of fresh flowers. We stored them at 4°C and then removed some trichomes from the labellum. They were placed on a microscope slide, and stained with droplets of different solutions before applying cover slips and observing cellular responses under a light microscope (Olympus BX43F, Olympus, Japan). We used a purple reaction product with 0.2% (w/v) aqueous ninhydrin (indane-1,2,3-trione hydrate) solution for protein, a blue reaction product with iodine–potassium iodide (IKI) for starch and an orange reaction product with a saturated solution of Sudan III in 70% (v/v) ethanol for lipids.

Identification of trichomes from exoskeletons and guts of flower visitors

To determine whether pollinators collected and/or ate the tufts of labellum trichomes, we examined the pollen loads and gut content of the main foragers using dried insect specimens under the SEM as prepared above. We randomly selected 1-4 specimens for each bee species and 1-2 specimens for each hoverfly species (two specimens for Baccha maculata) to examine pollen loads and gut content. In total twelve bees and five hoverflies were checked. For each bee specimen, we took one hind leg to observe under SEM to check the presence of trichome cells. We counted number of pollen grains and trichome cells within a randomly selected area of 100 × 100 μm using the SEM photos taken for each bee specimen. We also counted the number of trichome cells of gut of flies within an area of 100 × 100 μm. To determine whether foragers of C. wardii consumed pollen, we constructed a pollen library of co-flowering species by harvesting and drying their pollen for later identification under the SEM.

Responses of insect visitors to the removal of the paired trichomes tufts on the labellum rim

To test the putative function(s) of the white trichomes in the pollination process, we used razor blades to remove the paired trichome tufts on the labellum rims of 33 tagged flowers. We observed the behaviors of visitors to these scalped flowers from 10:00–16:00, in 2020. Because trichome removal cause scars on the flower, to avoid damage effect on pollinator visitation and behavior, we conducted the removal experiment in the morning, and did the observation at the same day. We compared the behaviors of these foragers to foragers observed visiting ∼100 flowers of unmanipulated controls. We checked fruit set for both shaved and unmanipulated flowers at the end of the fruit season in mid-October. We evaluated the attractiveness of trichome tufts based on the frequencies of insect visits and fruit set ratios between shaved flowers and unmanipulated controls using the χ2 tests.
