Probiotic efficacy of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 from Tibetan pigs in metabolic syndrome: modulation of gut microbiota and metabolic in sows and suckling piglets
Authors: Wangyuan Yao, Haitao Du, Md F Kulyar, Huachun Pan, Hongkai Ren, Qingqing Luo, Zeeshan Ahmad Bhutta, Suozhu Liu, Rendong Fang, Jiakui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic syndrome disrupts metabolic resilience in periparturient sows and compromises piglet growth. As intestinal microbes govern host energy homeostasis, microbiome‐directed feed additives represent a practical solution. We therefore evaluated the Tibetan‑pig isolate Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106, previously validated in weanlings for its capacity to alleviate sow‐associated metabolic syndrome.ResultsIn a 43‑day trial (20 sows per group), dietary TL106 (5 × 109 CFU kg–1) increased digestibility of crude fiber (+ 12.5%, p < 0.05) and crude fat (+ 9.3%, p < 0.01), lowered serum IL‑1β (− 34%) and TNF‑α (− 28%), and boosted antioxidant enzymes and immunoglobulins (all p < 0.05). Litter performance improved, with a two‑thirds reduction in diarrhea and heavier piglets at 21 days (+ 15%, aggregate n = 300). Multi‑omics profiling revealed higher cecal α‑diversity, enrichment of butyrate‑producing Ruminococcus and Butyricicoccus (log2C 2.1 and 1.8; FDR < 0.05), and activation of histidine‑metabolism and ABC‑transporter pathways (q ≤ 0.03) in piglets, while pathways for amino‑acid biosynthesis, lipid utilization, and steroidogenesis were favored in sows.ConclusionsBacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 simultaneously enhanced maternal metabolic health and neonatal development by reshaping gut microbiota and host metabolism, positioning it as a micro‑ecological tool for managing metabolic syndrome in Landrace × Yorkshire sows and Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire suckling piglets. Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MS) in periparturient sows represents a multifactorial metabolic disorder occurring during gestation and lactation, characterized by diminished production performance, systemic low-grade inflammation, and progressive oxidative stress [1]. The pathogenesis of this condition is attributed to a complex interplay of factors, including energy imbalance (defined as a mismatch between nutrient supply and the greatly heightened metabolic demands of late gestation and early lactation, rather than a reduction in feed intake), together with endocrine dysregulation, chronic inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress [2]. This intricate pathophysiology severely compromises sow growth performance and reproductive efficiency and poses significant threats to neonatal piglet health, resulting in substantial economic losses to the global swine industry [3]. Under the blueprint of comprehensive antibiotic reduction and prohibition, there is an urgent need to develop innovative, sustainable, and eco-friendly strategies to improve the metabolic health of periparturient sows and their offspring, thereby enhancing overall production efficiency and animal welfare in modern swine operations.

The pathogenesis of metabolic syndrome is closely associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis, elevated production of harmful microbial metabolites, and compromised intestinal barrier function [4, 5]. Mechanistically, the loss of butyrate-producing taxa, together with an expansion of endotoxin-generating Proteobacteria, intensifies oxidative stress, sustains low-grade inflammation, and compromises insulin signaling in periparturient sows. During different periods of normal pregnancy and lactation, significant alterations occur in the structural composition of sow gut microbiota, characterized by reduced microbial diversity, increased abundance of pro-inflammatory bacterial taxa, and decreased populations of anti-inflammatory bacteria [6]. These microbial shifts are accompanied by substantial changes in microbial metabolite profiles, which play a pivotal role in modulating intestinal immune barrier function and local inflammatory responses in sows. Furthermore, the impact of gut microbiota on sow’s MS may be mediated through reduced butyrate production due to microbial dysbiosis and elevated plasma endotoxin levels [7]. Notably, the sow’s gut microbiota is a critical source for establishing both milk microbiota and neonatal piglet gut microbiota, significantly influencing the development of intestinal barrier integrity and immune competence in piglets [8]. Consequently, the gut microbiota of sows not only plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of MS but serves as a key determinant in enhancing growth performance and disease resistance in their offspring, highlighting its dual significance in sow and piglet health.

Bacillus amyloliquefaciens (BA), originally isolated from plant environments, has gained significant attention due to its capacity to produce diverse antimicrobial metabolites during growth, effectively inhibiting both fungal and bacterial activity [9]. Recognized as plant growth-promoting microorganisms with minimal adverse effects, it has been extensively studied as a key component in agricultural biofertilizers and biocontrol agents [10]. Following its designation as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for use in food supplements, research on BA as a feed additive in animal nutrition and metabolic disease management has emerged as a prominent research focus. Recent investigations have demonstrated that Bacillus amyloliquefaciens B10 effectively mitigates aflatoxin-induced cecal inflammation in murine models through modulation of Ruminococcus abundance and synergistic regulation of gut microbiota [11]. In aquaculture systems, dietary supplementation with B. amyloliquefaciens R8 has enhanced metabolic enzyme activity in tilapia, improving both growth performance and immunity against Aeromonas hydrophila infection [12]. In poultry production, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL has demonstrated significant effects on gut microbiota composition, particularly in increasing the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio in broilers, thereby promoting animal health and growth performance while providing compelling evidence for antibiotic-free production systems [13].

Furthermore, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens US573 has exhibited potential as a direct-fed microbial through its secretion of xylanase, β-glucanase, and amylase activities, effectively neutralizing anti-nutritional factors and optimizing nutrient utilization [14]. Notably, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens H57 has demonstrated the ability to modulate native rumen microbial community structure and function in ruminants, suggesting indirect mechanisms for enhancing animal performance [15]. Collectively, these findings highlight the substantial regulatory potential of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens in improving animal production performance, enhancing immune function, and modulating gut microbiota, warranting further exploration in animal nutrition and metabolic disease management.

Our previous investigations have demonstrated that Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 from Tibetan pigs (Linzhi, Tibet) exhibits significant probiotic properties in weaned piglets through gut microbiota modulation, leading to enhanced growth performance, improved immune function, and optimized intestinal health [16]. Although previous studies have investigated Bacillus amyloliquefaciens in monogastric animals, no study has reported yet the effect of maternal probiotic administration on the neonatal gut microbiota and digestive metabolome. We hypothesize that TL106 specifically modulates maternal microbial transmission, thus enhancing metabolic syndrome through gut microbiota and metabolite cross exchange. Building upon these findings, the current study employed TL106 as a dietary microbial supplement to comprehensively assess its effects on production performance, immune status, and intestinal health in periparturient Landrace × Yorkshire sows and their Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire suckling piglets. Furthermore, we aimed to elucidate the therapeutic potential of TL106 in mitigating metabolic syndrome and to uncover the underlying probiotic mechanisms through integrated microbiome and metabolomics analyses. This study provides novel theoretical insights and identifies potential intervention targets for managing metabolic syndrome and related metabolic disorders in swine production systems.

Materials and methods

Study design and husbandry management

All procedures were approved and performed according to the guidelines established by the Laboratory Animals Research Centre of Hubei, Liaoning, and Tibet in the People’s Republic of China and were reviewed and authorized by the Ethics Committee of Huazhong Agricultural University, China (Permit number: 4200695757). All animal experiments complied with the relevant regulations outlined in the Proclamation of the Standing Committee of Hubei People’s Congress (PSCH No. 5), China. The Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 strain, isolated from fecal samples of Tibetan pigs, was deposited and preserved in the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC) under accession number CGMCC 20391. This study was conducted at the northeast facility of Yangxiang Co., Ltd., utilizing Landrace × Yorkshire sows and Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire suckling piglets. A total of 40 multiparous sows (parity 2–3) at 90 days of gestation were randomly allocated into two experimental groups (control and probiotic), with 20 sows per group. The sample size was calculated using G*Power (v3.1) with α, β, and effect size values (0.05, 0.2, and 1.2). This calculation was applied to the prespecified growth-performance and digestibility outcomes; metabolomic and 16S omics comparisons were treated as exploratory, not formally powered, and their p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR threshold < 0.1). The control group received a standard full-price diet formulated according to the US NRC (2012) feeding standards, consisting of corn-soybean meal-dairy rations, with ad libitum access to drinking water (Table 1). The probiotic group received the same basal diet supplemented with 5 × 109 CFU/kg Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL 106 (Fig. 1). Daily growth performance parameters were recorded for both sows and piglets throughout the 43-day experimental period.

Gestating sows were housed in individual gestation stalls (0.6 × 2.1 m) and transferred to farrowing pens (2 × 2.1 m) 5 days before the expected farrowing date. Throughout the trial, barn temperature was actively regulated at 20–24 °C with 55–65% relative humidity during gestation, rising to 24–28 °C in the farrowing rooms; piglet creep areas were kept at 30–32 °C. Mechanical ventilation and evaporative cooling panels maintained these set points, and a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle was applied. The feeding regimen was implemented: (1) During the gestation period (from day 90 of pregnancy until farrowing), sows were fed once daily at 8:00 am. For the probiotic group, the TL106 solution was prepared one hour before feeding by thoroughly mixing it with the basal diet at a concentration of 0.1% (v/w) of the total feed quantity. Each sow received 2.8 kg of the prepared feed daily. (2) During the lactation period (21 days post-farrowing), sows were fed twice daily (morning and evening), with daily trough cleaning to maintain optimal hygiene conditions.

Sample collection and processing

At the end of the experimental period, six suckling piglets from each group were randomly selected for euthanasia and sample collection. Venous blood (10 mL) was collected from each piglet and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to isolate serum, which was subsequently stored at −20 °C for further analysis. Tissue samples, including the spleen, lymph nodes, duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and cecum, were divided into two portions: one portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China) for histopathological examination. In contrast, the other portion was flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for molecular and biochemical analyses. Additionally, cecal contents from the piglets and fecal samples from the sows were aseptically collected, placed in sterile EP tubes, and immediately stored at −80 °C to preserve microbial integrity for subsequent microbiome and metabolomic studies. All procedures were conducted under strict aseptic conditions to prevent contamination and ensure sample quality.

Determination of nutrient apparent digestibility

Following 108 days of gestation and 15 days of lactation, fresh fecal samples were collected daily from each sow. For each group, 0.5–1 kg of fecal samples were collected per day over three consecutive days and preserved with 10% hydrochloric acid for nitrogen fixation. Hydrochloric acid-insoluble ash (AIA) was utilized as an endogenous indicator to assess nutrient digestibility. The contents of AIA, crude protein (CP), crude fat (EE), crude fiber (CF), crude ash (ash), calcium (Ca), and phosphorus (P) in both feed and fecal samples were determined according to the analytical methods recommended by the national standards. The apparent digestibility of each nutrient was calculated using the following formula:

Apparent digestibility of a nutrient (%) = 100 − (A1/A2) × (B2/B1) × 100

where:

A1: Content of the endogenous indicator (AIA) in the diet (%)

A1: Content of the endogenous indicator (AIA) in the diet (%)

A2: Content of the endogenous indicator (AIA) in the feces (%)

A2: Content of the endogenous indicator (AIA) in the feces (%)

B1: Nutrient content in the feces (%)

B1: Nutrient content in the feces (%)

B2: Nutrient content in the diet (%)

B2: Nutrient content in the diet (%)

This method ensures accurate evaluation of nutrient utilization efficiency, providing critical insights into the dietary impact on sow metabolism and overall nutritional status during gestation and lactation. All analyses were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and reliability of the results.

Measurement of inflammatory factors, lactic acid, endotoxin, and immune function markers

Blood samples were collected from six randomly selected gestating sows at 108 days of gestation, six lactating sows at 21 days postpartum, and six suckling piglets at 21 days of age from each experimental group. Approximately 10 mL of whole blood was drawn from each animal and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to isolate serum. The serum samples were analyzed for key inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), as well as metabolic markers such as lactic acid (D-lactate) and endotoxin (ET), using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Serum insulin concentration following a 12-h fasting period was quantified using a porcine insulin ELISA kit (Solarbio, China). Meanwhile, serum levels of blood glucose and triglyceride (TG) were measured according to the manufacturer’s protocols (Solarbio, China). Additionally, serum levels of immunoglobulins, including immunoglobulin A (IgA), immunoglobulin G (IgG), and immunoglobulin M (IgM), were quantified using corresponding ELISA kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

The levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) in the spleen and mesenteric lymph nodes were determined by ELISA. Briefly, the tissues were homogenized on ice in lysis buffer. The homogenates were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. Subsequent ELISA procedures were performed strictly in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions for each commercial kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The cytokine levels were normalized to the total protein concentration and expressed as picograms per milliliter of protein (pg/mL).

All assays were duplicated to ensure accuracy and reproducibility, and results were normalized to standard curves provided with each kit. This comprehensive analysis provides critical insights into systemic inflammation, intestinal barrier integrity, and immune status in both sows and piglets.

Analysis of biochemical indicators and antioxidant parameters

Blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to obtain serum for subsequent analyses. Serum levels of total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), malondialdehyde (MDA), and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) were measured using commercially available assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance readings were recorded using a semi-automatic biochemical analyzer, and the final concentrations of each parameter were calculated based on the standard curves and formulas provided in the respective kit protocols. All measurements were performed in duplicate to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. This comprehensive evaluation of antioxidant capacity and oxidative stress markers offers critical insights into the experimental animals’ systemic redox status and metabolic health.

Measurement of ammonia and hydrogen sulfide in feces

Fresh fecal samples were collected from 108-day gestation sows and 21-day lactating sows immediately after defecation, ensuring that bottom-layer feces were excluded to avoid contamination. Approximately 500 g of feces from each sow were placed into a 5L airtight plastic container. The concentrations of ammonia (NH₃) and hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) in the headspace of the containers were measured after 24 h and 48 h of storage using a QRAE PLUS PGM-2000 multi-gas detector (RAE Systems, USA). The gas detector was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions before use. Measurements were taken for one minute per sample, and the average concentration was recorded. This method provides a reliable assessment of fecal gas emissions, reflecting microbial fermentation activity and intestinal health status in sows during different physiological stages. All measurements were performed in triplicate to ensure accuracy and consistency.

Histological analysis and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

At the end of the experiment, six suckling piglets from each group were randomly selected for euthanasia and tissue collection. Immediately after slaughter, the spleen, mesenteric lymph nodes, duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were excised and rinsed thoroughly with sterile physiological saline to remove blood and residual luminal contents. Tissue segments (3–5 cm long) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 90%, and 95% ethanol) for 2, 2, 1, and 1 h, respectively, followed by a final dehydration step in absolute ethanol for 1 h. Tissues were then cleared in xylene and embedded in paraffin wax. Sections of approximately 5 μm thickness were cut using a microtome, mounted onto glass slides, and dried at 37 °C for 12 h. The sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) using standard protocols, air-dried, and mounted with neutral resin undercover glasses for microscopic examination. This procedure ensures high-quality tissue preservation and staining for detailed histological tissue morphology and structural integrity evaluation.

RNA and DNA extraction and reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Following the manufacturer’s protocol, total RNA was extracted from the jejunum and ileum of suckling piglets using the Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The extracted RNA was then reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a cDNA synthesis kit (TOYOBO, Japan). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, genomic DNA was isolated from the cecal and ileal contents for microbial community analysis using the BIOG DNA extraction kit (BAIDAI, China). The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA and DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to ensure high-quality nucleic acids for downstream applications.

RT-qPCR was performed using the Step One-Plus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) with the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 5 s, annealing at 58 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. Each 50 μL reaction mixture consisted of 16 μL of nuclease-free water, 25 μL of SYBR PCR Master Mix, 2 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μM), and 5 μL of cDNA or DNA template (Table 2). All reactions were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility, and appropriate controls (no template and no reverse transcription) were included to validate the specificity and accuracy of the amplification. This protocol enables precise quantification of gene expression and microbial community dynamics in the intestinal tissues and luminal contents of suckling piglets.

Western blotting analysis

Intestinal tissue samples were thoroughly homogenized in liquid nitrogen, and total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, China). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, protein concentrations were quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Beyotime, China). Protein samples were denatured by boiling at 100 °C for 5 min in SDS-PAGE sample buffer and then separated by electrophoresis on a polyacrylamide gel. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane using a semi-dry transfer system. To prevent nonspecific binding, the membrane was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Biosharp, China) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature.

The membrane was then incubated with a primary antibody (rabbit polyclonal antibody, diluted 1:1500 in TBST) overnight at 4 °C with gentle agitation. The following antibodies were used: rabbit anti-TLR2 (1:1500 for WB, ABclonal, A11225), rabbit anti-TLR4 (1:1500 for WB, ABclonal, A5258), rabbit anti-Myd88 (1:1500 for WB, ABclonal, A0980), rabbit anti-NF-kB P65 (1:1500 for WB, ABclonal, A11204), rabbit anti-ZO-1(1:800 for WB, BBI, D364329), rabbit anti-Claudin-1(1:1500 for WB, Wanlei bio, WL03073), rabbit anti-Occludin (1:1500 for WB, Wanlei bio, WL01996), rabbit anti-GAPDH (1:5000 for WB, ABclonal, A19056). Membranes were then incubated with Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (Abcam: ab6721) at a 1:120,000 dilution in 5% BSA/TBST for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate, and the images were captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system. The relative intensity of the protein bands was quantified using image analysis software, and the gray values were normalized to the corresponding internal control. This protocol ensures accurate detection and quantification of target proteins in intestinal tissues, providing insights into protein expression and signaling pathways.

16 s RNA sequencing of gut microbial diversity analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from fecal samples of gestating sows, lactating sows, and cecal contents of suckling piglets using the FastDNA® SPIN Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3-V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the universal primers 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). The PCR reaction was performed in a 20μL volume containing 4μL of 5 × TransStart FastPfu buffer, 2μL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8μL each of forward and reverse primers (5 μM), 0.4μL of TransStart FastPfu DNA polymerase, and 10 ng of template DNA, with the remaining volume adjusted to 20 μL using nuclease-free water.

The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 27 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were purified using a 2% agarose gel and the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, USA), followed by quantification using a Quantus™ Fluorometer (Promega, USA).

Library preparation was performed using the NEXTFLEX® Rapid DNA-Seq Kit, which included the following steps: (1) adapter ligation, (2) removal of self-ligated adapter fragments using magnetic bead purification, (3) PCR amplification to enrich the library, and (4) final library purification using magnetic beads. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (Illumina, USA) to generate high-quality paired-end reads for downstream microbial community analysis. This comprehensive approach enables robust characterization of gut microbiota composition and diversity in sows and piglets. Bioinformatic analysis of the fecal microbiota was carried out using the Majorbio Cloud plattorm (https://cloud.majorbio.com).

Analysis of gut microbial metabolism

Cecal content samples were thawed at 4 °C and processed for metabolite extraction. Pre-cooled methanol/acetonitrile/water solution (2:2:1, v/v) was added to the samples, followed by thorough vortex mixing and low-temperature sonication for 30 min. The samples were then incubated at −20 °C for 10 min and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 20 min. The supernatant was collected and vacuum-dried. For mass spectrometry analysis, the dried extracts were reconstituted in 100μL of acetonitrile/water solution (1:1, v/v), vortexed, and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was used for subsequent analysis.

Chromatographic conditions

Metabolite separation was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system with a HILIC column. The column temperature was maintained at 25 °C, with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min and an injection volume of 2μL. The mobile phase consisted of (A) water containing 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonia, and (B) acetonitrile. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–0.5 min, 95% B; 0.5–7 min, linear decrease from 95 to 65% B; 7–8 min, linear reduction from 65 to 40% B; 8–9 min, 40% B; 9–9.1 min, linear increase from 40 to 95% B; and 9.1–12 min, 95% B. Samples were maintained at 4 °C in the autosampler throughout the analysis.

Mass spectrometry conditions

Metabolite detection was performed using an AB Triple TOF 6600 mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX) in positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes. The ESI source parameters were set as follows: nebulizer gas 1 (Gas1): 60 psi, nebulizer gas 2 (Gas2): 60 psi, curtain gas (CUR): 30 psi, ion source temperature: 600 °C, and ion spray voltage (ISVF): ± 5500 V. The mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) detection ranges were 60–1000 Da for primary and 25–1000 Da for secondary mass spectrometry. The accumulation times were 0.20 s/spectrum for primary mass spectrometry and 0.05 s/spectrum for secondary mass spectrometry. Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode was used for secondary mass spectrometry, with dynamic exclusion of isotope ions within a 4 Da range and collection of 10 fragment spectra per scan.

Data processing and analysis

Raw data in Wiff format were converted to MzXML format using ProteoWizard software. Peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction were performed using XCMS software. The processed data underwent metabolite structural identification, quality assessment, and statistical analysis, including univariate and multivariate analyses, differential metabolite screening, correlation analysis, and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. This comprehensive metabolomic approach provides detailed insights into microbial metabolic activity and its impact on host physiology.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with at least five independent replicates to ensure robustness and reproducibility. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7 software (GraphPad Software, USA). Differences between experimental groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test for pairwise comparisons or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test for multi-group analyses. Data normality was assessed by using Shapiro Wilk tests. The two-tailed t-tests and Mann–Whitney U tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons (FDR < 0.1) were used between groups. This rigorous statistical approach ensures the experimental findings’ reliability and validity in assessing interventions’ effects on metabolic and microbial parameters.

Results

Probiotic effects of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 on sow and piglet growth performance

Piglets nursed by TL106-fed sows exhibited higher daily weight gain and average body weight during the 21-day lactation period than those in the control group. Furthermore, the diarrhea rate in piglets from the TL106 group was significantly reduced, suggesting improved gut health and resilience. In neonatal piglets, we observed substantially lower IL-8 and higher IL-10 levels in the TL106 group than in controls. Although lactating sows showed no statistically significant differences in cytokine profiles, the TL106 group demonstrated more favorable anti-inflammatory trends. Even without statistical significance, the upward drift in IL-10 is noteworthy, as this cytokine is the primary brake on periparturient inflammation; similar small rises have been linked to better milk composition and lower postpartum complications in commercial sow herds [17, 18]. Our recent studies have shown that TL106, isolated from Tibetan pigs, exhibited favorable probiotic properties in weaned piglets. In this study, we evaluated the effect of TL106 on the growth performance of sows and piglets during gestation and lactation, respectively. As shown in Table 2, during gestation, sows in the TL106 group exhibited fecal scores closer to the ideal range, with statistically significant improvements compared with the control group. This indicates that TL106 supplementation stabilized the intestinal digestive system of sows, enhancing their digestion and absorption efficiency. During lactation, sows supplemented with TL106 demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of stillbirths compared to the control group, with no occurrence of mummified fetuses.

These results indicate that the enhanced growth performance of suckling piglets in the TL106 group may be attributed to improved milk quality from TL106-supplemented sows or indirect exposure to TL106 in the surrounding environment. Feed intake remained statistically identical between groups (Table 3), underscoring that the observed benefits stem from more efficient nutrient utilization rather than greater consumption. While TL106 did not influence all growth and production metrics in sows and piglets, it significantly improved several key indicators, demonstrating its probiotic potential. These findings provide preliminary evidence supporting the probiotic efficacy of TL106 in alleviating metabolic syndrome and enhancing growth performance in swine production systems, highlighting the transgenerational benefits of TL106.

Probiotic effects of TL106 on apparent digestibility of nutrient substances in perinatal sows

The perinatal period represents a critical phase for nutrient digestion and absorption in sows, which significantly influences maternal growth performance and reproductive efficiency while directly impacting neonatal piglet health. This study investigated the effects of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 supplementation on nutrient apparent digestibility in perinatal sows, with a particular focus on major dietary components, including crude protein, crude fat, crude fiber, crude ash, phosphorus, and calcium. Our findings revealed that TL106 administration significantly enhanced the apparent digestibility of crude ash, fiber, and fat in sows at 108 days of gestation compared to the control group. This improvement in fat utilization is consistent with the extracellular lipase production and bile salt hydrolase activity reported for B. amyloliquefaciens, which together liberate fatty acids and enhance micellar solubilization of dietary lipids, thereby facilitating absorption [19, 20]. While no significant differences were observed in TL106’s regulatory effects on crude protein, calcium, and phosphorus digestibility during gestation, the probiotic demonstrated remarkable improvement in crude ash, crude fat, calcium, and phosphorus utilization during lactation (Fig. 2). The core pathophysiology of peripartum metabolic syndrome in sows extends beyond mere nutrient digestion and absorption to encompass systemic insulin resistance. To investigate this, we evaluated fasting blood glucose and insulin levels at 108 days of gestation and 5 days postpartum. Sows managed with the TL106 regimen exhibited significantly lower concentrations of both biomarkers compared to the control group. Subsequent calculation of the insulin resistance index (HOMA-IR) at these two timepoints revealed that a majority of peripartum sows developed varying degrees of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR > 3.5 during gestation and > 2.5 during lactation). In contrast, sows in the BA group consistently maintained values below these critical thresholds (Fig. 2g, h, i). We finally assessed serum triglyceride (TG) dynamics. Notably, gestational TG levels were significantly higher in control sows than in the BA group. While a postpartum decline was observed in both groups, the reduction was not statistically significant; nevertheless, BA sows consistently demonstrated lower TG concentrations (Fig. 2j). These results provide compelling evidence that Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 enhances nutrient utilization efficiency and relieves insulin resistance, particularly for crude nutrients, blood glucose, and triglyceride in perinatal sows, suggesting its potential as a probiotic intervention in sow nutrition management.

Probiotic effects of TL106 on antioxidant capacity of sows and piglets

Serum biochemical profiling serves as a crucial indicator of physiological homeostasis and reflects the intrinsic regenerative capacity of animals. As illustrated in Fig. 3, dietary supplementation with Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 significantly enhanced the serum antioxidant enzyme activities, including total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), while markedly reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations in sows at gestational day 108. Although no significant differences were observed in MDA and GSH-Px levels during the lactation period (day 21), TL106 supplementation notably increased T-SOD activity in lactating sows. Remarkably, the maternal supplementation with TL106 exerted transgenerational effects, as evidenced by significantly improved T-SOD and GSH-Px activities and reduced MDA levels in 21-day-old suckling piglets. TL106 increased T-SOD activity by 28% (15.4 ± 1.2 vs. 12.0 ± 1.1 U/mL, p < 0.01) and reduced MDA levels by 34% (3.2 ± 0.3 vs. 4.9 ± 0.4 nmol/mL, p < 0.05) in gestating sows (Fig. 3). These findings demonstrate that Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 enhances the antioxidant defense system and cellular repair mechanisms in both perinatal sows and their offspring through maternal transfer. Furthermore, our results underscore the critical role of maternal physiological status in fetal programming and neonatal development.

Probiotic effects of TL106 on immune ability (body resistance) in sows and piglets

Metabolic syndrome poses significant challenges to perinatal sows, mainly through immunosuppression and systemic inflammation. The bioactive metabolites produced by Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 demonstrate potent antimicrobial properties and immunomodulatory effects, potentially enhancing systemic immunity and gastrointestinal health. To investigate the immunoregulatory potential of TL106 as a probiotic feed additive, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of immunoglobulin profiles and inflammatory cytokine networks. As illustrated in Fig. 4a, TL106 supplementation significantly elevated serum IgA, IgG, and IgM serum concentrations in gestating sows compared to the control group. This maternal immunomodulation exerted transgenerational effects, as evidenced by the considerably increased IgG and IgM levels in neonatal piglets. Although lactating sows did not show statistically significant differences, a notable upward trend was observed in the TL106-supplemented group, suggesting potential immunomodulatory benefits.

The inflammatory cytokine profile serves as a crucial indicator of systemic immune status. We extensively monitored pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8), anti-inflammatory mediators (IL-10), and TNF-α, lactate, and endotoxin levels. Quantitative analysis revealed that TL106 administration significantly reduced IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α concentrations while maintaining elevated levels of IL-10 in gestating sows. Probiotic effect of TL106 on developing suckling piglets’ immune organs (spleen, mesenteric lymph nodes)

To further investigate the immunomodulatory effects of TL106 on neonatal piglets, we performed a comprehensive histomorphological analysis of immune organ architecture. Histological spleen and mesenteric lymph node sections from five randomly selected piglets per group were prepared using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. As demonstrated in Fig. 5a, the BA group exhibited enhanced splenic microarchitecture, characterized by increased density and expansion of lymphoid nodules compared to controls. Similar histological improvements were observed in mesenteric lymph nodes (Fig. 5b), suggesting systemic immunomodulatory effects.

For quantitative analysis, we employed digital pathology techniques using Aperio ImageScope software to evaluate lymphoid tissue morphology. Five biological replicates per group were analyzed, with standardized unit areas used for comparative quantification of lymphoid nodule density and surface area. Statistical analysis revealed that TL106 supplementation significantly increased both the number (p < 0.05, p < 0.01) and surface area (p < 0.05) of lymphoid nodules compared to controls (Fig. 5c, d).

Finally, we quantified key inflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) in the spleen and mesenteric lymph nodes of piglets. The levels of these cytokines were consistently low in both groups, approaching the detection limit of the assay kit. Although the BA group showed a trend toward lower cytokine concentrations compared with the control group, the differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 5e, f, g). Combined with histological evidence, we speculate that the low and insignificant cytokine levels reflect the absence of pathogenic stimulation under the experimental conditions. Nevertheless, these results do not preclude the possibility that, upon pathogen challenge, piglets in the BA group may mount a more effective immune response compared with the control group.

These findings demonstrate that TL106 administration promotes structural development and functional maturation of immune organs in neonatal piglets, potentially enhancing their immunocompetence through improved lymphoid tissue architecture. The observed histological improvements correlate with enhanced immune responsiveness, supporting the immunomodulatory potential of TL106 in early postnatal development.

Probiotic effect of TL106 on the development of digestive organs (duodenum, jejunum, ileum) in suckling piglets

While our previous research has demonstrated the beneficial effects of direct TL106 administration on intestinal development in weaned piglets, the transgenerational impact of maternal TL106 supplementation on neonatal intestinal development remained unexplored. We conducted a comprehensive histomorphological analysis of duodenal, jejunal, and ileal segments to investigate this, focusing on villus architecture, crypt morphology, and ileal lymphoid tissue development.

The neonatal gastrointestinal tract undergoes critical development during the suckling period, with intestinal maturation heavily dependent on maternal factors. Histopathological examination revealed distinct differences between groups: control piglets exhibited structural abnormalities, including disorganized villus architecture, epithelial damage, and reduced goblet cell populations (Fig. 6a–c), likely associated with growth retardation and diarrheal incidence. In contrast, TL106-exposed piglets demonstrated well-developed intestinal morphology, characterized by intact villus structure, proper epithelial organization, and abundant goblet cell populations. Quantitative analysis using Aperio ImageScope software (five biological replicates per group) revealed significant improvements in villus height across all intestinal segments in TL106-exposed piglets (Fig. 6d). Although crypt depth differences did not reach statistical significance, a notable increasing trend was observed in the TL106 group, suggesting enhanced digestive and absorptive potential.

The ileal lymphoid tissue analysis demonstrated significant advantages in lymphoid nodule density and surface area in TL106-exposed piglets (Fig. 6e), indicating improved intestinal immune competence. Molecular analysis of Toll-like receptor (TLR) pathway components revealed significant downregulation of TLR2, TLR4, and NF-κB P65 expression in TL106-exposed piglets. In contrast, MyD88 expression remained unchanged (Fig. 6f), suggesting modulated but not suppressed immune responsiveness.

The expression of intestinal tight junction proteins is critically linked to the integrity of the intestinal barrier, directly influencing gut health and nutrient absorption. To investigate this relationship in suckling piglets, we quantitatively assessed the expression of key tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Claudin-1, and Occludin). Our results demonstrated that the expression levels of Claudin-1 and Occludin were significantly higher in piglets treated with TL106 compared to the control group. Although no statistically significant difference was observed in ZO-1 expression, the BA group exhibited a trend toward more stable tight junction expression relative to controls (Fig. 6h). To further assess the functional consequences of the improved intestinal barrier integrity, we evaluated the gene expression of key nutrient transporters (SGLT1, GLUT2, PepT1, and FABP2). As shown in Fig. 6g, the dietary supplementation with TL106 up-regulated the mRNA levels of SGLT1 and GLUT2, which are responsible for the apical uptake and basolateral efflux of glucose, respectively. This suggests an enhanced carbohydrate absorption capacity. Concurrently, PepT1, a critical transporter for di- and tripeptides, was markedly increased, indicating greater potential for protein assimilation. Furthermore, the elevated expression of FABP2, which facilitates the intracellular trafficking of long-chain fatty acids, points to an improved lipid absorption pathway.

These findings collectively demonstrate that maternal TL106 supplementation promotes structural and functional development of the neonatal intestine, enhancing digestive capacity and immunological competence through transgenerational effects. The observed improvements in intestinal architecture, lymphoid tissue development, and modulation of the TLR pathway provide compelling evidence for TL106’s role in supporting neonatal intestinal health and immunity. Importantly, the enhancement of tight junction proteins by TL106 is associated with a coordinated up-regulation of major nutrient transporters, which may underpin the improved nutrient digestibility and growth performance observed in suckling piglets.

Probiotic effect of TL106 on the composition and functional outcomes of the gut microbiota in sows and piglets

The abundance and structure of intestinal microorganisms are closely related to intestinal health, which is crucial to the health and reproduction of perinatal sows. To clarify the potential effects of TL106 on the gut microbiome of gestating sows, lactating sows, and piglets, we analyzed the feces of sows and the cecal contents of piglets using 16S rRNA sequencing and multiple analysis and statistical methods to display the results. We have processed the sequencing results using different analysis methods. First, we displayed the diversity and richness of intestinal microorganisms through alpha and beta analysis. Compared with the sows in the control group, the richness (Chao index and ACE index) and the diversity (Shannon index, Simpson index) of the intestinal flora of the pregnant sows and lactating sows in the BA group showed different degrees of up-regulation and down-regulation, but there is no statistical difference. Interestingly, compared with the control group, the richness of the intestinal flora of suckling piglets in the BA group was not significantly different. Still, the Shannon index was increased considerably, and the Simpson index (D value between 0–1) was significantly decreased, indicating that TL106 may have contributed to the significant increase in the species diversity of the gut microbiota in piglets (Supplementary Fig. 2). Of note, all indices showed good homogeneity across the BA group. The dimensionality reduction was performed on the multi-dimensional differences between samples, then the differences between the two samples were compared. The principal coordinate analysis projects the sample distance matrix to reduce the dimensionality and preserve the distance relationship of the samples to the greatest extent. From the results of PCoA, the control group of gestating sow (R_B) and the TL106 group (R_A) could be separated to a certain extent; the control group of lactating sow (B_B) was separated from the TL106 group (B_A); the control group of suckling piglet (Z_B) and TL106 group (Z_A) were not significantly separated; the PCoA analysis of each group could be divided to a certain extent, and the difference was highly significant (Fig. 7a). We then used the Venn diagram (Venn) for community analysis to roughly clarify the number of species shared among samples and the number of unique species. Interestingly, the unique species proportion of gut microbes in the TL106 group (20.36%) was found to be higher than that in the control group (13.23%) only during lactation (Fig. 7b). The specific composition of microbial communities at various classification levels is of great significance for evaluating the function of probiotics. Then, we analyzed and displayed the abundance of the top 20 microbial communities. Our study found that compared with the control group, the abundance of Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Treponema, Turicibacter, Romboutsia, and Streptococcus. increased in the gut microbiota of sows in the TL106 group. Meanwhile, in the gut microbiota of lactating piglets, Cloacibacillus, Phascolarctobacterum, Bacteroides, and Prevotella were upregulated in the TL106 group (Fig. 7c). From this, after the administration of TL106, both the gut microbiota of sows and piglets have undergone adjustments, especially the emergence of dominant microbiota.

Probiotic effect of TL106 on the gut microbiota metabolism of sows and piglets

The structure of the intestinal microbiota and microbial metabolites mainly regulates animal metabolism. In this study, the faces of pregnant and lactating sows and the cecal contents of lactating piglets were analyzed by non-targeted metabolomics to assess the effect of TL106 involvement on metabolic levels in sows and piglets. We performed deep mining and analysis of metabolomic data by orthogonal corrected partial least squares discriminant analysis model OPLS-DA to visually show whether there is a significant difference separation trend in the overall metabolic profile between groups (Fig. 8a). In both positive and negative ion modes, compared to the control group, the BA group exhibited 334, 301, and 241 differential metabolites in gestating sows, lactating sows, and suckling piglets, respectively. These metabolites had VIP values greater than 1 and showed significant differences (p < 0.05) among the groups. Subsequently, we performed univariate differential analysis on all detected metabolites (criteria for identifying differential metabolites: upregulated by more than 1.5-fold, down-regulated by less than 0.67-fold, p < 0.05) and visualized the results using volcano plots (Fig. 8b). The results revealed that the differential metabolites between the TL106 group and the control group in gestating sows, lactating sows, and suckling piglets were predominantly concentrated in the following categories: alkaloids and derivatives, benzoic acids, lignans, lipids, nucleotides, organic acids, organic nitrogen compounds, organic oxygen compounds, organic heterocyclic compounds, phenylpropanoids and polyketides, as well as their analogs, derivatives, and related molecular species. Compared to the control group, the BA group of gestating sows exhibited 12 metabolites down-regulated and 18 upregulated in the NEG mode. In contrast, 15 metabolites were down-regulated in the POS mode, and 15 were significantly upregulated. KEGG analysis of the differential metabolites revealed that the significantly expressed metabolites were enriched in pathways such as Protein digestion and absorption, Arginine biosynthesis, Central carbon metabolism in cancer, ABC transporters, and Central carbon metabolism in cancer, etc. (Fig. 8c, d). Note that KEGG classifies several core metabolic maps (e.g., “Central carbon metabolism in cancer”) within its Human-Disease category because they were annotated initially from tumor datasets; enrichment here denotes modulation of universal glycolytic/TCA nodes rather than any neoplastic process.

In contrast to the control group, the BA group of lactating sows showed 19 metabolites down-regulated and 11 upregulated in the NEG mode, while in the POS mode, 21 metabolites were down-regulated and nine were significantly upregulated. KEGG analysis of the differential metabolites revealed that the significantly expressed metabolites were enriched in the following pathways: Sphingolipid signaling pathway, Protein digestion and absorption, ABC transporters, Arginine biosynthesis, Sphingolipid metabolism, Galactose metabolism, Glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism, Lysine degradation, Pyrimidine metabolism, Tyrosine metabolism, Tryptophan metabolism, and Biosynthesis of amino acids (Fig. 8e, f). For the BA group of suckling piglets, in the NEG mode, 24 metabolites were down-regulated, and six were upregulated. In contrast, 21 metabolites were down-regulated in the POS mode, and seven were significantly upregulated. KEGG analysis of the differential metabolites revealed that the significantly expressed metabolites were enriched in the following pathways: Protein digestion and absorption, Alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, ABC transporters, Pyrimidine metabolism, and Phenylalanine metabolism (Fig. 8g, h).

Through in-depth analysis of the upregulated metabolites in gestating, lactating, and suckling piglets, we found that both lactating and suckling piglets exhibited upregulation of ala-ala and L-pyroglutamic acid in the gut. These two metabolites regulate physiological metabolism: Ala-Ala participates in protein and energy metabolism, while L-pyroglutamic acid possesses antioxidant, neuroregulatory, and metabolic regulatory functions. This suggests that the gut metabolites in the BA group of lactating sows and suckling piglets benefit metabolic regulation and enhance the organism’s stress resistance. Meanwhile, pro-leu, Bendiocarb, and Hippuric acid were downregulated in these two groups. These three metabolites inversely reflect metabolic status: the upregulation of Pro-Leu may indicate enhanced protein breakdown or metabolic disorders, the detection of Bendiocarb may suggest environmental exposure or toxicity risks, and the upregulation of Hippuric acid may imply increased exposure to benzoic acid or abnormalities in liver and kidney function. This indicates that harmful substances hindering normal metabolism in the intestines of lactating sows and suckling piglets in the BA group were significantly reduced.

Interestingly, through KEGG pathway analysis, we found that the differential metabolites in pigs across all three stages were significantly enriched in the pathways of Protein digestion and absorption and ABC transporters and Central carbon metabolism in cancer (Fig. 8d, f, h). In the ABC transporters pathway, L-histidine, glycine, and glycerophosphate were significantly upregulated in the BA group of gestating sows and suckling piglets. L-histidine plays a crucial regulatory role in the immune system, glycine exhibits antioxidant functions and may reflect increased glutathione synthesis in response to oxidative stress, and glycerophosphate plays a key role in energy storage and transport. Because glycine is the third and often rate-limiting substrate for γ-glutamylcysteine synthetase, higher glycine availability enlarges the precursor pool for glutathione, directly strengthening antioxidant defenses [21, 22]. Aspartic acid is upregulated in lactating and pregnant sows. Aspartic acid is an acidic amino acid considered a non-essential amino acid in mammals. Numerous studies have demonstrated that Asp plays a significant and active role in various physiological and biological processes. In the Protein digestion and absorption pathway, the metabolites Indole, L-tryptophan, L-lysine, and Phenol were significantly upregulated in the gut of lactating sows and suckling piglets. Indole is a product of tryptophan breakdown by gut microbiota, reflecting the metabolic activity of intestinal microbes and influencing the host's intestinal barrier function and immune response. Additionally, gut microbiota can metabolize L-tryptophan into indole and its derivatives, impacting gut health and immune response. Changes in lysine levels may be associated with protein, energy, or skeletal health metabolism. Phenol is involved in detoxification and gut microbial metabolism.

In summary, supplementation with Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 has improved the metabolic functions of both sows and piglets to a certain extent. This improvement is attributed to TL106’s ability to reshape the gut microbial metabolic profile, increasing beneficial metabolites and reducing harmful substances. Consequently, through metabolic pathways such as ABC transporters, protein digestion and absorption, and steroid hormone biosynthesis, TL106 promotes growth performance, enhances the immune system, boosts antioxidant capacity, and optimizes nutrient metabolism in pigs.

Discussion

Periparturient sows, particularly during the critical period spanning 15 days, antepartum and postpartum, are susceptible to a complex metabolic syndrome characterized by oxidative stress, immunosuppression, and insulin resistance, often accompanied by subclinical inflammation [23, 24]. These pathophysiological alterations are primarily attributed to dramatic fluctuations in hormonal profiles and nutritional metabolism during this transitional phase. Emerging evidence from gut-microbiota-host interaction studies has established a strong correlation between metabolic syndrome and intestinal dysbiosis, characterized by elevated levels of deleterious microbial metabolites and compromised intestinal barrier integrity [4, 5]. To our knowledge, this is the first study demonstrating maternal TL106 supplementation effect on neonatal gut microbiota, involving Ruminococcus and Butyricicoccus, directly linking these taxa to histidine metabolism and antioxidant enhancement in piglets. Our previous investigations have demonstrated that Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106, a spore-forming probiotic strain, exerts beneficial effects on growth performance, immune modulation, and stress resilience in weaned piglets [16]. Our hypothesis that TL106 improves metabolic syndrome via gut microbiota modulation is supported by the enrichment of butyrate-producing Butyricicoccus and reduced pro-inflammatory Campylobacter. Building upon these findings, the current study aims to elucidate the probiotic potential of TL106 in ameliorating metabolic syndrome in periparturient sows and their offspring, with particular emphasis on growth performance parameters, immune status, gut microbiota composition, and nutrient metabolism dynamics.

Bacillus species exhibit remarkable metabolic versatility through the secretion of diverse digestive enzymes, including cellulase, protease, lipase, amylase, glucanase, xylanase, and phosphatase, which collectively enhance intestinal nutrient digestibility and absorption efficiency. Wang et al. demonstrated that dietary supplementation with Bacillus amyloliquefaciens induces significant modifications in the gut microbiota composition of sows, which subsequently influences the microbial ecosystem of piglets through vertical microbial transmission, ultimately leading to enhanced short-chain fatty acid production in both sows and their offspring [25]. In a parallel study, Li et al. investigated the effects of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL (4 × 109 CFU/kg) supplementation in broiler diets, revealing significant improvements in average daily gain (ADG), crude protein utilization efficiency, and cecal Lactobacillus populations, concomitant with reduced feed conversion ratio, intestinal Escherichia coli counts, and enhanced slaughter performance [26]. Furthermore, Su et al. isolated a strain of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens exhibiting robust enzymatic activity, particularly in protease, amylase, lipase, and cellulase production, which mediated its beneficial effects through gut microbiota modulation and digestive function enhancement [27]. The cumulative evidence from these studies substantiates the pivotal role of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens in enhancing nutrient digestibility and production performance in livestock. In the present investigation, we observed that dietary supplementation of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 during gestation and lactation significantly improved fecal consistency scores in pregnant sows and enhanced the apparent digestibility of key nutrients, including crude fiber, crude fat, calcium, and phosphorus. However, lactating sows’ overall growth performance parameters did not reach statistical significance, with only specific indicators (live birth rate, average litter weight, and diarrhea incidence) demonstrating significant improvements.

Progressive oxidative stress, a hallmark of animal pregnancy, is characterized by a substantial elevation in systemic reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, which play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of metabolic syndrome during the perinatal period [28]. In high-yielding sows, the accumulation of ROS during this critical phase, if not promptly mitigated, can induce cellular damage, metabolic dysregulation, and impaired production performance, often with unfavorable outcomes [29, 30]. This oxidative burden is evidenced by significant increases in biomarkers such as 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHDG), malondialdehyde (MDA), and protein carbonyls in the peripheral circulation of periparturient sows. The enzymatic antioxidant defense system, including key enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), often compromises its capacity to neutralize ROS, exacerbating oxidative stress during the perinatal transition [31]. Recent studies have elucidated the protective roles of specific gut microbiota in mitigating oxidative stress. Wang et al. demonstrated that Ruminococcus spp. attenuates intestinal oxidative stress, primarily through the production of butyrate, a key microbial metabolite [32]. Similarly, Li et al. reported that Lactobacillus fermentum HFY06 effectively counteracts D-galactose-induced aging in murine models, potentially through activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway and subsequent upregulation of downstream antioxidant enzymes and anti-inflammatory mediators [33]. These findings collectively underscore the critical role of gut microbiota in maintaining physiological homeostasis and redox balance, particularly under oxidative stress conditions. In the present study, supplementation with Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 significantly enhanced the antioxidant capacity of both sows and piglets, as evidenced by increased serum levels of total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD) and GSH-Px, along with reduced MDA concentrations in 108-day pregnant sows and 21-day suckling piglets. These results demonstrate that Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 effectively augments the antioxidant defense systems in maternal and neonatal subjects, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic intervention in periparturient oxidative stress management.

The periparturient period in sows is characterized by significant immunological alterations marked by dynamic inflammatory mediators and immune status changes. These physiological adaptations impact maternal health and influence neonatal immunity and growth performance through the vertical transmission of inflammatory factors via milk [34]. Pomorska et al. demonstrated a pro-inflammatory shift in the immediate postpartum period, evidenced by elevated C-reactive protein and interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels and a significant reduction in the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 on the third day postpartum [35]. This inflammatory state persists throughout the reproductive cycle, as Palm et al. observed sustained elevation of plasma IL-6 levels in pregnant women, which remained elevated even post-parturition. Emerging evidence suggests that probiotic intervention can modulate host-microbiota interactions, thereby ameliorating systemic inflammation and insulin resistance. The production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) by gut microbiota is pivotal in restoring intestinal barrier integrity, enhancing mucosal immunity, and attenuating systemic inflammation [36]. Given the heightened inflammatory state during the periparturient period, strategic probiotic supplementation represents a promising approach to modulate inflammatory responses and restore immune homeostasis in sows. Immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, and IgM), critical components of the humoral immune system, play essential roles in pathogen neutralization, toxin clearance, and immune potentiation. Substantial evidence indicates that probiotic administration can enhance systemic immunoglobulin levels, bolstering livestock humoral immunity [37]. In the present study, Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 supplementation significantly elevated serum immunoglobulin levels in pregnant sows and suckling piglets. Furthermore, TL106 administration markedly reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) while increasing anti-inflammatory IL-10 concentrations in 108-day pregnant sows. A similar anti-inflammatory effect was observed in 21-day-old suckling piglets, demonstrating the transgenerational immunomodulatory potential of TL106.

The Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling pathway represents a fundamental mechanism in innate immunity, mediating host responses to microbial challenges through pattern recognition [38]. Key components of this pathway, including TLR2, TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB P65, are subject to modulation by gut microbiota and their metabolites [39]. Our findings reveal that TL106 significantly downregulated the expression of TLR2, TLR4, and NF-κB P65 proteins in the ileum of lactating piglets, providing mechanistic insights into its immunomodulatory effects. These results align with previous research and elucidate the potential mechanisms underlying TL106-mediated immune enhancement. Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106 demonstrates significant potential in attenuating inflammatory responses in late-gestation sows while enhancing anti-inflammatory capacity in suckling piglets. This probiotic intervention effectively mitigates the immunosuppressed state characteristic of late pregnancy and improves immune competence in neonatal piglets, highlighting its therapeutic potential in periparturient sow management. Unlike B. subtilis, TL106 uniquely modulates maternal microbial transmission, a mechanism unreported in prior studies.

The disruption of gut microbiota homeostasis in sows during the perinatal period represents a critical etiological factor in the development of metabolic syndrome. This dysbiosis compromises maternal health and potentially influences piglet microbial colonization through vertical transmission via milk and fecal–oral routes [40]. During late gestation, significant alterations in gut microbial composition are observed, characterized by an increased abundance of pro-inflammatory phyla (Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria) and decreased populations of butyrate-producing bacteria with anti-inflammatory properties, particularly Clostridium spp. These changes are accompanied by substantial variations in α-diversity and β-diversity indices [41]. Current research indicates that Proteobacteria and Clostridium spp. are markedly enriched during early lactation, with Clostridium proliferation strongly associated with enteritis pathogenesis [42].

Furthermore, gut microbiota dysbiosis and colonic epithelial dysfunction facilitate extensive Proteobacteria expansion [43]. These findings collectively demonstrate that the perinatal period, especially early lactation, represents a critical window of gut microbiota instability in sows. The neonatal piglet’s gastrointestinal tract, initially sterile at birth, undergoes rapid microbial colonization influenced by maternal vaginal microbiota and environmental factors, establishing a strong maternal-offspring microbial linkage [44]. Previous studies have demonstrated the regulatory effects of specific probiotic strains on gut microbiota. Zhang et al. reported that Bacillus amyloliquefaciens fsznc-06 and Bacillus pumilus fsznc-09 modulated microbial composition by enhancing beneficial bacteria while suppressing pathogenic populations in ruminants [45]. Similarly, Bao’s research showed that TL106 effectively stabilized gut microbiota disrupted by E. coli O157:H7 in murine models, demonstrating its capacity to modulate microbial populations [46].

In the present study, TL106 administration significantly enhanced cecal microbiota diversity in lactating piglets, suggesting beneficial intergenerational microbial transmission between sows and their offspring. Because colostrum and milk microbiota were not sampled here, the lactational pathway remains unconfirmed; forthcoming work will profile sow milk to trace this vertical route, as milk-borne taxa are recognized contributors to early piglet gut colonization [47–49]. Dynamic changes in Firmicutes’ proportion during late gestation are particularly noteworthy, as this phylum plays a crucial role in energy acquisition, supporting fetal growth, and establishing lactation energy reserves [50]. Microbial taxonomic analysis revealed that TL106 supplementation significantly increased the abundance of several beneficial genera in sow gut microbiota, including Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Romboutsia, Butyricicoccus, Christensenaceae, Ruminooccus, and Turicibacter. These genera are functionally significant for lactic acid production, resistant starch and fiber digestion, intestinal barrier stabilization, and diarrhea prevention through fermentation. Their presence is negatively correlated with metabolic disorders and positively associated with healthy mucosal markers and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, particularly butyrate [51–54].

Notably, Veillonella (Firmicutes phylum) plays a pivotal role in host physiology, particularly in inflammation modulation and lipid metabolism. Clinical observations have consistently shown reduced Veillonella abundance in patients with inflammatory bowel diseases [55, 56]. In TL106-treated piglets, we observed increased abundance of Christensenellaceae, Butyricicoccus, and g__unclassified_f__Clostridiaceae strongly associated with SCFA production, macronutrient metabolism, and intestinal inflammation alleviation [43, 54]. Interestingly, our findings of reduced Lactobacillus abundance in both TL106-treated sows and piglets corroborate previous reports by Bao et al. [46]. Based on these robust findings, we conclude that TL106 demonstrates significant probiotic potential through its ability to modulate gut microbiota in gestating and lactating sows and piglets. This microbial regulation subsequently influences systemic anti-inflammatory responses, intestinal immune homeostasis, and nutrient utilization efficiency, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic intervention in perinatal swine nutrition.

Emerging evidence has established a strong correlation between gut microbiota composition, host nutritional metabolism, and disease pathogenesis. Metabolomics has emerged as a powerful analytical tool for elucidating the underlying mechanisms of these interactions. The gut microbiota plays a multifaceted role in host physiology, regulating energy homeostasis, facilitating nutrient digestion and absorption, and modulating lipid and amino acid metabolism [57]. The biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids contributes to enhanced antioxidant capacity in animals [58]. In contrast, cysteine, glutamic acid, and glycine metabolism are crucial for glutathione synthesis, a pivotal endogenous antioxidant. Intestinal probiotics facilitate fatty acid β-oxidation, generating metabolic energy for weaned piglets. Nucleotides are essential in maintaining normal growth, immune function, and stress responses while alleviating piglet diarrhea, enhancing serum immunoglobulin levels, and improving intestinal mucosal integrity across different physiological stages [59, 60]. Our investigation revealed that TL106 supplementation significantly modulated multiple metabolic pathways. In late-gestation sows, TL106 upregulated pathways related to protein digestion, mineral absorption, amino acid biosynthesis, steroid hormone biosynthesis, ABC transporters, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis (Supplementary Fig. 3, 4). In lactating sows, TL106 enhanced tryptophan metabolism and sphingolipid signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 3, 5). For lactating piglets, TL106 improved histidine metabolism, protein digestion and absorption, and the CAMP signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. 3, 6).

KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated that TL106 administration significantly upregulated key intestinal metabolites, including L-histidine, glycine, and glycerophosphate, in suckling piglets. Histidine, through decarboxylation in mammalian and bacterial cells, is converted to histamine, which plays a crucial immunoregulatory role [61]. Despite being classified as a non-essential amino acid, glycine exhibits multiple beneficial effects on metabolism, immunity, and development. It has been shown to regulate mucosal immunity and gut microbiome composition in weaned piglets [62], promoting intestinal development, regulating tight junction protein expression, and enhancing mucosal barrier function [63]. In TL106-supplemented sows, we observed upregulation of aspartic acid (Asp) in lactating and pregnant animals. Asp is a primary metabolic fuel for maintaining small intestinal function and integrity while playing significant roles in immune function. Asp has demonstrated protective effects against LPS-induced intestinal damage in piglets, exhibiting anti-inflammatory properties and improving intestinal integrity [64]. Notably, TL106 supplementation significantly reduced sorbitol levels in pregnant sows, lactating sows, and piglets. Sorbitol, a naturally occurring polyol with limited intestinal absorption, is typically metabolized by gut microbiota, particularly Clostridium species. In inflammatory bowel disease, sorbitol and other polyols are fermented by a broad range of Clostridium species; this activity can deplete luminal polyols and aggravate epithelial stress and inflammation [65, 66]. Although C. difficile itself is uncommon in adult pigs (0–23% prevalence) [67], many other porcine Clostridium spp. possess this sorbitol-utilization trait, so the TL106-associated drop in sorbitol remains biologically relevant. Antibiotic-induced disruption of the gut microbiota has been shown to worsen polyol intolerance [68], underscoring the TL106-mediated reduction in sorbitol. These findings collectively demonstrate that TL106 exerts beneficial effects by modulating gut microbiota metabolite pathways, promoting the production of valuable metabolites while reducing harmful compounds. This metabolic regulation suggests that TL106 has the potential to mitigate metabolic syndrome in both sows and piglets. It is consistent with previous research findings and supports its potential as an effective probiotic intervention in swine nutrition and health management. Limitations include a single-dose design (5 × 109 CFU/kg) and the absence of metagenomic sequencing to resolve microbial contributions at the strain level. Future studies should investigate larger cohorts and explore the dose–response relation.

Our study has several limitations that should be considered for the appropriate interpretation of the findings and for informing future research directions. First, the investigation was performed in healthy sows, which may spontaneously develop varying degrees of metabolic disturbances, with an unpredictable incidence of affected individuals, rather than in animals with pre-established metabolic syndrome. The current lack of a standardized approach necessitated this approach and validated experimental model capable of reliably inducing this complex multifactorial condition in sows, a recognized challenge in the field. Therefore, the potential preventive effect of TL106 on sow metabolic syndrome requires further evaluation in specifically designed interventional studies, once such reliable animal models become available. Second, although TL106 supplementation in sows resulted in significant improvements in the intestinal health of suckling piglets, likely mediated through environmental microbial transmission and gut colonization, as supported by our microbiota colonization data, the underlying mechanistic pathway has not been fully elucidated. While our multi-omics analyses provide correlative evidence of microbial and metabolic alterations, interventional approaches, such as direct administration of identified differential metabolites or targeted pathway inhibition experiments, are necessary to establish causal relationships. To address these limitations, we have outlined clear objectives for subsequent research. Through systematic investigation of these aspects, we aim to establish a solid theoretical foundation for enhancing the health management of perinatal sows and supporting the healthy development of their offspring.

This study was specifically designed to evaluate the probiotic effects of Tibetan pig-derived TL106 on metabolic syndrome in periparturient sows and thus did not extend these investigations to their suckling piglets. This represents an important limitation, as suckling piglets have emerged as a valuable model for studying obesity and metabolic syndrome, given their notable physiological and metabolic parallels with humans including similarities in digestive system structure and key metabolic pathways. These common features make piglets inherently susceptible to developing MS-like phenotypes, such as increased adiposity and insulin resistance, particularly when exposed to adverse prenatal or postnatal conditions. Consequently, the absence of piglet-centered data in the current work precludes any assessment of potential transgenerational metabolic benefits conferred by maternal TL106 supplementation. To address this gap, future studies will include comprehensive phenotypic evaluations in suckling piglets, focusing on growth performance, body composition (e.g., body fat rate), and systemic metabolic parameters (such as blood glucose and insulin levels). Through these follow-up investigations, we aim to validate whether TL106 administration in sows confers lasting metabolic advantages to their offspring.

Conclusion

Bacillus amyloliquefaciens TL106, isolated from Tibetan pigs, demonstrates significant probiotic efficacy in metabolic syndrome. This strain enhances nutrient digestibility, modulates inflammatory responses, and improves immunological and antioxidant status in both sows and piglets. Through its extensive colonization in the ileum and cecum, TL106 promotes SCFA production and fosters anti-inflammatory bacterial communities while regulating critical metabolic pathways, including amino acid metabolism, mineral absorption, and nutrient utilization. These multifaceted effects collectively contribute to alleviating periparturient metabolic syndrome, stabilizing sow production performance, and enhancing piglet growth and development, establishing TL106 as a promising probiotic candidate for swine nutrition and health management.
