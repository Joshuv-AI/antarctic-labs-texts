Unveiling the microbial shift: the impact of group B Streptococcus on vaginal microbiota and inflammation in late pregnancy
Authors: Wenkai YU, Mengyi SUN, Renzhe LI, Yanming Xin, Kang Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGroup B Streptococcus (GBS) commonly colonizes the female reproductive tract and rectum. An estimated 19.7 million pregnant women worldwide are colonized with GBS. Infection during late pregnancy can lead to adverse pregnancy outcomes. Pregnant women infected with GBS face a significantly increased risk of transmitting the bacteria to their newborns during delivery, potentially resulting in serious conditions such as sepsis, pneumonia, and meningitis major contributors to neonatal morbidity and mortality. While previous studies have primarily focused on the prevalence of GBS colonization in pregnant women, its effects on the vaginal microbiota and systemic inflammation remain underexplored. This study aims to investigate the alterations in vaginal microbiota associated with GBS colonization during late pregnancy and to examine correlations between microbiota composition and systemic inflammatory markers in GBS-positive pregnant women.MethodsPregnant women in late gestation undergoing routine prenatal care were classified into GBS-negative (n = 19) and GBS-positive (n = 20) groups based on their GBS infection status. Vaginal microbiota composition was analyzed using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing to assess the impact of GBS infection. Additionally, the relationship between microbiota profiles and systemic inflammatory markers was evaluated in the GBS-positive group.ResultsGBS-positive women exhibited significant shifts in vaginal microbiota, notably a reduction in Lactobacillus dominance and an increased abundance of pathogenic taxa such as Gardnerella vaginalis and Streptococcus agalactiae. These microbial changes were associated with elevated systemic inflammatory markers, including neutrophil count (NEU), neutrophil percentage (NEU%), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), procalcitonin (PCT), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), suggesting a link between vaginal dysbiosis and systemic inflammation. LEfSe analysis revealed an enrichment of Gemella in GBS-negative women and a predominance of Gardnerella in the GBS-positive group. Correlation analysis showed that Bifidobacterium was positively associated with hs-CRP and IL-6 levels, while Lactobacillus was inversely correlated with hs-CRP, IL-6, and ESR (P < 0.05). Additionally, Dialister and Prevotella were positively associated with ESR (P < 0.05).ConclusionGBS colonization disrupts the balance of the vaginal microbiota, contributing to a pro-inflammatory state that may heighten the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes. In comparison to women who are not colonized by GBS, GBS colonization may be associated with a decrease in the abundance of Lactobacillus jensenii and Lactobacillus reuteri, as well as an increase in Gardnerella vaginalis and Lactobacillus johnsonii. Restoring Lactobacillus (specific microbial species) dominance may help mitigate inflammation and promote maternal and fetal health. Future research should focus on longitudinal monitoring of microbiota dynamics and the development of targeted interventions to prevent or manage GBS-related complications.

Introduction

Vaginal microbiota plays a crucial role in maintaining reproductive health, particularly during pregnancy. A stable vaginal ecosystem dominated by Lactobacillus species helps prevent pathogenic infections by maintaining a low pH environment and producing antimicrobial compounds [1–3]. During pregnancy, however, levels of estrogen and progesterone rise significantly. These hormonal changes affect the composition and function of the vaginal microbiota. High levels of estrogen promote the proliferation of vaginal epithelial cells and store glycogen. Under normal circumstances, Lactobacillus decomposes glycogen into lactic acid to maintain the acidic environment of the vagina, thereby inhibiting pathogens. If the abundance of Lactobacillus decreases (such as low immunity or flora imbalance), glycogen will not be effectively metabolized, which may lead to an increase in pH. At this time, the vaginal environment is more conducive to the colonization of conditional pathogenic bacteria such as Group B Streptococcus (GBS). Similarly, progesterone is also a key hormone to maintain pregnancy health, but it suppresses the maternal immune response. This immunosuppressive state reduces the ability of the vagina to clear GBS, making it easier to colonize [4–6]. GBS is a leading cause of perinatal infections, associated with adverse outcomes, including preterm birth, neonatal sepsis, and chorioamnionitis [7]. While previous studies have largely focused on the prevalence and clinical management of GBS, its influence on vaginal microbiota composition and systemic inflammatory responses remains insufficiently understood.

GBS, also known as Streptococcus agalactiae, commonly colonizes the female reproductive tract and rectum and is considered an opportunistic pathogen. According to estimates, approximately 19.7 million pregnant women worldwide are colonized with GBS [8], with an average infection rate of approximately 17.1% [9]. In China, colonization rates among pregnant women range from 10 to 20% [10]. Studies have shown that GBS-positive mothers have About a 50% chance of transmitting the bacteria to their newborns, and among those infants, 1–2% may develop early-onset GBS disease [11, 12]. Moreover, GBS is a major pathogen in neonatal pneumonia, with detection rates reported as high as 63% [13]. Despite extensive research into GBS colonization rates, the mechanisms by which GBS disrupts the vaginal microbial ecosystem and promotes inflammatory responses remain unclear. One study in healthy pregnant women found an inverse relationship between GBS colonization and Lactobacillus abundance [14]. Similarly, Kubota et al. reported that the detection rates of coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, Prevotella, and Lactobacillus were significantly lower in GBS-colonized pregnant women than in their GBS-negative counterparts [15]. However, other studies have reported no significant difference in Lactobacillus abundance between the two groups [16]. A 16 S rRNA gene sequencing study in Guatemalan pregnant women found that Corynebacterium species were more prevalent in GBS-positive individuals, while Aerococcus species were more abundant in those without GBS colonization [17]. Recent research has increasingly highlighted the close relationship between vaginal microbial imbalance and GBS infection in pregnant women [18]. Vaginal dysbiosis marked by reduced dominance of beneficial microbiota and increased colonization by pathogenic species may contribute to adverse pregnancy outcomes by fostering a pro-inflammatory state [19]. However, the specific microbial shifts associated with GBS colonization and their correlation with systemic inflammatory markers (e.g., hs-CRP, IL-6, PCT) remain inadequately studied.

This study aimed to: (1) Compare the composition of the vaginal microbiota between GBS-positive and GBS-negative pregnant women using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing; (2) Investigate the relationship between GBS-associated microbial alterations and systemic inflammatory markers; (3) Identify potential microbial biomarkers linked to GBS infection and inflammation that could inform therapeutic strategies.

The findings of this study are expected to deepen our understanding of the interactions among GBS colonization, vaginal microbiota composition, and systemic inflammation. By addressing these knowledge gaps, this research may lay the groundwork for novel diagnostic and therapeutic approaches aimed at mitigating GBS-related adverse pregnancy outcomes and improving maternal and neonatal health.

Specimen collection and detection

Vaginal secretions and fasting venous blood samples (collected from the antecubital vein) were obtained from all participants. Vaginal secretion samples were immediately processed for bacterial DNA extraction and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis.

In the hospital’s clinical laboratory, complete blood counts were performed using impedance and flow cytometry methods with a Sysmex XN-2000 automated hematology analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan). High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels were measured by nephelometry using the PA-800 specific protein analyzer (Pumen, Shanghai, China). Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and procalcitonin (PCT) levels were determined via chemiluminescence using the UniCel DxI 800 immunoassay system (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was measured using the capillary dynamic photometry method with the TEST1 fully automated ESR analyzer (ALIFAX, Italy).

Vaginal secretions collection

According to the Chinese Experts Consensus on Prevention of Perinatal Group B Streptococcal Disease [20], vulvar secretions were first removed using sterile gauze. A sterile cotton swab was then inserted approximately one-third of the way into the vagina and rotated gently to collect vaginal secretions. The swab was subsequently placed into a non-nutritive transport medium and submitted for analysis within 24 h. Participants were instructed to avoid vaginal douching, the use of intravaginal medications, or sexual intercourse for at least 24 h prior to sample collection.

DNA extraction and PCR protocol

Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted from the vaginal secretion samples using the nucleic acid extraction reagent included in the Group B Streptococcus Nucleic Acid Detection Kit (Liferiver, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The method of DNA extraction was as follows: add 1mL sterile saline to the specimen tube, mix thoroughly, then transfer the liquid to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuge at 13,000 rpm for 5 min. Discard the supernatant, resuspend the precipitate in 1 mL of sterile saline, mix well, centrifuge again at 13,000 rpm for 5 min, and repeat the washing again. Add 100 µL of nucleic acid extract to the precipitate, mix thoroughly, incubate in a water bath at 99 ℃ for 10 min, and then centrifuge at 13,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant contains the extracted DNA. The quality and concentration of DNA were determined by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, United States) and stored at −80 ℃ prior to further analysis. The PCR reaction mixture consisted of 0.4 µL of Pro Taq enzyme, 36 µL of PCR master mix, 4 µL of template DNA, and nuclease-free water (ddH₂O) to a final volume of 40 µL. PCR amplification was performed under the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 94 ℃ for 2 min; followed by 40 cycles of denaturing at 93 ℃ for 15 s, annealing and extension at 60 ℃ for 60 s; with a final hold at 10 ℃. Diagnosis of GBS infection in this study was based on the revised guidelines for the Prevention of Perinatal Group B Streptococcal Disease issued by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in 2010, as well as recommendations from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, the American Academy of Pediatrics, the American College of Nurse-Midwives, the American Academy of Family Physicians, and the American Society for Microbiology [21]. GBS colonization was determined by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), with positive results indicating GBS infection.

Illumina sequencing

Using the extracted DNA as a template, the V3-V4 region was amplified using primers 338 F and 806 R [22] and sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) following standard protocols provided by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Data processing

Raw FASTQ files were initially de-multiplexed using a custom perl script. Quality control was performed using fastp (version 0.19.6) [23], and paired-end sequences were merged using FLASH (version 1.2.7) [24]. The high-quality, merged sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a 97% similarity threshold using UPARSE (version 11) [25]. The most abundant sequence within each OTU was selected as the representative sequence. To minimize the influence of sequencing depth on diversity analyses, the 16 S rRNA gene sequences were rarefied to 20,000 reads per sample, achieving an average Good’s coverage of 99.09%. The taxonomy of each OTU representative sequence was analyzed by RDP Classifier (version 2.2) against the 16 S rRNA gene database (e.g. Silva v138) using confidence threshold of 70% [26].

Bioinformatic analysis

Bioinformatic analyses of the vaginal microbiota were conducted using the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com) [27]. Rarefaction curves and alpha diversity indices, including observed species (Sobs), Chao1, and Shannon index, were calculated using Mothur v1.30.2 [28]. Beta diversity was assessed using unweighted UniFrac distances, and differences in microbial community structure between groups were visualized by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS), and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). To identify differentially abundant bacterial taxa between GBS-positive and GBS-negative groups, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was performed, with an LDA score > 3 and P < 0.05 considered statistically significant [29]. Finally, Spearman correlation analysis was used to explore associations between vaginal microbiota composition and systemic inflammatory markers, and results were visualized as a heatmap using R software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and R software (R Core Team, Auckland, New Zealand). Continuous variables with a normal distribution and equal variances were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed data or data with unequal variances were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as counts (n) or percentages (%) and compared using the Pearson χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All laboratory analyses were conducted in an ISO 15189-certified facility, ensuring compliance with international quality standards.

To assess the association between vaginal microbiota and clinical indicators, multicollinearity analysis was first performed. A correlation heatmap was generated to compute and visualize pairwise correlation coefficients among clinical indicators. Dimensionality reduction was performed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression method [30]. LASSO applies a penalty to regression coefficients, progressively shrinking those of less informative predictors toward zero, thereby selecting a subset of variables with the strongest influence. The λ value corresponding to the minimum cross-validation error was identified as the optimal model parameter and used to determine clinical indicators significantly associated with microbiota variations.

Microbial diversity

Alpha diversity indices were calculated to evaluate microbial richness and diversity between the two groups. Although the Shannon and Chao1 indices were slightly higher in the GBS-positive group compared to the GBS-negative group, these differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 3A, B).

Microbiome differences between GBS-negative and GBS-positive women were further examined. To evaluate beta diversity differences, PCoA, NMDS, and PLS-DA were performed based on unweighted UniFrac distances. Results revealed significant structural differences in vaginal microbiota between groups. Hierarchical clustering analysis demonstrated that most samples within the same group clustered together (Fig. 3C-G).

Comparison of relative taxa abundance

We compared the relative abundance of bacterial taxa at the phylum, genus, and species levels. The dominant phyla in both groups were Firmicutes, Actinobacteriota, Bacteroidota, and Proteobacteria collectively accounting for over 99% of the microbial community. But the abundance of Firmicutes is notably higher in the GBS-negative group. Meanwhile, Actinobacteria and Bacteroidota are relatively more abundant in the GBS-positive group (Fig. 4 A, F). At the genus level, Lactobacillus was predominant in both groups, but the relative abundance of Lactobacillus was higher in the GBS-negative group compared to the GBS-positive group. In contrast, the GBS-positive group exhibited higher proportions of Prevotella, Bifidobacterium, and Gardnerella (Fig. 4B, G). At the species level within the Lactobacillus genus, Lactobacillus johnsonii (11.00%) was more abundant in the GBS-positive group, whereas Lactobacillus reuteri (0.20%) and Lactobacillus jensenii (0.01%) were present at lower levels. Conversely, in the GBS-negative group, Lactobacillus jensenii exhibited the highest abundance, accounting for approximately 1.99%, while Lactobacillus reuteri (0.38%) and Lactobacillus johnsonii (0.27%) were relatively less abundant (Fig. 4 C, D, E). Relationship with clinical indicators

To explore the relationship between vaginal microbiota and clinical indicators in pregnant women with GBS infection, we conducted Spearman correlation analyses. Prior to this, multicollinearity among clinical variables was assessed by calculating correlation coefficients, which were visualized via a heatmap of the correlation matrix (Fig. 6H). Several clinical indicators showed Spearman correlation coefficients exceeding 0.6, indicating strong correlations and potential multicollinearity. To address this, we applied LASSO regression, which effectively reduces multicollinearity by shrinking the coefficients of less influential variables to zero as the penalty parameter (λ) increases (Fig. 7 A, B). This process enabled the selection of five key clinical indicators with minimal multicollinearity for further analysis: NEU%, hs-CRP, IL-6, PCT, and ESR. As depicted in the correlation heatmap (Fig. 7 C), the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium was positively correlated with hs-CRP (P < 0.001) and IL-6 (P < 0.001). Discussion

Vaginal infections impose substantial societal burdens [31], and integrating clinical data with microbiome profiles holds promise for developing personalized health prediction models [32]. This study highlights the complex interplay between GBS colonization, vaginal microbiota dysbiosis, and systemic inflammation during late pregnancy. Our results show that GBS-positive pregnant women exhibited notable microbial shifts, including decreased Lactobacillus dominance and increased abundance of pathogenic taxa such as Gardnerella vaginalis and Streptococcus agalactiae. These changes correlate with elevated systemic inflammatory markers (e.g., hs-CRP, IL-6, PCT, ESR), indicating a strong link between vaginal dysbiosis and inflammation. These findings underscore the potential role of GBS-induced microbial imbalance in adverse pregnancy outcomes and suggest the clinical therapeutic potential of Lactobacillus-based microecological intervention strategies in improving pregnancy outcomes.

Alpha and beta diversity metrics are commonly used to evaluate microbial biodiversity. Our observation of significant differences in beta diversity but not alpha diversity between the GBS-positive and GBS-negative groups aligns with Liao et al. [33], who attributed such patterns to microbial structural reorganization rather than changes in richness. Contrastingly, studies by Li et al. [34] and Segovia-Rodríguez et al. [19] reported alterations in alpha diversity, which may reflect differences in cohort demographics or sequencing methodologies. These inconsistencies highlight the critical influence of population characteristics and technical factors in microbiome research. Statistical tests (PCoA, NMDS, and PLS-DA) show that there are significant differences in microbial composition between the GBS-negative and GBS-positive groups. GBS-positive samples show greater variation and heterogeneity in microbial communities. The GBS-positive group exhibits higher compositional variability, with a wider range of dominant OTUs compared to a more stable and consistent microbial community in GBS-negative individuals. This could suggest that GBS colonization destabilizes the vaginal microbiome, potentially allowing for the promotion of opportunistic pathogens or weakening defense mechanisms provided by the microbial community.

Our analysis of the vaginal microbial community revealed that the GBS-negative group exhibited a higher relative abundance of Firmicutes and Lactobacillus compared to the GBS-positive group. At the species level, Gardnerella vaginalis and Streptococcus agalactiae were significantly enriched in the GBS-positive group. These results suggest that GBS positive and negative individuals are significantly different in the composition of the microbiota, which may be related to the microecological dysregulation associated with GBS infection. Previous studies have consistently shown that the vaginal microbiota of healthy women is predominantly composed of Firmicutes [19]. Similarly, Ceccarani et al. reported Firmicutes enrichment in bacterial vaginosis (BV)-negative cohorts, corroborating our findings [35]. Numerous studies have established that Lactobacillus species dominate the healthy vaginal microbiota by inhibiting pathogenic microorganisms through lactic acid production and secretion of antimicrobial substances such as cytokines, surfactants, and hydrogen peroxide [36]. Moreover, Lactobacillus enhances host defense by modulating the immune response to lipopolysaccharides via the release of inflammatory mediators [37]. Research in healthy pregnant women has shown an inverse correlation between GBS detection and Lactobacillus abundance [14], with reduced Lactobacillus levels positively associated with GBS proliferation [38, 39]. A decline in Lactobacillus disrupts the vaginal microenvironment, weakening its protective acidic pH and antimicrobial defenses, thereby facilitating GBS colonization. This aligns with studies emphasizing the pivotal role of Lactobacillus in maintaining vaginal health through lactic acid production and biofilm formation, which inhibit pathogen adherence and growth [35, 36]. At the species level within the Lactobacillus genus, Lactobacillus johnsonii (11.00%) was more abundant in the GBS-positive group, whereas Lactobacillus reuteri (0.20%) and Lactobacillus jensenii (0.01%) were present at lower levels. Conversely, in the GBS-negative group, Lactobacillus jensenii exhibited the highest abundance, accounting for approximately 1.99%, while Lactobacillus reuteri (0.38%) and Lactobacillus johnsonii (0.27%) were relatively less abundant. These findings contrast with those of Maidment et al., who reported a higher abundance of Lactobacillus crispatus in GBS-negative women, and a significant increase of Lactobacillus iners in the GBS-positive group [38]. Maidment et al. also observed a marked increase in Gardnerella vaginalis abundance at 24 weeks of gestation, suggesting its potential as an early biomarker for GBS colonization. Consistent with our study, we found elevated Gardnerella vaginalis levels in GBS-positive women compared to GBS-negative counterparts. The enrichment of Gardnerella vaginalis in GBS-positive women may promote GBS persistence via synergistic interactions. Gilbert et al. demonstrated that Gardnerella vaginalis can promote GBS vaginal colonization [40]. The co-occurrence of Gardnerella vaginalis and GBS suggests the presence of a pathogenic consortium that exacerbates vaginal dysbiosis and disrupts immune homeostasis.

The vaginal microbiota of healthy women of reproductive age is typically dominated by Lactobacillus species, accompanied by lower abundances of Gardnerella, Corynebacterium and Bifidobacterium. In our study, the Bifidobacterium genus was more abundant in the GBS-positive group relative to the GBS-negative group. The enrichment of Bifidobacterium in GBS-positive group suggests potential ecological changes or compensatory mechanisms in response to microbial shifts induced by GBS colonization. Mu et al. (2023) reported associations between the presence of genera such as Megasphaera, Faecalibacterium, Bifidobacterium, Xanthomonadales, classes Gammaproteobacteria, and Alphaproteobacteria with an increased risk of preterm prelabor rupture of membranes [41]. It is hypothesized that Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium have a complex relationship within the vaginal microenvironment, encompassing both competitive and cooperative interactions. A decline in Lactobacillus abundance may enable Bifidobacterium and other potential pathogens to proliferate and dominate. LEfSe analysis further identified Gemella as a discriminative taxon enriched in GBS-negative women. Like Lactobacillus, Gemella contributes to maintaining vaginal acidity, highlighting its possible role in preserving microbial homeostasis [42]. Its depletion in GBS-positive women may indicate a broader collapse of protective microbiota, thereby facilitating colonization by opportunistic pathogens.

The vaginal microbiota plays a crucial role in female reproductive health and is closely linked to adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as preterm birth and premature rupture of membranes, as well as gynecological conditions like bacterial vaginosis [43]. Typically, the vaginal microbiota is dominated by Lactobacillus species, and a decline in their abundance is directly associated with vaginal dysbiosis, which can trigger inflammatory responses and increase disease risk. Although our study showed that that the incidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes was higher in the GBS-positive group compared to the GBS-negative group. However, not all Lactobacillus species are indicative of positive pregnancy outcomes. Research has shown that variations in the type and abundance of Lactobacillus correlate with the risk of spontaneous preterm birth. For example, Shan Sun et al. reported that women whose vaginal microbiota was dominated by Lactobacillus crispatus had a significantly lower risk of spontaneous preterm birth compared to those dominated by Lactobacillus iners [44]. This suggests that a high abundance of Lactobacillus alone should not be taken as a sole marker of a healthy vaginal microbiome. Furthermore, differences in vaginal microbiota composition have been observed among different ethnic groups. Vaginal bacterial communities dominated by species of Lactobacillus were found in 80.2% and 89.7% of Asian and white women, respectively, but in only 59.6% and 61.9% of Hispanic and black women, respectively [42]. Notably, the proportion of vaginal Lactobacillus in Black women was significantly lower than that in white women during early and mid-pregnancy. These disparities may be influenced by a variety of factors, including hygiene practices, contraceptive use, sexual behaviors, and other lifestyle factors [45].

In recent years, the role of inflammatory responses in the pathogenesis and progression of vaginal diseases has received increasing attention. Hematological parameters including complete blood count, hs-CRP, PCT, and IL-6 exert diverse biological functions and serve as key inflammatory markers and immune regulators involved in the pathophysiology of various diseases [46, 47]. This study highlighted significant differences in inflammatory biomarkers between GBS-negative and GBS-positive individuals. The findings strongly suggested that markers such as hs-CRP, IL-6, and PCT could be effective tools for identifying and monitoring inflammatory responses to GBS infections. These biomarkers were also highly intercorrelated, demonstrating their collective involvement in systemic immune activation. Dong et al. reported elevated levels of IL-6, TNF-α, PCT, and hs-CRP in the peripheral blood of pregnant women with GBS infection, consistent with the findings of this study [48]. The significant increase in inflammatory markers (NEU, hs-CRP, IL-6, PCT, ESR) among GBS-positive women highlights the systemic consequences of localized vaginal dysbiosis. The observed inverse correlation between Lactobacillus abundance and inflammatory markers (hs-CRP, IL-6, ESR) suggests that depletion of Lactobacillus disrupts immune homeostasis, facilitating pathogen-driven inflammation. This pattern parallels observations in gut microbiota research, where reduced Lactobacillus levels are associated with elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines [49]. Mechanistically, pathogenic overgrowth likely induces endotoxin release, potentially activating Toll-like receptors and NF-κB pathways, as suggested by prior studies, thereby amplifying cytokine production [37, 50]. Interestingly, the positive association between Bifidobacterium and inflammatory markers hs-CRP and IL-6 contrasts with its usual characterization as a beneficial genus. This paradox may reflect strain-specific or context-dependent behavior, highlighting the complexity of host-microbe interactions. In dysbiotic environments, Bifidobacterium proliferation may contribute to inflammation, similar to opportunistic pathogens like Escherichia coli [49]. These findings emphasize the intricate crosstalk between microbiota and the immune system and highlight the importance of strain-level analyses to better understand their roles.

The strong association between GBS-induced dysbiosis and systemic inflammation underscores the need for clinical monitoring of inflammatory markers in GBS-positive pregnancies. Interventions targeting vaginal microbiota, such as Lactobacillus probiotics or prebiotics, may help restore microbial balance and reduce inflammation, though clinical trials are needed to confirm efficacy. Glick demonstrated the efficacy of Lactobacilli in reducing inflammation in vaginitis, supporting this approach [51]. However, challenges remain in translating microbiota modulation into clinical practice, including strain selection, delivery methods, and personalized dosing.

This study has limitations, including its cross-sectional design and modest sample size, which preclude causal inferences. Future research should: Expand cohort size and diversity to validate findings across populations. Employ longitudinal designs to explore causal relationships and long-term impacts on maternal-fetal health. Investigate therapeutic efficacy of Lactobacillus probiotics or vaginal microbiota transplantation in randomized trials [51, 52].

Conclusion

Our study elucidates for the first time the intricate interplay between GBS colonization, vaginal microbiota dysbiosis, and systemic inflammation in late pregnancy. GBS colonization disrupts the balance of the vaginal microbiota, contributing to a pro-inflammatory state that may heighten the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes. In comparison to women who are not colonized by GBS, GBS colonization may be associated with a decrease in the abundance of Lactobacillus Jensenii and Lactobacillus reuteri, as well as an increase in Gardnerella vaginalis and Lactobacillus johnsonii. Restoring Lactobacillus (specific microbial species) dominance may help mitigate inflammation and promote maternal and fetal health. Future research should focus on longitudinal monitoring of microbiota dynamics and the development of targeted interventions to prevent or manage GBS-related complications.
