Factor XI Inhibitors in Atrial Fibrillation: Insights and Implications from Recent Trials
Authors: Rosemary B Falama, Lisa R Matsumoto, Marie E Piatski, Lukas Seifer, Aditya Malhotra, Owen J T McCarty, Joseph J Shatzel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStroke prevention remains a cornerstone of the management of atrial fibrillation (AF) given the increased risk of thromboembolic events. While direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have significantly advanced stroke prophylaxis in AF, bleeding risks remain a challenge and preclude use in some populations. Furthermore, evidence indicates significant undertreatment in AF, with up to 40% of patients receiving incorrect anticoagulant dosing or no anticoagulation at all, further elevating their stroke risk. Novel anticoagulants targeting factor (F)XI are emerging as a promising alternative, offering the potential to reduce thromboembolic events whilst minimizing bleeding risks. Inhibitors under development include small molecules, monoclonal antibodies, and antisense oligonucleotides. These agents have demonstrated favorable safety profiles when compared to DOACs in early phase and some late phase trials. For example abelacimab, an anti-FXI monoclonal antibody, demonstrated superior safety with lower bleeding rates when compared to rivaroxaban in patients with AF. However, the efficacy of these drugs remains uncertain. However, the OCEANIC-AF trial, which studied the small molecule FXI inhibitor asundexian, was terminated early due to lack of efficacy as there was an increased rate of stroke and systematic embolism compared to the DOAC apixaban, raising new concerns about the utility of targeting FXI. However, many FXI inhibitors are currently undergoing phase 3 trials, and as such the true efficacy of the full class of FXI inhibitors remains to be elucidated. This review aims to provide a comprehensive synopsis of the landscape of FXI inhibitors in development for AF, their advantages, challenges, and known utility with a focus on how they may be applied to future anticoagulation therapy for AF.Keywords: Factor XI inhibitors, Anticoagulation, Stroke prevention, Thromboembolism, Direct oral anticoagulants, Hemostasis, Monoclonal antibodies

Key Summary Points

Factor XI (FXI) inhibitors aim to uncouple thrombosis from hemostasis, potentially reducing stroke risk in atrial fibrillation (AF) while causing fewer bleeding complications than current direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs).Early-phase trials show strong safety signals across multiple drug classes (small molecules, monoclonal antibodies, antisense oligonucleotides), with reduced or comparable bleeding compared to DOACs/low molecular weight heparin.Efficacy in AF remains uncertain—the first phase III trial (asundexian, OCEANIC-AF) was terminated early due to increased stroke/systemic embolism versus apixaban.Ongoing phase III trials of milvexian and abelacimab will be crucial to determine whether FXI inhibition can provide effective stroke prevention in AF.These agents may still fill an unmet need for patients who cannot tolerate standard anticoagulation, particularly those with high bleeding risk or renal impairment.

Introduction

Stroke prevention is a cornerstone of the management of atrial fibrillation (AF) [1, 2], a condition characterized by atrial hypercoagulability and increased stroke risk within hours of onset [2]. Strokes attributed to AF are increased in severity, and may be recurrent, resulting in substantial disability [3–7].

Treatment options for primary and secondary stroke prevention in AF have advanced significantly from vitamin K antagonists to the emergence of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) as the modern standard of care[2]. Despite this progress, however, DOACs still carry a residual risk of major bleeding, estimated at 1.5–3.6% per year [1]. This is particularly concerning for patients with renal insufficiency, as well as those with bleeding risk factors, which limits the clinical utility of DOACs [8–10]. Furthermore, some data suggest that approximately 40% of AF patients remain undertreated, either by receiving suboptimal dosing (9.5%) or no treatment at all, leaving them vulnerable to life-threatening thromboembolic events [8, 11, 12]. This highlights a critical gap in modern anticoagulation strategies that have not yet found the ideal balance between efficacy and safety [11].

Factor (F)XI is a minor component in normal hemostasis, and the development of inhibitors targeting FXI leverages the natural observation that individuals born with FXI deficiency have a mild bleeding phenotype, presenting a unique opportunity for therapeutic targeting [9, 13]. The development of novel FXI inhibitors that are potentially safer than the current standard suggests great promise by potentially reducing thrombotic events without imparting the bleeding risks associated with traditional anticoagulants [8]. The enhanced safety profiles demonstrated by select agents in phase I and II trials, along with pooled meta-analyses, have suggested significant safety benefits compared to existing treatments [11, 14, 15]. Several prevailing hypotheses have emerged supporting the pharmacologic development of FXI inhibitors; notably upstream inhibition of the coagulation cascade has the potential to uncouple the mechanisms underlying thrombosis from hemostasis, making FXI an even safer target to provide anticoagulation than the mechanisms underlying DOACs [16, 17]. Additionally, patients with AF who experienced ischemic events have been found to have significantly higher levels of FXIa despite anticoagulation, elucidating novel markers of their resultant prothrombotic state [18, 19]. With this rationale in mind, multiple drugs focused on FXI are in development, incorporating numerous different mechanisms and pharmacokinetics [16].

Thus far, FXI inhibitors have demonstrated a promising clinical safety profile, showing reduced or similar bleeding risks when compared to the current practice use of DOACs [11, 14, 15]. Beyond safety data, phase II trials reveal early signals that FXIa inhibition may play a more pivotal role in reducing rates of venous thromboembolism (VTE) compared to low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) [16]. However, the efficacy of targeting FXI in AF remains uncertain, with at least one trial halted early for efficacy concerns, leaving a critical gap in our understanding [16]. Additionally, significant areas of unmet need, particularly for patients with contraindications to DOACs, highlight a significant research gap [20]. This review will survey the current landscape of FXI inhibitor development in cardiology, providing insights into successes and failures of completed and ongoing trials to evaluate their potential in the management of AF and whether this will eventually transform the current management of AF.

Role of Factor XI in Coagulation

Factor XI is a member of the initial phase of the intrinsic pathway of coagulation. FXI, factor XII (FXII), plasma prekallikrein (PK), and the nonenzymatic cofactor high molecular weight kininogen (HK) together make up the contact pathway (Fig. 1) [21, 22]. FXI activation is amplified by thrombin and can be triggered downstream of the activation of FXII by foreign surfaces [23]. Uniquely, FXI appears to play a key role in the pathology of thrombosis, but also appears to be largely unnecessary for the fibrin generation needed to staunch bleeding (hemostasis) in most settings [8, 24]. Although both thrombosis and hemostasis fundamentally rely on the initiation and amplification of thrombin generation, the rate and location of thrombin generation leads to disparate results: the formation of a hemostatic plug to stop bleeding and mend vessel injury, or rampant fibrin formation that pathologically occludes the vessel lumen [24, 25].

Clinical observation in those born with FXI deficiency formed the biomechanistic rationale for the development of FXI inhibitors [22]. Bleeding in patients with congenital FXI deficiency, known formally a hemophilia C, a rare disorder characterized by decreased levels of coagulation factor XI, remains relatively mild or nonexistent in comparison to patients with hemophilia A or B [13]. Recent work suggests that select biomarkers including plasma levels of tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) may help distinguish FXI-bleeders from nonbleeders [26]. Surgical procedures or trauma typically trigger bleeding in FXI-deficient patients, rather than the spontaneous muscular or gastrointestinal bleeds seen with other coagulation factor deficiencies [13, 27]. FXI deficiency also appears to be associated with decreased risks for major adverse cardiac events and VTE [27]. Conversely, elevated levels of FXI have been linked to increased thrombotic risk, particularly deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and stroke [28]. These data suggest that FXI may be a pharmacologic target to achieve an antithrombotic effect, while preventing the bleeding phenotype seen with other forms of anticoagulation [28].

Current FXI Inhibitors in Development and Their Clinical Development Pathway

Multiple FXI and FXIa inhibitors with differing mechanisms of action have been developed thus far and are in various stages of clinical development [16]. These include antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), which inhibit the biosynthesis of FXIa, as well as small molecules, monoclonal antibodies, and aptamers, which directly inhibit FXI activation of FXIa activity [16]. Of these inhibitors, three classes have been clinically tested to date: ASOs, small molecules, and monoclonal antibodies. Small molecules tend to have a “fast onset–fast offset” profile while ASOs and antibody-based therapies tend to have prolonged pharmacodynamics [16]. Importantly, dose-dependent decreases in either FXI concentration or FXIa activity do not appear to be accompanied by dose-dependent increases in both neurological or non-neurological bleeding in clinical trials [15]. There are several advantages of developing drugs narrower in spectrum than established anticoagulants such as vitamin K antagonists or heparin [29]. For instance, these drugs offer unique pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic properties which include variable duration of action, minimal renal elimination, and minimal drug–drug interactions (Table 1) [29]. The versatility and heterogeneity across this class of inhibitors allows for more indications to be explored in patient subsets generally not suitable for traditional anticoagulation.

Synthetic Small Molecules

FXI is dimeric protein consisting of two units linked by a disulfide bond. Each unit consists of a catalytic domain and four apple (A1–A4) domains, a conserved, non-catalytic protein domains that mediate protein–protein interactions and regulatory functions in FXI [30]. The apple domains allow for interaction with other proteins in the coagulation cascade. In general, small molecule FXI inhibitors have been developed to reversibly bind to the active catalytic domain of FXIa [31]. They have a relatively low molecular weight and are classified by their central domain chemistry (i.e., acyclic, monocylic, bicyclic) [15]. Their low molecular weight allows for better metabolism and cellular penetration [15]. However, the specificity of these inhibitors remain a challenge given the homology of the FXI active domain with other serine proteases of the coagulation cascade [30]. Still, small molecules tend to have fast onset–fast offset profile which can be clinically favored [16]. However, some agents have not demonstrated complete inhibition of FXIa activity at current doses, while longer-acting agents may be more efficacious in certain clinical settings [32].

Asudexian: A 2-Oxopyridine-Based Molecule

Phase 2 trials of the small molecule FXIa inhibitor asundexian resulted in lower rates of neurological and non-neurological bleeding compared with standard dose apixaban in patients with AF [33]. However, the asundexian phase 3 trial evaluating AF (OCEANIC-AF) was terminated early due an increased rate of stroke or systemic embolism in the intervention group compared to the group receiving apixaban [34]. OCEANIC-AF was the first large multicenter phase 3 trial in patients with AF with an indication for anticoagulation based on their CHA2DS2-VASc score. A total of 14,810 patients were randomized with a mean age of 73.9 years, of which 64.8% were men. Despite positive safety signals in PACIFIC-AF, a phase 2 dose-finding and safety evaluation trial, there was notably three times higher risk of strokes in the asundexian arm compared to the apixaban arm. These effects were also noted early, with early separation of curves and therefore the increased stroke was attributed to asundexian. At the dose studied in this trial, this does not support substitution of DOACs in routine practice. What remains unknown is whether alternative dosing strategies or different agents in the class such as milvexian will be comparable DOACs [34].

Milvexian: A Macrocyclic Derivative

Phase 2 trials of the oral FXIa inhibitor milvexian have also demonstrated that it is safe and well tolerated [35]. A total of 1242 patients undergoing elective knee arthroplasty were randomized to different doses of milvexian or enoxaparin, with primary efficacy outcome being venous thromboembolism and primary safety outcome being bleeding. There was a 12% incidence of venous thromboembolism in the twice daily milvexian group compared to 21% incidence in patients taking enoxaparin (p < 0.001). Rates of bleeding were comparable between the groups. It provided proof of principle that milvexian is effective and reduced the incidence of venous thromboembolism after elective surgery. Despite the promising results, it still remains unknown whether it would be effective in preventing arterial disease in patients with AF, where the mechanism of thrombosis may differ [36]. The phase 3 trial LIBREXIA-AF is currently investigating milvexian compared to apixaban for the prevention of stroke in patients with AF [37].

Monoclonal Antibodies

Monoclonal antibodies with activity against FXI, FXIa, or both the zymogen and activated serine protease forms of FXI include abelacimab, BAY 1831865, gruticibart (Xisomab, 3G3, AB023), and osocimab [38]. They are administered subcutaneously or intravenously with rapid onset of action of hours to days. However, their pharmacologic effect can lasts weeks, independent of renal or liver excretion or metabolism and may depend on phagocytic cells [15]. These properties can allow for rapid suppression of FXI activity with continued maintenance that can support monthly administration [38]. Additionally, because of the favorable in vivo profile, there is an exceedingly low risk of drug–drug interactions [38]. Some have surmised this administration profile to achieve increased adherence to therapy [38]. Some of the concerns surrounding antibody-based therapies may be their tendency to cause immunogenicity; however, to date, neither pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic studies have seen development of autoantibodies to FXI-based monoclonal antibodies out to 170 days [38].

Abelacimab

High-affinity binding of abelacimab to the catalytic domain of FXI locks the protein in a zymogen formation, thereby preventing its activation by FXIIa or thrombin [31]. This is unique to abelacimab as the only monoclonal antibody in development with this specific mechanism [38]. In patients post-knee replacement, abelacimab has demonstrated superiority to enoxaparin in DVT prevention [39]. Phase 2 trials of abelacimab demonstrated 62–69% lower bleeding risk and fewer non-neurological bleeding events compared to rivaroxaban in patients with AF (AZALEA-TIMI 71) [40]. While this is an impressive safety signal observed, phase 3 trials are needed to determine true efficacy and therefore this drug remains experimental and cannot be viewed as a replacement for DOACs [40]. Abelacimab is currently undergoing a phase 3 trial evaluating the rate of stroke and systemic embolism in patients with AF (LILAC-TIMI 76) [9].

Antisense Oligonucleotides (ASOs)

ASOs are pharmacologically generated single-stranded DNA segments, usually between 12 and 30 bases, which bind and degrade the mRNA of the target protein, thus reducing intrahepatic synthesis [15, 31]. Antisense therapy is based on base pair hybridization, which allows for selective binding of complementary mRNA. The ability of an ASO to degrade targeted mRNA occurs via a nuclease RNAse H1-dependent mechanism which leads to a reduction in the corresponding protein (Fig. 1) [28]. FXI is synthesized in the liver, which is a receptive tissue for ASO therapy. In terms of pharmacokinetics, ASOs have prolonged elimination half-lives and therefore they are often administered once weekly subcutaneously with a slow onset of action of weeks. They also require parenteral administration, without the need for hepatic and renal metabolism and excretion [41]. ASOs targeting FXI that have undergone phase 2 clinical trials include fesomersen (IONIS-FXI-Rx). In phase 2 trials, 300 mg ISIS-FXIRx demonstrated superiority to enoxaparin in reduction of rate of venous thromboembolism in patients who have undergone total knee arthroplasty [42]. Phase 2 trials of fesomersen have demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction of FXI without major bleeding events in patients with end-stage renal disease undergoing hemodialysis [43]. No phase III trials utilizing ASOs against FXI have been reported or are ongoing in AF at the time of this writing.

Safety Data

As an emerging class of anticoagulants, evidence from phase II trials and meta-analyses suggest that FXI inhibitors provide similar or improved rates of major and clinically relevant non major bleeding when compared to DOACs or LMWH [44].

Preclinical and Phase I Trials: Early Safety and Tolerability Data

Preclinical studies have provided strong mechanistic rationale for the development of FXI inhibitors, with FXI knockout mice data affirming the observations seen in humans, namely reduced thromboembolism with preserved hemostasis [45]. In clinical studies, FXI inhibitors have demonstrated excellent tolerability, and minimal adverse events in early data [46]. For example, the most commonly reported adverse events with milvexian were low grade hypertension and constipation [35].

Additionally, no convincing evidence of immunogenicity in regards to FXI monoclonal antibodies has emerged [38, 47]. Early trial data of anti-FXI mAbs suggest a significant reduction in biomarkers of coagulation factor activity without a corresponding increase in clinically relevant bleeding [47].

Phase II Trials: Clinical Safety and Efficacy Signals

Phase II trials exploring the safety of these novel inhibitors include testing in healthy populations as prophylaxis for VTE undergoing orthopedic surgery, where venography was used to detect subclinical thrombosis within 2 weeks [44]. Studying this selected population with clear pathological mechanisms for VTE is key for dose findings, proof of concept for efficacy, and bleeding outcomes. All doses of FXI inhibitors were more effective than LMWH at preventing VTE across studies that used the orthopedic surgery model [44]. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews confirmed that bleeding rates were non-inferior compared to DOACs and superior compared to LMWH [44]. Even more remarkably, recently published abelacimab data, reported as part of the AZALEA-TIMI 71 trial, found the monoclonal antibody provoked minimal gastrointestinal bleeding, which is a rare but relevant issue with DOACs which currently limits their use [40]. These phase II findings reinforce that FXI inhibition provides antithrombotic protection while sparing primary hemostatic function and highlights the potential clinical advantage of these agents in populations where bleeding risk may limit the use of conventional anticoagulants (Table 2) [44]. Despite the promise that this class of anticoagulants may offer, robust phase 3 investigations are required to determine the true efficacy of FXI inhibition.

Efficacy of FXI Inhibitors in AF

Phase III trials are being performed to obtain definitive clinical evidence for whether FXI inhibitors offer effective stroke prophylaxis in AF. Several current trials are ongoing in AF including the small molecule inhibitor milvexian and the monoclonal antibody abelacimab [37, 39]. In terms of efficacy, ongoing trials may soon determine the efficacy of these drugs in patients with AF. Through the phase 2 trial AXIOMATIC-TKR, milvexian has been shown to be effective after knee arthroplasty in significantly reducing the incidence of venous thromboembolism compared to enoxaparin, with a low risk of bleeding [35]. AXIOMATIC-SSP, however, did not demonstrate a consistent dose–response with a combination of milvexian with standard antiplatelet treatment in patients with acute ischemic stroke. Investigators are utilizing the results from AXIOMATIC-SSP to inform the design of their phase 3 trials [35]. As mentioned earlier, milvexian is currently undergoing a phase 3 trial (LIBREXIA AF) to determine its efficacy in stroke prevention while limiting bleeding risk in patients with AF [37]. With LIBREXIA AF, investigators aim to compare milvexian with apixaban in participants with AF or atrial flutter [37]. Similarly, abelacimab has demonstrated superiority to enoxaparin in DVT prevention in patients post-orthopedic surgery, and has progressed to phase III trials [39]. With the ongoing LILAC-TIMI, investigators may determine the efficacy of abelacimab in patients with AF [9].

Why OCEANIC-AF Failed?

The OCEANIC-AF trial was the first phase III study conducted evaluating FXI inhibitors in AF [34]. While asundexian showed a promising safety profile compared to apixaban in earlier trials [33], patients taking asundexian had an unacceptable rate of systemic embolism—almost four times more likely—which led to early termination of the trial [34]. It will be worth analyzing the potential reasons for the failure of this trial and what this could mean for FXI inhibitors in general and for use in AF in particular [48].

One possible explanation for the failure is that asundexian may have been underdosed and failed to achieve a complete therapeutic effect [33]. Earlier dose-finding phase II trials showed that asundexian inhibited 92% of FXIa activity at trough level and 94% of FXIa activity at peak concentrations [33]. However, optimal efficacy may require higher (perhaps even 99%) suppression of FXIa activity. Insufficient suppression of FXIa activity could allow escape levels of thrombin generation, which may explain the trial results [48, 49]. Given that this dose may be too low to have achieved thromboembolic protection, further studies could be used to evaluate whether higher doses would have non-inferior outcomes when compared to DOACs. It is notable that much of the data that encouraged the development of FXI inhibitors is taken from epidemiological studies of patients with inborn FXI deficiency [50, 51]. In these studies, a decrease in stroke and systemic embolisms is seen in patients with moderate-severe deficiency (usually defined as FXIa activity < 30%)—by this definition, asundexian, with only 8% FXIa activity remaining at trough, should largely have the same benefits as seen in patients with inborn moderate-severe FXI deficiency [50].

Another important consideration when contemplating the failure of the OCEANIC-AF trial is the choice of apixaban as a comparator. Apixaban is a well-established, highly effective anticoagulant for preventing strokes in AF, with robust safety data. However, apixaban is contraindicated in several populations such as those with high risk of bleeding or prohibited organ functions; exploring FXI inhibitors in these populations, where a comparator is no treatment, may still show a potential benefit [48]. For example, the trial excluded patients with EGFR < 25 or those on dialysis, both populations in whom DOACs are contraindicated, but in whom asundexian may have been preferable. Additional subgroup data from the OCEANIC-AF trial suggested better response in patients who were oral anticoagulant-naive (≤ 6 consecutive weeks of treatment with oral anticoagulant prior to randomization), while the majority of the population was OAC experienced (OAC experienced 12,317 (83.2%) vs naive 2493 (16.8%)) [34]. Further studies aim to identify subpopulations in which asundexian might lead to more promising results [52]. Another phase 3 trial, OCEANIC-AFINA, is set to evaluate asundexian against a placebo in patients with AF who are at high risk of stroke but are considered unsuitable for anticoagulant therapy due to high bleeding risk or prohibited organ function [52, 53].

Finally, one last consideration is whether FXI is an ideal target for preventing systemic embolism in AF [49]. Mechanistic insights into whether FXI is a suitable target for AF can be extrapolated from preclinical data [54]. FXI may not represent the dominant mechanism of thrombogenesis in AF [54]. Despite a reasonable number of early-phase trials in orthopedic patients demonstrating good safety profiles, the mechanisms of venous thromboembolism following orthopedic surgery primarily involve local venous inflammation, whereas in AF, thrombogenesis is more closely related to stasis and altered flow dynamics [54]. Escape mechanisms and bypass pathways may still permit clot formation and propagation; for instance, tissue factor (TF)-mediated activation may continue to drive thrombosis independently of FXI. A secondary mechanism whereby kallikrein directly interacts with FIX, although not yet validated in human studies, has been also proposed [54]. While some studies have shown elevated levels of FXI in patients who presented with strokes and were anticoagulated [55], FXI inhibition may not be potent enough in isolation to prevent embolism in AF [48]. Perhaps, only inhibition of one factor in the intrinsic pathway is not sufficient to cause suppression in the final common pathway of coagulation to suppress pathological thrombin generation [48].

It is too early to determine whether this represents a class failure. When comparing asundexian with milvexian, another small molecule inhibitor, there are currently no data from direct head-to-head clinical trials [54]. However, some evidence suggests that milvexian may exhibit greater potency, as higher doses have demonstrated cross-reactivity with other serine proteases, which may indicate increased robustness [54]. Within the same class, abelacimab has shown near-complete suppression of FXI activity [56]. The termination of OCEANIC-AF raises concerns regarding the broader context of FXI inhibitors in AF. Whilst this class of drugs has attractive pharmacokinetics and safety profiles, the results were not anticipated and multiple factors as described may have led to this outcome. It remains too early to say how the entire class of FXI inhibitors may fare in this indication based on this sole trial.

Ongoing Trials Actively Recruiting

The phase III LILAC-TIMI 76 trial evaluating abelacimab is a currently recruiting study comparing abelacimab to placebo. The primary efficacy outcome is stroke and systemic embolism, while the principal safety outcome is BARC type 3c/5 bleeding. The population targeted for this trial consists of patients with AF who are not candidate for traditional anticoagulation. The expected completion date is in 2026. Milvexian is being evaluated in a phase III clinical trial (LIBREXIA-AF trial) that is currently recruiting patients. This trial aims to compare milvexian 100 mg twice daily with apixaban 2.5/5 mg twice daily. The primary efficacy outcome includes the incidence of stroke and systemic embolism. The population targeted for this study consists of patients with AF who are indicated for anticoagulation therapy (Fig. 1).

Conclusion

FXI inhibitors represent an evolving class of anticoagulants that can provide robust efficacy with improved safety compared to the current standard, offering the potential for safer anticoagulation therapy in AF. However, their clinical utility remains uncertain, with ongoing trials to assess their safety and efficacy profile. Despite the termination of the first phase III trial due to increased systemic embolism, researchers are still exploring other drugs with different pharmacokinetics for the same pathway. Their success will depend on whether they can prove robust anticoagulation without compromising safety.
