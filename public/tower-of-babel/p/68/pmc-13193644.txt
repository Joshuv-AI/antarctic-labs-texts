Peripheral Neuropathy-Predominant Adult-Onset Autosomal Recessive Spastic Ataxia of Charlevoix-Saguenay: Novel Variant in the SACS gene
Authors: Anil Dash, Farsana Mustafa, Ayush Agarwal, Divyani Garg, Awadh K Pandit, Achal K Srivastava, Divya M Radhakrishnan
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Autosomal recessive spastic ataxia of Charlevoix–Saguenay (ARSACS) was first described by Bouchard et al. (1978) in the Charlevoix–Saguenay region of Quebec.[1] The exact worldwide incidence and prevalence of this disease remain unknown. The disease is caused by biallelic mutations in the SACS gene on chromosome 13q12, which encodes the sacsin protein. It manifests in early childhood with progressive gait unsteadiness, lower limb spasticity, and peripheral neuropathy. Other associated features may include dysarthria, nystagmus, paraparesis, pes cavus, hypertrophy of retinal myelinated fibers, intellectual disability, hearing loss, and cognitive decline. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain reveals atrophy of the superior cerebellar vermis, linear T2 hypointensities in the paramedian pons (tigroid appearance), thickened middle cerebellar peduncles, and a hyperintense rim around the thalami. Given its phenotypic overlap with other hereditary ataxias and spastic paraplegias, ARSACS may be underdiagnosed or misdiagnosed. Here, we describe a case presenting in early adulthood, focusing on the atypical presentation and the novel genetic variant to contribute to the growing understanding of this rare neurodegenerative disorder.

A 34-year-old male, born to nonconsanguineous parents, presented with a 7-year history of markedly reduced sensation to touch on his soles, a cotton-wool sensation in both feet, and worsening balance in the dark. His gait instability and lower-limb symptoms had progressed gradually over the years. Over the past 5 years, there had been a noticeable change in his speech quality. When asked in detail, he reported occasional falls dating back to early childhood. His family history was negative. On examination, he had hammer toes, pes cavus, atrophy of the legs and feet, paraparesis with hypotonia, generalized areflexia, and extensor plantar responses. Sensory examination revealed a bilateral glove-and-stocking pattern of sensory loss with a positive Romberg’s test. He had subtle cerebellar dysarthria and impaired tandem gait. He was diagnosed with probable chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) at an outside centre and had been on immunomodulators for a year.

Based on the patient’s history and clinical examination, a syndromic diagnosis of hereditary demyelinating neuropathy and cerebellar ataxia was considered, prompting further evaluation. The differential diagnoses included Charcot–Marie–Tooth (CMT) disease, Friedreich’s ataxia, complicated hereditary spastic paraplegias, ARSACS, cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis, abetalipoproteinemia, ataxia with vitamin E deficiency, and Troyer syndrome [Table 1]. Routine evaluation was within normal limits. Nerve conduction studies revealed a predominantly demyelinating sensorimotor polyradiculoneuropathy with secondary axonal involvement, as evidenced by slowed conduction velocities, prolonged distal motor latencies, prolonged F-wave latencies, and mildly reduced compound muscle action potential amplitudes. No definite conduction block or temporal dispersion was identified. Although the patient had previously been treated for CIDP, several features argued against an acquired immune neuropathy, including a gradual onset in early childhood, slow progression, lack of response to immunomodulation, and the absence of conduction block or dispersion on electrophysiology. The foot deformities, distal muscle wasting, and subtle cerebellar signs further supported a hereditary neuropathy–ataxia syndrome.

MRI brain [Figure 1] revealed atrophy of the superior cerebellar vermis and linear T2 and Fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hypointensities in the pons. Optical coherence tomography showed thickening of the retinal nerve fiber layer. Peripheral venous blood was sent for whole-exome sequencing, which revealed compound heterozygous variants in the SACS gene, as shown in Table 2. Segregation analysis could not be performed because the parents were unavailable for testing. The nonsense variant c.7273C>T (p.Arg2425Ter) is documented in the ClinVar database, while the frameshift variant c.5670_5680del was absent from both ClinVar and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), suggesting that this represents a novel variant.

Our case highlights the growing recognition that ARSACS can present with considerable phenotypic heterogeneity, challenging the classical pediatric presentation paradigm. Traditionally, ARSACS is considered a childhood-onset disorder, with symptoms appearing between 12 and 18 months of age. However, adult-onset cases are increasingly recognized, with some patients presenting symptoms as late as the fifth decade of life.[23]

While the classical ARSACS triad comprises cerebellar ataxia, spasticity, and peripheral neuropathy, recent studies have identified cases in which isolated peripheral neuropathy predominates clinically. This “neuropathy-predominant” phenotype, as in our patient, can initially masquerade as CMT, potentially leading to misdiagnosis and delayed recognition.[4] Although not specific to ARSACS, demyelinating peripheral neuropathy serves as an important differentiating feature from other common hereditary ataxias, where neuropathy is predominantly axonal. This electrophysiological distinction, combined with the characteristic neuroimaging and retinal findings, forms a diagnostic constellation that strongly suggests ARSACS even before genetic confirmation.[56]

The identification of a novel SACS variant (c.5670_5680del) in our patient adds to the growing catalogue of over 200 pathogenic mutations reported worldwide. Both variants in this report are predicted to cause protein truncation via different mechanisms; nonsense mutations and frameshift deletions, respectively. The compound heterozygous state in our patient is consistent with the typical inheritance pattern of ARSACS, in which most non-Quebec cases present with compound heterozygosity rather than homozygous founder mutations.[78] The location of these variants within the SACS gene is noteworthy. The SACS gene comprises nine exons, with exon 10 accounting for approximately 75% of the coding sequence. Most pathogenic variants cluster within this gigantic exon, which encodes critical functional domains of the sacsin protein, which is involved in molecular chaperone activities and neurofilament regulation.[9] A recent case series and literature review identified the c. 8793delA variant as the most common in India, which may suggest a founder effect.[10]

Our case illustrates an expanded phenotypic expression of ARSACS, characterized by adult onset and a neuropathy-predominant initial presentation. The delayed emergence of cerebellar and pyramidal features in our patient contributed to early diagnostic uncertainty. Recognition of this neuropathy-predominant, late-presenting phenotype has important diagnostic implications, particularly in adults with unexplained demyelinating neuropathy and subtle cerebellar signs, where targeted neuroimaging and retinal evaluation may prompt timely genetic confirmation.
