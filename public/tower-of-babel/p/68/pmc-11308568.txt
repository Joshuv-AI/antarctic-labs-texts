Nomogram for intraoperatively acquired pressure injuries in children undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass: a retrospective study
Authors: He Lin, Haiyan Chen, Jiehui Wang, Xiangai Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe aimed to develop and validate a nomogram for predicting the risk of intraoperatively acquired pressure injuries (IAPIs) in children undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB).MethodsThis study retrospectively included 208 children aged 21 days to 8 years who underwent cardiac surgery with CPB in a tertiary hospital in China between January 2020 and October 2023. All patients’ data were collected from the hospital’s medical record system and randomly divided into the training (n = 146) and validation (n = 62) cohorts by a ratio of 7:3. Logistic regression analysis was conducted in the training cohort to identify independent risk factors and establish the nomogram. Finally, calibration curves, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) were performed in both cohorts to validate the predictive ability of the nomogram.Results43 (14.7%) children developed IAPIs. Multivariate analysis showed that low Braden Q scores, use of steroids, skin abnormalities, and low intraoperative SpO2 were independent risk factors for IAPIs. A nomogram integrating the 4 factors was established. The areas under the curve (AUCs) of the nomogram were 0.836 and 0.903 in the training and validation cohorts, respectively. Furthermore, calibration curves and DCA demonstrated good calibration and clinical applicability of the nomogram.ConclusionWe constructed a reliable nomogram based on specific risk factors for children undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB, which could be used as an effective and convenient tool for prevention of IAPIs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-024-04989-4.

Introduction

Intraoperatively acquired pressure injuries (IAPIs) refer to the skin and subcutaneous tissue damage caused by prolonged pressure and shear during surgery, typically located at the bony prominence or at interfaces with medical devices/equipment [1]. In addition to causing pain and negative impacts on body image, IAPIs can lead to infection, reoperation, extended hospitalization, increased treatment costs, and decreased quality of life for patients [2–4]. According to the report from the National Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel (NPUAP) in the United States, IAPIs accounted for 45% of health care-associated pressure injuries, posing additional risks and harm to surgical patients [5]. Importantly, IAPIs are recognized as serious, costly, but preventable medical problems, and the identification of high-risk individuals and risk factors for IAPIs is the first step in preventing IAPIs.

A recent systematic review showed that cardiac surgery patients were among the most susceptible populations to pressure injuries [6]. Compared to other surgical procedures, cardiac surgery exhibits certain characteristics that may contribute to IAPIs, such as prolonged time on the operating table, additional external forces during thoracotomy, as well as the perfusion impairment and hypothermia induced by cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) [7, 8]. It has been reported that the incidence of hospital-acquired pressure injuries in pediatric patients with congenital heart disease ranges from 9.7 to 15.6% [9, 10]. The occurrence of pressure injuries not only causes physical and psychological pain to pediatric patients, but also increases the risk of complications, significantly hindering their postoperative recovery.

The literature on the occurrence and risk factors for pressure injuries in cardiac surgery patients mainly focuses on adult populations. Little is known about the development of pressure injuries in pediatric cardiac surgery patients. These young patients may be susceptible to similar risk factors as their adult counterparts, such as prolonged immobility during complex surgical procedures [11]. However, due to unique anatomical and physiological characteristics, children exhibit certain differences in the occurrence and risk factors of pressure injuries compared to adults. For example, the brain weight of a newborn is approximately 25% of that in adulthood, while the body weight is only around 5% of adult weight. This leads to a higher incidence of occipital pressure injuries in infants, whereas common pressure injury sites in adults include the sacrum, buttocks, and heels [12]. Moreover, some health-related factors of IAPIs development in adults, such as smoking and diabetes, were relatively rare in pediatric patients [13, 14].

In the update of the Prevention and Treatment of Pressure Ulcers: Clinical Practice Guideline 2019 (referred to as Guideline 2019), it was recommended that risk assessment of pressure injury should encompass surgery-related factors and specific factors for children [15]. This suggested that adequate attention should be given to the identification and management of risk factors for pressure injury in the pediatric surgical populations. Currently, there is no valid predictive tool for the risk of IAPIs in pediatric surgical populations, especially in children undergoing cardiac surgery, making accurate risk assessment of IAPIs in these patients relatively challenging. Common pediatric pressure injury scales, such as the Braden Q scale [16] and the Norton scale [17], evaluate relatively few intraoperative factors and lack predictive validity for IAPIs. Relevant research is of great importance, but existing evidence is limited [18]. Accurate literature searches revealed only a few articles reporting the incidence and influencing factors of pressure injuries in children undergoing cardiac surgery; however, these studies focused on hospital-acquired pressure injuries rather than IAPIs [9, 10]. No studies have yet developed a risk prediction model for IAPIs in pediatric cardiac surgery.

To address this issue, we developed and validated a nomogram by retrospectively investigating the specific risk factors for IAPIs in children undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB, with the aim of providing theoretical guidance for clinical preventive interventions in this group.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study.

Setting

All surgeries for patients of this study were performed in the operating room of the Second Affiliated Hospital and Yuying Children’s Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. Data collection took place from October 2023 to December 2023 in our cardiothoracic surgery unit and operating room.

Participants

The study subjects were children who underwent cardiac surgery with CPB in the operating room of a tertiary hospital in China from January 2020 to October 2023. Inclusion criteria: (1) aged between 21 days and 8 years (recommended age range for use of the Braden Q scale [19, 20]); (2) underwent cardiac surgery with CPB. Exclusion criteria: (1) pressure injuries occurred before surgery; (2) presence of skin disease affecting the observation of IAPIs; (3) intraoperative death (no skin observation record for patients who died intraoperatively); (4) postoperative installation of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) (no skin observation record for this group of patients due to restricted mobility); (5) data missing.

Variables

Based on literature, guidelines, and clinical experience, a total of 18 potential risk factors were included: (1) Demographic: sex, age, and weight. (2) Preoperative factors: serum albumin, hemoglobin, use of steroids, condition of skin, the Braden Q scores, and the grade of the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification system. (3) Intraoperative factors: duration of surgery, duration of CPB, operative position, intraoperative blood loss, intraoperative urine output, minimum oxygen saturation (SpO2), minimum temperature, and use of heating blankets. The primary outcome variable was the diagnosis of IAPIs, and the remainder included the stages and sites of pressure injuries, as well as duration of hospitalization.

The diagnostic criteria for IAPIs were adopted from the definition of IAPIs in the Prevention and Treatment of Pressure Ulcers: Clinical Practice Guideline 2019 [1]. According to the National Pressure Ulcer Expert Advisory Panel (NPUAP) Pressure Injury Staging System, IAPIs were classified as stages 1 to 4, unstageable, and deep tissue pressure injury (refer to Supplementary Material 1). The preoperative Braden Q scores were assessed on admission. This scale ranges from 7 to 28, with lower scores indicating higher risk for pressure injuries [20]. The definitions and rationale for inclusion of the remaining variables are detailed in Supplementary Material 2.

Data sources

Data were collected from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. To maintain consistency in the results of the review, two trained researchers were assigned to collect the data, and another researcher was responsible for checking the accuracy of the data.

Study size

According to the Kendall’s theory, the sample size is approximately 10 times the number of variables [21]. Considering a 10% sample loss rate, we estimated that about (18 × 10 ÷ 0.9=) 200 samples were needed. Since there were about 60–90 pediatric cardiac surgeries with CPB per year in our hospital, we collected 4-year clinical data. Finally, a total of 43 cases of IAPIs occurred in 208 patients. And 4 predictors of IAPIs were included in the multivariable Logistic regression. The number of events per predictor variable (EPV) was (43/4≈) 11, meeting the criterion of 10–20 EPV proposed by Harrell and colleagues [22, 23].

Statistical methods

The data was randomly divided into training and validation cohorts in a ratio of 7:3 [24]. Non-normally distributed continuous variables and categorical variables were represented by the median (interquartile ranges) and number (proportion), respectively. The differences between variables were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests and chi-square tests by SPSS 27.0. Multivariate Logistic regression analysis was conducted for variables with P-value < 0.1 in univariate analysis to identify independent risk factors for IAPIs. Based on the results of the multivariate analysis, a nomogram was constructed using the “ggplot2” package in R 4.3.2 software. Finally, calibration curves, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) were performed in both cohorts to validate the predictive ability and clinical utility of the nomogram. A P-value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital and Yuying Children’s Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University [2024-K-054-01]. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, the informed consent of patients was waived by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital and Yuying Children’s Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University according to the national regulations [25]. The procedures of this research adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Participants

A total of 208 eligible patients were included in the study [median age: 12 (5, 48) months; male: 112 (53.8%)], of which 43 developed IAPIs, with an incidence of 20.7%. The patient enrollment process was shown in Fig. 1. The 208 patients were divided into training and validation cohorts in a ratio of 7:3, and there were no significant differences in variables between the two cohorts (Table 1). Table 2 demonstrated the characteristics of patients’ IAPIs. As can be seen, the majority of patients (91.1%) with IAPIs were at stage 1 and the most common site of pressure injury was the occipital (22.2%), followed by the sacrococcygeal (18.5%) and ankle/heel (18.5%). No medical device-related pressure injuries occurred during surgery.

Risk factors analysis

We performed univariate analysis in the training cohort, which showed P-value less than 0.1 for 4 variables (Table 3): use of steroids (P = 0.075), skin abnormalities (P = 0.019), Braden Q scores (P = 0.002), and intraoperative SpO2 (P<0.001). The 4 variables were then integrated into the multivariate Logistic regression analysis. The results revealed that use of steroids (odds ratio [OR], 3.113; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.113–8.807) and skin abnormalities (OR, 3.266; 95%CI, 1.062–10.044) were independent risk factors for IAPIs, whereas high Braden Q scores (OR, 0.672; 95%CI, 0.476–0.948) and intraoperative SpO2 (OR, 0.882; 95%CI, 0.825–0.943) were independent protective factors for IAPIs (Table 4).

Development and validation of the nomogram

Based on the 4 risk predictors, we developed a nomogram predicting the probability for IAPIs in children undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB (Fig. 2). To better illustrate the usage of the nomogram, we have annotated the characteristics of a patient within the nomogram. As depicted in Fig. 2, the patient had a Braden Q score of 24, an SpO2 level of 80%, and exhibited neither skin abnormalities nor steroid use. The points associated with the four variables are 36.3, 57.5, 0, and 0, respectively. Therefore, the patient’s total point is approximately 94, which corresponds to a 65% risk probability for developing IAPIs.

Fig. 2Nomogram predicting the probability for IAPIs in children undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass

Nomogram predicting the probability for IAPIs in children undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass

Subsequently, we tested the prediction performance of nomogram in both training and validation cohorts. The areas under the curve (AUCs) of the nomogram were 0.836 (95%CI: 0.764–0.907) and 0.903 (95%CI: 0.824–0.982) in the training and validation cohorts, demonstrating a relatively good predictive accuracy (Fig. 3A and D). Furthermore, calibration curves demonstrated good consistency between predicted probabilities and actual probabilities (Fig. 3B and E). In DCA, the horizontal line represents that none of the patients received clinical pressure injury intervention, whereas the other oblique line represents that all of the patients received clinical intervention. When the net benefit of the nomogram exceeds that of the two extreme curves, it indicates that the nomogram possesses clinical utility at a specific threshold. We observed that the net benefit of applying the nomogram was significantly higher than “All” and “None” over a wide range of the threshold probability of IAPIs occurrence, suggesting good clinical applicability of the nomogram (the area between the two dotted lines in the Fig. 3C and F).

Discussion

The present study developed the first nomogram for predicting the risk of IAPIs in children undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB by retrospectively analyzing their clinical data. Among several potential factors for IAPIs, we finally screened 4 independent risk factors to construct a nomogram for predicting the risk of developing IAPIs. ROC curves and calibration curves demonstrated the nomogram’s good accuracy and calibration. DCA indicated that the nomogram possesses excellent clinical applicability.

This study showed that Braden Q score was an important predictive factors of IAPIs. The Braden Q scale, adapted from the Braden scale by Quigley and Curley in 1996, was the most commonly used scale for assessing pressure injury risk in pediatrics [26, 27]. Although recent studies have noted that the Braden Q scale has moderate predictive accuracy and that there is a need for further modification or development of new tools, it remains the more reliable pressure injury assessment tool available [28, 29]. In this study, Braden Q scores demonstrated a significant association with IAPIs (P < 0.01). We believed that the nomogram model, which combines specific perioperative risk factors on the basis of the Braden Q scores, can effectively improve the predictive validity of IAPIs.

Consistent with the findings of Shields and Lu et al. [30–32], the incidence of IAPIs was higher in patients using steroids. Reduction of growth factors induced by steroids may be an important factor in their promotion of pressure injury development [33]. Skin abnormalities, such as moisture, dryness, and edema, can weaken the skin’s barrier function and also contribute to pressure injury [34]. Compared to adults, children have a thinner and functionally immature epidermal layer, which carries a high risk for excess water loss and a higher permeability to chemicals, making them more susceptible to skin abnormalities [35]. Therefore, it’s recommended to provide more meticulous skin care for this surgical group, including utilizing skin barrier products and highly absorbent incontinence products [1].

Low intraoperative oxygen saturation was the most significant factor in this study. The principle of low oxygen saturation leading to IAPIs was mainly due to hypoxia causing tissue cell damage along with reduced tissue tolerance to pressure and shear [36]. The Guideline 2019 also highlighted specific risk factors to consider when assessing PI risk in pediatric patients, such as oxygen saturation, hemoglobin levels, and other indicators of oxygenation status [1]. Kulik et al. [37] proved that SpO2 below 85% was a predictor of the occurrence of hospital-acquired pressure injuries in children with congenital heart disease. This study further supported that intraoperative SpO2 was significantly associated with the development of IAPIs in children undergoing cardiac surgery.

In previous studies, Gao et al. found that length of operation ≥ 6 h (β = 2.66, OR = 14.30) and intraoperative blood loss (β = 0.67, OR = 1.95) were the risk factors for IAPIs in pediatric patients [38]. Other studies exhibited that the age of pediatric population was inversely related to the risk for pressure injury, which is the opposite of adults [39, 40]. In our study, variables such as age, length of surgery, and intraoperative blood loss were not significant factors affecting IAPIs. This inconsistency may be caused by differences in study populations. Besides, the effect of procedure duration and CPB duration on IAPIs remained controversial. Chen et al. [41] found that CPB duration was an independent influencing factor on the occurrence of IAPIs in patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery (both adults and children; P < 0.001). However, a study of 286 adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery by Shen et al. [42] emphasized that the duration of surgery, instead of CPB, was a risk factor for IAPIs. These controversies still need more in-depth research to be resolved.

Nomograms were commonly used clinical prediction models with the advantages of convenience and ease of use [43]. The nomogram we constructed had reliable predictive performance in cross-validation. Through this tool, clinical nurses were able to predict the probability of IAPIs in pediatric patients before cardiac surgery, screen patients at high risk of IAPIs, and take preventive measures in advance. The nomogram also suggested that measures such as ensuring that the skin is not excessively moist or dry and maintaining normal intraoperative SpO2 levels can play a positive role in preventing the development of IAPIs. Additionally, for children requiring medications to prevent postoperative complications of CPB, the use of steroids may be unavoidable. Operating room nurses may consider applying gel pads or dressings intraoperatively for such high-risk patients to reduce the incidence of IAPIs.

Limitations

This retrospective study still had some limitations. Firstly, the study population was relatively small, and the number of included variables were limited. Therefore, further studies should be conducted in high-volume centers to investigate more potential risk factors, such as nutritional status and medical device factors. Secondly, in this model, the variable SpO2 was the minimum value recorded during surgery. While this value effectively represents the patient’s worst oxygen saturation state, it does not account for the duration of hypoxemia. In addition, we selected the patients with complete medical records, which may have resulted in some selection bias. Hence, our findings require validation through prospective studies in the future.

Conclusion

Independent risk factors for IAPIs in children undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB included low Braden Q scores, use of steroids, skin abnormalities, and low intraoperative SpO2. The nomogram based on these 4 factors exhibited good predictive performance and clinical applicability. It can serve as an effective and convenient tool to assist nurses in early identification of high-risk patients for IAPIs and making informed clinical decisions, thereby reducing the risk of IAPI occurrence and improving patient outcomes.
