Neuromechanical synergy patterns explain metabolic efficiency differences during the sit-to-walk transition
Authors: Wanli Zang, Jiarong Wu, Chen Zhu, Qiuxia Zhang, Xianzuo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe sit-to-walk (STW) transition is a key component of the Timed Up and Go test, yet the neuromuscular and cross-modal coordination features associated with metabolic cost during STW remain incompletely understood. This study developed a simulation-informed synergy analysis framework combining OpenSim–Umberger metabolic estimation, non-negative matrix factorization (NMF), and mixed-matrix factorization (MMF). Seventy-one healthy participants performed STW trials with kinematics, ground reaction forces, and eight-channel surface electromyography recorded. Model-derived metabolic cost was estimated using participant-specific musculoskeletal simulations, and an STW oxygen-consumption agreement analysis in a validation subsample supported the metabolic estimation workflow. Participants were stratified into residual-based metabolic-cost groups after adjustment for body mass, height, and sex. Across groups, joint kinematics and simulation-derived muscle-level outputs were broadly comparable. NMF identified localized differences in temporal activation coefficients after correction for the number of synergies, with group effects confined to early and late time windows rather than extending across the full analyzed cycle. Exploratory NMF weight comparisons further suggested potential differences in selected muscle weights. MMF characterized non-negative temporal coefficients and cross-modal EMG–biomechanical weight patterns; corrected analyses of MMF temporal coefficients identified limited, localized differences in selected MMF synergies, and exploratory weight comparisons indicated localized differences in selected EMG and biomechanical channel–synergy combinations. These findings suggest that residual-based metabolic-cost phenotypes during STW are associated with localized synergy-related features under broadly similar external movement patterns. Introduction

The sit-to-walk (STW) transition is not only a highly frequent functional task in daily life, but also one of the most mechanically and neurally demanding movements [1–3]. As a key component of the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test [4], STW requires a smooth transition from static seated balance to dynamic gait initiation within a very short time window [5–7]. During this process, the body must rapidly shift the center of mass and coordinate multiple joints and muscle groups to balance power generation with stability control. STW is also clinically relevant because it is embedded in widely used mobility assessments. Because similar task completion may still involve different internal energetic and coordination demands, STW provides a useful task model for examining how movement economy relates to neuromuscular control under broadly comparable external performance.

Metabolic cost is commonly used to quantify movement economy because it reflects the energetic demand required to generate and sustain movement [8–10]. For STW, such quantification is particularly relevant because the task combines postural transition and gait initiation within a short time window. Although respiratory gas analysis provides a reference measure of whole-body metabolic rate during steady-state or repeated locomotor tasks, its temporal resolution is limited for resolving phase-specific energetic changes during a brief and highly transient task such as STW. Respiratory measurements are also affected by delayed dynamic responses, measurement noise, and the need for repeated-trial averaging [11–13]. Physics-based musculoskeletal modeling therefore provides a complementary approach for estimating time-aligned metabolic cost within the STW task window. Platforms such as OpenSim can integrate kinematics and ground reaction forces to perform muscle-driven or tracking simulations, thereby estimating internal muscle states and task-phase-aligned energetic outputs [14, 15]. At the energetic estimation level, widely used muscle metabolic models (e.g., the Umberger series) can be used to compute time-varying metabolic power and have been compared and validated across a range of movement tasks [16–18]. Moreover, even in healthy individuals, energetic cost may exhibit substantial heterogeneity due to natural variability in movement organization [19]. Therefore, simulation-derived metabolic estimates provide a useful way to examine inter-individual variability in metabolic cost during healthy STW.

To interrogate the neural organization underlying this multi-degree-of-freedom task, muscle synergies have been widely used as an operational low-dimensional representation [20–22]. By decomposing high-dimensional muscle activation patterns into a small number of synergy modules and their temporal recruitment trajectories, this framework enables quantitative characterization of neuromuscular “modular control” strategies [20, 23]. Although prior work has described synergy structures during STW, it remains unclear whether these synergy features are related to the energetic cost of performing the transition. This question is important because different patterns of muscle coordination may alter how force generation, momentum transfer, and movement-related mechanical demands are distributed, even when the observable task outcome is similar. Therefore, examining the relationship between synergy organization and metabolic cost may provide a more complete description of healthy STW control than kinematics or muscle activation alone.

Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) is currently the predominant approach for extracting muscle synergies from surface electromyography (sEMG) [23–25]. Because NMF constrains the extracted synergy weights and temporal coefficients to be non-negative, it provides an additive, parts-based representation that is compatible with the physiological interpretation of rectified EMG signals [25, 26]. However, the strict non-negativity constraint limits the extent to which derived synergies can be directly aligned, within the same latent space, with kinematic outputs that contain signed information (e.g., joint angular velocity/acceleration), thereby constraining quantitative characterization of “neural drive–mechanical output” coupling [27]. For highly coupled tasks such as STW, reliance on single-modality EMG synergies alone may be insufficient to explain how synergy recruitment is jointly organized with specific movement outputs, and how this relates to inter-individual differences in metabolic cost. Mixed-matrix factorization (MMF) addresses this issue by allowing constrained and unconstrained signals to be factorized within a unified framework [27]. In the present context, EMG-channel weights can remain non-negative, whereas biomechanical-channel weights can retain signed values. MMF therefore provides a cross-modal representation in which muscle activity and angular-acceleration features can be described within shared components. Used together, NMF and MMF provide complementary information: NMF characterizes EMG-derived muscle-synergy timing and muscle weighting, whereas MMF characterizes EMG-biomechanical weighting patterns that may reflect cross-modal coordination features associated with model-estimated metabolic cost.

Against this background, this study aimed to develop and evaluate a simulation-informed synergy analysis framework for examining neuromuscular and cross-modal coordination features associated with model-estimated metabolic cost during STW. Using multimodal data from 71 healthy participants, we first estimated metabolic cost through participant-specific OpenSim–Umberger simulations and performed residual-based metabolic-cost stratification after accounting for body mass, height, and sex. We then used NMF to characterize EMG-derived muscle synergies and MMF to describe cross-modal weighting patterns linking EMG channels with biomechanical angular-acceleration features. We hypothesized that residual-based metabolic-cost groups would show localized differences in synergy timing and/or cross-modal weighting, even when gross joint kinematics and muscle-level outputs were broadly comparable.

Experimental protocol and multimodal data acquisition

All experiments were conducted in the Biomechanics Laboratory of Soochow University. Before data collection, body mass and BMI were measured using InBody. Kinematic data were recorded using a Vicon three-dimensional motion capture system (16 infrared cameras; MX13; 100 Hz), with 38 reflective markers attached according to a modified Vicon marker set (14 mm diameter). The full marker labels and anatomical placement descriptions are provided in Supplementary Table S1, and representative marker placement is shown in Supplementary Figure S1. GRF was synchronously collected using four embedded Kistler three-dimensional force plates (1000 Hz). Kinematic signals were filtered using a zero-phase second-order Butterworth low-pass filter (6 Hz).

Surface electromyography (sEMG) was collected using a wireless bipolar surface electrode system (YW-wireless; 1000 Hz), recording eight lower-limb muscles to characterize neuromuscular synergies (Supplementary Figure S1). Eight recorded muscles were located in the right lower limb, namely the biceps femoris (BF), lateral head of the gastrocnemius (GL), medial head of the gastrocnemius (GM), rectus femoris (RF), semitendinosus (ST), tibialis anterior (TA), vastus lateralis (VL), and vastus medialis (VM). Electrode placement followed the recommendations of the Surface Electromyography for the Non-Invasive Assessment of Muscles, performed by the same trained tester who palpated the muscle belly, prepared the skin, and secured the sensors (double-sided adhesive plus medical tape reinforcement, with straps added if needed for stability). After placement, signal quality was checked via selective activation of the target muscle, with minor adjustments made as required. The sEMG signals were processed using a zero-phase fourth-order Butterworth band-pass filter (20–400 Hz) to remove motion artifacts and high-frequency noise. The filtered signals were then full-wave rectified and subsequently low-pass filtered at 6 Hz to obtain a smoothed linear envelope.

The STW task was derived from the initial phase of the TUG test. Representative images illustrating the STW task sequence are provided in Supplementary Figure S2. Participants were seated on an adjustable-height chair without a backrest or armrests, with their hands placed alongside their thighs and the hip and knee joints flexed to approximately 90°. In this study, the STW analysis window was defined from the initiation of the sit-to-stand movement to the completion of one full gait cycle, excluding the static sitting phase. Therefore, the chair-contact phase was not included in the kinetic analysis window, with the focus placed on the continuous, lower-limb–driven transition after seat-off. To enhance the robustness of phase identification, multi-signal fusion was employed for event detection and cycle segmentation, integrating vertical pelvis displacement/velocity with bilateral hip, knee, and ankle joint angles and angular velocities, along with duration constraints and threshold-based criteria for automatic segmentation. The STW was ultimately divided into four functional phases: P1 (Flexion), P2 (Extension), P3 (Unload), and P4 (Stance). Specifically, P1 corresponded to trunk forward flexion and momentum generation, P2 to trunk and lower-limb extension and lift, P3 to load transfer and swing-limb unloading, and P4 represented the first gait-cycle window after the transition into stance support and propulsion. Prior to formal data collection, participants completed approximately 10 min of warm-up and task familiarization. A total of 71 healthy individuals without known neurological disorders or recent lower-limb injuries were recruited for this study. All studies and experiments were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Soochow University (Approval No.: SUDA20251015H11). Three successful trials were recorded for each participant. All data were imported into Python 3.11 for post-processing and, following time normalization, resampled to 101 points (0–100%) across the task cycle.

OpenSim–Umberger metabolic power estimation

In OpenSim, the gait2392 musculoskeletal model was used to estimate muscle metabolic expenditure [14, 15, 28]. The model includes three rotational degrees of freedom at the hip, one rotational degree of freedom at the knee, and parameterizes the primary joint motions of the ankle–foot complex (based on the model’s default joint settings). The model was first scaled to each participant based on measured marker positions and estimated joint centers (e.g., hip joint centers were estimated using methods described by Harrington et al. and Kainz et al. 2015), followed by refinement of marker locations/model geometry to better match individual anthropometry [29, 30].

Kinematic and kinetic simulations were performed using a standard inverse kinematics (IK)–residual reduction algorithm (RRA)–computed muscle control (CMC) workflow. First, IK was applied to the experimental kinematic data to obtain joint angle trajectories [14, 31]. RRA was then used to optimize dynamic consistency, and the RRA outputs were used as inputs to CMC [14, 32]. CMC solves for muscle activations via feedback control, enabling forward dynamic simulations to track experimental kinematics and ground reaction forces [32]. This workflow yielded joint angles, angular velocity/angular acceleration, and state variables including muscle activations, muscle power, muscle fiber length, and shortening velocity.

To evaluate the applicability of the OpenSim–Umberger metabolic-power estimation workflow, 20 participants were selected from the 71 healthy participants as a validation subsample for agreement analysis. For each participant, four to five successful STW trials were collected. Respiratory metabolic data and kinematic data were recorded synchronously during the trials, with respiratory metabolism measured using a portable gas-analysis system, MetaMax 3B (Germany) [35].

MMF

MMF was applied to a mixed data matrix containing non-negative EMG channels and signed biomechanical angular-acceleration channels following the standard mixed-matrix factorization procedure described by Scano et al. [27]. In the decomposition, EMG rows in W were constrained to be non-negative, whereas biomechanical rows were left unconstrained. The temporal coefficient matrix C was constrained to be non-negative throughout the optimization, consistent with the additive parts-based representation of MMF. Because the factorization rank (i.e., the number of extracted synergies, N) is a free parameter, MMF was invoked repeatedly within a loop, with N increasing from 1 up to the number of rows of the data matrix D. Thus, C represented non-negative time-varying component-recruitment coefficients, whereas the directional information of biomechanical angular-acceleration channels was encoded by the signed biomechanical rows of W. Iterative updates stopped when the convergence criterion was met. The input matrix for MMF consisted of preprocessed sEMG signals and kinematic angular acceleration signals. The kinematic variables were obtained from the OpenSim outputs and included the angular accelerations of the main bilateral lower-limb joints: hip flexion/extension, hip adduction/abduction, hip internal/external rotation, knee flexion/extension, and ankle plantarflexion/dorsiflexion, resulting in a total of 10 channels. The sign convention followed the default OpenSim joint coordinate definitions, where positive values correspond to hip flexion, hip adduction, hip internal rotation, knee flexion, and ankle dorsiflexion. Because angular acceleration signals are directional and can contain both positive and negative values, these variables were treated as unconstrained signals in MMF and were not subjected to non-negativity constraints in the corresponding biomechanical rows of W. To avoid disproportionate contributions of signals with different units and amplitude ranges to the reconstruction error, all channels were amplitude-normalized before factorization. Specifically, the kinematic signals were linearly scaled to the range of [−1,1], whereas the sEMG signals were full-wave rectified, normalized to their maximum amplitude, and further rescaled to the range of [0,2], thereby ensuring comparable amplitude spans between the two modalities. The same trial-concatenation strategy was applied to MMF, with three trials concatenated to form a participant-level EMG–kinematic matrix and the non-negative temporal coefficients averaged within participants after trial-wise splitting.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted at the participant level, with three STW trials selected for each participant. For both NMF and MMF, the number of extracted synergies/components was determined using a unified reconstruction-quality criterion based on the total variance accounted for (VAF). Reconstruction quality was calculated as:

The minimum number of synergies/components N satisfying VAFtotal ≥ 0.80 was selected as the final rank [38]. To evaluate the robustness of this rank-selection criterion, additional sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative VAF thresholds of 0.75, 0.80, 0.85, 0.90, and 0.95. To resolve the inherent permutation ambiguity in synergy ordering, a global matching procedure was performed using cosine similarity and the Hungarian algorithm. NMF synergies were aligned based on muscle-weight vectors, whereas MMF components were matched according to the EMG-weight submatrix of W. The global reference template was updated through two iterative rounds, after which the weight matrices and temporal coefficient matrices were reordered synchronously to ensure correspondence. Inter-group differences in synergy temporal profiles were assessed using one-dimensional statistical parametric mapping (SPM1D). Before performing the three-group SPM one-way ANOVA for each synergy, the temporal coefficient curves were normalized to their maximum absolute values. The significance threshold was Bonferroni-corrected according to the number of extracted synergies: α = 0.05/4 for NMF and α = 0.05/7 for MMF. Suprathreshold clusters exceeding the SPM critical threshold were identified as regions of significant difference. Differences in synergy weight structure, including NMF muscle weights, MMF EMG weights, and absolute MMF biomechanical angular-acceleration weights, were visualized using grouped bar plots. Bar heights represent means, and error bars represent 95% confidence intervals. Channel-wise weight comparisons were performed using Kruskal–Wallis tests and Mann–Whitney U tests. Asterisks in the figures indicate exploratory annotations based on uncorrected raw p values < 0.05 and were used as a qualitative guide to localized weighting differences.

Results

Model accuracy validation

Tracking simulations showed that the key movement trajectories generated by the simulations were consistent with the experimentally recorded kinematic data in terms of overall trends and phase-specific changes (Supplementary Figure S3). For muscle-activation validation, the P4 phase was selected as the gait-cycle analysis window. According to the STW phase definition, P4 represented the first gait-cycle window after the transition into stance support and propulsion. Because movement during this phase was relatively continuous and stable, it was used to compare recorded EMG envelopes with OpenSim-derived muscle activation trends. Considering that surface EMG is susceptible to crosstalk from adjacent muscles and that OpenSim outputs represent activations of individual muscles or muscle groups, representative superficial muscles with clearer EMG–model correspondence were selected, including TA, GM, GL, ST, and BF. The P4 phase of all valid trials was time-normalized to 0–100% of the gait cycle, and the recorded and simulated signals showed high temporal consistency (Supplementary Figure S4).

In addition, the STW agreement validation of the OpenSim–Umberger metabolic-power estimation workflow showed that the model-estimated metabolic rate was generally consistent with the oxygen-consumption-derived metabolic measurements during STW trials (R2 = 0.804, Supplementary Figure S5). Across different trial conditions, the simulated metabolic rate captured the overall trend of measured metabolic changes, supporting the use of this workflow for estimating individual metabolic energy expenditure during movement.

Efficiency stratification based on simulation-derived metabolic power and baseline characteristics

After establishing a metabolic-power prediction model based on body mass, height, and sex, the residual method was used to classify the 71 participants into low-, moderate-, and high-cost groups. No significant between-group differences were observed in sex, height, body mass, or BMI (all p > 0.05), indicating that the stratification did not introduce obvious body-size bias. The metabolic power index (Low = 100) showed an increasing trend across the three groups, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.151). In contrast, the relative residual (%), which served as the basis for group classification, showed a significant gradient across groups (Low: −6.4 ± 2.9%; Moderate: −0.5 ± 1.3%; High: 8.0 ± 7.8%; p < 0.001, SMD = 2.46). These results indicate that residual-based stratification effectively distinguished individual metabolic burden relative to body-size- and sex-based expectations (Table 1).

Synergy weights

The spatial weight distributions of the matched NMF synergies are summarized in the right panels of Fig. 4. After global synergy matching, the dominant muscle composition of each matched synergy was broadly consistent across the three metabolic-cost groups. Syn1 was primarily weighted by TA, with RF as a secondary contributor. Syn2 was dominated by the plantarflexor muscles, mainly GM and GL. Syn3 was characterized by high weights in the hamstring muscles, particularly ST and BF. Syn4 showed a quadriceps-dominant pattern, with high weights in VL, VM, and RF.

The matched synergies showed generally consistent dominant muscle profiles across groups, with local differences observed in selected muscle weights. Exploratory, uncorrected Kruskal–Wallis tests suggested potential group effects for GM in Syn1 (H = 8.21, p = 0.016), GL in Syn2 (H = 8.92, p = 0.012), BF in Syn3 (H = 7.68, p = 0.022), and GL in Syn3 (H = 7.33, p = 0.026). No exploratory Kruskal–Wallis group effect was observed for the remaining muscle weights. Uncorrected pairwise comparisons indicated that the Syn1 GM and Syn2 GL differences were mainly driven by lower weights in the high metabolic-cost group, whereas Syn3 BF and GL weights were lower in the moderate group than in the low and high groups.

Discussion

Main findings

In this study, we developed a simulation-informed synergy analysis framework to characterize neuromuscular and cross-modal coordination during the transition from seated forward lean to the completion of the first gait cycle. Using multimodal data from 71 healthy participants and model-validation analyses, we found that the OpenSim–Umberger workflow supported the feasibility of estimating metabolic cost within this task window. Joint kinematics and muscle-level outputs were broadly comparable across residual-based metabolic-cost groups. However, NMF identified localized differences in temporal activation coefficients after correction across synergies. These differences were confined to early and late time windows rather than extending across the whole analyzed cycle. Exploratory comparisons of NMF muscle weights also showed group differences in selected muscle channels. MMF further characterized non-negative temporal coefficients and cross-modal weighting patterns linking EMG channels with biomechanical angular-acceleration features. Bonferroni-corrected SPM1D analysis identified localized between-group differences in MMF temporal coefficients only for Synergies 2 and 5. Uncorrected exploratory comparisons of MMF weights showed localized differences in selected channels, specifically in EMG (Synergies 2, 3, 7) and biomechanical channels (Synergies 2, 5, 7). Across the Low, Moderate, and High residual-based metabolic-cost groups, the MMF weight matrices showed subtle, localized differences in EMG and biomechanical channels. Overall, these findings indicate that residual-based metabolic-cost stratification in healthy participants was associated with localized, synergy-specific differences, while overall external movement patterns remained broadly similar, rather than reflecting a wholesale reorganization of motor strategy.

Validation and interpretation of residual-based metabolic-cost stratification

By combining OpenSim-based musculoskeletal simulation with the Umberger metabolic model, this study enabled time-aligned estimation of model-derived metabolic cost during the transition from seated forward lean to the completion of the first gait cycle. This approach partially addressed limitations of conventional respiratory gas analysis for transient tasks, including delayed dynamic responses, higher noise, and the need for repeated stepwise averaging [39–41]. The OpenSim–Umberger workflow has been widely used to infer internal muscle dynamic states from kinematics and external forces, and to generate verifiable simulation-based inferences linking kinematics/muscle behavior to energetic outputs [14–16, 42]. In the present study, the credibility of the simulation workflow was supported by complementary validation steps. First, the tracking simulations reproduced the main experimental kinematic trajectories and their phase-specific changes, supporting the ability of the OpenSim workflow to capture the global kinematic patterns of the analyzed task. Second, model-derived muscle activations were compared with recorded sEMG envelopes during P4, which covered one complete gait cycle after transition into standing support and propulsion. This phase was selected because the movement was relatively continuous and stable, allowing a more appropriate comparison between OpenSim activation outputs and surface EMG trends. Representative superficial muscles with clearer EMG–model correspondence, including TA, GM, GL, ST, and BF, showed broadly consistent temporal trends between recorded and simulated signals. Third, in an STW agreement-validation analysis involving 20 participants from the present cohort, the model-estimated metabolic rate showed good agreement with oxygen-consumption-derived metabolic measurements, with an overall average R2 of 0.804. This validation provides empirical support for the OpenSim–Umberger implementation within the STW task context. Together, these checks supported the use of simulation-derived metabolic estimates to define residual-based metabolic-cost phenotypes after adjustment for body mass, height, and sex.

Residual-based metabolic-cost stratification allowed participants to be characterized as low-, moderate-, and high-cost phenotypes after accounting for body mass, height, and sex. Under this grouping scheme, the three groups showed broadly comparable macroscopic joint angle and angular velocity waveforms (Fig. 2), indicating that group classification was not accompanied by pronounced differences in overall movement patterns. This coexistence of broadly similar external movement profiles and different residual-based metabolic-cost phenotypes is compatible with the motor abundance perspective, whereby similar task outputs can be achieved through different combinations of internal degrees of freedom and neuromuscular organization [18, 43, 44]. Accordingly, the group-level differences observed in NMF and MMF features may reflect localized variation in internal coordination features, such as synergy timing, selected muscle weights, or cross-modal weighting, rather than gross differences in movement execution. Similar neuromuscular and biomechanical differences have been reported to influence metabolic cost even under comparable task conditions, such as speed or trajectory [45, 46]. Therefore, these groups should be interpreted as simulation-informed metabolic-cost phenotypes rather than direct measurements of whole-body energetic efficiency during STW.

Localized NMF-derived differences in temporal coefficients and muscle weights

NMF provided the main group-level evidence for phase-specific differences in synergy timing. After global synergy matching, the temporal coefficients were compared across residual-based metabolic-cost groups using SPM1D with correction for the number of extracted synergies. Corrected effects were observed only for Syn1 in the early portion of the analyzed cycle and for Syn3 in the late portion, whereas Syn2 and Syn4 showed no corrected between-group differences. This pattern indicates localized temporal variation in matched NMF coefficients, rather than a cycle-wide shift in synergy recruitment.

The timing of these effects is relevant to the task structure. The early Syn1 difference occurred near the preparatory-to-rising phase, whereas the late Syn3 difference occurred close to the completion of the first analyzed gait cycle. These periods correspond to phases in which postural transition and early locomotor progression are organized [2, 47]. Previous studies have shown that muscle recruitment timing contributes to posture regulation, momentum transfer, and gait initiation [48, 49]. In the present study, however, these findings are best interpreted as phase-specific differences in synergy timing. Further analyses of COP/COM dynamics, anticipatory postural adjustments, or stability-related variables would be needed to define the underlying postural-control mechanism more directly.

The NMF weight results provided complementary exploratory information. Across residual-based metabolic-cost groups, the dominant muscle composition of each matched synergy was broadly preserved, suggesting that the overall muscle-synergy structure remained stable. Uncorrected exploratory comparisons suggested differences in selected muscle weights, including GM in Syn1, GL in Syn2, and BF and GL in Syn3, while Syn4 showed no clear group-level weight difference. These localized weight differences may reflect subtle redistribution of muscle contributions within otherwise similar synergy structures. Taken together, the NMF results suggest that residual-based metabolic-cost phenotypes were accompanied by localized timing and muscle-weight differences, with the main synergy organization largely preserved.

MMF-derived EMG-angular-acceleration coupling features

Conventional NMF captures non-negative muscle co-activation patterns but does not directly represent signed biomechanical variables within the same latent space [50]. MMF extends this framework by combining non-negative EMG-channel weights with signed biomechanical-channel weights, allowing muscle activity and angular-acceleration features to be described within a shared cross-modal structure [27]. This distinction is relevant for the present task because the transition from seated forward lean to the first gait cycle involves both muscle coordination and rapid changes in lower-limb segmental acceleration.

In the present study, MMF extracted seven components with distinct non-negative temporal coefficient profiles and cross-modal weight matrices. Corrected temporal coefficients showed localized between-group differences only for Synergies 2 and 5, indicating limited phase-specific effects. In addition, exploratory analyses revealed subtle, localized differences in selected EMG (Synergies 2, 3, 7) and biomechanical (Synergies 2, 5, 7) channels. Across the Low, Moderate, and High residual-based metabolic-cost groups, the MMF weight matrices visualized in Fig. 7 showed subtle, localized variations in both EMG and biomechanical channels, while the overall synergy-specific patterns remained identifiable. These findings suggest that residual-based metabolic-cost stratification influenced phase- and channel-specific aspects of neuromuscular coordination, without altering the global synergy organization. In this sense, MMF complements NMF by extending the analysis from muscle co-activation alone to the cross-modal weighting of muscle activity and movement-related mechanical features. Together, these results indicate that NMF provided clearer corrected evidence for temporal differences, while MMF captured both limited corrected temporal effects and exploratory EMG–angular-acceleration coupling features, offering candidate insights for future mechanistic analyses.

These observations regarding the MMF weight matrices require cautious interpretation. Because MMF-derived weights may be sensitive to signal scaling, normalization, rank selection, and selected biomechanical inputs, they should be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Regression-based approaches that estimate the relationship between EMG-derived synergies and kinematic or mechanical outputs outside the joint factorization step may provide a complementary strategy for future work, because they do not require EMG and biomechanical signals to compete within a single reconstruction-error term and may therefore be less sensitive to cross-modal normalization choices [51]. Such approaches could be used to test whether the MMF-derived coupling features identified here generalize to prediction-oriented mappings between muscle coordination and movement output.

Integrated interpretation of localized synergy-related features

Integrating the NMF and MMF results, the present findings suggest that residual-based metabolic-cost phenotypes were not distinguished by large changes in overt movement or by a wholesale reorganization of synergy structure. Instead, the differences were localized across timing and weighting layers of coordination. NMF identified corrected, phase-specific differences in temporal coefficients for Syn1 and Syn3, together with exploratory differences in selected muscle weights. MMF further showed limited corrected temporal differences for Synergies 2 and 5, as well as exploratory cross-modal weight differences in selected EMG and biomechanical channel–synergy combinations. This layered pattern is consistent with the view that similar STW performance can be achieved through different internal coordination solutions, in which synergy timing, muscle weighting, and muscle–movement coupling may vary across individuals [43, 44].

This interpretation reframes the efficiency-related signal as fine-scale neuromechanical coordination rather than a discrete motor-strategy shift. NMF primarily captured localized timing and muscle-weight differences within muscle synergies, whereas MMF extended this interpretation by describing how selected EMG and biomechanical channels were weighted within cross-modal synergies. Together, these analyses indicate that individual variation in model-estimated metabolic cost may be related to localized timing and channel-specific weighting features that are not apparent from external kinematics alone.

Methodological and translational implications

The primary contribution of this study lies in its methodological framework. By combining OpenSim–Umberger metabolic estimation with NMF and MMF, the proposed simulation-informed framework enables a finer-grained characterization of metabolic-cost phenotypes, muscle-synergy timing, muscle-weight distribution, and EMG–biomechanical coupling during a transient functional task. This is particularly relevant for STW, where task completion and gross kinematics may appear similar even when estimated metabolic cost and internal coordination features differ. In this sense, the framework extends conventional descriptive movement analysis by linking model-derived energetic estimates with latent neuromuscular and cross-modal coordination features.

From a translational perspective, the present findings provide a healthy-reference framework rather than direct clinical evidence. The localized NMF timing differences, together with the MMF-derived localized temporal effects and exploratory cross-modal weighting features identified here, may guide future studies in older adults and neurological populations, where their stability, clinical interpretability, and links to functional outcomes can be tested. The framework may therefore support the development of more mechanism-informed assessments. Clinical use, however, will require validation in target populations and evidence of relevance to functional or therapeutic outcomes.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study included healthy participants; therefore, the identified residual-based metabolic-cost phenotypes and synergy-related features should be interpreted as a healthy-reference framework. In addition, although the residual-based stratification model adjusted for body mass, height, and sex, age showed a moderate standardized difference between the low- and high-cost groups. Therefore, age may represent a potential confounding factor and should be further controlled or stratified in future studies.

Second, although the OpenSim–Umberger workflow was supported by tracking performance, EMG–activation trend comparisons, and an STW agreement-validation analysis in a subsample, metabolic stratification in the main STW analysis was still based on model-derived estimates. Respiratory gas measurements have limited temporal resolution for isolated transient transitions; although agreement validation against oxygen-consumption-derived metabolic measurements was performed during STW trials, respiratory metabolic measurements during brief transient tasks may still be influenced by delayed physiological responses and trial-to-trial variability. Therefore, the metabolic-cost phenotypes should be interpreted as simulation-informed estimates rather than direct measurements of whole-body energetic efficiency during STW. Model-derived estimates of internal muscle states may also be affected by participant-specific scaling, muscle–tendon parameters, activation dynamics, and solution strategies. Surface EMG is sensitive to crosstalk, electrode placement, and the imperfect correspondence between recorded surface signals and model-derived muscle activations.

Third, synergy extraction and comparison depend on methodological choices. Although global synergy matching was used to reduce ordering ambiguity, the number and structure of NMF/MMF components may still vary with reconstruction thresholds, signal normalization strategies, rank selection, and the selected biomechanical input channels. In MMF, temporal coefficients were constrained to be non-negative at each iteration in accordance with the standard procedure; nevertheless, the extracted cross-modal synergies may still be influenced by preprocessing and model-selection choices. This issue is particularly relevant for MMF because EMG and angular-acceleration features differ in scale, variance, and physiological meaning; consequently, the chosen normalization strategy may influence the relative contribution of each modality to the global reconstruction error and, in turn, the extracted cross-modal components.

Finally, the temporal coefficient and weight analyses differed in inferential strength. Temporal coefficients were tested using SPM1D with correction for the number of extracted synergies, whereas channel-level weight comparisons were treated as exploratory and reported using uncorrected Kruskal–Wallis and pairwise tests. Therefore, the weight findings should be interpreted as descriptive, hypothesis-generating evidence for selected channel-level differences rather than confirmatory evidence of broad synergy reorganization. Future studies should examine the robustness of these findings under alternative preprocessing and rank-selection choices and test their associations with direct mechanical, functional, or clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

This study developed and evaluated a simulation-informed NMF/MMF framework to characterize neuromuscular and cross-modal coordination during the transition from seated forward lean to the completion of the first gait cycle. In healthy participants, residual-based metabolic-cost stratification identified groups with broadly comparable macroscopic kinematics and simulation-derived muscle-level outputs. These groups nevertheless showed localized synergy-related differences. Corrected temporal analyses identified time-window-specific differences in NMF activation coefficients, and MMF temporal coefficients showed limited corrected between-group differences confined to selected synergies and time windows. Exploratory, uncorrected weight analyses further suggested selected muscle-weight and EMG–angular-acceleration weighting differences. Together, these findings indicate that model-estimated metabolic cost may relate to subtle internal coordination features that are not apparent from gross movement patterns alone. This framework provides a healthy-reference and hypothesis-generating approach for future studies. Further work should test whether these candidate features are stable, clinically interpretable, and linked to mechanical, functional, or therapeutic outcomes.
