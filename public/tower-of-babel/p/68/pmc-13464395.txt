Lipid Metabolic Rewiring During Continuous Prostate Cancer Progression Defines a Biologically Relevant Four-Gene Prognostic Signature
Authors: Rongna Li, Hongying He, Hui Sun, Yili Long, Nan Huang, Yingbing Zhu, Hongtao Chen, Guanmin Jiang, Xiaohui Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLipid metabolic reprogramming is increasingly recognized as a critical feature of prostate cancer progression, but the lipid metabolism-related genes that remain continuously dysregulated from normal tissue to primary tumor and metastatic disease have not been systematically characterized, and their biological and prognostic relevance remains incompletely understood.ObjectiveTo identify lipid metabolism-related genes associated with continuous prostate cancer progression and develop a prognostic signature for survival stratification.MethodsClinical prostate cancer specimens and a high-fat diet (HFD)-driven RM-1 tumor model were first used to evaluate lipid metabolic alterations in vivo. GSE6919 transcriptomic data were used to identify genes shared between the Normal–Primary and Primary–Metastatic transitions. These genes were intersected with a curated lipid metabolism-related gene set, followed by GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. TCGA prostate adenocarcinoma expression and clinical data were used for LASSO regression to construct a prognostic model. The four core genes were further evaluated by clinicopathological correlation analysis, protein- and transcript-level validation in clinical tissues and prostate cancer cell lines, and functional assays under oleic acid-induced lipid stress. Immune infiltration analysis, ssGSEA, and nomogram analysis were performed to assess the biological and clinical relevance of the model.ResultsClinical tissues showed increased PLIN3 expression, and HFD feeding promoted tumor growth and reinforced lipid metabolic alterations in vivo. A total of 44 lipid metabolism-related genes were identified as continuously dysregulated during prostate cancer progression. These genes were mainly enriched in fatty acid metabolism, lipid catabolism, peroxisome, lipid droplet, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, arachidonic acid metabolism, and PPAR signaling. Eight genes were significantly associated with overall survival in TCGA, and a four-gene signature comprising ALDH3A2, ENO2, PPP1CB, and PTGIS was established. This model effectively stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups with significantly different survival outcomes. The risk score was positively associated with clinical T stage and Gleason score. The four core genes were also associated with lipid metabolic enzymes, immune infiltration patterns, and multiple metabolism-related pathways. Protein- and transcript-level validation in clinical tissues and prostate cancer cell lines supported the biological relevance of the signature, although PTGIS showed a more context-dependent pattern. Functionally, silencing ENO2 reduced oleic acid-induced lipid peroxidation, whereas silencing PPP1CB enhanced it, while ALDH3A2 showed a more context-dependent effect. A nomogram integrating the risk score with clinical variables improved individualized survival prediction.ConclusionWe identified lipid metabolism-related genes continuously dysregulated during prostate cancer progression and established a four-gene prognostic signature with potential value for survival prediction and risk assessment. These findings highlight lipid metabolic rewiring as an important component of prostate cancer evolution and provide candidate biomarkers for future mechanistic and translational studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12575-026-00345-1.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most frequently diagnosed malignancies in men and remains a major cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. While localized disease can often be effectively managed by surgery, radiotherapy, and androgen deprivation-based therapies, a substantial proportion of patients eventually progress to aggressive, recurrent, or metastatic disease, which is associated with poor prognosis and limited long-term therapeutic benefit [2, 3]. The transition from primary tumor to metastatic progression represents a crucial biological turning point, reflecting not only increased tumor plasticity and therapeutic resistance, but also profound rewiring of cellular programs that sustain survival under metabolic and microenvironmental stress [4]. Despite considerable advances in genomic classification and systemic treatment strategies, the molecular determinants that couple tumor progression with metabolic adaptation in PCa remain incompletely understood [5].

Metabolic reprogramming is increasingly recognized as a defining feature of tumor progression, and among the altered metabolic processes in PCa, lipid metabolism has emerged as a particularly prominent and biologically consequential axis [6, 7]. In contrast to many other solid tumors that are dominated by glycolytic phenotypes, PCa displays marked dependence on lipid uptake, fatty acid synthesis, β-oxidation, cholesterol homeostasis, and membrane lipid remodeling [8, 9]. These changes are not merely passive metabolic consequences of growth, but actively support membrane biosynthesis, ATP generation, redox balance, signal transduction, and adaptation to therapy-induced stress. Accumulating evidence suggests that dysregulated lipid metabolism contributes to multiple malignant properties of PCa, including tumor growth, metastatic dissemination, castration resistance, and immune escape [10, 11]. Thus, lipid metabolic rewiring is increasingly viewed as a central component of PCa aggressiveness rather than a secondary metabolic byproduct.

Importantly, lipid metabolic alterations in tumors are frequently shaped by both intrinsic genetic programs and extrinsic nutrient conditions [12]. A lipid-rich environment may provide tumor cells with metabolic flexibility, allowing them to increase lipid uptake, utilization, and remodeling in response to proliferative and stress-related demands [13]. Consistent with this concept, increasing clinical and experimental evidence suggests that PCa often exhibits a lipid-enriched metabolic state [14, 15]. In our study, this concept was further supported by increased PLIN3 expression in clinical PCa specimens and by enhanced tumor growth and lipid metabolic alterations in a high-fat diet-driven in vivo model, suggesting that altered lipid metabolism is not only a theoretical hallmark but also a directly observable feature of PCa progression. However, although the relevance of lipid metabolism in PCa is increasingly appreciated, several key questions remain unresolved. Most previous studies have relied on tumor-versus-normal comparisons or on prognostic signatures derived from single patient cohorts, while relatively few have examined genes that remain persistently dysregulated across the full trajectory of PCa progression, from normal prostate tissue to primary tumor and subsequently to metastatic lesions [16–18]. Such continuously altered genes are more likely to represent biologically meaningful drivers of progressive metabolic adaptation rather than stage-restricted bystanders [19]. Moreover, the prognostic significance of these progression-associated lipid metabolism-related genes, as well as their relationship with immune infiltration and pathway-level functional rewiring, has not been systematically clarified.

In the present study, we investigated lipid metabolic remodeling in PCa from a progression-oriented perspective. We first examined clinical and in vivo evidence indicating that PCa is associated with an altered lipid metabolic state. We then analyzed the GSE6919 cohort, which includes normal prostate tissues, primary PCa tissues, and metastatic PCa tissues, to identify genes that were continuously dysregulated across both the Normal–Primary and Primary–Metastatic transitions [20]. By intersecting these genes with a curated lipid metabolism-related gene set, we defined a panel of progression-associated lipid metabolic candidates. Integrating transcriptomic and clinical follow-up data from TCGA, we further evaluated their prognostic value and identified eight survival-associated genes, from which a four-gene prognostic signature was further derived using LASSO regression. To strengthen the biological relevance of the model, we further performed clinicopathological correlation analysis, protein- and transcript-level validation in clinical tissues and prostate cancer cell lines, and functional assays under oleic acid-induced lipid stress. Finally, immune infiltration analysis, ssGSEA, and nomogram construction were performed to explore the biological and clinical relevance of this model. Collectively, our study provides a tumor metabolism-oriented framework for understanding lipid metabolic rewiring during PCa progression and identifies candidate biomarkers with potential value for prognosis evaluation and individualized management.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Statement

Tumor tissues were obtained from donors who provided informed consent, in accordance with the ethical guidelines and regulations of the Medical Ethics Review Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. K161-1). Mouse experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Ethics Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Authorization No. 00608).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC was performed to detect the expression of PLIN3, ALDH3A2, ENO2, PTGIS, and PPP1CB in clinical prostate cancer tissues, and to evaluate PLIN3 expression in tumor sections from the mouse subcutaneous xenograft model under regular diet (RD) and high-fat diet (HFD) conditions. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene (3 times, 5 min each), rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 85%, 75%, and 50%), and rinsed in distilled water. Antigen retrieval was carried out by boiling the sections in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 15 min, followed by cooling to room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min at room temperature, and nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at 37 °C.

The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: PLIN3 (Proteintech, 10694-1-AP, 1:200), ALDH3A2 (Proteintech, 15090-1-AP, 1:200), ENO2 (Proteintech, 10149-1-AP, 1:200), PTGIS (Proteintech, 27061-1-AP, 1:200), and PPP1CB (Proteintech, 10140-2-AP, 1:200). After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) three times (5 min each), the sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at 4 °C. Immunoreactivity was visualized using DAB chromogen, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. The sections were then dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted.

Images were captured under a light microscope for quantitative analysis. For PLIN3 staining, three randomly selected fields were analyzed for positive staining rate. For ALDH3A2, ENO2, PTGIS, and PPP1CB, staining intensity and positive area were evaluated and quantified as required for subsequent analysis.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

FFPE clinical prostate cancer tissue sections were dewaxed and hydrated according to the same procedure as immunohistochemistry. Antigen retrieval was performed with citrate buffer (pH 6.0) by microwave heating for 10 min, followed by natural cooling. Non-specific binding was blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min at 37℃. The sections were incubated with primary antibody against PLIN3 (dilution ratio 1:80) overnight at 4℃. After washing with PBS 3 times (5 min each), the sections were incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated secondary antibody (dilution ratio 1:200) in the dark for 1 h at 37℃. 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was used for nuclear staining for 5 min at room temperature. The sections were rinsed with PBS to remove excess DAPI, and mounted with anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium. Fluorescent signals were observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope, and images were captured. The fluorescence intensity of PLIN3 was analyzed using ImageJ software, and three independent fields were selected for each section to ensure the reliability of the results.

Mouse Models

Male nude mice (6–8 weeks old, weight 18–22 g) were randomly divided into two groups: regular diet (RD) group and high-fat diet (HFD) group, with 4 mice in each group. The RD group was fed with standard normal feed (fat content 10%), and the HFD group was fed with high-fat feed (fat content 45%) for 2 weeks to establish a high-fat model. After 2 weeks, prostate cancer cells RM-1 (1 × 10⁶ cells/mouse) were suspended in 100 µL of PBS and subcutaneously injected into the right axilla of each nude mouse. During the experiment, the feeding conditions of the mice were maintained consistently, and the body weight of the mice was measured once a week. The volume of subcutaneous xenograft tumors was measured every day using a vernier caliper, and the tumor volume was calculated according to the formula: Tumor volume (mm³) = (length × width²)/2. After 2 weeks of tumor inoculation, the mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, and the subcutaneous xenograft tumors were dissected and weighed. The tumor tissues were collected and divided into two parts: one part was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent histological staining, and the other part was stored at -80℃ for future experiments. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the institution and complied with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Oil Red O

The dissected subcutaneous xenograft tumor tissues from RD group and HFD group were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, and then embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound for frozen sectioning. Frozen Sect. (10 μm thick) were prepared and air-dried at room temperature for 30 min. The sections were rinsed with PBS 3 times (5 min each), and then stained with Oil Red O working solution at 37℃ for 15 min. After staining, the sections were rinsed with 75% ethanol to remove excess dye, and then rinsed with distilled water. Hematoxylin was used for counterstaining for 2 min, and distilled water was used to rinse off excess hematoxylin. The sections were mounted with glycerol gelatin mounting medium and observed under an optical microscope. The lipid droplets in the tumor tissues were stained red, and the distribution and accumulation of lipid droplets were observed. ImageJ software was used to quantify the positive staining area of Oil Red O, and the lipid content in the tumor tissues of the two groups was compared.

Cell Lines and Cell Culture

Human prostate cancer cell lines LNCaP, VCaP, PC3, and DU145, as well as murine RM-1 cells, were obtained from American Type Culture Collection. LNCaP cells were cultured in 1640 medium, PC3 cells were cultured in DMEM medium and DU145 cells were cultured in MEM medium. All cells were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. VCaP cells were cultured in DMEM High Glucose Medium supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics. RM-1 cells were cultured in DMEM medium. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Western Blot Analysis

Protein was extracted from clinical tissues, xenograft tissues, and cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PLIN3, ALDH3A2, ENO2, PTGIS, PPP1CB, GAPDH, or β-actin [PLIN3: proteintech, 10694-1-AP,1:2000; ALDH3A2: proteintech, 15090-1-AP, 1:2000; ENO2: proteintech, 10149-1-AP,1:5000; PTGIS: proteintech, 27061-1-AP, 1:6000; PPP1CB: proteintech,10140-2-AP,1:1000, GAPDH: proteintech, 60004-1-Ig, 1:50000; β-actin: proteintech, 66009-1-Ig, 1:20000]. After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and normalized to the loading control.

SiRNA Transfection

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting human PPP1CB, ALDH3A2, and ENO2, together with a negative control siRNA, were purchased from GenePharma. The siRNA sequences (sense/antisense, 5′→3′) were as follows: PPP1CB si-446, 5′-GCAGAUGACUGAAGCAGAATT-3′/5′-UUCUGCUUCAGUCAUCUGCTT-3′;PPP1CB si-911, 5′-GGAGCAGAUUCGGAGAAUUTT-3′/5′-AAUUCUCCGAAUCUGCUCCTT-3′;PPP1CB si-1277, 5′-CCAGUAUGGUGGACUGAAUTT-3′/5′-AUUCAGUCCACCAUACUGGTT-3′;ALDH3A2 si-361,5′-GACCUGUGCAAGAGUGAAUTT-3′/5′-AUUCACUCUUGCACAGGUCTT-3′;ALDH3A2 si-1061,5′-GGAUCAUCAAUCUUCGUCATT-3′/5′-UGACGAAGAUUGAUGAUCCTT-3′;ALDH3A2 si-1163,5′-CAACAGUACUUACCGAUGUTT-3′/5′-ACAUCGGUAAGUACUGUUGTT-3′; ENO2 si-158,5′-GGAUCUCUAUACUGCCAAAA TT-3′/5′-UUUGGCAGUAUAGAGAUCCTT-3′;ENO2 si-536, 5′-GGCCUUCAACGUGAUCAAUTT-3′/5′-AUUGAUCACGUUGAAGGCCTT-3′;and ENO2 si-1319, 5′-GCUCAUGAGAAUUGAGGAATT-3′/5′-UUCCUCAAUUCUCAUGAGCTT-3′.

VCaP and DU145 cells were transfected with the indicated siRNAs using Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, L3000015) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h of transfection, knockdown efficiency was confirmed by western blot analysis.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from prostate cancer cells using TRIzol reagent, and 1 µg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA. qPCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II with the following primers: ALDH3A2 (Forward: AAAAGCCTCTGGCTCTTTAT, Reverse: GACCGAGTTTTTCTTTGTTG), ENO2 (Forward: AGCTGGCCATGCAGGAGTTC, Reverse: GGCTTCCTTCACCAGCTCCA), PPP1CB (Forward: TCTATAATTCAATTGCCAGA, Reverse: ATAGCAGGGAGTTATATGAG), PTGIS (Forward: TTTATTTGGGAGTGGGTTAGGTT, Reverse: ATAACAACAACAACAACAATACAAC), and GAPDH (Forward: TCGGAGTCAACGGATTTGGTCGTAT, Reverse: AGCCTTCTCCATGGTGGTGAAGAC) as an internal control. Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method. All reactions were performed in triplicate.

Identification of Lipid Metabolism-Related Genes

Lipid metabolism-related genes were compiled from published studies and publicly available pathway resources, with a focus on genes involved in lipid droplet formation, lipid synthesis, fatty acid oxidation, and related lipid metabolic processes. After removal of duplicate entries, a curated lipid metabolism-related gene set was generated and used for downstream intersection analyses.

Data Acquisition and Preprocessing

Gene expression data for prostate cancer were obtained from two public databases. The GSE6919 dataset was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database and used as the discovery cohort. This dataset includes normal prostate tissues, primary prostate cancer tissues, and metastatic prostate cancer tissues, allowing the investigation of gene expression changes across the continuous disease trajectory from normal tissue to localized tumor and then to metastatic lesions.

For the GEO dataset, the Series Matrix file and corresponding platform annotation file were downloaded. Probe identifiers were converted into official gene symbols according to platform annotation information. Probes without valid gene annotation were removed. When multiple probes corresponded to the same gene symbol, the average expression value was used to represent that gene. Genes with duplicated entries were filtered to retain a non-redundant expression matrix. After annotation and preprocessing, samples were classified into three groups according to tissue origin: Normal, Primary, and Metastatic.

For prognostic validation and model construction, RNA sequencing expression profiles and corresponding clinical follow-up data for prostate adenocarcinoma were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. Expression data were matched with survival and clinicopathological information. Samples lacking complete survival information or key clinical variables were excluded from prognostic analyses.

Differential Expression Analysis

Differential expression analyses were performed separately for the Normal versus Primary and Primary versus Metastatic comparisons using the limma package in R. A linear model was fitted for each gene, and empirical Bayes moderation was applied to obtain stable estimates of differential expression. Genes meeting the criteria of |log2 fold change| > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Volcano plots were generated to visualize the distribution of differentially expressed genes in each comparison.

Identification of Progression-Associated Lipid Metabolism-Related Genes

To identify genes continuously dysregulated during prostate cancer progression, the differentially expressed genes from the Normal versus Primary and Primary versus Metastatic comparisons were intersected. The overlapping genes were defined as progression-associated genes shared across the two disease-transition stages. These shared genes were then intersected with the curated lipid metabolism-related gene set to identify lipid metabolism-related genes associated with continuous prostate cancer progression. Venn diagrams were used to visualize the overlap among the gene sets.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

To explore the biological significance of progression-associated genes and lipid metabolism-related candidate genes, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler package in R. GO analysis included the categories of biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). Enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways with adjusted P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The enrichment results were visualized using bar plots and bubble plots.

Identification of Prognostic Genes and Construction of a Prognostic Signature

To evaluate the prognostic relevance of progression-associated lipid metabolism-related genes, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was first performed in the TCGA cohort using the survival package in R. Genes with P < 0.05 were considered prognostically significant and were subsequently included in multivariate Cox regression analysis for further screening.

To construct a robust and simplified prognostic model, candidate prognostic genes were further analyzed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression with the glmnet package in R. Ten-fold cross-validation was applied to determine the optimal regularization parameter (lambda), and the lambda value corresponding to the minimum cross-validation error was selected. Genes retained at the optimal lambda value were incorporated into the final prognostic signature.

For each patient in the TCGA cohort, a risk score was calculated as the weighted sum of the expression values of the selected genes, with the regression coefficients derived from the LASSO-Cox model used as weights. The risk score was calculated according to the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Risk}\;\mathrm{score}=\sum\;\left(\mathrm{gene}\;\mathrm{expression}\times\mathrm{corresponding}\;\mathrm{coefficient}\right)$$\end{document}

Patients were categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median risk score of the cohort.

Survival Analysis and Evaluation of the Prognostic Model

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to compare overall survival between the high-risk and low-risk groups, and statistical significance was evaluated by the log-rank test. In addition, Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed for each prognostic gene individually by dividing patients into high-expression and low-expression groups according to the median expression level.

If applicable, the predictive performance of the prognostic signature was further assessed using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of the model for survival prediction at different time points.

Immune Infiltration Analysis

To investigate the relationship between the core prognostic genes and the tumor immune microenvironment, immune cell infiltration analysis was conducted in the TCGA cohort using a computational deconvolution algorithm. The relative abundance of multiple immune cell subtypes was estimated for each sample. Correlation analysis was then performed between the expression levels of the core prognostic genes and immune cell infiltration scores. The resulting correlations were visualized as heatmaps, with both correlation coefficients and statistical significance displayed.

Single-Sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis

To further characterize the biological differences between the high-risk and low-risk groups, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed using the GSVA package in R. Enrichment scores were calculated for predefined pathways, including metabolism-related pathways, immune-associated pathways, and inflammation-related biological programs. Differentially enriched pathways between the two risk groups were identified and visualized to reveal the functional coupling between the prognostic signature and underlying biological processes.

Construction of the Nomogram

To improve the clinical applicability of the prognostic signature, a nomogram was constructed by integrating the risk score with available clinicopathological variables, including age and T stage. The nomogram was used to estimate the probabilities of 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year overall survival for individual patients. If performed, calibration curves were used to assess the agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities, and decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the clinical net benefit of the nomogram.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0). Correlation analyses employed either Pearson’s or Spearman’s methods based on specific circumstances. Survival analyses utilized the Kaplan-Meier method and Cox proportional hazards regression models. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, Version 10.2). Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Group comparisons were performed using appropriate statistical tests, including unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests for comparisons between two groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Altered Lipid Metabolism is Evident in PCa Tissues

Aberrant lipid metabolism is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of prostate cancer (PCa). Tumor cells reprogram lipid uptake, synthesis, and utilization to sustain growth and adaptation to metabolic stress. To determine whether lipid metabolic alterations are present in clinical PCa, we first examined PLIN3, a metabolism-associated marker linked to intracellular lipid handling and commonly used as a practical phenotypic indicator of altered lipid metabolic state, in human specimens. Compared with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), PCa tissues showed markedly higher PLIN3 expression via immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence (Fig. 1A-D). These findings support the presence of enhanced lipid metabolic activity in clinical PCa tissues.

High-Fat Feeding Promotes PCa Growth and Reinforces Lipid Metabolic Alterations in Vivo

To evaluate whether increased lipid availability affects tumor progression in vivo, we established an RM-1 subcutaneous tumor model under regular diet (RD) or high-fat diet (HFD) conditions. HFD-fed mice developed larger tumors and showed accelerated tumor growth compared with RD controls (Fig. 2A-B), indicating that a lipid-rich nutritional environment promotes PCa growth. Histological analysis further showed enhanced neutral lipid staining in HFD tumors, together with increased PLIN3 expression (Fig. 2C-E), suggesting that high-fat feeding reinforces lipid metabolic alterations within the tumor. Collectively, these findings indicate that lipid metabolic remodeling is closely associated with PCa growth both in clinical tissues and under lipid-rich in vivo conditions.

Continuous Prostate Cancer Progression is Accompanied by Global Transcriptomic Remodeling

Given that both clinical specimens and HFD-driven tumors exhibited prominent lipid metabolic alterations, we next sought to define the broader molecular landscape underlying prostate cancer (PCa) progression. To this end, we analyzed the GSE6919 cohort, which includes normal prostate tissues, primary PCa tissues, and metastatic PCa tissues, thereby enabling stepwise interrogation of transcriptomic alterations during disease evolution. Differential expression analysis was performed for the Normal vs. Primary and Primary vs. Metastatic comparisons. Volcano plots revealed substantial transcriptional reprogramming in both transitions (Fig. 3A-B), indicating that progression from normal prostate tissue to localized tumor, and subsequently to metastatic disease, is accompanied by extensive gene expression changes.

In the Normal–Primary comparison, genes highly expressed in normal tissues, including ACTA2, ACTG2, MYLK, TAGLN, and CNN1, were mainly related to smooth muscle structure and tissue homeostasis, whereas genes such as RPL29, RPL36, and OR51E2 were upregulated in primary tumors, suggesting activation of tumor-associated transcriptional and translational programs. In the Primary–Metastatic comparison, AR expression was markedly reduced in metastatic lesions, while genes including HSPD1, MALAT1, CELF1, MGAT4B, MLL5, OS9, HPRT1, GNL3L, and NUCB2 showed pronounced alterations, reflecting enhanced stress adaptation, RNA processing, and metastatic progression.

To obtain an unbiased overview of the biological processes involved in these stage-specific transcriptomic changes, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using all differential genes from the two comparisons. The enriched pathways were mainly related to cytoskeletal organization, focal adhesion, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, tight junctions, integrin signaling, Wnt signaling, Hippo signaling, and selected metabolic pathways such as glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and nucleotide metabolism (Fig. 3C-D). These results indicate that continuous PCa progression is characterized primarily by coordinated remodeling of structural plasticity, cell adhesion, and adaptive transcriptional programs. Importantly, although this unbiased analysis mainly highlights broad architectural and stromal-associated changes rather than lipid metabolism itself, it establishes the molecular background against which lipid metabolic alterations emerge during tumor progression.

A Lipid Metabolism-Focused Analysis Identifies 44 Genes Continuously Dysregulated During Prostate Cancer Progression

Because the global enrichment analysis mainly reflected broad transcriptomic remodeling, we next focused specifically on the lipid metabolic component suggested by our clinical and in vivo observations. To identify lipid metabolism-related genes that remain persistently dysregulated throughout PCa progression, we first intersected the differential genes from the Normal–Primary and Primary–Metastatic comparisons and then overlapped this shared gene set with the curated lipid metabolism-related gene list. This integrative strategy yielded 44 candidate genes that were continuously dysregulated across both progression stages and functionally linked to lipid metabolism (Fig. 4A).

The identification of these 44 genes suggests that lipid metabolic abnormalities are not confined to a single stage of tumor development, but instead represent a sustained molecular feature spanning tumor initiation, progression, and metastatic dissemination. These genes are therefore more likely to reflect biologically meaningful drivers of disease evolution rather than stage-restricted bystanders. Consistent with this, the expression heatmap showed a clear separation of the three sample groups based on these 44 genes, with distinct expression patterns across Normal, Primary, and Metastatic tissues (Fig. 4B). In particular, a subset of genes displayed progressive upregulation or downregulation along the Normal-to-Primary-to-Metastatic trajectory, further supporting the notion that lipid metabolic reprogramming accompanies the continuous evolution of PCa rather than representing an isolated late-stage event.

To further characterize their functional relevance, GO enrichment analysis was performed. The enriched biological process terms were mainly associated with fatty acid metabolic process, unsaturated fatty acid metabolic process, lipid catabolic process, fatty acid catabolic process, monocarboxylic acid catabolic process, cellular lipid catabolic process, and alcohol metabolic process. Cellular component terms included peroxisome, microbody, caveola, plasma membrane raft, and mediator complex, whereas molecular function terms were mainly enriched in C-acyltransferase activity, oxidoreductase activity, NAD binding, carbon–carbon lyase activity, and lyase activity (Fig. 4C). Overall, these findings indicate that the candidate genes are primarily involved in lipid degradation, membrane-associated lipid organization, and peroxisome-related metabolic processes.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further demonstrated that these 44 genes were significantly enriched in carbon metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, citrate cycle (TCA cycle), arachidonic acid metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, biosynthesis of amino acids, PPAR signaling pathway, and peroxisome, as well as several amino acid metabolism-related pathways (Fig. 4D). Collectively, these results indicate that the progression-associated lipid metabolism-related genes participate in a broad metabolic network involving lipid catabolism, membrane lipid remodeling, central carbon metabolism, and energetic adaptation. In the context of our clinical and animal findings, these data support the interpretation that altered lipid metabolism in PCa is not merely a secondary histological change, but a manifestation of sustained metabolic rewiring during tumor progression.

Identification of Prognostic Lipid Metabolism-Related Genes and Construction of a Four-Gene Risk Signature

Having identified 44 lipid metabolism-related genes that were continuously dysregulated during prostate cancer progression, we next asked whether a subset of these genes also carried clinical prognostic significance. To address this, we analyzed their expression profiles together with follow-up data from the TCGA prostate adenocarcinoma cohort. Survival-associated screening identified 8 genes significantly associated with overall survival, namely ACOX2, ALDH3A2, ALDOA, AOC3, ENO2, PPP1CB, PTGIS, and UBE2I. These findings indicate that a subset of lipid metabolism-related genes persistently altered during PCa progression also exhibits stable prognostic relevance in an independent clinical cohort. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis further demonstrated that these genes could effectively stratify patients according to survival outcome. Specifically, high expression of ACOX2, ALDH3A2, AOC3, PPP1CB, and PTGIS was associated with a more favorable prognosis, whereas elevated ALDOA, ENO2, and UBE2I expression predicted worse overall survival (Fig. 5A). Thus, among the 44 progression-associated lipid metabolism-related genes, several not only tracked tumor evolution but also reflected clinically meaningful differences in patient outcome.

To construct a concise and robust prognostic model, we next subjected the candidate prognostic genes to LASSO-Cox regression analysis. Cross-validation identified the optimal penalization parameter, and coefficient trajectory analysis further supported feature shrinkage and model optimization (Fig. 5B-C). Ultimately, ALDH3A2, ENO2, PPP1CB, and PTGIS were retained to establish a four-gene prognostic signature. Importantly, these four genes appear to represent distinct but complementary biological dimensions of PCa progression, including lipid redox homeostasis (ALDH3A2), metabolic adaptation (ENO2), signaling/phosphatase regulation (PPP1CB), and lipid mediator metabolism (PTGIS). Notably, this reduced model preserved genes with distinct biological implications, including lipid redox homeostasis, metabolic adaptation, signal regulation, and lipid mediator-related functions, suggesting that it may capture complementary aspects of PCa metabolic progression.

Based on the expression values and corresponding coefficients of these four genes, a risk score was calculated for each patient in the TCGA cohort. Patients were then divided into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the median risk score. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that patients in the high-risk group had significantly poorer overall survival than those in the low-risk group (Fig. 5D), indicating that the four-gene signature possesses substantial prognostic stratification capacity. Collectively, these data suggest that a progression-derived lipid metabolism gene set can be further distilled into a clinically informative prognostic model for PCa. We next examined whether this model was associated with clinicopathological aggressiveness and whether the four core genes could be validated in clinical specimens.

The Four-Gene Signature is Associated with Clinicopathological Aggressiveness and is Validated in Clinical Specimens and Prostate Cancer Cell Lines

To further strengthen the clinical relevance of the four-gene prognostic signature, we first examined the associations between the standardized risk score and major clinicopathological parameters in the TCGA-PRAD cohort. As shown in Fig. 6A, the standardized risk score increased significantly with advancing clinical T stage and Gleason score, indicating that the four-gene signature is positively associated with tumor aggressiveness and pathological severity.

We next evaluated the protein expression of the four core genes in paired clinical prostate cancer tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues. Western blot analysis showed that, compared with the paired non-tumor controls, the protein levels of ALDH3A2, ENO2, PTGIS, and PPP1CB were all altered in prostate cancer tissues (Fig. 6B), providing direct protein-level support for the biological relevance of the signature in human specimens.

To assess whether these expression changes were linked to pathological risk stratification, we examined the four core genes in clinical specimens from different Gleason grade groups. Representative immunohistochemical staining images (Fig. 6C) showed distinct localization and expression patterns of the four proteins in low-risk tumors (Grade Group 1, Gleason score ≤ 6) and very high-risk tumors (Grade Group 5, Gleason scores 9–10). Quantitative H-score analysis (Fig. 6D) demonstrated that ENO2 and PTGIS were significantly increased in Grade Group 5 tumors, whereas ALDH3A2 and PPP1CB were significantly decreased. These patterns were broadly consistent with the pathological risk gradient and further supported the clinical relevance of the four-gene signature.

We then examined the basal expression of the four genes in prostate cancer cell lines. Western blot analysis detected the protein levels of ALDH3A2, ENO2, PTGIS, and PPP1CB in LNCaP, VCaP, PC3, and DU145 cells, and densitometric quantification showed distinct expression patterns across the different cell lines (Fig. 6E-F). Specifically, ALDH3A2 and PPP1CB were relatively more abundant in the less aggressive LNCaP/VCaP backgrounds and reduced in the more aggressive PC3/DU145 cells, whereas ENO2 showed the opposite pattern, with markedly higher expression in PC3 and DU145. In parallel, qPCR analysis demonstrated that the mRNA expression levels of these four genes in the same prostate cancer cell lines were generally consistent with their protein expression trends (Fig. 6G), further validating the expression characteristics of the signature genes in vitro. These findings broadly mirrored the clinicopathological gradient observed in human specimens, in which ENO2 was enriched in more aggressive disease states, whereas ALDH3A2 and PPP1CB were reduced.

Notably, PTGIS showed a more complex pattern. Although PTGIS was retained in the four-gene signature as a favorable prognostic gene in the TCGA survival model, its protein expression was increased in higher-grade clinical specimens and also tended to be elevated in more aggressive disease contexts, including castration-resistant prostate cancer-related models. In the cell-line panel, PTGIS likewise displayed a non-linear expression pattern, with relatively higher expression in more aggressive cellular backgrounds compared with hormone-sensitive cells. This apparent discrepancy suggests that the biological role of PTGIS may be context-dependent and not fully captured by a unidimensional survival coefficient alone. Given that PTGIS is involved in prostaglandin/lipid mediator metabolism, its elevated expression in advanced tumors may reflect compensatory inflammatory signaling, stromal interaction, or adaptive responses during malignant progression rather than a simple tumor-suppressive effect. Therefore, PTGIS was retained in the prognostic signature because of its statistical contribution to survival stratification, but its expression pattern in advanced disease indicates that its biological function may be more complex than that of the other three genes.

Taken together, these results indicate that the four-gene signature is not only statistically associated with survival outcome, but is also closely linked to clinicopathological aggressiveness and validated at both the protein and transcript levels in clinical samples and prostate cancer cell lines. Given the directional consistency between the prognostic model and the clinicopathological/cell-line validation results for ALDH3A2, ENO2, and PPP1CB, together with the more context-dependent behavior of PTGIS, we next prioritized ALDH3A2, ENO2, and PPP1CB for functional interrogation under oleic acid-induced lipid stress.

Functional Validation of Core Signature Genes Under Oleic Acid-Induced Lipid Stress in Prostate Cancer Cells

To further validate the functional relevance of the prognostic signature, we selected ALDH3A2, ENO2, and PPP1CB for loss-of-function analysis under oleic acid (OA)-induced lipid stress, as these three genes showed expression patterns more directionally consistent with both the prognostic model and clinicopathological validation. In contrast, PTGIS displayed a more context-dependent pattern in advanced clinical specimens and was therefore not prioritized for the initial functional analysis.

Western blot analysis confirmed efficient siRNA-mediated knockdown of ALDH3A2, ENO2, and PPP1CB in both VCaP and DU145 cells after 48 h of transfection (Fig. 7A-B). We then exposed the cells to OA to mimic a lipid-rich environment and measured malondialdehyde (MDA) levels as an indicator of lipid peroxidation (Fig. 7C). In both cell lines, OA treatment alone significantly increased MDA levels compared with the negative control group, confirming that lipid overload directly induces oxidative lipid stress in prostate cancer cells (Fig. 7D-E).

Among the three genes tested, ENO2 showed the most consistent risk-associated phenotype. In both VCaP and DU145 cells, silencing ENO2 significantly reduced OA-induced MDA accumulation, indicating that ENO2 promotes lipid peroxidation under lipid-rich conditions. By contrast, silencing PPP1CB significantly increased MDA levels beyond those observed in the OA-treated control group, especially in VCaP cells, suggesting that PPP1CB protects prostate cancer cells from OA-induced oxidative lipid damage.

ALDH3A2 also affected OA-induced lipid peroxidation, but in a manner distinct from the prognostic direction suggested by survival analysis. In both VCaP and DU145 cells, silencing ALDH3A2 reduced MDA levels compared with the NC-OA group, indicating that ALDH3A2 contributes to OA-associated lipid oxidative stress in these experimental settings. Notably, the suppressive effect of ALDH3A2 knockdown on MDA accumulation was observed in both androgen-dependent and androgen-independent cellular backgrounds, suggesting that its role in lipid stress regulation may be influenced by cell-specific metabolic context and may not be linearly predicted by its protective coefficient in the prognostic model.

Taken together, these results provide functional support for the biological relevance of the prognostic model and indicate that core signature genes actively participate in OA-induced lipid stress responses in prostate cancer cells. In particular, ENO2 and PPP1CB displayed effects fully consistent with their adverse and protective prognostic roles, whereas ALDH3A2 showed a context-dependent functional pattern, highlighting the biological complexity underlying lipid metabolism-related prognostic genes.

The Four-Gene Signature is Associated with Clinical Outcome, Immune Infiltration, and Pathway-Level Functional Coupling

To further evaluate the broader biological and clinical relevance of the four-gene prognostic signature, we next integrated the risk model with clinicopathological prediction, immune infiltration patterns, and pathway-level functional analyses. A nomogram was constructed by incorporating the four-gene risk score together with conventional clinical variables, including age and T stage (Fig. 8A). The nomogram indicated that the risk score and T stage contributed substantially to survival prediction, whereas age had a relatively smaller effect. Using this model, individualized probabilities of 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year overall survival could be estimated. In the illustrated example, a patient aged approximately 55–60 years with a low-risk score and T2 disease had a total score of about 69.7, corresponding to estimated survival probabilities of approximately 0.984 at 1 year, 0.950 at 3 years, and 0.920 at 5 years. These findings support the potential utility of the four-gene signature as a clinically relevant tool for individualized prognosis evaluation in prostate cancer.

To explore the relationship between the four-gene signature and the tumor immune microenvironment, we analyzed the correlations between the core genes and immune cell infiltration levels. The results showed that the four genes were associated with multiple immune cell populations, but with distinct patterns of correlation (Fig. 8B). PTGIS and PPP1CB displayed relatively broad immune-related associations, with positive correlations observed for naive B cells, resting mast cells, and resting CD4 memory T cells, whereas negative correlations were observed with several other immune subsets, including T follicular helper cells and selected NK- or T-cell populations. In contrast, ENO2 showed a different immune profile, with positive correlations with regulatory T cells (Tregs) and certain T-cell subsets, but negative correlations with M1 macrophages and plasma cells, suggesting a closer association with an immunosuppressive microenvironment. ALDH3A2 also exhibited a bidirectional pattern, with positive correlations with naive B cells, resting mast cells, and plasma cells, but negative correlations with macrophage-related populations and Tregs. Collectively, these data indicate that the four-gene signature is not only linked to lipid metabolic remodeling, but is also coupled to heterogeneous immune infiltration states in PCa.

To further define the pathway-level functional programs associated with risk stratification, ssGSEA was performed between the high-risk and low-risk groups. Significant differences were observed in several pathways related to metabolism, RNA processing, and signaling (Fig. 8C). In particular, arachidonic acid metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and RNA degradation differed significantly between the two groups, suggesting that the four-gene risk score captures coordinated alterations in inflammatory lipid signaling, energy metabolism, and post-transcriptional regulation. In addition, multiple metabolism-related pathways, including glycerolipid metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation, and ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, also showed distinct trends between the two groups, further supporting a broad metabolic divergence associated with risk stratification. Beyond metabolism, pathways such as focal adhesion, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, vascular smooth muscle contraction, and insulin signaling also varied between risk groups, indicating that the prognostic model reflects not only altered lipid metabolism but also broader functional coupling among metabolic adaptation, inflammatory signaling, and tumor plasticity.

Taken together, these results indicate that the four-gene signature has value beyond survival stratification alone. By integrating clinical variables, immune infiltration patterns, and pathway-level alterations, the model captures multiple dimensions of PCa biology and supports its potential utility as a clinically informative and biologically meaningful prognostic framework.

Discussion

Prostate cancer (PCa) progression is increasingly recognized as a process shaped not only by genetic and transcriptional alterations, but also by profound metabolic rewiring. In the present study, we combined clinical observations, in vivo validation, and progression-oriented transcriptomic analyses to investigate the role of lipid metabolism in PCa. We first found that PLIN3 expression was markedly elevated in clinical PCa tissues compared with benign prostatic hyperplasia, supporting the presence of an altered lipid metabolic state in human tumors. Consistent with this observation, high-fat feeding accelerated RM-1 tumor growth in vivo and was accompanied by enhanced neutral lipid enrichment and increased PLIN3 expression. These findings provided phenotypic and experimental evidence that lipid metabolic remodeling is closely associated with PCa progression. Building on this basis, we identified 44 lipid metabolism-related genes that were continuously dysregulated during the transition from normal tissue to primary tumor and then to metastatic disease, and further established a four-gene prognostic signature composed of ALDH3A2, ENO2, PPP1CB, and PTGIS. Importantly, we further strengthened the biological relevance of this signature through clinicopathological association analysis, protein- and transcript-level validation in clinical tissues and prostate cancer cell lines, and functional interrogation under oleic acid-induced lipid stress. Collectively, these results suggest that lipid metabolic rewiring is not merely a secondary consequence of tumor growth, but a sustained and biologically relevant feature of PCa evolution.

A major strength of this study is that it was designed around the concept of continuous progression rather than a conventional tumor-versus-normal comparison. This progression-oriented framework allowed us to prioritize genes that remained persistently altered across multiple disease stages and are therefore more likely to represent biologically meaningful drivers of tumor evolution [21]. Unbiased transcriptomic analysis first showed that PCa progression is accompanied by broad remodeling of cytoskeletal organization, focal adhesion, cell plasticity, and adaptive transcriptional programs. On this global background, our lipid metabolism-focused analysis revealed a distinct subset of 44 genes enriched in fatty acid metabolism, lipid catabolism, peroxisome-related processes, membrane-associated lipid organization, and central carbon metabolism. These findings are consistent with growing evidence that PCa depends heavily on lipid metabolic plasticity, including alterations in fatty acid utilization, phospholipid remodeling, and lipid-derived signaling [10]. This also helps explain why lipid metabolism was selected as the central entry point of the present study. Although glycolysis is a canonical hallmark of cancer metabolism, our clinical specimens, HFD-driven tumor model, and progression-based transcriptomic analysis converged more strongly on lipid metabolic alteration than on a predominantly glycolytic phenotype. In this context, lipid metabolism provided a more disease-relevant and data-driven framework for understanding PCa progression. Thus, rather than representing an isolated pathway, lipid metabolism appears to be embedded in a broader adaptive network that supports tumor progression and metastasis.

The four-gene signature identified in this study may provide a compact molecular readout of this altered metabolic state. ALDH3A2 is involved in fatty aldehyde detoxification and maintenance of lipid redox homeostasis, whereas ENO2 is more closely linked to glycolytic adaptation and aggressive tumor behavior [22]. PPP1CB may reflect altered phosphatase-mediated signaling and cytoskeletal regulation, while PTGIS may connect lipid mediator metabolism with inflammatory and microenvironmental signaling [23]. Notably, the model includes both protective and risk-associated genes, suggesting that PCa prognosis may depend not only on activation of malignant pathways but also on the relative preservation or disruption of metabolic homeostasis [24]. This balance may partly explain why the four-gene model retained prognostic value despite its relatively simple composition. Importantly, our HFD model also showed that ENO2 was significantly altered and Ptgis displayed a directional change under lipid-rich conditions, further suggesting that at least part of the four-gene signature is responsive to in vivo lipid metabolic perturbation, although the exact mechanisms require further investigation. Beyond statistical modeling, our expanded validation showed that the risk score was positively associated with T stage and Gleason score, and that the expression patterns of ALDH3A2, ENO2, and PPP1CB in clinical specimens and prostate cancer cell lines were broadly consistent with their prognostic directions. These findings support the idea that the model captures clinically meaningful metabolic heterogeneity rather than representing a purely computational output.

Among the four genes, PTGIS deserves special attention because its behavior appeared more complex than that of the other three genes. Although PTGIS was retained as a favorable prognostic gene in the TCGA-derived model, its protein expression was increased in higher-grade clinical specimens and tended to be elevated in more aggressive disease settings, including castration-resistant prostate cancer-related models. This apparent inconsistency suggests that the biological role of PTGIS may be context-dependent and not fully explained by its survival coefficient alone. Given that PTGIS participates in prostaglandin and lipid mediator metabolism, its increased expression in advanced tumors may reflect adaptive inflammatory signaling, stromal crosstalk, or compensatory metabolic responses during tumor progression rather than a simple tumor-suppressive role. In this regard, PTGIS may represent a prognostically informative but biologically non-linear component of the signature.

Another notable finding is that the four-gene signature was associated not only with lipid metabolism-related genes, but also with immune infiltration patterns and pathway-level functional changes. Correlation analysis showed that the four core genes were linked to multiple immune cell populations, with ENO2 showing a profile more compatible with an immunosuppressive environment, whereas PTGIS and PPP1CB displayed broader associations with adaptive and stromal-related immune components. In parallel, ssGSEA demonstrated that the risk model was related to arachidonic acid metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, RNA degradation, and multiple signaling pathways linked to tumor plasticity, suggesting functional coupling among lipid metabolism, inflammatory signaling, and microenvironmental remodeling [25]. These results are biologically plausible, as lipid metabolites and membrane-associated lipid signaling are increasingly recognized as regulators of immune cell behavior and tumor–microenvironment interactions [26]. Together, our data suggest that the prognostic value of the four-gene model may reflect not only metabolic state, but also broader biological heterogeneity in PCa.

Under oleic acid-induced lipid stress, silencing ENO2 significantly reduced MDA accumulation in both VCaP and DU145 cells, indicating that ENO2 promotes lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress in a manner consistent with its adverse prognostic role. In contrast, silencing PPP1CB further increased OA-induced MDA levels, supporting a protective role for PPP1CB against lipid oxidative damage. These two genes therefore showed a strong concordance between prognostic direction and functional phenotype. ALDH3A2 displayed a more context-dependent pattern: although it behaved as a favorable prognostic gene in the survival model, its knockdown reduced OA-induced MDA levels in both cell lines. This discrepancy suggests that the long-term prognostic behavior of ALDH3A2 may not be linearly translated into acute in vitro responses under lipid overload, and may instead reflect broader adaptive functions in redox balance and lipid stress regulation. Accordingly, the present functional data should be interpreted as preliminary evidence that core signature genes actively participate in lipid stress responses, while also underscoring the biological complexity of lipid metabolism-related prognostic genes.

From a clinical perspective, the four-gene signature may serve as a useful molecular complement to conventional clinicopathological parameters. The nomogram integrating the risk score, age, and T stage improved individualized survival prediction, supporting the potential value of this model in patient stratification [27]. At the same time, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was based largely on retrospective public datasets, and the model has not yet been validated in an independent external cohort [28]. Second, although the clinical specimens and HFD model support an altered lipid metabolic state in PCa, the mechanistic roles of the four core genes have not yet been fully dissected, particularly for PTGIS and ALDH3A2, which exhibited more context-dependent behavior than ENO2 and PPP1CB [29–32]. Third, because the TCGA prostate adenocarcinoma cohort contains relatively limited overall survival events, caution is needed when interpreting the stability of OS-based prognostic modeling [33]. Finally, our functional assays focused on OA-induced lipid peroxidation as an initial readout of lipid stress and therefore do not fully capture the entire spectrum of lipid metabolic regulation, such as fatty acid oxidation, phospholipid remodeling, or prostaglandin flux. Nevertheless, by integrating clinical observation, in vivo evidence, and progression-stage transcriptomics, our study provides a biologically coherent framework for identifying lipid metabolism-related biomarkers in PCa.

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrate that prostate cancer is associated with an altered lipid metabolic state at both the clinical and experimental levels. By integrating clinical specimens, a high-fat diet-driven tumor model, and progression-oriented transcriptomic analyses, we identified 44 lipid metabolism-related genes continuously dysregulated during PCa progression and established a four-gene prognostic signature with potential value for survival stratification and individualized risk assessment. Through additional clinicopathological validation, tissue- and cell-level expression analyses, and oleic acid-based functional assays, we further show that this signature is not only prognostically informative but also biologically relevant to lipid stress responses in PCa. These findings highlight lipid metabolic rewiring as an important component of PCa evolution and provide candidate biomarkers for future mechanistic and translational studies.
