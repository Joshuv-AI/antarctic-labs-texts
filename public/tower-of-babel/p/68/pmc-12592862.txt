Introduction

The first dental experience is important in molding a child's attitude toward dentistry and dental outcomes. Dental anxiety during visiting the clinic or treatment can have unwanted consequences, including uncontrollable behavior, lack of cooperation, and adverse psychological effects for patients. The results of these consequences are effective in the treatment outcome. Behavior management techniques are designed to enhance a child's coping mechanisms, ensure acceptance of dental care, and diminish their perception of stress or harm during appointments.

Pediatric dentists have access to various strategies, including the Tell-Show-Do (TSD) method, desensitization, modeling, positive reinforcement, voice control, distraction, parental presence or absence, restraint/protective stabilization, nonverbal communication, hand-over-mouth sedation, and general anesthesia.1 A key method in behavior management is the “TSD” approach. This method involves verbally explaining the procedures in a child-friendly manner (tell), demonstrating the procedure's sensory aspects without causing intimidation (show), and then carrying out the procedure as described (do). Recently, the TSD technique has evolved into the Tell-Play-Do (TPD) technique, which incorporates hands-on learning to help reduce children's fear and anxiety about dental treatments and encourage adaptive behavior.2

Currently, children from all age groups interact with mobile phones for playing games, but excessive use of phones is harmful for them. So, we have designed an indoor game, which aims to familiarize the child with dental procedures as they play with toys imitating dental instruments in a simple and fun way. However, literature on the use of the TPD technique in managing pediatric patients remains limited.3 Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of the TPD technique using a self-designed game compared to the TSD technique in managing anxious children. Study Setting and Eligibility of the Participants

The protocol of this clinical study is approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee of Dr HSRSM Dental College and Hospital, Hingoli (HDCH/IEC/2023-24/75). All the participants who reported to the out-patient department of pediatric and preventive dentistry between the age group of 6–10 years, visiting the clinic for the first time, who require dental intervention (extraction, restoration, pulp therapy, and oral prophylaxis) and belonged to negative, positive and definitely positive behavior (Wright's Modification of Frankl's behavior scale) were included in the study. Patients who belonged to definitely negative behavior (Wright's Modification of Frankl's behavior scale), had medical and/or developmental compromised conditions, and patients with mental/cognitive problems were excluded from the study.

Study Design

The study consisted of 40 children visiting the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry. They were graded according to Wright's modification as soon as they came to the operatory. Next, their pulse rate was recorded by the operator while examining them. Children were then randomly allocated into two groups.

Children in group 1 were briefed about the treatment and then were made to play the self-designed game: Hop-A-Dent, as guided by the operator (Fig. 1). In this game, a child was asked to hop on each number, and with each number, there was a toy associated that imitated the dental instrument. Instruments (Figs 2 and 3) and self-designed flashcards (Fig. 4) were incorporated for four treatment procedures—pulp therapy, restorative therapy, extraction, and oral prophylaxis. All the steps of the procedure were marked from 1 to 10, which explained the treatment procedure as well as the postoperative instructions. The postoperative instructions were on the flashcards with illustrations. After the game, their anxiety and behavior were rated by the operator according to Venham's anxiety and behavioral rating scale. After 7 minutes of the initiation of the treatment, their pulse rate was again recorded.

For children in group 2, the TSD technique was used. Participants were given a verbal explanation for the dental procedure, and a demonstration of various instruments was done in a friendly manner to build their confidence, followed by the actual procedure.

Statistical Analysis

All the data was entered into Microsoft Excel 2010. Descriptive statistics for age were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for each group. Two groups were compared for various parameters by the Unpaired t-test. Before and after comparison was done by a paired t-test. The Fisher's exact test was used to check the association between gender distribution and groups. Simple/multiple bar charts were used for graphical representation. For all the above tests, the p-value was considered statistically significant when it was < 0.05. The software used was SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) version 19.

Results

Demographic Data of the Participants

A total of 40 children were included in the study. There were 8 (40%) males and 12 (60%) females in group 1, while in group 2, there were 5 (25%) males and 15 (75%) females. The difference between the two groups was not statistically significant (p = 1). The mean age in group 1 was (7.90 ± 1.252), and in group 2, it was (7.85 ± 1.226). There was no significant statistical difference between the two groups regarding age (p = 0.899) (Fig. 5 and Table 1).

Discussion

The initial treatment a child experiences with a dentist is crucial, as it sets the tone for future dental care throughout their life. If the initial dental experience is unpleasant, it can lead to a lasting distrust of dentists and negatively impact the child's oral health in the long term. Gaining a child's trust in dental procedures is often a key to successfully completing treatments and fostering a positive attitude toward future visits.4 Creating a child-friendly environment, with elements such as cartoon-themed masks, scrubs, and dental instruments, can significantly reduce anxiety and stress in young patients. Crafting a welcoming and playful ambiance in the dental clinic is essential for effectively managing pediatric dental care.

According to Bahrololoomi et al., Tell-Play-Do is a newly developed behavior modification strategy, considered to be a variant of the TSD method. Play therapy, which involves a dynamic interpersonal relationship between the child and the therapist, helps the child manage negative emotions and pressures. In stressful situations, play therapy can be used as a coping mechanism, providing the child with a sense of control over the situation.5 Similar outcomes were found in our study. In 2017, Vishwakarma et al. compared the TPD technique and live modeling among children aged 5–7 years. They found that the TPD technique was more effective than live modeling in reducing children's fear and promoting cooperative behavior during treatment. The study revealed that the average heart rate at various intervals was significantly lower in the TPD group compared to the live modeling group, both during the first visit and the second visit.6 In our study, it was found that there was a statistically significant reduction in pulse score among group 1.

In today's era of the smartphone market, India is the third largest in end users. Research has shown that, in India, the frequency of mobile phone use was 68.6% and the rate of use by children was 56.6%. Rates also show that around 40% of children aged 10 years use a smartphone, and this number increases gradually with age.7 In 2017, Patil et al. conducted a study involving 60 children who used a mobile dental app called “My Little Dentist,” developed by Tenlogix Games. The children's anxiety levels were measured using the face imaging scale before and after playing the game. The results were highly significant: 86.67% of patients shifted from negative to positive behavior, 11.67% from positive to definitely positive, and 1.67% from definitely negative to negative according to Frankl's behavior rating scale. The study concluded that the mobile dental app is effective in reducing children's fear and anxiety toward dental treatment and can be used as an adjunct to traditional behavior management techniques.8 However, excessive use of phones can be detrimental to children, potentially leading to issues such as decreased attention span, sleep disturbances, and other developmental concerns.

To address this, we have created an indoor game specifically designed to introduce children to dental procedures in a more engaging and interactive way. In our study, we found a reduction in pulse rate after playing the game as compared to the TSD method. This self-designed game has successfully demonstrated that most of the children in this group were more relaxed and comfortable compared to those in the conventional group, which showed mild discomfort. Hence, the TPD technique could be an alternate behavioral modification technique to the TSD in pediatric dentistry.

Conclusion

Both the TPD and TSD approaches effectively reduced the children's anxiety. Nevertheless, the TPD behavior management technique was more successful in alleviating anxiety than the TSD method. This game helps the child reduce fear and anxiety and also to know the procedure that is going to be performed. Indoor games develop the cognitive and problem-solving skills of the child. It builds focus and attention and improves short-term memory. This game helps the child think faster and come up with the right solution as they get to explore the instruments. Also, jumping aids in whole-body exercise. This is a good exercise to utilize their energy in the right way.
