Negative pressure wound therapy for the prevention of surgical site complications in emergency laparotomies: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Carlos Eduardo Rey Chaves, Claudia Milena Orozco Chamorro, Camilo Ramírez-Giraldo, Antonio Pesce, Juan Pablo Blanco-Pulido, David Guillermo Gómez Garnica, Jorge Andrés Gutiérrez Segura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSurgical site infections (SSIs) are common after emergency laparotomy and are associated with substantial morbidity, prolonged recovery, and increased health care costs. Negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) has been increasingly used prophylactically on closed incisions in high-risk surgical patients; however, evidence supporting its effectiveness after emergency laparotomy remains heterogeneous. This updated systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to reassess the effectiveness of NPWT compared with standard postoperative dressings for the prevention of SSI and other wound-related complications following emergency laparotomy.Materials and methodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook and PRISMA 2020 guidelines. PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane CENTRAL, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched for studies published from April 2, 2022, to December 10, 2025, updating a prior review. Randomized clinical trials and comparative observational studies evaluating NPWT versus standard dressings in adult patients undergoing emergency laparotomy were included. The primary outcome was SSI. Secondary outcomes included superficial and deep SSI, wound dehiscence, seroma, hematoma, and length of hospital stay. Random-effects meta-analyses were performed. Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2 and ROBINS-I, and certainty of evidence was evaluated with GRADE.ResultsTwenty-three studies involving 5371 patients were included, of whom 2528 received NPWT. NPWT was associated with a significantly lower risk of SSI compared with standard dressings (risk ratio, 0.53; 95% CI 0.41–0.68), with moderate to substantial heterogeneity (I² = 68%). NPWT was also associated with reductions in superficial and deep SSI, seroma formation, and wound dehiscence, but not with organ/space SSI, hematoma, or length of hospital stay. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent direction of effect. Funnel plot asymmetry and a significant Egger test suggested small-study effects.ConclusionAmong patients undergoing emergency laparotomy, prophylactic NPWT applied to closed incisions was associated with a lower risk of SSI and several wound-related complications compared with standard postoperative dressings. However, the certainty of evidence remains very low due to heterogeneity, risk of bias, and small-study effects, highlighting the need for large pragmatic multicenter randomized trials to better define the magnitude of benefit and optimal patient selection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13017-026-00716-9.

Introduction

Surgical site infections (SSIs) remain among the most frequent and consequential complications following surgery and constitute a major contributor to postoperative morbidity worldwide. Their incidence varies substantially across health systems, with consistently lower rates reported in high-income countries and markedly higher burdens observed in low- and middle-income settings, reflecting differences in patient characteristics, surgical complexity, and perioperative resources [1, 2]. Importantly, in high-risk surgical scenarios such as emergency laparotomy, SSI rates are considerably higher than those observed after elective procedures [3, 4]. Beyond increasing healthcare utilization and costs, SSIs adversely affect postoperative recovery, delay wound healing, prolong hospitalization, and contribute to long-term sequelae such as incisional hernia and impaired quality of life [5–8].

Emergency laparotomy is performed globally for a wide range of unplanned conditions, including inflammatory, ischemic, traumatic, and neoplastic diseases, and represents a substantial proportion of general surgical activity. Patients undergoing emergency laparotomy frequently present with physiological derangement, sepsis, hemodynamic instability, and a higher degree of intraoperative contamination. Additional factors such as stoma formation, limited preoperative optimization, malnutrition, and comorbidity burden further increase susceptibility to SSI and other wound-related complications, including hematoma, seroma, wound dehiscence, and prolonged length of hospital stay [9–11].

Negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) is an established modality for the management of acute and chronic wounds and has increasingly been adopted prophylactically for closed surgical incisions in patients at elevated risk of wound complications. Proposed mechanisms include continuous removal of wound exudate, reduction of tissue edema, improved microvascular perfusion at the incision edges, and limitation of bacterial contamination [12–14]. Initially introduced in orthopedic and trauma surgery, closed-incision NPWT has since been applied across multiple general surgical procedures. International guidelines for the prevention of SSIs, including those from the World Health Organization and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, recommend consideration of NPWT in selected high-risk patients [15, 16].

However, evidence supporting routine NPWT use following emergency laparotomy remains heterogeneous. While benefits have been demonstrated in specific contexts such as ventral hernia repair and high-risk elective surgery, studies focused on emergency laparotomy vary widely in design, populations, and reported outcomes, resulting in persistent uncertainty regarding its effectiveness in this setting. An earlier systematic review and meta-analysis included studies published only up to April 2022 [17]. Since then, several randomized controlled trials and observational studies have expanded the evidence base, providing more granular data on wound-related complications and clinically relevant outcomes such as postoperative length of stay.

Accordingly, an updated systematic review and meta-analysis is warranted. The objective of this study was to reassess whether NPWT applied to closed incisions improves outcomes compared with standard postoperative dressings in patients undergoing emergency laparotomy, focusing on SSI as the primary outcome and wound-related complications and length of hospital stay as secondary outcomes.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

This study was a systematic review and meta-analysis of the existing evidence on NPWT following emergency laparotomy. The review was designed and performed in accordance with the methodological guidance of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [18]. Reporting followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement (supplement 1) [19]. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD420251268432).

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and ClinicalTrials.gov to identify relevant studies evaluating NPWT in emergency laparotomy. The search covered the period from April 2, 2022, to December 10, 2025.It used a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords, including: “negative pressure wound therapy,” “negative pressure wound dressing,” “NPWT,” “NPWD,” “NPD,” “NPT,” “PICO,” “Prevena,” “laparotomy,” “abdominal wound,” and “abdominal incision.”This review was designed as an updated meta-analysis of the previously published systematic review by Lakhani et al., which included studies published between January 1, 2005, and April 1, 2022 [17]. Therefore, we applied the same search terms and strategy described by Lakhani et al. to ensure methodological consistency and capture newly published evidence beyond the original review period. In addition, the reference lists of all included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened using backward and forward snowballing, without any restrictions on publication date, to identify additional eligible articles. No language restrictions were applied. Study selection was performed independently by two reviewers (C.E.R C and C.M.O C) using Rayyan Rayyan® [20]. After removal of duplicates, titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (C.R-G).

Study selection

This systematic review and meta-analysis included comparative studies evaluating the effectiveness of NPWT versus standard postoperative surgical dressings in adult patients undergoing emergency laparotomy. Both randomized clinical trials (RCTs) and observational interventional studies (prospective or retrospective cohorts) were considered eligible to capture the whole body of available evidence in this clinical setting.Studies were included if they directly compared NPWT with conventional postoperative wound dressings and reported sufficient quantitative data to calculate effect estimates, specifically risk ratios (RRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for at least one predefined outcome of interest.

The following exclusion criteria were applied: single-arm studies without a comparator group; case reports or case series; narrative reviews; systematic reviews or meta-analyses; trial registrations without published results; conference abstracts lacking sufficient data; and animal or preclinical studies. There were no restrictions based on patient sex, geographic location, or publication language. Studies involving elective laparotomies, laparoscopic procedures, or non-emergency surgical populations were excluded to maintain clinical homogeneity.

By including both randomized and non-randomized comparative studies, this meta-analysis aimed to provide a comprehensive and pragmatic assessment of the effectiveness of NPWT in real-world emergency laparotomy practice.

Data collection

Two reviewers (C.R-G. and A.P.) independently extracted data from all included studies using a predefined and standardized data collection form. Extracted information included study characteristics (title, first author, year of publication, study design, country, and study period), type of NPWT) device used, wound classification according to CDC, duration of follow-up, and reported clinical outcomes of interest. Additional relevant methodological or clinical remarks specific to each study were recorded when applicable. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus.

All analyses were conducted using aggregate data as reported in the published manuscripts. Individual participant data were not available, as none of the included studies provided access to patient-level datasets.

In studies reporting overlapping patient cohorts, only the publication with the most comprehensive dataset and the largest sample size was retained to avoid double counting of participants. When propensity score matching (PSM) was performed, outcome data were extracted exclusively from the post-matching population. This approach was chosen because PSM aims to reduce confounding and baseline imbalance between comparison groups, thereby providing effect estimates that more closely approximate those of randomized comparisons. Pre-matching data were not used, as they may reflect systematic differences between groups and potentially bias pooled effect estimates.

Outcomes and subgroup analysis

The primary outcome of interest for this systematic review and meta-analysis was the incidence of SSI following emergency laparotomy. Secondary outcomes included specific wound-related complications, namely superficial SSI, deep SSI, wound dehiscence, hematoma, and seroma formation, as well as postoperative length of hospital stay (LOS). These outcomes were selected to comprehensively capture the spectrum of surgical site complications potentially influenced by NPWT.

Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of clinical and methodological heterogeneity and to assess the robustness of the primary outcome across different settings. Subgroup comparisons included: (1) wound management strategy (open versus primarily closed incisions), (2) indication for emergency laparotomy (trauma-related versus nontrauma-related causes), (3) study design (RCTs versus observational studies), and (4) wound contamination level. Because individual studies often included mixed wound classes, contamination was categorized at the study level according to the reported distribution of CDC wound classes. Studies were classified as predominantly contaminated or dirty when ≥ 50% of wounds were CDC class III–IV and as predominantly clean or clean-contaminated otherwise. A sensitivity analysis using a more stringent threshold (≥ 80% CDC class III–IV wounds) was also performed to evaluate the robustness of this classification). Subgroup analyses were performed only when sufficient data were available within each category to allow meaningful comparisons. These analyses were considered exploratory and were interpreted cautiously.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers (C.E.RC. and J.P.B-P). Randomized clinical trials were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool version 2 (RoB 2). In contrast, nonrandomized comparative studies were assessed with the Risk of Bias in Nonrandomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool.

Studies were classified according to their overall risk of bias following the guidance of each instrument. Randomized trials were considered high quality if they were judged to have a low risk of bias. In contrast, nonrandomized studies were considered high quality only if they were assessed as having a low risk of bias. Studies with higher risk-of-bias judgments were categorized accordingly.

Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion, with adjudication by a third reviewer (A.P.) when consensus could not be reached.

The overall certainty of the evidence for each outcome was subsequently evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach and rated as high, moderate, low, or very low [21].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted within a predefined superiority framework, as NPWT requires additional resources compared with standard postoperative dressings. Accordingly, effect estimates were interpreted to determine whether NPWT demonstrated statistically and clinically superior outcomes. For dichotomous outcomes, pooled effect estimates were calculated as RRs with corresponding 95% CI. Continuous outcomes were summarized using mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs.

Random-effects meta-analyses were performed to account for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Between-study variance (τ²) was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method. Dichotomous outcomes were pooled using the Mantel–Haenszel method, and Knapp–Hartung adjustments were applied to derive confidence intervals, providing more conservative inference in the presence of heterogeneity. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and quantified using the I² statistic, which was categorized as low (< 25%), moderate (25%–50%), or substantial (> 75%). A P value < 0.10 for the Q test was considered indicative of statistically significant heterogeneity. Prespecified subgroup interaction tests were assessed using a P < 0.10 threshold, in accordance with Cochrane recommendations. Sensitivity analyses included leave-one-out analyses and Baujat plots to evaluate the influence of individual studies on pooled estimates and heterogeneity. Random-effects meta-regression analyses were also conducted to explore whether study-level covariates, including the proportion of contaminated or dirty wounds (CDC class III–IV) and skin closure strategy, were associated with the magnitude of the treatment effect. Trial sequential analysis was performed for the primary outcome of SSI using a random-effects model to control for random errors due to sparse data and repeated significance testing. A 2-sided α of 5%, 80% power, and a prespecified relative risk reduction of 25% were applied. The required information size was adjusted for heterogeneity, and cumulative Z-curves were assessed against sequential monitoring boundaries for benefit.

When the length of hospital stay was reported as median and range (minimum–maximum), means and standard deviations were estimated using the method proposed by Hozo et al. [22]. When reported as median and interquartile range, estimates were derived using the method described by Wan et al. [23]. Given the typically right-skewed distribution of hospital stay, these estimates should be interpreted with caution.

Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically using Egger’s regression test when at least 10 studies were available for a given outcome.

All analyses were performed using Rstudio® (version2023.12.1 + 402) [24].

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The study selection process is presented in Fig. 1. A total of 23 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis, including 7 studies reassessed from a previous version of the review. Overall, the studies comprised 5371 patients, of whom 2528 received NPWT and 2843 received standard postoperative surgical dressings. 11 studies were randomized clinical trials, enrolling 1497 patients, while the remainder were observational (Supplement 2 shows reasons for exclusion).

All included studies evaluated patients undergoing emergency laparotomy, with variability related to the indication for surgery rather than the surgical setting. 14 of 23 studies included emergency laparotomies for all indications, 5 focused exclusively on intestinal perforation, and 2 included trauma-related laparotomies. The remaining 2 studies excluded trauma and included only nontraumatic emergency abdominal procedures.

In 15 of the 23 studies, primary skin closure was performed in both treatment groups prior to application of the intervention. In the study by Costa et al., three treatment arms were included, two of which used different NPWT devices; these were combined into a single intervention group as they represented comparable therapeutic strategies. Similarly, Lozano-Balderas et al. reported a three-arm study in which one group received NPWT, while two control groups underwent delayed primary closure and primary closure; these latter two were merged into a single comparator group. In the study by Rafaqat et al., two distinct patient cohorts were analyzed, comparing NPWT versus standard dressings after full abdominal closure and fascia-only closure, respectively.

Primary outcome

Twenty-one studies including 4972 patients reported SSI. NPWT was associated with a significantly lower risk of SSI compared with standard dressings, demonstrating superiority over standard postoperative dressings (risk ratio, 0.53; 95% CI 0.41–0.68; p < 0.001). Moderate to substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (Q = 66.0; p < 0.001; I² = 68%) (Fig. 2). Visual inspection of the funnel plot for SSI showed asymmetry (Fig. 3). Egger’s test demonstrated evidence of small-study effects (p = 0.0005).

NPWT was associated with lower risks of superficial SSI (14 studies; 3417 participants; risk ratio, 0.50; 95% CI 0.39 to 0.64; heterogeneity I² = 0%)(Forest plot: Supplement 3a) and deep SSI (11 studies; 2957 participants; risk ratio, 0.43; 95% CI 0.29 to 0.63; I² = 0%)(Forest plot: Supplement 3b). Sensibility analysis

Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses showed that exclusion of any single study did not materially change the pooled effect estimate for SSI, which consistently favored NPWT. The magnitude and direction of the effect remained stable across all iterations, and heterogeneity persisted at a moderate level, suggesting that no individual study unduly influenced the results. Baujat plots were consistent with these findings, indicating that although some studies—most notably the SUNRISE trial—contributed more to overall heterogeneity and influence on the pooled estimate, their exclusion did not meaningfully alter the magnitude, direction, or statistical significance of the overall effect (Supplement 4).

Subgroup analysis

In prespecified subgroup analyses, the association between NPWT and reduced risk of SSI was consistent across study design and closure strategy. NPWT was associated with a lower risk of SSI in both randomized controlled trials and nonrandomized studies, with no significant interaction by study design (P for interaction = 0.31), and similar risk reductions were observed regardless of skin closure technique (P for interaction = 0.66). In contrast, the magnitude of the association differed by surgical indication (P for interaction = 0.002), with the strongest effect observed in studies excluding trauma patients, followed by procedures for intestinal perforation, while studies including mixed surgical indications demonstrated a more modest but still significant reduction in SSI risk.

Additional subgroup analyses were conducted according to wound contamination level. When studies were categorized using a majority threshold (≥ 50% of wounds classified as CDC class III–IV), NPWT was associated with a lower risk of SSI both in studies predominantly including clean or clean-contaminated wounds (RR, 0.43; 95% CI 0.24–0.79) and in those predominantly including contaminated or dirty wounds (RR, 0.58; 95% CI 0.42–0.80), with no significant interaction between groups (P for interaction = 0.30). Using a more stringent definition of predominantly contaminated surgical fields (≥ 80% CDC class III–IV wounds), NPWT remained associated with a lower risk of SSI in studies with highly contaminated wounds (RR, 0.50; 95% CI 0.39–0.63; I² = 10%). In contrast, studies including mixed or predominantly clean/clean-contaminated wounds showed a smaller and non–statistically significant reduction (RR, 0.62; 95% CI 0.36–1.07; I² = 78%), although the test for subgroup interaction was not statistically significant (P for interaction = 0.36).

Overall, these findings suggest that the association between NPWT and lower SSI risk was generally consistent across several study-level characteristics, including study design, wound closure strategy, and wound contamination level. However, evidence of effect modification was observed for surgical indication, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of trauma studies (Supplement 5).

Meta-regression analysis

To further explore potential sources of heterogeneity, random-effects meta-regression analyses were performed using study-level covariates. In univariable meta-regression, the proportion of contaminated or dirty wounds (CDC class III–IV) was not significantly associated with the magnitude of the treatment effect (β = −0.12; 95% CI − 1.01 to 0.78; P = 0.79) (Fig. 6). Similarly, skin closure strategy was not associated with the treatment effect (β = −0.08; 95% CI − 0.61 to 0.45; P = 0.76). In the multivariable model including both covariates, neither wound contamination proportion (β = −0.19; 95% CI − 1.18 to 0.81; P = 0.70) nor skin closure strategy (β = −0.12; 95% CI − 0.71 to 0.46; P = 0.67) significantly explained between-study variability in treatment effect.

Trial sequential analysis

Trial sequential analysis for the primary outcome of SSI showed that the cumulative Z-curve crossed the trial sequential monitoring boundary for benefit early and remained beyond this boundary throughout subsequent study accumulation. Although the required information size adjusted for heterogeneity was not fully reached, the observed effect remained statistically significant after sequential adjustment (RR, 0.52; 95% sequentially adjusted CI 0.32–0.69), indicating that the reduction in SSI associated with NPWT is unlikely to represent a spurious finding due to random error (Supplement 6).

Risk of bias and study quality

Risk of bias was assessed using the RoB 2 tool for randomized trials and the ROBINS-I tool for nonrandomized studies (Figs. 7 and 8) (Supplement 7). Among randomized trials, the risk of bias arising from the randomization process was generally low. However, several trials raised concerns in other domains, particularly due to deviations from intended interventions and outcome measurement, reflecting the open-label nature of surgical interventions and variability in postoperative wound assessment. Consequently, while some trials were judged as having only some concerns, a substantial proportion were classified as being at high overall risk of bias due to the accumulation of domain-level concerns. In contrast, nonrandomized studies frequently exhibited moderate to serious risk of bias, predominantly driven by confounding and, to a lesser extent, selection of participants. Although classification of interventions and completeness of outcome data were often adequate, residual confounding related to patient severity, surgical decision-making, and perioperative management remained a major methodological limitation. Overall, these findings indicate that both randomized and nonrandomized evidence are affected by important methodological constraints, which should be carefully considered when interpreting the estimated effects of negative pressure wound therapy after emergency laparotomy.

Discussion

In this updated systematic review and meta-analysis, NPWT applied to closed incisions after emergency laparotomy was associated with a significantly lower risk of SSI compared with standard postoperative dressings. This association was supported by a large evidence base comprising more than 5,000 patients and remained directionally consistent across sensitivity analyses, prespecified subgroup analyses, and trial sequential analysis. In addition to overall SSI, NPWT was associated with reductions in superficial and deep SSI, seroma formation, and wound dehiscence, whereas no statistically significant differences were observed for organ/space SSI, hematoma, or LOS.

These findings meaningfully extend prior evidence by incorporating a substantial number of recently published randomized and observational studies beyond earlier syntheses, including large contemporary trials such as the SUNRRISE study [42]. Compared with prior meta-analyses [17], the present work provides more precise estimates across several wound-related outcomes and better reflects modern emergency surgical practice across diverse indications and health systems. The observed reduction in incisional complications is biologically plausible given proposed mechanisms of NPWT—improved wound perfusion, reduced edema, mechanical stabilization of the wound edges, and protection from external contamination—which are well described in experimental and clinical studies [12–14]. These mechanisms would be expected to preferentially influence superficial and deep incisional infection rather than organ/space SSI, which is more closely related to intra-abdominal source control.

Despite the consistency in direction of effect, moderate to substantial heterogeneity was observed for the primary outcome. Prespecified subgroup analyses suggested that the magnitude of benefit varied by surgical indication, with stronger effects in studies excluding trauma patients and in procedures performed for intestinal perforation. In contrast, no significant effect modification was observed by study design or closure strategy, supporting robustness across methodological and technical contexts. However, these subgroup analyses should be interpreted cautiously, as they are based on study-level characteristics and therefore remain vulnerable to residual confounding and ecological bias.

Because wound contamination and incision management represent key determinants of postoperative infection risk, additional analyses were conducted to explore whether these factors modified the observed treatment effect. Subgroup analyses based on wound contamination level showed that the reduction in SSI associated with NPWT was present both in studies with predominantly clean or clean-contaminated wounds and in those including predominantly contaminated or dirty wounds. Notably, when a stricter definition of highly contaminated surgical fields was applied (≥ 80% CDC class III–IV wounds), the reduction in SSI remained significant and was accompanied by substantially lower between-study heterogeneity. However, formal tests for interaction were not statistically significant. Consistent with these findings, meta-regression analyses demonstrated that neither the proportion of contaminated wounds nor the skin closure strategy was significantly associated with the magnitude of the treatment effect. Together, these results suggest that the potential benefit of NPWT may extend across a wide spectrum of wound contamination levels and incision management strategies, although these analyses remain exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously.

Importantly, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses and Baujat plots indicated that no individual study, including influential large trials, unduly drove the pooled estimate, and the overall association consistently favored NPWT. Trial sequential analysis further supported robustness, with the cumulative Z-curve crossing monitoring boundaries for benefit and remaining significant after sequential adjustment, suggesting that the observed reduction in SSI is unlikely to be explained by random error alone [18]. Nonetheless, the required information size adjusted for heterogeneity was not fully reached, indicating that additional high-quality data—particularly from large pragmatic multicenter trials—could still refine the magnitude of benefit and better define effect modifiers.

A key consideration for interpretation is the presence of funnel plot asymmetry and statistically significant small-study effects for SSI. This may reflect selective publication of smaller studies with larger treatment effects; however, alternative explanations are plausible. Emergency laparotomy encompasses a heterogeneous clinical spectrum, and smaller single-center studies may preferentially represent higher-risk populations or settings where the absolute benefit of NPWT is greater, inflating apparent effects [3, 4]. Critically, small-study effects should be interpreted in the context of substantial between-study heterogeneity and the inclusion of several large contemporary trials with more conservative estimates, including registry-based and multicenter randomized studies [29, 42]. While smaller studies tended to favor NPWT, larger trials showed effect estimates closer to the null, suggesting that the pooled effect may reflect an average across diverse clinical contexts rather than a uniform treatment benefit. Accordingly, small-study effects do not negate the overall direction of association but warrant caution when interpreting the magnitude of effect.

Several limitations merit emphasis. First, the evidence base included both randomized and nonrandomized studies, and nonrandomized studies commonly carried moderate risk of bias, particularly due to confounding despite the use of propensity score matching in some cohorts [29, 32, 43]. Second, clinical heterogeneity was substantial, including variability in surgical indication, wound contamination class, adjunctive measures, and NPWT protocols (device type, pressure settings, and duration of therapy). In addition, wound contamination categories were frequently reported as mixed distributions within individual studies, requiring study-level classification for subgroup analyses, which may introduce ecological bias. Third, visual funnel plot asymmetry and a statistically significant Egger test suggested the presence of small-study effects or publication bias, which may have inflated the observed effect size. Finally, estimation of length of hospital stay relied on indirect methods in several studies, limiting the interpretability of this outcome.

Consistent with these considerations, the certainty of evidence for SSI was rated as very low using the GRADE approach [21], reflecting risk of bias, heterogeneity, and suspected small-study effects rather than inconsistency in the direction of effect. From a clinical standpoint, these findings support NPWT as a potentially valuable component of SSI prevention strategies after emergency laparotomy, particularly in high-risk emergency surgical settings where wound contamination and physiologic stress are common. Future research should prioritize adequately powered pragmatic multicenter randomized trials with standardized NPWT protocols, and prespecified high-risk strata to clarify which patients derive the greatest absolute benefit. Parallel cost-effectiveness analyses are also essential, given the substantial resource implications of prophylactic NPWT and the known economic burden of SSI [5–8].

Conclusion

Among patients undergoing emergency laparotomy with closed incisions, NPWT was associated with a lower risk of SSI and several wound-related complications compared with standard postoperative dressings. The observed association appeared consistent across different levels of wound contamination and incision management strategies. However, the certainty of evidence remains very low due to methodological limitations, heterogeneity, and small-study effects, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation of effect magnitude and for further high-quality multicenter trials to better define optimal patient selection and implementation.
