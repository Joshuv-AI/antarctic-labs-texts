Recurrent severe hypersensitivity reaction following repeated paclitaxel-eluting balloon angioplasty in the coronary arteries: a case report
Authors: De Kui Gao, Qian Cai, Huizhen Wang, Wenyuan Wang, Xin Li, Yan Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPaclitaxel drug-coated balloons (P-DCBs) are widely used for selected coronary lesions, but severe hypersensitivity reactions during coronary P-DCB angioplasty are rarely recognized. We report a rare case of recurrent severe hypersensitivity reaction temporally associated with repeated coronary P-DCB (Note: For ease of expression, the "Paclitaxel-eluting balloon" used in this case will be uniformly referred to as "P-DCB".) angioplasty.Case presentationA 60-year-old man with multivessel coronary artery disease developed two episodes of acute hypotension, transient loss of consciousness, and subsequent cutaneous manifestations after repeated coronary P-DCB angioplasty. Background

Drug-coated balloon (DCB) angioplasty delivers antiproliferative agents (e.g., paclitaxel) directly to the vessel wall without permanent implants. This approach obviates the need for stents, shortens dual antiplatelet therapy duration, and is particularly beneficial for high-bleeding-risk patients. DCB angioplasty has been proven to be an effective treatment strategy for coronary small vessel disease and bifurcation lesions, especially in cases involving branch vessels. Paclitaxel demonstrates pronounced lipophilicity and potent anti-proliferative activity, which has established P-DCBs as a commonly used DCB platform in contemporary clinical applications. While systemic paclitaxel administration in oncology is associated with a 10–40% incidence of hypersensitivity reactions [1], allergic complications following local vascular delivery via P-DCBs remain exceptionally rare, with only a limited number of reported cases [2–5], most of which occurred in peripheral interventions, and to our knowledge, no previously reported coronary cases. We report a rare coronary case of recurrent severe allergic reaction following repeated P-DCB angioplasty. This case highlights a potentially underrecognized complication and emphasizes the need for heightened vigilance during coronary P-DCB therapy.

Case report

A 60-year-old male with a 9-year history of recurrent chest tightness was diagnosed with multivessel coronary artery disease, with severe atherosclerotic stenosis in both the left and right coronary arteries. His medical history included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia, but no known drug allergies, though he reported allergies to Toxicodendron vernicifluum (raw lacquer) and Toona sinensis (Chinese toon sprouts). Follow-up management

Follow-up management focused mainly on secondary prevention of coronary artery disease and avoidance of re-exposure to the suspected culprit drug-coated device. Despite intensified statin therapy, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) remained elevated (3.54–5.46 mmol/L). Evolocumab was initiated in May 2024 with a 420 mg loading dose, followed by 140 mg every two weeks, reducing LDL-C levels to 2.06–2.66 mmol/L. Although target LDL-C levels were not fully attained, angina symptoms significantly improved. Follow-up coronary angiography on April 1, 2026 showed recanalization of the occluded second-order diagonal branch, with no obvious in-stent restenosis or marked progression of the other target lesions (Supplementary Figure S9).

Discussion

According to the EAACI guidelines and the Brown criteria for severe anaphylaxis, this case clinically fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for severe anaphylaxis [6, 7]. The acute onset of cardiovascular collapse, including profound hypotension and transient loss of consciousness, together with subsequent pruritus and urticarial rash after exposure to a potential trigger, supported the diagnosis. The Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale (Supplementary Table 1) yielded a total score of 9, and the Drug Interaction Probability Scale (Supplementary Table 2) scored 9 points, both indicating a highly probable association with paclitaxel [8, 9]. A review of the patient’s examinations and procedures involving contrast agents and implantable materials over the past 12 years is summarized in Table 1. The patient had previously tolerated multiple iodinated contrast agents on several occasions, including iohexol (the excipient of the P-DCB used in this case). Therefore, hypersensitivity to both the contrast medium and the P-DCB excipient was deemed unlikely. Although an acute hypersensitivity reaction to the RDES II stent cannot be completely excluded, the clinical characteristics of paclitaxel-eluting stent and drug-coated balloon allergy (Supplemental File Table 3), along with the recent follow-up coronary angiography findings (Supplementary File Figure S10 and Figure S11: no significant in-stent restenosis was observed at the RDES II implantation site), further reduce the likelihood of an allergic reaction related to the RDES II. Together with the subsequent cutaneous manifestations, these findings made a P-DCB-related hypersensitivity reaction clinically the most likely explanation. Nevertheless, acute serum tryptase, baseline tryptase, skin testing, basophil activation testing, and isolated component challenge were not performed, partly because paclitaxel skin testing and basophil activation testing were not available at our institution. The absence of these tests limit direct allergologic confirmation and causal assessment. To our knowledge, severe hypersensitivity reactions associated with coronary P-DCB angioplasty have not been previously reported.

While systemic paclitaxel hypersensitivity occurs in 10–40% of oncologic applications [1], allergic reactions to P-DCBs remain exceptionally rare, with only a limited number of previously reported cases, most of which involved peripheral vascular interventions [2–4]. Previously reported peripheral vascular adverse reactions associated with P-DCBs have shown heterogeneous timing and severity, ranging from delayed hypersensitivity reactions to acute hemodynamic instability or Kounis-like presentations. In our case, hemodynamic collapse occurred shortly after paclitaxel release, followed by pruritus and rash, and recurred more rapidly after re-exposure. No angiographic or electrocardiographic features suggestive of Kounis syndrome were observed. This heterogeneity may be explained by differences in the treated vascular bed, paclitaxel exposure, prior sensitization, balloon excipients, and the underlying allergic or pseudoallergic mechanisms. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of severe hypersensitivity reaction associated with coronary P-DCB angioplasty and also the first reported instance of re-exposure after a severe P-DCB-related hypersensitivity episode. In systemic paclitaxel chemotherapy, rapid retreatment or desensitization strategies have been used to allow continued treatment after hypersensitivity reactions [10]. However, suspected paclitaxel hypersensitivity should generally prompt avoidance of further P-DCB exposure. If re-exposure is considered unavoidable because of compelling clinical need, it should only be undertaken after careful multidisciplinary assessment, full informed consent, preparation of emergency equipment and rescue medications, and intensive intraprocedural monitoring. Premedication with corticosteroids and H1/H2 antihistamines, extrapolated from systemic paclitaxel therapy, may theoretically reduce the risk of recurrent reactions, but evidence in P-DCB angioplasty is lacking; therefore, such an approach should be considered with great caution and requires further study. In our institutional retrospective review, we included 640 consecutive patients who underwent a total of 826 coronary P-DCB procedures at our institution between May 14, 2018 and June 4, 2025. Allergic reactions occurred in 2 patients with 4 episodes, and severe recurrent hypersensitivity occurred in only 1 patient. These institutional data suggest that severe allergic reactions during coronary P-DCB angioplasty are rare, although the incidence estimates should be interpreted cautiously because adverse events were identified retrospectively from clinical and procedural records rather than collected through a predefined prospective adverse-event registry. Although the patient had reported allergies to Toxicodendron vernicifluum (raw lacquer) and Toona sinensis (Chinese toon sprouts), potential cross-reactivity with paclitaxel remains uncertain. The subtle and atypical presentation of coronary P-DCB -related reactions may lead to under-recognition in clinical practice.

Diagnostic challenges arise from overlapping clinical features with procedural complications, including hypovolemia, vasovagal responses, thromboembolism, vascular dissection, coronary perforation, slow flow, no-reflow, and cardiac tamponade. These diagnostic challenges were further compounded by intraoperative physical examination limitations and the delayed appearance of characteristic cutaneous manifestations. In the present case, vasovagal syncope was initially considered because both episodes involved hypotension, bradycardia, and transient loss of consciousness. However, isolated vasovagal syncope does not readily explain the subsequent pruritus and urticarial rash, nor the recurrence of a similar reaction after re-exposure to P-DCB angioplasty. Procedural complications were considered less likely because coronary angiography during the episodes showed vessel patency without evidence of perforation, spasm, slow flow, or no-reflow. Nitroglycerin-related hypotension was also considered during the first episode; however, it could not explain the second episode, during which nitroglycerin was not used. Although a small second-order diagonal branch occlusion and mild postoperative troponin elevation were noted after the second procedure, these findings did not explain the recurrence of a similar systemic reaction or the subsequent cutaneous manifestations. Kounis syndrome was also considered because allergic reactions during coronary intervention may be accompanied by coronary spasm, thrombosis, or ischemic electrocardiographic changes [4]. However, angiography showed no acute vessel occlusion, and no persistent ischemic ECG changes were observed.

The typical clinical manifestation of shock is usually characterized by hypotension accompanied by tachycardia. Although severe anaphylaxia presenting with hypotension is commonly associated with tachycardia, a subset of severe anaphylaxis cases may present with bradycardia due to various pathophysiological mechanisms [11]. In the present case, both episodes featured acute hypotension with bradycardia, closely mimicking vasovagal syncope. The diagnosis would have been particularly challenging without the subsequent appearance of cutaneous rash and recurrence upon re-exposure. The mechanism underlying bradycardia remains uncertain. Potential contributors include a vasovagal component, impaired compensatory tachycardia related to chronic β-blocker use, and autonomic responses during severe hypersensitivity [12–14]. Therefore, bradycardia should not be interpreted as excluding anaphylaxis. In patients with severe coronary artery disease, vasopressor therapy during suspected anaphylaxis requires careful risk-benefit assessment because excessive vasoconstriction may aggravate myocardial ischemia. However, profound hypotension itself can also cause coronary hypoperfusion and circulatory collapse. In the present case, norepinephrine was used as emergency hemodynamic support under continuous monitoring, and repeat coronary angiography confirmed vessel patency without coronary spasm, perforation, slow flow, or no-reflow.

In conclusion, hypersensitivity reactions associated with P-DCB products are rare but may be underrecognized, particularly during coronary P-DCB angioplasty, where reactions may be difficult to distinguish from procedural complications. Unlike peripheral vascular reactions, which may present with local manifestations around the target region, coronary P-DCB-related reactions may present as unexplained hypotension, arrhythmia, syncope, or even life-threatening anaphylactic shock, and Kounis-like presentations should also be considered. Clinicians using paclitaxel-related devices should carefully review previous unexplained intra-procedural reactions before re-exposure, closely monitor patients during the procedure, and promptly initiate standardized emergency management when P-DCB-related hypersensitivity is suspected.
