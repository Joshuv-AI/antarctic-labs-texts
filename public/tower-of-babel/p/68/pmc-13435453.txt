Comparative effectiveness of exercise interventions on postural balance and activity ability in stroke patients: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Yan Shi, Yiming Lu, Lihong Zhao, Cong Yu, Guo Zeng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis systematic review and network meta-analysis aims to synthesize and compare the evidence on the effects of different exercise interventions on postural balance and activity ability in stroke survivors, and to determine their relative effectiveness through probability ranking, to provide to offer preliminary evidence for clinical practice in post-stroke rehabilitation.MethodsA systematic literature search was performed across the following electronic databases from inception until January 2026, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, Chinese Scientific Journal Database, Cochrane Library, Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and MEDLINE. Version 2 of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB-2) was used to assess the quality of included studies. The study protocol was registered a priori with PROSPERO CRD420251031404.ResultsThis study included a total of 24 randomized controlled trials, which compared the effects of six types of exercise interventions on the postural balance and activity ability of stroke patients: robot-assisted training, treadmill training, core stability training, resistance training, aquatic therapy, and overground gait. The analysis demonstrated that exercise interventions significantly improved postural balance (SMD = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.03, 0.51) and activity ability (SMD = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.15, 1.76) of stroke patients. Probability ranking further indicated that, compared to other exercise interventions, core stability training showed a superior potential in enhancing both postural balance (SUCRA = 75.4%; SMD = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.04, 0.96) and activity ability (SUCRA = 96.0%; SMD = 2.68, 95% CI: 0.66, 4.70).ConclusionsThis network meta-analysis indicates that core stability training shows the greatest potential in improving postural balance and activity ability in stroke patients. In clinical rehabilitation practice, it may be considered as a preferred option; however, given the limitations of evidence certainty, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and further validated by high-quality studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05022-0.Keywords: Stroke, Exercise intervention, Postural balance, Activity ability, Network meta-analysis

Introduction

According to the Global Burden of Disease 2021 estimates, stroke affects approximately 93.8 million people globally, with 11.9 million new cases reported that year. As the third leading cause of death worldwide, stroke was responsible for 7.3 million fatalities, accounting for 10.7% of all global deaths [1, 2]. Functional sequelae, especially motor dysfunction, are primary determinants of long-term disability and reduced quality of life for stroke survivors [3]. The underlying pathophysiology involves complex disruptions in motor control and coordination, which compromise postural stability and reduce physical capacity [4, 5]. Among these impairments, deficits in postural balance and functional activity ability are particularly common and disabling. More than 80% of stroke survivors experience clinically significant balance dysfunction during the subacute phase, which directly limits their ability to perform daily activities [6]. These functional limitations are often sustained; approximately 40% of survivors continue to struggle with basic activities of daily living even six months after the event [7]. This substantial and enduring burden underscores the critical need for effective rehabilitation strategies focused on restoring postural balance and activity ability [8, 9].

Exercise intervention is the cornerstone of post-stroke rehabilitation, and a wealth of evidence supports its crucial role in promoting neural plasticity, enhancing muscle strength, improving cardiovascular health, and boosting overall functional capacity [10, 11]. An existing meta-analysis explored the impact of core stability training on trunk function, balance ability, and gait performance in stroke patients [12]. Another study [13] emphasized the impact of gait training on balance and motor function in patients with chronic stroke. However, such pairwise comparisons make it difficult to determine the relative efficacy of different interventions within a broader range of exercise therapies. Moreover, although multiple studies have investigated the effects of exercise on related outcomes such as gait speed [14, 15], the specific outcome of activity ability has not been sufficiently explored in previous meta-analyses. Therefore, it remains unclear which specific exercise intervention is the most effective in improving postural balance and activity ability in stroke survivors.

Network meta-analysis complements the limitations of traditional pairwise meta-analysis and extends its methodological framework. By combining direct and indirect evidence from individual studies while preserving within-trial randomization [16], it enables simultaneous comparison of multiple interventions and provides a hierarchical ranking of their relative effectiveness [17]. These methodological advantages are particularly important for clinical guideline development, as guidelines increasingly rely on comparative effectiveness evidence to inform exercise prescription recommendations [18]. Therefore, this study aims to conduct a systematic review and network meta-analysis to compare and rank the effectiveness of various exercise interventions for improving postural balance and activity ability in stroke survivors. The results will synthesize the available evidence to help healthcare professionals select optimal, personalized exercise rehabilitation programs, ultimately enhancing the precision and efficacy of post-stroke care.

Methods

This systematic review and network meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [19] and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. The study protocol was registered a priori with PROSPERO (CRD420251031404).

Database and search strategy

A comprehensive systematic literature search was performed in both Chinese and English databases from their inception until January 2026. The databases searched included China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, the Chinese Scientific Journal Database, the Cochrane Library, Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and MEDLINE (via Ovid). The search strategy employed a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords. To identify additional relevant literature, we also searched Google Scholar and ProQuest for grey literature. The full electronic search strategy for all databases is detailed in Appendix A. To ensure comprehensiveness, related research topics were manually checked to avoid missing relevant literature during the online search process. Titles and abstracts of the studies included in the initial screening were independently assessed by two authors in back-to-back sessions, and any discrepancies were resolved through consultation with a third author. The languages of the included studies were English and Chinese.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies were identified and selected based on the PICOS framework (Participants, Interventions, Comparators, Outcomes, Study design) [20].

(1) Participants: Adult patients diagnosed with stroke, as defined by the diagnostic criteria established at the 4th National Conference on Cerebrovascular Diseases [21], with confirmation by head CT or MRI. No restrictions were applied regarding gender or race.

(2) Interventions and comparators: The intervention of interest was any structured exercise program. Control groups could receive usual care, a different form of exercise therapy.

(3) Outcomes: Studies were required to report at least one quantitative measure of postural balance and activity ability, such as the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), Six-Minute Walk Test (6MWT), or Two-Minute Walk Test (2MWT).

(4) Study design: Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) intervention and control groups received identical exercise; (2) publication type was non-original (e.g., review, case report, conference abstract, or protocol); (3) full text was unavailable or data were incomplete; or (4) the study was a duplicate publication.

Data selection and extraction

EndNote software was used to import the studies that initially met the criteria, and duplicate studies were removed. Two investigators separately screened all study titles and general content and checked whether the entire text content of the literature could be obtained. When a study was published in different forms, the most comprehensive study was selected for inclusion. If there are discrepancies in the process, they are resolved by consensus and arbitration of the review panel. The detailed study selection process is outlined in the PRISMA flow diagram [20].

Two investigators independently extracted and recorded the valid information and values in every study using a self-made structured data extraction table. Two independent reviewers extracted data from each included study. The extracted information encompassed study characteristics, including the first author, publication year, and country; participant details, such as sample sizes per group, mean age with standard deviation, and time since stroke onset; intervention specifics, covering total duration, weekly frequency, and session length for both the experimental and control procedures; and the outcome measures with their respective assessment tools. All the extracted data were independently crosschecked by a third reviewer to ensure accuracy and reliability. Where possible, the authors of the original studies were contacted for data on missing data. If two investigators had problems in the process of data extraction, a third investigator was added to solve the problem.

Risk of bias assessment

Two researchers independently applied the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2.0) [22], which evaluates five key domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Each domain was assessed to classify studies as having a low risk of bias, some concerns, or a high risk of bias. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

Assessment of evidence certainty

The certainty of the evidence was assessed using CINeMA (confidence in network meta-analysis) [23], an online tool developed by the Cochrane Comparing Multiple Interventions Methods Group. CINeMA adapts the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) method for network meta-analysis. For each comparison, we assessed potential biases, including within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. Similar to GRADE, we initially considered the evidence for each comparison and then downgraded the certainty rating on the basis of identified concerns, resulting in final confidence grades of high, moderate, low, and very low.

Statistical analysis

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

A frequentist network meta-analysis was performed using Stata software 16.0 to compare the effects of different exercise interventions on postural balance and activity ability in stroke survivors. We used the corrected standardized mean difference (SMD) or Hedges g as an effect measure, along with the corresponding 95% CI [24]. We estimated the degree of heterogeneity and inconsistency among the studies using the Cochran Q statistic test (a P-value of < 0.05 was considered significant) and the inconsistency index (I2). The Cochran Q test allowed us to assess the presence of between-study heterogeneity and between-design consistency [25]. Despite its common use to assess heterogeneity, the I2 represented the percentage of variability in the estimate caused by between-study heterogeneity [26].

On the basis of the included research data, a network meta-analysis diagram was drawn via Stata 16.0 software to compare the effects of multiple intervention measures directly and indirectly in stroke survivors. In the network plot, nodes represent different interventions, and lines connecting nodes indicate direct comparisons of these interventions in the included studies, weighted by the number of studies that directly compared the interventions. A larger node indicates a larger sample size exposed to the intervention, whereas a thicker line represents a greater number of pairwise comparisons [27]. Furthermore, the complete evidence network was used to generate indirect estimates. We used the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) to rank the effectiveness of each non-pharmacological intervention. A larger area under the curve indicates a greater likelihood that the therapy is the best intervention, with values ranging from 0% to 100% [28].

Assessment of transitivity

In this study, the transitivity assumption was evaluated by comparing the distribution of key potential effect modifiers across different treatment comparisons. Specifically, we examined the following characteristics: session length, intervention duration, intervention frequency, stroke phase, and risk of bias. For the purpose of this analysis, stroke phase was categorized based on the time since stroke reported in each primary study as follows: acute (< 1 month), subacute (1–6 months), and chronic (> 6 months). This classification is consistent with commonly used frameworks in stroke rehabilitation research [29, 30].

Assessment of inconsistency

Inconsistency between direct and indirect effects was evaluated by global inconsistency and node splitting. The node-splitting method evaluates each node in a network meta-analysis by statistically comparing the disagreement between direct and indirect evidence. A P value < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistically significant inconsistency [31].

Assessment of publication bias and sensitivity analysis

If the number of studies included in the network meta-analysis was 10 or more, Egger’s test was employed to assess the presence of publication bias [32]. The risk of publication bias was evaluated using two methods: visual assessment of funnel plot symmetry generated by Stata software and Egger’s test [33]. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially excluding one study at a time to assess the dependability of the results.

Results

Identification of relevant studies

After searching the database and removing duplicate articles, 5,622 potentially eligible articles were identified. The full texts of 308 studies that might meet the inclusion criteria were retrieved, and ultimately, 24 randomized controlled trials involving 6 types of exercise therapy interventions were included. The reasons for the selection of included studies and the detailed exclusion process are presented in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1).

The PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection

Characteristics of included studies

The general characteristics and specific details of the included studies are summarized in Table 1 and Appendix B. This network meta-analysis included a total of 24 randomized controlled trials, involving 1015 stroke survivors and published between 2006 and 2025. The evaluated interventions comprised six exercise interventions. These included robot-assisted training, examined in 5 trials; treadmill training, in 6 trials; core stability training, in 5 trials; aquatic therapy, in 5 trials; resistance training, in 4 trials; and overground gait, in 3 trials. Geographically, the studies were conducted across eight countries, with the largest contributions coming from Korea with 7 studies and China with 5 studies, followed by Sweden and Turkey with 3 studies each, Spain and Germany with 2 studies each, and finally Greece and Brazil with 1 study each.

Methodological quality of the studies

For each study, RoB 2 was used to evaluate the risk of bias, and the results are presented in Appendix C. Specifically, although all included studies reported randomization, the nature of the intervention made it impossible to blind participants and personnel, leading to an overall high risk of bias. In total, 3 studies were assessed as “low risk of bias”, 15 studies were classified as “some concerns”, and 6 studies were evaluated as “high risk of bias”.

Transitivity assumption

In this study, the transitivity assumption was evaluated by comparing the distribution of key potential effect modifiers—including session length, total intervention duration, intervention frequency, stroke phase, and risk of bias—across different treatment comparisons. As shown in Appendix D, the distributions of the above characteristics were generally balanced across comparisons, with no systematic asymmetry. Overall, these results support the plausibility of the transitivity assumption for most comparisons. However, findings from comparison groups with a limited number of studies should be interpreted with caution.

Analyses of outcome

Postural balance

A total of 17 randomized controlled trials assessed the effect of exercise interventions on postural balance. Because the included trials used different types of control groups (usual care vs. other active interventions), we performed subgroup analyses to address this heterogeneity. For the 14 trials in which the control group received usual care, a meta-analysis showed a significant improvement in postural balance (SMD = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.03–0.51, I² = 50.8%; Fig. 2). For the remaining 3 trials in which the control group was another active intervention, we did not pool effect sizes because of the heterogeneity of the comparators; the direct pairwise results are presented in Appendix E.1.

Network graph and forest plots of network meta-analysis results for postural balance. Note RAT: robotic-assisted training; UC: usual care; TT: treadmill training; CST: core stability training; RT: resistance training; AT: aquatic therapy; OG: overground gait

The SUCRA was used to probabilistically rank the effectiveness of the interventions (Appendix G.1 and G.2). Detailed pairwise comparisons are provided in the league table (Appendix F.1). Based on the SUCRA values and the league table, core stability training (SUCRA = 75.4%; SMD = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.04, 0.96) showed a relatively high probability of being beneficial for improving postural balance, whereas aquatic therapy (SUCRA = 34.7%; SMD = 0.11, 95% CI: -0.35, 0.57) showed a lower probability.

Activity ability

A total of 14 randomized controlled trials assessed the effect of exercise interventions on activity ability. Because the included trials used different types of control groups (usual care vs. other active interventions), we performed subgroup analysis to address this heterogeneity. In the 11 trials where the control group received usual care, the meta-analysis showed a significant improvement in activity ability (SMD = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.15–1.76, I² = 93.3%; Fig. 3). The remaining three trials had other active interventions as controls; due to heterogeneity in the comparator interventions, no effect sizes were pooled, and the individual direct comparisons are presented in Appendix E.2. Sensitivity analysis by sequentially excluding each study identified the study by Qin et al. [57] as the main source of heterogeneity. After excluding this outlier, the pooled effect estimate became more robust and precise, with substantially reduced heterogeneity (SMD = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.02–0.43, p = 0.031). The level of between-study heterogeneity was within an acceptable range (I² = 0.0%, Cochran’s Q = 4.54, p = 0.873). The forest plot is presented in Appendix H.

Network graph and forest plots of network meta-analysis results for activity ability. Note: RAT: robotic-assisted training; UC: usual care; TT: treadmill training; CST: core stability training; RT: resistance training; AT: aquatic therapy; OG: overground gait

The SUCRA was used to probabilistically rank the interventions (Appendix G.3 and G.4). Detailed pairwise comparisons are provided in the league table (Appendix F.2). The ranking revealed that core stability training (SUCRA = 96.0%; SMD = 2.68, 95% CI: 0.66, 4.70) might be linked to greater improvement in activity ability, whereas overground gait (SUCRA = 34.2%; SMD = -0.06, 95% CI: -2.00, 1.87) ranked the lowest.

Inconsistency test

The inconsistency test indicated good agreement between direct and indirect evidence. The global inconsistency test suggested a good model fit, and the entire evidence network was well-connected (Appendix I). Global inconsistency was evaluated using the node-splitting method. The analysis revealed no statistically significant disagreement between direct and indirect comparisons for any treatment node (all P > 0.05), supporting the assumption of global consistency (Appendix J).

Assessment of publication bias and sensitivity analysis

In this study, Egger’s test for publication bias in the postural balance and activity ability of stroke patients did not show significant results. The results are as follows: postural balance (p = 0.729) and activity ability (p = 0.562) (Appendix K). The statistical test of funnel plot asymmetry showed that there was little possibility of publication bias (Appendix L). Sensitivity analysis indicated that the results were stable (Appendix M).

Certainty assessment

The certainty of evidence for both postural balance and activity ability outcomes was assessed using the CINeMA framework. Regarding postural balance, evidence from direct comparisons was predominantly of moderate or low certainty, with only resistance training versus usual care rated as high certainty. Limitations primarily involved imprecision, often reflected in wide confidence intervals, and in some instances, heterogeneity. For activity ability, no high-certainty evidence was identified, with all ratings being low or moderate. The downgrading was chiefly attributed to imprecision from small sample sizes and wide confidence intervals, alongside notable heterogeneity or within-study bias risks in several comparisons. Importantly, for both outcomes, the certainty for comparisons informed solely by indirect evidence from the network model was low, highlighting the inherent uncertainty in such comparisons and indicating that the relative intervention rankings should be interpreted with caution. No significant concerns regarding publication bias or incoherence were detected. Detailed ratings are provided in Appendix N.

Discussion

This systematic review and network meta-analysis aimed to compare the effectiveness of various exercise interventions on postural balance and activity ability in stroke survivors. The primary analysis demonstrated a significant overall benefit of exercise compared to control conditions for both outcomes. More importantly, through probability ranking, core stability training consistently emerged as the most promising intervention for improving both postural balance and activity ability. However, caution is needed when interpreting these findings, particularly for activity ability, where substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed in the primary analysis. Sensitivity analysis suggested that the study by Qin et al. [57] may be the main source of this heterogeneity. Possible reasons include: first, the completeness of methodological reporting was limited, as the study did not adequately describe key details such as random sequence generation and blinding of outcome assessors, which may affect the accurate judgment of its risk of bias; second, the intervention intensity and frequency in this study were relatively high, and previous meta-analyses have suggested that higher doses of walking training may be associated with greater improvements in walking ability [58], which may be a potential factor contributing to the larger effect size of this study.

For stroke survivors, neurological damage often leads not only to significant limb dysfunction but also directly impairs the coordination and control of trunk muscles, thereby compromising core stability—the fundamental ability to maintain postural alignment and balance [59, 60]. This network meta-analysis found that, among various exercise interventions including robot-assisted gait training and treadmill training, core stability training demonstrated considerable potential in improving postural balance and activity ability in stroke patients. This finding can be explained through the mechanism of action of core stability training. It is a targeted strategy aimed at improving trunk performance through neuromuscular re-education [61]. Essentially, it enhances control over the position and movement of the trunk relative to the pelvis and lower limbs, optimizing force production and transfer to the distal segments within the kinetic chain during functional activities, thereby establishing a stable “proximal base” [62, 63]. This principle of “proximal stability for distal mobility” forms the theoretical foundation for its effectiveness in improving postural control and activity capacity [59]. Substantial evidence supports these benefits: core strengthening has been shown to effectively improve balance [64–66], functional mobility [67], gait performance, and reduce fear of falling [68] in stroke patients. Despite these positive findings, caution is warranted when translating them into clinical practice.

For both postural balance and activity ability outcomes, most indirect comparisons were rated as having low confidence due to imprecision (wide confidence intervals) and within-study bias (Appendix N). Direct comparisons based on single studies were similarly limited in confidence. This suggests that the ranking of interventions and the estimates of comparative effects derived from indirect evidence should be interpreted with caution. In clinical practice, core stability training may be tailored to stroke stage. According to an umbrella review, exercise-based interventions are effective across all recovery phases, with stage-specific considerations: acute phase benefits from core stability combined with bed rest, while subacute phase may focus on strength training [69]. A network meta-analysis further reported that, for improving walking ability, core stability combined with resistance exercise is most effective in the subacute phase, whereas core stability alone is more advantageous for activities of daily living in the chronic phase [70]. These findings suggest that clinicians should consider recovery phase when prescribing core stability training. Future studies are needed to clarify optimal timing, dosage, and combination strategies across the full spectrum of stroke recovery.

Limitations

This network meta-analysis compared the effects of various existing exercise interventions on postural balance and activity ability in stroke patients. However, this study has some limitations: Firstly, the included studies showed moderate to high heterogeneity, and despite conducting sensitivity analyses, these heterogeneities were not effectively resolved. Secondly, this study included a total of six exercise intervention measures, but the sample size of each intervention was relatively small. In the future, it is advisable to conduct more large-sample randomized controlled trials to further explore the specific impact of exercise intervention programs on the postural balance and activity ability of stroke patients. Third, according to the CINeMA framework, the quality of evidence for most direct and indirect comparisons was rated as “low,” indicating that the findings should be interpreted with caution. Finally, this analysis only retrieved literature from English and Chinese databases, and studies published in other languages, from other sources, and unpublished studies were not included, which may introduce publication bias.

Implications

This network meta-analysis provides a clearer comparative understanding of the effects of different exercise interventions on the postural balance and activity ability of stroke survivors. The research results offer valuable evidence for clinical decision-making by healthcare professionals, especially in the design of rehabilitation strategies. When implementing exercise-based rehabilitation, core stability training may be a priority, especially when improving postural balance and activity ability is the main goal. In clinical practice, therapists can incorporate core stability training as a fundamental intervention strategy and integrate it into personalized, task-oriented comprehensive rehabilitation programs.

Conclusions

This network meta-analysis indicates that core stability training shows the highest potential in improving the postural balance and activity ability of stroke patients. Based on this evidence, in clinical rehabilitation practice, therapists might consider prioritizing core stability training as part of an individualized, task-oriented comprehensive rehabilitation plan. However, given the low certainty of the evidence, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Future large-scale, high-quality randomized controlled trials are still needed for further verification.
