Adsorptive removal of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs from wastewater samples using modified activated carbon derived from potato peels
Authors: Aluwani Sharon Nephiphidi, Rabelani Mudzielwana, Shirley Kholofelo Selahle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nonsteroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs) are widely used pharmaceuticals that play an important role in the health of both humans and animals. However, improper disposal of these drugs, along with pharmaceutical and hospital waste, has led to their presence in water sources, making them emerging pollutants that require remediation. In this study, a green magnetic activated carbon composite was prepared by incorporating Fe3O4 onto activated carbon synthesized from potato peels. This sorbent was then used to adsorb NSAIDs (aspirin and ibuprofen) from wastewater treatment plant samples. The morphology and structure of the synthesized material were characterized using various techniques. The magnetic activated carbon exhibited a well-defined surface with a Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area of 57.96 m²/g and a pore volume of 0.099 cm³/g. The Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) image showed that incorporating iron oxide into the activated carbon resulted in the formation of small, well-defined pores. The NSAIDs were analyzed and quantified using High-Performance Liquid Chromatography with a Photodiode Array Detector. The adsorption studies indicated that the data fitted well with the Langmuir isotherm model and followed the pseudo-first-order kinetics. The adsorption mechanism involved electrostatic interactions, π–π interactions, and pore filling. cycle. The magnetic activated carbon derived from potato peelings demonstrated strong reusability and regeneration, and its adsorption capacity declined after the sixth cycle. MAC-KOH30%-PPW shows promise as an affordable sorbent for removing aspirin and ibuprofen from wastewater samples.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01800-6.

Introduction

The use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as aspirin (Asp) and ibuprofen (Ibu) is very common in modern-day human health care [1]. Asp and Ibu are listed on the World Health Organization’s list of essential medicines and can be easily purchased over the counter [2]. It is estimated that over 35,000 tons of NSAIDs are consumed worldwide each year, and consequently, they have been recognized as the emerging water contaminants posing risks to aquatic ecosystems [3]. Reported concentrations of Asp in wastewater and the Msunduzi River in South Africa range from 13.7 to 25.3 mg/L [4]. In Kazakhstan, levels as high as 60.0 mg/L have been detected in wastewater [5]. For Ibu, the maximum concentration reported in the Nairobi River basin in Kenya is 30.0 mg/L [6], while 0.372 mg/L has been found in the River Ganges in India [7]. The minimum concentrations of Ibu in Xiamen wastewater in China were 34.8–406 ng/L, and in Bangkok wastewater in Thailand, 22–149 ng/L [8]. Asp was detected in WWTP influent samples in Denmark, with a minimum concentration of 1,600 ng/L [9]. There are regulations for NSAIDs, and there are levels that are higher than recommended. The EFSA (2024) found that 0.19% of NSAID samples didn’t meet the standards. Residue limits were also exceeded in beef (0.30%), horsemeat (1.15%), pork (0.05%), mutton and kidney meat (0.17%), and milk (0.33%). Notably, these residues originate from animal sources [10].

Due to the widespread presence of certain compounds in water bodies, efforts are underway to develop efficient and sustainable methods for removing these contaminants from water sources. The aquatic environment contamination by NSAIDs creates major health problems, which may result in endocrine-disrupting activity linked to reproductive and developmental toxicity, the development of drug resistance, and decreased therapeutic effectiveness [11]. Additionally, the lack of resources, the energy intensity, and the high costs associated with advanced water treatment methods, such as reverse Osmosis and advanced oxidation, make it even more difficult to address these issues [12]. Activated carbon (AC) is a highly porous, carbonaceous material that has been specially processed to exhibit a large surface area and numerous active sites capable of adsorbing organic pollutants [13]. Researchers have focused on the adsorption capabilities of activated carbon, particularly granulated and powdered activated carbon (PAC), for NSAIDs [14]. Several studies have explored the synthesis of activated carbon from agricultural wastes due to their low cost, ease of design, and use, which include rice husk [15], fruit peels [16], strawberry seeds, and pistachio shells [17]. Additionally, chemicals such as potassium hydroxide (KOH) have been shown to be effective for activating activated carbon when used to adsorb NSAIDs. Research indicates that chemical activation with KOH can effectively remove NSAIDs, such as ibuprofen and naproxen, from solutions [18].

Potatoes rank as the fourth most vital agricultural crop worldwide, following wheat, rice, and maize. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) estimated that global potato production reached 359 million tons in 2020 [19]. which means that a very large amount of potato peels is regularly thrown away, contributing to environmental pollution. Potato peels are rich in functional groups, such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, and phenolic groups, which enable interactions with organic pollutants via hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions [20]. Potato peels have a high surface area and porosity, especially when processed into activated carbon, thereby enhancing their adsorption capacity [21]. These properties make them suitable as adsorbents for water treatment, particularly for removing organic pollutants. Sami D and coworkers [22] demonstrated that AC from potato peels (ACPP) had a maximum cyanide adsorption efficiency of 98.2%, and Njewa et al. [23] further supported this, showing ACPP’s high surface area and well-developed pores.

A sustainable adsorbent is produced by recycling agricultural waste to improve the adsorption of pharmaceutical complexes from water [24]. Several recent studies have reported the synthesis of magnetic activated carbons from agricultural wastes, highlighting their potential for contaminant removal and practical recovery. For example, Njewa et al. [23] synthesized magnetic activated carbon (MAC) from walnut green husk, which exhibited high adsorption capacity and reusability for norfloxacin removal from water. Similarly, Spessato et al. [25] Synthesized Bamboo-based magnetic activated carbon, which has been developed as a recyclable adsorbent for antibiotic contaminants, achieving high uptake and facile magnetic separation. This study aimed to recycle potato peel waste (PPs) and convert them into a green adsorbent. The obtained PPs were used to synthesize a MAC adsorbent by incorporating Fe3O4 into the activated carbon derived from potato peels. According to the literature, no study has reported the use of magnetic activated carbon derived from potato peels for the adsorption of NSAIDs from wastewater. This modification will enable efficient separation of the adsorbent from water using an external magnet. Additionally, it includes functional groups such as Fe-O and Fe-OH, which can interact with contaminants through electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding. As a result, the adsorption isotherm and kinetic behavior shift, leading to different best-fit isotherms and changes in pseudo-first-order/pseudo-second-order fits, as well as improved reusability with high adsorption efficiency for removing target analytes from the aqueous solution. Recent studies, such as those by Yang et al. [26] and Yan et al. [27], have shown that the synergistic integration of magnetic nanoparticles into porous carbon matrices enhances both adsorption efficiency and operational practicality. The method used for removal was the adsorptive removal batch experiment, a common technique in adsorption studies due to its simplicity, reliability, affordability, and effectiveness in removing emerging contaminants from water samples [28]. This approach enables precise control over parameters such as pH, adsorbent amount, initial concentration, contact time, and temperature, allowing independent determination of each parameter’s effect. Many studies also highlight that the removal batch experiment using a magnetic adsorbent is easy, as quick magnetic separation eliminates the need for filtration or centrifugation and reduces material loss [29, 30]. In this work, we hypothesized that the magnetic activated carbon prepared from potato peels coated with Fe3O4 will provide an eco-friendly, sustainable water-treatment option. The addition of Fe3O4 will enhance the material’s applicability for NSAIDs. It will also enable effective recovery and reuse by making it simple to separate them from water using a magnetic field.

Experimental

Chemicals and reagents

Potassium hydroxide (KOH 98%), ferrous sulphate (FeSO4·7H2O,98%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH > 98%), Ferric chloride (FeCl3.6H2O, 28%), and hydrochloric acid (HCl, 95%) were all purchased from Merck. HPLC-grade solvents, Acetonitrile (ACN) and methanol (MeOH), were also purchased from Merck. Acetylsalicylic acid (Aspirin (Asp), Sigma Aldrich, > 99%) and ibuprofen (Ibu), Sigma–Aldrich, > 98%) analytical standards were also purchased from Merck. A model solution containing 1000 mg/L of both Aspirin and ibuprofen was prepared using ACN. This solution was stored in the refrigerator at 4 °C. Used solutions were diluted with ultra-pure water from the Direct-Q 3UV-R purification system. Prior to analysis, samples were filtered using 0.22 μm polytetrafluoroethylene filters purchased from Separations Scientific SA (Pty) Ltd. Potato peels were collected from the University of Venda Fish and Chips in Thohoyandou, Limpopo, South Africa.

Instrumentation

The bonding structures and key functional groups of the synthesized magnetic activated carbon adsorbent were investigated using a PerkinElmer Spectrum 100 FTIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer, USA). The functional groups of the adsorbents were analyzed by Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy over the range 4000–400 cm⁻¹. The adsorbents’ elemental composition, nanostructure, and morphology were determined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) with a TESCAN VEGA 3XMU (LMH) instrument, Czech Republic). An X’Pert XRD (PANalytical BV, Netherlands) equipped with a Cu Ka (k = 0.15406 nm) radiation was used to study the crystallinity of the synthesized magnetic activated carbon. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms (Autosorb 6200 EPDN XR-AG-AG, Micrometrics Instrument Corporation, Norcross, USA) were utilized to analyze how pore diameters and the specific surface area of the adsorbent at a temperature of 77 K. Prior to the analysis, the samples were degassed for 5 h at 300 °C. A sonication bath (Eins-Sci Ultrasonic Cleaner, South Africa) operating at the standard 25 °C setting was used to disperse the solutions containing analytical components. The sample separation process was performed using a Fisher Scientific benchtop centrifuge to remove any remaining fine particles from the sample solution, ensuring it was clean and safe for HPLC analysis.

Chromatography conditions

A Shimadzu HPLC system (Prominence-i LC-2030 C 3D Plus) with a photodiode-array detector was used to analyze the targeted compounds, Asp and Ibu. The chromatographic data were collected at 260 nm for both analytes. The analysis was conducted using a Shimadzu Shim-pack GIST C18 column (150 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) maintained at 30 °C in the oven. A gradient elution method was employed, mixing water (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile (mobile phase B) in the following programmed ratios: 80:20 (A: B) at 0 min, transitioning to 50:50 at 3 min, 30:70 at 6 min, and finally 60:40 at 9 min. The HPLC system operated with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min.

Sample collection

Water samples were collected from the effluent at the Muledane Wastewater Treatment Plant in Thohoyandou and the Tzaneen Wastewater Treatment Plant in Tzaneen, both located in Limpopo, South Africa. The municipality granted permission to access the wastewater treatment plants. These samples were collected in clean amber glass bottles and stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C for up to 1 week before analysis. Samples were filtered with 0.22 μm polytetrafluoroethylene filters prior to use. During the sampling process, the ISO International Standards for Water Sampling (ISO 5667 Series) were also considered, namely ISO 5667-1, ISO5667‑3, and ISO 5667‑14. 10.3403/BSENISO5667.

Preparation of activated carbon and magnetic activated carbon

Preparation of activated carbon from potato peel waste

The synthesis procedure followed the methods of Spessato et al. [25] and Blachnio et al. [31], with modifications. Potato peels were diced into small pieces, washed with deionized water, and dried overnight at 90 °C, then ground into a fine powder. To prepare the activated carbon (AC), 75 g of powdered potato peels were carbonized in a muffle furnace at 600 °C for 2 h. Thereafter, the obtained carbon was divided equally and soaked in 10%, 30%, and 50% KOH solutions separately, and the mixtures were left for 24 h. The mixture was then filtered and dried at 100 °C for 1 h. Next, the chemically activated powders were pyrolyzed in a muffle furnace at 600 °C for 2 h to develop pores and increase surface area. They were then washed with 0.1 M HCl and deionized water to remove impurities and adjust their pH to 7.0. Finally, the three activated carbons were dried in an oven at 100 °C for 2 h. The prepared activated carbon samples were labelled AC-KOH10%-PPW, AC-KOH30%-PPW, and AC-KOH50%-PPW. The synthesis procedure is summarized in Fig.S1 below.

Combination of Fe3O4 with activated carbon from potato peel waste

The combination of activated carbon with iron oxide was prepared following the method reported by Lestari et al. [32], with modifications. The magnetic-activated carbon was prepared by dissolving 4 g of FeSO4.7H2O and 8 g of FeCl3.6H2O in deionized water (200 mL). The solution was heated to 90 °C on a hot plate. Thereafter, 4 g of the above-prepared 10%, 30%, and 50% activated carbon were each added to separate portions of the same solution, resulting in three individual mixtures. Subsequently, 100 mL of 5 M sodium hydroxide was added gradually to each solution, and the solutions were stirred for 10 min. The concoction was then placed in a water bath at 80 °C for 90 min. The formed MAC was collected using magnetic separation, washed, and dried at 80 °C for 24 h. The adsorbents prepared were labelled MAC-KOH10%-PPW, MAC-KOH30%-PPW, and MAC-KOH50%-PPW.

Optimization strategy

Univariate optimization

The Univariate approach was used to identify the best sorbent and the impact of KOH concentration. This was done to determine which sorbent achieved the highest percentage recoveries for removing NSAIDs from water. The selection of adsorbent involved testing three different KOH compositions (10%, 30%, and 50%) for chemical activation to determine which worked best. These experiments were conducted under the following parameters: sample volume, 5 mL; mass of adsorbent, 20 g/L; interaction time (IT), 30 min; and sample pH, 6.

Multivariate optimization

A multivariate method was used to optimize the adsorptive removal batch process. During screening with a Fractional factorial design (FFD), four significant factors were identified. The method was then further refined using a central composite design (CCD) to determine the optimal conditions for removal. The study focused on parameters such as pH, mass of adsorbent, interaction time, and initial concentration (initial conc), with their respective numerical ranges shown in Table S1. The optimization procedure followed the method of Selahle [33] with modifications.

Adsorptive removal batch experiment

The adsorptive removal of Asp and Ibu was carried out in 50 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. The effects of initial concentration and interaction time were investigated through adsorptive removal batch experiments, following the method reported by Zhe Jiang et al. [29]. and using Central Composite Design (CCD) to optimize the variables influencing the adsorption process. In a typical experiment, 20 mg of MAC-KOH 30%-PPW was added to 50 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 10 mL of the target analytes. The pH of the samples was maintained at 6.5 (adjusted with small amounts of 0.01 mol/L HCl or NaOH). For the effect of initial concentration, the solutions ranged from 5 mg/L to 60 mg/L; the flasks containing the samples were sonicated for 25 min to achieve adsorption equilibrium. For the effect of interaction time, the initial concentration was set at 10 mg/L, and the samples were sonicated for 5–60 min. After sonication, the adsorbents were then recovered by magnetic decantation. Quantification of the initial concentration and the supernatant was performed using HPLC-PDA at 260 nm for both Asp and Ibu.

Adsorption kinetics

Adsorption kinetics are determined to identify the rate-limiting steps and adsorption mechanisms for aspirin and ibuprofen using the MAC-KOH30%-PPW adsorbent. The pseudo-first-order (PFO) and pseudo-second-order (PSO) reaction kinetic models, as well as the Weber-Morris intraparticle diffusion model, were employed to study adsorption kinetics. The data for adsorption kinetics studies were obtained using a Mass of adsorbent of 60 mg, an initial concentration of 10 mg/L, a sample pH of 6.5, and an interaction time of 10–35 min. The experiments were conducted in triplicates. Table S3 presents the equations used to estimate the kinetic model parameters.

Statistical analysis of adsorption studies

The statistical analysis of the model was performed to validate the suitability of the kinetics, and isotherm models were derived from the experimental data through the mean square error (RMSE), residual sum of squares (RSS), chi-squared (χ2), coefficient of determination (R2, and the adjusted correlation coefficient (adjusted R2).

Reusability and regeneration

Multiple adsorption-desorption experiments were performed to evaluate the reusability and stability of the synthesized adsorbent. This approach followed the method by Selahle et al. [24], with minor adjustments. In brief, 5 mL of a model solution containing 10 mg/L of Asp and Ibu was prepared in a sample bottle, to which 20 mg of the MAC-KOH30%-PPW adsorbent was added. The mixture was then sonicated for 25 min. The supernatant was analyzed by HPLC-PDA. The desorbed adsorbent was dried and reused for subsequent adsorption cycles to assess regeneration and reusability. This process was repeated multiple times until MAC-KOH30%-PPW no longer effectively adsorbed high percentages of NSAIDs.

Point of zero charge

The point of zero charge (pHpzc) of MAC-KOH30%-PPW was determined using a modified method described in studies [21]. Solutions with pH levels ranging from 2 to 12 were prepared and adjusted using diluted HCl and NaOH. Following this, 20 mg of the sorbent was added to each sample bottle, and the mixture was sonicated for 48 h. After sonication, the final pH values were recorded. The pHpzc was then determined by plotting the change in pH (ΔpH) against the initial pH (pHi).

Characterizations

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra were obtained to assess the functional groups of the various synthesized activated carbons (10%, 30%, and 50%) and their magnetization. Figure 1A is the spectrum of the potato peel powder before activation. Figure 1B is the spectrum of the potato peeling powders activated with KOH at three different concentrations. The broad OH peak confirmed that these carbons were activated. Figure 1C is the spectrum of the magnetized activated carbons. The magnetization was confirmed by the Fe-O peak at approximately 500 cm− 1 [35]. The band observed at 500–699 cm⁻¹ is present in the raw potato peel and activated carbon spectra and may be attributed to mineral/ash components naturally present in biomass and to carbon skeletal vibrations. However, after magnetization, this band becomes more intense and well-defined in the MAC spectrum (≈ 560–650 cm⁻¹), characteristic of Fe–O stretching vibrations in iron oxide. All the other peaks were similar in all the spectra, such as the one peak at 1367–1451 cm− 1, which was assigned to the aromatic ring, and another peak at 1009–1148 cm− 1 was attributed to C-O stretching of phenols. Subsequently, other peaks were observed at 856–873 cm− 1 and were assigned to the C-H of the aromatic rings, and the peak at 2922 cm− 1 was assigned to C-H stretching. A peak was also observed at 1624–1647 cm− 1 and was assigned to the C = O stretching of carboxylic acids. However, it is notable that after activation in Fig.1B, the bends became less pronounced and more intense due to the formation of the K-O-Si, which also depended on the KOH concentration [36]. Furthermore, in Fig. 1B, as the KOH concentration increased, the transmittance intensity decreased for the O-H, C-H, C = O, and C-O in such a way that the aliphatic C-H for all ACs spectra in Fig. 1B was due to dehydroxylation during carbonization [37]. The C = O in Fig. 1C for Fig. 2B disappeared in all MAC spectra due to the interaction of Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺species with the carboxyl functional group on the ACs surface during the magnetization [35]. The disappearance of the C = O band is attributed to the interaction of –C = O groups with the metal oxide surface, where they typically deprotonate to form –COO⁻ species. Metal oxides (Fe–O⁻) behave as Lewis bases and readily accept the proton. Once deprotonated, the functional group is no longer a carboxylic acid but a metal carboxylate. This is also the same reason for the peak in the range 1367–1451 cm− 1, which appears strong in the activated carbon but disappears in the MACs.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was also conducted to study the morphology of the synthesized carbons. The AC figures revealed that increasing KOH concentrations (10%, 30%, and 50%) improved the surface morphology and porosity. Figure 2A shows no pores and a smooth surface due to the low KOH concentration that was supposed to etch the carbon structure. However, Fig. 2B and C show small porosity with irregular morphology; the structure appears fractured but lacks a well-developed pore network. After the introduction of iron particles, the pores of the MAC (Fig. 2D and E) became visible and bigger with a well-developed pore network [38]. Figure 2F shows pore blockage as compared to Fig. 2E [39]. This proved that Fe3O4 was successfully incorporated into the AC. The incorporation of iron strengthens the AC, making it more stable in aqueous environments. This is crucial for preventing degradation of the adsorbent over time.

adsorption-desorption isotherm analysis of the ACs and MACs

BET analysis of the activated carbons and magnetic activated carbons was conducted to determine surface area, pore volume, and pore size using nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms. According to Table 1, the surface area of the AC-30%KOH-PPW exhibited the highest value at 34.55 m²/g, compared to the AC-10%KOH-PPW and AC-50%KOH-PPW, which had a surface area of 27.94 m²/g and 46.99 m²/g. After magnetization, the surface area increased. The BET surface area of the MAC-KOH30%-PPW adsorbent exhibited the highest surface area of 57.96 m²/g and a pore volume of 0.099 cm³/g, indicating a well-developed pore structure. In contrast, MAC-KOH10%-PPW demonstrated a moderate surface area of 46.99 m²/g and a pore volume of 0.046 cm³/g. Meanwhile, MAC-KOH50%-PPW showed the lowest surface area at 40.758 m²/g and a pore volume of 0.033 cm³/g. All three MACs demonstrated a similar average pore diameter, ranging from 2.34 to 2.35 nm, confirming that they lie at the boundary between microporous and lower mesoporous structures, indicating uniformity in pore size. The higher surface area and pore volume of MAC-KOH30%-PPW might enhance the physical sorption of the targeted NSAIDs. Additionally, Fig. 4 illustrates the adsorption and desorption characteristics of the activated carbons (A, B, and C) and Magnetic activated carbon composites (D, E, and F). For MAC-KOH10%-PPW(D), the type II isotherm indicates a microporous structure with limited porosity and a low surface area, characterized by a very narrow hysteresis loop. MAC-KOH30%-PPW(E) and MAC-KOH50%-PPW (F) exhibited a type IV isotherm, indicating the presence of a mesoporous structure, specifically H3, characterized by slit-like pores formed by plate-like particles observed within a relative pressure range of 0.2 to 1.0 (P/Po) according to the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) [40]. The only difference is that MAC-KOH50%-PPW mesoporous has a lower pore volume than MAC-KOH30%-PPW.

Point of zero charge of MAC-KOH30%PPW

Experiments to determine the point of zero charge (pHpzc) were conducted to analyze the pH at which the MAC-KOH30%-PPW surface exhibits equal positive and negative charge magnitudes. This approach provides valuable insights into the surface properties of the synthesized MAC-KOH30%-PPW. The results showing changes in pH were plotted against the pH values of the aqueous solutions, which ranged from pH 2 to pH 12, as illustrated in Figure S2. Diluted HCl and NaOH were used to adjust the pH. It was observed that the surface of MAC-KOH30%-PPW maintained a positive charge at pH values below about 6.5, transitioning to a negative charge above this pH. Consequently, the pHpzc for the MAC-KOH30%-PPW composite was determined to be 6.5 from the plotted data.

Optimization strategy

Univariate optimization (investigations of the KOH concentration)

The optimal adsorbent for removing NSAIDs from water was determined by evaluating the effectiveness of prepared MACs chemically activated with varying KOH concentrations (10%, 30%, and 50%). As shown in Fig. 5, the MAC-KOH30%-PPW exhibited a higher removal efficiency than the MAC-10%KOH-PPW and MAC-50%KOH-PPW. The higher percentage removal by MAC-KOH30%-PPW can be attributed to its higher surface area and pore volume, as depicted in Table 1. During magnetization, the pores of the MAC-KOH30%-PPW became visible and larger, with a well-developed pore network, as shown in the SEM image.

Fig. 5Percentage removal of aspirin and ibuprofen using different MACs

Percentage removal of aspirin and ibuprofen using different MACs

KOH is known for its ability to promote pore development and surface functionalization of carbonaceous starting materials, thereby enhancing the adsorption performance of contaminants onto the adsorbent surface [41]. From the SEM analysis, it was observed that as the KOH percentage increased from 10% to 30%, pore development also increased. Additionally, the BET results indicated an increase in both surface area and pore volume as the KOH concentration increased from 10% to 30%. The moderate KOH activation at 30% provided a stable platform for incorporating iron oxide, resulting in a well-developed magnetic sorbent with well-developed pores. In contrast, the lowest KOH activation at 10% was less stable for iron oxide incorporation, resulting in a magnetic adsorbent with underdeveloped pores. However, when the KOH concentration was increased to 50%, a decrease in surface area and pore volume was observed, as indicated by the SEM analysis, which showed pore blockage. This excessive activation compromised the material’s structure at 50% KOH, leading to nanoparticle aggregation and subsequent pore blockage when iron oxide was incorporated. As shown in Fig. 5, increasing the concentration from 10% to 30% significantly boosts activation, while 50% was excluded due to excessive activation.

The impact of the independent variables MA, IT, sample pH, and initial conc on NASID removal was investigated via a fractional factorial design (FFD) screening. The above-mentioned significant variables (Interaction time, Initial conc, and pH) were further optimized using the central composite design.

Response surface methodology

The major effects’ interactions and quadratic effects were examined using RSM based on CCD, with three independent experimental parameters. Fig. S3 displays 3D surface plots that illustrate how separate factors and their interactions affect the analytical response. All three factors, pH, interaction time, and initial concentration, significantly affected the adsorption of the NSAIDs. Fig. S3A and S3D show that as the pH increases, removal remains high, indicating that MAC-KOH30%-PPW is effective under alkaline conditions. The surface forms a broad elevated plateau. Conversely, in Fig. S3D, the removal efficiency is high at low initial concentrations and decreases as the concentration increases, reflecting surface saturation. Desirability function

Fig. 7Desirability function for optimization of independent parameters for the targeted NSAIDs, (A) Asp and (B) Ibu

Desirability function for optimization of independent parameters for the targeted NSAIDs, (A) Asp and (B) Ibu

The desirability function shown in Fig. 7 enables the simultaneous determination of optimal values for all the studied factors. Desirability ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 represents the least favourable results (1–2%), 0.5 is the midpoint (40–48%), and 1 corresponds to the most favourable value (80–94%). Based on the desirability profiles, the optimal conditions for removal were a pH of 6.5, an initial concentration of 10 mg/L, and an interaction time of 25 min. Under these parameters, the expected removal efficiencies for the NSAIDs were approximately 80%-94%, with a desirability score near 1.0, indicating excellent model accuracy and adsorption capacity. Experimental results closely matched the predictions, confirming the model’s validity. These optimal conditions were then used for further research.

Effect of interaction time and adsorption kinetics

The effect of interaction time on the simultaneous adsorption of Asp and Ibu in an aqueous solution by the MAC-KOH30%-PPW composite was investigated over 5–60 min under optimized conditions. As shown in Fig. S4, for both NSAIDs, the adsorption processes showed a rapid increase in percentage removal within the first 25 min, followed by a gradual slowdown. This initial quick removal is likely due to the abundance of binding sites on the adsorbent surface [42]. As adsorption continues, the number of binding sites decreases, slowing the process. The maximum percentage removal for both NSAIDs was recorded as 96 − 94% at 25 min. The equilibrium interaction time for MAC-KOH30%-PPW was 25 min.

The kinetics investigation provides essential information on reaction pathways and the time required for adsorption. To evaluate the mechanism controlling adsorption, the pseudo-first-order (PFO) and pseudo-second-order (PSO) kinetic models were considered. The adsorption data for the NSAID studies fit the PFO model better. This is evidenced by low χ2, SEE, RSS, and RMSE values as shown in Table 2. The non-linear graphs for the PFO (shown in Fig. 8A and B) revealed a good correlation coefficient (R2). In contrast, the non-linear graphs for the PSO, shown in Figures A and B, also revealed a good R². Although the linear plots indicate that the R² for the PSO model is slightly lower than that of the PFO model. These findings suggest that NSAID uptake by the MAC-KOH30%-PPW composite involves physisorption, including electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and weak van der Waals forces, as well as surface complexation between NSAIDs and functional groups on the MAC-KOH30%-PPW [42]. The adsorption of Ibu and Asp onto MAC-KOH30%-PPW is also due to pore-filling and intraparticle diffusion mechanisms, as indicated by the SEM Fig. 2E of the MAC-KOH30%-PPW that shows the presence of a porous surface structure. The NSAIDs molecules diffuse into mesopores and become retained within the carbon structure.

Effects of initial concentration and adsorption isotherms

The effect of the initial concentration of the adsorbate on the adsorption efficiency of the NSAIDs composite was determined between 5 and 60 mg/L (Fig. S5). The removal decreased from 96.3% to 43.6% for Asp and from 94.0% to 41.3% for Ibu. The decrease in removal percentage with increasing initial NSAID concentration is shown in Fig. S5 for the MAC-KOH30%-PPW. This could be due to the increasing adsorbate ion ratio to the limited number of active sites in the MAC-KOH30%-PPW. At low concentrations of NSAIDs, a relatively high number of binding sites will be available, increasing the chances of fast removal [43].

The adsorption isotherms of the Langmuir, Freundlich, Sips, and D-R models were investigated to determine the adsorption behaviour of the MAC-KOH30%-PPW adsorbent towards NSAIDs. The non-linearized forms of both the isotherm models were applied to the experimental data, as indicated in Table 4; Fig. 10. It was observed that the linear plots for both the Langmuir and the Freundlich models exhibited good R² values, as shown in Fig. 10a and b, indicating stronger linear relationships. However, the Langmuir model provided a better fit than the Freundlich model, with significantly higher R² values. Based on RMSE, RSS, SEE, and χ² values, the results support the Langmuir fit, suggesting that adsorption occurs homogeneously for the NSAIDs. The Sips isotherm data displayed a good fit, with R² values of 0.95 for both aspirin and ibuprofen. The heterogeneity factors indicate values of 1.22 for Asp and 1.35 for Ibu, suggesting that the MAC-KOH30%-PPW surface is predominantly homogeneous and that adsorption occurs primarily as a monolayer. This aligns with the Langmuir model’s findings, as the slight deviation from unity indicates a limited number of higher-energy adsorption sites available. Additionally, the D-R model was used to examine the composite’s porous structure and determine whether the adsorption process was physisorption or chemisorption. The mean free energy (E) associated with the adsorption process is defined as the energy change that occurs when 1 mol of adsorbate moves from the solution to the surface of the solid. According to the classification, if the mean free energy lies between 8 and 16 kJ/mol, it indicates chemisorption, whereas values below 8 kJ/mol suggest physisorption. Table 4 provides detailed parameters for the adsorption of NSAIDs on the MAC-KOH30%-PPW composite, showing that the calculated E value was within the 8–16 kJ/mol range. This confirms that the adsorption process primarily involves chemisorption, which is influenced by the composite’s surface characteristics and composition. Comparison study

Compared with previously reported studies shown in Table 5, the MAC-KOH30%-PPW demonstrates strong performance in removing both NSAIDs, despite its low adsorption capacity of 10–11 mg/g. This suggests that the adsorption capacity does not significantly contribute to adsorption. While the sorbents reported by Boushara et al. [44] and Liyanage et al. [45] exhibit very high adsorption capacities (up to 434.79 mg/g and 144.63 mg/g, respectively), which often require higher sorbent dosages and longer contact times than those reported in the present study. Several studies, including [43–47], also follow Langmuir isotherm behaviour similar to this study. This study also achieved high removal efficiencies (96% for aspirin and 94% for ibuprofen) at a very low dose of only 20 mg/L. This indicates a more efficient utilisation of active sites. In terms of kinetics, most studies [44–49] reported that the PSO model fits best, suggesting chemisorption, whereas the MAC-KOH30%-PPW follows PFO for both NSAIDs. This reflects a quicker and diffusion-controlled process, making it suitable for fast treatment applications. Although Sherazi et al. [7] reported good performance under Freundlich conditions and Kollarahithlu et al. [48] reported up to 97% removal of ibuprofen, their efficiency across multiple NSAIDs is not as consistent as that of this study [42, 44].

Reusability and regeneration

Fig. 12The variation of percentage recovery of Asp and Ibu with continuous regeneration and reusability cycles

The variation of percentage recovery of Asp and Ibu with continuous regeneration and reusability cycles

Regeneration and reusability evaluation revealed that MAC-KOH30%-PPW from potato peels could be reutilized for the removal of NSAIDs up to the 6th cycle. Figure 12 illustrates a decline in percentage removal, dropping from 98 − 95% in the first cycle to less than 50% in the fourth cycle. After the fifth cycle, a significant decrease in percentage removal was observed. This could either be due to inadequate regeneration or structural degradation of the MAC. The repeated extraction cycles alter and damage the MAC-KOH30%-PPW pore structure [53]., resulting in a decline in removal during subsequent adsorption cycles. Therefore, considering the number of cycles (6) of NSAIDs removal using MAC-KOH30%-PPW that can withstand, it was considered stable and exhibited excellent reusability properties.

Leaching studies and environmental assessment

Leaching studies were conducted to assess Fe levels in the solution after adsorption. This was conducted to assess the adsorbent’s stability in water. It is known that elements such as iron (Fe) can leach in water, especially under acidic conditions, and cause secondary pollution. This analysis was conducted by dispersing MAC-KOH30%-PPW in water (20 g/L) for 12 h, then measuring the remaining Fe concentration in the water using ICP-OES. Fe was abundantly found in the solution, with leaching concentrations in the range of 0.536-0.945 mg/L. This is expected because HCl was used to adjust the pH, and it has a strong affinity for a wide range of metals, including Fe. However, when the pH increased to neutral (the basic pH of normal water), the quantities of Fe leached dropped significantly (below 10 mg/L). The same drop was slightly maintained when the pH further increased to 10. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) and the European Union’s drinking water guidelines, the maximum allowable limits for Fe are ≤ 0.05 and ≤ 0.3 mg/L. Therefore, these findings indicated that the adsorbent could be reliably used in real wastewater remediation without generating secondary pollution.

Conclusion

Magnetic activated carbon was successfully synthesized by incorporating Fe3O4 onto activated carbon derived from potato peels. The magnetization process not only imparts magnetic properties but also induces structural modifications, creating and opening pores in the sorbent, thereby enhancing its adsorption capacity. EDS confirmed the presence of Fe, FTIR spectra indicated all the needed functional groups such as O–H and C = O, and Fe-O, and SEM micrographs revealed a highly porous and cracked surface structure, providing more accessible adsorption sites. Using the adsorptive removal batch experiment technique, the optimal parameters for NSAIDs removal were determined to be an initial concentration of 10 mg/L, a pH of 6.5, and an interaction time of 25 min. Adsorption kinetics indicated that the studied NSAIDs adsorb via physisorption, while isotherms confirmed monolayer adsorption. The adsorption studies indicated that the data fitted well with the Langmuir isotherm model and followed the pseudo-first-order kinetics. The adsorption mechanism involved electrostatic interactions, π–π interactions, and pore filling. The reusability and regeneration of the presented magnetic activated carbon from potato peelings indicated to be strong, as its adsorption decreased after the sixth cycle. MAC-KOH30%-PPW shows promise as an affordable sorbent for removing aspirin and ibuprofen from wastewater samples. Therefore, the MAC-KOH30%-PPW is a suitable material and has the potential to adsorb Asp and Ibu in wastewater samples.
