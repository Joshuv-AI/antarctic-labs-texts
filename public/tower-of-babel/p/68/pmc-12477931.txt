Transforming Dental Morphology Education: The Impact of Three-Dimensional Digital Simulation on Practical Skill Development
Authors: Ming-Sung Hsu, Chun-Liang Yeh, Shih-Jung Cheng, Chun-Pin Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study evaluated the effectiveness of integrating three-dimensional (3D) digital simulation technology in dental morphology practical courses for dental technician students.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was conducted with 86 second-year dental technology students assigned to experimental (n = 43) and control (n = 43) groups. The experimental group received preclass digital learning using exocad software and interactive 3D tooth models, supplemented by a clinical instructor-led 3D digital tooth modelling course. The control group followed traditional instruction with teacher demonstrations and manual practice. Learning outcomes were assessed through semester grades, a practical carving test, and a satisfaction survey.ResultsThe experimental group showed a significant increase in semester grades (pretest: 67.4 ± 6.2; post-test: 71.9 ± 7.9; P < .001), while the control group declined (pretest: 62.8 ± 2.8; post-test: 60.5 ± 7.6; P = .037). Practical test scores were significantly higher in the experimental group (60.3 ± 8.5 vs 47.0 ± 5.9; P < .001). Strong correlations existed between semester grades and practical test scores (r = 0.775).Conclusions3D digital simulation enhances comprehension, engagement, and carving proficiency, optimizing prosthodontic training. Preclass exposure to digital models improves hands-on performance, but hands-on instruction remains essential. A blended learning approach is recommended.Key words: Practical skill development, 3D digital simulation, Dental morphology education, Hybrid teaching approach, Educational technology

Introduction

Dental morphology is a foundational skill in dental technician education,1 requiring a combination of theoretical knowledge and manual dexterity. Traditional teaching methods, such as plaster carving based on two-dimensional (2D) images, often inadequately represent the complex 3D anatomy of teeth, leading to student disengagement and learning difficulties.1,2 Consequently, students frequently struggle to develop accurate spatial understanding and manual proficiency, which are essential for their future professional practice.3

In recent years, digital technology has significantly influenced medical and dental education, enhancing learning precision and engagement. The University of Iowa, for instance, has successfully integrated digital imagery into dental education programs, notably improving clinical skills and individual learning outcomes.4, 5, 6 Digital anatomy education, including 2D and 3D visualization methods, has been particularly effective in promoting learning outside traditional classroom settings, improving accessibility and interactivity.7

Despite promising outcomes associated with digital teaching methods, particularly in morphology carving courses,3 uncertainty remains regarding students’ overall professional competence acquisition. Dental technology uniquely integrates artistic and scientific components; however, research into educational effectiveness for dental technicians remains limited compared to broader medical training. This research gap highlights a critical need for targeted curriculum reform.8 This study is grounded in the constructivist learning theory,9 which emphasizes knowledge construction through immersive learning engagement.9 Digital simulation technology provides an immersive learning environment aligned with this pedagogy. The Technology Acceptance Model further indicates that perceived usefulness and ease of use drive digital tool adoption.10,11 Therefore, we hypothesize that integrating 3D digital simulation technology into dental morphology training will significantly improve students’ conceptual understanding and technical skills.11

Despite the increasing adoption of digital technologies in dental education, a gap remains in training for dental technicians. Many institutions rely on traditional methods like manual carving and physical modelling, which may not adequately prepare students for digital clinical workflows. Furthermore, technological innovation often outpaces curricular reform, exacerbated by limited educational resources. Addressing this requires investments in infrastructure and the development of integrative curricula combining traditional and digital competencies.12, 13, 14 Developing competent dental technicians requires a strong foundation in dental anatomy, manual skills, and visual precision. However, even as digital systems are introduced, students may lack a comprehensive understanding of morphology or practical proficiency, signalling a mismatch between curriculum content and skill requirements.15,16

Furthermore, recent advancements highlight the transformative potential of artificial intelligence (AI) in dental education. AI-driven platforms offer adaptive learning environments, real-time feedback, and personalized instruction, potentially revolutionizing dental morphology education. Comprehensive overviews by Samaranayake et al17 and Tuygunov et al18 highlight AI’s capabilities in enhancing diagnostic accuracy, providing automated feedback, and facilitating simulation-based training. Specifically, AI technology can objectively evaluate students’ carving accuracy and provide detailed personalized guidance, addressing inherent subjectivity and bias associated with manual assessments. The aim of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of integrating 3D digital simulation, specifically, into dental morphology courses. It aims to empirically determine if this approach significantly enhances students’ academic performance and practical carving skills, providing essential evidence to inform future educational practices and curriculum innovations in dental technician training.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study adopted a randomized controlled trial design involving 86 second-year dental technology students at a junior college. Participants were randomly assigned to either the experimental group (n = 43) or the control group (n = 43) using a block randomization procedure (block size = 4) generated through a computerized algorithm. Allocation was concealed using sealed opaque envelopes prepared by a third-party administrator to minimize selection bias. All participants provided written informed consent. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of National Cheng Kung University (No. NCKU HREC-E-113-231-2).

Intervention procedures

The study intervention spanned 3 instructional weeks. Each week included 4 hours of scheduled instruction in the dental morphology course, during which all research-related activities, including digital teaching, self-directed learning, and hands-on carving, were conducted. In Week 1, both groups attended the regular class. Additionally, the experimental group received a 90-minute digital course led by a clinical instructor after scheduled class hours. This session introduced the morphology of the permanent maxillary right first molar (PM1M) using the Exocad (exocad GmbH) design software and interactive 3D modelling. The Exocad software, a dental CAD/CAM platform widely adopted by laboratories and clinics in over 150 countries, was selected for its global prevalence and user-friendly interface, which is suitable for educational use.19 As shown in Figure 1, the instructor demonstrated various features of the 3D tooth model, including occlusal surface details and marginal ridges. Both groups continued the regular course in Week 2; the experimental group engaged in self-directed practice outside scheduled class hours using the provided 3D models on mobile or desktop devices. In Week 3, both groups participated in the same traditional hands-on plaster carving session on the PM1M. In contrast, the control group received only traditional instruction. Instructors demonstrated the carving process using 2D textbook diagrams and physical models, after which students completed the carving independently. To ensure equitable learning opportunities, the control group was subsequently provided with the same digital course after the intervention period. This poststudy instruction, conducted in Week 4, served as an ethical compensation and was not included in the outcome evaluation of this study. It was intended to address potential learning disparities and fulfil ethical considerations. The sequence of instructional and evaluation procedures is illustrated in Figure 2. The control group received the digital learning module after the practical test to ensure equity; semester grades were unaffected because the research practical test carried 0% weight in the course grade.

Assessment tools

In the field of dental technology, assessment practices remain largely reliant on evaluators’ subjective judgments. To enhance the objectivity and fairness of the evaluation process in the present study, all personally identifiable information (including student names and numbers) was removed through a deidentification procedure and replaced with anonymized codes prior to scoring. Furthermore, students were not informed of the evaluators’ identities before the assessment. This double-blind approach minimized the potential for performance bias driven by students’ attempts to align their work with perceived individual preferences of specific evaluators, thereby promoting a more impartial evaluation of technical competence. Although blinding evaluators was not feasible, all student work was anonymized before scoring to minimize potential bias.

Regarding semester performance, the primary outcome was the score on a 60-minute standardized practical carving test of the permanent maxillary right first molar (PM1M) administered in Week 3. Five calibrated assessors independently scored the anonymized carvings using a unified rubric. Secondary outcomes were (1) semester course grades (previous-semester dental morphology course grade = pretest; current-semester dental morphology course grade = post-test); the grading scheme comprised theoretical examinations (40%), practical exercises (40%), and class participation and assignments (20%), and (2) students’ satisfaction with the digital course.

To ensure the reliability of the practical assessment, an inter-rater calibration session was conducted prior to scoring. This session involved evaluating dental morphology carvings produced by individuals not participating in the main study, which served as reference materials for rater alignment. Through structured discussion, the evaluators reached a consensus on the scoring rubric, resulting in an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.85, which demonstrates a high level of inter-rater reliability. Subsequently, five calibrated faculty evaluators independently assessed the students’ carving tests using a standardized evaluation rubric (Table 1). The practical carving test was administered to all students in Week 3 and lasted 60 minutes. Students were instructed to replicate the PM1M, and performance was evaluated based on six criteria: overall size and proportion, anatomical shape, morphological accuracy, clarity of feature presentation, carving technique, and surface texture. The survey on perceptions and satisfaction with the digital course was administered in Week 4, after both groups had completed the same digital course. A 15-item questionnaire assessed students’ perceptions and satisfaction with the digital course using a five-point Likert scale. At the time of responding, all participants had used the digital resources at least once. The questionnaire assessed acceptability, perceived usefulness, and satisfaction, and was analysed only for exploratory purposes to examine potential relationships with performance outcomes. It was not included as a primary measure of the intervention’s effectiveness. The questionnaire’s content validity was established through expert consultation, and internal reliability was confirmed with a Cronbach’s α of 0.86.

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM). To examine within-group progress in academic performance over the semester, paired-sample t tests were conducted, which are appropriate for comparing repeated measures within the same group. Independent-sample t tests were used to compare the outcome differences between the experimental and control groups. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to confirm the normal distribution of the data, and Levene’s test ensured homogeneity of variance, thereby meeting the assumptions for parametric testing.

Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to identify linear relationships between students’ test performance, semester grades, and satisfaction levels. Multiple linear regression was further applied to evaluate the predictive strength of performance and satisfaction on semester grades. These analyses were chosen to align with the study’s objective of exploring not only group differences but also predictive relationships among key variables.

Results

Primary outcome: practical test performance

As shown in Table 3, the experimental group achieved a significantly higher mean score (60.3 ± 8.5) than the control group (47.0 ± 5.9) in the practical PM1M carving test (P < .001). This suggests that integrating 3D modelling prior to the hands-on course enhanced technical carving performance.

Secondary outcome: semester grades comparison

Table 2 presents the pretest and post-test semester grades for both groups. In the experimental group, mean scores significantly improved from 67.4 ± 6.2 to 71.9 ± 7.9 (P < .001). In contrast, the control group showed a decline from 62.8 ± 2.8 to 60.5 ± 7.6 (P = .037). Post-test grades between the two groups differed significantly (P < .001), indicating a positive effect of the digital course on academic performance.

Correlation analysis

Pearson correlation analysis (Table 3) revealed a strong positive relationship between semester grades and practical test scores (r = 0.775, P < .001). A moderate correlation was also observed between satisfaction with the digital course and practical test scores (r = 0.453), suggesting a potential influence of digital engagement on skill acquisition.

Regression analysis

Multiple linear regression analysis (Table 4) confirmed that semester grades were a significant predictor of practical test performance (β = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.53-0.91, P < .001). Satisfaction with the digital course also had a statistically significant but smaller effect (β = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.08-0.48, P < .05). These findings indicate that both cognitive understanding and perceived digital engagement contribute to practical outcomes.

Student perceptions and satisfaction

The satisfaction questionnaire results (Table 5) indicated that students positively perceived the digital simulation course. They found the course engaging (mean = 4.0 ± 0.8), beneficial for understanding complex dental morphology (mean = 3.9 ± 0.8), and effective in complementing traditional instructor demonstrations (mean = 4.0 ± 0.8). However, students remained neutral regarding the digital course’s capacity to entirely replace hands-on instruction (mean = 3.2 ± 0.9), underscoring the importance of integrating digital simulation within a blended learning environment rather than as a standalone educational method.

Discussion

This study, which integrates 3D digital simulation technology specifically using exocad software into dental morphology instruction, significantly improved students’ academic performance and practical carving skills. These findings support the constructivist learning theory, which emphasizes active engagement and visual-spatial exploration in knowledge construction.9 Our results corroborate existing literature,3,15,16 highlighting significant improvements in semester grades and superior practical carving performance in the experimental group compared to the control group. This study supports prior research demonstrating that 3D visualization and modelling substantially improve spatial comprehension and technical execution in dental morphology tasks. Additionally, the strong positive correlation observed between semester grades and practical test scores reinforces the critical relationship between theoretical understanding and skill acquisition.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, although student work was anonymized, complete blinding of evaluators was not feasible due to the identifiable characteristics of individual carving styles, which may have introduced assessment bias. Second, as the study was conducted at a single educational institution, the generalizability of the findings is limited. Third, the intervention period spanned only 3 instructional weeks, limiting the evaluation of long-term knowledge retention or skill transfer. Lastly, while students reported high satisfaction with the digital course, their neutral stance regarding its ability to replace traditional instruction suggests that further research is needed to optimize blended pedagogical approaches in dental technology education.

This research addresses a notable gap identified by Evans et al20 and Park14 in dental technician education, wherein traditional instructional methods may inadequately prepare students for contemporary digital workflows prevalent in modern dental laboratories. By empirically demonstrating the effectiveness of digital simulation tools integrated prior to traditional hands-on sessions, our findings provide valuable evidence to inform curriculum development and educational practices. Baseline results showed some significant differences between groups, as shown in Table 2, indicating they were not entirely equivalent at the start. To reduce this limitation, we emphasized within-subject changes by comparing each student’s pre- and post-test scores. Both assessments were aligned in criteria and scoring rubrics, ensuring consistency in evaluation. Still, these initial disparities should be considered when interpreting outcomes. Future studies could apply randomized allocation or statistical adjustments (eg, ANCOVA) to strengthen internal validity.

The methodological rigor of the study, including randomization, controlled instructional conditions, and inter-rater reliability (ICC = 0.85), contributes to the internal validity of the findings. The ICC value was 0.85, indicating good to excellent reliability, according to the criteria suggested by Koo and Li.21 However, limitations should be clearly noted. The absence of full assessor blinding due to the inherently practical nature of the assessments and recognizable carving styles introduces potential bias. Although anonymization of carvings mitigated some bias, complete elimination was not feasible. Additionally, this study was conducted within a single educational institution, thus limiting the generalizability of results. Future studies should consider multicentre trials to enhance external validity. Another limitation relates to the design of the satisfaction questionnaire. Although the instrument included some general items not directly targeting digital learning experiences, our analyses focused only on the items that specifically addressed the content, delivery, usability, and perceived educational value of the digital course. The development of the questionnaire was grounded in the Technology Acceptance Model, emphasizing perceived usefulness, ease of use, and satisfaction as key constructs. In addition, the item pool was refined through expert panel review, and internal consistency reliability was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.86). While the presence of broader items may not perfectly align with international survey conventions, the use of a theory-driven framework, expert validation, and reliability testing supports the academic rigour and interpretability of the findings. The inherent subjectivity of practical assessments is not unique to this study; similar challenges exist in national licensing examinations for dental technicians, where carving tests are typically evaluated by expert assessors based on their professional judgment. These evaluators, often instructors from dental technology institutions, may have implicit preferences for certain morphological styles aligned with their training background. As a result, scoring can be influenced by these stylistic preferences, highlighting the unavoidable subjectivity present in manual skill assessments. While we attempted to mitigate this issue through a consensus meeting and anonymized submissions, the potential for assessor bias remains and should be acknowledged as a limitation of both our study and the broader field of practical dental evaluations.

Student feedback indicated strong satisfaction with the digital course component, especially in terms of understanding tooth morphology and improving preclinical readiness. The moderate correlation between satisfaction levels and practical test performance suggests that positive engagement with digital content could indirectly facilitate skill improvement. However, students remained neutral regarding the complete replacement of traditional instructor-led feedback, underscoring the importance of a blended instructional approach.22

Building upon these findings, future research should specifically explore how AI technologies can further enhance digital simulation methodologies in dental technician education. AI-driven platforms can offer adaptive and personalized learning environments, real-time skill performance tracking, and automated, unbiased feedback mechanisms, which are increasingly utilised in healthcare education.18,23 Samaranayake et al17 and Tuygunov et al18 said that such platforms could objectively assess carving accuracy, provide precise, personalized guidance, and potentially overcome limitations inherent in subjective human assessments. Subsequent studies should evaluate these AI-enhanced digital simulation systems’ effectiveness, usability, and cost-efficiency to better understand their potential benefits and integration into dental technician training curricula.

Conclusions

Digital simulation technology significantly enhances learning outcomes in dental morphology practical courses. This study confirms that digital tools, particularly 3D modelling, improve student understanding and carving skills. While digital simulation enhances engagement, traditional instruction remains critical for hands-on training. Future research could explore digital course applications across other dental technology practices, potentially integrating VR and AR to enrich student learning experiences further.
