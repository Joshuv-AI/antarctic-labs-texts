Association Between Childbirth and Overactive Bladder Symptoms: A Nationwide Internet‐Based Study of 4800 Japanese Women
Authors: Katsuya Yamaguchi, Akihito Hashizume, Hiroki Ito, Yasushi Yumura, Shinnosuke Kuroda, Teppei Takeshima, Hisashi Hasumi, Ryosuke Jikuya, Daiki Ueno, Kazuhide Makiyama, Hiroji Uemura, Jun‐ichi Teranishi, Takashi Kawahara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionOveractive bladder (OAB) is a prevalent condition, particularly among women, and is associated with substantial economic and quality‐of‐life burdens. Although stress urinary incontinence during and after pregnancy is well documented, the association between childbirth and OAB symptoms remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between childbirth history and OAB symptoms among Japanese women using a large‐scale internet‐based survey.MethodsA total of 4800 Japanese women aged 40 years and older were recruited through an online survey platform. Participants were stratified by age and completed the Overactive Bladder Symptom Score (OABSS) questionnaire. Subjects were grouped based on childbirth history (parous vs. nulliparous), and OABSS scores were compared between the groups. Subgroup analyses were conducted for women aged 40–60 years and for parous women based on delivery mode (vaginal vs. cesarean).ResultsThe OABSS total score and scores for nocturia, urgency, and urgency incontinence were significantly higher in the parous group compared to the nulliparous group (all p < 0.001). These trends persisted in the 40–60‐year‐old subgroup. No significant differences in OABSS scores were found between women who had vaginal deliveries and those who had cesarean sections. BMI showed minimal correlation with OABSS, while symptom severity increased with age.ConclusionA history of childbirth may be associated with more severe OAB symptoms among Japanese women, independent of age. These findings highlight childbirth as a potential risk factor for OAB and underscore the need for long‐term urinary health monitoring in women.Keywords: childbirth, Japanese women, OABSS, overactive bladder, urinary symptoms

Introduction

Overactive bladder (OAB) is a common condition observed in the general population [1]. Irwin et al. reported that the global number of individuals with OAB was approximately 455 million in 2008, and it was projected to increase to 546 million by 2018 [2]. The reported prevalence of OAB varies widely, ranging from 4% to 33% [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. The prevalence of OAB in women has been reported across various regions. McKellar et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study using a US outpatient database including 122 606 women and reported an OAB prevalence of 4.41% [4].

Regarding sex differences in OAB prevalence, the findings are inconsistent. Some studies suggest a higher prevalence in women, while others report higher rates in men. Chuang et al. analyzed data from 8284 participants in China, Korea, and Taiwan and reported a significantly higher prevalence of OAB in women than in men (22.1% vs. 19.5%, p = 0.004). Conversely, Funada et al. used the Overactive Bladder Symptom ScoreScore (OABSS) to survey 3124 Japanese individuals aged 30–74 years and found that the prevalence was higher in men than in women (15.3% vs. 10.1%) [8]. Similarly, the NOBEL study by Stewart et al. in the United States involving 5204 adults reported nearly equal prevalence between sexes: 16.0% in men and 16.9% in women [9].

With regard to OAB accompanied by urinary incontinence, many studies report a higher prevalence in women. The EPIC study reported an overall OAB prevalence of 11.8%, with 12.8% in women and 10.8% in men. Among those with OAB, the proportion with urgency incontinence was 28.7% in men and nearly half in women (6.3% out of 12.8%) [1]. OAB imposes a significant economic burden. Ganz et al. estimated that in 2007, the annual per capita cost of OAB in the United States was $1925, with total national costs reaching $65.9 billion [10]. Projected costs for 2015 and 2020 were $1944 and $1966 per capita, respectively, with national expenditures estimated at $76.2 billion and $82.6 billion. Taken together, OAB not only has a substantial negative impact on women's quality of life but also represents a significant socioeconomic burden [9].

Pregnancy and childbirth are critical life events for women, and stress urinary incontinence during and after pregnancy is well recognized. The prevalence of stress urinary incontinence in women is reported to be approximately 20% [11]. A review by Sangsawang and Sangsawang of 28 studies reported a prevalence of 41% (range 18.6%–60%) for stress urinary incontinence during pregnancy [12]. Wesnes et al. found that women who experienced urinary incontinence during pregnancy were significantly more likely to have postpartum incontinence at 6 months compared to those without prenatal symptoms (adjusted relative risk 2.3, 95% CI 2.2–2.4) [13]. Stress, urgency, and mixed urinary incontinence have all been reported to be associated with childbirth [14]. However, the association between childbirth and the risk of developing OAB remains controversial, with conflicting findings across studies [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Furthermore, few studies have comprehensively evaluated how pregnancy, delivery, and childbirth influence the later development of OAB.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the association between childbirth history and the presence of OAB symptoms among Japanese women, using a large‐scale, internet‐based survey.

Materials and Methods

This study was conducted using an internet‐based survey. The survey was administered through “freeasy,” an online research service provided by iBridge Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). Using this platform, data were collected from 4800 Japanese women. Participants were stratified by age into four groups—40s, 50s, 60s, and 70 years and older—with 1200 women randomly selected from each age group (Figure 1). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yokohama City University Medical Center (Yokohama, Japan; approval number: F250300046). Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before they proceeded to the online questionnaire.

Sample selection. The study included a total of 4800 participants, with 1200 individuals each in their 40s, 50s, 60s, and 70s and older. Of these participants, 1528 were nulliparous (had no history of childbirth) and 3272 were parous (had a history of childbirth). Among the parous participants, 367 had experienced only C‐sections (cesarean sections), and 2842 had experienced vaginal delivery.

The OABSS is a validated questionnaire that evaluates OAB‐specific symptoms and is used for diagnosis, assessment of severity, and treatment response. The OABSS consists of four items: daytime frequency (Item 1), nighttime frequency (Item 2), urgency (Item 3), and urgency urinary incontinence (Item 4). The total score ranges from 0 to 15, with scores of 0–5 indicating mild symptoms, 6–11 moderate, and ≥ 12 severe. All 4800 participants were first asked about their history of childbirth and responded to the OABSS questionnaire. Participants were then categorized into two groups: those with and without childbirth experience. OABSS total scores and individual item scores were compared between the two groups. To minimize the potential confounding effect of advanced age, the same comparisons were conducted in a subgroup of women aged 40–60 years.

Lastly, among participants with childbirth experience, those who had undergone cesarean section (CS) only and those who had experienced vaginal delivery (VD) were identified, and OABSS total and individual item scores were compared between these two groups.

Statistical Analysis

Age, BMI, and comparisons of OABSS scores between women with and without childbirth experience, as well as between those who underwent CS and those who had VD, were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Smoking history and past medical history were analyzed using the chi‐squared test. The tests were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Logistic regression and multiple regression analyses of age, background, and sample size calculations were performed using EZR (Jichi Medical University, Tochigi, Japan). p values of < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was significantly lower in the nulliparous group compared to the parous group (54.78 ± 10.43 vs. 61.21 ± 11.23 years, p < 0.001). The mean BMI was also significantly lower in the nulliparous group (21.17 ± 3.94) than in the parous group (21.23 ± 3.39, p = 0.004). The coefficient of determination (R 2) for the relationship between the OABSS total score and age in the study population was 0.0203. Therefore, we concluded that the correlation between the two variables was weak.

Characteristics of the study population.

Regarding smoking history, the proportion of participants who had never smoked was significantly higher in the parous group (68.1% vs. 64.9%, p = 0.027), whereas the proportion of current smokers was significantly higher in the nulliparous group (17.1% vs. 13.7%, p = 0.018). As for comorbidities, the prevalence of hypertension (p < 0.001), diabetes mellitus (p < 0.001), cardiovascular disease (p = 0.028), cerebrovascular disease (p = 0.020), and rheumatoid arthritis (p = 0.042) was significantly higher in the parous group. There was no significant difference in the prevalence of Parkinson's disease (p = 0.070).

OABSS Total Score

The median OABSS total score in this study was 2 (mean 2.30 ± 2.28), and the most frequent score was 1 (26.29%) (Figure 2). The distribution of symptom severity according to age group showed that, among women in their 40s, 92.8% had mild symptoms, 6.7% had moderate symptoms, and 0.6% had severe symptoms. In the 50s age group, the corresponding percentages were 90.8%, 8.4%, and 0.8%, respectively. Among those in their 60s, 92.3% had mild symptoms, 7.5% had moderate symptoms, and 0.2% had severe symptoms. In women aged 70 years or older, 86.4% had mild symptoms, 13.2% had moderate symptoms, and 0.4% had severe symptoms (Figure 3). The proportion of mild symptoms was significantly lower in participants aged 70 and above compared to the younger age groups (p < 0.001). The coefficient of determination (R 2) between BMI and OABSS total score across all participants was 0.0107 (Figure 4).

Comparison Between Nulliparous and Parous Groups

The OABSS total score was significantly higher in the parous group than in the nulliparous group (2.368 ± 2.340 vs. 2.09 ± 2.20, p < 0.001). For daytime frequency (OABSS Q1), no significant difference was found between groups (0.512 ± 0.554 vs. 0.519 ± 0.597, p = 0.728). Nighttime frequency (Q2) was significantly higher in the parous group (0.837 ± 0.822 vs. 0.759 ± 0.838, p < 0.001). Similarly, urgency (Q3: 0.646 ± 1.019 vs. 0.548 ± 0.956, p < 0.001) and urge incontinence (Q4: 0.373 ± 0.801 vs. 0.263 ± 2.159, p < 0.001) scores were also significantly higher in the parous group.

Subgroup Analysis: Women in Their 40s–60s

Patient characteristics are summarized in Table S1. The mean age was significantly lower in the nulliparous group compared to the parous group (52.47 ± 8.22 vs. 54.98 ± 8.17 years, p < 0.001). A logistic regression analysis was performed to compare the OABSS total score according to age. The cutoff values were set as OABSS total score ≤ 5, which was defined mild OAB group. The results showed no clear association between age and the severity of OAB (odd ratio, 1.000; 95% CI, 0.989–1.020).

As for comorbidities, the prevalence of hypertension (p < 0.001) was only significantly higher in the parous group. The coefficient of determination (R 2) for the relationship between the OABSS total score and hypertension in the study population was 0.0109. Therefore, we concluded that the correlation between the two variables was weak.

Among women aged 40–60 years, the OABSS total score was significantly higher in the parous group (2.167 ± 2.243) than in the nulliparous group (2.015 ± 2.159, p = 0.0317). There were no significant differences in daytime frequency (Q1: p = 0.835) or nighttime frequency (Q2: p = 0.274), but the parous group had significantly higher scores for urgency (Q3: p = 0.0234) and urge incontinence (Q4: p = 0.002) (Figure 5).

Comparison Between VD and CS

Among parous women, a subgroup analysis was conducted comparing those with a history of VD group and those with a history of CS group. There were no significant differences in the OABSS total score between the VD group (2.350 ± 2.281) and the CS group (2.280 ± 2.370, p = 0.244). Similarly, there were no significant differences in the individual item scores for daytime frequency (Q1: p = 0.865), nighttime frequency (Q2: p = 0.073), urgency (Q3: p = 0.234), or urge incontinence (Q4: p = 0.707).

These findings suggest no significant differences in OABSS scores between women who underwent VD and those who had a CS.

Discussion

Significance of the Study

There have been limited studies investigating the influence of pregnancy and childbirth on postpartum OAB symptoms. Blomquist et al. reported the incidence of OAB symptoms using the Epidemiology of Prolapse and Incontinence Questionnaire (EPIQ) in a cohort of 1528 women [20]. Hendrickson et al. also utilized EPIQ to evaluate OAB onset from pregnancy to 1 year postpartum in 579 primiparous women [21]. van Brummen et al. assessed changes in OAB symptoms during pregnancy (at 12 weeks), and 3 months and 1 year postpartum in 344 women using the Urogenital Distress Inventory (UDI) [22]. Furthermore, Rortveit et al. evaluated the prevalence of stress, urge, and mixed urinary incontinence in 27 900 women, comparing parous and nulliparous women [14]. However, this study did not comprehensively assess OAB. Compared to these previous studies, the current investigation has clinical and epidemiological significance as it examines the impact of childbirth on OAB symptoms in a large population‐based sample of Japanese women.

OABSS Scores

In our study, the median OABSS was 2 (mean 2.30 ± 2.28), with the most frequent score being 1 (26.29%) (Figure 2). In comparison, Ninomiya et al. surveyed 1360 Japanese women aged 20–79 using the OABSS and reported a mean score of 1.3 ± 1.6 (PMID: 28762631). This lower score may be attributable to the younger age distribution in their study. On the other hand, Yoshioka et al. reported a median OABSS of 2.0 (mean 2.7 ± 2.2) among 994 women with a mean age of 73.2 years, which is comparable to our findings [23]. Additionally, during the development of the OABSS, Homma et al. reported a mean score of 8.36 ± 3.12 in patients with OAB (n = 83), compared to 1.82 ± 1.22 in controls (n = 34). The OABSS scores observed in our study are consistent with those reported previously and are considered representative of the general Japanese female population.

Risk Factors for OAB

Advancing age and higher BMI have been reported as major risk factors for OAB [15, 16, 17, 18, 24]. In our study, the prevalence of mild OAB was significantly lower in women aged 70 and older compared to other age groups (p < 0.001, Figure 2), suggesting a worsening of symptoms with age. However, BMI showed only a weak correlation with OABSS (R 2 = 0.0107, Figure 4), and no significant association was observed. This may be due to the generally lower BMI of Japanese women compared to Western populations. According to a 2016 report from Our World in Data, the mean BMI of Japanese women was 21.82 kg/m2. In our study, the mean BMI was 21.21 ± 3.57, supporting the hypothesis that BMI distribution differences may obscure associations with OAB.

Relationship Between Childbirth and OAB

When comparing OAB symptoms by childbirth experience, the total OABSS as well as scores for nocturia, urgency, and urgency incontinence were significantly higher in women with childbirth experience. van Brummen et al. reported that the prevalence of urinary frequency symptoms was 24.2% at 36 weeks gestation, decreasing to 9.6% 1 year postpartum (p < 0.001), whereas urgency incontinence persisted from 16.9% to 14.8% [22]. Hendrickson et al. found OAB prevalence rates of 18% at 3 months of pregnancy, 23% at 8 weeks postpartum, and 19% at 1 year postpartum, indicating peak symptoms at 8 weeks after delivery [21]. Rortveit et al. reported a significantly higher risk of urinary incontinence in parous women, particularly among those aged 20–34 years, compared to nulliparous women [14]. However, no clear association was found for urgency incontinence. Consistent with these findings, our study showed that childbirth was associated with worsening of various OAB symptoms, especially nocturia, urgency, and urgency incontinence.

Analysis Excluding Elderly Women

To eliminate the effect of aging, we reanalyzed the data excluding women aged 70 years and older. As a result, the total OABSS and scores for urgency and urgency incontinence remained significantly higher in the childbirth group (all p < 0.05), suggesting that childbirth experience may contribute to OAB symptoms independently of age.

VD vs. CS

Among parous women, we compared those who had vaginal deliveries with those who had CS. No significant differences were observed in total OABSS or in the sub scores for daytime frequency, nocturia, urgency, or urgency incontinence (Figure 6). Previous studies have reported mixed findings. Blomquist et al. found a significantly lower adjusted hazard ratio for OAB in the CS group compared to the VD group over a 15‐year follow‐up (adjusted HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.34–0.76) [20]. Handa et al. also reported a higher prevalence of OAB in the VD group [25]. However, our study did not find significant differences between the groups. While VD is known to impact the urethral sphincter and pelvic floor muscles [26], such mechanical effects may be less directly related to urgency and nocturia, which are characteristic symptoms of OAB.

The present study was associated with several limitations. First, this study was a questionnaire survey and constitutes a retrospective cross‐sectional study. As a result, there is a possibility that response bias and recall bias, including social desirability bias, have occurred. Second, while it confirmed that the history of pregnancy tends to increase urinary incontinence, it did not establish how the time since pregnancy and childbirth affects urinary symptoms, including incontinence. Third, this study did not adjust for menopausal status, hormonal therapy, or parity number. Future research should examine the impact of the time since childbirth and parity number on urinary symptoms. Fourth, the sample size was insufficient for a comparison between the VD group and CS group; the results are speculative.

Conclusion

A history of childbirth may be associated with more severe OAB symptoms among Japanese women, independent of age. However, the mode of delivery may not significantly affect symptom severity. These findings highlight childbirth as a potential risk factor for OAB and underscore the need for long‐term urinary health monitoring in women.
