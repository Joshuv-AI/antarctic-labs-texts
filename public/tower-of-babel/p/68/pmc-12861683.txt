Device Embolization After Transcatheter Patent Foramen Ovale Closure

Recent guidelines from the Society for Cardiovascular and Angiography Interventions (SCAI) for the management of patent foramen ovale (PFO)–mediated stroke recommend percutaneous closure over antiplatelet therapy in patients aged 18 to 60.1 Stroke reduction is predicated on successful PFO closure with eradication of right-to-left shunting; however, variable clinical practice may lead to device failure in some situations. Current sizing techniques are primarily based on two-dimensional (2D) echocardiography despite three-dimensional (3D) transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and 3D intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) now being widely employed for percutaneous structural interventions. Residual right-to-left shunting remains as high as 25% after closure, and it carries a 3-fold higher risk of recurrent stroke of transient ischemic attack.2 Herein, we report a case of device failure due to undersizing, and we illustrate how 3D echocardiography resulted in larger device selection and successful closure.Take-Home Messages•Percutaneous PFO closure is recommended over medical therapy for cryptogenic stroke in patients younger than 60 years.•Appropriate closure device selection is enhanced with the use of multiplanar 3D echocardiography to assess PFO tunnel width.

Case Summary

A 40-year-old woman with a recent left middle cerebral artery stroke underwent evaluation at an outside hospital for source of embolism, including cardiac rhythm monitoring and hypercoagulability laboratory testing, which were negative. She was found to have a PFO by transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), and a multidisciplinary team recommended closure for PFO-mediated stroke. Transcatheter closure was performed at the outside facility using a 25-mm Cardioform septal occluder (Gore Medical) using ICE and fluoroscopic guidance. After closure, the device was not seen on postprocedure TTE. The patient returned to the cardiac catheterization laboratory, and the device was visualized in the abdominal aorta by fluoroscopy. The device was percutaneously retrieved and removed with an arterial cut-down.

The patient subsequently presented to our institution for a second opinion regarding PFO closure. Transcatheter PFO closure was performed using intraprocedural 3D TEE to better evaluate the PFO morphology and dimensions. Using 3D TEE multiplanar imaging, the PFO tunnel length measured 5 mm (Figure 1A). The tunnel height (ie, the maximum separation between the septum primum and secundum at the right atrial [RA] opening) was 11 mm (Figure 1A), consistent with the presence of an atrial septal aneurysm. The tunnel width (ie, the major axis of the PFO measured orthogonal to the tunnel length) was 24 mm at both the RA and left atrial (LA) openings (Figures 1B to 1G, Video 1). The tunnel width measurement was also performed with biplane imaging in the bicaval view (Figures 1H to 1J, Video 2) and after a wire was passed through the PFO, facilitating measurement of this dimension, as it allows for mechanical separation of the septum primum from secundum (Figures 2A to 2C, Video 3).Figure 1Sizing of PFO Tunnel on 3D Transesophageal Echocardiography(A) Three-dimensional rendering of bicaval view depicting PFO tunnel length (horizontal dotted yellow line) and height (vertical dotted yellow line) at the RA opening. After identifying the presence of a large tunnel width, a 35-mm Amplatzer cribiform occluder (Abbott Laboratories) was chosen to ensure that both the LA and RA disc sizes exceeded the tunnel width (ie, “tunnel width oversizing”)3 to ensure device stability after deployment. A 35-mm PFO occluder with an LA disc size of 25 mm was not chosen because of concern that the device might embolize into the right heart. The RA and LA anteroposterior dimensions were also assessed to ensure the discs could be accommodated and not impinge on adjacent cardiac structures (Figure 2D). After occluder deployment under fluoroscopic (Figure 2E) and TEE guidance, the device was well seated along the interatrial septum without impingement on the posterior atrial wall or aortic sinuses (Figures 2F and 2G, Video 4). There was no evidence of residual right-to-left shunting with agitated saline injection on the postdeployment TEE images or on the 6-month postprocedural TTE (Figure 2H).

Why Beyond the Guidelines

Our case demonstrates that application of conventional manufacturer guidelines for PFO occlusion device selection may result in inappropriate device sizing at times. The decision to incorporate tunnel width to aid device sizing extends beyond current guidelines.

Outcomes and Follow-Up

The patient is doing well in outpatient follow-up. A repeat TTE with bubble study at the 6-month follow-up did not show any residual right-to-left shunting. Clinically, she has not had any atrial arrythmias or stroke recurrence.

Discussion

Preventing recurrent stroke and ensuring device stability after deployment (including avoiding device embolization and associated complications) depends on appropriate device sizing and successful PFO closure. Current guidelines from the American Society of Echocardiography and SCAI describe the use of 3D TEE and ICE for assessment of the interatrial septum and guidance of PFO closure, but they do not provide specific recommendations on the assessment of anatomic PFO dimensions or the sizing of the occlusion device.4 Similarly, the American College of Cardiology does not provide specific guidelines on a standard approach to PFO assessment before percutaneous closure. Consequently, device sizing remains highly variable in clinical practice5 and can be challenging given variable and complex PFO anatomy.6 Although routine measurement of the PFO width at both the RA and LA opening prior to transcatheter closure has been previously described,7 manufacturer sizing recommendations are based on 2D echocardiographic assessment of the tunnel length.8 We have previously proposed standardizing the terminology for anatomic PFO dimensions as assessed on 3D TEE (Table 1).3Table 1Proposed Standardized Terminology and Definitions for Anatomic PFO DimensionsTunnel lengthThe distance between the RA opening at the fossa ovalis and the crescentic LA openingTunnel widthThe major axis of the PFO at either the RA or LA side as seen during en face 3D TEE imagingTunnel heightThe dynamic separation between the septum primum and secundum (minor axis of the PFO) at the RA or LA side3D = 3-dimensional; LA = left atrial; PFO = patent foramen ovale; RA = right atrial; TEE = transesophageal echocardiography.

Our case highlights several important observations. First, multiplanar 3D TEE can support more accurate PFO sizing by incorporating tunnel width measurements into device selection. In our case, it was not surprising that a 25-mm device embolized, as the size of the device was the same size as the tunnel width at the RA opening and was unable to anchor properly around the margins of the PFO. We suspect that the initial selection of a 25-mm device was based on the short tunnel length and the failure to recognize the much larger tunnel width. Secondly, the presence of short tunnel length can belie the true size of the PFO as described by the tunnel width measured with multiplanar 3D TEE, and that a PFO with a short tunnel length may at times require a larger sized device. Although the presence of an atrial septal aneurysm should have indicated the need for a larger sized device, an atrial septal aneurysm is dynamic and may not always be appreciated at the time of closure. Lastly, selection of a closure device with disc sizes that exceed the tunnel width should be considered (ie, “tunnel width oversizing”) to ensure device stability along the interatrial septum (Figure 2I) and prevent complications, as described in this report.

Conclusions

Multiplanar 3D assessment of PFO tunnel width by TEE or ICE before percutaneous PFO occlusion should be considered to more accurately size PFO occlusion device selection, thereby enhancing the safety and efficacy of the procedure.
