Patient Perceptions of Delays in Audiology Services in Johannesburg: A Secondary Analysis of Waiting Times and Alternative Service Models
Authors: Katijah Khoza‐Shangase, Theresa‐Joy Munyembate
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDelays in accessing audiology services are a persistent challenge in South Africa's public healthcare sector, leading to late diagnosis, reduced hearing aid uptake and poor rehabilitation outcomes. Beyond individual access barriers, such delays reflect systemic misalignment between service demand, workforce capacity, procurement systems and service delivery models.ObjectiveThis study explored patient perspectives on waiting times and possible service innovations, using a secondary qualitative analysis of interview transcripts.MethodsFifteen de‐identified interviews with adult hearing aid users from Johannesburg's public sector were re‐analysed thematically to identify experiences of service delays, systemic barriers and views on alternative delivery models.ResultsParticipants reported prolonged delays—ranging from several months to over a year—for assessments, hearing aid fittings and follow‐up care. Reported systemic barriers included audiologist shortages, inefficient scheduling and delays in hearing aid procurement. Financial constraints and transport difficulties further hindered access, particularly for unemployed participants. Mobile audiology units and tele‐audiology were suggested by many participants as potential strategies to improve accessibility, indicating patient support for decentralised and digitally enabled service delivery models, although not all respondents were supportive of remote options.ConclusionsFindings highlight key planning‐relevant leverage points for improving audiology service delivery in resource‐constrained urban health systems. Larger‐scale, multi‐site studies are needed to evaluate the feasibility, cost‐effectiveness and acceptability of mobile and digital service models before broad policy adoption.Keywords: accessibility, audiology services, hearing loss, mobile health clinics, public healthcare, South Africa, tele‐audiology, waiting times

Introduction

There is a growing burden of hearing loss and the need for timely audiology services globally, but more so in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs). The World Health Organisation [1, 2] states that hearing loss is a significant global public health issue, currently affecting over 1.5 billion people worldwide, with approximately 430 million individuals requiring rehabilitation. Without timely intervention, untreated hearing loss leads to communication barriers, reduced educational and employment opportunities, social isolation and decreased quality of life [3, 4, 5, 6]. These consequences position hearing loss not only as a clinical concern, but as a health system and development challenge. In LMICs, where audiology services are often centralised in tertiary hospitals, long waiting times and limited service accessibility contribute to delays in diagnosis and rehabilitation, worsening the burden of hearing impairment [7, 8, 9]. This manuscript reports a secondary qualitative re‐analysis of interview transcripts collected in related projects on public sector hearing aid users and on awareness/referral gaps in Johannesburg [10, 11].

Timely access to audiology assessments, hearing aid fittings and rehabilitative care is critical for mitigating the negative impacts of hearing loss [2, 12, 13]. Research in high‐income countries (HICs) has demonstrated that early detection and intervention significantly improve speech and language outcomes, psychosocial well‐being and workforce participation [3, 14]. However, in resource‐constrained settings, systemic challenges—including audiologist shortages, poor service distribution, inefficient scheduling systems and limited funding—result in prolonged waiting periods and limited access to care [15, 16, 17]. These challenges reflect failures in service planning, workforce allocation and coordination across levels of care, rather than isolated access problems.

Delays in audiology services are a critical global challenge [9, 12, 14]. In many LMICs, individuals with hearing loss wait months or even years before receiving a hearing assessment or intervention [12, 15, 16, 18]. Research in South Africa, Malawi, Zambia and Nigeria has identified key factors contributing to delays in audiology services, including (1) severe shortages of audiologists, with most specialists concentrated in urban hospitals; (2) overburdened healthcare systems, where audiology is not prioritised within national health strategies; (3) inefficient referral and appointment scheduling systems, leading to backlogs and prolonged waiting times and (4) geographic barriers, where patients from rural areas must travel long distances to receive audiology care, resulting in missed or delayed appointments [7, 9, 15, 18, 19, 20]. Evidence from Malawi shows that less than 20% of individuals with hearing loss had received audiological care, with most patients citing transportation challenges, long waiting lists and a lack of awareness of available services as major barriers [21, 22, 23, 24]. Similarly, in South Africa, evidence indicates that patients often waited more than 6 months for audiology appointments in public healthcare facilities, largely due to limited personnel and outdated scheduling systems [7, 15, 16, 25]. Importantly, these problems span the entire patient pathway; extending from upstream referral and awareness gaps at primary care to downstream delays in assessment, fitting and maintenance. This upstream–downstream disconnect has been documented in the related referral/awareness study from this project, which demonstrates how primary care knowledge and referral bottlenecks delay entry into audiology services [11].

South Africa's public healthcare system serves nearly 84% of the population but is characterised by workforce shortages, centralised specialist services and systemic inefficiencies [8, 26]. Audiology services are primarily hospital‐based, meaning patients must navigate multiple referral steps before reaching an audiologist [27, 28]. Several key challenges contribute to delayed access in the South African audiology sector. First, severe workforce shortages and maldistribution of audiologists, where most audiologists work in urban centres, leaving rural areas underserved [8, 26]. The national patient‐to‐audiologist ratio is far below WHO recommendations—currently ratios in Sub‐Saharan Africa are as low as 0.57 audiologists per 10,000 people or 39 audiologists per million [8], worsening service backlogs and extended waiting periods. Second, long waiting times for appointments and hearing aid fittings, where public sector patients face appointment delays of several months due to limited audiologists and inefficient booking systems [7, 15, 16, 25]. Hearing aid procurement and fitting services are slow, leaving many patients without assistive devices for extended periods [6, 12, 29, 30, 31]. Third, geographic and financial barriers to accessing services, where rural patients must travel long distances to urban hospitals, making follow‐up appointments inconvenient and costly. Many patients miss their appointments due to transportation difficulties, lost wages or long wait times at facilities. Finally, inefficient referral systems and limited integration of audiology into primary healthcare (PHC) further delay access, as many PHC providers lack training in hearing loss detection and referral [32, 33]. Collectively, these challenges point to structural weaknesses in health service planning, coordination and decentralisation, rather than individual‐level non‐compliance.

To address long waiting times and limited service reach, several alternative models of service delivery have been implemented globally with positive outcomes. Firstly, decentralisation of audiology services is one such alternative model. Fo example, in Brazil, integrating audiology services into primary healthcare clinics significantly reduced waiting times and improved early detection rates [34, 35]. In Malawi, task‐shifting strategies, where trained nurses conducted hearing screenings and referred patients to audiologists, improved service reach and referral efficiency [22]. Secondly, mobile audiology clinics and tele‐audiology services have also been implemented globally with positive outcomes. Studies from India and South India specifically have examined needs and readiness for telepractice and tele‐audiology implementation demonstrating both feasibility and important contextual issues to consider (e.g. infrastructure, acceptability, training) [36, 37]. In other LMICs (e.g. Uganda), mobile audiology units have been used to provide community screening and hearing aid services, reducing transport barriers [9, 38]. In Australia, tele‐audiology models have allowed remote hearing assessments and hearing aid programming, reducing the need for multiple in‐person visits [39, 40, 41]. These models function not merely as technological innovations, but as service delivery instruments that redistribute care across levels of the health system. Lastly, efficient appointment and referral management systems form part of the alternative models of service delivery. In high‐income settings, digital scheduling systems and automated patient reminders have been shown to reduce no‐show rates and improve appointment efficiency [42, 43]. Adapting such mechanisms within South Africa's public sector requires consideration of governance, infrastructure and workforce implications.

Against this background, this study examines delays in accessing audiology services in South Africa's public healthcare system as a health system planning challenge, rather than solely an access issue. Specifically, this study seeks to (1) investigate patient experiences with waiting times for audiology services, including assessments and hearing aid fittings; (2) identify systemic and logistical barriers contributing to appointment delays in public healthcare; (3) explore the potential of mobile audiology clinics and decentralised service models as solutions for reducing waiting times and (4) assess the feasibility of tele‐audiology and digital appointment systems for improving service delivery. By drawing on interview data from related projects in Johannesburg that examined both patient hearing aid experiences and primary care referral awareness, this analysis aims to situate downstream service delays within the full patient pathway from first contact to ongoing maintenance. The study aims to generate analytically transferable insights for health planners and policymakers seeking to improve the organisation, coordination and equity of audiology services within resource‐constrained public health systems. Addressing delays in audiology services is not only a clinical imperative but a public health priority aligned with South Africa's National Health Insurance (NHI) goals and the global pursuit of Universal Health Coverage.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study employed a secondary qualitative data analysis approach to investigate delays in audiology service access, including waiting times, service inefficiencies and alternative models of care in South Africa's public healthcare system. Secondary data analysis enables systematic re‐examination of pre‐existing qualitative datasets, offering new insights into themes that were not the primary focus of the original study [44]. In this study, secondary analysis was used strategically to interrogate health system and service delivery issues, specifically delays and planning‐relevant service models, from a patient perspective. The dataset used here derives from related projects that examined hearing aid users' experiences and primary‐care referral/awareness gaps in Johannesburg; details of the parent studies and their primary methods are available in the project publications [10, 11]. This secondary analysis intentionally re‐framed the original transcripts to focus specifically on ‘delay’ phenomena (waiting for assessment, fitting, repairs and follow‐up) and on participant suggestions for alternative service models (e.g. mobile clinics, tele‐audiology). As a high‐demand, resource‐constrained urban setting, Johannesburg represents a ‘stress test’ environment in which systemic planning failures become especially visible. The descriptive qualitative approach and thematic analysis were chosen to ensure findings remained grounded in participants' lived experiences while enabling systematic identification of service inefficiencies and planning‐relevant opportunities for service redesign [45, 46, 47].

The original (parent) studies from which these data were derived were designed to explore (i) hearing aid users' experiences of provision, utilisation and follow‐up care within the public sector, and (ii) primary healthcare referral pathways and awareness of audiology services in Johannesburg [10, 11]. While delays were not the primary analytic focus in these studies, the interview guides explicitly included probes on timing of services, waiting periods, follow‐up experiences and perceived system challenges. These domains are directly aligned with the present study's focus on service delays. The current analysis therefore represents a theoretically coherent re‐framing of the dataset, focusing specifically on temporal access barriers (‘delays’) and patient‐identified service improvement strategies. By re‐analysing these narratives through a health systems lens, the study extends the utility of the original dataset beyond its initial scope while remaining grounded in the original data structure and intent.

Data Source and Study Population

Primary Data Source

The dataset used for this secondary analysis comprised 15 in‐depth semi‐structured interview transcripts originally collected at 2 public healthcare facilities: (1) Chris Hani Baragwanath Academic Hospital (CHBAH)—a tertiary‐level hospital serving a high‐volume audiology patient population and (2) Hillbrow Community Health Centre (CHC)—a primary healthcare facility offering audiology services at a community level. These parent study sites, recruitment procedures, interview guide and ethical approvals are described in the primary publication [10].

Participant Characteristics

The original study included adult hearing aid users (ages 18–66+) who had received government‐funded audiology services within the past 12 months. Participants were purposively sampled to ensure maximum variation across age, gender, employment status and facility level; those who had never used their issued hearing aids, those with profound hearing loss requiring sign‐language interpretation, and individuals with cognitive impairments were excluded. Interviews lasted approximately 30–45 min, were audio‐recorded with participant consent, and interviewers took contemporaneous field notes to capture non‐verbal cues and contextual observations.

Why This Dataset Can Address ‘Delays’ and ‘Alternative Models’

Although the original study's principal focus was hearing aid experiences, the interview guide explicitly included prompts about the timing of services, waiting periods, repair/maintenance experiences and recommendations for service improvement (e.g.: ‘How long did you wait between assessment and fitting?’ and ‘What changes do you think could improve hearing aid services in public healthcare?’). These probes enabled focused secondary analysis of delay mechanisms and patient‐informed service delivery alternatives relevant to health system planning and management.

It is important to note that this study focuses on patient‐reported experiences of delays rather than administrative or clinical records of waiting times. From a health systems perspective, these experiential accounts are critical, as they capture how delays are perceived, navigated, and responded to by users of the system. Such perceptions influence help‐seeking behaviour, appointment adherence, and satisfaction with care, and are therefore highly relevant to service design and planning. While this approach does not quantify delays in objective terms, it provides essential insight into the lived consequences of system inefficiencies, complementing record‐based studies.

To operationalise this re‐analysis a transparent, reproducible procedure was followed: two researchers independently reviewed all 15 transcripts to extract segments relevant to the categories above. An initial codebook was developed through independent open‐coding of three purposively selected transcripts (≈20% of dataset) and iterative discussion; once consensus on code definitions was reached, the final codebook was applied to the full dataset. Any discrepancies in coding were resolved by discussion and consensus, and unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by a third senior researcher. All coding decisions, codebook versions and analytic memos were documented to form an audit trail [47].

Thematic Analysis Approach

Thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke's six‐step framework [45]. The analytic process involved familiarisation, code generation, theme development, theme review, definition and naming of themes and structured reporting. Themes were explicitly developed to inform service planning by linking patient experiences to systemic constraints and potential delivery mechanisms. Analytic rigour was strengthened through double‐coding, cross‐case comparison by facility level and participant characteristics, and extensive use of analytic memos.

Saturation and Secondary Analysis Considerations

The primary study reported thematic redundancy by the 15th interview, providing confidence that the dataset contained sufficient depth for secondary analysis. For the current analytic focus, theme adequacy was assessed through consistency across participants and facility types rather than through additional data collection. As with all secondary qualitative analyses, this approach prioritises analytic depth and reinterpretation over the generation of new empirical data.

Ethical Considerations

Since this study utilised pre‐existing, de‐identified qualitative data, it posed minimal ethical risk. Ethical clearance for the original study was granted by the authors' institute. Participants provided written informed consent for interview participation and for secondary use of anonymised data as described in the parent study. Additional ethical measures included:

Trustworthiness and Rigour

To enhance credibility and reliability, the study adhered to Lincoln and Guba's trustworthiness criteria [48], with strategies clearly distinguished between the original data collection and this secondary analysis. Credibility was supported in the original study through purposive maximum‐variation sampling, iterative data collection with early coding checks, peer debriefing and interviewer field notes. For the re‐analysis, credibility was reinforced through independent double‐coding, consensus‐building, cross‐case comparisons and triangulation with existing literature [47]. Dependability was ensured by maintaining a clear audit trail of coding decisions and analytic memos. Confirmability was strengthened by using original field notes for context and reflexive journaling during re‐analysis to minimise researcher bias. Transferability was supported by thick description of participant experiences, while acknowledging the small, urban dataset and its limited generalisability. Limitations specific to secondary analysis included the inability to conduct member checking and reliance on analytic memos rather than firsthand interviewer reflexivity; these are addressed transparently in the discussion.

Results

Participant Characteristics

The secondary dataset analysed in this study consisted of 15 de‐identified interview transcripts from adult hearing aid users in Johannesburg's public healthcare sector. Participants had received government‐funded audiology services at either Chris Hani Baragwanath Academic Hospital (tertiary level) or Hillbrow Community Health Centre (primary level) within the past 12 months. Including participants from both tertiary and primary care settings allowed for comparison across levels of service delivery within a single urban health system.

Most participants were middle‐aged or older (66%), with a nearly equal gender distribution. Forty percent were unemployed, highlighting the financial barriers to transport and follow‐up care. Facility type was evenly split, with nine participants accessing services at CHBAH (tertiary level) and six at Hillbrow CHC (primary level). These characteristics highlight how delays were experienced across different service levels and socioeconomic positions, rather than being confined to a single subgroup.

Long Waiting Times for Audiology Services

Theme 1: Extended Delays for Initial Audiology Appointments

Most participants reported long waiting times for their first audiology appointment, with delays ranging from several months to over a year. Interview probes such as ‘How long did you wait for your first appointment?’ and ‘What did you do while waiting for your appointment?’ elicited these narratives. Key findings were: (1) waiting times for audiology assessments ranged from 3 months to over a year; (2) patients frequently visited healthcare facilities multiple times before securing an appointment and (3) some participants felt their hearing loss was not taken seriously, leading to delayed referrals and frustration. These experiences illustrate how weak referral pathways and scheduling systems can prolong entry into specialist care, even within urban settings.

Participant Quotes:

I waited almost 9 months before I got an appointment. By the time they called me, my hearing had already gotten worse.

(Participant 3)

Each time I went to the clinic, they said they were full and that I should come back next month. It took me a whole year to see the audiologist.

(Participant 7)

I had to keep pushing. If I had just waited quietly, I don't think they would have called me at all.

(Participant 11)

Theme 2: Delays in Hearing Aid Fitting after Assessment

Even after receiving a hearing assessment, most participants faced additional delays in getting their hearing aids fitted. These delays were often due to stock shortages, limited audiologists and inefficient scheduling systems. These findings were elicited through probes such as ‘What was your experience after your hearing test?’ and ‘How long did it take to receive your hearing aid?’ Key findings were: (1) some participants waited 6 months or longer after their assessment before receiving a hearing aid; (2) stock shortages of hearing aids and earmold materials contributed to prolonged waiting periods and (3) participants were often not informed of expected waiting times, leading to frustration and uncertainty. From a service delivery perspective, these accounts point to procurement and communication failures that compound clinical delays.

Participant Quotes:

After my hearing test, they told me I'd get my hearing aid soon. “Soon” turned out to be another 8 months.

(Participant 5)

They ran out of hearing aids, so I had to wait. I kept calling, but they just said I should be patient.

(Participant 10)

Nobody told me how long it would take. If I knew it would take this long, I would have tried to get help somewhere else.

(Participant 14)

Systemic and Logistical Barriers Contributing to Delays

Theme 1: Shortage of Audiologists and Overburdened Facilities

Participants described long queues at public hospitals and limited availability of audiologists, which contributed to extended waiting times. Probes such as ‘What challenges did you experience at the hospital?’ helped elicit these responses. Key findings were: (1) limited audiologist availability resulted in months‐long waiting lists; (2) high patient volumes in urban hospitals led to delayed service delivery and (3) patients often felt that audiologists were overworked and unable to spend sufficient time on individual cases. These narratives reflect structural workforce constraints rather than isolated service inefficiencies.

Participant Quotes:

There were too many people waiting. The audiologist only saw a few patients each day, so I had to wait for months.

(Participant 2)

I finally got an appointment, but the audiologist was rushing because there were so many people waiting.

(Participant 8)

They told me there were only two audiologists at the hospital. How can two people see so many patients?

(Participant 12)

Theme 2: Geographic and Financial Barriers to Appointment Attendance

Most participants struggled to attend appointments due to transportation costs, long travel distances or financial constraints. Interview questions such as ‘Did you experience challenges in getting to the hospital?’ drew out these issues. Key findings were: (1) many patients travelled long distances to access audiology services, making follow‐ups difficult; (2) some missed appointments due to transport costs, leading to further scheduling delays and (3) rural patients faced the greatest barriers, as audiology services were centralised in urban hospitals. These barriers reveal how centralised service models disproportionately affect economically vulnerable users.

Participant Quotes:

The hospital is far, and I had no money for transport. I had to borrow money to go for my appointment.

(Participant 6)

I missed my first appointment because I couldn't take time off work. When I tried to rebook, they told me I had to wait another 4 months.

(Participant 9)

If you live far away, you have no choice but to wait for months because you can't keep traveling back and forth.

(Participant 15)

Patient Perspectives on Alternative Service Models

Theme 1: Support for Mobile Audiology Clinics

Several participants believed that mobile audiology services would reduce waiting times and improve access, especially for patients in remote areas. Many suggested that mobile clinics could provide basic hearing assessments, hearing aid fittings and follow‐ups closer to their homes. These perspectives were elicited with probes such as ‘What could make audiology services easier for you to access?’ Key findings were: (1) participants supported the use of mobile audiology clinics to reduce travel and waiting times; (2) many believed rural areas would benefit the most from mobile services and (3) some suggested mobile units could also provide minor hearing aid repairs to reduce hospital visits. Participants thus framed mobile clinics as a mechanism for decentralising routine care.

Participant Quotes:

If they had mobile clinics in the community, we wouldn't have to wait so long to be seen.

(Participant 4)

People in rural areas struggle the most. A mobile clinic could help them get tested sooner.

(Participant 13)

I don't need to go to the hospital just to fix my hearing aid. If there was a mobile service, they could do that quickly.

(Participant 1)

Theme 2: Interest in Tele‐Audiology for Appointment Scheduling and Follow‐Ups

Some participants expressed interest in tele‐audiology as a potential solution for scheduling and follow‐ups, reducing the need for multiple hospital visits. Key findings were: (1) many participants supported digital scheduling systems to improve appointment efficiency; (2) some felt virtual follow‐ups could help with minor issues and counselling and (3) others remained sceptical, believing in‐person consultations were necessary for effective care. This divergence highlights the importance of patient choice and service design in digital health implementation.

Participant Quotes:

It would help if we could book appointments online instead of going to the hospital just to schedule…and calling and calling and calling with no answer.

(Participant 3)

Maybe they can do follow‐ups over the phone for small problems, so we don't have to come all the way.

(Participant 14)

I don't think online appointments would work for me. I prefer seeing the audiologist in person.

(Participant 5)

Discussion

This study conceptualises delays in audiology services not simply as access barriers, but as manifestations of misalignment between service demand, workforce planning, procurement systems and service delivery models within a centralised public health system. By foregrounding patient experiences, the findings illuminate how these systemic weaknesses are encountered in everyday care pathways. The participant group consisted of 15 adults who had received audiology care through Johannesburg's public healthcare system. Most respondents (66%) were middle‐aged or older, reflecting international patterns where age‐related hearing loss (presbycusis) is a major contributor to acquired hearing impairment [2]. Notably, over one‐third (34%) of participants were under 50, highlighting the growing need to recognise and address hearing issues in younger adults. This suggests that hearing care should not be viewed only as an ‘older adult’ problem; in South Africa, where occupational noise exposure and untreated ear infections are prevalent, younger adults also require timely access to care. A significant proportion of participants (40%) were unemployed, a factor that has considerable implications for healthcare access and continuity of care. Unemployment, especially in LMICs, frequently correlates with difficulties in affording transport to health facilities and maintaining hearing aids [7, 9, 12, 14, 18, 20, 21, 22, 41]. In South Africa, where public healthcare serves the majority (84%) of the population, these financial constraints can act as critical barriers to service uptake. These findings therefore highlight the importance of inclusive public health strategies that recognise the socioeconomic vulnerabilities of patients. Examples include outreach to younger adults at risk of early‐onset hearing loss, subsidised transport options and piloting mobile audiology services to reduce cost burdens. The conceptual model developed from these findings (Figure 1) provides a systems‐level interpretation of how multiple interacting constraints produce delays, while also identifying potential intervention points for policy and service planning.

Participants reported long waiting times for audiology appointments, often exceeding 6 months to a year. These findings are consistent with studies in other LMICs, where delays in accessing audiology services stem from limited personnel, centralised healthcare systems and inefficient scheduling processes [8, 9, 12, 15, 17, 18, 20, 21, 22, 38]. In South Africa, waiting times of over 6 months for hearing assessments and hearing aids are common in the public sector, leading to delayed intervention and worsening communication difficulties. However, given that this dataset was small and urban‐based, the current findings should be seen as illustrative rather than definitive. They nonetheless resonate with broader literature documenting systemic inefficiencies in audiology access. Globally, HICs have adopted measures to reduce audiology waiting times, including digital scheduling systems, task‐shifting models and mobile hearing clinics. Evidence from India [36, 37] and Brazil demonstrates that even in LMIC contexts, tele‐practice and mobile services can improve efficiency and reach. South Africa's continued reliance on hospital‐based audiology services—rather than integrating hearing screening into PHC—exacerbates delays and contributes to low service utilisation. The findings highlight the need for decentralising audiology services into PHC facilities, which could reduce appointment backlogs at tertiary hospitals. Additionally, they highlight the potential role of digital scheduling systems and automated reminders in managing high patient volumes more efficiently.

Even after completing a hearing assessment, many participants waited an additional 6 months or longer to receive their hearing aids. This aligns with findings from Zambia, Malawi, Nigeria and Brazil, where hearing aid stock shortages, slow procurement processes and limited personnel contribute to prolonged waiting times. In South Africa, similar issues have previously been highlighted, noting that government‐funded hearing aid distribution is frequently delayed due to supply chain inefficiencies and a lack of standardisation in procurement policies. Participants in this study were often not informed about expected waiting times, creating frustration and uncertainty. This lack of communication reflects broader challenges in public health service delivery, where patients are not always given clear timelines for their treatment [49]. From a systems perspective, these findings suggest two preliminary action points: (a) standardising procurement and distribution pathways to reduce bottlenecks and (b) ensuring patients receive clear, realistic information about expected waiting times. Even small improvements in communication could enhance patient trust and adherence.

A key barrier to timely audiology service access is the severe shortage of audiologists in the public healthcare system. South Africa has a significant shortage of audiologists working in the public sector, with most concentrated in urban tertiary hospitals, leaving rural areas largely underserved. Similar workforce shortages exist in Kenya and India, where low audiologist‐to‐patient ratios result in long waiting lists and rushed consultations. In contrast, HICs like Australia and Canada have implemented task‐shifting models, where trained nurses and community healthcare workers conduct basic hearing screenings, freeing up audiologists for more complex cases. In the South African context, exploring task‐shifting should be approached carefully: current findings highlight patient frustration with delays, but the study did not collect direct perspectives from clinicians or policymakers. Thus, while training PHC nurses to conduct basic hearing checks may be promising, evidence from larger‐scale mixed‐methods studies will be needed before recommending widespread adoption.

Many participants reported missing appointments due to transport costs and long travel distances to hospitals, particularly those from rural and peri‐urban areas. These challenges have also been reported in Malawi, Nigeria and Uganda, where centralised audiology services force patients to travel long distances, leading to appointment dropouts. In South Africa, patients from rural provinces are reported to be more likely to miss follow‐up appointments due to transport difficulties, leading to higher rates of hearing aid abandonment [15, 50]. Decentralising audiology services through community‐based clinics and mobile units could improve access for these populations. The current study participants' support for mobile units therefore provides valuable, if preliminary, user‐driven evidence in favour of piloting such models in underserved communities.

Participants overwhelmingly supported mobile audiology clinics as a potential solution to reduce waiting times and improve access, particularly for patients in rural and underserved areas—consistent with earlier findings by Khan et al. [15]. Studies from India, Kenya and Brazil have shown that mobile clinics effectively reduce hearing healthcare disparities, providing basic hearing screenings, hearing aid fittings and minor repairs. In the current dataset, mobile services were perceived as especially valuable for repairs and follow‐ups, which participants felt did not always require hospital visits. Some participants expressed interest in tele‐audiology for scheduling and follow‐ups, similar to telemedicine models used in Australia and the UK, where remote consultations and digital hearing aid programming have improved service efficiency [39, 40, 41, 42, 43]. It is important to note, however, that not all participants supported remote models, with some expressing scepticism and a preference for in‐person consultations. This divergence suggests that patient education and careful service design would be required before tele‐audiology could be scaled. Pilot initiatives from Brazil and Kenya have demonstrated that mobile units can be highly effective when backed by consistent funding and local stakeholder engagement. In South Africa, such pilots could test feasibility and acceptability before national rollout.

Overall, the discussion positions mobile clinics, tele‐audiology, task‐shifting and improved scheduling not as isolated innovations, but as interconnected planning instruments that require governance alignment, financing and workforce strategies. We propose a three‐level planning framework for reducing audiology service delays in LMIC public systems: (1) workforce and task allocation, (2) service decentralisation and delivery modality and (3) scheduling and procurement coordination.

While this study provides valuable insights into audiology service delays and potential solutions, several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, the study was limited to 15 participants, which may not fully represent all public healthcare audiology patients in South Africa. Secondly, the study was urban‐centric, with data collected from Johannesburg, meaning findings may not fully capture the experiences of rural populations. Thirdly, as a secondary data analysis, we were constrained by the scope of the original interviews and did not include perspectives from audiologists, administrators or policymakers. These limitations mean that current findings should be viewed as exploratory, providing hypotheses and directions for future work rather than definitive conclusions. Future research should include larger‐scale, multi‐site studies across rural and urban provinces; comparative perspectives from patients, clinicians and policymakers; and economic evaluations of mobile and tele‐audiology models. Such research would generate the robust evidence base needed to inform national policy reforms and integration into the NHI framework.

Conclusions

This study highlights the persistent delays in accessing audiology services in South Africa's public healthcare sector, emphasising long waiting times, workforce shortages, logistical barriers and inefficiencies in service delivery. These delays reflect structural constraints in service planning and coordination rather than individual patient behaviours. Participants described waiting months to over a year for audiology appointments and hearing aid fittings, often experiencing uncertainty due to poor communication from healthcare providers. Financial and geographic barriers further compounded these delays, making it difficult for unemployed and rural patients to access services in a timely manner. These findings align with global evidence from other LMICs, where limited numbers of audiologists, centralised service delivery models and inefficient referral systems contribute to delayed hearing loss intervention. In contrast, HICs have successfully reduced audiology waiting times through mobile health units, task‐shifting strategies, tele‐audiology, and improved scheduling systems.

Given the small, urban‐based sample and the secondary nature of the analysis, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. However, they identify clear leverage points for health system reform, including decentralisation, task‐shifting and digital coordination mechanisms. They highlight several promising directions for reform. Participants expressed strong support for mobile audiology clinics and tele‐audiology services, which could help reduce appointment backlogs, improve service reach and minimise financial and transport‐related barriers. Addressing these challenges requires structural, technological, and workforce‐related interventions.

The study's recommendations are therefore framed as hypotheses for further testing rather than definitive policy prescriptions. As South Africa advances toward NHI, hearing healthcare planning must explicitly address workforce distribution, procurement pathways and service decentralisation to avoid perpetuating current delays. Specifically, larger‐scale research should evaluate: (a) the feasibility of digital scheduling systems and automated reminders in the public sector; (b) the effectiveness of task‐shifting and mid‐level worker training to expand reach; (c) the cost‐effectiveness and acceptability of mobile audiology units in rural South Africa and (d) the integration of tele‐audiology into PHC.

Delays in accessing audiology services remain a significant barrier to equitable hearing healthcare in South Africa. Without targeted reforms and new service models, individuals with hearing loss will continue to face unnecessary delays, undermining rehabilitation outcomes and quality of life. Collaboration between policymakers, healthcare providers and researchers is essential to ensure that audiology services are delivered efficiently, sustainably and equitably. As South Africa progresses toward NHI, hearing healthcare must be explicitly recognised as a priority, with clear timelines, funding commitments and human resource strategies to ensure that audiology does not remain on the margins of primary healthcare reform.

Patient or Public Contribution

Patient perspectives were central to this study. The analysis drew on in‐depth qualitative interviews with adult hearing aid users who shared their lived experiences of accessing audiology services in South Africa's public healthcare system. Although patients were not involved in the design of the secondary analysis or manuscript preparation, their narratives directly informed the study focus, analysis and interpretation of findings, including the identification of service delivery challenges and patient‐prioritised solutions such as mobile clinics and tele‐audiology. Formal patient or public involvement beyond data generation was not feasible due to the secondary nature of the analysis and the use of de‐identified transcripts.
