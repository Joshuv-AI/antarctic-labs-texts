Institutional and interpersonal determinants of dietary practices of persons with intellectual disability in institutional accommodation in Switzerland: a qualitative study
Authors: Franziska Pfister, Katja Uhlmann, Loraine Olalia, Beatrice Kaufmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals with intellectual disability (ID) experience persistent health inequalities compared to the general population. Individual health behaviors, such as dietary practices, are strongly influenced by institutional and interpersonal determinants. Nevertheless, little is known about these determinants in Swiss institutions.AimTo explore institutional and interpersonal determinants of dietary practices among residents with ID living in institutional accommodation in Switzerland.MethodsA qualitative descriptive research design was chosen. Convenience sampling was used to recruit three social institutions from German-speaking Switzerland, as well as caregivers and parents of their residents. Thirteen semi-structured interviews with caregivers and parents of individuals with ID and four separate staff interviews on the institutional food environment were conducted between May and November 2022. They were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using qualitative content analysis.ResultsAt the institutional level, the extent of residents’ self-determination regarding dietary practices, their involvement in food processing and cooking, institutional approaches towards body weight management and resource constraints were important dietary practices determinants. At the interpersonal level, the most important influencing factor was the opinion and knowledge of carers and parents regarding nutrition.ConclusionsThe adoption of a health promotion ethos in institutions would create health-promoting environments and encourage more balanced dietary practices among individuals with ID. Attitudes among residents and staff could be improved through nutrition and health promotion training provided to caregivers by nutrition and health specialists. Additional resources are essential for sustainable health promotion to improve the long-term health of people with ID.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26794-7.Keywords: Health promotion, Nutrition, Dietary practices, Intellectual disability, Settings approach

Introduction

Intellectual disabilities (ID) are defined as significant impairments in intellectual functioning and adaptive behavior that manifest early in life [1]. Individuals with ID experience persistent health inequalities compared to the general population [2, 3], as demonstrated by a higher prevalence of overweight and obesity [4–8]. As the life expectancy of individuals with ID increases, it becomes crucial to develop inclusive health promotion strategies that support long-term well-being [9]. Promoting more physical activity and a healthier diet are two key approaches towards this goal [4, 9–11].

Although individuals with ID demonstrate interest in healthy eating and basic knowledge about food, they often do not meet dietary recommendations (e.g. low intake of fruits, vegetables, and dietary fiber; high intake of snacks and highly processed foods [12–15].

According to the socio-ecological model, individual health behaviors - such as dietary practices - are shaped by multiple, interacting factors at the intrapersonal, interpersonal, institutional, community and policy level [16–18]. Their level of influence varies. Previous research has indicated that the dietary practices of individuals with ID are significantly influenced by factors arising from the school, institutional accommodation and home settings in which they live [19–22]. For instance, the transition from school to adulthood is often associated with excess weight gain in individuals with ID [23]. Furthermore, adults with ID residing in more autonomous settings showed significantly higher rates of sedentary time and less balanced dietary habits than their peers living in more controlled settings [4, 24]. Barriers to dietary behavior change in controlled settings have nonetheless also been identified, and included residents’ reliance on others for food, cooking and feeding, as well as limitations related to care and support [25]. Food availability and accessibility play a pivotal role in institutions for individuals with ID; previous research has shown an inadequate offer of all food groups [26]. Caregivers are key stakeholders in health promotion for this client group. They serve as role models, support healthy lifestyles, and decide what menus and snacks are available [10, 21]. Previous studies have suggested that caregivers’ own dietary behavior influences that of residents [27]. However, poor to moderate nutritional knowledge and limited cooking skills have been reported among caregivers. This resulted in poorly planned meals containing semi-finished or ready-made food of little variety [28–31]. Further barriers to healthy dietary habits included organizational issues such as understaffing and frequent shift changes [30]. Institution management teams exerted influence on dietary habits by how they provided caregivers with guidelines, decided on available resources and prioritized healthy lifestyle promotion in the institution’s vision statement [10]. In the home environment, certain parenting practices and time constraints were identified as significant barriers to balanced diets [32–34]. In summary, many powerful institutional and interpersonal determinants influence the dietary practices of individuals with IDl.

In Switzerland about 85’000 people (roughly 1% of the population) live with ID [35]. With the Swiss ratification of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) in 2014 came a shift away from promoting residential care towards more independent living for people with ID. Accommodation may broadly be divided into the categories “institutional accommodation” and “private accommodation”, with both offering more and less intensive care [36]. While progress has been made in the area of independent living, health remains a significant concern; individuals with ID continue to face a higher risk of health inequalities, as observed in other countries [2]. Little is known about the determinants of dietary practices for adults with ID living in Swiss institutional accommodation. This study, which is part of a larger research project, aimed to explore these factors.

Methods

Aim of the study

This study was conducted as part of the interdisciplinary project IntegrEAT, which aimed to lay the foundation for the development of nutrition education tools adapted to adults with ID. To achieve this goal, a comprehensive understanding of the intra-, interpersonal and institutional factors influencing their dietary practices was necessary. Due to the volume of data, this publication focuses on interpersonal and institutional factors. A second publication (Pfister et al. in preparation) will present intrapersonal factors, as well as the observed interactions aimed at changing individual behavior, as proposed by Golden et al. (2012) [16].

Study design

A qualitative descriptive research design was chosen to achieve the objective of developing an understanding and description of a phenomenon [37]. A semi-structured interview was conducted with at least one staff member at each of the three institutions, to gain insight into the respective food environments. Semi-structured interviews were also conducted with resident proxies (caregivers and relatives) from the three same institutions, to gather information on the residents’ dietary practices and the influencing factors thereof, in both institution and at home. The different perspectives of caregivers and relatives allowed for the triangulation of information. As mentioned above, the results are described in two separate publications.

Recruitment of institutions and participants

We aimed to recruit three residential institutions from different regions of German-speaking Switzerland, each of which would be asked to select two living groups of adults with ID for inclusion in the study. The selected groups would ideally be diverse in terms of resident independence level. Since this study began during the Covid-19 pandemic, social institutions were under great pressure due to stringent regulations and staff shortages. Therefore, we decided to resort to convenience sampling to select the living groups and interviewees, a method commonly used for predefined populations such as medical clinics [38]. In two recruitment rounds, a total of 12 institutions from five cantons in German-speaking Switzerland were contacted via email by the project manager FP. The e-mail explained the project and its aims. Most of the contacted individuals expressed interest in the topic and distributed the information provided to the living group staff. If the staff of a living group agreed to participate, they took responsibility for recruiting a colleague for the food environment interview and caregivers and relatives for the proxy interviews. They provided the project flyer to potential participants. The target was a minimum of 12 proxy interviews, as this is approximately the number at which data saturation is reached [39].

Despite the general interest of institutions in the study, it proved difficult to identify three institutions willing and able to participate, in light of the challenging circumstances they were facing. Following two rounds of recruitment, three institutions in Canton Solothurn agreed to take part (Table 1). A relationship with several caregivers was established by the project team during project initiation activities in the respective institutions. The institutions then identified staff members willing to participate in the food environment interviews (Table 2) and fourteen interviewees for the proxy interviews including eight caregivers (Table 3) and five relatives (Table 4). One of the caregivers was willing to participate in both a food environment and a proxy interview. As one of the relatives did not show up for the online interview and subsequently did not respond to e-mails for unknown reasons, a total of thirteen proxy interviews were finally conducted.

Setting

Three social institutions participated in this study (Table 1). All of them offer institutional accommodation with more intensive care, as well as institutional accommodation in community-based living groups with greater independence. Institution 3 also offers residential training for independent living (Table 1).

In all of the participating institutions, meals are mainly prepared in the central kitchen for residents needing more intensive care, while community-based living groups prepare their own meals and eat lunch at their workplace (see Tables 1 and 1 supplementary material).

Data collection

Two semi-structured interview guides were developed by UK (MSc) and FP (PhD), both experienced female nutrition and public health researchers working at the Department for Health Professions at the Bern University of Applied Sciences. The guides were based on the SPSS method by Helfferich [40] and discussed within the interdisciplinary research team, which included BK and OL, two experienced female experts in participatory health design (MSc or equivalent) working at the Bern Academy of the Arts. One guide focused on the food environment of the participating institutions, exploring topics such as meal patterns, self-determination, rules, and access to food. The second guide was designed to be more open-ended, deliberately avoiding prompts on the specific aspects outlined in the food environment guide. It explored where proxies encounter dietary practices and nutrition in the daily life within the living group, allowing space for the topic to develop organically. Further areas of exploration included residents’ individual preferences and nutritional needs, communication about nutrition with the individuals in their care, and the tools required for nutritional communication and education. FP pre-tested the semi-structured proxy interview guide by conducting an interview with the mother of a resident of one of the participating institutions via MS Teams. As only one minor adjustment was necessary, the pretest was included in the data set.

KU and FP conducted all semi-structured interviews between May and November 2022. Depending on the preferences and availability of the interviewees, interviews were held either face-to-face or online via MS Teams. All interviews were conducted in Swiss German or German, lasted between 30 and 60 min, and were voice-recorded. No one else was present during the interviews, with one exception: in an online interview with a mother, the father was present in the background and contributed occasionally. For publication purposes, all quotations were translated into English.

Data analysis

The four food environment interviews were summarized, while all recordings of the proxy interviews were transcribed ad verbatim. All data were anonymized to ensure confidentiality. The transcripts were then analyzed using qualitative content analysis [41]. BK and KU, with the assistance of a student, read the interviews several times to familiarize themselves with the data. They then separately developed and discussed codes based on a data-driven approach for three transcripts. Iteratively, a common coding system was established, based on which all transcripts were coded by either BK or UK using MAXQDA software (2022). The codes were subsequently organized into three thematic levels - intrapersonal, interpersonal and institutional - in accordance with the first three layers of the socio-ecological model as proposed by Golden et al. [16].

Due to the wealth of data, the results were divided into two publications: This initial publication will describe the institutional and interpersonal factors in detail. A subsequent publication will examine the intrapersonal factors, including the communication and education around nutrition delivered at the individual level, as put forward by Golden et al. (2012) [16] (Pfister et al. in preparation).

Ethical approval

Study procedures were reviewed by the Ethics Committee of Bern Canton and deemed exempt in accordance with Swiss law. Therefore, no full ethics proposal had to be submitted. Prior to their participation in the study, all participants were provided with an information flyer that outlined the study’s objectives and procedures. They were asked to provide written consent. Before each interview, the researcher reiterated the study’s purpose, assured participants of confidentiality and informed them that they were free to withdraw at any time during the interview if they so wished.

Results

Four food environment interviews and 13 proxy interviews with eight caregivers and five parents were conducted. Three women and one man provided the information on the food environment (Table 2). Their ages ranged from 28 to 49 years, and two of them had completed at least 3 years of professional training as social pedagogues, while another interviewee was in her last year of training. All of them had work experience.

The caregivers interviewed in the proxy interviews were seven women and one man (Table 3). Three of them had completed professional training in social pedagogy or a related field, while a fourth one was in her final year of training. The others had other backgrounds or did not want to specify this information. Their ages ranged from 21 to 63 years. Most of them had more than five years of work experience in the field. None had completed any training in nutrition.

The relatives we interviewed were all mothers between the ages of 46 and 75 (Table 4). All of them had a profession. The individuals with ID in their charge were between 18 and 50 years old and lived in seven different living groups belonging to three institutions. One living group in each institution offered a more independent living environment for its members. These will be referred to as “supported community settings” hereafter.

We found eleven codes and three overarching themes (see Table 2 supplementary material). The two themes “influencing factors at the institutional level” and “influencing factors at the interpersonal level” and the related codes will be presented below. The third theme “influencing factors at the intrapersonal level” will be presented in a second publication (Pfister et al. in preparation).

Influencing factors at the institutional level

Patterns and composition of meals

The four interviews on the food environment revealed that on weekdays all the living groups had a clear meal routine with three meals a day, and in some cases one or two snacks. More independent persons with ID who worked outside the institution, sometimes ate lunch and some snacks at the workplace. On weekends, many living groups had brunch, a small snack for lunch and dinner in the evening (see supplementary material).

Meal composition varied between institutions. In some, salad or vegetables were served with every meal, while in others, meat or fish was served daily. Some living groups were provided with dessert daily, while others received it only on certain days of the week. One group paid particular attention to ensuring that the snacks they prepared themselves were healthy. In the proxy interviews, parents also described a clear meal pattern on the weekends at home. Several families only ate twice: Brunch and dinner. Sometimes a small lunch or something sweet was additionally eaten in the early afternoon.

Extent of self-determination

The residents’ extent of self-determination was found to be a critical factor influencing their dietary practices. It was linked to meal planning, food choice, food access and the institutional approaches towards body weight management.

In institutions with a central kitchen, menus were usually planned there on weekdays, even for living groups which prepared their own meals. However, residents often had minimal choice (e.g. a vegetarian or meat option). Residents’ dislikes regarding certain foods were considered, as they were given the opportunity to eat something else like leftovers or cold options if they did not like the menu. None of the institutions forced residents to eat a particular meal or food against their will.

Residents of supported community settings often planned their own meals, on some days independently and in accordance with their preferences. However, usually it was the same residents who got involved with planning. One caregiver mentioned that although residents’ self-determination is enhanced when they are given more opportunities to choose and prepare their meals independently, with limited support from staff, this was not possible in her living group because of the residents’ long working days. In general, planning meals together gave caregivers the opportunity to talk to the residents about nutrition.

Residents in all settings were typically more involved in meal planning on weekends, which gave them greater self-determination in dietary practices. Those who were able to express themselves verbally were more frequently asked, as using pictures was found to be a time-consuming process. In some living groups, the repeated selection of certain meals by residents led to an adaptation of the food stored, which further reduced the range of possible weekend meals to chicken nuggets, French fries, and spaghetti with pesto.

In addition to food and menu choice, a caregiver highlighted the importance of self-determination regarding the social aspects of eating, reporting that residents in her group could choose with whom and where they wanted to eat.

Food access outside of regular mealtimes in the living groups was determined by access to the refrigerator. While residents in some living groups had autonomous access to the food in the refrigerator, in one group the refrigerator was locked, thereby limiting the residents’ ability to help themselves excessively.

Maybe I haven’t mentioned that yet, but it happens very often that they just, well, we’ve locked the fridge, um, because otherwise a lot of them would just eat out of it and empty all the milk and stuff. (ID3, caregiver)

Particularly in supported community settings, food was accessible at any time.

During home visits, the interviewed parents generally described a high degree of autonomy regarding food. Their adult children were usually allowed to decide what they wanted to eat on weekends. In some cases, there were no restrictions, and sweets were readily available. A mother explained how important it was to her son to choose his own food. To support his desire for self-determination, she provided him with a selection of four foods that he could freely choose from. Unlike in the institution, this also included a dessert. Another interviewee explained that she collaborated with her daughter in the planning of menus, which were sometimes based on the food available in the refrigerator.

Although numerous aspects of self-determination were discussed in the interviews, interviewees also mentioned several approaches to restricting food intake or portion size, primarily in the presence of concern over body weight. In these cases, the caregivers established rules regarding quantity, meal composition and dessert. In several living groups there were established rules for the serving of food. Typically, a modest amount was initially served, in order to avoid excessive food intake and minimize food waste. Subsequently, residents were usually permitted to serve themselves again. In some groups, individuals for whom there was concern over body weight were not provided with any additional food, while in others, they were offered additional vegetables or salad only. A caregiver described the necessity for the team to continuously monitor food quantities.

You’re usually only allowed to serve two or three times at the table. Erm, that’s also an issue, perhaps because they sometimes want to eat more. (ID3, caregiver)

In some cases, caregivers discussed appropriate quantities with the residents in question.

The care team’s “softer” strategies included influencing menu composition for all residents, such as serving salad first to reduce main course intake. One team attempted to limit carbohydrate intake and offer sufficient protein, in order to improve satiety. Another strategy was to offer balanced meals and reduce energy intake by cooking with calorie-reduced ingredients such as low-fat milk or semi-skimmed cream. Meal composition was also influenced by rules regarding dessert. While in one living group, residents were permitted to request a small bowl of something sweet or a banana two or three evenings per week, in others, dessert consumption had been reduced from three times per week to once per week. In some living groups, the caregivers modified the menus requested by the residents to include healthier foods, thereby improving the meals’ nutritional balance.

Living groups with a high degree of autonomy faced different challenges. One rule was that residents were required to purchase their own soft drinks and alcohol. However, residents were not permitted to get drunk or bring large quantities of strong alcohol into the building. This was a topic of frequent debate. In these groups, the likes and dislikes of roommates could also restrict the residents’ dietary practices, as described by a parent:

[…] now he always says “no, he [name of first roommate] and [name of second roommate] don’t like this, [name of first roommate] doesn’t like that, the other one doesn’t like that” and then he just adapts to everyone and I said “just make a good pumpkin soup now […] for dinner and then a piece of bread […]”, but then he says “she doesn’t like this” […] so it’s all getting difficult now. (ID16, mother)

Some of the parents also described the restrictions on dietary practices they imposed at home. In some cases, although the adult children were permitted to select from the food on the table, they did not have any influence on the menu. One parent also stated that her son was not permitted to consume snacks at home. However, neighboring family members did not enforce these rules.

When I say enough is enough, there’s nothing left, then he goes into the house next door, either to the right or to the left. My sister and her family live on the left, my brother, and his family live on the right (laughs), and [son’s name] is simply the king. […]. He goes and helps himself, [son’s name] is allowed, [son’s name] is allowed everything. (ID1, mother)

Institutional approaches towards body weight management

In the institutional setting, body weight management emerged as a relevant determinant influencing dietary practices of individuals with ID. In the interviews, overweight and obesity were perceived as a health problem by caregivers and parents, while underweight was rarely mentioned. This was especially true for individuals living in a controlled setting.

Some group homes kept charts of residents’ body weight, but only for residents who agreed to be weighed.

We have tables that we keep. We only weigh voluntarily, no one is forced […] um, we used to […] just weigh through […] and I found that very unpleasant and exhausting and […, I] am very glad that we don’t do it like that anymore […]. I don’t force anyone onto a scale. (ID12, caregiver).

In some institutions, measures to palliate against residents’ progressive weight gain were implemented at the living group level. Caregivers restricted free access to food, changed the order of serving meal components or the nutrient composition of meals, and decreased sugar and fat content of foods served. In other institutions, individual dietary restrictions were applied only to individuals with perceived progressive weight gain, such as smaller portions or second servings limited to vegetables (see section “Extent of Self-Determination”). In living groups with a high level of self-determination, often no direct measures were implemented to curb progressive weight gain, which was criticized by the interviewed parents.

Sometimes I bring it up because I just think, “You’ve really put on weight” (smiles), but now he’s said he has a new goal, which is to weigh himself every Thursday, so I asked him, “What do you do after you’ve weighed yourself?” (imitates son) “Well, you know, now I just step on the scale and then we write it down”. (ID14, mother)

These institutional practices also shaped how parents addressed weight at home. One mother explained:

Now that she’s in the new place, they don’t check so much anymore. Well, I can’t always keep an eye on things when she’s at home so rarely; that makes it even more difficult. […] Then I can’t always nag her [about her weight] when she comes home for two days. (ID11, mother)

Involvement in food processing and cooking

The caregivers described how residents were expected to take responsibility for the living group and help with household chores. This often included meal-related tasks such as setting the table and cleaning the kitchen. As a result, most residents were involved in food preparation in one way or another.

In some supported community settings, residents were encouraged to plan a meal, purchase the necessary ingredients and prepare the meal independently or with minimal assistance. In other institutions, residents and their caregivers prepared meals together. Weekends were often used for this activity, when some residents also went grocery shopping. This approach aimed to increase the independence of some residents in areas such as shopping and cooking.

Where there was a central kitchen, meals were usually prepared without systematic resident involvement. In some cases, the central kitchen also served as “supermarket” where some living groups went “shopping” for their food items, even on weekends.

However, the role of the central kitchen was seen to challenge empowerment and health by caregivers and parents:

So I think that um just an important point would also be the self… um empowerment, especially in cooking. We have the chef. A lot of things are already prepared for us by the central kitchen, afterwards it’s often the employees who perhaps make something from the leftovers in the evening or something and… um… sometimes I think it would be hm… (thinking) much easier to think about food if we had a lot more to do with food. (ID8, caregiver)

Or yes, we also shop here in the house, so we go down to the central kitchens to get the food. […] so we buy everything down there, but the selection is very limited. […] We try to make sure they eat a balanced and varied diet, but I just think the choice is so limited that it’s monotonous and not necessarily healthy. (ID6, caregiver)

Interviewees mentioned that addressing nutrition and diet would be easier if the residents were more closely involved in food preparation. Cooking in their living groups allowed residents to have their own experiences with a more diverse range of healthy food, whereas a central kitchen with “standardized menus” deprived them of this experience.

One mother felt that the central kitchen was not designed to involve the residents, as it was busy and noisy. The cooks had no connection with the target group and the residents who worked there were separated from them, which she felt was negative in a social institution.

The parents themselves rarely involved their adult children in the kitchen or in grocery shopping during their home visits. In some cases, the practice of empowering their adult children in this area had been abandoned. For example, one mother mentioned that she no longer “practiced” shopping with her son. Another mother said that her son used to cook regularly when he still lived at home, but now that he lived in an institution, she would cook when he came for a home visit.

Time and resource constraints

A lack of time and human resources was a recurring theme in the interviews. For instance, the strictly timed daily (meal) schedule caused stress during lunchtime care, which affected some residents’ food intake, especially the most vulnerable: One mother described how her son sometimes did not eat because the caregivers lacked the time to cut his food. A caregiver described how a resident who ate too slowly was given finger food, without discussing the matter with him first. The caregiver felt that this was inappropriate because it reduced the resident’s autonomy and quality of life.

The scarcity of resources also influenced meal choices. Staff usually selected meals that everyone liked and, in practice, rarely consulted residents due to time constraints and high stress levels. In one interview, staff convenience was also mentioned as a reason for meal selection.

I think we could do the same [organize whose turn it is] for menu planning […] the residents plan […] the menus for this week. So that it is really defined and because I think a lot of staff, me too sometimes, so, we like to be comfortable, don’t we? So, if it’s too time-consuming, even though you know it’s the right thing to do, you’d rather do it your own way. So, just quick and easy and it’s done, but that’s not the point of our work. […] I think sometimes it actually has to be a must that it gets done, then I think […] you would involve them [the residents] more. (ID6, caregiver)

The lack of time also had an impact on the residents’ involvement in meal preparation. As one caregiver explained, it was likely that everything would come from the central kitchen in the future due to time pressure. This would mean that residents would no longer be able to cook for themselves.

Influencing factors at the interpersonal level

Relatives’ and caregivers’ opinion and knowledge regarding food and nutrition

Caregivers’ opinions about food and nutrition influenced how these issues were addressed in the living groups. The food environment interviews revealed that while some teams shared a common attitude toward food and nutrition, others didn’t. The proxy interviews also revealed disagreements among caregivers about portion size and how to limit it: One respondent disagreed with the small portions given to residents in his resident group, arguing that eating was related to quality of life. Another caregiver, however, felt that although the cuisine in her institution was very good and the food was of good quality, it was a problem that the portions given to the residents were not limited or not limited enough and that dessert was offered daily. She felt that as a result, people experiencing progressive weight gain were not supported enough in her institution.

However, caregivers agreed that residents should be able to choose what they eat. One respondent was opposed to banning unhealthy foods from the institutions but felt it was her responsibility to provide healthy options.

It’s important to us that they eat healthily, which is why we usually offer fruit and Darvida [Swiss wholegrain crackers] or something like that, but if they want chocolate we can’t forbid them that. And it’s just… I think it’s our job to offer something healthy (smiles) and then it’s their responsibility and decision whether they want to eat healthy or unhealthy. (ID6, caregiver)

Another caregiver felt that although food was not as highly regulated as in the past, there were still many subliminal prohibitions. The interviews also revealed that some caregivers were strongly influenced by personal food preferences or had developed their own theories regarding healthy eating behavior.

But everyone has a different opinion, so, for example, what I think is a balanced, healthy meal might not be the same for other staff members, or I might think it’s important, for example, that our residents also eat meat, that they get enough protein, but then we also have staff members who prefer vegetarian food (laughs). So, I can’t say what is really healthy because we all eat a little different. (ID6, caregiver)

[Caregivers could be] simply more open, that one [caregivers] recognizes that the human body has its own intelligence and does not simply say “this is good for you”, but that one listens to what the body may need or how it needs it. (ID12, caregiver).

Another caregiver used sweets to discipline inappropriate behavior.

Sure, it’s a threat and you shouldn’t communicate it like that, I know that it’s a bit like that, but yes, […] as a child you didn’t always get sweets, if you did not behave, you didn’t get anything, and you always communicate that to them. (ID7, caregiver)

As a result, the ways in which dietary practices were influenced and discussed with residents varied among caregivers, even within the same living group.

The interviews further revealed limitations in the nutritional knowledge of some caregivers, for instance a lack of understanding of the individual nutritional needs of the persons in their care, the seasonal availability of food products and the ecological sustainability of dietary choices. None of the caregivers had received any formal training or education in the field of nutrition (Table 3).

The personal opinions expressed by relatives regarding nutrition tended to be critical of institutions and caregivers. For example, one mother felt that institutions should pay more attention to nutrition and exercise. While she herself passed on a lot of nutritional knowledge to her son, she felt that the expertise and input of caregivers was missing. She felt that they should have an understanding of a balanced diet when working in a supported community setting. Another mother expressed the wish to collaborate with the institution about nutrition.

Parents also raised the issue of the quality of the food provided and wished for clearer prioritization of a more balanced diet. One mother suggested that the food should be part of the institutions’ quality control, as it is in some day care centers. She suspected that the residents were not supervised closely enough regarding nutrition and that therefore they did not pay enough attention to their diet.

Several other mothers criticized the fact that meals offered by the institutions were not balanced.

They used to serve salad every day, but that’s no longer the case. They prepare vegetables one day and salad the other day […] yes, it’s just a bit sad. (ID 16, mother)

He’s in [name of institution] during the week. I don’t know what they eat. Probably a lot of… um… bread. They used to always just eat bread with cheese. And then you can’t just… um… reduce calories. And that’s not of much use if I only pay attention with him on the weekends. (ID 1, mother)

Parents also criticized the lack of control and the unrestricted access to snacks in the institution.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge this is the first study to examine the interpersonal and institutional determinants of the dietary practices of adults with ID in Switzerland. It contributes to the limited evidence base on this topic in general [19, 42, 43].

At the institutional level, we found several, often interdependent determinants of dietary practices. These included the extent of residents’ self-determination regarding dietary practices, attitudes toward and approaches to managing perceived progressive weight gain, the residents’ involvement in meal-related tasks, and time and resource constraints. At the interpersonal level, the opinion and knowledge of caregivers and parents regarding nutrition was the key influencing factor. Our results offer a novel perspective on caregivers’ and relatives’ lived experience of self-determination in institutional accommodation. They indicate possibilities and challenges with regards to dietary habits and health promotion. A lack of focus on health promotion such as optimizing institutional and interpersonal determinants of dietary practices to promote healthy diets was observed.

A key finding in the present study was the importance of self-determination, including the choice of dietary practices. Given the close relationship between self-determination and the quality of life of individuals with ID [44], professionals such as social workers and social educators focus on promoting and supporting self-determination in the ID population [45]. This can lead to a lack of support, as caregivers may refrain from assisting people with ID in decision-making processes in an effort not to unduly affect their autonomy [46]. Regarding dietary practices, this “duty of supporting autonomy” results in residents, especially those with mild to moderate ID, independently purchasing and preparing food which may result in unhealthy diets in the long term, causing harm to health [42]. In our study, caregivers attempted to fulfill the self-determination ideal by giving residents autonomy over food choices and menu planning where possible. However, promoting a healthy lifestyle amongst individuals with ID is another of the caregivers’ central tasks [47]. The volume and diversity of health-related support needs among individuals with ID are considerable [48]. It requires training, “nudging”, institutional processes and relevant support materials. Thus, caregivers find themselves in a dilemma between respecting the residents’ right to self-determination and the professional duty to protect them from harm [45], which they perceive as challenging [42, 49].

In the literature, the importance of resident autonomy as a barrier to balanced dietary practices is discussed controversially. In one study, caregivers found self-determination to be one of the most important barriers to balanced diets for individuals with ID living in community residences [42], while in another study it was identified as the least important factor [43]. The difference in the findings was attributed to the interpersonal level, namely caregivers’ increased knowledge about health promotion in the second study, which led to a better understanding of the opportunities to influence residents’ health behavior without undermining residents’ autonomy [43].

In our findings, residents’ self-determination regarding dietary practices was limited in certain ways. Caregivers regulated portion sizes, meal composition, or limited access to food between meals, especially for residents with perceived progressive weight gain. This weight-centric approach stigmatizes individuals with progressive weight gain, rather than enabling all residents and staff to adopt healthier dietary habits. Therefore, the institutions’ management and caregivers follow a disease prevention rather than a health promotion approach, as evidenced by Caron [50]. The focus is on targeting individual behaviors instead of a setting-based health promotion approach in line with existing literature [49, 51]. Previous research has indicated that many organizations providing care for individuals with ID lack a strong health promotion ethos [47].

Nudging the residents towards healthier choices could encourage healthier behaviors in people with ID, while respecting their autonomy [10]. Visual stimuli like healthy-food symbols, traffic-light labeling, and happy emojis have been demonstrated to be effective in promoting healthy eating behaviors in both adults [52] and children [53].

Our findings substantiate the perception of the central kitchen as a barrier to balanced dietary practices in institution-based ID populations, as it prevents residents from participating in food planning and preparation. Staff in central kitchens may have limited understanding of a balanced diet and show little flexibility regarding residents’ individual dietary needs [54]. In addition, adults with ID have identified accessible cooking facilities as a facilitator of a healthy lifestyle in previous studies [55].

A central kitchen, however, may also represent an opportunity for residents to develop balanced dietary practices. Simple cafeteria interventions like food choice modification and creative naming/branding have been shown to have a positive impact on the consumption of fruit and vegetables in schools [56] and among university students [57], for instance. Whole-institution nutritional intervention, including recipe modifications, environmental changes in the cafeteria or lunchrooms and nutrition education programs have been shown to improve food quality and food choice diversity [58].

For adolescents with ID, food availability was found to significantly influence eating habits [21]. While a comprehensive analysis of the meals provided was beyond the scope of this study, the findings show that institutions placed a clear emphasis on regular meals. However, the energy provided may exceed the energy requirements of the ID population, which usually has a low level of physical activity [8, 59]. This is particularly likely in institutions which offer five meals a day. In contrast, Hamzaid [12] found that the mean energy intake of individuals with ID community-based group homes in Australia was too low. Therefore, more research on food availability and nutritional adequacy is needed.

Finally, resources are a crucial determinant at the institutional level. The findings of the present study suggest that constraints in time and staffing have a significant impact on the dietary practices of individuals with ID, as they are closely intertwined with other crucial aspects like shared meal choice and involvement in food preparation. This finding further supports Ruud et al. [42], who suggested that limited manpower appeared to significantly constrain caregivers’ capacity to encourage a balanced diet [42].

In individuals with ID, a multi-component dietary intervention including nudging, nutritional training for staff, practical healthy eating courses and the use of behavior change techniques for the target group resulted in a reduction in waist circumference and an increase in fruit and berry intake in the intervention group [60].

At the interpersonal level, our findings indicate a lack of consensus among caregivers regarding balanced dietary practices. None of them had received formal training or education in nutrition, despite their significant influence on the dietary habits of their care recipients, and their responsibility for health promotion. This finding is consistent with an Australian study that identified a limited understanding of nutrition among caregivers working in group homes [28]. As a result, it is challenging for caregivers to create suitable meals, appropriate portion sizes and employ suitable nudging or education techniques for individuals with different needs without the support of a dietitian. Moreover, caregivers’ personal beliefs and preferences, which may be influenced by non-evidence-based food trends and may not reflect residents’ needs, can affect their caregiving practices [42]. This was also evident in the statements made during our interviews. Caregivers are in a unique position to promote healthier dietary practices [42]. Engagement of caregivers has also been found to be critical for other aspects of a healthy lifestyle in adults with ID, such as physical activity promotion [61]. It is, however, essential for caregiver teams to establish a shared, evidence-based understanding of the topic [43]. This requires support from institutional management in terms of defining health promoting processes, and providing sufficient resources for caregivers to implement relevant activities. In general, there is a need for commitment to balanced dietary practices and a healthy lifestyle by the institutions’ management and staff [43].

Furthermore, research indicates that the support of parents and teachers is crucial for promoting healthy behaviors in neurotypical children and adolescents [62]. Parents also have a strong influence on a healthy lifestyle in children and adolescents with ID [21]. This is a crucial period during which balanced dietary practices are acquired [63, 64]. Interestingly, the fact that caregivers and parents can model healthy behaviors did not arise in our study. The fact that parents of individuals with ID tend to be overprotective and limit the self-determination of their adult children was not mentioned either [65]. Parents in the present study tended to ascribe total responsibility for the lack of balanced dietary practices to the institutions, yet they seldom considered the role that they themselves modelled.

The present study generated important insights into institutional and interpersonal determinants of dietary practices of people with ID living in Swiss institutions. As is the case with all studies, the present study is not without its limitations. Firstly, the sample size of thirteen participants is relatively small. While 12 interviews are considered to be sufficient to achieve code saturation [39], it may not be enough for meaning saturation [66]. It is possible that some data was missed due to the heterogeneous nature of our proxy sample, which included both caregivers and parents. Secondly, the present study employed a convenience sampling scheme, which may have resulted in a selection bias as parents and caregivers interested in nutrition may be overrepresented in the sample. Furthermore, staff members recruited the interviewees, which may have led to the inclusion of participants motivated by a healthy lifestyle and/or with a positive perception of the institution. Nonetheless, the interviewed parents did express themselves critically regarding the institutions. The conclusions drawn are based on a limited number of respondents associated with three Swiss social institutions situated in the same region. They may not be easily transferable to other regions and countries with different living and framework conditions for adults with ID. Finally, social desirability may have influenced the responses of caregivers and parents. The caregivers may have highlighted their professional duty regarding self-determination in the interviews, but this may not always describe their actions in practice. The parents may have sought to underline positive parenting practices.

The results are strengthened by the fact that the involved living groups included a range of individuals with ID regarding age, degree of ID, level of independence and living arrangements. Furthermore, the inclusion of two groups of proxies allowed for the inclusion of different perspectives and data source triangulation. A further strength of the present study is researcher triangulation, as two authors participated in the data analysis and discussed themes and codes until consensus was reached.

Conclusions

Several institutional and interpersonal determinants of the dietary practices of adults with ID in Switzerland were identified in this study: the extent of residents’ self-determination regarding dietary practices, institutional attitudes toward and approaches to weight issues, residents’ involvement in meal preparation and related tasks, time and resource constraints and the opinions and knowledge of caregivers and parents regarding nutrition.

In general, the institutional environment does not promote a balanced diet amongst residents with ID. Institutional management should therefore develop a health promotion ethos and use a whole-institution approach to taking the lead on creating a health-promoting environment. This would make the healthier choice the easier choice for all residents, and improve attitudes towards more balanced eating habits in residents and staff. Moreover, the role of facilities like the central kitchen needs to be reconsidered.

Caregivers should receive training in nutrition and health promotion from nutrition and health specialists. This could provide them with food literacy, evidence-based knowledge and tools, and thus contribute to reducing their dilemma between providing self-determination for individuals with ID and caring for their health. It would also contribute to an increase in the food literacy and food skills of the individuals with ID in their care and reduce tensions with relatives. To achieve this, additional financial resources for staffing at the institutional level are crucial to improving the long-term health of people with ID.
