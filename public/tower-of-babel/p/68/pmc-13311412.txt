Prime editing updates: technological evolution, methodological expansion, and delivery strategies for in vivo applications
Authors: Uijin Jong, Dongjae Lim, Seung Hwan Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrime editing is a next-generation genome editing technology that enables precise base substitutions, insertions, and deletions at target genomic loci without inducing double-strand breaks or requiring exogenous donor DNA. This system operates through a fusion protein composed of Cas9 nickase and reverse transcriptase together with prime editing guide RNA; it has emerged as a precise genome editing platform that overcomes the limitations of conventional double-strand break-inducing CRISPR-Cas9 systems. Since first reported in 2019, diverse methodological improvements from PE1 to PE7 have been achieved, leading to rapid advances in editing efficiency, expansion of the editable target range, correction of large genomic regions, and development of in vivo delivery technologies. In this review, we comprehensively discuss the fundamental working mechanism of prime editing, its methodological evolution, recent expansion strategies, and delivery platforms for therapeutic applications, and provide perspectives for future development.Keywords: Cas9, Double-strand break, Gene therapy, Prime editing, Reverse transcription

FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLES OF PRIME EDITING

CRISPR-Cas9-based genome editing technologies have transformed life science and biomedical research (1, 2). However, conventional CRISPR systems that rely on double-strand breaks are associated with imprecise insertions and deletions (indels), chromosomal rearrangements, and cellular toxicity (3-7). Prime editing, which was developed to overcome these limitations, enables more precise and predictable genome modification by utilizing single-strand nicking and reverse transcription-mediated DNA synthesis (Fig. 1, top) (8-12). A prime editor consists of a fusion protein of Cas9 nickase (nCas9, H840A) and reverse transcriptase (RT) and a prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA) containing an RT template and primer binding site (PBS) at the 3’–end of the sgRNA (13, 14). By selectively inducing a nick without generating a complete double-strand break, this system enables diverse forms of DNA editing (15, 16). This review systematically describes the working mechanism and evolutionary process of prime editing and considers recent research trends with a focus on expansion technologies and delivery strategies.

METHODOLOGICAL EVOLUTION OF PRIME EDITING

Since its inception in 2019, prime editing has evolved rapidly (8). The initial system represented a proof-of-concept based on the fusion of Cas9 nickase and RT. Subsequent integration of protein engineering (17, 18), pegRNA design optimization (19), and modulation of intracellular DNA repair pathways (20) have further improved editing efficiency and precision. These developments can be summarized as sequential improvements from PE1 to PE7 (Fig. 1, middle and bottom).

From PE1 to PE3: establishment of RT-based prime editing platform

The initial PE1 system consisted of SpCas9 nickase (H840A) fused to wild-type M-MLV RT (8). Although this system was innovative in that it induced a nick in the target DNA and synthesized a new sequence from the 3’–end without generating a double-strand break, its editing efficiency was limited. This result can be attributed to the low processivity of RT, structural instability of pegRNA, and competition with endogenous DNA repair pathways that restore the original sequence (21). PE2 was developed by introducing multiple mutations into RT to enhance enzyme stability, thermostability, and DNA synthesis activity, resulting in significant improvements in various base substitutions and editing events (Fig. 1, middle) (8). To overcome the low efficiency attributed to DNA repair, PE3 introduced an additional nick on the strand opposite to the initially nicked non-target strand, thereby biasing the cellular repair machinery toward incorporation of the edited strand (Fig. 1, middle) (8). This strategy increases the probability of prime editing by directing the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) pathway toward the intended edit. However, double nicking increases indel formation, which was minimized in PE3b by inducing the second nick only after editing had been completed. These developments established the fundamental operational framework of prime editing.

PE4 and PE5: high-efficiency prime editing through mismatch repair modulation

A major factor limiting prime editing efficiency is activation of the Mismatch Repair (MMR) pathway (20, 22), which recognizes mismatched base pairs and tends to restore the original sequence, thereby interfering with fixation of the newly introduced sequence. To overcome this, PE4 and PE5 were designed to transiently express a dominant-negative form of MLH1 (MLH1dn) to inhibit the MMR pathway (Fig. 1, middle) (20). MMR pathway recognizes mismatched pairs and preferentially restores the original sequence, thereby reversing prime editing outcomes. Transient inhibition of MMR (via MLH1dn) prevents this reversion and promotes retention of the edited strand. This strategy represents a conceptual shift beyond the original framework and markedly increases prime editing efficiency, particularly in MMR-proficient cells. However, prolonged suppression of MMR may compromise genome stability, reflecting the importance of transient regulation (23).

PE6: compact prime editors for enhanced activity and adeno-associated virus (AAV) delivery

Doman et al. combined phage-assisted continuous evolution (PACE) with structure-guided protein engineering to develop next-generation compact prime editors (PE6 variants) with smaller sizes and enhanced editing efficiency (Fig. 1, bottom) (18). After screening diverse RTs and establishing a PE–PACE selection system, they evolved both RT and Cas9, improving the editing activity of compact RTs by up to 22-fold and demonstrating that specific RTs exhibit optimal performance depending on the type of edit. PE6 variants showed efficiency comparable to or higher than the optimized PEmax while reducing the coding size by up to 810 bp, making them suitable for dual-AAV delivery (24, 25). These optimized PE6 systems improved therapeutic editing in primary human T cells and patient-derived fibroblasts and increased loxP insertion in mouse brain tissue by up to 24-fold, thereby providing direct in vivo evidence for AAV-mediated prime editing.

PE7: integration of pegRNA stabilization and cellular determinants

PE7 is a next-generation system that integrates pegRNA stabilization with regulation of cellular determinants in addition to protein optimization (Fig. 1, bottom) (17). The stability of the pegRNA 3’–end was established as a key limiting factor for prime editing (26, 27). Genome-scale CRISPRi screening identified the small RNA-binding protein La as the most potent positive regulator of prime editing efficiency (17). La binds to the polyuridylated 3’–end of pegRNA and maintains its stability, promoting the intended editing event. Based on this mechanism, fusion of the RNA-binding N-terminal domain of La to the prime editor significantly improved editing efficiency across diverse targets, edit types, cell lines, and delivery methods while maintaining low indel levels. PE7 also showed high efficiency in disease-related targets, primary human T cells, and hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, particularly when combined with synthetic pegRNAs that ensure La accessibility (17).

Recent studies focused on overcoming intrinsic limitations of prime editing, including low intracellular editing efficiency, restricted editable range, and inefficient delivery (Fig. 2).

Strategies for improving editing efficiency

Optimization of pegRNA design, including PBS length, target sequence GC content, and RT template length, strongly determines editing efficiency (8, 27-29). Large-scale library screening has identified optimal PBS ranges and structural constraints, and deep learning-based models for predicting pegRNA efficiency have been developed (29). AI-driven design incorporating secondary structure stability, RT template synthesis feasibility, off-target potential, and cell type-specific success probability reduces experimental iterations and increases success rates (19, 29-33). Auto-inhibitory secondary structures within pegRNA can limit RT accessibility, and their removal enhances editing efficiency (34). Attachment of a pseudoknot structure to the 3’–end protects pegRNA from exonuclease degradation and increases intracellular stability (Fig. 2A) (26). pegRNA is susceptible to degradation by endogenous exonucleases such as TREX1 in vivo, which limits editing efficiency. Structural RNA motifs such as evopreQ1 further enhance pegRNA stability by protecting it from exonuclease-mediated degradation. Generating a circularized form of the pegRNA using ribozyme sequences can protect the RNA from exonuclease-mediated degradation and thereby enhance prime editing efficiency (Fig. 2B) (35, 36). Regulation of flap processing and DNA repair factors is another key strategy. Fusion of 5’–flap exonucleases to the prime editor biases flap competition toward incorporation of the designed 3’–flap (Fig. 2C) (37). Recent development of nick-relaxing Cas9 variants reduced indel formation by enhancing selective degradation of the 5’–strand, achieving edit-to-indel ratios of several hundred to one without external modulation of cellular pathways (Fig. 2D) (38). In other cases, prime editing efficiency was increased by regulating DNA repair factors. Simultaneous inhibition of DNA–PK and Polθ (2iPE) increases precise editing efficiency, identifying pharmacological modulation of DNA repair pathways as a broadly applicable strategy (Fig. 2E) (39). Very recently, generative AI and protein language models have emerged as core engineering approaches for genome editing (Fig. 2F) (40, 41). PLM-based in silico directed evolution (EVOLVEpro) rapidly improves editor activity with minimal experimental data (42). De novo design of compact MLH1 binders using RFdiffusion and AlphaFold3 selectively inhibits MMR complex formation without increasing cellular toxicity and enables direct fusion to the prime editor (43). Rational redesign of the HNH domain and RT and La-based stabilization strategies expand the editing window and enable high efficiency even under PE2 conditions with minimal indels (44).

Expansion of the editable target range

The nicking position generated by the Cas9 nickase and PAM-dependent targeting intrinsically constrain the accessible editing window of prime editors. Recently, multiple studies have substantially expanded these targeting constraints through the use of engineered CRISPR modules and modified prime-editing components (Fig. 2G-I). Introduction of PAM-relaxed Cas9 variants such as SpRY (45) and SpCas9-NG (46) markedly expands the targetable range of prime editing (47). Target range expansion through the use of orthologs such as CjCas9 (48) and LbCas12a (35) further diversifies PAM recognition for prime editors (Fig. 2G, H). Prime editing systems based on Cas variants that generate nicks at diverse positions enable additional targeting flexibility (38, 49). pegRNA designs that induce nicking on the targeting strand instead of the non-target strand provide an alternative strategy for expanding the editable range (Fig. 2I) (44).

Large-scale genomic editing

The most innovative expansion of prime editing is kilobase-scale DNA insertion (Fig. 2J-M) (50). Paired pegRNA strategies such as PRIME-Del (51) and twinPE (52) generate complementary flaps that enable precise large deletions, replacements, and insertions with high precision compared with homology-directed repair (Fig. 2J-L). These approaches demonstrate potential for modifying genetic information across a substantially broader genomic scope than with a single prime editor (51, 53-63). Another strategy for large-scale DNA modification involves the sequential integration of donor DNA via site-specific recombinases following prime editing-mediated landing pad installation, enabling multi-kilobase DNA insertion in human cells and highlighting the potential for in vivo correction of full-length functional genes (Fig. 2M) (64). Through extensive engineering of their individual components, these technologies have achieved substantial improvements in both efficiency and precision, enabling their application across diverse biological systems, including human-derived cells, for the precise and versatile rewriting of genetic information at a broad genomic scale (54, 65-67).

Viral delivery systems

AAV has been the most widely utilized platform for in vivo delivery owing to its established clinical safety and favorable tissue tropism (68). However, the exact packaging limit (∼4.7 kb) of AAV as the primary biological bottleneck for PE systems (which typically exceed 6.0 kb). Due to the limited cargo capacity of the AAV necessitates split-intein (69) or dual-AAV (70, 71) vector strategies to accommodate prime editor components. Recently, optimized dual-AAV systems achieved therapeutically relevant editing efficiencies in mouse brain, liver, and heart without detectable toxicity (24, 25).

Non-viral delivery systems

Alternatively, engineered virus-like particles (VLPs) can transport prime editors as ribonucleoprotein complexes, enabling transient expression and improving biosafety (72). Non-viral approaches such as lipid nanoparticles enable repeat dosing, transient expression, and unrestricted cargo size (73, 74). Co-delivery of prime editor mRNA and chemically stabilized pegRNA enables efficient in vivo genome editing (75), and the optimization of prime editor ribonucleoproteins and dosing regimens is critical for maximizing editing outcomes (76, 77).

Physical and ex vivo approaches

Other physical delivery methods, such as microinjection (78) and electroporation (79), have also demonstrated efficient induction of prime editing across diverse biological systems, particularly in ex vivo settings.

PRECLINICAL AND CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

From a disease-centric perspective, prime editing has demonstrated broad therapeutic potential by precisely correcting diverse pathogenic variants, including single-nucleotide substitutions and small indels, across multiple genetic disorders. In hematologic disease, the first-in-human application was realized through ex vivo prime editing of autologous CD34＋ hematopoietic stem cells for the treatment of chronic granulomatous disease (CGD), with early clinical reports indicating functional restoration (80). In the ocular field, precise in vivo correction of the pathogenic Rpe65 mutation in the rd12 mouse model of Leber congenital amaurosis restored RPE65 expression and visual function, providing a therapeutic framework for point mutation-driven inherited retinal disorders (24). Applications in the central nervous system and metabolic organs have further demonstrated the installation of protective alleles and correction of disease-associated variants in post-mitotic tissues such as the brain and liver, supporting the feasibility of prime editing in otherwise intractable target organs (25, 81-83). Prime editing has also enabled the precise generation of clinically relevant animal disease models that were previously difficult to establish using conventional genome editing approaches. A representative example is the successful introduction of the HEXA ins TATC mutation associated with Tay–Sachs disease in rabbits, which more faithfully recapitulate human ganglioside metabolism than do mouse models (84). In skeletal muscle disease, high-efficiency correction of a pathogenic Ryanodine Receptor 1 (RYR1) mutation in human myoblasts has provided a proof-of-concept for ex vivo or cell-based therapeutic strategies against RYR1-related myopathies (85). Highly efficient prime editing at the embryonic stage has enabled rapid generation of precise genetic disease models and same-generation phenotypic analysis, underscoring its utility for both mechanistic studies and preclinical therapeutic development (78). Collectively, these studies demonstrate that prime editing is being translated across hematologic, ocular, neurologic, and muscular disorders, and its clinical application in CGD marks a pivotal transition from preclinical validation to therapeutic genome surgery in humans.

CONCLUSIONS

Prime editing presents a new paradigm in precision genome engineering. Although remarkable progress has been achieved in efficiency, target range, large-scale editing, and delivery, major challenges remain, including delivery cost, efficiency in vivo, cell-type-dependent variability, pegRNA instability, off-target editing, and immune responses (86, 87). Continued advances in structure-guided protein engineering, machine learning-based RNA design, compact prime editors, and tissue-specific delivery platforms will promote the clinical translation of prime editing as a precision gene therapy technology.
