Molecular analysis of polymyxin B resistance in clinical carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates from a large tertiary care teaching hospital in Southwest China
Authors: Siying Wu, Quanfeng Liao, Ya Liu, Jin Deng, Weili Zhang, Mei Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the molecular basis of polymyxin B resistance in clinical carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) isolates.MethodsA total of 52 polymyxin B-resistant CRKP clinical isolates were collected at West China Hospital between 2018 and 2021. Polymyxin B susceptibility testing was performed using the broth microdilution method. Whole-genome sequencing was conducted to determine sequence types (STs), capsular loci, resistance genes, virulence genes and mutations in genes associated with polymyxin B resistance. Transcriptome sequencing was used to compare changes in gene expression between selected high-minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) isolates after polymyxin B exposure and controls cultured without polymyxin B.ResultsThe majority (98.08%, 51/52) of CRKP isolates produced carbapenemases, predominantly KPC-2 (92.16%, 47/51). ST11 (88.46%, 46/52) and capsular type KL64 (71.15%, 37/52) were the most prevalent. Eleven isolates (21.15%) were found to carry genetic markers associated with hypervirulence, including rmpA, rmpA2, iucABCD, iutA and peg-344. Genetic alterations in the mgrB gene were identified in 80.77% (42/52) of the polymyxin B- resistant isolates. These alterations were primarily mediated by insertion sequences (88.10%, 37/42), with IS5 family elements being the most common. Isolates with mgrB alterations exhibited significantly higher polymyxin B MICs (geometric mean 28.51 mg/L, MIC₅₀ 32 mg/L, MIC₉₀ 64 mg/L) than those without (geometric mean 11.31 mg/L, MIC₅₀ 12 mg/L, MIC₉₀ 32 mg/L) (P = 0.003). Several amino acid substitutions were identified in polymyxin B resistance-related proteins, including D86E, E57G, and A41T in PmrA; G345E, T157P, and L213M in PmrB; D191A in PhoP; A63E and A20S in PhoQ; and S257L and M54I in PmrC. Notably, some mutations, such as M54I in PmrC and A20S and A63E in PhoQ, have not been previously reported. No mcr genes were detected. Transcriptomic analysis of isolates P40 (MIC > 64 mg/L) revealed upregulation of pmrA (log₂FC = 1.02, adjusted P < 0.001), but downregulation of ompA (log₂FC = -2.85), arnA (log₂FC = -1.46), ugd (log₂FC = -1.01) and tolC (log₂FC = -1.66) (adjusted P < 0.001 for all) after polymyxin B exposure.ConclusionsIn southwest China, polymyxin B resistance in the studied CRKP isolates was most frequently associated with chromosomal mutations, particularly insertional inactivation of the mgrB gene. The emergence of polymyxin B- and carbapenem-resistant ST11-KL64 hypervirulent clone is a potential threat to antiinfection treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05116-2.

Background

The dissemination of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) is an emerging global threat [1, 2]. Among CRE, carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) poses a major challenge due to its extensively drug-resistant phenotypes and potential for rapid spread in health care settings, with a remarkable impact on morbidity and mortality [2–4].

The polymyxins (polymyxin B and colistin) were introduced into clinical practice in the 1950s [5]. However, due to concerns regarding neurotoxicity and nephrotoxicity, their use was restricted in 1980s and limited to agriculture and graziery [6]. With the emergence of multidrug-resistant gram- negative bacteria, particularly CRE all over the world, clinicians have been forced to turn to polymyxins once again as a last-line therapy for infections caused by CRKP due to the scarcity of new antibiotics. As one of the few classes of antimicrobials that remain active against CRKP, polymyxins are a key component of the combination antimicrobial regimens used for the treatment of severe CRKP infections [2, 7]. Consequently, polymyxin B was approved for human use by the China Food and Drug Administration in 2017.

Polymyxins disrupt the outer membrane integrity of gram-negative bacteria, an action that, in addition to providing rapid bactericidal activity, may enhance the efficacy of other antibiotic classes [5]. The disruption relies on the interaction with the lipid A of the lipopolysaccharide (LPS). In response, bacteria have developed resistance primarily through modification of the LPS. This modification involve chromosome-mediated regulatory pathways and plasmid-borne mobile colistin resistance (mcr) genes. Mutations of pmrAB and phoPQ encoding two-component systems or their regulators (e.g., mgrB) can lead to the increased addition of 4-amino-4-deoxy-L-arabinose (L-Ara4N) and phosphoethanolamine (PEtN) to lipid A. Alternatively, the MCR protein, a PEtN transferase encoded by the mcr gene, catalyzes the addition of PEtN to lipid A, resulting in a more cationic LPS molecule [8].

However, the reintroduction of polymyxins has led to a corresponding rise in resistance. The emergence of polymyxin-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) has been frequently reported and is a matter of major concern [9–13]. In our hospital, K. pneumoniae exhibits the highest polymyxin B resistance rate among clinical CRE isolates, reaching 11% in intensive care units—a figure consistent with the 2024 China Antimicrobial Surveillance Network report (11.2%). While previous studies at our hospital - the largest hospital in Southwest China, with 4,300 hospital beds - have largely focused on colistin resistance among clinical K. pneumoniae isolates and have been limited to case reports, systematic investigations into polymyxin B resistance are still lacking [14, 15]. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive genomic analysis of 52 polymyxin B-resistant isolates collected from 2018 to 2021.

Methods

Clinical isolates

A total of 52 polymyxin B-resistant CRKP isolates, collected from 52 distinct patients at West China Hospital between August 2018 and December 2021, were included in this study. One polymyxin B-susceptible non-CRKP isolate (P8) was included as a reference for comparative genomic analysis. All isolates were identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (Bruker, Bremen, Germany).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Vitek 2® system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France) with AST-GN67 and XN04 cards. Polymyxin B susceptibility was determined by the broth microdilution method using commercial panels (BIO-KONT®, Wenzhou, China) in cation-adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth. Results were interpreted according to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) breakpoints (version 14.0, 2024) (susceptible ≤ 2 mg/L, resistant > 2 mg/L). Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 and Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 served as quality control strains.

Whole genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq X10 platform (San Diego, CA, USA) with 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads and an average insert size of 350 bp. The resulting reads were quality-trimmed using Sickle (GitHub) prior to de novo assembly with SPAdes v3.8. Assembly quality was assessed using QUAST, yielding an average N50 of 112,500 bp (range: 80,000–150,000 bp), an average L50 of 9 (range: 6–12), and an average genome coverage of > 100×. Genome completeness was estimated to be > 98% for all isolates based on BUSCO analysis. The assembled genomes were annotated using the RAST [16]. Sequence types (STs) were determined with the multilocus sequence typing (MLST) tool from the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (http://genomicepidemiology.org), while resistance genes and capsular types were identified using ResFinder (https://cge.cbs.dtu.dk/services/ResFinder/) and Kaptive (https://github.com/katholt/Kaptive), respectively. Virulence genes were predicted via the VFanalyzer tool (http://www.mgc.ac.cn/cgi-bin/VFs/v5/main.cgi). Contigs from the 11 isolates carrying genetic markers associated with hypervirulence were aligned against the reference virulence plasmid pLVPK (GenBank accession no. NC_005249.1) - a 208,166-bp plasmid harboring rmpA, rmpA2, iucABCD, iutA, iroBCDN and peg-344, - using BRIG v0.95 software.

Mutations in polymyxin B resistance genes

Mutations in genes associated with polymyxin B resistance (mgrB, pmrA/pmrB/pmrC, and phoP/phoQ) were identified by alignment with reference genome K. pneumoniae subsp. pneumoniae MGH78578 (GenBank accession number NC_009648.1), the susceptible isolate P8 (MIC=1 mg/L) and sequences of wild-type alleles obtained from GenBank (accession number MG243718.1, MG243719.1, MF431844.1, MG243705.1, MG243706.1 MG243712.1 and MG243713.1) [17]. Insertion sequence (IS) elements were screened using the ISfinder database (https://www-is.biotoul.fr/) and confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification with previously published primers [18]. Plasmid-mediated mobile genes (mcr-1 to mcr-10) were detected by aligning the assembled genomes against reference sequences from the NCBI database. The functional impact of amino acid substitutions was predicted using the PROVEAN tool v1.1.5 (http://provean.jcvi.org/index.php) [19]. PROVEAN score ≤ − 2.5 was deleterious for protein function, and a score > − 2.5 was considered to have a neutral effect on protein function.

Transcriptome sequencing

Two isolates, P3 and P40, exhibiting high polymyxin B MICs (> 64 mg/L), were randomly selected for transcriptome sequencing. The experimental group was cultured in medium supplemented with 64 mg/L polymyxin B for 24 h, while strains cultured without polymyxin B served as the control group, with three biological replicates in each.

Total RNA was extracted, and ribosomal RNA was depleted. The RNA was then fragmented, and first-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamers. During the second strand synthesis, dUTPs were replaced with dTTPs in the reaction buffer. Directional libraries were constructed through end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, size selection, USER enzyme digestion, PCR amplification, and purification. Library quality was assessed by Qubit quantification, real-time PCR, and bioanalyzer profiling. Finally, qualified libraries were pooled based on effective concentration and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Differentially expressed genes were identified using a threshold of |log₂FC| > 1 (equivalent to a 2-fold change) and an adjusted P-value < 0.05.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using the Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis H test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Antimicrobial susceptibility

Among the 52 CRKP isolates, 51 were positive for carbapenemase. The remaining isolate, P13, which was carbapenemase-negative, exhibited susceptibility to imipenem and meropenem but resistance to ertapenem. This isolate produced the DHA-1 β-lactamase and may possess additional resistance mechanisms, such as altered membrane permeability. Among the 51 carbapenemase-producing isolates, K. pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC) -2 (92.16%, 47/51) was the predominant carbapenemase, followed by NDM-5 (5.88%, 3/51). Two isolates (3.92%) co-produced two carbapenemase types (NDM-5 and OXA-1). Isolate P36 carried a blaKPC−2 variant, blaKPC−86, which harbored an A533G mutation resulting in a D179G substitution in the protein sequence. Other carbapenemase genes, including blaOXA−48, blaVIM and blaIMP were not detected in any of the isolates (see Additional file 1).

Molecular typing and virulence analysis

Among the 52 K. pneumoniae isolates, seven STs were identified. The most prevalent ST was ST11 (88.46%, 46/52), while the remaining isolates belonged to ST15, ST16, ST37, ST307, ST656, and ST709. A total of eight capsular types were found, with KL64 (71.15%, 37/52) being the most common, followed by KL47 (17.31%, 9/52). Eleven isolates carried genetic markers associated with hypervirulence and showed alignment to most regions of the pLVPK plasmid, except for the region containing the iroBCD locus (Fig. 1). With the exception of one ST15-KL64 isolate, all hypervirulent isolates belonged to the ST11-KL64 clone (see Additional file 1).

Mutations in polymyxin B resistance genes

The polymyxin B MICs for the 52 isolates ranged from 4 to > 64 mg/L, with a median of 32 mg/L and an MIC90 of 64 mg/L. Of these isolates, 50 (96.15%) tested positive for the mgrB gene, while two (3.85%) were mgrB-negative. Among the 50 mgrB-positive isolates, 37 (74.00%) produced amplicons larger than the normal size of the mgrB gene, suggesting the presence of insertions within the coding region. The most frequently identified insertion sequence was an IS903B-like element from the IS5 family, found in 15 (40.54%) isolates. This was followed by ISKpn14 (8, 21.62%), ISKpn26-like (6, 16.22%), ISKpn26 (3, 8.11%), ISKpn18 (3, 8.11%), IS5-like (1, 2.71%), and ISEc68 (1, 2.71%). In two isolates (3.85%), a point mutation introduced a premature stop codon at position 30, thereby producing a truncated protein of 29 amino acids, in contrast to the 47-amino-acid wild-type protein. One isolate harbored a single-nucleotide insertion (A) between nucleotides 3 and 11, resulting in a frameshift and a non-functional truncated protein of 25 amino acids. The remaining 10 isolates carried a wild-type mgrB gene (see Additional file 2).

Eleven amino acid substitutions were identified relative to the reference strain MGH78578 and susceptible isolates. These included D86E, E57G, and A41T in PmrA; G345E, T157P, and L213M in PmrB; D191A in PhoP; A63E and A20S in PhoQ; and S257L and M54I in PmrC. According to PROVEAN analysis, the substitutions D86E, T157P, D191A, A63E, and S257L were predicted to be deleterious, whereas E57G, A41T, G345E, L213M, A20S, and M54I were classified as neutral (see Additional file 2). Furthermore, none of the isolates carried mcr genes.

The distribution of polymyxin B MICs differed significantly between isolates with mgrB alterations (n = 42) and those without mgrB alterations (n = 10) (P = 0.003). Specifically, strains with mgrB alterations exhibited markedly higher MIC values: the geometric mean MIC was 28.51 mg/L compared to 11.31 mg/L in strains without mgrB alterations. Similarly, the MIC₅₀ (32 vs. 12 mg/L), MIC₉₀ (64 vs. 32 mg/L), and MIC range (4 to > 64 vs. 4 to 32 mg/L) were all substantially higher in the mgrB-altered group. Among the 9 isolates with the highest MICs (≥ 64 mg/L), the majority (7/9, 77.8%) harbored insertion sequences within mgrB, while one isolate each (11.1%) carried a frameshift mutation producing a non-functional truncated protein or had a complete deletion of the mgrB gene. No statistically significant differences in MIC values were observed among the various IS types (IS903B-like, ISKpn14, ISKpn26, ISKpn26-like and ISKpn18, P = 0.791).

Transcriptome sequencing

In isolate P40, five genes associated with polymyxin B resistance showed significant differential expression: pmrA was upregulated (log₂FC = 1.02, adjusted P < 0.001), while arnA (log₂FC = -1.46), ugd (log₂FC = -1.01), tolC (log₂FC = -1.66), and ompA (log₂FC = -2.85) were downregulated (adjusted P < 0.001 for all).

In isolate P3, the same expression trends were observed, with tolC and ompA showing significant downregulation (adjusted P < 0.001 and P = 0.001, respectively), although the fold changes were less pronounced. A summary of the expression data for both isolates is provided in Additional file 3 (Table S3).

Discussion

In this study, most of the CRKP isolates were found to produce carbapenemases, with KPC-2 being the most prevalent type, which was consistent with previous studies from China [13, 20–22]. We also identified a blaKPC−2 variant, blaKPC−86. This variant was originally reported in a ceftazidime-avibactam-resistant K. pneumoniae isolate derived from a KPC-2-producing CRKP via in vitro induction [23]. The molecular epidemiology of polymyxin-resistant K. pneumoniae exhibits distinct geographic heterogeneity. Specifically, ST11 is dominant in China [11–13, 24], whereas ST258 is the most prevalent clone in Greece, Brazil, and United States [9, 10, 25, 26]. ST231 and ST383 are the predominant types in Egypt and India, respectively [27–29]. Since 2016, the ST11-KL64 clone has progressively replaced ST11-KL47 to become the most prevalent hypervirulent CRKP lineage in China [30]. Furthermore, several nosocomial outbreaks caused by polymyxin- and carbapenem-resistant ST11-KL64 hypervirulent K. pneumoniae have been documented. These findings underscore the necessity for enhanced clinical surveillance of ST11-KL64 clones and the implementation of effective infection control measures to prevent their dissemination [31, 32].

MgrB, a small transmembrane protein with 47 amino acids mediates potent negative feedback on the PhoQ/PhoP regulatory system and its inactivation results in PhoP/PhoQ activation which in turn activates the PmrA response regulator. mgrB gene alterations were identified in most polymyxin-resistant K. pneumoniae isolates, primarily involving disruptions caused by insertion sequences, deletions, premature stop codons, and frameshift mutations [9, 24, 33]. Among these, insertion sequences from the IS5 family—particularly ISKpn26 and IS903B—were the most prevalent [33–35]. Complementation with wild type MgrB protein restored colistin susceptibility in resistant isolates with mgrB modifications [17, 25, 36]. This verifies the resistance conferring role of mgrB alterations.

Jayol A et al. reported that the amino acid substitution (T157P) in PmrB protein was responsible for the resistance phenotype by expression and complementation assays [18]. However, complementation with wild type pmrB gene in isolates with L213M substitution was not able to restore colistin susceptibility [17]. When the mutated genes phoP (P47L and A95S), phoQ (N253T, T281M, G385C and V446G) and pmrB (T140P) were introduced into a polymyxin B-susceptible isolate, only complementation with phoQ (T281M, G385C) conferred a consistent polymyxin B-resistant phenotype, whereas isolates carrying the other mutations remained polymyxin B-susceptible [25]. Zhu Y et al. [26]. found that colistin-susceptible isolates had a mutated phoQ compared to their reference genomes, while the corresponding colistin-resistant isolates obtained from the same patients after colistin treatment contained either an intact or a secondary mutation in phoQ. This indicated that certain genetic variations in phoP, phoQ, pmrA and pmrB may not contribute to polymyxin resistance, while other genetic variations may lead to polymyxin resistance. Notably, several previously unreported mutations in pmrC (M54I) and phoQ (A20S and A63E) were identified in our study. Among these, the A63E mutation in phoQ was predicted to be deleterious by PROVEAN analysis. Whether these amino acid changes confer a resistance phenotype warrants further investigations.

In our study, three of the 42 isolates containing an mgrB variant were accompanied by one or more missense mutations in pmrABC. Pitt ME et al. [25] reported several mutations co-segregating with the IS element-disrupted mgrB, including phoP (P74L, A95S), phoQ (N253T, V446G) and pmrB (T140P). Complementation with wild-type phoP, phoQ and pmrB in these isolates resulted in a ≥ 2-fold increase in MIC. Furthermore, when the mutated phoP, phoQ and pmrB were introduced into a strain containing only the mgrB IS element disruption, all variants except pmrB (T140P) caused an apparent reduction in MIC, suggesting that several phoPQ variants co-segregating with the mgrB insertion are potential partial suppressor mutations. It remains unclear whether the PmrCAB system is involved in similar suppressive regulation among the pmrCAB variants co-segregating with mgrB disruption in this study.

Three isolates (P13, P36 and P43) harbored no detectable mutations in mgrB, pmrA, pmrB, pmrC, phoP and phoQ (Additional file 2). The basis for resistance in these isolates remains unclear. However, polymyxin resistance in K. pneumoniae can involve multiple pathways beyond mgrB inactivation and pmrAB/phoPQ mutations. Cheng et al. [37] identified mutations in the CrrAB two-component regulatory system that were associated with overexpression of pmrHFIJKLM and pmrCAB operons in colistin-resistant isolates. Therefore, it is plausible that these three isolates harbor resistance-conferring mutations in alternative regulatory systems not analyzed in our study.

None of the K. pneumoniae isolates carried mcr genes, indicating that polymyxin B resistance among these isolates was primarily mediated by chromosomal mutations, a finding consistent with previous reports [9, 13, 38, 39]. The currently low prevalence of mcr genes may be attributed to the withdrawal of polymyxins as a growth promoter in food animals [40]. In China, agricultural sales of colistin declined approximately ninefold between 2015 and 2018, while colistin resistance and the prevalence of mcr-1 carriers also fell sharply, with reductions of roughly eight- to tenfold [41–43]. The prevalence of colistin-resistant E. coli and Salmonella in patients as well as carriage of mcr-1-positive E. coli, also showed an obvious decline from 2017 [41, 43, 44]. These observations indicate that the extensive use of colistin in animals served as a major selective force driving the dissemination of colistin resistance and the mcr-1 gene [40]. Furthermore, the mcr-1 gene may also affect the activity of the novel polymyxin B derivative SPR206 (also known as EVER206). Zhang Y et al. [45] reported that SPR206 exhibited reduced activity against colistin-resistant isolates, and all strains carrying the mcr-1 gene showed elevated MICs to SPR206.

In our study, isolates with mgrB alterations exhibited significantly higher polymyxin B MICs than those with a wild-type mgrB gene. Among isolates with mgrB alterations caused by insertion sequences, with the exception of one isolate harboring IS903B-like (MIC 4 mg/L) and one harboring ISKpn18 (MIC 8 mg/L), all remaining isolates showed MICs of ≥ 16 mg/L. Strains with frameshift, premature stop, and deletion mutations also exhibited MIC values ≥ 16 mg/L, but the number of such strains was limited. According to literature reports, the polymyxin B or colistin MIC values for strains carrying the C88T (Q30stop) mutation, frameshift mutations, and complete deletion mutations ranged from 8 to > 128 mg/L, 8 to 32 mg/L, and 8 to 64 mg/L, respectively [10, 11, 17, 24, 28, 36, 46]. Previous studies have reported variable associations between specific IS types and polymyxin B or colistin MIC levels. For instance, Martins et al. [10] reported that IS903B-like insertions were associated with MICs ranging from 128 to > 128 mg/L, while Zhu et al. [35] reported different MIC ranges (32 to 64 mg/L) for the same insertion sequence. The different MIC values among isolates with identical genetic alterations in the studied loci can be attributed to alterations in other unknown loci or involvement of other resistance mechanisms, such as increased amount of capsular polysaccharide or efflux pumps [47, 48].

In polymyxin-resistant strains, the expression levels of phoP, phoQ, pmrK, pmrA, pmrB, and pmrC were upregulated compared to susceptible wild-type strains [17, 24, 46, 49]. Zhang H et al. [50] reported that polymyxin B monotherapy can induce resistance within 1–2 days, whereas combination with amikacin or tigecycline delays its emergence by up to 6 days. RNA-seq, quantitative reverse transcription PCR further revealed significant upregulation of AcrAB-TolC efflux pump genes in strains with rapidly induced resistance, in contrast to the downregulation observed in strains with delayed resistance under combination therapy. Consistent with these, our study observed upregulation of pmrA and downregulation of tolC in polymyxin B-resistant strains after being incubated with polymyxin B for 24 h.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although we detected previously unreported mutations in pmrC and phoQ, their functional consequences remain unknown and require further investigation. Second, the transcriptomic analysis was performed on only two high-MIC isolates, which may not fully capture the heterogeneity of resistance-associated gene expression profiles among the entire collection.

Conclusions

In this study, the ST11-KL64 clone was the predominant lineage among polymyxin B-resistant CRKP isolates in Southwest China, and a subset of these isolates carried genetic markers associated with hypervirulence. mgrB alterations, primarily insertional inactivation by IS5 family insertion sequences, were the most frequent genetic variations associated with polymyxin B resistance and correlated with higher MIC levels. Previously unreported mutations in pmrC and phoQ were identified but require functional validation. These findings fill a regional knowledge gap and highlight the urgent need for enhanced surveillance of this emerging high-risk clone.
