Implementation determinants of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices in US health systems: a qualitative descriptive study of nurse leaders
Authors: Hyunmin Yu, April J Ancheta, Dalmacio Dennis Flores, Stephen Bonett, Steven Meanley, Seul Ki Choi, José Arturo Bauermeister
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesDespite efforts to implement lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer or questioning, and other sexual and gender diverse (LGBTQ+) inclusive practices to address health disparities faced by LGBTQ+ individuals, factors that facilitate the uptake of these practices remain underexplored. Informed by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), this study explores nurse leaders’ perspectives across diverse US healthcare systems regarding the facilitators and barriers to implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices.DesignWe used a qualitative descriptive design. Semistructured interviews guided by the CFIR framework were conducted from October to December 2023. The data were analysed using thematic analysis.SettingDiverse healthcare settings (eg, acute care hospitals and public health centres) across the USA.ParticipantsWe purposively recruited 21 nurse leaders, such as chief nursing officers or chief nurse executives, who oversee nursing strategy, staffing and quality across their organisations.ResultsConsistent with prior frontline-focused studies, nurse leaders confirmed key inner setting and individuals facilitators (eg, LGBTQ+ specific training, electronic health record adaptation, visible executive engagement). Our findings add system-level detail from an executive perspective. Leaders identified actionable levers such as establishing LGBTQ+ clinical and social services, allocating protected time and budgets, and deploying dedicated implementation teams. We also identified a cross-cutting barrier: a reactive, crisis-driven organisational culture that hinders proactive inclusion efforts. Beyond the organisation, sociopolitical and legal climates shaped readiness and resourcing, with anti-LGBTQ+ laws influencing inclusion initiatives. Finally, nurse leaders highlighted the need for rigorous multilevel evaluation (eg, patient, staff, institution) and noted that common surveys inadequately capture LGBTQ+ inclusion, revealing measurement gaps that impede continuous improvement.ConclusionsImplementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices in healthcare is essential for optimal health outcomes and social justice. Understanding the context of implementation at multiple levels is crucial. Future research should focus on testing implementation strategies, developing inclusive healthcare surveys, and supporting the role of organisational culture and leadership in promoting LGBTQ+ inclusivity.Keywords: Sexual and Gender Minorities, Health Equity, Nursing research

Introduction

Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer or questioning, and other sexual and gender diverse (LGBTQ+) individuals constitute an average of 9% of adults across 30 countries.1 Despite social progress over the past several decades, they continue to face unique health disparities and challenges when accessing healthcare, including discrimination, stigmatisation, and a lack of culturally responsive care,2 3 and experience higher rates of negative mental health issues, substance misuse, sexually transmitted infections, cancer-related outcomes, victimisation and cardiovascular diseases compared with non-LGBTQ+ counterparts.3 Moreover, the fear of anticipated discrimination may lead to healthcare avoidance or delay seeking necessary healthcare.4 5

Discrimination against LGBTQ+ individuals in healthcare settings can manifest in various ways. Interpersonal discrimination against LGBTQ+ patients in healthcare encompasses various forms of mistreatment, such as the use of insensitive or offensive language, judgement, microaggressions, bias and even instances of sexual harassment or violence perpetrated by healthcare workers.5,7 Structural discrimination or structural stigma for LGBTQ+ patients refers to systemic barriers and policies that disadvantage individuals based on sexual orientation or gender identity. This includes denial of appropriate healthcare services and difficulties in accessing health insurance. Structural stigma can foster environments that perpetuate unequal policies and practices across health systems.8 9 Recognising the necessity to combat individual-level and system-level discrimination, there is a crucial need to prioritise inclusive practices for LGBTQ+ populations in the healthcare system.

Guided by recommendations from national organisations,10 11 healthcare institutions in the USA have gradually recognised the significance of implementing organisational-level LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. Key components in LGBTQ+ inclusive practices include (a) recognising and affirming diverse sexual orientations and gender identities through the systematic collection of these identities in health records, (b) acknowledging that LGBTQ+ individuals have specific health needs and experiences that necessitate tailored care, with a strong emphasis on creating safe, welcoming and affirming environments and (c) providing clinically and culturally responsive healthcare services, including routine screenings, preventive care and treatment options that address the unique health concerns of LGBTQ+ individuals such as gender-affirming care and HIV prevention and treatment.3 10 12 13

The absence of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices in healthcare settings contributes to a non-affirming clinical environment for LGBTQ+ individuals, further increasing their vulnerability to stigmatisation and discrimination.14 15 The implementation of these inclusive practices holds significant potential to greatly impact the healthcare experiences and outcomes of LGBTQ+ individuals.3 16 However, the persistent reports of discrimination against LGBTQ+ individuals in healthcare2 5 may indicate the existence of barriers to the implementation of inclusive practices within the healthcare system.

To systematically assess implementation determinants within the healthcare system, including facilitators and barriers, an implementation science framework is essential. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) was developed as a comprehensive typology that consolidates theories to elucidate the determinants of implementation, aiming to expedite the translation of interventions into effective healthcare practices.17 An updated version of the framework (CFIR 2.0) includes five domains: innovation, inner setting, outer setting, individuals and implementation process.18 The innovation domain focuses on the specific practice or intervention being implemented, in this case, LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. The inner setting domain refers to the specific setting where the innovation is implemented, such as a hospital. The outer setting domain refers to the broader context in which the inner setting exists, such as a state. The individuals domain pertains to the roles and characteristics of the individuals involved in implementing the intervention. Lastly, the implementation process domain encompasses the activities and strategies used to implement the innovation effectively.18

The CFIR framework emphasises capturing the perceptions of facilitators and barriers to an intervention or practice from multiple perspectives, including those involved in the design, implementation and those directly affected by it.17 18 Online supplemental material 1 summarises qualitative studies, identified in PubMed using MeSH terms (eg, sexual and gender minorities, health personnel) and keywords (eg, facilitators, barriers), assessing facilitators and barriers to components of LGBTQ+ inclusive practice, including cultural competency training and the collection of sexual orientation and gender identity (SOGI) data.19,29 Despite the importance of multiple perspectives, most studies have focused on frontline healthcare providers, and leadership perspectives remain underexamined, even though CFIR encourages inclusion of individuals with power and influence over implementation outcomes.18 Learning from healthcare leaders is vital because they play a crucial role in shaping the formal organisation-wide policies within their respective organisations. The limited leadership-focused studies to date are largely confined to federally qualified health centres or community mental and behavioural health centres in the USA.19,21 Additionally, there is a lack of studies that explore the perceptions of nurse leaders across different healthcare systems.

Examining nurse leaders’ perspectives on implementation determinants of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices can yield concrete guidance for policy, workflow and resource allocation at the system level. As stewards of the largest healthcare workforce, nurse leaders set priorities and control levers that directly affect adoption and sustainment.30 31 They shape staffing models (eg, protected time for training and champions), establish training requirements (eg, orientation and annual competencies), govern electronic health record (EHR) documentation standards (eg, the placement and visibility of SOGI fields and the use of patients’ names and pronouns across clinical views) and design accountability structures (eg, metrics embedded in quality dashboards, audit and feedback cycles, and leadership performance reviews). By identifying these policy, workflow and resource-allocation determinants and specifying how to operationalise them, nurse leaders’ perspectives reveal multilevel levers that improve the adoption, fidelity and long-term sustainability of LGBTQ+ inclusive care.

In the current US policy climate, LGBTQ+ health and diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) initiatives face heightened political scrutiny and ongoing legal and regulatory shifts.32 Several jurisdictions have introduced or enacted restrictions affecting gender-affirming care, SOGI data collection and use, and DEI infrastructure in healthcare settings, with guidance and enforcement varying by state and over time.33 These dynamics create uncertainty for health systems, shaping policy compliance, workforce training, quality measurement, reimbursement and research activity, and they can deter the adoption of inclusive practices. This study comprehensively examined nurse leaders’ perspectives on barriers and facilitators to implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. The findings provide a pragmatic roadmap for healthcare organisations seeking to implement and sustain inclusive care amid a rapidly changing policy and funding environment.

Methods

Approach and design

The project had two distinct aims: (1) to characterise implementation determinants (eg, facilitators and barriers to LGBTQ+ inclusive practices) from nurse leaders’ perspectives using CFIR 2.0 and (2) to identify implementation strategies using the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change taxonomy by cataloguing, mapping and prioritising concrete actions. This study addresses the first aim; the second has been published elsewhere.34 In brief, this article explains what helps or hinders, whereas the published paper34 specifies what to do. Although both papers draw on the same sample and intervention context (ie, LGBTQ+ inclusive practices) to maintain comparability, they pose different research questions and apply different implementation science lenses. Accordingly, the frameworks, units of analysis and resulting outputs are conceptually distinct.

For this study, we used a qualitative descriptive design to explore nurse leaders’ perspectives on the facilitators and barriers to implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. Individual semistructured interviews were conducted with diverse nurse leaders across the USA. Data were analysed using a coding reliability thematic analysis method.35,37 Online supplemental material 2 presents the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines, which were used to ensure rigorous and comprehensive reporting in this qualitative study.

Sample and setting

The study sought to recruit senior nursing leaders from a variety of healthcare institutions, including children’s hospitals, public health centres, community and academic hospitals, and Veterans Affairs facilities. Targeted roles included (1) C-suite nurse leaders (chief nursing officers (CNOs) and chief nurse executives (CNEs)), (2) C-suite executives (chief executive officers (CEOs) and chief operating officers (COOs)) and (3) nurse directors who oversee multiple units or departments. In US healthcare organisations, CNOs and CNEs are the most senior nurses responsible for nursing strategy, professional practice standards, budgets and quality and safety. CEOs provide organisation-wide strategic leadership and are accountable for mission, financial performance and external relations. COOs oversee day-to-day operations and translate strategy into practice by coordinating clinical and operational workflows, service line integration, throughput and performance improvement. Nurse directors oversee multiple units or departments and the unit managers who translate organisational nursing strategy into unit-level operations. They typically supervise several service lines and report to the CNO or CNE.

Recruitment covered all four US Census regions (Northeast, Midwest, South and West)38 and included both metropolitan and non-metropolitan areas. We classified counties using the 2023 US Department of Agriculture Rural–Urban Continuum Codes. In this scheme, metropolitan counties are those within an Office of Management and Budget metropolitan area, which contains an urban core of at least 50 000 people. Non-metropolitan counties lie outside metropolitan areas.39

Inclusion criteria were: (1) being a nurse; (2) holding one of the leadership positions listed above, with involvement in designing institutional policies; (3) working in any type of healthcare facility; (4) being located in any US region and (5) being fluent in English.

Recruitment

Purposive and snowball sampling techniques were used to identify potential participants.40 In 2023, the first author gave a brief presentation on this study during a multiday workshop attended by nurse leaders from various healthcare institutions and regions, and he gathered email addresses from these participants. After obtaining Institutional Review Board approval, invitations were sent to nurse leaders via email. Interested individuals underwent a screening process to determine eligibility based on predefined inclusion criteria. Eligible participants were scheduled for semistructured interviews via Zoom. Using a snowball sampling technique, participants were asked if they could refer other eligible nurse leaders. Prior to the interviews, the purpose, methods, benefits and potential effects of the study were explained to the participants. Written informed consent and permission to record the interview were obtained from all the participants. The final number of participants was determined by reaching data saturation, where no new insights emerged from subsequent interviews.41 A total of 136 nurse leaders were invited. 30 responded (22.1%), and 21 agreed to participate (15.4%), including five recruited through snowball sampling.

Data collection

In-depth semistructured individual interviews explored the perspectives of nurse leaders within diverse healthcare systems participating in the study. The first author, a male PhD student at the time of the study with training in qualitative research and a focus on health equity for LGBTQ+ individuals, enrolled participants and led all interviews using Zoom from October to December 2023. Each participant selected a unique pseudonym. Interviews lasted between 31 min and 64 min, with an average length of 53 min. The first author determined that data saturation42 43 was reached after conducting 18 interviews, as no new themes or insights emerged from subsequent interviews. To ensure the robustness of the identified themes, three additional interviews were conducted, which confirmed the saturation point.

For each interview, two main instruments were used: a pilot-tested, semistructured interview guide (online supplemental material 3) and a demographic questionnaire administered via Qualtrics at the interview’s conclusion. The interview guide was collaboratively developed by four authors (HY, DDF, SB and JAB) using the CFIR interview guide tool44 to align questions with our study aims and domains. We pilot tested the guide with one nursing faculty member and two doctoral students, then made minor revisions to wording, sequencing and probe clarity before finalising it. The questionnaire covered participant backgrounds, including age, sexual orientation, gender identity, race, ethnicity, education, leadership role, religion, political affiliation, length of service and years in their current role. Participants received a US$50 gift card as compensation. Field notes were taken immediately after each interview to capture key discussion points and non-verbal behaviours.45 Professional transcriptionists, compliant with Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, transcribed each interview verbatim for subsequent analysis.

Data analysis

Interview transcripts were imported to NVivo (V.14) for analysis. We used thematic analysis35,37 that combined inductive coding with a deductive framework analysis guided by CFIR 2.0.18 In CFIR, a domain is a broad category that groups related determinants, whereas a construct is a specific, theory-informed factor within a domain that can function as a barrier or facilitator to implementation. Within each domain, specific constructs offer more detailed insights into the implementation determinants, serving as more granular elements or components (eg, the local attitudes construct within the outer setting domain). We followed established guidance for thematic analysis, attending to the accuracy and reliability of coding, reflexivity regarding researcher bias, and the transparency and replicability of analytic procedures.35

Inductive coding occurred first. After iterative familiarisation with the data, the first coder (HY) conducted line-by-line open coding and generated 388 content-driven codes that preserved participants’ language and context. HY and a second coder (AJA) met repeatedly to collapse overlapping codes, clarify boundaries and draft operational definitions with inclusion and exclusion criteria, producing a finalised codebook with 46 codes and illustrative examples. To calibrate the application of the codebook, both coders jointly coded two transcripts and then independently coded the remaining interviews. Disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus,46 47 with decision rules documented in an audit trail and analytic memos maintained throughout.

Deductive mapping to CFIR followed the inductive phase. We created a framework matrix with CFIR constructs as columns and cases as rows. Each inductive code and its supporting excerpts were then linked to one or more CFIR constructs on a best-fit basis. Where a code clearly spanned more than one construct, cross-links were recorded. We also noted CFIR constructs for which there was no evidence in our data to avoid overinterpretation. Theme construction proceeded within CFIR domains. We synthesised construct-level patterns into themes and examined variation by leader role (eg, CNO, CNE or nurse director) and organisational context (eg, metropolitan or non-metropolitan location, census region, facility type). Representative quotations were selected to illustrate each construct-level theme and to retain the specificity of leaders’ language.

Reflexivity and positionality

Two coauthors led the data analysis: HY, a male hospital-based nurse practitioner and PhD student at the time of the study whose research centres on LGBTQ+ health equity, and AJA, a female nurse and postdoctoral research fellow focused on the well-being of LGBTQ+ youth. HY’s academic training and clinical practice informed his understanding of the barriers LGBTQ+ individuals encounter in healthcare and helped create an interview environment in which nurse leaders felt comfortable discussing inclusivity. His familiarity with LGBTQ+ health inequities enabled him to pose targeted follow-up questions that enriched the data. AJA’s expertise in LGBTQ+ youth provided a complementary lens during analysis and supported the identification of implementation determinants. Their joint work fostered rigorous team dialogue and sharpened the interpretation of findings.

To support reflexivity, they held ongoing discussions about their own assumptions and potential biases, with the goal of limiting their influence on the study and keeping nurse leaders’ perspectives at the forefront. This reflective approach strengthened the analysis of strategies for implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices in healthcare settings.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in our research’s design, conduct, reporting or dissemination.

Results

Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the 21 participants. The mean age was 53.5 years (SD: 5.5). Most were women (76.2%) and white (85.8%); 28.6% identified as gay or bisexual, and 4.7% as transgender. Educational preparation was advanced, with 85.7% holding doctoral degrees. Roles included 62.0% C-suite positions (eg, CNE, CNO, CEO, COO) and 38.0% nurse directors. Institutions were located in 12 states across all US regions, including South (n=8), Northeast (n=7), Midwest (n=4) and West (n=2), and were predominantly metropolitan (71.4%). Participant demographics

Theme I (innovation): centring human needs as the highest priority

The overarching theme of the innovation domain is prioritising human needs as the highest concern. All participants deemed the implementation of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices crucial due to the practices’ commitment to prioritising human needs and protecting human rights. This involves placing LGBTQ+ individuals at the forefront of decision-making processes, policies and practices within the healthcare system. By centring human needs, the focus shifts to ensuring care is tailored to meet the unique needs of LGBTQ+ individuals. Participants underscore that healthcare should be patient-centred, culturally responsive and inclusive, fostering an affirming environment where individuals feel valued, heard and cared for throughout their healthcare journey.

Facilitator I (innovation relative advantage): patient-centred nature

Participants emphasised that the main advantage of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices is their patient-centred approach. By focusing on patients, these practices ensure equitable and affirming care for individuals of all sexual orientations and gender identities, enhancing care quality and fostering trust between providers and patients. This approach reflects a commitment to holistic well-being and addressing the unique healthcare needs of LGBTQ+ individuals with compassion and sensitivity.

Facilitator II (innovation relative advantage): vital element of health equity

Participants also highlighted that implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices is essential for achieving health equity. They noted that these practices address barriers faced by marginalised communities, ensuring equitable access to quality care for LGBTQ+ individuals. By implementing inclusive practices, healthcare organisations can contribute to reducing disparities and advancing health equity.

Facilitator III (innovation relative advantage): benefits across multiple levels

Participants described multilevel benefits of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices that facilitate their implementation. They noted improved health outcomes and positive experiences for patients, respectful care that builds family trust, and increased job satisfaction and performance for employees. Participants emphasised that these practices also enhance institutional reputation, attract diverse populations and fulfil societal obligations for equitable care, upholding ethical and DEI principles.

Barrier I (innovation complexity): rapid evolution of LGBTQ+ issues

Participants reported that the rapid increase in visibility and complexity of LGBTQ+ issues poses significant implementation barriers. They noted that this complexity stems from emergent LGBTQ+ identities and newer terminologies, which healthcare providers may struggle to understand. Pronoun use presents a unique challenge. Nurse leaders described that persistent changes with pronoun use acknowledgement and guidelines make it difficult for staff and leaders to stay updated. They conveyed the challenge of reconciling the ingrained culture of routinisation and standardisation in healthcare with the evolving landscape of LGBTQ+ issues.

Barrier II (innovation complexity): reactive stance in healthcare

Participants identified the healthcare system’s reactive nature as a barrier. They reported that decisions often follow negative events rather than proactively addressing potential or foreseeable challenges, highlighting the need for systemic shifts towards preventive and inclusive healthcare practices.

Barrier III (innovation complexity): small LGBTQ+ population size

Several participants noted that a small LGBTQ+ population in their region poses an implementation barrier. They described that the limited number of LGBTQ+ individuals accessing healthcare makes it challenging to prioritise tailored programmes, and this smaller population also leads to less awareness and understanding of LGBTQ+ health issues among staff and leaders.

Barrier IV (innovation complexity): lack of standardised best practices

Participants identified the lack of standardised best practices for LGBTQ+ patients as a barrier. They highlighted that without clear national guidelines or evidence-based protocols, healthcare providers struggle to deliver culturally responsive care, leading to inconsistency in care. Several nurse leaders cited patient room placement for transgender patients as one practice lacking clear guidelines.

Theme II (inner setting): organisational flexibility and openness

The overarching theme for the inner setting domain centres on organisational flexibility and openness within healthcare settings. This theme emphasises the need for institutions to adapt and be receptive to change for effective implementation of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. Participants noted that organisational flexibility involves adjusting structures, policies and practices to accommodate LGBTQ+ individuals’ needs, while openness reflects a culture that promotes transparency, communication and collaboration among staff, administrators and patients. They described that such a culture encourages an environment where diverse perspectives are welcomed, discussions on LGBTQ+ inclusion are supported, and continuous learning about LGBTQ+ health issues improves care quality.

Facilitator I (access to knowledge and information): LGBTQ+ training for leaders and staff

Participants unanimously emphasised LGBTQ+ specific training as crucial for implementing inclusive practices in healthcare settings. They underscored that these programmes equip healthcare professionals with knowledge, skills and sensitivity needed to engage effectively with LGBTQ+ patients and deliver culturally responsive care. They highlighted the critical role of training leaders in establishing a culture of inclusivity and ensuring that organisational policies are revised or developed to support LGBTQ+ inclusion.

Facilitator II (information technology infrastructure): EHR adaptation

Participants emphasised that adapting EHR systems facilitates inclusive and tailored care for LGBTQ+ individuals. They noted that EHRs play crucial roles by allowing accurate collection and storage of patient information, including data collection for gender identity, sexual orientation and pronouns, and this enables healthcare providers to better understand and address the unique needs of LGBTQ+ patients. Participants also reported that EHRs enhance communication and coordination among healthcare teams, ensuring all members are informed about patients’ identities and the language with which they would like to be referred.

Facilitator III (work infrastructure): LGBTQ+ clinical services

Participants stressed that establishing LGBTQ+ clinical services in healthcare systems is pivotal for catalysing broader implementation of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. They noted that introducing these specialised services prompts healthcare systems to review and update their missions, policies and practices to better meet the needs of LGBTQ+ individuals. Nurse leaders also highlighted that LGBTQ+ services demonstrate a commitment to DEI, creating a more affirming environment for both LGBTQ+ patients and staff, and this process often includes integrating LGBTQ+ inclusive language and protocols into organisational guidelines and ensuring staff and facilities are equipped to provide culturally responsive care.

Barrier I (compatibility): conflicting current practices with LGBTQ+ inclusive practices

Participants identified the incompatibility of some LGBTQ+ inclusive practices with current procedures as a barrier. Specifically, they highlighted challenges such as the inability to include detailed SOGI questions in patient admission forms due to their already lengthy nature, preventing the collection of essential information for LGBTQ+ care. Additionally, they mentioned the difficulty of mandating LGBTQ+ training for staff due to the already high educational demands on healthcare professionals, which could overwhelm staff and hinder their effectiveness.

Barrier II (culture): lack of workforce diversity

Nurse leaders in the South, primarily in rural areas, identified the lack of workforce diversity as a barrier to implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. They noted that the limited diversity among healthcare staff hinders their ability to fully understand and meet the unique needs of diverse populations, including LGBTQ+ patients, leading to ineffective bias mitigation.

Theme III (outer setting): DEI emphasis in sociopolitical context

The overarching theme for this domain centres on DEI considerations within a broader sociopolitical context. All participants acknowledged the pivotal role of the increasing emphasis on DEI principles at both national and local levels. They highlighted how these principles significantly influence, both positively and negatively, the formulation of policies, the implementation or denial of practices, and the development of or push back against initiatives within healthcare systems, when centring the needs of LGBTQ+ communities.

Facilitator I (local attitudes): national DEI concerns

Participants highlighted that national emphasis on DEI principles contributes to fostering the implementation of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices within their organisations. They elaborated on how the growing recognition of the importance of DEI, triggered by national incidents such as the murder of George Floyd, has prompted healthcare organisations to reevaluate their policies and practices. As a result, they noted that there has been a notable shift towards prioritising LGBTQ+ initiatives within the broader framework of DEI efforts.

Facilitator II (local attitudes): societal shifts in LGBTQ+ visibility

Participants described that the evolving societal landscape of LGBTQ+ awareness and visibility consequently serves as a facilitator. They elaborated on how societal shifts towards greater acceptance and understanding of diverse gender identities and sexual orientations, along with increased awareness and visibility of LGBTQ+ specific issues, have led to greater recognition of the healthcare needs. They stated that this heightened awareness has thus prompted healthcare organisations to reevaluate policies and practices to ensure they are inclusive and responsive.

Facilitator III (financing): funding for DEI initiatives

Participants emphasised that external funding plays a crucial role in driving change in LGBTQ+ inclusion within healthcare. They explained that the availability and allocation of financial resources significantly influence the extent to which healthcare organisations prioritise and implement LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. They described that funding dictates the feasibility of initiatives such as staff training programmes, policy development and the provision of specialised services for LGBTQ+ individuals. Participants highlighted the importance of securing adequate funding to support ongoing efforts to enhance LGBTQ+ inclusivity in healthcare settings.

Facilitator IV (local conditions): regional context

Participants noted that urban locations facilitate inclusive policies due to abundant resources like community organisations and support networks. They added that urban areas foster greater LGBTQ+ awareness among healthcare professionals due to their diverse populations. Participants also stated that in regions with progressive political climates, broader societal acceptance of LGBTQ+ rights extends to healthcare settings, creating a supportive environment for inclusive practices tailored to LGBTQ+ individuals.

Barrier I (policies and laws): anti-LGBTQ+ legislation

Participants leading organisations in regions with ongoing anti-LGBTQ+ legislation highlighted that these legal constraints impede the implementation of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices in healthcare. They explained that such legislation creates a hostile legal environment for LGBTQ+ individuals and healthcare providers, leading to fear, uncertainty and reluctance to prioritise LGBTQ+ inclusivity initiatives. Additionally, participants expressed concerns about the potential legal repercussions of implementing inclusive practices that may contradict or violate existing anti-LGBTQ+ laws. They noted that, as a result, healthcare organisations in these regions face challenges in developing and implementing policies and services that support LGBTQ+ individuals’ unique healthcare needs and rights.

Barrier II (local conditions): regional characteristic

Participants emphasised that geographical context, particularly states in the Midwest and South where numerous anti-LGBTQ+ and anti-DEI laws have been introduced or enacted, presents significant barriers. They noted that these areas pose unique challenges due to their distinct sociocultural landscapes, with prevailing conservatism complicating efforts to promote LGBTQ+ inclusivity in healthcare. They reported that resistance to initiatives aimed at advancing LGBTQ+ rights and health equity is particularly pronounced in ‘red states’ or Republican-leaning communities, creating hurdles for implementing inclusive practices.

Additionally, participants highlighted rural settings as presenting barriers compared with urban environments. They noted challenges such as limited access to resources, healthcare infrastructure and specialised training opportunities. They also reported that rural communities often uphold more conservative social norms and attitudes, hindering efforts to promote LGBTQ+ inclusivity. They noted that, as a result, these factors contribute to a lack of awareness, understanding and support for LGBTQ+ individuals and their healthcare needs within rural healthcare settings.

Theme IV (individuals): identification and active engagement of key stakeholders

The overarching theme for this domain centres on the active engagement of key stakeholders in implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices within healthcare settings. Participants consistently underscored that high-level leaders, implementation facilitators and innovation deliverers play critical roles in driving change and ensuring the success of these initiatives. They described that engagement by these roles is vital for garnering support, securing resources and addressing challenges, and effective stakeholder engagement fosters collaboration, enhances communication and promotes a shared sense of ownership, accountability and commitment to LGBTQ+ inclusion goals within the healthcare organisation.

Facilitator I (high-level leaders): active leadership engagement

Participants unanimously emphasised that senior leadership support and active engagement are crucial facilitators. They highlighted the importance of leaders not only endorsing inclusive initiatives but also actively participating and demonstrating commitment through tangible actions, including advocating for policy changes, allocating resources and promoting and participating in LGBTQ+ training. They noted that such engagement creates a supportive organisational culture that values DEI and signals to staff that LGBTQ+ inclusivity is a priority, fostering accountability and motivation.

Facilitator II (innovation deliverers): younger generation

Participants highlighted the presence of younger staff as a facilitator due to their higher levels of acceptance and openness to diversity. They noted that younger staff members, more exposed to LGBTQ+ diversity through education and media, are more receptive to LGBTQ+ inclusivity initiatives and willing to engage in promoting inclusion, and their familiarity with evolving social norms helps create a more inclusive work environment and can bridge generational gaps, fostering education and awareness among older staff members to build a more inclusive organisational culture.

Barrier I (innovation deliverers): personal bias

Participants unanimously identified personal biases as significant barriers to implementation. They highlighted that these biases manifest as stereotypes, prejudices or discriminatory behaviours towards LGBTQ+ patients and colleagues, leading to implicit or explicit discrimination and creating hostile environments. They noted that biases influence decision-making, resulting in policies or practices that exclude or marginalise LGBTQ+ individuals.

Barrier II (innovation deliverers): religious belief

Participants underscored that conflicting religious beliefs regarding LGBTQ+ identities hinder the implementation of inclusive practices in healthcare settings. They reported that this conflict arises when personal religious convictions clash with LGBTQ+ inclusivity principles, leading to tension, resistance or opposition to adopting and promoting inclusive practices. They described that healthcare professionals holding such beliefs may struggle to reconcile them with the ethical mandate for non-discriminatory care, resulting in biased or discriminatory attitudes and behaviours.

Barrier III (innovation deliverers): generational barrier

Participants highlighted generational barriers, referring to differences in attitudes, beliefs and values between age groups in healthcare settings, as hindrances to LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. They noted that older generations often hold more biases and prejudices against LGBTQ+ individuals due to upbringing and societal influences, and these differences lead to communication breakdowns, resistance to change and challenges in aligning perspectives, impeding progress towards LGBTQ+ inclusion.

Theme V (implementation process): strategic planning and multilevel evaluation

The overarching theme for this domain revolves around strategic planning in collaboration with LGBTQ+ individuals and multilevel evaluation, including patient, staff and institutional assessments. Participants underscored inclusive planning processes that engage LGBTQ+ perspectives in strategy development for healthcare settings. They also noted that multilevel evaluation should assess the impact of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices on patient experience, staff engagement and organisational outcomes to ensure effectiveness and sustainability.

Facilitator I (assessing needs and evaluating): engagement with LGBTQ+ individuals

Participants emphasised engaging with LGBTQ+ individuals, both patients and staff, throughout the implementation process. They shared that engagement spanned planning, implementation and evaluation phases to ensure LGBTQ+ voices are heard and perspectives considered at every step. They noted this approach helps healthcare organisations better understand LGBTQ+ needs and informs inclusive practice development. Participants expressed that collaboration fosters inclusivity, ownership, trust and confidence among LGBTQ+ individuals in healthcare. Nurse leaders noted that engaging LGBTQ+ individuals validates their experiences, ensuring initiatives reflect their needs and concerns authentically.

Facilitator II (teaming): dedicated team

Participants highlighted the crucial role of a dedicated team tasked with overseeing LGBTQ+ inclusive DEI initiatives for successful implementation. They described that this team acts as a driving force behind integrating LGBTQ+ inclusive practices in healthcare settings, ensuring effective planning, launch and evaluation of initiatives. They noted that by coordinating efforts, setting clear goals and providing expertise and support, the team helps allocate resources systematically and fosters collaboration among staff to promote inclusivity.

Facilitator III (reflecting and evaluating): multilevel evaluation

Participants stressed the importance of gathering patient-level outcomes to evaluate LGBTQ+ inclusive practices, emphasising systematic data collection on patient experiences, satisfaction, quality of care, access and health outcomes specific to LGBTQ+ individuals. They shared that this approach allows healthcare organisations to assess effectiveness, identify improvements and monitor disparities compared with non-LGBTQ+ patients. They also underscored collecting staff-level outcomes to gauge staff experiences, attitudes, competencies and organisational culture related to LGBTQ+ inclusivity. They stated that these data inform improvements in job satisfaction, retention and staff engagement in providing LGBTQ+ inclusive care. Additionally, participants highlighted the importance of institution-level outcomes, such as US national recognition through the Healthcare Equality Index (HEI), a voluntary benchmarking programme that evaluates organisational policies and practices for LGBTQ+ inclusivity for patients, visitors and employees.48 They reported that achieving HEI recognition signifies institutional commitment, enhances reputation and promotes continuous improvement in LGBTQ+ healthcare practices.

Barrier I (reflecting and evaluating): inadequately designed patient feedback surveys

Participants noted in the evaluation phase that patient feedback surveys are crucial for assessing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. They highlighted that inadequately designed surveys, whether standardised or internal, pose significant barriers by often lacking components to capture key LGBTQ+ inclusivity information such as SOGI data and detailed DEI measures. They noted that without these, surveys fail to comprehensively understand LGBTQ+ patient experiences and needs.

Barrier II (assessing needs and evaluating): insufficient tools for staff assessment

Participants noted that assessing LGBTQ+ inclusion in healthcare settings often relies on staff surveys. However, they highlighted a significant challenge with the inadequacy of available tools for staff assessment, including existing or internally developed surveys. They stated that these tools frequently lack specific measures or questions on LGBTQ+ inclusivity and DEI practices, hindering accurate evaluation of organisational efforts and progress. They underscored that without tailored questions addressing LGBTQ+ issues, staff surveys fail to capture insights into staff perceptions, experiences and behaviours related to LGBTQ+ inclusion, so healthcare organisations struggle to gather comprehensive data on staff attitudes, knowledge and practices in this area, impeding effective evaluation and improvement of LGBTQ+ inclusivity initiatives.

Discussion

The implementation of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices in healthcare settings emerges as a multifaceted endeavour influenced by factors operating at multiple levels. By adopting the CFIR 2.0 framework,18 this study explored the implementation process from various perspectives, including organisational, individual and external factors, providing a nuanced understanding of the facilitators and barriers involved. Through the systematic application of the CFIR framework, multilevel determinants influencing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices implementation were identified. Overall, most CFIR constructs were represented. However, several did not surface in our interviews. Specifically, we found no evidence for some innovation constructs (eg, cost, trialability), inner setting constructs (eg, incentive systems) and implementation process constructs (eg, tailoring strategies).

Interpretation within the context of the wider literature

Our findings align with prior work largely reported by frontline providers in outpatient settings (online supplemental material 1). Nurse leaders in our study identified the adaptation of healthcare infrastructure to include SOGI-relevant features and content, particularly in EHR systems, as a key facilitator for implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. These configurations enable more patient-centred, culturally responsive care and strengthen communication and trust.3 Despite their benefits, persistent barriers exist within healthcare practices. The standardised and routine-driven nature of healthcare systems often resists change, complicating the adoption of new approaches like LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. Nurse leaders in our study also highlighted that the evolving landscape of LGBTQ+ issues requires ongoing adaptation and careful consideration. The collection of SOGI data in EHR systems poses risks such as incomplete or inaccurate data entry, privacy concerns and the potential reinforcement of biases.49 Varying provider training levels on LGBTQ+ issues further impact data quality and sensitivity. Future research should explore the development of tailored EHR systems for LGBTQ+ needs and adapt existing platforms to better incorporate LGBTQ+ health data.

Consistent with previous studies,26,29 participants identified visible, sustained leadership engagement as a critical facilitator. Nurse leaders emphasised that this engagement must go beyond passive support. Executives should not only endorse LGBTQ+ or DEI initiatives but also model commitment by participating in training, advocating for policy changes and allocating resources (eg, dedicated teams and budgets). Such actions signal organisational priority, cultivate an inclusive culture and provide the operational support necessary for successful implementation.

Training, the most frequently identified driver of LGBTQ+ inclusivity in prior studies (online supplemental material 1), also emerged as crucial in our study. Nurse leaders in our study highlighted the need to train both staff and leaders and to use effective formats. Beyond raising awareness, training should build knowledge and skills for culturally responsive, clinically competent care and help translate policy into practice. Leadership training drives organisational change and models top-down commitment. Because effectiveness depends on specific features,50 organisations should adopt multimodal, interdisciplinary programmes that include interaction with LGBTQ+ individuals, clear theoretical framing, follow-up or periodic refreshers, and safe spaces for reflection and bias mitigation.50

Implications for policy, practice and research

While prior studies have primarily identified facilitators and barriers within the inner setting and individuals domains (online supplemental material 1), our findings add contextual insight into how sociopolitical factors shape healthcare operations within the outer setting domain. They also underscore the importance of multilevel implementation evaluation within the implementation process domain. This is particularly salient, as emerging evidence shows positive impacts of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices on patient,51 workforce52 and organisational outcomes.53

Our findings suggest that geographic, political and legislative barriers, especially anti-LGBTQ+ laws and policies at the federal and state levels, materially shape organisational readiness and leadership decisions about LGBTQ+ inclusive practice. This aligns with recent evidence showing that state policy climates are associated with hospitals’ voluntary participation in the HEI,54 a voluntary national benchmarking programme that evaluates facilities’ policies and practices for LGBTQ+ patients, visitors and employees.

These dynamics have major implications in the USA amid intensified policy contestation and, more broadly, caution that legal and political environments can enable or chill inclusive implementation. In the USA, federal-level attacks on LGBTQ+rights, health and research55 and the introduction of 600+ anti-LGBTQ+ bills in state legislatures as of 202556 pose critical challenges. Internationally, contexts diverge. ILGA World’s 2024 Laws On Us report documented legal developments affecting LGBTQ+ communities from January 2023 to April 2024 across all 193 United Nations (UN) Member States, as well as numerous non-UN Member States and subnational jurisdictions.57 According to this report, some countries are advancing decriminalisation, self-identification for legal gender recognition and protections for same-sex couples, including adoption, while parts of Africa and Asia are regressing with expanding speech-based criminalisation and shrinking civic space. Additionally, progress on anti-discrimination and hate-crime and incitement laws remains limited among UN Member States.57 Healthcare leaders and policy-makers should advocate for protective policies and ensure clear non-discrimination standards to sustain equitable care amid these conditions.

The current attacks on DEI in the USA are also concerning,32 since the centrality of DEI in society and within healthcare systems emerged as a focal point among participants and reflects the historical evolution and persistent challenges of addressing health disparities.58 Given that most organisations participating in this study have integrated LGBTQ+ inclusion initiatives into their broader DEI efforts, the emergence of bills aimed at restricting DEI in various sectors could undermine their progress in promoting LGBTQ+ inclusion and erode the protections and rights for LGBTQ+ individuals. Therefore, it is crucial for organisations to advocate against discriminatory measures that seek to roll back DEI initiatives. Additionally, proactive steps should be taken to strengthen LGBTQ+ inclusion efforts within organisations and ensure they remain resilient in the face of legislative challenges.59 60

Our findings also highlight the importance of multilevel evaluation. Incorporating patient, staff and institutional perspectives is essential throughout the planning, implementation and monitoring of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices. Input from LGBTQ+ community members, partnerships with LGBTQ+ staff, community organisations and specialised clinicians in LGBTQ+ health are essential for tasks such as needs assessments, developing training programmes and revising policies to ensure inclusivity and effectiveness. Nurse leaders in our study have emphasised that both data and personal stories can inspire action among healthcare systems, leaders and staff. However, current healthcare surveys such as Hospital Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems often fail to capture LGBTQ+ inclusion insights accurately. This underscores the need for more inclusive assessment tools or adjustments that better reflect the challenges and experiences of LGBTQ+ individuals, in line with national recommendations.3 The collection of SOGI information, however, necessitates clear communication about its purpose for both LGBTQ+ and non-LGBTQ+ individuals, as they may share common concerns about disclosing SOGI information without a clear understanding of the context.49 Additionally, participation in institution-level evaluations like the HEI48 provides insights into the status of LGBTQ+ inclusion and areas for improvement. Recent research has highlighted that active support from hospital administrators significantly facilitates hospital participation in the HEI, offering another opportunity for healthcare leadership to demonstrate their commitment to DEI efforts within their organisations.61

Strengths and limitations

This study explicitly solicited perspectives of nurse leaders regarding the implementation determinants of LGBTQ+ inclusive practices across diverse healthcare settings, grounded in an implementation science framework. However, this qualitative study has limitations. First, while we purposively recruited a diverse sample across geographic regions, health systems serving different patient populations, rural settings, leadership roles and representation of minority underserved populations, the sample may not fully represent all nurse leaders in the USA. Nevertheless, the deliberate efforts to include diverse perspectives strengthen the study’s credibility, transferability and applicability to diverse healthcare settings. Second, the voluntary nature of participation in the study could introduce selection bias, as participants who volunteered may have had a particular interest or experience related to LGBTQ+ inclusion in healthcare.

Future research should focus on testing implementation strategies for tailored EHR systems, effective and respectful collection of SOGI data, and LGBTQ+ focused training for staff and leaders across diverse healthcare contexts. Additionally, there is a pressing need for the development of more comprehensive and inclusive healthcare surveys for patients and employees. These surveys should be designed to capture the unique challenges and experiences faced by both LGBTQ+ patients and employees within healthcare settings. Moreover, future research can also include supporting the role of organisational culture and leadership in fostering LGBTQ+ inclusivity within healthcare organisations, including examining the impact of leadership training on promoting inclusivity.

Conclusions

This study provides valuable insights from US nurse leaders into the facilitators and barriers to implementing LGBTQ+ inclusive practices across diverse healthcare systems. By addressing these barriers and leveraging identified facilitators, healthcare systems can work towards creating more inclusive, affirming and equitable environments for LGBTQ+ patients, staff and communities, particularly amid current hostile political climates for LGBTQ+ people. Ultimately, fostering LGBTQ+ inclusion in healthcare is not only a matter of ethical responsibility but also essential for achieving optimal health outcomes and health equity, as well as promoting social justice for all individuals, regardless of sexual orientation or gender identity.
