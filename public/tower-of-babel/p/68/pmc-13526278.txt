GPR15-GPR15L axis controls colon natural TCRαβ cells residency and enteroendocrine cell homeostasis to calibrate metabolism
Authors: Borja Ocón, Nanna C Giercksky Nilssen, Belén Rivero-Gutiérrez, Nicole H Lazarus, Yuhan Bi, Junliang Pan, Eugene C Butcher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGPR15 is a colon homing receptor, but its pattern of expression among gut intestinal intraepithelial lymphocytes and its role on intestinal intraepithelial lymphocyte (IEL) and epithelial homeostasis, as well as systemic metabolism and hormones remain unknown. GPR15L is the only ligand for GPR15 described to date, but whether this is the only functional ligand for GPR15 has not been formally explored.Here we show that GPR15 is expressed by mouse colon IELs, namely natural TCRαβ CD8αα+/− cells. These are reduced in the Gpr15−/− and Gpr15L −/− mice colon intraepithelial compartment. This translates into an overrepresentation of enteroendocrine cells in the colonic epithelium, elevated plasma serotonin and increased insulin secretion in response to local glucose administration. Competitive bone marrow chimeric mice confirm that GPR15L is the only functional colon-expressed chemokine ligand for GPR15. Human GPR15 is expressed and functional, recognizes mouse GPR15L and rescues the phenotypes associated with GPR15 deficiency in our novel GPR15 humanized model, which recapitulates features of the human colon immunobiology more closely than wild type mice.This work extends to the colon and TCRαβ CD8αα T cells the complex natural gut-resident T cell vs intestinal enteroendocrine cells crosstalk that controls systemic hormones and metabolism.

Introduction

The intestinal epithelium is made up of a single layer of epithelial cells lining the gut lumen to create a barrier separating the environment from the core of the body. It comprises many different subsets that perform functions including digestion and nutrient / fluid absorption, secretion of antimicrobial peptides and regulation of systemic metabolism, among others. Interspersed within the epithelial monolayer, intraepithelial lymphocytes (IEL) with an estimated cell density of 1 per 10 epithelial cells,1,2 are a diverse gut-resident T cell pool that comprises induced TCRαβ CD4 and CD8αβ, as well as natural TCRαβ and TCRγδ CD8αα+/− T cells. Induced IEL are generated in the thymus undergoing the double and simple positive stages and subsequently activated peripherally prior to homing to the gut intraepithelial compartment.3,4 Natural TCRαβ CD8αα+/− IEL are also exclusively generated in the thymus undergoing a double positive stage from precursors that express either T-Bet or PD-1.5–9 Natural TCRγδ CD8αα+/− IEL are largely generated in the thymus stemming directly from the double negative stage,7,10 but a relatively small fraction has been shown to originate from local extrathymic lymphopoiesis within gut cryptopatches as well.11,12 In the mouse small intestine (SI) 40–60% of IELs are natural TCRγδ T cells whose recruitment is largely dependent on the CCR9-CCL25 chemotactic axis. However, in the mouse colon where 50–60% of IELs are natural TCRαβ, the trafficking mechanisms that control T cell homing and tissue residency remain unknown.2,13,14

GPR15 is a class A GPCR that was described as an HIV co-receptor and has more recently been shown to contribute to the recruitment of regulatory15 and effector T CD4 cells to the colon lamina propria (LP),16 both in mice and humans. This effect is not only driven by classic chemotaxis of GPR15-expressing cells following a gradient of its chemotactic ligand, but also by triggering T cell α4β7 activation on MAdCAM1-expressing gut high endothelial venules.17,18 GPR15 imprinting is controlled by AhR signaling induced constitutively by AhR ligands presumably generated by the commensal microbes and of dietary origin.19,20

Its colon epithelium-expressed ligand GPR15L (also known as AP57, colon derived Sushi domain binding factor (CSBF), or C10orf99),21,22 is a highly conserved atypical chemoattractant that activates GPR15 via its C-terminus23,24 to constitutively control lymphocyte migration to the colon and presumably to the inflamed skin where it is induced.25–27 It has also been reported to have antimicrobial peptide properties28 and inhibit colon cancer cell lines growth.29 Surprisingly, whether GPR15L is the only colonic functional ligand for GPR15 remains to be formally shown.

Intestinal IELs are constantly engaged in immune surveillance to contribute to the immune defense of the gut barrier and eliminate transformed epithelial cells.2,30 However, the factors modulating IEL local dynamics are poorly understood. Furthermore, IEL are thought to influence epithelial homeostasis and function due to its privileged positioning interspersed within the epithelial monolayer. The likely bidirectional crosstalk between IEL and epithelial cells remains as a big gap of knowledge in the mucosal immunology field.

Here we show that GPR15 is expressed by colon-resident IEL subsets and the GPR15-GPR15L axis is required for the normal homing / retention of natural DN TCRαβ CD8αα+/− T cells to the colon epithelium. We also present a novel humanized mouse for GPR15 that expresses a fully functional human GPR15. These recapitulate better than wild type mice the human colon GPR15 immunobiology. Finally, we show that natural TCRαβ CD8αα+/− cells in the colon intraepithelial compartment regulate normal epithelial enteroendocrine cells (EEC) abundance and homeostasis. Our data reveal a so far unnoticed role of the GPR15-GPR15L axis in calibrating natural colon IEL biology and glucose metabolism by regulating EEC homeostasis. We also present our novel humanized mouse for GPR15 that may become an essential element of the tool kit of any academic or pharmaceutical industry effort exploring human GPR15 as druggable target.

Materials and methods

Mice

All mice in this study were maintained in specific pathogen-free (SPF) facilities at the Veterans Affairs Palo Alto Health Care System (VAPAHCS). B6/SJL Prprc Pep3BoyJ (CD45.1), C57B6/J CD45.2 were purchased from Jackson laboratories, while the B6/SJL Prprc Pep3BoyJ - C57B6/J F1 mice were bred in house. Gpr15−/− mice were shared by D. Littman. The Gpr15l−/− mouse strain was obtained from the Mutant Mouse Resource and Research Center (MMRRC) at University of California at Davis, an NIH-funded strain repository. These mice were backcrossed to C57B6/J background for 14 generations before use. The BAC humanized mice for GPR15 were generated by Cyagen (Santa Clara, CA) and were backcrossed to C57B6/J background for 14 generations and finally into a mouse Gpr15−/− background before use. Animals were maintained in accordance to US National Institutes of Health guidelines, and experiments were approved by Stanford University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were used at the age indicated in the corresponding figure legend.

Human intestinal intraepithelial cell preparation

Human intestinal segments specimens were obtained from the Donor Network West (San Ramon, CA) and informed consent was obtained. The specimens were processed applying the following protocol31 with minor modifications.

Isolation of intestinal lamina propria and intraepithelial lymphocytes

Isolation of intestinal lamina propria and IEL cells were done as described previously32 with minor modifications. In brief, mice (weaning age – adults of 8–10 up to 16 weeks of age, as indicated in the figure legends) intestines were isolated, attached fat and Peyer’s patches removed and tissues cut open longitudinally. Luminal contents were removed by shaking in cold HBSS and tissue was cut into 1–2 cm pieces. Next, the intestines were treated by shaking (220 rpm) with DTT 1 mM and EDTA 5 mM in HBSS without Ca2+ / Mg2+ with 2% FBS for 20 min and this step was repeated without DTT. IELs were isolated from this fraction, and the remaining tissue was treated with Liberase TL and DNAseI (Roche, Santa Clara, CA, USA) for 25 min using a heated gentle MACS Octo dissociator (Miltenyi, Auburn, CA, USA) to obtain lamina propria leukocytes. Finally, the mononuclear cells were isolated from both fractions using a 40/80 Percoll density gradient.

Flow cytometry analysis

The following antibodies were used for flow cytometry in the mouse studies: α4β7–APC and PE (DATK32), CD103-FITC, mouse GPR15-PE (S150421), TCRγδ-PercpCy5.5 (GL3), CD45-AF700 (30-F11), CD3-APCCy7 (145-2C11), CD8β-BV421(YTS156.7.7), CD8α-BV650 (53–6.7), CD4-BV711 (RM4–5), CD19-APC (1D3) and CD45.1-APC (A20) from Biolegend. CD45.2-BUV737 (104) and CD38 (90)-BV421 from BD. TCRβ-PECy7 (H57–597) from Tonbo.

The following antibodies were used for flow cytometry in the human studies: human GPR15-PE (SA302A10), CD3-FITC (HIT3a), Vγ9-AF700 (B3), Vα7.2-APCCy7 (3C10), CCR9-BV421 (L053E8), TCRδ2-BV711 (B6), CD4-BV786 (RPA-T4) and Vα24 Jα18-PECy7 (6B11) from Biolegend. Vδ1-Percp5.5 (REAL277) and TCRγδ-APC (11F2) from Miltenyi. CD103-BV605 (Ber-ACT8), CD8α-BV650 (RPA-T8), CD8β-BUV395 (2ST8.5H7) and TCRαβ-BUV737 (T10B9.1 A-31) from BD.

Before mAb staining, mouse and human cells were stained with a live-dead dye (BV510-Aqua) and subsequently with anti CD16/32 antibody or mouse/rat serum, respectively, to block surface Fc receptors. Data was acquired on a Fortessa cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC).

Competitive bone marrow chimeras

Bone marrow cells from congenic (B6.SJL: C57BL6/J F1) WT mice were mixed with equal numbers of bone marrow cells from either Gpr15−/−(CD45.1) or B6.SJL WT (CD45.1) mice and injected i.v. (5 × 106 bone marrow cells per donor) into lethally irradiated 6–8 weeks old C57BL6/J (CD45.2) WT or Gpr15L−/− recipients to generate “experimental” or “control” bone marrow chimeras, respectively. Tissues and blood were harvested 8–10 weeks after bone marrow transplantation and cell suspensions were stained with mAbs for flow cytometric analysis. Data is normalized to the average of total T CD4, total T CD8 and B cells from blood, spleen and PLN.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Tissues were harvested and fixed in 4% PFA for one hour, washed in PBS, and incubated in 30% sucrose overnight at 4 °C. Tissues were then embedded in OCT, flash frozen on dry ice, cut into 10 μm cryosections and stored at −20 °C. For immunofluorescent staining, slides were thawed, washed consecutively in PBS, PBST (PBS, 0.1% TWEEN® 20, 1% BSA) and blocked in 5% NDS in PBS-T for one hour at room temperature. Slides were then incubated overnight at 4 ° C with primary antibodies, anti SP-1 Chromogranin A (Immunostar) 1:200. Slides were then washed in PBST and incubated for two hours at room temperature with PE-conjugated anti rabbit IgG (Jackson Immunolaboratories) 1:100. Fluoromount-G™ with DAPI (Cat. # 00–4959–52, ThermoFisher Scientific) was used for nuclear staining and coverslipping. Imaging was performed with a Zeiss 880 confocal microscope and the Zeiss Zen, Image J and QPath software were used for image processing.

Intestinal epithelial cell sorting and bulk microarray studies

Colon epithelial cells from Gpr15™/™ and WT 10–12 weeks old mice were isolated with a gentle DTT and EDTA-based isolation protocol in PBS without Ca2+ and Mg2+, followed by an additional incubation in RPMI with 10% FBS. Then, the isolated cells were incubated with a live-dead dye (BV510-Aqua) and subsequently with anti CD16/32 antibody, followed by staining with APC-anti-mouse EpCAM (G8.8), PE-anti-mouse CD44 (IM7) and FITC-anti-mouse CD45 (30-F11), all from Biolegend. Next, live cells displaying a CD45- and EpCAM high phenotype (comprising both CD44+ and CD44− epithelial cells) wCD8ere isolated by FAC sorting (Aria III, BD) into 350 μl of RLT RNA lysis buffer and vor-texed for 1 min for adequate cellular lysis. Qiagen RNeasy micro kit was used to extract RNA from the epithelial cell lysates. Samples were sent to Expression Analysis Inc. where a quality check of the samples was performed followed by probe hybridization with Affymetrix Mouse Array V1. Raw data was processed (baseline correction applied) and differential gene expression analysis was performed using the Genespring GX software (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA).

Glucose tolerance tests

For glucose tolerance tests, mice fasted for 6 h were injected i.p., intrarectally or by oral gavage with glucose (2 g/kg body weight). Blood glucose levels were measured at the basal level and at 15, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min after glucose administration using One Touch Ultra2 Blood Glucose Meter (OneTouch, LifeScan, PA).

Insulin and serotonin ELISA

Serotonin was measured in plasma of mice without any intervention, using the serotonin competitive ELISA kit (Cat# EEL006, Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Mouse insulin was measured in mouse plasma obtained 15 min after rectal glucose (2 g/kg) administration, using the mouse insulin sandwich ELISA kit (Cat# KE10089, Proteintech) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise noted, statistical analysis was performed with unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test (for comparisons of two groups) or one-way ANOVA (for comparison of three or more groups) with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test, using GraphPad Prism. Results were expressed as mean ± SEM. *P ≤ 0.05, **P ≤ 0.01, ***P ≤ 0.001, ****P ≤ 0.0001, NS: non-significant statistical differences.

Results and discussion

GPR15 is expressed by mouse colon intraepithelial lymphocytes

The mouse intestinal intraepithelial compartment evolves rapidly during the first days of life. At birth the colon and SI intraepithelial compartment is largely occupied by natural TCRγδ cells. During weeks 1–2 after birth when the thymic function matures, induced TCRαβ CD4+ cells take over. However, one week later the gut intraepithelial compartment is largely dominated by CD8αα + natural T cells, TCRαβ in the colon and TCRγδ in the SI. This makeup remains rather stable ever after (Fig. 1A),2 so after weaning the gut epithelium is constitutively surveilled and regulated largely by natural CD8αα+/− IEL.

The body of work that describes GPR15 and its ligand GPR15L as a colon homing axis for lymphocytes, has so far exclusively focused on induced TCRαβ CD4+ or CD8αβ isolated from the gut LP.15,16,18–21 Here we decided to focus on the previously neglected, despite they account for most of the intestinal T cells under homeostasis,2,30 gut-resident IELs. We first analyzed GPR15 expression at the protein level by flow cytometry in adults and mice at weaning. As expected GPR15 expression was low among SI IEL, but very high (up to 60%) in those isolated from the colon where GPR15L is expressed.21,22 Although GPR15 expression was detected in all colon IEL subsets, natural TCRαβ IEL showed by far the highest levels. Especially the presumably relatively recent immigrants with a CD8αα− CD103low phenotype (account for ~30% of colonic natural TCRαβ IEL) (Fig. 1 B, C) still developing the resident memory program. As this subset turns on the resident memory program and upregulate CD8αα and CD103, GPR15 seems to be partially internalized / downregulated. This is typical of GPCR that function as chemokine receptors when cells reach a milieu with high concentrations of their chemokine ligand, here the colonic epithelium that constitutively expresses GPR15L.

Natural DN TCRαβ CD8αα+/− IEL home to the colon intraepithelial compartment in a GPR15-GPR15L-dependent manner

Considering the significant GPR15 expression displayed by colon IEL, we profiled the T cell subsets frequency and proliferation in this compartment in Gpr15−/− and Gpr15L−/− mice. Whereas the GPR15-GPR15L axis does not control local IEL proliferation constitutively (Supplementary Fig. 1B), both mouse lines at weaning and adult stages phenocopied each other with a substantial decrease in number and relative abundance of natural DN TCRαβ CD8αα+/− IEL compared to their WT littermates. This effect is sharper in CD8αα− CD103low subset compared to their CD8αα + CD103high counterparts, correlating with the GPR15 pattern of expression in Fig. 1 B. Therefore, other IEL subsets less dependent on the GPR15-GPR15L axis (i.e. natural TCRγδ) become relatively overrepresented in the two germline deficient mouse lines compared to WT controls (Fig. 1D and E).

GPR15L is the only colon-expressed functional chemokine ligand for GPR15

In 2017 we and others deorphanized GPR15 with the discovery and functional profiling of the atypical chemottractant GPR15L as the first ligand for GPR15.21,22 However, whether this is the only colonic ligand for GPR15 or there is another functionally overlapping chemoattractant produced by the colon epithelium has not been explored. To do so, we set up competitive (WT F1 CD45.1/CD45.2 vs Gpr15−/− CD45.1) bone marrow chimeric mice in WT or Gpr15L−/− (CD45.2) irradiated hosts (Fig. 2A). Two months after reconstitution the gut LP and intraepithelial compartment, as well as the lungs and control organs were analyzed by flow cytometry. As expected,15,16 the WT / Gpr15−/− ratio was moderately high for colon LP induced TCRαβ CD4 but not CD8αβ cells, while no phenotype was observed in the B cell pool (Fig. 2B). In the colon intraepithelial compartment the competitive advantage for GPR15-expressing vs -deficient cells was much more marked than in the LP (Fig. 2C). This suggests that the GPR15-GPR15L axis plays a more significant role in cell positioning the closer the cells are to the colonic epithelium. In line with the GPR15 pattern of expression across IEL subsets shown in Fig. 1, natural DN TCRαβ CD8αα− CD103low > CD8αα + CD103high IEL displayed the highest dependence on the expression of GPR15 (up to 15 WT/Gpr15−/− ratio) (Fig. 3 C, D). Importantly, these phenotypes disappeared when the host mice lacked GPR15L, confirming that this is the only colon-expressed functional chemokine ligand for GPR15.

Human GPR15 expressed in our novel GPR15-humanized mouse line rescues the Gpr15−/− colon IEL phenotype

In 2015 we functionally profiled GPR15 as a colon homing receptor that contributes to T cell driven experimental colitis by recruiting T CD4 effector T cells to the large intestines.16 In this article we also identified mouse specific SNPs in the presumable GPR15 regulatory regions (GATA-3 binding site) that could potentially explain the different mouse vs human GPR15 pattern of expression across T CD4 subsets. Briefly, whereas the majority of colitis-driving T CD4 cells in mice are Th17, T CD4 cells isolated from the colon LP of ulcerative colitis patients are largely biased towards the Th2 flavor.16 Inspired by this, we leveraged the bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) technology to insert a 150 Kb segment of the human genome comprising GPR15, its promoter and the expected 3′ and 5′ regulatory regions (Fig. 3A). We backcrossed this mouse into a C57B6/J Gpr15−/− background and confirmed that human GPR15 was successfully expressed among colon IEL. The human GPR15 pattern of expression in the humanized mice (BAC-hGPR15) was in general comparable to that of mouse GPR15 in non-transgenic control mice except for TCRγδ IEL. These cells expressed human GPR15 in BAC-hGPR15 more frequently than mouse GPR15 in WT C57B6/J mice (BAC-hGPR15 mice; ~25% vs WT mice; ~6%) (Fig. 3B). As we did in Fig. 1 for mouse GPR15 in WT mice, we analyzed by flow cytometry the relative expression of CD103 and now human GPR15 among CD8αα + or – natural DN TCRαβ colon IEL. Surprisingly, whereas mouse GPR15 and CD103 expression are largely mutually exclusive in WT mice (Fig. 1C) with only 5–8% of cells co-expressing both markers, in BAC-hGPR15 mice human GPR15 and CD103 were co-expressed in 35–60% of the cells (Fig. 3C). Taken together, this suggests significant human vs mouse differences in the GPR15 expression regulation that functionally apply to colon natural DN TCRαβ CD8αα+/− cells.

We next compared the gut intraepithelial compartment of Gpr15−/− and BAC-hGPR15 mice. This revealed that the BAC-hGPR15 mice phenocopied non-transgenic control mice by rescuing the colon IEL phenotype associated with GPR15-deficiency (Fig. 3 D–E). Of note, in these mice human GPR15 especially rescues the Gpr15 −/− phenotype in natural DN TCRαβ+ CD8αα + colon IELs. This agrees with the preferential human GPR15 expression by colon natural TCRαβ+ CD8αα + IEL, relative to their CD8αα− counterparts, in the BAC-hGPR15 mice. Furthermore, we confirmed that human GPR15 is expressed by gut homing natural IEL precursors in the thymus of young adult mice (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Taken together, these results confirm that the human GPR15 receptor expressed in the BAC-hGPR15 mice is biologically functional responding to the endogenous colon-expressed mouse GPR15L.

GPR15 is constitutively expressed by human IEL, matching the human GPR15 vs CD103 pattern of expression of BAC-hGPR15 mice

In light of our data on the BAC-hGPR15 mice, we explored the GPR15 and CCR9 pattern of expression in T cells isolated from the thymus, as well as healthy human intestinal intraepithelial compartment, covering the jejunum, ileum, ascending and transverse colon. First, both GPR15 and CCR9 are well expressed by gut homing natural IEL precursors in the neonatal human thymus (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Focusing now on the human gut intraepithelial compartment, induced TCRαβ CD8αβ and TCRγδ cells are the most abundant T cell subsets in the colon, whereas the former dominates the SI epithelium. Unlike in the mouse, where natural TCRαβ IEL are detectable in the SI and account for up to 60–70% of total T cells in the colon epithelium, these are barely detectable in the human gut (Supplementary fig. 4 A). Induced TCRαβ CD4+ and mucosae-associated invariant T cells (MAIT) were also detected but at very low frequency (Supplementary fig. 4 A). We next profiled the relative abundance of GPR15+, CCR9+ and GPR15 CCR9 double positive cells within each one of the isolated human intraepithelial T cell subsets (Supplementary fig. 4 B–J). As expected, the expression of GPR15 and CCR9 increases and decreases, respectively, as IEL are recovered following a proximal to distal axis along the GI tract. Focusing on the colon intraepithelial compartment, we observe that GPR15 is expressed by 30–60% of induced TCRαβ CD8αβ and TCRγδ (~50% of them are Vδ1+ Vγ9−) (Supplementary Fig. 4 G and H–J), similar to natural TCRαβ cells both in WT and the BAC-hGPR15 mice (Figs. 1B,C and 3B,C, respectively).

Furthermore, we analyzed the relative expression of CD103 and GPR15 in our human intestinal IEL samples. A representative flow cytometry dot plot with ileum TCRγδ cells shows that most (>70%) of these cells co-express the two markers (Supplementary Fig. 4, middle). This is the opposite GPR15 vs CD103 expression pattern we observed for mouse GPR15 in WT mice (Fig. 1C) but matches that for human GPR15 in BAC-hGPR15 mice (Fig. 3C). These results confirm that our novel BAC-hGPR15 humanized mice recapitulate more faithfully than WT C57B6/J mice the GPR15 immunobiology of the human colon intraepithelial compartment.

Natural TCRαβ CD8αα IEL calibrate enteroendocrine cell homeostasis to control metabolism

Considering that IELs are in intimate contact with the gut epithelium, we hypothesized that the dramatic decrease in natural TCRαβ CD8αα+/− IEL after blocking the GPR15-GPR15L axis, may perturb the neighboring colonic epithelium homeostasis. We first FACS sorted colon epithelial cells (CEC) from 10 to 12 weeks old WT and Gpr15−/− mice and performed bulk RNA transcriptomics. We did not observe any consistent pattern among the genes downregulated in Gpr15−/− compared to WT CEC. However, most of the top upregulated genes (Tph1, Chgb, Sst, Chga, Sct, Gcg, Nts) in the GPR15-deficient epithelium were associated with EEC (Fig. 4A). He et al, reported in 2019 that the loss of natural TCRγδ IEL in the SI of β7−/− mice provokes an expansion of GLP1-producing enteroendocrine-L cells,33 but did not look at the complete EEC pool. Therefore, we assessed the relative EEC (identified by chromogranin A (Chga) immunostaining) abundance in the colon epithelial lining of adult WT, Gpr15−/− and BAC-hGPR15 mice. The loss of most of the natural IEL (TCRαβ CD8αα+/− ) in the colon of Gpr15−/− triggers an expansion of EEC in the neighboring intestinal epithelium, whereas BAC-hGPR15 mice, which have no colon IEL phenotype (see Fig. 3), phenocopied WT control mice (Fig. 4B). This strongly suggests that the natural TCRαβ IEL loss is directly causing the colonic EEC expansion. The specific mechanism/s by which natural IEL control EEC lineage frequency warrants dedicated investigation in the immunometabolism field.

Inspired by the elegant work of He et al,33 we also asked whether the elevated EEC frequency in the colon epithelium of Gpr15−/− mice had an impact on glucose metabolism. We performed glucose tolerance tests (GTT) where glucose was administered systemically (i.p.), by oral gavage or intrarectally. Ccr9−/− mice that have a reduced natural IEL (TCRγδ) pool in the SI intraepithelial compartment,34,35 performed better than WT or Gpr15−/− mice when glucose was administered orally (Fig. 4C). Ccr9−/− and Gpr15−/− mice performed slightly better without reaching statistical significance when glucose was administered i.p., while Gpr15−/− mice did so when it was administered intrarectally and thus locally on the colonic epithelium (Fig. 4 D, E). Of note, BAC-hGPR15 mice phenocopied their WT littermates in the intrarectal GTT (Fig. 4E). To better understand the connection between the colon EEC phenotype and the Gpr15−/− mice performance under GTT, we measured insulin in plasma of WT and Gpr15−/− mice 15 min after intrarectal glucose administration. Plasma insulin was significantly elevated in Gpr15−/− mice, suggesting that the gut EEC (GLP-1) – pancreatic β-islet insulin axis is behind the better performance of Gpr15−/− mice in the intrarectal GTT (Fig. 4F). Approximately 90% of the serotonin (5-HT) in mice is produced by intestinal EEC36 and Tph1 (enzyme essential in 5-HT synthesis) is one of the most upregulated genes in the Gpr15−/− colon epithelium (Fig. 4A). To further understand the systemic impact of the Gpr15−/− mice colonic phenotype we measured plasma serotonin and observed a significant elevation of plasma serotonin in Gpr15−/− compared to WT or BAC-hGPR15 mice (Fig. 4G). Our findings uncover a colon-specific IEL–EEC axis that calibrates systemic metabolism and serotonin levels, revealing a distinct regulatory node from the small intestinal GLP-1 pathway previously described.

Conclusions

In this study we report a constitutive calibration of the systemic glucose metabolism by the GPR15-GPR15L axis. We first reveal that this chemotactic axis classically associated with induced TCRαβ CD4+ lymphocyte homing to the large intestines,15,16 is required for natural DN TCRαβ IEL homing / retention and normal representation in the colon intraepithelial compartment. Our data also strongly suggest that colon natural DN TCRαβ IEL constitutively control EEC homeostasis by negatively regulating their frequency in the colon epithelium. Therefore, when this IEL subset is reduced, as in Gpr15−/− mice, all flavors of EEC expand, translating into heightened insulin production and reduced glycemia in response to glucose reaching the brush border side of the large intestinal epithelium. In summary, our study draws a picture where natural DN TCRαβ CD8αα+/𢄡 IEL, the most abundant IEL subset in the adult mouse colon, calibrates systemic glucose metabolism. Notably, a previous study reported that natural TCRγδ IEL in the SI also negatively control GLP-1-producing EEC frequency in their neighboring epithelium.33 Taken together, these evidence suggest that in the mouse intestines natural IELs broadly regulate the complete EEC lineage representation, not only GLP1+ EEC as shown by Het et al.,33 suggesting a generalized epithelial reprogramming effect upon immune displacement.

Future research should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanism/s by which natural IEL negatively regulate EEC development in the gut epithelium under homeostasis. Furthermore, our work underscores the need of a more precise understanding of the effects of dietary and feeding/fasting pattern interventions on gut IEL subset numbers and their relative frequency and transcriptomic signature, to better predict and potentially manipulate their influence on systemic metabolism through EEC biology.

Our study also presents for the first time our humanized mice for GPR15 (BAC-hGPR15). These, unlike WT C57B6/J mice, express human GPR15 in mouse gut IELs in a human-like fashion relative to CD103. Therefore, BAC-hGPR15 mice recapitulate more closely the human GPR15 colon immunobiology than WT mice, at least regarding the GPR15 vs CD103 pattern of expression. This novel preclinical tool provides a powerful platform to interrogate immune–epithelial crosstalk in translational contexts. We believe this tool will be very appreciated in future academic and industry-based research exploring human GPR15 as a potential drug target for ulcerative colitis, because it offers more possibilities of manipulation and in a more cost-effective fashion compared to the alternative of drug testing in humans.

In summary, this work along with that from He et al.33 supports a broader conceptual framework in which intestinal natural TCRαβ (colon) and TCRγδ (SI) IEL link the gut-resident T cells to systemic metabolism through an IEL-EEC-GLP1-insulin axis and CNS homeostasis through a gut-brain axis driven by serotonin. Taken together, these findings suggest regional specialization in IEL–EEC crosstalk and identify GPR15 as a key regulator of gut-derived metabolic signaling. Finally, GPR15 is considered as a potential drug target for ulcerative colitis. However, in light of this work, this possibility should be carefully explored considering now that GPR15 blockade by modifying human colon IEL representation may indirectly alter systemic glucose metabolism homeostasis.
