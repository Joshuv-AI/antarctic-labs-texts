Association of Busulfan Exposure with Event-Free Survival in a Chinese Pediatric Cohort: A Multicenter, Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Chenrong Huang, Xiaohuan Du, Ling Xue, Lin Song, Xiaoxue Liu, Zhiyao Chen, Xiaoling Wang, Maoquan Qin, Ximu Sun, Dao Wang, Jing Yang, Dongjun Li, Hua Jiang, Wenjiao Ding, Yuhua Qu, Zengyan Zhu, Libo Zhao, Jinjie Yuan, Ji Dong, Depei Wu, Shaoyan Hu, Liyan Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTherapeutic drug monitoring-based individualized busulfan (Bu) administration improves hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) outcomes, but optimal Bu exposure in Chinese pediatric patients remains unclear; we aim to identify the optimal exposure level of Bu in such patients receiving Bu-based conditioning regimens of different intensities.Patients and MethodsPediatric patients from 5 transplant centers in China who received Bu-based myeloablative conditioning regimens were included. Bu exposure, defined as the area under the concentration–time curve (AUC), was estimated using a population pharmacokinetic model, including the first-dose AUC and cumulative AUC (cAUC). The primary and secondary outcomes included event-free survival (EFS) and transplantation-related complications. Restricted cubic splines and Cox proportional hazards models were used to analyze the exposure‒response relationships between the Bu AUC and outcomes.ResultsA total of 455 pediatric patients were enrolled, with 326 receiving a 3-day Bu-based (Bu3) regimen and 129 receiving a 4-day Bu-based (Bu4) regimen. Among all patients, a first-dose Bu AUC ≥ 4.5 mg×h/L was associated with a significantly higher 2-year EFS compared with AUC < 4.5 mg×h/L (84.6% vs 76.4%; P = 0.02). In the Bu3 cohort, a cAUC < 54 mg×h/L was associated with a lower 2-year EFS rate (77.7% vs 86.4%; P=0.04). In the Bu4 cohort, a cAUC of 70–90 mg×h/L was associated with a greater 2-year EFS rate (88.9%) than an exposure <70 mg×h/L (71.0%; P = 0.03) or >90 mg×h/L (66.1%; P = 0.04).ConclusionThis study demonstrated an association between Bu exposure and EFS in Chinese pediatric HSCT patients, with optimal cumulative AUC values of ≥ 54 mg×h/L and 70–90 mg×h/L identified for the Bu3 and Bu4 regimens, respectively. Additionally, a first-dose Bu AUC ≥ 4.5 mg×h/L was associated with a favorable 2-year EFS.

Introduction

Busulfan (Bu) is a cornerstone agent used in conditioning regimens prior to allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). However, this agent is known for its significant pharmacokinetic (PK) variability and narrow therapeutic window, particularly in pediatric patients.1,2 Population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) models have identified multiple covariates that affect the PK properties of Bu, including body weight, age, body composition, use of concomitant medications, and genetic polymorphisms.2 Glutathione S-transferase A1 (GSTA1) is the primary enzyme that mediates the metabolic conjugation of Bu, and its genetic polymorphisms may alter the metabolism and PK profiles of Bu. The incorporation of the GSTA1 genotype into PopPK models may enable more accurate prediction of Bu exposure and facilitate individualized dose adjustment from the first dose according to each patient’s metabolic capacity.3,4 Systemic exposure to Bu, defined as the area under the concentration–time curve (AUC), is strongly correlated with posttransplant clinical outcomes.5–9 Therefore, therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) combined with PopPK analysis is recommended to optimize personalized Bu administration and reduce the risks of graft failure, disease relapse, and transplantation-related mortality.10,11

Advanced optimization strategies for conditioning regimens have been proposed to increase the efficacy and safety of HSCT.12–14 For Bu specifically, strategies to attenuate the conditioning intensity involve either reduced myeloablative dosing (eg, 75% of the standard doses) or lower exposure targets (eg, 70% of the conventional 4-day cumulative AUC [cAUC] threshold of 100 mg×h/L). One study demonstrated that a Bu exposure target of 75 (70–80) mg×h/L optimally achieved high-level stable myeloid chimerism in pediatric patients with nonmalignant disorders.8 In submyeloablative protocols for chronic granulomatous disease and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis in pediatric patients, a cAUC range of 45–65 mg×h/L is associated with a higher rate of successful engraftment and reduced transplantation-related toxicity.15,16 The European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation/European Society for Immunodeficiency guidelines further recommend regimen-specific exposure targets for children with inborn immune deficiencies: a cAUC of 90 (85–95) mg×h/L for 4-day Bu-based (Bu4) protocols and a cAUC of 65 (60–70) mg×h/L for 3-day Bu-based (Bu3) protocols.17 Bu4 is a standard myeloablative regimen, whereas Bu3 is typically used as a reduced-intensity or safer alternative tailored to the clinical condition of pediatric patients.

Although TDM for Bu represents a standard clinical practice in the United States and Europe, it has not been widely adopted in China. In a previous single-center study conducted by our team, the optimal exposure to Bu under a 4-day myeloablative regimen was established.18 However, similar to previous pediatric studies in China,19,20 our findings were limited to a single center with a small sample size. Notably, the modified Bu (3.2 mg/kg/day for 3 days) –cyclophosphamide (Cy) regimen—termed the “Beijing Protocol”—is utilized at up to 59% of Chinese transplantation centers.21 The correlation between Bu exposure under this reduced-intensity 3-day Bu regimen and clinical outcomes in Chinese pediatric patients undergoing HSCT remains poorly characterized. Considering the variations in the clinical practices associated with HSCT across China and Western countries, the optimal target Bu exposure for 3-day and 4-day Bu regimens (with varying intensities) must be further clarified.

Thus, we conducted this multicenter, prospective cohort study involving Chinese pediatric HSCT recipients, aiming to explore the optimal target Bu exposure for 3- and 4-day regimens. Our approach involved 1) constructing a multicenter PopPK model using raw patient data supplemented by GSTA1 diplotype analysis to refine the characterization of the PK variability of Bu and 2) investigating the associations between Bu exposure as evaluated by the PopPK model and clinical outcomes after HSCT.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patients

This multicenter, prospective study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (No. NCT04786002) and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (2020087). Investigators from each center explained the study to the participants and obtained written informed consent from their legal guardians. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) being under 18 years of age and undergoing allogeneic HSCT for the first time; (2) receiving an intravenous Bu-based conditioning regimen, including 3-day and 4-day protocols; and (3) having whole-blood and TDM plasma samples available for the Bu analysis or pharmacogenetic metabolic enzyme polymorphism data and corresponding TDM records available. Patients who did not meet the predefined inclusion criteria were excluded. In addition, patients with a follow-up duration of less than 3 months were excluded from the correlation analysis of Bu exposure and clinical outcomes. Data were collected from January 2018 to January 2023 across five Chinese pediatric HSCT centers. Some patients from two single‑center studies that met the inclusion criteria were also included in the analysis.18,19

All patients received standardized treatment in isolation units according to institutional protocols. Specifically, they received myeloablative conditioning regimens including BuCy-, BuCy-fludarabine (Flu)-, and BuFlu-based protocols, and the 3‑day or 4‑day Bu regimen was selected on the basis of the disease type, risk stratification, patient age, organ function, and donor type.21 All patients underwent pretransplant gut decontamination and antimicrobial prophylaxis; seizure prophylaxis with phenytoin or levetiracetam during Bu administration; in vivo T-cell depletion with anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) for haploidentical/unrelated donor transplants; veno-occlusive disease (VOD) prophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin, alprostadil, or ursodeoxycholic acid; and graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis using calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine/tacrolimus) or sirolimus combined with mycophenolate mofetil.

Pharmacokinetics of Busulfan

The Bu dosing regimen was determined at each center on the basis of the patients’ actual or adjusted body weight, with individual doses ranging from 0.8–1.2 mg/kg administered intravenously four times daily over 3 or 4 consecutive days. The Bu infusion was delivered via a central venous catheter as a 2-hour continuous drip, with no dose adjustments performed during treatment. A predefined sampling protocol was adopted at all the participating centers, with blood samples routinely collected at 2, 4, and 6 hours after the start of the first Bu infusion. Additional sampling was performed at 0.5, 1, and 2.5 hours after the first infusion in some patients. For some patients, repeated sampling was performed 30 minutes before the fifth Bu dose or at the same time points as with the first dose. The actual sampling times were accurately recorded. The samples were stored at temperatures of −20°C or lower until analysis. Pretreatment peripheral blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes and genotyped to identify polymorphisms in glutathione S-transferase (GST) genes, includingGSTA1, M5, P1, M1, and T1. GSTA1 diplotypes were categorized as rapid or slow metabolizers (including intermediate and poor metabolizers) according to their enzymatic activity.22

Bu concentrations were quantified by a consistent liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry assay across all participating centers. Interlaboratory methodological comparison and validation were conducted across centers, with analytical deviation controlled within 10%. The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of Bu varied slightly across centers (10, 80, and 100 ng/mL). A lower LLOQ (10 ng/mL) was used in a center to accurately measure low-concentration samples including 0.5‑hour post‑dose and pre‑dose samples.

The data were analyzed using NONMEM (version 7.5.1) and Wings for NONMEM (version 7.5.1). A one-compartment pharmacokinetic model with first-order elimination was used to determine the pharmacokinetic properties of Bu. The model was parameterized using clearance (CL) and the distribution volume (V). All the parameters were estimated using the first-order conditional estimation method with interaction (FOCEI) in NONMEM. The Bu AUC (including the first-dose AUC and cAUC) was calculated by dividing the dosage by the CL value of each individual, which was estimated with the Bayesian method.

Body size was also evaluated in this study on the basis of a theory-based allometric model. Nonparametric bootstrap analysis including normal fat mass (NFM) was performed to evaluate the effects of fat mass on CL and V. The standard NFM was calculated using Equations S1 and S2. The variability in CL and V was predicted using body size via a theory-based allometric model (Equations S3 and S4).

Demographic characteristics and the use of drugs in combination (such as fludarabine, phenytoin, and metronidazole) were examined as potential covariates, and the covariates were divided into continuous and categorical groups. The continuous covariates included age, height, and weight. The categorical covariates included sex, genes, and the use of combined drugs. The categorical covariates were flagged with different digits in the data file. The continuous covariates were introduced into the model using Equation S5. The categorical covariates were introduced into the model using Equation S6. Covariate selection was performed using a standard stepwise approach (Table S1).

Goodness-of-fit plots, bootstrap resampling, and visual predictive checks (VPCs) were used to evaluate the accuracy and robustness of the final model. The median values and 2.5–97.5 percentiles of the parameter estimates obtained from the bootstrap replicates were compared with the parameter estimates obtained from the final model on the basis of the original dataset. The goodness-of-fit plots of the final model were generated with Pirana (version 23.10.1). Bootstrap resampling and VPCs were performed with Wings for NONMEM and R (version 4.2.3).

Clinical Outcomes

The primary endpoint was event-free survival (EFS), defined as the time from transplantation to the first occurrence of graft failure, disease relapse, or nonrelapse-related mortality (NRM).6 The secondary endpoints included transplantation-related complications, such as VOD,23 acute GVHD (aGVHD),24 hemorrhagic cystitis (HC),25 and oral mucositis.26 Follow-up was defined as the duration from the date of transplantation until the occurrence of an endpoint event, death, secondary transplantation, or last contact. Patients were censored if they were alive without endpoints at the last contact or if they were lost to follow-up before the endpoint occurred.

Statistical Analysis

Survival rates and cumulative incidence rates were estimated using the Kaplan‒Meier method. First, the relationships between Bu exposure (including the first-dose AUC across all patients, cAUC3 days for patients who underwent the Bu3 regimen, and cAUC4 days for patients who underwent the Bu4 regimen) and EFS were investigated using restricted cubic spline (RCS) models. The thresholds for dichotomizing Bu exposure were defined at points at which the hazard ratio (HR) was greater than 1 on the RCS curves. The categorized Bu exposure variable was then incorporated into Cox proportional hazards models for evaluating EFS and posttransplant complications. Additionally, clinical covariates, including patient sex, age, disease diagnosis (malignant vs nonmalignant), graft source (matched related donor, matched unrelated donor, mismatched related donor, or mismatched unrelated donor), donor cell type (bone marrow ± peripheral blood stem cells [PBSCs], PBSCs alone, or cord blood), conditioning regimen (BuCy-, BuCyFlu-, or BuFlu-based regimen), and ATG use were incorporated into the models. All the statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 and R 4.5.1. Variables with P < 0.1 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable model with bidirectional stepwise selection, and P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

PopPK Model and Busulfan Exposure

The PopPK model was developed using 2,103 Bu concentration measurements across 457 patients (Table 1). Only three measurements were below the LLOQ, which were included using their actual determined values. A one-compartment model with first-order elimination effectively described the PK properties of Bu. The estimated CL and V values for Bu were 10.8 L/h (2.5–97.5th percentiles: 10.4–11.3 L/h) and 46.4 L (2.5–97.5th percentiles: 45.0–47.8 L), respectively. The between-subject variabilities (BSV) for CL and V were 33.6% and 25.2%, respectively, whereas the between-occasion variability for CL was 11.2%. The variability in CL and V was predicted using allometric theory after accounting for body size and composition specifically to predict fat-free mass (FFM). The fraction of fat mass (Ffat) contributing to the NFM was also estimated for Bu. The estimated Ffat for CL was 1.08 (2.5–97.5th percentiles: 0.662–1.575), and that for V was 0.653 (2.5–97.5th percentiles: 0.436–0.911). These findings suggest that the biologically effective body size influencing CL is proportional to FFM plus 108% of the fat mass, whereas for volume, it is proportional to FFM plus 65.3% of the fat mass. Furthermore, a maturation function was incorporated into the CL model, which indicated that the postmenstrual age (PMA, in weeks) at which maturation reached 50% of adult clearance was 41.6 weeks (2.5–97.5th percentiles: 23.7–62.5 weeks). Notably, the CL for patients with slow GSTA1 metabolism was 13.5% lower than that for patients with fast metabolism. For the entire study population, the median Bu AUCfirst derived from the PopPK model was 4.5 mg×h/L (range, 1.8–10.5). For patients who underwent the Bu3 and Bu4 regimens, the median cAUC values were 54.2 mg×h/L (range, 26.5–119.0) and 70.5 mg×h/L (range, 30.2–109.9), respectively.

Patient Characteristics

Two patients with follow-up durations < 3 months were excluded from the analyses of the clinical outcomes, resulting in a final cohort of 455 patients. A total of 326 patients received the Bu3 regimen, and 129 patients received the Bu4 regimen. The demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 2. The patients had a median age of 7.5 years (range, 0.5–17.0) and a median weight of 24.5 kg (range, 5.0–96.5); 63.3% and 36.7% of the patients were male and female, respectively. A total of 336 patients (73.85%) had malignant disorders, whereas 119 (26.15%) had nonmalignant disorders. Mismatched unrelated donors accounted for 54.07% of the graft sources. Busulfan Exposure and Clinical Outcomes

EFS

The median follow-up for the entire study population was 2.2 years (range, 0.02–2.7). The estimated EFS rates at 1 and 2 years after HSCT were 83.7% (95% CI, 79.95–86.78) and 80.5% (95% CI, 76.52–83.91), respectively. Eight patients experienced graft failure. At 2 years, the rate of disease relapse was 9.3% (95% CI, 6.89–12.53), and the NRM was 9.8% (95% CI, 7.29–12.99).

The relationships between the three Bu exposure parameters (AUC first, cAUC3 days, and cAUC4 days) and EFS in the RCS models are shown in Figure 3. As shown in Figure 3A, the risk of the composite endpoint of graft failure, disease relapse, or NRM decreased as Bu AUC first increased across all the patients. Using a Bu AUC first cutoff of 4.5 mg×h/L as a potential predictor of EFS, we incorporated AUC first into the Cox model as a dichotomous variable. The results indicated that a Bu AUC first < 4.5 mg×h/L was independently associated with an inferior EFS (HR, 1.63; 95% CI, 1.06–2.50; P = 0.02; Table S2a). As illustrated in Figure 4A, compared with patients with an AUC first < 4.5 mg×h/L, patients with a higher first-dose Bu AUC (≥ 4.5 mg×h/L) exhibited a more favorable 2-year EFS profile (84.6% versus 76.4%; P = 0.02). Subgroup analyses stratified by the duration of the Bu regimen revealed distinct exposure‒outcome associations. Among patients receiving the Bu3 regimen (n=326), the RCS model indicated that the relationship between cAUC3 days and event risk was consistent with the trend of the first-dose Bu exposure. A cAUC3 days value less than 54 mg×h/L was identified as a potential risk factor for shorter EFS (Figure 3B). The Cox analysis revealed that a Bu cAUC3 days < 54 mg×h/L tended to be an independent prognostic factor for poor EFS (HR, 1.73; 95% CI, 1.01–2.94; P = 0.04; Table S2b). Furthermore, as shown in Figure 4B, patients with cAUC3 days < 54 mg×h/L experienced shorter EFS than those with higher Bu exposure did. The 2-year EFS rates of patients with a cAUC3 days < 54 mg×h/L and those with an exposure ≥ 54 mg×h/L were 77.7% (95% CI, 70.36–83.49) and 86.4% (95% CI, 80.11–90.86), respectively.

In the Bu4 cohort, the RCS model revealed a nonlinear relationship between the 4-day cumulative exposure and the risk of the composite outcome. The event risk was lower when the cAUC4 days value was within the range of 70–90 mg×h/L (Figure 3C). The Cox analysis revealed that, compared with patients with a cAUC4 days value of 70–90 mg×h/L, those with cAUC4 days values lower than 70 mg×h/L (HR, 2.94; 95% CI, 1.09–7.92; P = 0.03) or higher than 90 mg×h/L (HR, 3.22; 95% CI, 1.05–9.85; P = 0.04) had inferior EFS (Table S2c). As shown in the Kaplan–Meier survival curve (Figure 4C), patients with a cAUC4 days value of 70–90 mg×h/L had a 2-year EFS rate of 88.9% (95% CI, 75.34–95.22). In contrast, patients with cAUC4 days values < 70 mg×h/L and > 90 mg×h/L had 2-year EFS rates of 71.0% (95% CI 57.57–80.93) and 66.1% (95% CI 43.42–81.44), respectively.

VOD

VOD occurred in only 5 patients across the entire cohort. Among the three patients in the Bu3 group, two had a cumulative 3-day Bu exposure < 54 mg×h/L, and one had a Bu exposure ≥ 54 mg×h/L. Among the two patients in the Bu4 cohort, one had a cumulative 4-day Bu exposure < 70 mg×h/L, and one had a Bu exposure within the 70–90 mg×h/L range. Given the very limited number of events, no meaningful association between Bu exposure and VOD could be determined in this study.

aGVHD

Among all patients, the 100-day cumulative incidences of grade 2–4 and grade 3–4 aGVHD were 32.0% and 16.3%, respectively, with 141 cases of grade 2–4 aGVHD and 72 cases of grade 3–4 aGVHD. No significant association between cumulative Bu exposure and the development of aGVHD was observed (data not shown). Among patients receiving the Bu3 regimen, compared with matched related donor recipients, patients who underwent mismatched unrelated donor transplantation had a higher risk of grade 2–4 aGVHD (HR, 3.63; 95% CI, 1.55–8.51; P <0.01; Table S3a). Those receiving BuCyFlu-based conditioning regimens had a higher risk of grade 3–4 aGVHD than those receiving BuCy-based regimens (HR, 2.01; 95% CI, 1.12–3.61; P = 0.02; Table S3b).

HC

In the overall cohort, the 100-day cumulative incidences of grade 2–4 and grade 3–4 HC were 22.7% and 8.5%, respectively. A total of 100 patients developed grade 2–4 HC and 37 developed grade 3–4 HC. Among patients receiving the Bu3 regimen, ATG use (HR, 4.48; 95% CI, 1.06–18.97; P=0.04) and older age (6–12 years: HR, 3.58; 95% CI, 1.67–7.68; P < 0.01; >12 years: HR, 6.47; 95% CI, 2.91–14.41; P < 0.01) were independent risk factors for grade 2–4 HC (Table S4a). Grade 3–4 HC occurred exclusively in patients with malignant disease who received ATG. Compared with patients with a Bu cAUC3 days ≥ 54 mg×h/L, those with a cAUC3 days < 54 mg×h/L had a lower risk of grade 3–4 HC (HR, 0.38; 95% CI, 0.15–0.98; P=0.05; Table S4b), which was of borderline statistical significance. In the Bu4 cohort, Bu exposure was not associated with either grade 2–4 or grade 3–4 HC. Relative to children aged 0–6 years, older age was significantly associated with an increased risk of grade 2–4 HC (P < 0.01; Table S4c).

Oral Mucositis

The overall 100-day cumulative incidence of grade 2–4 oral mucositis was 12.4%. Most cases were grade 2 (43 cases, 77%), with 10 cases of grade 3 and 3 cases of grade 4. No significant association was observed between Bu exposure and oral mucositis in the groups receiving either the 3-day or 4-day Bu regimen. Among patients receiving the Bu3 regimen, compared with BuCy-based regimens, BuCyFlu-based conditioning was associated with a higher risk of grade 2–4 oral mucositis (HR, 2.55; 95% CI 1.26–5.15; P < 0.01; Table S5a). In the Bu4 cohort, older age was the only factor that was significantly associated with an increased risk of grade 2–4 oral mucositis (P < 0.01; Table S5b).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this work represents the largest study in which the relationship between Bu exposure and clinical outcomes in Chinese pediatric patients undergoing HSCT was specifically investigated, with a particular focus on exploring the optimal target exposure for both 3-day and 4-day Bu regimens. The results indicate an association between Bu exposure and EFS after HSCT. Among patients receiving the Bu4 regimen, the most favorable EFS was observed when the 4-day cumulative exposure (cAUC4 days) was within 70–90 mg×h/L. Among the larger Bu3 cohort, higher Bu exposure (ie, cAUC3 days ≥ 54 mg×h/L) was associated with improved EFS. In addition, regardless of the number of days of Bu administration, if the first-Bu dose exposure (AUC first)—as predicted by the PopPK model—is greater than or equal to 4.5 mg×h/L, the adverse effects of suboptimal cumulative exposure on EFS can be avoided.

Four-day Bu-based myeloablative conditioning regimens remain a cornerstone of standard protocols. Because subtherapeutic Bu exposure is associated with graft failure or disease relapse, whereas supratherapeutic exposure increases toxicity and transplantation-related mortality, most studies and individualized dosing protocols follow the FDA and EMA recommendations for Bu exposure targets. These targets include first-dose AUC targets of 3.7–5.6 mg×h/L (FDA) or 3.7–6.2 mg×h/L (EMA), with cAUC4 days thresholds of 59–89 mg×h/L (FDA) and 58–99 mg×h/L (EMA), respectively.27 Notably, the results of the largest multicenter study conducted in children and young adults demonstrated that elevated cumulative Bu exposure (78–101 mg×h/L) was associated with increased EFS, irrespective of the underlying disease malignancy status of the patients.6 A recently published study involving 562 children and young adults with inborn errors of immunity demonstrated superior 2-year EFS in patients with cAUC4 days values within the 70–90 mg×h/L range than in those with exposures <70 mg×h/L or >90 mg×h/L.7 Consistent with these findings, our study revealed that patients receiving the Bu4 regimen achieved optimal EFS when the cAUC ranged from 70–90 mg×h/L (Figure 2C). Notably, the results revealed modest statistical effect sizes, which are likely related to the relatively limited sample size of the Bu4 subgroup. Nevertheless, the consistent optimal exposure range of 70–90 mg×h/L observed in both our study and the large international cohort study supports the clinical relevance of this exposure range for Bu4-based conditioning regimens.

Among the 326 pediatric patients who predominantly received the Bu3 regimen, the risks of overall adverse events decreased progressively with rising Bu exposure. With 54 mg×h/L defined as the cut-off value, patients who attained cAUC3 days ≥ 54 mg×h/L achieved higher 2-year EFS than those in the low-exposure group did (Figure 2B). After multivariable adjustment including age, cAUC3 days < 54 mg×h/L was independently associated with higher NRM risk (HR, 2.48; 95% CI, 1.07–5.78; P = 0.04; Table S6). Accordingly, the poorer EFS seen in the low-exposure Bu3 subgroup is primarily attributable to increased NRM rather than graft failure or leukemic relapse. This target aligns with the mean value of the submyeloablative cAUC range (45–65 mg×h/L) recommended for children with chronic granulomatous disease or hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis.15,16 Moreover, the cutoff closely aligns with the lower limit of the 60–70 mg×h/L therapeutic window for Bu3 regimen proposed by European guidelines, which specify a target of 65 (60–70) mg×h/L for reduced-intensity myeloablative regimens in children with inborn immune deficiencies.17 Notably, no upper exposure limit was identified for the Bu3 cohort, which differs from the well-defined upper exposure limit of the Bu4 regimen. This finding may be attributed to the favorable safety profile of the treatment regimen in Bu3 cohort: only 18% of patients had a cAUC3 days > 70 mg×h/L, with only 1 patient having NRM and no patients having VOD. The absence of a definable upper exposure limit in our Bu3 cohort is consistent with the results of a previous study that included adult and pediatric patients with acute myeloid leukemia and myelodysplastic syndromes, in which patients with a cAUC3 days ≥ 59.5 mg×h/L achieved higher 5-year overall survival but no upper safety limit was established.28 Notably, although EFS did not decline with higher cAUC3 days in Bu3 recipients, we do not support the simplistic assumption that higher exposure equates to superior outcomes. Elevated Bu cAUC3 days showed a trend toward increased grade 3–4 HC risk in this cohort. We advise maintaining cAUC3 days above 54 mg×h/L while balancing efficacy and toxicity risks to achieve optimal Bu exposure. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate the upper safety limit for the Bu3 conditioning regimen.

The abbreviated duration of Bu administration underscores the importance of achieving the target drug exposure early in the treatment course. In accordance with the prescribing information, the US FDA and EMA recommend target first-dose or per-dose Bu AUC ranges of 3.7–5.6 mg×h/L and 3.7–6.2 mg×h/L, respectively. In our study, in which the association between AUC first and EFS was assessed across all patients regardless of the treatment duration, we identified an AUC first ≥ 4.5 mg×h/L as a significant predictor of improved EFS (Figure 2A). These findings highlight the clinical value of first-dose TDM for the early identification of patients who may be at risk of inferior outcomes due to inadequate Bu exposure. Importantly, however, the clearance of Bu decreases over time (approximately 10–20% decrease during treatment), with substantial intraindividual variability, which may limit the reliability of using the first-dose TDM approach alone to estimate cumulative exposure throughout treatment. Therefore, repeated TDM during subsequent doses remains necessary to ensure optimal exposure.29–31 Furthermore, implementing PopPK-derived nomograms or algorithm-based strategies could increase the accuracy of the initial dose predictions and facilitate earlier attainment of target exposure levels.4,22,32,33

Previous studies have reported that elevated Bu exposure (defined as an AUC >5.6 mg×h/L,34 Cmax >1.88 mg/L,35 cAUC4 days >78 mg×h/L36 or >85 mg×h/L)37 is associated with an increased risk of severe, potentially fatal VOD. However, no significant association between these parameters and VOD was reported in other studies.38,39 In our study, the incidence of VOD was markedly low (n=5), which may be partly attributable to the predominant use of the Bu3 regimen in our cohort, as this protocol is theoretically associated with a lower total Bu exposure than the Bu4 regimen. In addition, the proactive and consistent implementation of VOD prophylactic measures in our patients may have contributed to this low incidence. HC is a multifactorial complication following HSCT, with well‑established risk factors including Bu exposure, ATG use, age, male sex, HLA mismatch, and cytomegalovirus reactivation.40–42 Among patients receiving the Bu3 regimen, cAUC3 days < 54 mg×h/L exhibited a borderline protective trend against grade 3–4 HC (HR, 0.38; 95% CI, 0.15–0.98; P = 0.05). This finding aligns with a previous pediatric study showing higher HC incidence at Bu steady-state concentrations >600 ng/mL (approximately equivalent to a cAUC4 days 57.6 mg×h/L).40 To balance efficacy and safety, a target cAUC3 days ≥ 54 mg×h/L should be maintained to optimize EFS, whereas intensive preventive measures (eg, hyperhydration, mesna and forced diuresis) are recommended to reduce HC risk.43

However, this study has several limitations that should be considered. First, potential confounding factors that may influence survival outcomes were not fully included, and disease heterogeneity was not subjected to stratified analysis. Second, several variables showed borderline significance in the multivariable Cox model, which should be interpreted with caution due to limited statistical power. Third, bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS) assessment was not predefined in the study design, so we could not analyze its incidence and risk factors related to Bu exposure.44,45 Further large-scale, standardized studies are warranted to verify and complement these findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrated an association between Bu exposure and EFS in Chinese pediatric patients who underwent HSCT. A first-dose Bu AUC ≥ 4.5 mg×h/L predicted better 2-year EFS. The optimal Bu exposure levels were a cumulative AUC ≥ 54 mg×h/L for patients receiving the Bu3 regimen and a cumulative AUC of 70–90 mg×h/L for those receiving the Bu4 regimen. Importantly, both the PopPK model developed in this study and the proposed optimal Bu exposure targets require rigorous validation in independent external cohorts to confirm their generalizability.
