Central Diabetes Insipidus and Brain Herniation Secondary to Cryptococcal Meningitis in a Dog
Authors: Owen Hurst, Alicia Mastrocco, Jennifer Prittie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCentral diabetes insipidus (CDI) should be considered in neurologic patients with persistent polyuria and rising sodium concentrations despite appropriate fluid therapy. Earlier recognition, serial urine concentration monitoring, and desmopressin administration may help prevent severe hypernatremia, although outcomes may remain poor in patients with progressive intracranial disease.

Introduction

Diabetes insipidus (DI) is a rare syndrome in people and animals caused by impaired renal water reabsorption [1, 2]. Diabetes insipidus is classified as nephrogenic or central. Central DI (CDI) results from inadequate production of antidiuretic hormone (arginine vasopressin, AVP) in the hypothalamic paraventricular nuclei or release of AVP from the pituitary gland, whereas nephrogenic DI (NDI) involves antagonism of AVP at the V2 receptors in the renal collecting tubules and/or dysfunction of the aquaporin 2 channels [1, 2]. Either type of DI can be congenital or acquired.

Acquired CDI typically results from structural CNS injury [3, 4, 5]. Reported causes in dogs include meningoencephalitis of unknown etiology, neoplasia, trauma, and bacterial CNS infections [3, 4, 5, 6]. Idiopathic CDI is also reported. Treatment of CDI includes reversal of the underlying disease and administration of oral, ophthalmic, and/or nasal synthetic desmopressin (DDAVP).

Cryptococcus spp. are neurotropic fungal pathogens reported to cause Cryptococcal meningitis and acquired CDI in people and are generally responsive to antifungal therapy and DDAVP administration until underlying meningitis resolves [7]. To the authors' knowledge, acquired CDI secondary to central nervous system (CNS) Cryptococcal infection has not been previously documented in dogs [8, 9]. This report describes a dog with confirmed central nervous system (CNS) Cryptococcal infection that developed findings consistent with presumptive acquired CDI, including severe hypernatremia, polyuria, and hyposthenuria. The patient ultimately developed brain herniation despite intensive management and was euthanized.

Case History

A 1‐year‐old, 8‐kg, intact, male, Golden Retriever‐Poodle mix presented to our Emergency Service as a referral for progressive dull mentation and hyporexia. The dog ingested tampons 2 weeks prior and subsequently vomited them, after which the vomiting resolved. The dog was also witnessed to ingest a large volume of goose feces 1 week prior to presentation in Long Island, New York. Diagnostics performed by the referring veterinarian included a CBC and chemistry panel, pre‐ and postprandial bile acids, and cortisol level which were all normal. Thoracic radiographs were normal and an abdominal ultrasound showed nonobstructive gastric foreign material. The dog was negative for heartworm, Lyme disease, Ehrlichia, and Anaplasma on the SNAP test. He was hospitalized there on intravenous (IV) fluids and anti‐nausea medications; however, progressive dull mentation prompted referral to our Emergency Room for an MRI.

On presentation to our Emergency Room, the dog was dull and demonstrated low head carriage. Point‐of‐care bloodwork showed no clinically significant abnormalities; serum sodium concentration was 142 mmol/L (RR: 145–157 mmol/L). Urinalysis showed a urine specific gravity (USG) of 1.018, but the patient had previously received IV fluids. No other abnormalities were noted. He was hospitalized overnight on IV fluids (Plasma‐Lyte 45 mL/kg/day) and gastrointestinal support along with methadone (0.1 mg/kg every 6 h).

Investigations and Treatments

On day 2 of hospitalization, an MRI was performed and findings were consistent with diffuse meningitis, characterized by contrast‐enhancing and multi‐focally thickened pachymeninges and leptomeninges, along with mild enlargement of the retropharyngeal lymph nodes (Figure 1, panel A). Infectious or inflammatory etiologies were prioritized and a CSF tap was performed without complication. Upon extubation from anesthesia post‐MRI, the patient became apneic and required reintubation and manual ventilation. During this time, he was administered IV boluses of mannitol (1 g/kg), to decrease presumed increased intracranial pressure, and solumedrol (30 mg/kg) to aid in the management of CNS inflammation associated with Cryptoccoccus meningitis, but did not demonstrate return of spontaneous ventilation and mechanical ventilation was initiated. The patient was placed on mandatory pressure‐controlled ventilation with physiologic settings (peak inspiratory pressure 12 cm H2O, tidal volume 10 mL/kg, respiratory rate of 25 breaths per minute, and PEEP of 5 cm H2O), as the absence of spontaneous breathing was attributed to neurologic dysfunction rather than primary pulmonary pathology.

T2‐weighted sagittal MRI images showing thickened, contrast enhancing meninges (A, initial patient MRI) and subsequent caudal foramen magnum and transtentorial brain herniation on repeat imaging (B) 24 h later.

A urinary catheter was placed for patient comfort and to facilitate urine output (UOP) monitoring. Urine output was measured via and indwelling urinary catheter with continuous closed collection and serial quantification. Urine output volumes were retrospectively collected and calculated into ml/kg/h based on volumes included within the record. At this time, he was on 90 mL/kg/day of isotonic crystalloids, gastroprotectants, dexamethasone SP (0.1 mg/kg/day), and enrofloxacin (15 mg/kg IV every 12 h) for potential bacterial meningitis.

A blood gas performed at the time of initiation of mechanical ventilation showed a normal sodium of 145 mmol/L. The patient was administered a 20 mL/kg isotonic crystalloid bolus (Plasma‐Lyte A) for cardiovascular support and then was maintained on 120 mL/kg/day of Plasma‐Lyte A. Hourly UOP ranged between 25 and 55 mL/kg/h for the first 6 h of ventilation. Recheck blood gas at hour 6 of mechanical ventilation demonstrated a new hypernatremia (Na 162 mmol/L).

At this time, the patient fluid rate was increased to 120 mL/kg/day isotonic crystalloids and polyuria progressed to (37–131 mL/kg/h). A blood gas performed 12 h after initiation of ventilation revealed a markedly elevated sodium of 185 mmol/L. The patient was assessed as euhydrated and was switched to ½ strength Plasma‐Lyte A with sterile water at the same rate (120 mL/kg/day) and his UOP was matched with Plasma‐Lyte to keep up with losses, suspected to be from mannitol administration.

The patient remained euhydrated based on subjective parameters. Because of persistent hypernatremia, the patient's free water deficit was calculated to be 1.3 L using a normal target sodium for a dog. A fluid plan was made to replace this deficit over 12 h with IV D5W (in addition to the isotonic crystalloid flat rate). The USG was not assessed at this time, but the dog remained very polyuric. The patient's weight was also unable to be consistently assessed on the ventilator and therefore could not act as support of hydration status.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

On the morning of day 3 of hospitalization, the dog demonstrated a significant decline in neurologic status, showing first a left mydriatic unresponsive pupil progressing to mid‐range, unresponsive pupils bilaterally. Concern for progressive brainstem injury prompted repeat MRI, which showed severe caudal foramen magnum and transtentorial brain herniation, likely secondary to diffuse brain swelling, vascular compromise (brain ischemia), and progressive meningitis (Figure 1, panel B). The cytology from the CSF tap performed the previous day returned consistent with Cryptococcus fungal infection with eosinophilic inflammation (Figure 2). Despite the convincing evidence of worsening intracranial pathology on neurologic exam and MRI, euthanasia was not yet pursued, mechanical ventilation was continued, and fluconazole therapy (15 mg/kg IV every 12 h) was initiated. Serum latex agglutination later confirmed Cryptococcal infection (positive at 1:2048) and no evidence of disseminated Cryptococcosis outside the CNS was identified.

Patient CSF cytology consistent with Cryptococcus fungal infection with eosinophilic inflammation.

Despite the administration of free water, the patient's serum sodium continued to increase to 196 mmol/L (18 h after ventilation; UOP: 22–35 mL/kg/h) and then to 198 mmol/L (24 h after ventilation; UOP: 13–22 mL/kg/h). Urine specific gravities measured concurrently at these time points were 1.004 and 1.002, respectively.

Given the concern for diabetes insipidus, desmopressin (0.01% nasal solution, 1 drop in the right eye) was administered. A blood gas assessed 6 h after the initial dose showed a sodium of 156 mmol/L. A USG measured at this time was 1.016. The sodium continued to normalize to 147 mmol/L 12 h after DDAVP administration (USG at this time was 1.014) and to 146 mmol/L 18 h later (USG 1.016), and DDAVP administration was continued as 1 drop in alternating eyes every 12 h.

On the morning of day 4 of hospitalization, the patient remained mechanically ventilated with no improvement in neurologic status. The sodium remained normal at 147 mmol/L and the USG was 1.018. After DDAVP administration, the UOP rapidly and markedly decreased to 2–5 mL/kg/h from 13 to 22 mL/kg/h. Despite normalization of sodium after DDAVP, the lack of improvement in neurologic status prompted the owners to pursue euthanasia later that day, after approximately 48‐h on the mechanical ventilator. Discussion

Diagnosis of DI is based on evaluation of UOP (and daily water intake, when available), serial USG measurements to confirm presence of hyposthenuria, serial plasma sodium concentration (in sick, hospitalized pets), and response to synthetic AVP administration [1]. Diabetes insipidus leads to severe aquaresis and hyposthenuric polyuria and can result in hypertonic dehydration and hypovolemia when water intake is impaired. The combination of profound hyposthenuric polyuria, development of severe hypernatremia, and dramatic response to DDAVP in this patient strongly supports a presumptive diagnosis of CDI, although definitive confirmation is limited by incomplete early diagnostic data in this case. Serial urine specific gravity measurements were not consistently obtained in this case, especially prior to intervention, and as such, the presumptive diagnosis of CDI was made retrospectively based on clinical progression and response to treatment with DDAVP.

Prompt recognition of DI in neurologic patients is vital, as loss of free water without adequate replacement can result in acute hypernatremia, increased serum osmolarity, and cerebral dehydration, potentially exacerbating further neurologic deterioration. In rapidly‐developing hypernatremia, water shifts from the intracellular (neuronal) space to the extracellular space, before cerebral adaptive mechanisms (electrolyte shifts, idiogenic osmole accumulation) have sufficient time to develop [1, 2, 3]. As a result, the risk of cerebral edema during correction is generally considered lower during correction of acute hypernatremia than in chronic hypernatremia, although optimal sodium correction rates are unknown.

Given the acute onset of this patient's hypernatremia, a more rapid correction strategy was pursued to mitigate ongoing cerebral dehydration. The patient described here had rapidly progressive hypernatremia secondary to unregulated aquaresis and despite the aggressive free water replacement, the sodium continued to increase. Despite this undertaking, hypernatremia progressed and the urine remained hyposthenuric, indicating excessive renal free water loss consistent with presumptive untreated CDI. Desmopressin, a synthetic AVP analogue, was administered to this patient with a favorable response (normalization of sodium and isosthenuric urine), confirming intact renal responsiveness to AVP and supporting a presumptive diagnosis of acquired central DI, possibly associated with the confirmed CNS Cryptococcal infection. Despite the rapid fluid shifts in this patient, the neurologic deteriation and caudal foramen magnum and transtentorial brain herniation was presumed more likely secondary to the diffuse brain swelling, vascular compromise (brain ischemia), and meningitis.

Alternative contributors to the observed polyuria and hypernatremia, including osmotic diuresis from mannitol administration and high‐volume administration of sodium‐containing fluids, must be considered. However, the persistence of marked polyuria and hyposthenuria beyond the expected duration of mannitol's effects, along with continued, worsening hypernatremia and a rapid, sustained response to DDAVP, support a process independent of osmotic diuresis and is most consistent with a primary defect in water conservation suggestive of DI.

Acquired CDI in dogs has been documented in association with multiple clinical syndromes, but is most commonly associated with structural CNS disease, including neoplasia, trauma, and inflammatory disease [1, 2, 4, 5, 6]. Although sterile inflammatory CNS disorders are most commonly reported to cause acquired CDI in veterinary patients, infectious etiologies including bacterial and fungal encephalitis have also been reported [1, 3, 4]. Cryptococcosis is an uncommon fungal pathogen affecting the CNS of dogs and cats. Diagnosis of this fungal infection is achieved via identification of the characteristic organism on cytologic evaluation or latex agglutination. The treatment of choice is aggressive antifungal therapy with fluconazole or equivalent drug [8]. Glucocorticoid therapy is frequently utilized in combination to mitigate secondary inflammatory injury [1, 7, 8, 10]. Cryptococcosis primarily affects the CNS and may present as changes in mentation, vestibular ataxia, or seizure activity. Ocular and spinal cord involvement have been reported less frequently [10]. Central DI is a documented complication of Cryptococcal meningitis in people, particularly those that are immunosuppressed, but has not been previously reported in dogs. In the current case, imaging did not describe any specific involvement of structures associated with AVP production or release (i.e., hypothalamus or pituitary gland), but the findings associated with diffuse meningitis and subsequent progressive brain herniation, nay have contributed to the development of CDI.

Central DI can be challenging to recognize in neurologic patients, and failure to restore water balance in these animals can exacerbate neuronal injury and adversely affect outcome. Prompt identification of CDI and early administration of synthetic DDAVP may prevent aquaresis and avoid rapid neurologic decompensation.
