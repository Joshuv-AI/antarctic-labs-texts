Choroidal watershed infarction due to medial posterior ciliary artery occlusion with hemi-retinal ischemia, a case report
Authors: Wael A Alsakran, Nada Alyousef, Maram Alenazi, Abdulrahman H Badawi, Moustafa S Magliyah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Although retinal vascular occlusion is very common in practice, a combined CRA occlusion in conjunction with the MPCA occlusion is a rare phenomenon reported infrequently in the literature [1–3]. This phenomenon has been attributed to the anatomical origin of the common trunk of both vessels exiting the ophthalmic artery before the corresponding branches divide to perfuse the globe [3, 4]. Hence, understanding the anatomical branching varieties of the ophthalmic artery can facilitate the explanation of some cases that present such a combined occlusion. Angiographic investigations with fluorescein and indocyanine green angiography corroborate the posterior ciliary circulation deficit by showing delayed or absent choroidal filling with accompanying amalric infarction [5]. Choroidal ischemia has been associated with a variety of causes, including atherosclerotic disease, giant cell arteritis, sickle cell coagulopathy, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and iatrogenic injury [1, 3, 6, 7]. Because the short posterior ciliary arteries (SPCAs) supply the prelaminar region of the optic nerve, which is frequently located in the watershed area between the medial and lateral long posterior ciliary arteries, ischemic optic neuropathy is commonly associated with choroidal ischemia [8].

We present a rare report of nasal choroidal ischemia caused by MPCA occlusion, which resulted in a watershed infarction traversing the optic nerve head, as well as an accompanying hemi-retinal occlusion involving the nasal portion of the retina, which corresponded to the region of choroidal ischemia.

Case presentation

A 65-year-old diabetic and hypertensive female patient presented with a complaint of ocular pain and irritation involving the right eye, in addition to blurring of the temporal visual field that had started suddenly about three weeks before presentation, which had worsened for the last few days. Her ocular history was unremarkable with no prior ocular interventions or injuries. On initial examination, the vision in the right eye was 0.6 logMAR (20/80), and the left eye was 0.4 logMAR (20/60). The intraocular pressure was 34 mmHg in the right eye and 18 mmHg in the left eye. The anterior segment examination was remarkable for iris neovascularization involving the right eye. Angle assessment showed 360 degrees of open angle in both eyes with no angle neovascularization or peripheral anterior synechiae. Humphrey and confrontational visual fields confirmed temporal field depression not respecting the vertical meridian in the right eye, while the left eye was full. The fundus assessment for the right eye was remarkable for retinal degenerative whitening with sclerosed retinal vessels involving the medial half of the retina, which aligns with retinal infarction, in addition to intraretinal hemorrhages and large white wedge-shaped triangular-like area inferiorly corresponding to amalric infarction due to choroidal ischemia within the nasal half of the posterior segment (Fig. 1). Fundus autofluorescence (FAF) was unremarkable for the left eye, however it showed scattered hypofluorescent spots correlating with the intraretinal hemorrhages nasally and also around the superotemporal vein, in addition to abnormal and reduced vascular density with the medial ischemic area in the right eye (Fig. 2). Fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) demonstrated absence of nasal choroid filling and delayed retinal artery filling in the retina’s medial half, as well as non-filling of the nasal half of the optic disc. It also showed delayed filling of the superior venous arcade that is within the temporal half of the retina (Fig. 3). Indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) showed markedly delayed filling of the choroidal vessels medially, especially in the superior and inferior quadrants within the medial half of the choroid, which aligns with the delayed arm-to choroid circulation time of 19 s (Fig. 4). The fundus assessment of the left eye, in addition to FFA and ICGA, was unremarkable with no sign indicating either retinal or choroidal ischemia. The patient received a sectoral retinal laser to the ischemic area in addition to a single intravitreal aflibercept injection (2.0 mg in 0.05 ml), which effectively regressed her iris neovascularization. The patient was started on anti-glaucoma drops, bimatoprost and dorzolamide, which considerably controlled her ocular pressure. The patient underwent a full work-up after the emergency interventions, which included unremarkable magnetic resonance imaging of brain and orbits and vascular imaging, as well as a normal echocardiogram and carotid Doppler. Laboratory investigations for inflammatory and hypercoagulable etiologies were all unremarkable except for elevated glycosylated hemoglobin of 7.1%. The patient was instructed to address her atherosclerotic diseases, which involved diabetes and hypertension, with the primary internist. On follow-up visits, with a follow up period of 6 months, the patient showed a stable condition with stable visual acuity and ocular pressure and no recurrence of neovascularization or any incidence of new ocular ischemic events.

Discussion and conclusions

Posterior ciliary artery circulation is the primary source of vascular perfusion to the optic nerve, choroid, retinal pigment epithelium, and the outer retina. There are about 10 to 20 SPCAs that pierce the sclera close to the optic nerve head, in addition to the MPCA and lateral long posterior ciliary arteries (LPCA), which enter the globe in the medial and lateral horizontal planes, respectively [4]. The watershed zone between the medial and lateral posterior ciliary arteries, defined as the border area between their territories, is quite variable. Hayreh’s seminal anatomical investigations demonstrated that vertically oriented watershed zones exist between the MPCA and LPCA, frequently crossing into the optic disc [8, 9]. When the MPCA is occluded, perfusion to the nasal choroid and prelaminar optic nerve is disrupted [8, 9]. Because the optic disc is the site of confluence between the CRA and SPCA regions and is frequently in the watershed area between MPCA and LPCA, ischemia in one circulation at the area of the optic nerve head might affect the local perfusion gradient in the other circulation [8, 9].

Retinal vascular occlusions are much more common than choroidal ischemia. However, some cases have been reported with a combined occlusion of ciliary and retinal circulation involving MPCA and CRA [1–3]. The mechanism of such a combined occlusion was attributed to the common trunk, which includes both arteries branching from the ophthalmic artery [3, 4]. Although our patient clearly had MPCA occlusion, evidenced by ischemia in the nasal choroidal region and the medial half of the optic nerve, it was accompanied by hemiretinal ischemia rather than complete CRA occlusion, as seen in earlier documented cases. The coexistence of choroidal and retinal ischemia within the same anatomical distribution is a rare but instructive example of vascular interdependence between the retinal and ciliary circulations. To our knowledge, no prior investigation or report has shown the presence of such simultaneous hemiretinal and hemichoroidal ischemia as in our case.

The retinal findings in this case are inconsistent with a main CRA or branch retinal artery obstruction. Instead, the delayed filling of the nasal retinal arteries is most likely due to the hemodynamic extension of ischemia over the ciliary-retinal circulation boundary. Paula et al. revealed overlapping perfusion regions for the CRA and SPCAs inside the prelaminar ONH, indicating that tiny peripapillary capillaries from both systems coexist in the laminar and surface layers [10]. When perfusion pressure in one system (here, the MPCA) drops rapidly and significantly, flow within the overlapping microvascular bed might reverse or stall, temporarily occluding adjacent SPCA and CRA branches. Konno et al. observed comparable disc-level collateral activity following CRA occlusion, demonstrating that these latent anastomotic channels might become functionally relevant under ischemic stress [11]. This notably sectoral pattern implies that the ischemia process was limited initially to the perfusion area of the MPCA but spread secondary to neighboring retinal vascular zones. Developmental and structural variations may explain why some patients display cross-system extension and others do not. Watanabe et al. employed optical coherence tomography angiography to detect discrepancies in cilioretinal connections, including unusual nasal branches [12]. If such a microvascular relationship occurred in the present case, it might have aided pressure transfer from the MPCA to neighboring nasal CRA branches, emphasizing the hemiretinal pattern. Although assumptive, this concept is consistent with the observed vertical border of ischemia and the relative preservation of temporal flow.

The lack of late disc leakage on angiography is diagnostically significant. It separates this pattern from inflammatory or venous etiologies, such as papillitis or impending retinal vein occlusion, which exhibit leakage and hyperfluorescence in the late stages [13]. The patient might have had prior optic nerve swelling due to ischemia involving the medial half of the disc, which was not noticed during the acute stage and later followed by the atrophic stage at the time of presentation. The “silent” ischemic half-disc shown here represents pure vascular non-perfusion, not subsequent inflammation, matching the angiographic findings noted by Hayreh regarding ischemic optic neuropathy [8, 9]. However, choroidal congestion and disc swelling can raise interstitial pressure in the peripapillary tissues, compressing venules and producing minor venous outflow delay, which might explain the associated delayed emptying of the superotemporal vein in our case [14]. This mechanical interaction demonstrates how localized choroidal ischemia, especially that involving the major intersection landmark that is the optic nerve head, can cause a larger hemodynamic disturbance and worsening of blood flow in both choroidal and retinal circulations.

Diabetes and hypertension are systemic vascular diseases that exacerbate the risk of such occurrences by affecting microvascular autoregulation. Ciulla et al. and Kur et al. found that endothelial dysfunction and capillary basement membrane thickening suppress autoregulatory responses in both the retinal and choroidal circulations [15, 16]. In such cases, an abrupt decline in MPCA perfusion cannot be countered by vasodilation of adjacent CRA branches, exacerbating the spread of ischemia. Furthermore, vasoactive mediators such as endothelin-1, which is known to be elevated in ischemic ocular neuropathies, may worsen the perfusion imbalance via localized vasospasm [17]. These secondary processes are more likely to exacerbate the severity of the primary ischemic episode.

In conclusion, this example demonstrates a sectoral hemodynamic failure at the choroidal-retinal interface at the optic disc watershed zone. The major insult, MPCA blockage, caused ischemia of the nasal choroid and half of the optic disc. A further drop in perfusion pressure within the watershed border zone may have caused hypoperfusion of neighboring nasal CRA branches, resulting in nasal hemiretinal ischemia. The absence of inflammatory leakage, the maintenance of temporal retinal flow except for the superotemporal vein, and the anatomic distribution of non-filling by angiographies all point to a confined, non-inflammatory, perfusion-dependent process. Recognizing such situations is critical for understanding the continuum of choroidal, disc, and retinal vascular impairment and avoiding misdiagnosis as atypical CRA or venous occlusions.
