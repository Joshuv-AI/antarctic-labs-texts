12(S)-HETE regulates the expression of IL-6 and IL-10 in cardiomyocytes through two distinct receptors, TXA2R and BLT2
Authors: Shota Mori, Yuma Takeda, Masashi Tomimatsu, Yusuke Kametani, Naokazu Yoneda, Kaho Egawa, Hiroaki Hase, Shota Tanaka, Masahiro Okuda, Yoshiaki Okada, Masanori Obana, Yasushi Fujio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiac inflammation contributes to the development and progression of heart failure (HF). Although cytokines are key mediators of cardiac inflammation, lipid-derived metabolites also play essential roles; however, their underlying mechanisms remain largely unexplored. Among these metabolites, 12 S-hydroxy-5Z,8Z,10E, 14Z-eicosatetraenoic acid (12(S)-HETE), an arachidonic acid-derived metabolite produced primarily by 15-lipoxygenase (ALOX15), has not been fully characterized in the heart. In neonatal rat cardiomyocytes, 12(S)-HETE significantly upregulated the expression of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and interleukin-10 (IL-10). The 12(S)-HETE-induced upregulation of IL-6 was suppressed by a thromboxane A2 receptor (TXA2R) antagonist, whereas treatment with a TXA2R agonist alone increased IL-6 expression, suggesting the involvement of TXA2R signaling. Conversely, pharmacological activation of leukotriene B4 receptor 2 (BLT2) attenuated the 12(S)-HETE-induced upregulation of IL-6. Treatment with a BLT2 agonist alone did not affect IL-6 expression but significantly increased IL-10 expression. In addition, both 12(S)-HETE and the BLT2 agonist activated extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK); this activation was inhibited by a BLT2 antagonist but not by a TXA2R antagonist. In a mouse model of myocardial infarction (MI), serum levels of 12-HETE (the combined amount of 12(S)-HETE and 12(R)-HETE) and cardiac expression of ALOX15 were increased. Collectively, these findings identify 12(S)-HETE as an inducible lipid mediator following MI that modulates pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine expression in cardiomyocytes through coordinated activation of TXA2R and BLT2 signaling pathways. Targeting 12(S)-HETE signaling may therefore represent a potential therapeutic strategy for preventing inflammation-associated cardiac remodeling and HF.

Introduction

Cardiac inflammation contributes to myocardial injury and progression of heart failure (HF) [1]. Therefore, regulation of inflammatory responses represents a promising therapeutic strategy for HF. While cytokines have long been recognized as key mediators of cardiac inflammation, accumulating evidence suggests that lipid mediators also play critical roles in cardiac inflammation and homeostasis. For instance, our previous work demonstrated that the pro-resolving lipid mediator maresin-1 promoted physiological cardiac hypertrophy [2]. These findings indicate that lipid mediators may serve as potential therapeutic targets for HF; however, the mechanisms by which they regulate cardiac inflammation remain poorly understood.

12 S-hydroxy-5Z,8Z,10E,14Z-eicosatetraenoic acid (12(S)-HETE) is a metabolite of arachidonic acid that is primarily generated by 15-lipoxygenase (ALOX15) [3]. 12(S)-HETE has been reported to exert diverse biological functions, including platelet aggregation, inflammation, and cancer cell proliferation [4]. Several receptors—namely the thromboxane A2 receptor (TXA2R), leukotriene B4 receptor 2 (BLT2), and G protein-coupled receptor 31 (GPR31)—have been identified as potential mediators of 12(S)-HETE signaling in non-cardiac cells such as PC3 cells, endothelial cells, and human platelets [[5], [6], [7]]. According to the Human Protein Atlas database (https://www.proteinatlas.org), cardiomyocytes express TXA2R and BLT2, but not GPR31, suggesting that TXA2R and BLT2 may serve as functional 12(S)-HETE receptors in the heart. However, the roles of 12(S)-HETE and its receptors in cardiac physiology and inflammation remain unclear. In this study, we investigated the function of 12(S)-HETE in cardiac inflammation using neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs).

Methods

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the animal care guidelines of the University of Osaka and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, Eighth Edition, updated by the US National Research Council Committee in 2011. All experiments were approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee of the University of Osaka (Douyaku R03-16-3). Animals were euthanized using isoflurane (Wako), and all efforts were made to minimize suffering.

Reagents

The reagents used in this study, including 12(S)-HETE, brefeldin A, SQ29548, U46619, CAY10583, and LY255283, were purchased from Cayman Chemical. Final reagent and solvent concentrations used in cell culture were listed in Supplemental Table S1.

Cell culture

NRCMs were cultured as described in our previous report [8]. Isolated NRCMs were plated in 0.32-cm2, 3.8-cm2 or 9.5-cm2 dishes and cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 0.1 mM bromodeoxyuridine (Gibco/Life Technologies). After incubation in DMEM containing 10% FBS at 37 °C for 24 h in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 95% air, the NRCMs were cultured in serum-free DMEM for an additional 24 h and then used for experiments.

qRT-PCR

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed as described previously [8]. GAPDH was used as an internal control. The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplemental Table S2.

ELISA

Secreted IL-6 protein levels were measured using the Quantikine ELISA Rat IL-6 Immunoassay (R&D Systems). NRCMs (1.0 × 106 cells per 3.8-cm2 well) were stimulated with 12(S)-HETE for 24 h. Culture supernatants (50 μL) were collected for analysis. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Immunoblot analysis

Immunoblot analysis was performed as described in our previous report [8]. GAPDH was used as an internal control. The primary antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplemental Table S3.

MI model mice

Myocardial infarction (MI) was induced as described previously [9,10]. Male C57BL/6 J mice aged 9–16 weeks (Japan SLC) were intubated and anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane (Wako) under mechanical ventilation at a tidal volume of 0.8 mL and a respiratory rate of 110 breaths/min. MI was induced by ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery at mid-ventricular level using surgical No. 7-0 silk suture (Alfresa). Non-MI mice were defined as healthy mice without any surgical procedure.

Measurement of lipid mediators

Serum samples from non-MI mice and mice 14 days after MI were mixed with methanol containing 0.1% formic acid and stirred at 4 °C for 1 h. Lipid mediators in the supernatant were extracted by solid-phase extraction using Strata-X cartridges (Phenomenex). Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis was performed using an LCMS-8060 NX/Nexera X3 (Shimadzu), and data were analyzed using the Multi-omics Analysis Package (Shimadzu). Relative peak intensities were normalized to a mixture of deuterated internal standards (tetranor-PGEM-d6, 6-keto-PGF1α-d4, TXB2-d4, PGF2α-d4, PGE2-d4, PGD2-d4, LTC4-d5, LTD4-d5, PGA2-d4, LTB4-d4, 14,15-DiHET-d11, 15-HETE-d8, 12-HETE-d8, 5-HETE-d8, PAF-d4, 11,12-EET-d11, OEA-d4, and AA-d8). Because optical isomers could not be separated by this analysis, total 12-HETE (the combined amount of 12(S)-HETE and 12(R)-HETE) was reported. This experiment was supported by the Center for Supporting Drug Discovery and Life Science Research (Basis for Supporting Innovative Drug Discovery and Life Science Research [BINDS]) of the Japan Agency for Medical Research and Development (AMED).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed as described previously [10]. Hearts 4 days after MI were frozen in O·C.T. Compound (Sakura Finetek Japan) and sectioned into 10-μm-thick slices using a Leica CM3050S IV cryostat (Leica). Frozen sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 2 min, and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 30 min. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies (listed in Supplemental Table S4) for 90 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 546- or 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (listed in Supplemental Table S4) for 90 min at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (1:200; Nacalai Tesque). Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X700; KEYENCE).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability assay was performed using CellTiter-Blue assay kit (Promega) as described previously [8]. NRCMs (1.0 × 105 cells/0.32 cm2 well) were stimulated with 100 nM 12(S)-HETE and 100 nM CAY10583 for 96 h and incubated with 20 μL CellTiter-Blue reagent for 3 h. Fluorescence intensity was measured using SpectraMAX M5e (Molecular Devices), and cell viability was estimated from the fluorescence intensity.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments of qRT-PCR and immunoblot analysis were performed using biologically independent samples, and the sample size was indicated in each figure legend. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student's t-test. Multiple comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's test or Tukey–Kramer test using Statcel Ver. 3 (ISBN 978-4-434-15413-3; OMS Publishing). P value < 0.05 (two-tailed) was considered statistically significant.

Results

12(S)-HETE upregulates IL-6 and IL-10 expression in cardiomyocytes

A previous study reported that ALOX15 knockout mice exhibited enhanced resolution of inflammation after MI [11], suggesting that 12(S)-HETE, a product of ALOX15, may influence cardiac inflammation. To investigate the effects of 12(S)-HETE on inflammatory responses in cardiomyocytes, cultured NRCMs were treated with 100 nM 12(S)-HETE for 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 4 h or treated with 1, 10, 100, and 1000 nM 12(S)-HETE for 1 h. The mRNA expression levels of major pro-inflammatory cytokines, interleukin-6 (Il-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (Tnf-α), and interleukin-1β (Il-1β), were assessed by qRT-PCR. Tnf-α mRNA expression was significantly increased by 12(S)-HETE; however, the magnitude of upregulation was less pronounced than that observed for Il-6 (Fig. 1C and D). Il-1β mRNA level was not significantly altered (Fig. 1E and F). 12(S)-HETE-induced IL-6 and IL-10 expression is mediated through TXA2R and BLT2

To investigate which receptors mediate the effects of 12(S)-HETE in NRCMs, the public RNA sequencing dataset (GSE224822) confirmed that NRCMs expressed Tbxa2r and Ltb4r2, but not Gpr31 (Supplemental Fig. S1A). Also, qRT-PCR and immunoblot analysis showed that BLT2 (Ltb4r2) was indeed expressed in NRCMs (Supplemental Fig. S1B and C). These results suggest that TXA2R and BLT2 may be 12(S)-HETE receptors in NRCMs.

12(S)-HETE has been reported to act as a competitive antagonist of TXA2R in human platelets [7]. However, in our experiments, the TXA2R antagonist SQ29548 suppressed 12(S)-HETE-induced upregulation of Il-6 mRNA (Fig. 3A). BLT2, a low-affinity receptor for leukotriene B4, also exhibits high affinity for several arachidonic acid metabolites, including 12(S)-HETE [13]. While BLT1 expression is restricted to leukocytes, BLT2 is ubiquitously expressed, including in the heart [13,14]. Interestingly, treatment with the BLT2 agonist CAY10583 prevented 12(S)-HETE-induced upregulation of Il-6 mRNA (Fig. 3D). Although CAY10583 alone did not alter Il-6 mRNA, it significantly increased Il-10 mRNA expression within 30 min (Fig. 3E and F), suggesting that pharmacological activation of BLT2 exerts anti-inflammatory effects in cardiomyocytes by downregulating IL-6 and by upregulating IL-10. Considering that the upregulation rate in IL-10 was extremely high at 1000 nM compared to 100 nM of 12(S)-HETE (Fig. 2B) while the upregulation rate in IL-6 was approximately the same at 100 nM and 1000 nM of 12(S)-HETE (Fig. 1B), it may be possible that BLT2 has lower affinity than TXA2R as the 12(S)-HETE receptor.

We also demonstrated functional cardiomyocyte outcomes using cell viability assay. As a result, 100 nM 12(S)-HETE induced cardiomyocytes death, which was suppressed by BLT2 agonist (Supplemental Fig. S2). It suggests that BLT2 activation exhibits a cardiomyocyte survival effect as functional cardiomyocyte outcomes.

To investigate the involvement of BLT2 and TXA2R in the induction of IL-10 b y 12(S)-HETE, NRCMs were treated 12(S)-HETE with a BLT2 inhibitor LY255283. Unexpectedly, BLT2 inhibition by LY255283 did not suppress but instead enhanced 12(S)-HETE-induced Il-10 production (Supplemental Fig. S3A). In contrast, blockade of TXA2R by SQ29548 tended to reduce 12(S)-HETE-induced Il-10 expression (Supplemental Fig. S3B). These findings further suggest that IL-10 regulation by 12(S)-HETE involves receptor crosstalk or compensatory signaling among multiple lipid mediator pathways, rather than a simple linear BLT2-dependent mechanism.

12(S)-HETE activates ERK signaling through BLT2

Although TXA2R is known to activate the inositol trisphosphate pathway in cardiomyocytes [15], the downstream signaling of BLT2 in cardiomyocytes remains unclear. To investigate the downstream signaling of BLT2, we focused on the MAP kinase pathway, as BLT2 activation has been reported to induce ERK phosphorylation in primary mouse keratinocytes [16]. Immunoblot analysis revealed that both 12(S)-HETE and the BLT2 agonist CAY10583 induced ERK phosphorylation (Fig. 4A and B). 12(S)-HETE-induced ERK phosphorylation was suppressed by the BLT2 antagonist LY255283, but not by the TXA2R antagonist SQ29548 (Fig. 4C and D). Although Akt and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) have been reported downstream molecules of BLT2 in cancer cells [17,18], neither 12(S)-HETE nor CAY10583 affected their phosphorylation (Supplemental Fig. S4), suggesting that these molecules are not involved in 12(S)-HETE/BLT2 signaling in cardiomyocytes. MI increases serum 12-HETE levels and cardiac ALOX15 expression

The early inflammatory phase after MI (≈4 days in mice) is characterized by robust immune cell infiltration and tissue digestion, which is followed by a resolution and repair phase lasting approximately 10–14 days [19]. Interventions during this reparative phase may facilitate healing, prevent persistent inflammation, and ameliorate late heart failure. Thus, to examine the pathophysiological relevance of 12(S)-HETE, LC-MS/MS-based lipidomic analyses were performed using murine serum 14 days after MI. ALOX15, a major enzyme responsible for 12(S)-HETE biosynthesis [3], was upregulated in the hearts after MI, as determined by qRT-PCR (Fig. 5C). Immunofluorescence analysis of heart sections at day 4 post-MI revealed that ALOX15 was localized in cardiac troponin I+ cardiomyocytes in the infarct/border areas (Fig. 5D). Although macrophage infiltration, fibrosis, and angiogenesis occur in the infarct region, ALOX15 was scarcely detected in CD68+ macrophages, α-SMA+ myofibroblasts, or CD31+ endothelial cells (Supplemental Fig. S5). These results are consistent with a previous report using a murine ischemia-reperfusion model [20]. Taken together, these findings indicate that ALOX15/12(S)-HETE-mediated inflammation may contribute to cardiac remodeling after MI.

Discussion

The pathophysiological role of lipid mediators in cardiac homeostasis remains incompletely understood. In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that 12(S)-HETE directly regulates inflammatory cytokine expression in NRCMs. The novelty of our results is to identify TXA2R and BLT2 as key receptors mediating both pro- and anti-inflammatory effects by 12(S)-HETE and to reveal that BLT2 activates ERK which is known as a key regulator for cardiac homeostasis. Furthermore, we newly show that myocardial infarction increases circulating 12-HETE levels in parallel with cardiac ALOX15 upregulation in vivo. Collectively, our findings uncover a previously unrecognized 12(S)-HETE–receptor signaling axis that orchestrates inflammatory regulation in cardiomyocytes.

ALOX15 has been implicated in the pathogenesis of several cardiovascular diseases. Previous studies reported that ALOX15-deficient mice promoted resolution of cardiac inflammation and improved post-MI cardiac function [11], whereas ALOX15 overexpression induced inflammation and HF [21]. However, phenotypes in ALOX15-deficient mice likely reflect broad alterations in lipid metabolism, including reduced production of pro-inflammatory mediators other than 12(S)-HETE, and increased cytochrome P450 2 J-derived epoxyeicosatrienoic acids, which facilitate inflammation resolution [11]. In addition, intravenously administered 12(S)-HETE is rapidly metabolized, with over 80% cleared immediately [22], making it difficult to assess its in vivo functions directly. Thus, we focused on the direct effects of 12(S)-HETE on cardiomyocytes in vitro to delineate receptor-specific signaling mechanisms. Here, we have demonstrated that 12(S)-HETE induces IL-10 upregulation for the first time, proposing a possibility that 12(S)-HETE signaling could be a novel anti-inflammatory therapeutic target.

Clinical studies further support pathological roles for 12(S)-HETE in cardiovascular disease. Patients with stable coronary artery disease and plasma 12-HETE levels ≥17.39 ng/mL have been shown the elevated risk of acute MI [23]. Increased platelet and urinary 12(S)-HETE levels have been reported in essential hypertension [24], and elevated plasma 12(S)-HETE have been observed in diabetes [25]. These reports suggest that 12(S)-HETE may serve not only as a mediator but also as a potential biomarker for cardiovascular risk.

TXA2R has been shown to exert detrimental effects on the heart. Pharmacological inhibition of TXA2R with BAY u3405 (a.k.a. ramatroban) protected against MI and ischemia-reperfusion injury in animal models [26,27], whereas TXA2R activation by U46619 induced cardiomyocyte death and ventricular arrhythmias via inositol trisphosphate/Ca2+ signaling [15,28]. Along with our results, these data indicate that 12(S)-HETE-mediated TXA2R activation may drive adverse cardiac outcomes. It should be noted that G protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs), including TXA2R, can adopt distinct ligand-dependent conformations that preferentially activate specific downstream signaling pathways, a phenomenon known as biased agonism [29,30]. The limitation of this study is that upstream signaling selectivity was not examined. Therefore, it remains unclear whether 12(S)-HETE activates TXA2R as a non-selective agonist similar to U46619 or acts as a biased agonist preferentially engaging specific signaling pathways. Further studies evaluating individual signaling cascades, such as G protein– and β-arrestin–mediated pathways, would help clarify whether ligand-specific signaling bias is involved and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the pharmacological properties of 12(S)-HETE.

By contrast, the role of BLT2 remains poorly characterized, even in non-cardiac cells. Our data showed that the BLT2 agonist CAY10583 suppressed IL-6 and enhanced IL-10 expression in cardiomyocytes, suggesting anti-inflammatory effects and therapeutic potential. However, the precise contribution of BLT2 to the effects of 12(S)-HETE remains unclear. Given the complex pharmacological responses observed, 12(S)-HETE appears to regulate inflammatory responses in cardiomyocytes through a complex receptor network involving both TXA2R- and BLT2-related signaling pathways. In particular, the regulation of IL-10 expression is likely mediated through multiple receptor-dependent mechanisms rather than a simple linear BLT2-dependent pathway. Accordingly, while BLT2 activation appears sufficient to induce IL-10 expression and promote an anti-inflammatory phenotype, our data do not exclude the involvement of additional receptors or signaling networks that may be required for the full spectrum of 12(S)-HETE-induced effects. Furthermore, previous studies reported pro-inflammatory functions of BLT2 in mouse macrophages [31], suggesting that its biological role may be context-dependent and influenced by cell type and inflammatory environment. Finally, several limitations should be noted. First, the downstream signaling mechanisms of BLT2, as well as the causal relationship among IL-6, IL-10, and ERK activation, remain to be elucidated. These pathways likely involve complex cross-regulatory networks. Second, in vivo studies are needed to assess the effects of BLT2 activation on cardiac inflammation.

ERK signaling is known to regulate a wide range of cellular signals and functions in a context-dependent manner, particularly under different stress conditions. Previous studies showed that ERK1/2 activation coordinated adaptive transcriptional programs that preserved cellular integrity such as cardiac hypertrophy and cardiomyocyte survival under stress conditions [32]. Also, genetic disruption of ERK1/2 led to stress-induced apoptosis and heart failure [33]. Taken together, these findings support the concept that ERK functions as an integrative signaling hub linking stress sensing and cardiomyocyte homeostasis. Based on this evidence, ERK activation via BLT2 may contribute to cardiac homeostasis.

In conclusion, we identified a novel role for 12(S)-HETE signaling via TXA2R and BLT2 in the regulation of cardiac inflammation. These findings highlight 12(S)-HETE-dependent pathways as potential therapeutic targets to prevent inflammation-driven cardiac remodeling and HF.
