Peripheral Nerve Stimulation for Neuropathic Pain Management: A Narrative Review
Authors: Zhangyan Mao, Jing Lv, Yan Sun, Jiwei Shen, Yafen Gao, Shujun Sun, Dong Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This narrative review examines the therapeutic efficacy of peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS) in the treatment of neuropathic pain (NP), a type of pain arising from lesions or diseases of the somatosensory system with a global prevalence ranging from 6.90% to 10.00%. Traditional pharmacological interventions often fall short for many persons, highlighting the need for alternative treatments such as PNS, which has demonstrated significant promise with minimal side effects. The review summarizes the effectiveness of PNS in various NP conditions, including trigeminal neuralgia and postherpetic neuralgia, and underscores the need for further research to refine treatment approaches. The mechanism of PNS is discussed, involving the activation of non-nociceptive Aβ fibers and modulation of neurotransmitters, and offering pain relief through both peripheral and central pathways. Despite the proven efficacy of PNS, challenges remain, including the need for randomized controlled trials and the optimization of stimulation parameters. The review concludes that PNS is a promising treatment modality for NP, warranting additional high-quality trials to solidify its role in clinical practice.

Introduction

Neuropathic pain (NP) is characterized as “pain caused by a lesion or disease of the somatosensory system” and is not a single disease but rather a syndrome resulting from various underlying diseases and lesions, presenting with a range of symptoms and signs [1]. The International Association for the Study of Pain has recently proposed a new classification for chronic pain, which includes NP and distinguishes it into peripheral and central categories, encompassing nine specific conditions such as trigeminal neuralgia, herpes zoster, and spinal cord injury [2, 3]. There is an ongoing debate among scholars about the classification of certain acute pains, such as postsurgical pain, which some argue should be considered under the NP umbrella since the definition does not strictly require the pain to be chronic [4].

There is a notable deficiency in the evaluation of its incidence and prevalence, primarily due to the absence of straightforward diagnostic criteria suitable for large-scale surveys among the general population [5]. The best current estimates suggest that the prevalence of pain with neuropathic characteristics in the population likely falls within the range of 6.90–10.00% [6]. Furthermore, the prevalence of chronic pain, including NP, is observed to escalate with age and is more prevalent in women than in men [7, 8]. There is also growing concern among researchers that the incidence and prevalence of NP may experience a significant increase in the coming years, potentially linked to the ongoing prevalence of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19); thus, further investigation is warranted [9, 10]. Authors would like to point out the increasing and numerous populations with pain that make it significant to pay more attention. And we will not discuss epidemiology investigations further, as this issue is beyond the aims of this paper.

The daily lives of many people are severely disrupted by neuropathic pain and the cascade of accompanying symptoms, such as depression and anxiety, necessitating regular visits to their healthcare providers for treatment. Typically, they are prescribed drug therapy, including medications such as pregabalin, gabapentin, and duloxetine, all of which have strong recommendations for use and are considered first-line treatments. However, the efficacy of these pharmacological treatments for chronic NP is limited, with less than 50.00% of people with pain responding positively, and they may also be plagued by adverse effects that can restrict their clinical application [11]. The use of combined pharmacotherapy has not shown superior outcomes over monotherapy in terms of either efficacy or adverse event profiles [12, 13]. Therefore, the management of intractable NP should extend beyond analgesics to include other effective approaches, such as psychological therapy, physical therapy, integrative therapy, and procedural interventions, which we call multidisciplinary approaches. Among these, neuromodulation techniques have demonstrated promise across a variety of painful conditions [14], and we mainly discuss this issue in the manuscript. But that does not mean other approaches are not important. A systematic review has assessed psychological therapies (excluding headaches) and showed that cognitive behavioral therapy has less benefit when compared with usual treatment, but not with active controls [15]. And psychotherapy (cognitive behavioral therapy and mindfulness) is recommended as a second-line therapy, as an add-on to other therapies in a recent practice guideline [16]. Exercise is perhaps the most commonly recommended self-management strategy, and might improve sleep, facilitate weight loss, stimulate endorphin secretion, and reverse deconditioning [17]. Cohen SP and colleagues also support that best practices on pain management could use a multidisciplinary approach, and safer, less invasive treatments including self-care (weight loss, exercise) should be used before more invasive treatments [17].

Neuromodulation involves the precise application of a stimulus, such as electrical stimulation or chemical agents, to specific nerve locations within the body, with the aim of modifying the nerve’s electrical activity. This therapeutic approach encompasses various techniques, including spinal cord stimulation (SCS), brain stimulation, peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS), and non-invasive stimulation methods [18]. PNS offers distinct advantages over SCS, notably its lower infection rate, minimal impact on physical movement, cost-effectiveness, reduced trauma, extended placement duration, and the elimination of risks associated with central nervous system injury [19–21].

Although existing literature has documented the use of PNS for treating NP, a comprehensive review that encapsulates its mechanisms and actions is lacking. This paper provides an extensive overview of PNS’s role in managing NP, aiming to establish a foundation for its clinical utilization. In addition, in view of the extensive reports on the use of PNS in acute pain in the existing literature, the use of PNS in postoperative pain is also described as a subsection in our review.

Overview of PNS

Evolution and Innovation in PNS

PNS is a medical procedure that involves strategically positioning electrical stimulation leads in proximity to a specific peripheral nerve, with the primary goal of alleviating pain in the area served by that nerve [20, 22]. The concept of using natural electric sources for pain relief dates back to around 47 CE when Scribonius Largus pioneered a method that involved attaching electric rays to their skin or immersing limbs in water filled with these creatures to ameliorate pain [23]. Fast forward to the twentieth century, in 1967, Wall and Sweet documented the first modern instance of PNS, demonstrating that four persons achieved more than half an hour of pain relief following just 2 min of infraorbital nerve stimulation [24].

The groundbreaking report by Wall and Sweet brought PNS to the forefront of surgical interest as a viable treatment for chronic refractory pain. The pioneering clinical investigation in this field is attributed to Campbell JN and Long D in 1967. Their observations indicated that PNS not only was efficacious in managing intractable pain but also led to a reduction in opioid consumption, improvements in sleep quality, and a decrease in depressive symptoms in participants [25]. However, the broader application of implantable PNS was initially impeded due to the complexities of the surgical procedure. The traditional method involved dissecting and neurolysis to expose the targeted nerve, which carried risks of infection, erosion, and postoperative pain [26]. Advances in percutaneous placement techniques and the development of tunneling puncture systems have since mitigated some of these challenges, thereby easing the constraints on PNS usage. Furthermore, the advent of ultrasound-guided percutaneous placement has significantly broadened the range of indications for PNS [27]. An additional concern with percutaneously placed PNS electrodes is the potential for displacement, which can lead to a failure in providing analgesia if the target peripheral nerve is no longer adequately stimulated. The innovative design of electrodes, featuring a spiral shape that allows for flexibility and expansion under external forces, has been effective in minimizing such displacement. The coil design also enhances tissue contact within the body, further stabilizing the electrode’s position [28].

Mechanism of PNS

Our comprehension of PNS is fundamentally rooted in the gate control theory, which was introduced by Melzack and Wall in 1965 [29]. This theory (Fig. 1) posits that the activation of non-nociceptive Aβ nerve fibers can lead to the excitation of spinal dorsal horn interneurons, which play a crucial role in processing and transmitting nociceptive signals. This excitation diminishes the synaptic transmission efficiency of Aδ and C fiber discharges, thereby culminating in pain relief [30]. The analgesic effect of PNS is, in part, due to the downregulation of neurotransmitters, endorphins, and local inflammatory mediators at the molecular level [31]. Wong CE and colleagues researched the effect of sciatic nerve stimulation (SNS) via custom-made bipolar electrodes in L5 nerve root ligation (NRL) model. They observed reduced expression of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and NF-κB after SNS and implied decreased proliferation of actrocyte and activation of microglia, which may be involved in neuroinflammation [32]. A follow-up study by Wong CE showed SNS could suppress satellite glial cell hyperactivation, reduce the expression of NP-related ion channels, and induce M2 macrophage polarization, thereby alleviating NP and associated neuroinflammation in the dorsal root ganglia [33]. The gate control theory is not the sole mechanism through which PNS exerts its effects. Some researchers propose that a central mechanism contributes to PNS’s analgesic effects, as evidenced by the work of Swett and Law. Their study demonstrated that the analgesic effects of PNS can be observed at levels below the pain threshold but above the perception threshold [34]. The complex interaction between activated Aβ fibers and nociceptive Aδ and C fibers is believed to modulate higher centers within the central nervous system [35]. Within the spinal cord, there is evidence to suggest that serotoninergic, dopaminergic, GABAergic, and glycinergic pathways may all participate in the analgesic effects of PNS [36, 37]. Moreover, H.-G. Schaible and colleagues have noted the synergistic role of substance P and calcitonin gene-related peptide in spinal pain perception [38]. Additionally, PNS is thought to potentially disrupt interactions between Aβ non-nociceptive fibers and central pathways at the spinal dorsal horn, enhancing descending inhibition on wide dynamic range neurons [39]. Hewitt D et al. have reported a linear decline in activity within the ipsilateral midcingulate and operculo-insular cortex during low-frequency PNS in healthy individuals, indicating a significant interaction between the cerebral cortex and PNS [40]. The possibility that different PNS patterns may target specific sites of action is particularly intriguing and merits further investigation. In conclusion, it is likely that both peripheral and central mechanisms contribute to the analgesic effects of PNS, with commonalities existing at the level of afferent Aβ, Aδ, and C fibers, which are capable of conducting electrical stimuli to the central nervous system.

Therapeutic Efficacy of PNS for NPs

Trigeminal Neuralgia

Trigeminal neuralgia is a specific type of orofacial NP that affects one or more divisions of the trigeminal nerve [2]. It is categorized into three etiological groups: idiopathic trigeminal neuralgia, which has no discernible cause; classical trigeminal neuralgia, resulting from vascular compression of the nerve’s root; and secondary trigeminal neuralgia, which is a complication of another significant neurological condition [41]. The pharmacological management of trigeminal neuralgia typically involves carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine as first-line treatments. While these medications are effective in alleviating the pain associated with trigeminal neuralgia, their use can be limited by the side effects they may cause [42]. Mark D. Johnson and colleagues conducted a study where they implanted quadripolar electrodes on the supraorbital or infraorbital branches of the trigeminal nerve in 10 people suffering from trigeminal neuralgia. In the long-term follow-up period, which averaged 26.60 months with a standard deviation of 4.70 months, participants reported a significant mean pain relief of 58.00% with a standard deviation of 10.00%, and also demonstrated a decrease in medication usage [43]. Alberto Feletti and co-researchers reported a substantial decrease in pain scores, from an average of 10.00–2.70 on a visual analog scale, during a follow-up period that ranged from 0 to 32.00 months after the implantation of the stimulators. However, this finding was based on a small sample size of six cases [44]. Robert W. Bina and colleagues from a single institution presented a case series that paralleled these results by percutaneously placing electrodes on specific trigeminal nerve branches for stimulation. At the 6-month mark, the median pain reduction was 60.50% with a mean of 59.70%, ranging from 17.00% to 95.00%, and 70.0% of the volunteers reported more than a 50.0% reduction in pain. In the final follow-up, which included 12 participants, the median pain reduction was 47.50% with a mean of 52.30%, ranging from 0.00% to 100.00%, and half of the people reported a greater than 50.00% reduction in pain [45]. Moreover, a recent meta-analysis evaluated the outcomes of implantable nerve stimulation in 221 participants with trigeminal neuralgia across 13 studies. The meta-analysis found an estimated response rate of 61.30% (95% confidence interval 44.40–75.90%, I2 = 70.73%, p < 0.0001) to neuromodulation treatment at the last follow-up. The overall reduction in pain scores was significant, with a decrease of 2.36 points on the pain scale (95% confidence interval 1.41–3.32, I2 = 85.72%, p < 0.0001). Superior clinical outcomes were correlated with the stimulation of the peripheral branches of the trigeminal nerve (p < 0.0001) [46]. Additionally, improvements in quality of life and work status, along with a reduction in analgesic medication usage, have been observed following PNS [43, 47, 48].

The overall incidence of complications is low [49, 50], but it has been reported to be significantly higher compared with spinal cord stimulation (SCS) [26]. One study noted a 30.00% complication rate that necessitated reoperation [43], while another investigation involving 15 persons who underwent permanent system placement found no major adverse events or neurologic complications[45]. Furthermore, Junchi Li and colleagues utilized a percutaneously implanted electrode at the ipsilateral supraorbital notch, along the brow arch, and reported that PNS effectively ameliorated post-traumatic trigeminal NP, reducing pain scores from 9.00 to 1.00, and also alleviated hemifacial dystonia, as indicated by a reduced score from 23.00 to 5.00 on the Burke–Fahn–Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale [51]. They proposed that PNS might modulate the abnormal integration of sensory and motor information, thereby providing relief from dystonia [51]. Further in-depth investigation into the potential mechanisms at play is warranted.

The collective data clearly demonstrate that PNS is an effective, safe, and minimally invasive treatment option for certain people with trigeminal NP, despite the current absence of randomized controlled trials. It is imperative that future research includes high-quality, randomized trials to further substantiate these findings. Our understanding of the underlying mechanisms, particularly how efficacy varies with different etiologies of trigeminal NP such as trauma or herpes zoster, remains limited and requires further investigation [43]. Additionally, a subgroup analysis by Yuncheng Ni and colleagues revealed varying response rates among volunteers based on the specific target of stimulation: 77.60% for the peripheral branch, 29.30% for the trigeminal ganglion, and a comparison with the trigeminal nerve root [46]. Alberto Feletti and co-researchers also noted that positioning the electrodes at the periphery of the allodynia region could optimize pain control [44]. The shared mechanisms behind these phenomena are compelling and warrant additional exploration.

Postherpetic Neuralgia

Postherpetic neuralgia is characterized by pain that persists for over 3 months following the onset or resolution of herpes zoster [2]. Kawai K and colleagues have reported that the incidence rate of herpes zoster fluctuates between 3 and 5 cases per 1000 person-years across North America, Europe, and the Asia–Pacific region [52]. Focusing on China’s prevalence rates, Zhujiazi Zhang and colleagues have calculated a combined incidence of 4.28 per 1000.00 person-years for all age groups, with a notable increase in prevalence among individuals aged 60.00 years and older, reaching 11.69 per 1000.00 person-years. The aggregated risk of developing postherpetic neuralgia is estimated to be 12.60% [53]. The standard combination therapy typically involves the use of gabapentinoids alongside topical analgesics [54]. However, some participants find it challenging to tolerate the side effects of these medications, such as gastrointestinal and psychiatric dysfunction, as well as the risks of addiction and tolerance. Moreover, the efficacy of these drugs as a standalone treatment for intractable postherpetic neuralgia is often disappointingly low [55]. VAS visual analog scale, PNS peripheral nerve stimulation

Nearly 2 decades ago, Edwin Dunteman pioneered the use of a Pisces Quad-4 spinal cord stimulator lead (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) for the treatment of ophthalmic postherpetic neuralgia. The device was positioned over the supraorbital and supratrochlear branches of the trigeminal nerve, situated above the eyebrow’s arch. This approach resulted in documented reductions in pain scores and a decrease in medication requirements for persons suffering from persistent ophthalmic postherpetic pain [50]. Approximately 6 years later, Alexander E. Yakovlev and Irene Kouroukli, along with their respective teams, conducted electrostimulation therapy for postherpetic neuralgia affecting the thoracic region [56, 57]. Their observations during the 3- to 6-month follow-up period post-placement included improvements in pain sensation, a reduction in analgesic medication use, and enhancements in sleep and overall functional status. Remarkably, a subset of people was able to discontinue all pain medications.

Three recent retrospective studies have corroborated the therapeutic efficacy of PNS for postherpetic neuralgia, with no significant side effects reported. One of these studies conducted long-term follow-ups at 6 and 12 months to assess the enduring analgesic impact of PNS. The visual analog scale (VAS) scores indicated a notable reduction, with decreases of 4.80 ± 1.20 at the 6-month follow-ups and 5.40 ± 1.40 at 12-months, respectively [58]. Cheng-fu Wan and colleagues demonstrated that, among 68 participants who underwent short-term PNS, there was a significant alleviation of pain 3 days post-treatment, with a decrease from a baseline score of 7.33 ± 1.87 to 3.39 ± 1.45 on the numerical rating scale. Furthermore, the scores continued to decline gradually throughout the entire follow-up period, which spanned 7, 14, 30, 90, and 180 days after stimulation [59]. Dong-Yang Liu also confirmed these findings, reporting a reduction in pain scores and an improvement in sleep quality following electrostimulation [60]. These studies collectively indicate that short-term PNS can effectively mitigate pain associated with acute or subacute herpes zoster, potentially reducing the likelihood of developing postherpetic neuralgia. The documentation strongly suggests that PNS has the potential to prevent the onset of postherpetic neuralgia, underscoring its value as a therapeutic intervention.

In a study by Jiahui Ma et al. [55], participants were divided into two groups on the basis of neuromodulation approaches. Group 1 received PNS exclusively, whereas Group 2 received a dual modulation strategy, integrating PNS with pulsed radiofrequency treatment of the trigeminal ganglion. The dual approach was more effective in controlling pain than PNS alone, as indicated by visual analog scale (VAS) scores: 3.10 ± 1.20 versus 1.40 ± 1.40 at 1 month, 2.70 ± 1.20 versus 0.80 ± 1.20 at 3 months, and 2.40 ± 1.10 versus 0.40 ± 0.70 at 6 months [55]. These benefits persisted at the 12-month follow-up, with scores further decreasing to 1.60 ± 1.30 for Group 2 and 0.50 ± 0.80 for Group 1.

However, it is crucial to acknowledge that not all cases resistant to medication treatment respond equally to PNS. Disen Li et al. [61] reported a case where a person achieved 50.00% pain relief through PNS, with residual pain in the orbital area rated 4–5 on the visual analog scale. Upon combining PNS with botulinum toxin A treatment, pain scores of the person further decreased to 2–3 [61]. While numerous physicians have shown through their studies that PNS holds promise for treating intractable postherpetic neuralgia, it has only received a grade B recommendation in a recent meta-analysis due to the lack of randomized controlled trials [62]. There is an urgent need for additional high-quality evidence-based studies to reinforce clinical practice. Innovative PNS methods, particularly high-frequency paresthesia-free stimulation techniques that enable fully blinded experiments, merit greater consideration [63]. Lin Zhao and Tao Song have validated a novel PNS approach targeting the maxillary nerve near the external opening of the foramen rotundum, which provides more comprehensive paresthesia coverage for trigeminal postherpetic neuralgia [64]. This outcome aligns with the results reported by Yuncheng Ni et al. for trigeminal neuralgia, as previously mentioned [46]. The factors that contribute to the differential efficacy based on the stimulation location are noteworthy and require further investigation.

Postamputation Pain

Postamputation pain is a common and debilitating condition that can affect up to 90% of amputees, encompassing both residual limb pain and phantom limb pain [65]. Traditional medical therapies often fall short in adequately addressing this profound discomfort. PNS emerges as a viable adjunct to pharmacological interventions [66] (Table 3). Richard L. Rauck and colleagues were the first to report that electrical stimulation significantly alleviated residual limb pain by 60.00% from baseline levels by the conclusion of a 2-week home trial. Moreover, the mean pain interference scores for the Brief Pain Inventory-Short Form and the mean Pain Disability Index scores experienced a notable decrease after the second week of stimulation [67]. Rauck et al. subsequently confirmed these findings in an expanded group. Utilizing ultrasound guidance, they positioned stimulation leads 0.50–3.00 cm away from the sciatic and femoral nerves. Therapeutic efficacy for postamputation pain

PNS peripheral nerve stimulation

A multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled trial led by Christopher Gilmore involved 28 lower-extremity amputees experiencing postamputation pain. Half of the participants received active PNS throughout the study, while the other half underwent placebo stimulation for the initial 4 weeks, followed by active stimulation for an additional 4 weeks. A significant proportion of subjects receiving PNS—58.00% during weeks 1–4 and 67.00% at week 8—reported reduced postamputation pain, compared with 14.00% in the placebo group. After 8 weeks of therapy, 80.00% of those receiving active stimulation noted a decrease in pain interference, in contrast to 15.00% of the placebo group [69]. In a 12-month follow-up, 67.00% of the active stimulation group continued to report reduced weekly pain, a stark contrast to the 0.00% in the placebo group. Improvements in pain interference and depression were also statistically significant [70]. Brooke Albright-Trainer and colleagues conducted a similar study focusing on the efficacy of PNS for acute/subacute postamputation pain. The treatment group, consisting of five participants receiving electrostimulation alongside standard medical therapy, showed a more pronounced reduction in both phantom (76.00% versus 29.00%) and residual pain (86.00% versus 52.00%) compared with the control group of eight receiving standard therapy alone. Additionally, opioid usage decreased to 20.00% in the treatment group by the end of the 8-week study, compared with 50.00% in the control group [71].

Additionally, a study of six cases confirmed the potential of a hybrid approach combining PNS with local anesthetic infusion for challenging postamputation pain, which also led to a reduction in anesthetic dosages [72]. Amol Soin and colleagues explored high-frequency nerve block for postamputation pain management, applying a cuff electrode around the sciatic or tibial nerve. Using 10 kHz sinusoidal waveforms at up to 10 V for 30 min during each session, they achieved an average pain reduction of 75.00% at the 3-month mark in seven subjects, with efficacy sustained up to 12 months [73]. Furthermore, a recent study demonstrated that PNS could elicit tactile and proprioceptive feedback in phantom limbs, enabling two participants to discern the shape of objects using a stylus on a computer tablet with PNS feedback [74].

These studies collectively signal the promise of PNS in postamputation pain management, with significant reductions in pain scores, interference, analgesic use, and improved functionality. High-frequency stimulation stands out as a paresthesia-free alternative. However, a recent systematic review points out the limited evidence level due to clinical and methodological variability, the absence of comparative studies, and small sample sizes [75]. There is a clear need for further high-quality research in this area.

Painful Diabetic Neuropathy

Painful diabetic neuropathy, a prevalent form of polyneuropathy, represents the most frequent complication associated with diabetes mellitus. A cross-sectional study conducted in mainland China from 2015 to 2017, utilizing World Health Organization criteria, revealed a diabetes incidence of 11.20% [76]. An additional study indicated that 52.97% of people with diabetes were clinically diagnosed with distal symmetrical polyneuropathy through nerve conduction studies [77], implying that nearly 90 million individuals in China are affected by diabetic neuropathy. In 2013, Gaurav Thakral and colleagues demonstrated that electrical stimulation could serve as an effective alternative and adjunctive treatment for diabetic peripheral neuropathy [78]. Subsequent research has corroborated the benefits of spinal electrical stimulation for painful diabetic neuropathy, with current level I evidence endorsing the use of 10 kHz and tonic spinal cord stimulation (SCS) for this condition [79, 80].

To date, no specific studies have been identified that focus on the use of PNS devices for painful diabetic neuropathy. However, the closest related research is by M. A. Hamza et al. in 2000, which utilized percutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (PENS) for this condition. They applied PENS at an alternating frequency of 15–30 Hz for 3 weeks to persons with painful diabetic neuropathy, resulting in a significant reduction in pain scores from 6.20 ± 1.00 to 2.50 ± 0.80, as well as decreased reliance on analgesic medication. Additionally, there was an improvement in activity and sleep scores, increasing from 5.20 ± 1.00 and 5.80 ± 1.30 to 7.90 ± 1.00 and 8.30 ± 0.70, respectively [81]. A separate study involving electrical stimulation of the posterior tibial nerve in seven persons with diabetes with polyneuropathy also reported a decrease in pain scores [82]. While PNS shows promise for the management of painful diabetic neuropathy, its application is limited by the inherent characteristics of PNS devices, which are typically designed for named nerves rather than distal symmetrical polyneuropathy. Further long-term clinical trials are required to confirm its efficacy.

Postoperative Pain

Despite PNS being utilized for chronic pain management for many years, it remains a novel approach within the multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia strategies for managing acute and subacute postoperative pain (Table 4) [83, 84]. Research by Brian M. Ilfeld and colleagues has shown significant pain reduction in participants who underwent total knee arthroplasty with PNS. In one study, percutaneous PNS, guided by ultrasound, reduced pain by an average of 93.00% at rest, with complete pain resolution in 80.00% of the subjects. Pain during knee motion also decreased by an average of 27.00–30.00% [85]. Another study reported similar pain relief outcomes, although two subjects experienced prolonged pain beyond 90 days post-surgery [28]. Ilfeld’s team also documented improved pain control and reduced opioid use in persons undergoing various surgeries, including hallux valgus osteotomy [86], rotator cuff repair [87], anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction [88], and ambulatory orthopedic and breast procedures [89]. Additionally, a randomized, sham-controlled pilot study assigned participants to either 14 days of electrical stimulation (n = 32) or sham stimulation (n = 34) in a double-blind setup. The study found reduced pain intensity in the active group (mean ± SD of 1.10 ± 1.10) compared with the sham group (3.10 ± 1.70), as well as decreased opioid consumption [median (interquartile range) of 5.00 mg (0–30.00) for active versus 48.00 mg (25.00–90.00) for sham]. Pain interference with physical and emotional functioning was also diminished, with minimal side effects. Secondary endpoints indicated that analgesic and opioid benefits persisted beyond the 14-day postoperative period, albeit only as short-term effects [90]. As with other PNS applications, this method showed a low infection and erosion rate. Notably, an ongoing study with a planned 12-month follow-up may provide new insights into PNS’s role in postoperative pain management [91].

Oral or continuous transcatheter infusion of opioid analgesics remains the primary treatment for postoperative pain, yet these opioids can cause side effects such as nausea, vomiting, sedation, respiratory depression, and addiction [84]. The pursuit of new solutions is essential, and PNS emerges as a promising alternative, supported by proof-of-concept studies. PNS offers advantages such as a lower infection rate compared with catheter-based methods and fewer side effects than opioids, allowing electrodes to be safely retained at home for over 60 days. The authors are of the opinion that PNS has the potential to transform postoperative pain management [91].

However, challenges remain. Brian M. Ilfeld and colleagues found that implanting electrodes at the suprascapular notch did not provide analgesia after rotator cuff repair surgery. Subsequent attempts involving lead insertion through the middle scalene muscle, targeting the brachial plexus, also showed no reduction in pain scores during the initial 40 min, irrespective of whether subjects initially received active stimulation or sham treatment [86]. Additionally, they encountered electrode dislodgement and fracture [86, 87]. These findings underscore the need for further research to identify the optimal implantation sites and stimulation patterns for specific nerves. The observed risk of electrode migration also calls for refinements in insertion techniques and potential innovation in electrode design.

Conclusion

NP is a widespread issue, affecting an estimated 6.90–10.00% of the global population, with a potential increase expected due to aging demographics and the COVID-19 pandemic [6, 9, 10]. Over half of people with NP do not respond to conventional drug therapies, and even combined pharmacological approaches often prove ineffective [11]. There is an urgent need to explore new treatment options, making PNS a promising alternative. Extensive research on PNS for various NP conditions has shown significant therapeutic benefits in both the short and long term [74]. Additionally, PNS has demonstrated potential in restoring sensations in amputees. The incidence of complications such as infection and erosion is relatively low [19–21, 49, 50, 91]. While studies on the mechanisms of PNS have uncovered both central and peripheral pathways, further understanding of the specific signaling molecules and communication networks is required.

Despite proven efficacy, challenges in PNS for NP remain. Variations in stimulation sites can lead to different therapeutic outcomes, with some studies showing analgesia only after changing the site of stimulation [86], and others indicating different response rates [44, 46, 64]. High-frequency, paresthesia-free stimulations have also been effective in providing pain relief [63], underscoring the need for further research to identify optimal stimulation parameters, especially for painful diabetic neuropathy. While many persons seek endocrinology specialists for primary disease management and pain relief, conventional treatments often fall short in reducing pain perception and improving quality of life [81, 82]. PNS offers a potentially novel solution, but requires more studies to confirm its efficacy. Moreover, the potential of PNS in combination with other treatments for intractable pain conditions is intriguing [52, 61, 72], necessitating randomized clinical trials for validation. The migration tendency of PNS electrodes [85–87] can lead to muscle contraction and reduced effectiveness, highlighting the need for improved insertion techniques and electrode design. Lastly, the varied responses to electrostimulation based on different etiologies of NP [43, 49] emphasize the importance of identifying populations with optimal indications for PNS.

In summary, PNS holds promise in multi-approach pain management, but the need for additional high-quality clinical trials is paramount.
