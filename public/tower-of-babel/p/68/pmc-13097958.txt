Investigation of the Linker‐Length Preferences of Pantetheine Probes in the Cross‐Linking Reactions Between Adenylation Enzymes and Carrier Proteins
Authors: Iyo Arata, Kenji Nagata, Hakuto Miyoshi, Fumihiro Ishikawa, Taichi Chisuga, Toma Kashima, Genzoh Tanabe, Fumitaka Kudo, Tadashi Eguchi, Shinya Fushinobu, Akimasa Miyanaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdenylation enzymes transfer acyl substrates selectively onto carrier proteins (CPs) in natural product biosynthesis. Despite the importance of adenylation enzyme–CP interactions, structural information on these transient complexes remains limited. Previously, we developed a pantetheine cross‐linking probe (named C2Br), which contains an ethylenediamine linker with a reactive bromoacetamide group, and determined the structure of the cross–linked complex of the adenylation enzyme HitB with the CP HitD. Here, we investigated the linker‐length effects of pantetheine probes in the cross‐linking reactions of two adenylation enzymes, HitB and EntE, with CPs using probes with different diamine linkers, such as C2Br and C4Br, the latter containing a longer butanediamine linker moiety. Both adenylation enzymes formed cross‐linked complexes with CPs irrespective of the probe used, but the reaction efficiencies depended on the linker length. Crystal structural analysis showed that the HitB–HitD interface interactions in the HitB–C4Br‐HitD complex are essentially identical to those in the HitB–C2Br‐HitD complex. In contrast, the diamine moieties of probes adopt different interaction modes, accounting for the observed variations in cross‐linking efficiencies. A repertoire of pantetheine probes with varying linker lengths will facilitate structural studies on adenylation enzyme–CP interactions by enabling optimization for each adenylation enzyme.Keywords: adenylation enzyme, cross‐linking reaction, natural product biosynthesis, protein structure, protein–protein interactions

Introduction

Adenylation enzymes are involved in the selective incorporation of acyl units in the biosynthesis of nonribosomal peptides and related natural products [1, 2, 3]. Adenylation enzymes load carboxylic acid substrates onto a carrier protein (CP) through a two‐step reaction. In the first step, the adenylation enzyme specifically activates the carboxy group of the substrate to form an acyl adenylate intermediate. In the second step, canonical thioester‐forming adenylation enzymes transfer the acyl moiety to the terminal thiol group of the phosphopantetheine arm of a holo‐CP, thereby generating an acyl‐CP thioester (Figure 1A and Figure S1A), while amide‐forming adenylation enzymes transfer the acyl moiety onto the amino group of the aminoacyl unit attached to the phosphopantetheine arm of the CP (Figure S1B). The substrates of adenylation enzymes are not limited to proteinogenic α‐amino acids but include a wide range of carboxylic acids, such as nonproteinogenic amino acids, keto acids, and aryl acids [2, 4]. For example, the adenylation enzyme HitB transfers (S)‐β‐phenylalanine onto the standalone CP HitD in the biosynthesis of the macrolactam polyketide hitachimycin [5, 6]. The adenylation enzyme EntE transfers 2,3‐dihydroxybenzoic acid onto the aryl CP (ArCP) domain of EntB in the biosynthesis of the siderophore enterobactin [7, 8]. The substrate specificity of an adenylation enzyme is dictated by a defined set of amino acid residues within the substrate binding pocket [4, 9, 10].

Reaction of an adenylation enzyme with a carrier protein (CP). (A) Two step reaction catalyzed by adenylation enzyme. (B) Cross‐linking reaction using a pantetheine probe to generate an adenylation enzyme–CP covalent complex.

Because the substrate specificity of adenylation enzymes dictates the chemical structures of natural products, structural analyses of adenylation enzymes are important for their rational engineering to generate natural product derivatives for drug development. Adenylation enzymes distinguish their cognate CPs from other CPs through specific protein–protein interactions, which are crucial for the functional transfer of acyl substrates. Several studies have structurally characterized transient adenylation enzyme–CP interactions using acyl vinylsulfonamide adenosine inhibitors, which mimic acyl adenylate intermediates, as molecular probes. These studies revealed that the binding interface interactions are different among various adenylation enzyme–CP pairs [8, 11, 12]. Therefore, further structural information is required to achieve better understanding of the diverse mechanisms of CP recognition by adenylation enzymes. Recently, we developed an alternative strategy for structural analysis of adenylation enzyme–CP complexes. We determined the crystal structure of HitB–HitD cross‐linked complex using a bromoacetamide pantetheine‐type cross‐linking probe in combination with Cys mutation at the position of Asp221 of HitB, which interacts with the substrate amino group in the substrate binding pocket [6]. In this strategy, a bromoacetamide pantetheine probe (C2Br), composed of a pantothenic acid moiety linked to 2‐bromoacetylethylenediamine, was used for the cross‐linking reaction between HitB and HitD (Figure 1B and Figure S1A). The thiol group of the mutated Cys221 residue of HitB nucleophilically attacks the electrophilic bromoacetamide moiety of the C2Br probe loaded onto HitD, forming the HitB–HitD cross‐linked complex. We also performed structural analysis of the amide‐forming adenylation enzyme VinM, which uniquely transfers an aminoacyl moiety onto the amino group of the 3‐aminoisobutyryl unit attached to the phosphopantetheine arm of the standalone CP VinL [13]. Because the VinM–VinL cross‐linked complex was not formed using the C2Br probe, we tested bromoacetamide pantetheine probes with longer diamine linkers (namely C3Br, C4Br, and C6Br probes, containing 1,3‐propanediamine, 1,4‐butanediamine, and 1,6‐hexanediamine linkers, respectively). As a result, the VinM–VinL cross‐linked complex was generated efficiently in the cross‐linking reaction using the C6Br probe. This result indicates that a longer linker length is required for the cross‐linking reaction of amide‐forming adenylation enzyme VinM. The observed linker‐length preference is consistent with the unique aminoacyl acceptor specificity of VinM, which uses the amino group of the 3‐aminobutyryl unit attached to the phosphopantetheine arm (Figure S1B). In contrast, it remains unclear which probe linker length is most suitable for cross‐linking reactions of thioester‐forming adenylation enzymes with CPs, because only the C2Br probe was used in the previous cross‐linking experiment between HitB and HitD [6]. Here, we investigated the linker‐length effects of pantetheine probes in the cross‐linking reactions of thioester‐forming adenylation enzymes HitB and EntE with the CPs. Furthermore, we determined the crystal structure of the HitB–HitD cross‐linked complex using the C4Br probe.

Results and Discussion

Cross‐Linking Reactions between Adenylation Enzymes and CPs Using Various Probes

In this study, we investigated the effect of the diamine linker length of the probe on the cross‐linking efficiency between HitB and HitD. First, we enzymatically loaded C2Br, C3Br, C4Br, and C6Br probes onto Ser36 of apo‐HitD in a one‐pot reaction using CoaA, CoaD, CoaE, and Sfp, generating crypto‐HitD proteins (C2Br‐HitD, C3Br‐HitD, C4Br‐HitD and C6Br‐HitD, respectively) (Figure 1B). We also prepared the HitB D221C mutant, in which the active site residue Asp221 was mutated to Cys, as in the case of structural analysis of the HitB–C2Br‐HitD complex [6]. We then reacted the HitB D221C mutant with each crypto‐HitD, and analyzed the reaction products by SDS‐PAGE. As a result, the HitB D221C mutant formed cross‐linked complexes with each crypto‐HitD (Figure 2A, S2A and S3A). Interestingly, the probe linker length affected the efficiency of the cross‐linking reaction between HitB and HitD. The HitB D221C mutant showed increased cross‐linking efficiency with C4Br‐HitD (195% ± 22% of the amount with C2Br‐HitD) compared to that with C2Br‐HitD. In contrast, the HitB D221C mutant showed decreased cross‐linking efficiency with C3Br‐HitD (44% ± 15% of the amount with C2Br‐HitD) and a similar efficiency with C6Br‐HitD (113% ± 51% of the amount with C2Br‐HitD). The C2Br probe used previously for the structural analysis of the HitB–HitD complex [6] is not the best probe for the cross‐linking reaction between HitB and HitD.

To investigate whether the observed probe linker‐length preference for the HitB–HitD complex is consistent with that for other thioester‐forming adenylation enzymes, we investigated the probe linker‐length effects in the cross‐linking reaction of EntE with the EntB ArCP involved in enterobactin biosynthesis [7]. Similarly, we introduced a Cys mutation at the Asn235 position of EntE, corresponding to Asp221 of HitB, and enzymatically loaded C2Br, C3Br, C4Br, and C6Br probes onto apo‐ArCP to generate crypto‐ArCP proteins. We reacted the EntE N235C mutant with each crypto‐ArCP, and analyzed the reaction product by SDS‐PAGE. As a result, the EntE N235C mutant formed cross‐linked complexes with each crypto‐ArCP (Figure 2B, and Figures S2B and S3B). The efficiency of the cross‐linking reaction between EntE and ArCP depended on the linker length of the probe. The EntE N235C mutant showed increased cross‐linking efficiencies with C3Br‐ArCP (123% ± 18% of the amount with C2Br‐ArCP) and C4Br‐ArCP (167% ± 3% of the amount with C2Br‐ArCP) compared with that for C2Br‐ArCP. The cross‐linking efficiency with C6Br‐ArCP (89% ± 16% of the amount with C2Br‐ArCP) was similar to that with C2Br‐ArCP. Thus, both the HitB D221C mutant and the EntE N235C mutant efficiently formed cross‐linked complexes with CPs in the presence of C4Br probe. Interestingly, the EntE N235C mutant also showed high cross‐linking efficiency with C3Br‐ArCP, whereas the HitB D221C mutant showed low cross‐linking efficiency with C3Br‐HitD, suggesting that HitB and EntE have different probe linker‐length specificities.

Crystal Structure of the HitB–HitD Complex Formed with the C4Br Probe

To investigate why the HitB D221C mutant efficiently formed a cross–linked complex with C4Br‐HitD, we determined the structure of the HitB–C4Br‐HitD complex at 1.85 Å resolution (Table S1 and Figure 3), and compared it with the previously determined structure of the HitB–C2Br‐HitD complex (PDB entry 6M01). In the HitB–C4Br‐HitD complex structure, one HitB–HitD complex molecule is present in the crystallographic asymmetric unit. HitB consists of an N‐terminal Acore domain (Met1–Arg427) and a C‐terminal Asub domain (Gln431–Phe533) with a short hinge region (Val428–Ala430). The N‐terminal (Met1–Arg8) and C‐terminal regions (Thr515–Phe533) are disordered. HitB adopts a thioester‐forming conformation that would catalyze the covalent attachment of (S)‐β‐phenylalanine onto HitD, as also observed in the structure of the HitB–C2Br‐HitD complex. An ADP molecule was observed to be bound at the ATP‐binding site of HitB, as in the case of the structure of the HitB–C2Br‐HitD complex. HitD has a helix‐bundle fold with three major helices (helices I, II, and III). The overall structures of HitB and HitD in the HitB–C4Br‐HitD complex are similar (0.4 Å root‐mean‐square deviation (r.m.s.d.) for 490 Cα atoms and 0.2 Å r.m.s.d. for 78 Cα atoms, respectively) to those in the HitB–C2Br‐HitD complex.

Overall structure of the HitB–C4Br‐HitD complex (PDB entry 9L8H). The Acore domain and Asub domain of HitB are colored green and yellow green, respectively. HitD is colored cyan. An ADP molecule is shown in light gray sticks. The pantetheine linker is shown in cyan sticks.

To investigate whether the probe diamine linker length affects the HitB–HitD interface interactions, we analyzed the binding interface in the HitB–C4Br‐HitD complex structure. As a result, it was found that the HitB–HitD interactions in the HitB–C4Br–HitD complex structure are essentially the same as those in the HitB–C2Br–HitD complex structure. In the HitB–C4Br–HitD complex structure, HitD interacts with both the N‐terminal Acore subdomain and the C‐terminal Asub subdomain of HitB. The loop 1 region (Pro15–Leu34) of HitD interacts with the Asub subdomain of HitB. The side‐chain of Phe16 of HitD forms hydrophobic contacts (3.6–4.7 Å) with the side‐chains of Lys486, Ala487, Arg490, and Ile506 of HitB (Figure 4A). The helix II region (Ser36–Tyr50) of HitD interacts with the Acore subdomain of HitB. The side‐chain of Glu41 of HitD forms salt bridges with the side‐chains of Arg275 (2.8 Å) and His276 (2.7 Å) of HitB (Figure 4B). Glu47 of HitD forms salt bridges (2.8 and 3.1 Å) with Arg249 of HitB. Val40 of HitD makes hydrophobic contacts (3.7–4.1 Å) with Pro248, Pro272, and His276 of HitB (Figure 4C). These salt bridges and hydrophobic contacts are also present in the HitB–C2Br‐HitD complex structure. The importance of these four HitD residues (Phe16, Val40, Glu41, and Glu47) for interactions with HitB has been confirmed by previous mutational analyses [6].

The C4Br pantetheine probe is located in the pantetheine‐binding tunnel of HitB and covalently connected between Ser36 of HitD and the mutated HitB residue Cys221, although the electron density for the pantoic acid moiety was observed to be weak, which might be attributable to a lack of interactions between the pantoic acid moiety and HitB (Figure 5A and Figures S4). The C4Br probe‐derived phosphopantetheine arm shows a few interactions with HitB (Figure 5A). The methylene carbon of the β‐alanine moiety forms hydrophobic contacts (3.6–4.2 Å) with Pro217, Phe220, and Val266 of HitB (Figure S3A). The carbonyl group of the β‐alanine moiety forms a hydrogen bond (2.5 Å) with the side‐chain hydroxy group of Ser216 of HitB. The position of the side‐chain of Ser36 of HitD in the HitB–C4Br–HitD complex structure is shifted around 1 Å from that in the HitB–C2Br–HitD complex structure. This slight movement of the Ser36 side‐chain might be attributable to the different linker lengths of pantetheine probes. The pantoic acid moiety of the C4Br probe adopts a slightly bent conformation to accommodate the longer butanediamine moiety of this probe, while the pantoic acid moiety of the C2Br probe adopts a linear conformation. The slightly bent conformation allows the HitB–HitD interface interactions to be maintained in the structure of the HitB–C4Br–HitD complex. It should be noted that the position of the β‐alanine moiety of the C4Br probe is different from that of the C2Br probe (Figure 5B). Phe220 and Val266 of HitB contact with both the β‐alanine and 1,4‐butanediamine moieties of the C4Br probe in the HitB–C4Br–HitD complex structure (Figure S5A). In contrast, Phe220 and Val266 contact with only the β‐alanine moiety of the C2Br probe in the HitB–C2Br–HitD complex structure (Figure S5B). Although the β‐alanine carbonyl group of the pantetheine arm forms a hydrogen bond with the side‐chain of Ser216 of HitB in both HitB–HitD complex structures, the β‐alanine carbonyl group in the HitB–C4Br–HitD complex structure is shifted 2.5 Å from its position in the HitB–C2Br–HitD complex structure. Thus, the binding mode and interactions of the phosphopantetheine arm are different between these two HitB–HitD complex structures. Considering that the interactions with the C4Br‐derived pantetheine arm are mostly limited to hydrophobic interactions in addition to the hydrogen bond with Ser216, HitB apparently accommodates the C4Br probe in the pantetheine‐binding tunnel without electrostatic or steric repulsion. However, HitB disfavored the C3Br probe, probably because the position of the β‐alanine carbonyl group of the probe is not appropriate for formation of a hydrogen bond with Ser216 (Figure S6). The residues in the pantetheine‐binding tunnel are not conserved among adenylation enzymes (Figure 5C). For example, Ser216 and Phe220 of HitB are substituted by Ile230 and His234 in EntE, respectively. This suggests that the interactions of the pantetheine linker with EntE are different from those with HitB, which may explain why EntE had a different linker‐length preference from that of HitB in the cross‐linking reaction (Figure 2).

The probe linker‐length preference in the cross‐linking reactions may depend on the enzyme type and the position of the introduced Cys residue. For example, the C2Br probe has been used for cross‐linking reactions of acyltransferase (AT) domains (e.g., the vicenistatin trans‐AT VinK [14], the disorazole trans‐AT DisD [15], and type II fatty acid synthase AT FabD [16]) with CPs. In these cases, the catalytic Ser residue of each AT was mutated to Cys, which has a side‐chain of similar size. In contrast, cross‐linking of the cremimycin polyketide synthase split‐dehydratase domain with CP occurred with the C3Br probe but not with the C2Br probe [17]. In this case, the catalytic Asp residue was replaced with a smaller Cys residue. Therefore, a longer linker length was required for the bromoacetamide reacting group to reach the mutated Cys residue during the cross‐linking reaction. Thus, systematic testing of diverse pantetheine probes is necessary to optimize the cross‐linking conditions for structural analysis of biosynthetic enzyme–CP complexes. In this study, Asp221 of HitB and Asn235 of EntE were mutated to a smaller Cys residue. The linker length of the C2Br probe was acceptable, but the longer linker of the C4Br probe was more suitable for the bromoacetamide reacting group to access the mutated Cys residues of the adenylation enzymes such as HitB and EntE during the cross‐linking reactions with CPs.

The phosphopantetheine‐binding tunnel of adenylation enzyme appears to provide sufficient conformational space to accommodate linker variations. The structural comparison of the HitB–C2Br–HitD and HitB–C4Br–HitD complexes suggests that the overall adenylation enzyme–CP interface remains essentially unchanged despite the increased linker length. This structural tolerance implies that even an extended phosphopantetheine arm can be accommodated within the phosphopantetheine‐binding tunnel. Although the present study focuses on cross‐linking efficiency, variations in linker length may also influence the efficiency of the adenylation enzyme‐catalyzed substrate transfer to the phosphopantetheine thiol of CP. Addressing this question will require future kinetic analyses using nonelectrophilic pantetheine analogs containing various aminoalkylthiol linkers, in which the cysteamine moiety of the phosphopantetheine arm is replaced with longer aminoalkyl chains analogous to those used in the cross‐linking probes.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the effect of the diamine linker length of pantetheine probes on the cross‐linking reaction between adenylation enzymes and CPs. Although the efficiency of cross‐linking reactions of HitB and EntE depended on the diamine linker length of the pantetheine probe, in the crystal structure of the HitB–HitD cross‐linked complex with the C4Br probe, the interface interactions between HitB and HitD in the structure of the HitB–C4Br–HitD complex are essentially identical to those in the structure of the HitB–C2Br–HitD complex. This finding suggests that interface interactions between adenylation enzymes and CPs are not affected by the linker length of the pantetheine probe used. In contrast, the diamine moieties of pantetheine probes adopt different interaction modes, accounting for the observed variations in cross‐linking efficiencies. A repertoire of pantetheine probes with different linker lengths will facilitate structural studies on adenylation enzyme–CP interactions by enabling the selection of probes compatible with the linker‐length preference of the individual adenylation enzyme. Overall, these results provide important insights into structural studies of biosynthetic enzyme–CP complexes and contribute to a deeper mechanistic understanding of protein–protein interactions in natural product biosynthesis.

Experimental Section

Cross‐Linking Reaction of HitB with HitD

For HitB–HitD cross‐linking assay, apo‐HitD protein was first modified with each bromoacetamide pantetheine probe (C2Br, C3Br, C4Br, or C6Br) in one‐pot enzymatic reactions. 600 µM of bromoacetamide pantetheine probe was mixed with 15 mM MgCl2, 10 mM ATP, 1.5 µM CoaA, 2.1 µM CoaD, 1.8 µM CoaE, 7.5 µM Sfp, and 200 µM apo‐HitD in buffer containing 50 mM HEPES‐Na (pH 8.0) and 10% (v/v) glycerol (total solution volume 50 µl). The modified reaction was performed at 28ºC for 3 h. Complete conversion to crypto‐HitD was confirmed by HPLC analysis (Figure S7A–E). The cross‐linking reaction was then initiated by adding 50 µM of the HitB D221C mutant or wild‐type into the reaction mixture, which was then incubated at 20ºC for 2 min to 18 h. The total solution volume was increased to 100 µl, and the HitD concentration was diluted to 100 µM, by adding the HitB solution. After the reaction was quenched by the addition of the SDS‐PAGE loading buffer, the cross‐linked products were analyzed by SDS‐PAGE. The relative molar ratio of cross‐linked complexes in the 2 min reaction sample was quantified using GelAnalyzer (http://www.gelanalyzer.com). This experiment was performed in triplicate.

Cross‐Linking Reaction of EntE with ArCP

For EntE–ArCP cross‐linking assay, apo‐ArCP protein was first modified with each pantetheine probe (C2Br, C3Br, C4Br, or C6Br) in one‐pot enzymatic reactions. 300 µM of bromoacetamide pantetheine probe was mixed with 15 mM MgCl2, 10 mM ATP, 1.0 µM CoaA, 1.0 µM CoaD, 1.0 µM CoaE, 5 µM Sfp, and 50 µM apo‐ArCP in buffer containing 50 mM HEPES‐Na (pH 7.0) and 10% (v/v) glycerol (total solution volume 50 µl). The modified reaction was performed at 20ºC for 3 h. Complete conversion to crypto‐ArCP was confirmed by HPLC analysis (Figure S7F–J). The cross‐linking reaction was then initiated by adding 15 µM of the EntE N235C mutant or wild‐type into the reaction mixture, which was then incubated at 20ºC for 2 min to 18 h. The total solution volume was increased to 100 µl, and the ArCP concentration was diluted to 25 µM, by adding the EntE solution. After the reaction was quenched by the addition of the SDS‐PAGE loading buffer, the cross‐linked products were analyzed by SDS‐PAGE. The relative molar ratio of cross‐linked complexes in the 5 min reaction sample was quantified using GelAnalyzer. This experiment was performed in triplicate.

Purification of the HitB–C4Br–HitD Complex

450 µM C4Br probe was first mixed with 7.5 mM MgCl2, 5 mM ATP, 1.5 µM CoaA, 2.1 µM CoaD, 1.8 µM CoaE, 7.5 µM Sfp, and 300 µM HitD in buffer containing 50 mM HEPES‐Na (pH 8.0) and 10% (v/v) glycerol (total solution volume 1.0 ml). After incubation at 28ºC for 3 h, the cross‐linking reaction was initiated by the addition of 50 µM HitB D221C mutant directly into the reaction mixture. The total solution volume was increased to 2.0 ml and the HitD concentration was diluted to 150 µM by adding the HitB D221C mutant solution. After incubation at 20ºC for 14 h, the generated HitB–C4Br–HitD complex was purified using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column (Cytiva) and Mono Q column (Cytiva).

Structural Determination of HitB–C4Br–HitD Complex

Crystals of the HitB–C4Br–HitD complex were grown at 20ºC using the sitting‐drop vapor diffusion method after mixing protein solution (7.5 mg/ml in 5 mM HEPES‐Na (pH 8.0) and 10% (v/v) glycerol) with an equal volume of reservoir solution (90 mM Tris‐HCl (pH 8.5), 180 mM sodium acetate, 20% (v/v) PEG4000 and 5% (v/v) PEG400). Crystals were flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen before X‐ray data collection. Diffraction data were collected at beamline BL‐5A at the Photon Factory (PF) of the High Energy Accelerator Research Organization (KEK, Tsukuba, Japan) and then processed with XDS software [18]. The initial phase was determined using the Molrep program [19] with the HitB–C2Br–HitD structure (PDB entry 6M01) as the search model. Coot [20] was used for manual rebuilding of the model. Refmac [21] was used to refine the structure. Molecular graphic images were prepared using PyMOL (https://pymol.org/2/). The geometries of the final structure of the HitB–C4Br–HitD complex were evaluated using the program MolProbity [22]. A polder map was prepared using PHENIX [23]. The atomic coordinates and structure factors have been deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB entry 9L8H).
