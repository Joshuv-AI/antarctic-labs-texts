Plasma metabolomic profiling identifies a metabolic signature for non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer independent of hematuria
Authors: Roberto Speziale, Valerio Iacovelli, Guido Leoni, Luca Anzillotti, Francesca Puca, Chiara Cipriani, Marco Carilli, Alessandro Carugo, Carlo Toniatti, Pierluigi Bove
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) accounts for approximately 75% of bladder cancer cases and, despite a generally favorable prognosis, requires lifelong cystoscopic surveillance, resulting in substantial clinical burden. Non-invasive biomarkers with metabolic and translational relevance are needed to improve disease detection and patient stratification. Plasma represents a stable systemic matrix that captures tumor-associated metabolic alterations while minimizing pre-analytical variability.ResultsTargeted metabolomic profiling of 630 metabolites and 252 metabolic indicators was performed in plasma samples from 249 individuals, including 51 patients with NMIBC and 198 control individuals. Integrated univariate and multivariate analyses were used to identify discriminant metabolites, assess pathway-level perturbations, and develop diagnostic models. Model robustness was evaluated with respect to hematuria status. A total of 29 metabolites and 17 metabolic indicators were significantly altered in NMIBC. The dominant metabolic signature involved lipid and bile acid metabolism, characterized by reduced conjugated bile acids and increased lysophosphatidylcholines and polyunsaturated fatty acid species. Pathway enrichment analysis indicated perturbations in bile acid biosynthesis, PUFA turnover, glutathione metabolism, and glycolytic pathways. A diagnostic model based on 11 metabolites achieved high accuracy (AUC = 0.92 in the training set and 0.88 in the test set). Hematuria status did not affect clustering or model performance.ConclusionsPlasma metabolomic profiling identifies a systemic metabolic signature associated with lipid and bile acid dysregulation in NMIBC and supports the development of clinically applicable, non-invasive plasma-based approaches for bladder cancer detection and patient stratification. Background

Bladder cancer (BC) represents a major global health burden, ranking as the tenth most common malignancy worldwide [1, 2]. Approximately 75% of newly diagnosed cases are classified as non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), including Ta, T1, and carcinoma in situ (CIS). Despite a relatively favorable prognosis, NMIBC is characterized by high recurrence rates (up to 60–70%) and a variable risk of progression to muscle-invasive disease, necessitating lifelong surveillance through cystoscopy and urine cytology [1–3]. While effective, this approach is invasive, costly, and burdensome for patients, particularly during long-term follow-up. Consequently, there is a strong clinical need for accurate, non-invasive biomarkers to improve early detection and disease monitoring. Current non-invasive diagnostic strategies largely rely on urinary biomarkers, predominantly protein-based, which are often affected by substantial pre-analytical variability and limited reproducibility, particularly in early-stage disease. Urine has historically been the primary biofluid for biomarker discovery in bladder cancer due to its direct contact with the tumor and ease of non-invasive collection. Accordingly, numerous urinary biomarker signatures, including metabolomic profiles, have been proposed with promising diagnostic performance. However, urinary biomarkers are highly susceptible to pre-analytical variability driven by hydration status, urine flow, hematuria, inflammation, and other physiological factors, which limit their reproducibility and clinical translation, particularly in early-stage disease [4, 5]. In this context, plasma represents a physiologically stable and clinically accessible matrix that reflects tumor-associated systemic metabolic alterations and is less susceptible to external confounding factors [6–10]. Several studies have identified circulating metabolite panels capable of differentiating bladder cancer patients from healthy individuals or high-risk populations such as smokers, highlighting the potential of plasma-based assays to complement or potentially replace urine-based diagnostics in selected clinical settings [4, 8, 10].

Importantly, the biological heterogeneity of bladder cancer further underscores the need for robust and clinically applicable biomarkers. Recent multi-omics studies have shown that urothelial tumors can harbor highly diverse genomic, transcriptomic, and immunologic profiles, even within comparable pathological stages. Despite encouraging progress, plasma metabolomics in bladder cancer remains at an early translational stage, with limited standardization and independent validation, underscoring the need for rigorously designed clinical studies. Furthermore, most investigations have been conducted on relatively small and heterogeneous populations, restricting generalizability and delaying biomarker qualification.

Building upon this evidence, we systematically evaluated plasma metabolomic profiles in a well-characterized and statistically powered cohort of 249 individuals, including patients with early-stage NMIBC and control individuals. We hypothesized that NMIBC is associated with a reproducible plasma metabolic signature reflecting systemic metabolic remodeling and that this signature remains detectable independently of hematuria. Accordingly, the aims of this study were to (i) identify reproducible plasma metabolic signatures associated with NMIBC and (ii) evaluate their diagnostic performance, with the ultimate goal of advancing clinically applicable, non-invasive plasma-based assays for bladder cancer detection and monitoring.

Methods

Study population and sample collection

Plasma samples were collected at the Urology Unit of San Carlo di Nancy Hospital (Rome, Italy) between July 2022 and July 2023, following written informed consent from all participants. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of San Camillo Forlanini Hospital (Rome, Italy), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments (approval number: STS CE Lazio 1/Oss-823). Whole blood was collected in EDTA-coated tubes, kept at 4 °C, and processed within 4 h of collection. Plasma was isolated by centrifugation, aliquoted into cryovials, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. To preserve metabolite integrity and ensure analytical stability, all samples underwent a maximum of two freeze–thaw cycles prior to metabolomic profiling. Sample collection, processing, and storage were conducted according to the consensus standard operating procedures established by the Early Detection Research Network (EDRN) for plasma biomarker studies [11]. The study population comprised two groups: (1) patients diagnosed with non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), referred to as the case group, and (2) healthy control individuals. Exclusion criteria for the NMIBC group included prior diagnosis of genitourinary cancer, NMIBC recurrence, pelvic radiotherapy, intravesical chemo- or immunotherapy, and active urinary tract infection. In addition to clinical and pathological variables, structured information was collected on key demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, comorbidity burden assessed by the Charlson Comorbidity Index, ECOG performance status, and presence of gross hematuria. Both male and female participants were included in the study; sex was considered in descriptive and exploratory analyses but was not used as a stratification variable in model development. These data are summarized in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1. No participants were excluded after enrollment, and no missing data were observed for the variables included in the final analysis.

Reagents and analytical materials

All reagents and materials used for targeted metabolomics analysis were either provided within the MxP® Quant 500 kit (Biocrates Life Sciences AG, Innsbruck, Austria) or purchased separately. Ethanol, methanol, water, acetonitrile, and isopropanol were all LC-MS grade, Formic acid (LC-MS grade) was purchased from Sigma Supelco™ (533002-50ML). Phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC) ≥ 99% purity, suitable for protein sequencing, was purchased from Sigma (ref. 317861) and pyridine ≥ 99% purity, was purchased from Merck. Ammonium acetate was of LC-MS grade, and phosphate buffered saline (PBS) was of analytical grade (e.g., Merck P4417). All solvents used in the preparation of mobile phases and extraction buffers were LC-MS grade to ensure analytical quality and compatibility with the mass spectrometric platform. Sample preparation steps requiring filtration and extraction were carried out using a pressure manifold for 96-well plates (Presston 1000, Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) operated as a positive pressure processor (PPM-96). Metabolomic analysis was conducted using the MxP® Quant 500 kit (Biocrates Life Sciences AG, Innsbruck, Austria), a standardized, quantitative assay capable of detecting and quantifying up to 630 metabolites across 26 biochemical classes (13 small-molecule and 13 lipid classes). All analyses were performed in a Biocrates-certified laboratory, where the method had been previously validated according to the manufacturer’s guidelines to ensure analytical reproducibility and data comparability (see Supplementary Table S2). The assay workflow combines sample derivatization with phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC), solvent extraction, and dilution, followed by LC-MS/MS and FIA-MS/MS analysis. Briefly, 10 µL of plasma were applied to a 96-well filter plate pre-loaded with internal standards and dried under nitrogen. Derivatization was performed by adding 50 µL of 5% PITC solution, followed by incubation, drying, and extraction using 300 µL of 5 mM ammonium acetate in methanol. The extracts were diluted with running solvents for LC or FIA and transferred to the lower plate for analysis. Each run included a blank, zero sample, seven calibration standards (Cal 1–7), and three quality control samples (QC1–QC3). In accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications, intra- and inter-assay accuracy and precision were within ± 15% for most metabolites and within ± 20% at the lower limit of quantification, consistent with Biocrates quality criteria. (See supplementary material Table S3).

Targeted metabolomics by LC–MS/MS

Mass spectrometry analysis was carried out using a SCIEX QTRAP® 6500 + mass spectrometer (Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source and operated in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The instrument was coupled to a Waters Acquity UPLC H-Class system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation was performed using the Biocrates MxP® Quant 500 column system, consisting of a connector, pre-column mixer, and analytical column. Two independent LC methods (LC1 and LC2) were applied with injection volumes of 5 µL and 15 µL, respectively. The mobile phases consisted of solvent A (0.2% formic acid in water) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.2% formic acid). LC separation was performed using short reversed-phase gradients optimized for amines and other small molecules. Gradient and flow rate conditions are described in supplementary material Table S4 (UPLC and FIA Chromatographic Conditions for Metabolomic Analysis). FIA-MS/MS analysis was used for the detection of lipids and hexoses, using 20 µL injections and a 3-minute isocratic run with Biocrates FIA solvent (methanol-based running buffer). The FIA method was operated under constant flow conditions optimized for metabolite class specificity, as detailed in supplemental material Table S4. All mass spectrometric data were acquired using Analyst® software version 1.7 (Sciex), with optimized compound-specific MRM transitions and acquisition parameters provided by the kit manufacturer.

Data preprocessing and quality control

Raw data were acquired using Analyst® software (Sciex) and processed with WebIDQ™ Oxygen (Biocrates). Quantification was based on external calibration curves for LC–MS analytes and internal standard normalization for FIA–MS analytes, and concentrations were reported in µM. For values below the limit of detection (LOD), a logspline imputation method was applied. Metabolites with ≥ 20% of values below the LOD were excluded from statistical analyses in accordance with the 80% inclusion rule. Missing values due to technical reasons (i.e., non-quantified signals not attributable to LOD censoring) were handled separately: metabolites with more than 5% missing values across samples were excluded prior to downstream analyses. Metabolite sums and ratios (MetabolINDICATORs™) were computed using validated algorithms to support biological interpretation. Metabolomic data preprocessing and exploratory analyses were conducted without access to clinical outcome variables other than case–control assignment. Outliers were identified and removed prior to statistical evaluation. The analytical procedures underlying the Biocrates MxP® Quant 500 platform, including sample derivatization, chromatographic separation, detection, and quantification, are described in detail in European patents EP1897014B1 and EP1875401B1. Supplementary Table S5 provides a comprehensive list of metabolites analyzed in this study, including their abbreviations, full names, biochemical classes, and unique identifiers from the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) and Chemical Abstracts Service (CAS). No formal a priori power calculation was performed, as the study was based on the availability of well-characterized clinical samples. However, the cohort size is comparable to or larger than those reported in previous metabolomics biomarker discovery studies in bladder cancer.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0). Univariate comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. The MetaboAnalystR package (RRID: SCR_016723) [12]. was used to identify significantly enriched SMPDB metabolic pathways, defined by a p-value ≤ 0.05. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using established R packages (FactoMineR and factoextra) [13]. As this was an observational case–control study, no randomization was applicable. Prior to multivariate analysis, metabolites detected in less than 95% of samples were removed, data were log₂-transformed, and missing values were imputed using the imputePCA function from the missMDA R package [14], with the number of components set to two (ncp = 2). For model development, the cohort was split into training (70%) and test (30%) subsets. Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed using the ropls package (RRID: SCR_016888) on metabolites retained after preprocessing. Model validity was assessed by permutation testing (100 permutations) to evaluate the significance of R²Y and Q²Y values. All NMIBC samples from patients presenting with hematuria were included in the training set, whereas the test set was restricted to individuals without hematuria. This design was defined a priori to assess biomarker robustness in the clinically relevant hematuria-negative setting while minimizing potential confounding and data-driven optimization. Finally, metabolites with a Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) score > 1 were used to develop a Generalized Linear Model (GLM), which was trained and evaluated on the same training and test datasets used for the OPLS-DA. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and accuracy estimates were generated using the pROC package [15].

Results

Cohort characteristics and study workflow

A total of 249 individuals were included in the study, comprising 51 patients with early-stage non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC; stages Ta and T1) and 198 healthy controls. The study population included 48 participants presenting with gross hematuria (36 NMIBC and 12 controls) and 201 without hematuria. As expected, hematuria was markedly more prevalent among NMIBC patients (70.6%) than in controls (6.1%). A higher proportion of ever smokers (current or former) was observed in the NMIBC group compared with controls (58.8% vs. 19.7%), consistent with smoking being a major established risk factor for bladder cancer. No relevant differences were detected in age, body mass index (BMI), or other clinical parameters between groups (Table 1). To investigate whether selected clinical and demographic variables influenced the metabolic profiles, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed after stratification by smoking status, comorbidities, and gender. No distinct clustering was observed according to any of these variables, indicating that these factors did not drive the overall metabolic variability. A mild trend toward separation was, however, observed among participants with hematuria, consistent with the known clinical association between hematuria and bladder cancer presentation (see Supplementary Figure S1) [4, 16].

Peripheral blood was collected from all participants, and plasma was isolated and stored under standardized conditions. Quantification of 630 endogenous metabolites and 252 metabolic indicators was performed using the Biocrates MxP® Quant 500 kit [17–20]. After quality control filtering, 403 metabolites and 78 metabolic indicators were retained for statistical analysis (see Supplementary Table S6). The complete dataset, including all analytical measurements for the samples analyzed in this study, is provided in the Supplementary Material.

Univariate statistical analysis identified a total of 29 individual metabolites and 17 metabolic indicators that were significantly modulated in NMIBC patients compared to healthy controls (two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test, |log₂FC| ≥ 0.3; BH-adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05) [10, 20]. (Fig. 2A, B).

The most marked reductions involved bile acid conjugation processes, including glyco-conjugated forms of chenodeoxycholic acid (GCDCA) and deoxycholic acid (GDCA), as well as tauro-conjugated DCA. In parallel, decreases in GABA-related indicators and arginine-derived ratios (HA-arg/SDMA and HA-arg/ADMA) were observed. Conversely, activity scores for lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and phospholipase A₂ (PLA₂) were increased. A shift toward kynurenine pathway activation, reflected by higher indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) activity, was also detected. Additional variations included changes in glutamine utilization, xanthine metabolism, and trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) levels, together with elevated ω-3 fatty acids and monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA). The most prominent alterations affected lipid metabolism, with significant differences in several conjugated bile acids (TDCA, GCDCA, GCA, GDCA). Additional modulations were observed in lysophosphatidylcholines (e.g., LPC 18:2), phosphatidylcholines, ceramides, and multiple triglyceride species. Pathway enrichment analysis identified significant perturbations in bile acid metabolism, polyunsaturated fatty acid pathways, glutathione metabolism, and glycolysis. Altogether, these results highlight a coordinated pattern of metabolic alterations in NMIBC plasma, involving bile acid conjugation, lipid composition, amino-acid–related indicators, and energy-related pathways.

Pathway dysregulation and metabolic clustering

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the 29 plasma metabolites significantly modulated in NMIBC, to investigate inter-individual variability and uncover groups of BC with characteristic metabolic profiles. While a partial overlap between NMIBC and control samples was observed, a distinct subset of NMIBC patients (48%) clustered predominantly in Quadrant II, indicating a metabolically distinct tumor sub-group (Fig. 3A).

This subgroup exhibited coordinated elevations in free fatty acids (FAs), most notably arachidonic acid (FA 20:4n-6) and docosahexaenoic acid (FA 22:6n-3) [21]. accompanied by reductions in conjugated bile acids, including TDCA, GCDCA, and GCA (Fig. 3B). To gain biological insight into these metabolic shifts, an Over-Representation Analysis (ORA) was performed using curated pathway definitions from the Small Molecule Pathway Database (SMPDB). A total of 12 pathways were significantly enriched (Fig. 3C), with linoleic acid, alpha-linolenic acid, and arachidonic acid (FA 20:4n-6) metabolism ranking among the most significant, consistent with the accumulation of circulating PUFAs in this NMIBC subgroup [4, 8, 9, 21].

Hematuria does not impact plasma metabolic signatures

To evaluate whether hematuria influences the plasma metabolomic profile associated with NMIBC, we first assessed its distribution within the study cohort. As expected, hematuria was significantly associated with NMIBC diagnosis (Chi-square test, p ≤ 0.0001; Fig. 4A), consistent with its role as a primary symptom leading to urologic referral. Despite this association, metabolite profiles within both the NMIBC and control groups were broadly interspersed (Fig. 4B), indicating that hematuria represents an independent clinical feature rather than a driver of the observed metabolite-based discrimination.

Derivation and diagnostic performance of an NMIBC metabolite panel

To develop a predictive metabolite signature for NMIBC, we applied a supervised Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA), a well-established multivariate technique widely used in metabolomics for identifying class-discriminating features [8, 23, 24]. The dataset was divided into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%) using stratified random sampling. All NMIBC patients with hematuria were allocated exclusively to the training set, while the test set included only samples from individuals without hematuria (Fig. 5A). This design aimed to simulate a real-world diagnostic setting in which hematuria—although common—is not always present at the time of disease detection.

All hematuria-positive NMIBC samples were included in the training set; the test set contains only hematuria-negative individuals. (B) Cumulative R²Y and Q²Y values of the OPLS-DA model constructed using the full metabolomics dataset generated with the Biocrates MxP® Quant 500 kit. (C) Confusion matrix and classification accuracy of the OPLS-DA model on the training and test sets. (D) Selection of 11 discriminant metabolites based on VIP score ≥ 1, absolute log₂ fold change ≥ 0.3, and Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 from the univariate analysis. (E) Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves for the GLM model built on the selected metabolite panel. The area under the curve (AUC) is shown for both training (0.922) and test sets (0.880). Performance of the combined model is compared to that of individual metabolites

The resulting OPLS-DA model showed strong classification performance, with a cumulative R²Y of 0.622 and Q²Y of 0.386 across four components (Fig. 5B). The model achieved 94% accuracy in the training set and 89% in the test set (Fig. 5C), confirming its predictive ability even in the absence of hematuria. To enhance model interpretability and reduce potential overfitting, we subsequently developed a Generalized Linear Model (GLM) using a subset of 11 discriminant metabolites, selected based on univariate significance, VIP scores ≥ 1.1, and log₂ fold change ≥ |0.3| with Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 (Fig. 5D). The GLM showed excellent discriminatory performance, achieving an AUC of 0.92 on the training set and 0.88 on the test set (Fig. 5E). The combined metabolite panel significantly outperformed any individual metabolite, highlighting the added value of multivariate integration in biomarker discovery. Moderate Q² values are commonly observed in supervised multivariate models applied to biologically heterogeneous clinical cohorts and are consistent with previous metabolomics biomarker discovery studies. Importantly, model robustness was supported by permutation testing, independent test-set validation, and consistent diagnostic performance in hematuria-negative samples. Together, these analyses indicate that the observed discrimination reflects biologically meaningful metabolic differences rather than model overfitting.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that plasma metabolomics identifies distinct metabolic alterations associated with NMIBC and enables diagnostic discrimination independent of hematuria. The primary aim was to define a non-invasive plasma-based metabolic signature capable of distinguishing NMIBC patients from healthy individuals. Plasma was selected as the analytical matrix because it reflects systemic metabolic alterations associated with tumor biology and host responses, while offering greater analytical stability and lower pre-analytical variability than urine. Compared with urine, which primarily reflects local excretory processes, plasma provides a more consistent matrix for quantitative metabolomics and facilitates clinical translation by minimizing normalization-related challenges. Consistent with previous metabolomic investigations, urine metabolomic profiles are inherently influenced by pre-analytical factors such as hydration status, diet, and sample collection procedures [4, 8, 9, 17, 22]. The absence of clustering by smoking status, comorbidities, or gender further supports that the observed metabolic alterations are tumor-associated rather than driven by lifestyle or demographic factors [23]. Notably, recent integrative analyses have highlighted the marked molecular heterogeneity of bladder cancer. For example, Scimeca et al. reported a deeply profiled high–tumor mutational burden bladder carcinoma characterized by extensive genomic alterations, deregulated immune checkpoint expression, and pronounced hypoxia-related signatures, illustrating the biological complexity of the disease and the challenges it poses for clinical stratification [24]. To derive a diagnostic metabolic signature for NMIBC, we applied supervised OPLS-DA modeling, a widely used multivariate approach for identifying class-discriminating features in metabolomics [19, 25].

The model achieved an accuracy of 94% in the training set and 89% in the test set, including hematuria-negative patients, confirming robust discriminative performance despite moderate Q² values, which are acceptable for biologically heterogeneous clinical datasets. A panel of 29 metabolites and 17 metabolic indicators consistently differentiated NMIBC patients from controls. These findings are consistent with emerging evidence that systemic metabolic alterations can be detected in peripheral blood even in early-stage bladder cancer. As expected for discovery-phase metabolomic studies, these findings will require validation in larger, independent cohorts to confirm diagnostic generalizability.

The most prominent metabolic alterations involved the lipidome, characterized by reduced conjugated bile acids (TDCA, GCDCA, GCA, and GDCA) and increased levels of lysophosphatidylcholines and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). The detection of these alterations in peripheral plasma supports the presence of systemic remodeling of lipid and bile acid homeostasis rather than a purely local excretory phenomenon. Conjugated bile acids such as TDCA, GCDCA, GCA, and GDCA act as key ligands of FXR and TGR5, modulating enterohepatic signaling, glucose and lipid metabolism, and inflammatory tone. Their coordinated decrease in NMIBC, together with enrichment of circulating PUFAs and lysophosphatidylcholines, is consistent with early disruption of the liver–gut–tumor axis and enhanced membrane lipid turnover, as also suggested by increased PLA₂ activity scores. Importantly, these lipid classes are analytically robust and routinely measurable in clinical laboratories, supporting the potential translational applicability of the identified plasma signature as a metabolically informed, non-invasive biomarker panel [26, 27]. A subgroup of NMIBC patients exhibited distinct clustering in PCA, characterized by elevated arachidonic acid (FA 20:4n-6) and docosahexaenoic acid (FA 22:6n-3) together with reduced bile acids, suggesting the presence of a potential metabolic endotype. Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted perturbations in linoleic acid, α-linolenic acid, and arachidonic acid metabolism (Fig. 3C), consistent with previous reports linking fatty acid remodeling and bile acid homeostasis to tumor burden and disease progression in bladder cancer [4, 8, 18]. Reduced bile acid conjugation—including glyco-conjugation of GCDCA and GDCA and taurine conjugation of DCA—further supports perturbed bile acid metabolism in NMIBC, in line with mechanisms described in hepatobiliary and cancer-associated metabolic remodeling [6, 8, 16, 28]. Alterations in GABA synthesis and arginine-derived ratios (HA-arg/SDMA and HA-arg/ADMA) point to changes in neurotransmitter and nitric oxide–related pathways, while increased LDH and PLA₂ activity scores indicate enhanced glycolytic flux and membrane lipid turnover. Additional shifts in tryptophan (IDO activity), glutamine, xanthine, and TMAO metabolism suggest contributions from immunoregulatory and microbiota-associated pathways. Enrichment of ω-3 fatty acids and monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA) further supports extensive lipid remodeling in NMIBC [29]. Disruptions in glutathione, glutamate, folate, and glycolytic pathways align with known metabolic adaptations that support proliferation, redox balance, and survival in cancer cells [30–34].

Finally, the OPLS-DA model yielded reliable classification performance (Fig. 5B), consistent with expectations for biomarker discovery studies using targeted metabolomics [19]. Subsequent development of a Generalized Linear Model (GLM) based on 11 highly discriminant metabolites further improved diagnostic accuracy, demonstrating strong predictive performance in both training and test cohorts (Fig. 5E), including patients without hematuria. These findings highlight the advantage of integrated metabolomic modeling over single-metabolite approaches [25]. Despite these promising results, some limitations should be acknowledged. Small-molecule metabolites offer intrinsic advantages in analytical stability, quantitative accuracy, and inter-laboratory standardization compared with protein-based biomarkers, supporting their suitability for clinical translation. In this context, plasma-based metabolomic signatures may represent a complementary, non-invasive approach for NMIBC detection and patient stratification, providing a rationale for future longitudinal studies aimed at monitoring disease recurrence and progression. While the cohort was relatively large and well characterized for discovery, external validation in larger, independent, and preferably multicenter prospective cohorts will be required to confirm the reproducibility and clinical applicability of the identified plasma metabolic signatures and to evaluate their potential utility for long-term surveillance.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the clinical potential of plasma-based metabolomic profiling to capture systemic metabolic alterations associated with non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). The identified metabolic signature—comprising 29 metabolites and 17 pathway indicators—highlights coordinated lipid remodeling, bile acid dysregulation, and perturbations in redox and immune–metabolic pathways as hallmarks of early bladder carcinogenesis. These findings are consistent with previous metabolomic studies reporting comparable alterations in amino acid, lipid, and bile acid metabolism [4, 8–10], supporting the biological plausibility and reproducibility of the present results. Although the present study provides statistically robust evidence, further validation in larger, independent, and longitudinal cohorts will be essential to confirm temporal stability, predictive value, and clinical applicability. Ultimately, translation of this plasma metabolomic signature into a standardized, non-invasive assay may improve early detection, surveillance, and risk stratification in patients with NMIBC.
