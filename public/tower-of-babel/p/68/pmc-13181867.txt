Association of metabolic syndrome and its severity with liver fibrosis: a cross-sectional study based on NHANES 2017–March 2020, 2021–2023
Authors: Jiahui Yang, Xufeng Li, Fang Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver fibrosis has a high global prevalence and poses a substantial public health burden with significant implications for individual health and healthcare systems. To guide preventive and therapeutic approaches for liver fibrosis, this analysis explored the relationship between MetS, its severity grades, and hepatic fibrotic changes.MethodsData for this cross-sectional investigation were obtained from NHANES cycles spanning 2017–March 2020, 2021–2023. Weighted logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the associations of MetS and its severity with liver fibrosis. Potential nonlinear dose–response patterns were assessed through restricted cubic spline modeling. Subgroup analyses were performed to identify effect modification across population characteristics and to assess interaction effects. The stability of the results was verified through sensitivity analyses.ResultsA total of 3,940 participants were included, with a weighted prevalence of liver fibrosis of 10.6%. A significant positive relationship between MetS and liver fibrosis emerged from weighted logistic regression (OR = 2.225, 95% CI: 1.311–3.775). Increasing severity of MetS was associated with progressively elevated risk of liver fibrosis, with the highest risk observed among individuals in the most severe category (OR = 5.399, 95% CI: 2.527–11.54). A statistically significant nonlinear relationship between MetS severity and liver fibrosis was identified through restricted cubic spline modeling (P for nonlinear = 0.016). Subgroup analyses indicated that the association between MetS and liver fibrosis appeared stronger among participants who smoked or consumed alcohol.ConclusionsIndividuals with MetS exhibited a higher risk of liver fibrosis compared with those without MetS, and this risk increased progressively with greater disease severity. These findings elucidate the association pattern between MetS, its severity, and liver fibrosis risk, providing population-level evidence to inform liver disease prevention and control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04799-1.Keywords: Metabolic syndrome, Liver fibrosis, NHANES, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Characterized by collagen-predominant extracellular matrix accumulation and maladaptive wound healing following persistent liver injury, hepatic fibrosis is a progressive pathological state. This condition occupies a pivotal position along the continuum from chronic liver disease to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [1]. The global prevalence of significant and advanced liver fibrosis has been estimated at 7.3% and 3.5%, respectively, with higher rates reported in the United States at approximately 10.4% [2, 3]. Patients with significant liver fibrosis face elevated risks of vascular events and malignancies [4], which substantially increase healthcare costs and economic burden [5]. This condition also imposes considerable financial pressure on households, strains healthcare systems, and increases social security expenditures.

Recognized risk factors for liver fibrosis include alcohol intake, age, sex, central adiposity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, glycemic parameters, HDL cholesterol, triglyceride concentrations, chronic viral hepatitis, and metabolically dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [6, 7]. Recent mechanistic investigations have focused on hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation and the metabolite–immune cell–cytokine axis, seeking to distinguish metabolically driven fibrosis from fibrosis arising from classical hepatocellular injury [8]. Despite substantial progress in elucidating individual etiologies of liver fibrosis, most existing studies have examined isolated risk factors, with limited integration of multiple metabolic abnormalities to clarify their potential synergistic effects. As a complex pathological state encompassing multiple metabolic disturbances, the association between metabolic syndrome (MetS) and liver fibrosis remains an important knowledge gap warranting investigation.

A constellation of metabolic disturbances, including insulin resistance or hyperglycemia, obesity, dyslipidemia, and hypertension, defines MetS as a multifactorial clinical entity [9]. Several metabolic disturbances may contribute to the development of liver fibrosis. Hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia, for instance, can promote HSC activation through immune cell–mediated mechanisms involving macrophages and T cells, thereby accelerating fibrogenesis [8]. MASLD has been implicated in promoting hepatic fibrogenesis via HSC activation, a process that type 2 diabetes mellitus further accelerates [10]. MASLD represents a characteristic hepatic manifestation of MetS, while MetS in turn constitutes a significant risk factor for the progression of MASLD; the two conditions are mutually influential and closely interrelated. These observations suggest a potential link between MetS and liver fibrosis.

Based on these considerations, the present study utilized data from two cycles of NHANES (2017–March 2020, 2021–2023) to conduct a cross-sectional analysis examining the associations of MetS and its severity with liver fibrosis. This investigation provides novel evidence supporting early intervention in liver fibrosis from a metabolic regulation perspective and offers a scientific basis for integrated screening of hepatic and metabolic diseases in primary healthcare settings.

Methods

Data source

Administered by the National Center for Health Statistics, NHANES is a methodologically rigorous, multi-site survey program designed to characterize the nutritional and health profile of the U.S. population. NHANES is implemented as a comprehensive cross-sectional investigation biennially, with each survey cycle representing approximately 50,000 United States residents. Ethical approval for the NHANES protocol was granted by the Institutional Review Board of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Detailed information and datasets are publicly available at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm [11].

Initial screening encompassed 27,493 participants across the 2017–March 2020, 2021–2023 NHANES cycles. Participants were screened according to predefined criteria, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Participants were excluded for the following reasons: non-target race/ethnicity, specifically those not identifying as Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, or non-Hispanic Black (n = 4,286); excessive alcohol intake, defined as ≥ 4 drinks/day for men, ≥ 3 drinks/day for women, or ≥ 1 drink/day average over the preceding year (n = 2,988); age below 20 years (n = 8,537); pregnancy (n = 84); absent vibration-controlled transient elastography data (n = 2,809); and missing sampling weights or MetS information (n = 4,849). After applying all exclusion criteria, 3,940 participants were included in the final analysis.

Flowchart of inclusion and exclusion of the study population

MetS

MetS diagnosis required meeting at least three of five criteria: impaired glucose metabolism (fasting plasma glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL or glucose-lowering medication use); elevated blood pressure (systolic ≥ 130 mmHg, diastolic ≥ 85 mmHg, or antihypertensive medication); reduced HDL cholesterol (< 40 mg/dL in men, < 50 mg/dL in women, or lipid-lowering medication); elevated triglycerides (≥ 150 mg/dL or lipid-lowering medication); and abdominal obesity (waist circumference ≥ 102 cm in men, ≥ 88 cm in women) [9].

Additionally, a sex- and race-specific continuous MetS severity score developed using confirmatory factor analysis was applied to quantify disease severity. This score integrates waist circumference (cm), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (mg/dL), systolic blood pressure (mmHg), triglycerides (mg/dL), and fasting plasma glucose (mg/dL) to quantify MetS severity. The MetS severity score employed in the present study is consistent with the validated sex- and race-specific MetS Z-score model reported in previous literature [12] and has been further validated in subsequent studies [13, 14]. This approach enables graded assessment of metabolic burden beyond categorical diagnosis. The detailed calculation formula is presented in Table S1 [15].

Liver fibrosis

As the indicator for liver fibrosis assessment, the present study utilized the median stiffness value from hepatic transient elastography within the NHANES examination dataset to define the liver stiffness measurement (LSM). LSM was used to quantify fibrosis severity. Transient elastography is a first-line noninvasive technique for fibrosis assessment that provides objective evaluation of pathological fibrosis staging, making it the preferred screening tool in large-scale epidemiological studies such as NHANES [16]. Based on previous studies, an LSM value ≥ 8.2 kPa was used as the diagnostic threshold for liver fibrosis. The reliability and validity of this cutoff have been confirmed by multiple large clinical studies and epidemiological investigations [17–21].

Covariates

Covariates were selected on the basis of prior evidence and clinical importance, encompassing age (years), sex (male/female), race/ethnicity (Hispanic American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, or other), education level (below high school, high school, some college, or college graduate and above), marital status (married, cohabiting/widowed, divorced/separated, or never married), poverty income ratio (< 1.3, ≥ 1.3 to < 3.5, or ≥ 3.5), smoking history (yes/no), alcohol use (yes/no), hypertension (yes/no), and diabetes (yes/no). In addition, a directed acyclic graph (DAG) was employed to delineate the causal structure among variables, thereby further informing the final selection of covariates.

Participants who reported smoking ≥ 100 cigarettes over their lifetime were classified as smokers. Alcohol consumption status was determined by whether any type of alcohol had ever been consumed. Hypertension was present if any of the following applied: average systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg across three readings, self-reported physician-diagnosed hypertension, or current antihypertensive medication use [22]. Diabetes was ascertained through any of the following: self-reported physician diagnosis, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, fasting glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, glucose-lowering medication, or insulin therapy [23].

Statistical analysis

Fasting subsample weights (WTSAFPRP or WTSAF2YR) were incorporated to generate nationally representative estimates, accounting for the complex survey design of NHANES. The proportion of missing values for all covariates was below 20%; therefore, random forest-based multiple imputation was applied to all covariates to maximize sample retention and minimize missing data bias. The present study incorporated data from the 2017–March 2020 and 2021–2023 NHANES cycles. Sample weights were adjusted following the NHANES analytical guidelines: for the 2017–March 2020 cycle, weights were derived by multiplying the cycle-specific sample weight by 3.2/5.2, and for the 2021–2023 cycle, by multiplying the cycle-specific sample weight by 2/5.2 [24]. Normality of continuous variables was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk statistic. Normally distributed variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed with t tests; non-normally distributed variables were reported as median with interquartile range and analyzed with Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Frequencies and percentages summarized categorical variables, which were compared with chi-square tests. Associations of MetS and its severity grades with liver fibrosis were evaluated through weighted logistic regression models. A series of three progressively adjusted models was established: Model 1 included no covariates; Model 2 controlled for age and sex; Model 3 additionally incorporated race/ethnicity, education, marital status, poverty income ratio, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes Variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis was performed for all variables to assess multicollinearity, with a VIF value greater than 5 considered indicative of multicollinearity. Dose–response patterns and potential nonlinearity in the MetS severity–liver fibrosis relationship were examined through restricted cubic spline analysis. Effect heterogeneity was assessed through subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, poverty income ratio, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes. Likelihood ratio tests were conducted to evaluate potential interaction effects between subgroup and exposure variables. Two sensitivity analyses were conducted to verify the robustness of the results: (1) unweighted logistic regression was performed to assess robustness against model specification bias; and (2) weighted logistic regression was repeated after removing mediators identified from the DAG to prevent overadjustment bias. Unweighted logistic regression was applied in sensitivity analyses to verify the stability of the primary findings. Statistical analyses were completed in R version 4.5.2; two-sided P values below 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Table 1 presents the descriptive characteristics of the study population. Table 2 presents the weighted descriptive characteristics of the study population. Based on two survey cycles from 2017–March 2020, 2021–2023 and after application of strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, 3,940 participants were included. Among these, 417 cases of liver fibrosis were identified, corresponding to a prevalence of 10.6%. Liver fibrosis was more prevalent among older individuals, particularly those aged ≥ 60 years, and among men, with both associations reaching statistical significance (P < 0.05). Liver fibrosis was more prevalent among participants with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2), hypertension, and higher education levels, all reaching statistical significance (P < 0.05).

Subgroup analysis

Figures 3 and 4 illustrate the effects of MetS and its severity across different population subgroups. The effect estimates for the association between MetS and liver fibrosis were numerically larger among participants who smoked or consumed alcohol. The magnitude of these effects varied across subgroups. Significant interactions were observed between age and MetS, as well as between hypertension status and MetS (P for interaction < 0.05). Additionally, diabetes status showed a significant interaction with MetS severity (P for interaction < 0.05). No significant interactions were observed for sex, poverty income ratio, alcohol consumption status, or smoking status with either MetS or its severity (P for interaction > 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis

Table 4 summarizes unweighted logistic regression results from sensitivity analyses. Relative to non-MetS participants, those with MetS exhibited elevated liver fibrosis risk. Full adjustment did not attenuate the significant positive association (OR = 2.059, 95% CI: 1.541–2.767). Consistent with the primary analysis, increasing MetS severity was associated with progressively higher risk of liver fibrosis, with the highest risk observed in Q4. After full adjustment, the association remained significant (OR = 5.030, 95% CI: 3.431–7.523). Table 5 presents the sensitivity analysis conducted after removing potential mediators (hypertension and diabetes) identified from the DAG (Fig. S1). The results indicated that individuals with MetS had a significantly higher risk of liver fibrosis compared with those without MetS (OR = 1.844, 95% CI: 1.606–2.117), and the risk increased progressively with increasing MetS severity, peaking in the highest quartile (Q4) (OR = 8.091, 95% CI: 4.563–14.35), indicating that the removal of hypertension and diabetes from the covariates did not materially alter the findings. Concordance with the weighted logistic regression findings supports the reliability of the study conclusions.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated a nonlinear positive association of MetS and its severity with liver fibrosis risk. Individuals with MetS exhibited elevated risk of liver fibrosis, and this risk increased progressively with greater disease severity. The present results accord with prior research [25, 26]. Prior research on obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus documented significantly elevated liver fibrosis risk associated with both conditions in young adults [27], consistent with the obesity and glucose metabolism abnormalities encompassed by MetS. Another study investigating the effects of hypertension and MASLD on liver fibrosis demonstrated that hypertension was positively associated with liver fibrosis risk, with coexisting MASLD further elevating this risk [28], aligning with the role of blood pressure abnormalities in MetS. Additionally, a study exploring the associations among metabolism, metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis, and liver fibrosis indicated that metabolites such as saturated fatty acids and cholesterol activate macrophages and HSCs, establishing a positive inflammation–fibrosis feedback loop, with insulin resistance (IR) serving as a key upstream driver [29], consistent with dyslipidemia in MetS. Collectively, these convergent findings support the concept that liver fibrosis is not a pathological alteration confined to a single organ, but rather a complex disease closely linked to multisystem metabolic and pathophysiological disturbances, with its initiation and progression intrinsically connected to systemic functional and structural abnormalities.

From a mechanistic perspective, the association between MetS and liver fibrosis is not mediated by a single pathological pathway but instead reflects a complex cascade involving the interplay among lipid metabolism, insulin signaling, and inflammatory responses. These processes form an integrated regulatory network through intercellular signaling and collectively constitute the core pathological basis of liver fibrosis [30, 31]. Disordered lipid metabolism represents the initiating event of this cascade. IR-induced elevation of free fatty acids (FFAs) leads to excessive hepatic triglyceride accumulation and steatosis while simultaneously promoting the generation of lipotoxic lipid species. These processes jointly activate HSCs and drive their transdifferentiation toward a myofibroblast phenotype, thereby initiating hepatic fibrogenesis [32]. IR is not limited to its role in lipid metabolism dysregulation but also functions as a persistent injury amplifier throughout disease progression. Through both lipid accumulation and direct lipotoxic effects, IR disrupts hepatocyte structure and function and activates local hepatic immune response pathways, amplifying inflammatory signaling. Consequently, initially localized hepatocellular injury evolves into a sustained inflammatory microenvironment, further accelerating HSC activation and proliferation [33, 34]. Chronic inflammation serves as a critical mechanistic link among these pathological pathways. Dysregulated lipid metabolism combined with IR triggers immune-cell secretion of proinflammatory mediators, including TNF-α and IL-6, leading to downstream activation of hepatic Kupffer cells. Activated Kupffer cells secrete abundant profibrogenic mediators, inducing HSCs to synthesize collagen and other extracellular matrix components, ultimately leading to fibrotic lesion formation [32]. Notably, oxidative stress forms a bidirectional reinforcing relationship with inflammation. Saturated FFAs trigger ROS production and lipid peroxidation, initiating a self-perpetuating cycle of oxidative damage, inflammation, and fibrogenesis [29].

Beyond lipid metabolism, insulin signaling, and inflammatory responses as intrinsic core drivers, gut microbiota dysbiosis, obesity, and hypertension further promote liver fibrosis through peripheral regulation and synergistic effects. Gut microbiota dysbiosis represents a central regulatory node of the gut–liver axis and disrupts homeostatic balance between the intestine and liver. In individuals with MetS, reduced microbial diversity and increased abundance of pathogenic bacteria are commonly observed. Endotoxins such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) derived from dysbiotic gut microbiota enter the liver via the portal vein, leading to secondary activation of Kupffer cells and HSCs, thereby transmitting extrahepatic pathological signals into the liver and amplifying fibrogenic signaling [35]. Obesity further potentiates these pathological processes through additive effects. In the obese state, excessive expansion and dysfunction of peripheral white adipose tissue result in increased release of FFAs into the circulation, which are delivered directly to the liver through the portal vein, exacerbating hepatic steatosis. Concurrently, adipocytes secrete proinflammatory adipokines such as leptin, which act as peripheral signaling molecules that directly stimulate HSCs, activating pathways related to cell proliferation and extracellular matrix synthesis [36]. Hypertension, as a common comorbidity of MetS, participates in fibrosis progression through cross-system regulation. Hypertension promotes the release of fibrosis-related cytokines from Kupffer cells and activates the renin–angiotensin system (RAS). Angiotensin II (Ang II) accelerates HSC activation and matrix synthesis through receptor-mediated signaling pathways [37, 38].

The subgroup analyses in the present study further revealed population heterogeneity in the association of MetS and its severity with liver fibrosis risk, providing key evidence for the precise identification of high-risk subpopulations for liver fibrosis. First, at the lifestyle level, the effect estimates for MetS and its severity on liver fibrosis risk were numerically higher among smokers and alcohol consumers, suggesting that adverse lifestyle habits such as smoking and alcohol consumption may amplify hepatic injury risk and accelerate fibrosis progression through exacerbation of oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and chronic inflammatory responses, potentially acting synergistically with the pathological effects of MetS [39, 40]. Second, interaction analyses revealed significant interactions of age and hypertension status with the effect of MetS, while diabetes status demonstrated a significant interaction with the effect of MetS severity. These findings suggest that elderly individuals with concurrent hypertension or diabetes and MetS may represent core high-risk populations for liver fibrosis. As a risk factor for liver fibrosis, age exerts its effects primarily through age-related metabolic functional decline, which disrupts hepatic lipid and energy metabolic homeostasis, significantly reduces hepatocyte regenerative capacity, and exacerbates chronic inflammatory stress, thereby serving as an independent risk factor driving fibrosis progression [41, 42]. Hypertension may promote liver fibrosis through cross-system regulatory mechanisms, including activation of Kupffer cells and the renin–angiotensin pathway, which accelerate hepatic stellate cell activation and extracellular matrix synthesis [37, 38]. Insulin resistance constitutes the core pathological basis of type 2 diabetes mellitus, and the persistent hyperglycemia and dysregulated glycolipid metabolism associated with diabetes can further exacerbate insulin resistance, creating a vicious cycle that collectively drives fibrosis progression [32, 43, 44].

Although the present study provides several avenues for further investigation in liver disease research, certain limitations should be acknowledged. From a study design perspective, the cross-sectional nature of this analysis permits identification of associations between MetS and liver fibrosis risk but precludes determination of causal or temporal relationships. From a diagnostic perspective, liver fibrosis was defined using LSM values rather than histological assessment by liver biopsy as the diagnostic gold standard, which may have limited sensitivity for early-stage fibrosis and may result in misclassification or underdetection of some cases. From a variable selection perspective, important confounding factors such as chronic viral hepatitis infection were not included in stratified analyses, and potential synergistic effects between these factors and MetS on liver fibrosis risk cannot be fully excluded. Future studies based on multicenter, large-scale prospective cohorts may establish risk prediction models integrating the number and severity of MetS components with liver fibrosis biomarkers, thereby providing practical tools for early identification of high-risk populations in clinical settings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, MetS and its severity exhibited a nonlinear positive association with liver fibrosis. Individuals with MetS had elevated risk of liver fibrosis, and this risk increased progressively with greater disease severity. These findings align with current priorities in chronic disease prevention and control by integrating liver fibrosis prevention with metabolic disease management. The results provide population-level evidence to support the development of comprehensive liver disease prevention strategies and are consistent with contemporary public health trends emphasizing integrated management of chronic metabolic conditions, indicating broad potential for clinical and public health application.
