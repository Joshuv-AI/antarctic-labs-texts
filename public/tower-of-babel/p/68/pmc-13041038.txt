Pyogenic psoas abscess: clinical profiles, imaging features, and outcomes in a retrospective single-center study of 32 cases
Authors: Bircan Kayaaslan, Zeynep Oktay, Aysuna Galandarova, Fatma Eser, İmran Hasanoğlu, Ayse Kaya, Tuğba Başçıoğlu, Rahmet Güner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsoas abscess is a rare, complex condition often associated with various underlying diseases and nonspecific clinical signs, which can delay diagnosis and increase mortality. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical, epidemiological, and radiological characteristics, as well as treatment outcomes, of patients diagnosed with psoas abscess.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients with psoas abscess treated at Ankara Bilkent City Hospital between February 2019 and February 2022. Epidemiological data, comorbidities, microorganisms identified in abscess or blood cultures, imaging findings, and treatment outcomes were analyzed. Abscesses without an identifiable infectious source were classified as primary, while those secondary to nearby infections were classified as secondary. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 20.0.ResultsThirty-two patients were included: 9 with primary and 23 with secondary abscesses. The mean age was 60.5 ± 14.6 years, and 62.5% were male. Predisposing factors included prior surgery (31%), vertebral osteomyelitis (25%), trauma (12.5%), and foreign bodies (12.5%). Common symptoms were back pain (78.1%), radiating leg pain (50%), fever (53.1%), and chills (62.5%), with symptom duration exceeding 15 days in 46.9% of cases. Primary abscesses were often associated with immunosuppression (44.4%), whereas secondary abscesses most frequently involved musculoskeletal (82.6%) or cardiovascular systems (26%). Among pathogens isolated from abscess cultures, Staphylococcus aureus was the most frequently identified organism, followed by Streptococcus spp. and Enterococcus spp. Brucellosis and tuberculosis were identified in selected patients based on serological or immunological criteria. Half of the patients underwent percutaneous drainage plus antibiotics; others received either antibiotics alone (25%) or drainage with surgery (18%). The median duration of antibiotic therapy was 48 days. Complete cure was achieved in 46.9% of patients, 40.6% recovered with sequelae, and 12.5% died. Recurrence occurred in ten patients.ConclusionPsoas abscess remains a rare but serious condition with diverse etiologies. Prompt recognition and prolonged treatment are critical, and S. aureus is the most frequently isolated pathogen.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12666-3.

Introduction

Psoas abscess is a rare and complicated disease accompanied by many different diseases [1]. Its etiology is variable, and it is classified as primary and secondary abscess in the literature according to the source and the development of the infection. A primary psoas abscess occurs when an infection reaches the psoas muscle via hematogenous or lymphatic spread from a distant site [2]. A secondary psoas abscess develops when the infection directly spreads to the psoas muscle from a neighboring structure which are organs such as kidneys, sigmoid colon, jejunum, appendix, pancreas, abdominal aorta, and ureter [3, 4]. Although the clinical triad of the disease was originally described by Mynter, who was the first to detail it as back/flank pain, fever, and limping, patients typically present with nonspecific symptoms. These may include an insidious onset of malaise, low-grade fever, abdominal pain, or discomfort in the lumbar region [5]. Delays or missed diagnoses can lead to serious consequences. In untreated patients, mortality rates have been reported to reach as high as 100% [6]. Due to the rarity of the disease, psoas abscesses have not received adequate attention in the literature. In this study, it was aimed to evaluate the clinical, epidemiological-radiological characteristics and treatment responses of patients followed up with psoas abscess in our hospital for three years period.

Materials and methods

Study population and data collection

The retrospective study was conducted by the Infectious Diseases and Clinical Microbiology Department at Ankara Bilkent City Hospital between February 2019 and February 2022. Patients over the age of 18 who were diagnosed with psoas abscess and followed up as outpatients or hospitalized were included in the study.

The epidemiological characteristics, comorbidities, predisposing factors, presenting symptoms, laboratory and radiological findings, microbiological analysis of abscess and/or blood cultures, and treatment data (detailed antimicrobial therapy administered to patients and treatment duration) and clinical outcomes and recurrence were retrospectively screened and recorded into a standardized data collection form.

Each psoas abscess was classified as primary or secondary according to the source of infection. Abscesses occurring due to hematogenous spread from an occult focus, with no other infection source identified, are classified as primary psoas abscesses. Abscesses that have a known causative disease, condition, or event, with the infection spreading from an adjacent anatomical structure, are classified as secondary psoas abscesses [5]. Data were collected from the hospital automation database system and patient medical records.

Surgical history, including hip arthroplasty, foreign body presence, vertebral osteomyelitis/discitis, trauma history, intramuscular injections, and cardiac valve replacement, was recorded as predisposing conditions. Presumed infection source for secondary abscess were classified as musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, genitourinary and gastrointestinal systems. Concomitant infections at other sides were also recorded.

Microbiological evaluation

Microbiological evaluation was performed based on available clinical data, consistent with the retrospective nature of the study. Abscess cultures were obtained only from patients who underwent percutaneous or surgical drainage procedures. In patients managed without drainage, etiological evaluation relied on blood cultures and non-culture-based diagnostic methods when clinically indicated.

Blood culture isolates were considered causative pathogens if the same microorganism was isolated from abscess cultures or if no other apparent infectious focus was identified apart from the psoas abscess. Common skin flora, including Corynebacterium spp., Bacillus spp., Cutibacterium spp., viridans group streptococci, Micrococcus spp., and coagulase-negative staphylococci, were regarded as contaminants when detected in only one blood culture set. When more than one causative pathogen was isolated, all microorganisms were recorded, and infections were classified as polymicrobial.

Microbiological confirmation could not be achieved in all patients. In selected cases, etiological diagnoses were established based on serological or immunological testing combined with compatible clinical and radiological findings. Tuberculosis cultures, acid-fast bacilli staining, and Brucella standard tube agglutination tests were performed selectively according to clinical presentation, epidemiological risk factors, and radiological suspicion, rather than routinely in all patients. In addition, interferon-gamma release assays were selectively requested in patients with clinical or radiological features suggestive of tuberculosis.

Anaerobic cultures and molecular diagnostic methods, including polymerase chain reaction (PCR), were not routinely performed during the study period. Microbiological evaluation was based on conventional culture techniques and serological or immunological tests available in routine clinical practice at the study center.

Radiological evaluation

Abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) and lumbar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were used for the detection of psoas abscess and its dimensions. Even if imaging showed bilateral psoas abscesses, only the diameter of the largest abscess was recorded.

Outcome definitions

Clinical cure was defined as resolution of clinical signs and symptoms attributable to psoas abscess, with no requirement for additional antimicrobial therapy or drainage at the end of the planned treatment course.

Sequelae were defined as persistent clinical, functional, or radiological abnormalities related to psoas abscess that remained after completion of treatment and resolution of active infection, as documented at the last available follow-up.

Recurrence was defined as radiological reappearance of a psoas abscess at the same or contralateral site after completion of initial therapy, requiring re-initiation of antimicrobial treatment and/or drainage.

Due to the retrospective nature of the study, follow-up duration was not standardized and varied among patients. Clinical outcomes, including recurrence and sequelae, were assessed based on available inpatient and outpatient medical records, radiological follow-up when available, and re-admissions to the same institution after completion of therapy.

Mortality was defined as all-cause death occurring during hospitalization or during the available follow-up period after diagnosis of psoas abscess.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages (n, %), while continuous variables were presented as median (25th-75th percentiles) or mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The Student’s t-test was used to compare continuous variables between two groups. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 20.0 software version. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

From February 2019 to February 2022, a total of 32 patients diagnosed with psoas abscess, who were followed up either as inpatients or outpatients at the Ankara Bilkent City Hospital’s Infectious Diseases and Clinical Microbiology Department, were included in the study. Of the patients, 20 (62.5%) were male and the mean age was 60.5 ± 14.6 years. There were 5 patients (15.6%) involved in farming or livestock activities. Of the patients, 23 (71.9%) had at least one comorbidity. The most common comorbidities were hypertension (50%), diabetes mellitus (46.9%), coronary artery disease (18.8%), and immunosuppression (12.5%), calculated based on the total study population (Table 1). The most common predisposing factors were a history of surgery, osteoarticular infection and the presence of foreign bodies. Of the patients, 10 (31.3%) had a history of surgical operations including hip arthroplasty and cardiac surgery, 8 (25%) had vertebral osteomyelitis/discitis, and 5 (15.6%) had a foreign body (Table 1).

The most common complaints related to bone and joint involvement at admission were lower back pain (78.1%), limited hip movement (56.3%), and leg pain (50%). The most frequently reported systemic symptoms during the disease course were chills (62.5%), fever (53.1%), and fatigue (40.6%). The median duration of symptoms at hospital admission was 15 days (IQR, 7–60), and 15 patients (46.9%) reported symptom duration longer than 15 days. At the time of hospital admission, 11 patients (34.4%) had documented fever, and 12 patients (37.5%) had tachycardia. The clinical characteristics and laboratory findings of the 32 patients with psoas abscess at hospital admission are summarized in Table 1.

At hospital admission, laboratory findings reflected a marked inflammatory response. Median white blood cell count was 10.7 × 10⁹/L (IQR, 8.1–16.3), with neutrophil predominance. Inflammatory markers were markedly elevated, with a median C-reactive protein level of 164 mg/L (IQR, 65–221) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 80 mm/h (IQR, 49–110). Mild anemia was common, with a median hemoglobin level of 11.0 g/dL (IQR, 8.7–12.9), while renal and hepatic function parameters were generally within normal ranges at presentation.

Of the patients, nine (28.1%) had primary psoas abscess, and 23 (71.9%) had secondary psoas abscess. Among patients with primary psoas abscess, immunosuppression was identified as an underlying facilitating factor in four patients (44.4%), diabetes mellitus in two patients (22.2%), trauma in two patients (22.2%), and hematoma in one patient (11.1%).

The most common presumed sources of infection in patients with secondary psoas abscess were the musculoskeletal system and the cardiovascular system, identified in 19 patients (82.6%) and six patients (26%), respectively; sources were not mutually exclusive. Eight patients (25%) had a concurrent infection outside the primary presumed source of infection. In three patients, coexistence of spondylodiscitis, endocarditis, and psoas abscess was identified. In one patient, psoas abscess was accompanied by pulmonary tuberculosis and tuberculous spondylodiscitis (Table 2).

Eight patients were managed without abscess drainage. Among these patients, microbiological diagnosis was established by blood cultures in three patients. One patient was diagnosed with tuberculous psoas abscess based on compatible radiological and clinical findings, and one patient was diagnosed with brucellosis. In the remaining three patients, no microbiological pathogen was identified; however, a diagnosis of pyogenic psoas abscess was made based on clinical presentation and imaging findings.

Abscess cultures were obtained in 24 patients who underwent drainage procedures. Among patients with positive abscess cultures, Staphylococcus aureus was the most frequently isolated pathogen. Other commonly isolated microorganisms included gram-negative bacteria, Streptococcus spp., and Enterococcus spp., and polymicrobial growth was observed in four patients. Candida spp. were isolated in abscess cultures of five patients. In four of these cases, Candida spp. were considered the primary etiological agent based on clinical, radiological, and microbiological correlation. Staphylococcus aureus was also the most frequently identified pathogen in blood cultures. Detailed microbiological findings from abscess and blood cultures are summarized in Table 3.

Antimicrobial susceptibility patterns were evaluated for the most frequently isolated pathogens. Among the eight patients with Staphylococcus aureus infection, only one isolate was methicillin-resistant, while the remaining isolates were methicillin-susceptible. Escherichia coli was identified in three patients, two of whom harbored extended-spectrum beta-lactamase–producing isolates.

Seventeen of the 32 patients (53.1%) had a history of antimicrobial use prior to hospital admission. Among the 24 patients who underwent abscess drainage, 22 had received antimicrobial therapy before the procedure. Of note, all patients with negative abscess cultures had been exposed to antimicrobial therapy before the drainage procedure.

Brucella standard tube agglutination tests were performed in 23 patients, and 3 patients were found to be seropositive. In one of these patients, abscess cultures yielded different bacterial pathogens, whereas in the remaining two patients no microorganism was isolated from abscess or blood cultures, and the etiological diagnosis was based on serological findings.

Tuberculosis culture and acid-fast bacilli staining were performed on abscess material from 18 patients; Mycobacterium tuberculosis was not isolated in any of the samples. Three patients were diagnosed with tuberculosis based on positive interferon-gamma release assays combined with compatible clinical and radiological findings.

Psoas abscesses were identified in 27 patients (84.3%) by abdominal and pelvic CT imaging and in 22 patients (68.7%) by lumbar MRI. Both imaging modalities were performed in 10 patients. The mean longitudinal diameter of the identified psoas abscesses was 6.6 (± 3.6) cm, and the mean transverse diameter was 3.7 (± 2.0) (Table 4). Among psoas abscesses, 26 (81.2%) were unilateral, and 6 (18.8%) were bilateral.

Drainage management was evaluated in all patients. Among the antibiotics administered, carbapenems were used in 7 patients (22.5%), piperacillin-tazobactam in 5 patients (16.1%), other anti-pseudomonal agents in 4 patients (12.9%), ampicillin-sulbactam in 8 patients (25.8%), ceftriaxone in 4 patients (12.9%), cefazolin in 2 patients (6.4%), and rifampin in 5 patients (16.1%). Additionally, 9 patients (29.0%) received treatment targeting methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus spp., 3 patients (9.6%) received anti-tuberculosis therapy, and 3 patients (9.6%) were treated for brucellosis. Fluconazole was administered to 4 patients (12.9%).

During the treatment course, antimicrobial regimens were modified according to microbiological results, clinical response, and drug tolerance, and some patients received sequential or combination antimicrobial therapy. The median duration of treatment was 48 days (range: 26–83), with a mean duration of 73.4 days. No statistically significant difference in treatment duration was observed between patients treated for primary and secondary psoas abscesses (Table 6).

A complete treatment response was achieved in 15 patients (46.9%). Thirteen patients (40.6%) recovered with sequelae, while psoas abscess resulted in death in 4 patients (12.5%). Recurrence was observed in 10 patients (31.2%) after treatment. When clinical outcomes were compared between primary and secondary psoas abscesses, rates of clinical cure, recurrence, and mortality did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.243, p = 1.000, and p = 0.057, respectively). Recovery with sequelae was observed significantly more frequently in patients with secondary psoas abscess compared with those with primary abscess (60.9% vs. 0%, p = 0.002) (Table 6).

When clinical outcomes were compared according to drainage status, complete clinical response was observed in 11 of 24 patients who underwent drainage and in 4 of 8 patients managed without drainage. Recurrence occurred exclusively among patients who underwent drainage (10/24 vs. 0/8), and this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.035). There was no statistically significant difference between the drainage and non-drainage groups in terms of sequelae (10/24 vs. 3/8, p > 0.05) or mortality (3/24 vs. 1/8, p > 0.05). Outcome measures were not mutually exclusive; patients who initially achieved clinical cure could subsequently experience recurrence during follow-up.

Additional analyses were performed to evaluate factors associated with mortality, recurrence, and recovery with sequelae. No statistically significant associations were identified between age, comorbidity burden, Charlson Comorbidity Index, unilateral or bilateral involvement, or most microbiological characteristics and clinical outcomes. Mortality tended to occur in older patients with a higher comorbidity burden and primary psoas abscess; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Notably, mortality was significantly higher among patients with malignancy. In addition, all patients in whom Candida spp. was considered the etiological agent experienced recurrence and recovered with sequelae. Discussion

Psoas abscess is a rare condition with highly variable etiology and clinical presentation. While clinical suspicion and the use of imaging modalities are crucial for diagnosis [2], population-based data regarding its true prevalence remain limited. Earlier studies published in 1986 and 1992 reported the global incidence of psoas abscess as 3.9 and 12 cases per year, respectively [6, 7]. With the widespread use of imaging techniques over the years, the number of reported cases from different parts of the world has increased [8–13] however, psoas abscess remains a rare disease [14]. In this study, we present the clinical course, microbiological findings, treatment approaches, and outcomes of 32 patients diagnosed with psoas abscess and followed up over a three-year period.

The psoas muscle starts from the sides of the 12th thoracic and 5th lumbar vertebrae and runs down along the pelvic edge. It passes under the inguinal ligament and in front of the hip joint, joining the iliacus muscle to form a tendon that attaches to the lesser trochanter of the femur. Along its path, the psoas muscle is close to several important structures like the spine, abdominal aorta, sigmoid colon, appendix, hip joint, and lymph nodes, and infections in these areas can spread to the psoas muscle [15]. Abscesses that form this way are called secondary psoas abscesses. Primary psoas abscess happens when infection spreads through the blood or lymph from a distant site. While most early reported cases were primary [6], recent years have seen more secondary cases [7, 11–13, 16]. Mückley and colleagues emphasized the rise in secondary abscesses is linked to better imaging and the return of tuberculosis in developed countries [16], Lopez and others believe CT scans help find more secondary psoas abscesses [11]. In our study, secondary abscesses were more common, similar to recent reports, may be related to increasing use of invasive procedures and improved diagnostic imaging.

Risk factors for primary psoas abscess include diabetes, intravenous drug use, HIV infection, kidney failure, immunosuppressive drugs or conditions, trauma, and presence of hematoma [2, 6, 16]. All patients with primary psoas abscess in our cohort had at least one recognized predisposing factor. Risk factors for secondary psoas abscess are infections in adjacent tissues, trauma, or surgical procedures [3, 10, 16–18]. Tabrizan et al. studied 61 cases and found that 30% had infections from the gastrointestinal [9]. Mückley et al. showed that spine infections often cause secondary psoas abscess [16]. Lopez et al. reviewed 124 cases and found that 50.5% of secondary cases and 39.5% of all cases had infections in the skeleton, especially vertebral osteomyelitis [11]. In our study, the skeleton, especially spondylodiscitis, was the most common infection source for secondary psoas abscess. Using spinal imaging in patients with psoas abscess can help diagnose vertebral osteomyelitis or spondylodiscitis. In our study, the other potential source of the secondary psoas abscess is the cardiovascular system, mainly the endocarditis which was detected in 26% of the patients with secondary psoas abscess. Due to the frequent association between endocarditis and vertebral infections, the latest publication suggests that the presence of one should prompt a thorough investigation for the other. Given the high prevalence of concurrent endocarditis—reported in up to 33% of cases—transesophageal echocardiography is recommended in many patients with hematogenous spondylodiscitis [19, 20].

Microbiological diagnosis is most established through cultures obtained via drainage. The causative organisms vary depending on geography and whether the abscess is primary or secondary [11, 21]. Primary abscesses are usually monomicrobial, most commonly caused by S. aureus. In contrast, secondary abscesses of gastrointestinal or urinary origin often involve Gram-negative bacilli, especially E. coli, and may be polymicrobial [6, 10, 11, 13, 22–24]. In the study by Lopez et al., S. aureus was also frequently identified in secondary psoas abscesses of skeletal origin, similar to primary cases. Similarly, Gram-positive microorganisms, mainly S. aureus, were the most frequent pathogens in our study.

In a study from Turkey by Turunç et al. in 2009, involving 15 patients—most of whom (93.3%) had secondary psoas abscess—the most frequently identified pathogen was M. tuberculosis (33.3%) [25]. In our study, mycobacterial cultures and acid-fast staining were performed selectively in patients; however, no mycobacterial growth was detected. This finding is not unexpected, as microbiological confirmation of extrapulmonary tuberculosis, including psoas involvement, is often challenging. In such cases, the combination of compatible clinical presentation, characteristic imaging findings, and positive interferon-gamma release assays may support the diagnosis of tuberculous psoas abscess, even in the absence of culture confirmation. Therefore, tuberculosis should remain an important diagnostic consideration in endemic regions.

Brucellosis represents another important etiological consideration in the differential diagnosis of psoas abscess, particularly in endemic regions such as Türkiye. Musculoskeletal involvement is a well-recognized manifestation of brucellosis, and psoas abscess has been reported as a rare but documented complication [25]. In our study, brucellosis was diagnosed in three patients based on positive serological testing in the appropriate clinical context. In two of these patients, additional bacterial pathogens were isolated from abscess cultures, highlighting the possibility of concomitant infections. Given the challenges in microbiological isolation of Brucella spp. and the frequent reliance on serological methods, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for brucellosis in patients with psoas abscess, especially when epidemiological risk factors are present.

An interesting finding in our study is the isolation of Candida spp. in five patients with secondary psoas abscesses. This observation is noteworthy, as fungal involvement in psoas abscess is rare and has been reported predominantly in isolated case reports [26–28]. In our cohort, the clinical significance of Candida isolation was carefully evaluated in the context of each patient’s overall clinical presentation, radiological findings, and response to antibacterial therapy. Accordingly, Candida spp. was not considered the primary etiological agent in all cases, and antifungal treatment was initiated selectively. Given the challenges in obtaining microbiological confirmation and the possibility of mixed or secondary infections, Candida spp. should be considered as a potential etiological factor in selected patients with secondary psoas abscess, particularly in those with inadequate response to standard antibacterial treatment.

In our cohort seven patients, an etiological agent could not be identified. This is not unexpected in clinical practice, as microbiological confirmation of psoas abscess is often difficult, especially in patients who do not undergo drainage or who have already received antimicrobial therapy. In such cases, treatment decisions relied on clinical presentation and radiological findings, which is consistent with routine practice.

Treating a psoas abscess typically involves a combination of long-term antibiotic therapy and abscess drainage. Drainage can be performed either percutaneously or surgically [29, 30]. Yacoub et al. suggest that psoas abscesses smaller than 3 cm in diameter might be successfully managed with antibiotics alone, reserving surgical drainage for complicated or recurrent cases [10]. While surgical drainage carries higher risks of mortality and morbidity, percutaneous drainage is generally the preferred initial approach [31]. In fact, Mueller et al. pioneered the use of percutaneous drainage for psoas abscesses in 1984, achieving successful outcomes in 7 out of 8 patients in their study. Only one patient required surgery due to severe sacral osteomyelitis [32]. When a secondary psoas abscess stems from an underlying intestinal condition like Crohn’s disease, surgical drainage is often recommended as the primary intervention. This is because intestinal resection might be necessary to address the source of infection. Additionally, surgical treatment of the adjacent infected focus may be required in these cases [2, 16, 33]. Regardless of the drainage method, evacuating the abscess is crucial for obtaining microbiological samples. A treatment plan tailored to the culture and antibiogram results will significantly improve the patient’s response. If a rapid microbiological diagnosis isn’t possible, empirical treatment should be initiated. For primary psoas abscesses, S. aureus should be considered. In secondary psoas abscesses, empirical treatment should cover S. aureus, Gram-negative organisms, anaerobes, and even polymicrobial infections [11]. Empirical antifungal therapy may be considered in secondary psoas abscesses that do not respond to the initial treatment. In our study, 50% of the patients underwent percutaneous drainage in addition to antibiotic treatment, and 18.7% underwent both percutaneous and surgical drainage.

Recurrence rate may change study population, underlying disease and conditions, etiologic agents and the preffered surgical procedures. In a retrospective study of 26 patients with psoas abscess, Benkhadoura et al. reported a recurrence rate of 11.5%, which they attributed to early removal of the drainage catheter [34]. In the study by Cantaşdemir et al., which included 21 patients treated and followed with percutaneous drainage catheters, recurrence was observed during follow-up in three patients with primary tuberculous abscesses [34]. Yacoub et al. reported a recurrence rate of 9.7% in their study [10]. In contrast, Baier et al. found a higher recurrence rate of 37.5% in their retrospective analysis of 40 patients with psoas abscess. In that study, psoas abscesses secondary to surgical procedures or underlying bone infections were significantly associated with recurrence, highlighting the difficulty of adequately treating the primary source with antibiotics alone [23]. In our study, the recurrence rate was also relatively high. Notably, recurrence was observed exclusively in patients who underwent drainage procedures, whereas no recurrence was detected among patients managed without drainage. This finding should be interpreted with caution, as patients requiring drainage likely represented a subgroup with larger, more complex abscesses or persistent infectious foci, which may predispose to recurrence despite appropriate intervention. Therefore, the observed association between drainage and recurrence is more likely to reflect underlying disease complexity rather than a causal effect of the drainage procedure itself.

When outcomes were further analyzed according to abscess classification, patients with secondary psoas abscess were more likely to experience sequelae compared to those with primary abscess, whereas recurrence rates were comparable between groups. These findings suggest that secondary psoas abscesses, often associated with adjacent structural infections, may be more difficult to eradicate completely and are more likely to result in long-term morbidity.

Psoas abscesses can result in significant morbidity and mortality. In untreated cases, mortality may reach 100% [2]. In the literature, mortality rates among treated patients have been reported to range between 3% and 15% [9, 10, 13, 23]. In the study by Benkhadoura et al., no deaths were reported. The authors attributed this to the relatively young age of the patients and the absence of comorbid conditions [34]. Baier et al. identified older age, high APACHE II score, bilateral abscess formation, history of surgical intervention, and underlying bone infection as predictors of poor outcome, while microbiological findings were not found to affect prognosis [23]. In our study, the observed mortality rate was consistent with findings reported in the literature. Mortality tended to occur in patients with primary psoas abscesses and a higher burden of comorbid conditions. Although older age and higher Charlson Comorbidity Index scores were more frequently observed among patients who died, these associations did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited sample size. Notably, mortality was significantly higher among patients with underlying malignancy. No significant associations were identified between mortality and abscess laterality, most microbiological characteristics, or specific etiological agents. These findings suggest that mortality in psoas abscess is more closely related to overall disease severity and host-related factors, including underlying malignancy, rather than individual microbiological features.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design and relatively small sample size limit the ability to identify independent predictors of clinical outcomes. In some cases, classification of primary and secondary psoas abscesses was challenging, and an unrecognized secondary source cannot be entirely excluded in a subset of patients classified as having primary disease.

Microbiological confirmation was not available for all patients, particularly those managed without abscess drainage. In addition, anaerobic cultures and advanced molecular diagnostic methods, such as PCR or metagenomic sequencing, were not routinely performed during the study period. Therefore, the contribution of anaerobic pathogens to the etiology of psoas abscess cannot be fully excluded. Follow-up duration was also not standardized, which may have influenced the assessment of recurrence and long-term sequelae.

Despite these limitations, this study provides real-world clinical data from a relatively large single-center cohort, considering the rarity of psoas abscess, and offers practical insights into its diagnosis, management, and outcomes.

Conclusion

Our case series provides up-to-date data on the etiological agents, clinical and radiological features, and treatment outcomes of psoas abscesses—a condition that remains underrepresented in the literature due to its rarity and broad etiological spectrum. Based on our analysis, several key findings can be highlighted: Secondary psoas abscesses were more common, suggesting the frequent presence of an underlying infectious focus in adjacent structures that must be carefully investigated. The skeletal system, particularly vertebral spondylodiscitis, was the most frequent source of infection. The spectrum of causative pathogens was broad, with S. aureus being the most dominant organism. Treatment is often prolonged and associated with considerable rates of sequelae and mortality.
