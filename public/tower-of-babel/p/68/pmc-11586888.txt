Meta‐analysis of probiotics efficacy in the treatment of minimum hepatic encephalopathy
Authors: Yu‐Lian Zhou, Shu‐Tao Pu, Jian‐Bo Xiao, Jun Luo, Li Xue
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to systematically evaluate the efficacy of probiotics in treating minimum hepatic encephalopathy (MHE).MethodsA systematic search was conducted across three major databases: PubMed, China National Knowledge Infrastructure and Wanfang. The search period spanned from the inception of each database to 9 March 2023. The objective was to identify all randomised controlled trials (RCTs) examining the efficacy of probiotic preparations in treating MHE. The search terms included ‘probiotics’ along with other clinically relevant terms to comprehensively capture all pertinent studies.ResultsA total of 18 RCTs were included. The meta‐analysis showed that probiotic treatment outperformed control groups in reducing blood ammonia levels (standard mean difference [MD] = −2.68, 95% confidence interval [CI]: −3.90 to −1.46, p < .0001), improving the remission rate of MHE (risk ratio [RR] = 2.79, 95% CI: 1.23–6.35, p = .01) and lowering alanine aminotransferase levels (MD = −11.10, 95% CI: −16.17 to −6.03, p < .0001). It also significantly reduced the Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease scores (MD = −2.55, 95% CI: −3.56 to −1.54, p < .00001) and the incidence of MHE (RR = .18, 95% CI: .09–.34, p < .00001).ConclusionOur study demonstrates that probiotics effectively improve blood ammonia levels, liver function and cognitive function in patients with MHE. They significantly enhance the remission rate of MHE and effectively reduce its incidence, providing solid new evidence for treating MHE with probiotics.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) is a syndrome characterised by central nervous system dysfunction primarily stemming from the liver's impaired ability to metabolise ammonia and other toxins. This impairment is usually caused by severe liver dysfunction or diseases that disrupt its urea cycle and other metabolic processes critical for detoxification. It is mainly characterised by mental and neurological symptoms; the main clinical manifestations are recurrent transient and reversible neuropsychiatric abnormalities. 1 According to the severity of clinical manifestations, HE can be divided into overt HE (OHE) and minimum HE (MHE). Minimum HE is also called subclinical HE, an early form of HE that is often not recognised or diagnosed. 2 However, 20%–80% of patients with cirrhosis have MHE, with a rising trend observed annually. 3 , 4 , 5 Without effective treatment, some patients with MHE will experience a decline in cognitive ability within 1 year and develop MHE with a mortality rate of up to 50%. 6 , 7 However, because the pathogenesis of HE is not clear, there are no specific drugs recommended for its treatment, and there is no standard treatment for MHE. 8

Previous research has documented that lactulose, amino acids and antibiotics are effective in mitigating HE symptoms. However, these treatments are associated with distinct adverse reactions. Lactulose commonly causes gastrointestinal discomfort, including diarrhoea, bloating and gas; amino acids may lead to nausea, cramps and bloating; and antibiotics can result in various gastrointestinal and systemic side effects depending on the specific antibiotic used. 9 Several recent studies have shown that probiotic treatment can improve neuropsychological tests, lower blood ammonia levels and reduce the occurrence of MHE in patients. 10 , 11 , 12 Probiotics work within the central nervous system by altering gut flora composition, reducing gut lumen pH to inhibit the growth of urease‐producing bacteria and enhancing gut barrier function, thereby reducing the translocation of bacteria and endotoxins. These changes may reduce levels of neurotoxins such as ammonia in the bloodstream. Accumulation of these toxins in the brain can disrupt normal neurotransmission and brain metabolism, thereby causing symptoms of HE. 13 , 14 Some scholars have used randomised controlled trials (RCTs) to explore the efficacy of probiotics in the treatment of MHE, but these studies report different results. 15 , 16 , 17 Dalal et al. demonstrate the efficacy of probiotics in a meta‐analysis of their effects on HE. However, the study includes not only patients who received a placebo or blank control but also those undergoing any other treatment as a control, which could potentially affect the results. In addition, most of the trials in that study had a high risk of systematic error and random error. Therefore, we consider the evidence to be of low quality. 18

We conducted this meta‐analysis to evaluate the efficacy of probiotics in the treatment of MHE. By aggregating data from multiple high‐quality studies and excluding the effects of other treatments, we aim to provide clearer insights into the potential role of probiotics in the management of MHE, informing clinical diagnosis and treatment strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Search strategy

Three Chinese and English databases (PubMed, China National Knowledge Infrastructure and Wanfang) were searched systematically. The retrieval timeframe was from the establishment of the database to 9 March 2023. The search terms included ‘probiotics’, ‘Lactococcus lactis’, ‘Bacillus subtilis’, ‘Lactobacilli’, ‘Lactobacillus’, ‘Saccharomyces’, ‘Bifidobacterium’, ‘minimal hepatic encephalopathy’ and ‘overt hepatic encephalopathy’. Synonyms of each term were also used.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the study was an RCT; (2) the participants had a diagnosis of ‘mild HE’ or ‘subclinical HE’; (3) the intervention measures were probiotics, and the control was a blank or placebo.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non‐population studies; (2) conference articles, case reports, systematic reviews, non‐RCTs and other research types; (3) insufficient outcome information that could not be analysed; (4) duplicate reports of literature research; (5) reports with missing data from the results.

Outcome measures

In previous literature, blood ammonia level, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) level and Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease (MELD) Score were correlated with the severity of hepatic encephalopathy. Secondly, MHE Remission Rate and Incidence of overt OHE are related to the treatment effect of hepatic encephalopathy. In addition, the psychometric Hepatic Encephalopathy Score validated this test for the diagnosis of MHE in the non‐alcoholic cirrhosis population in Germany. It is a paper‐and‐pencil psychometric test battery that is composed of the following five tests: number connection test A (NCT A), number connection test B (NCT B), digit symbol test (DST), serial dotting test (SDT) and line tracing test (LTT). 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 The outcome measures selected for this study were highly relevant for assessing the efficacy of probiotics in treating HE: (1) blood ammonia level; (2) NCT reaction time; (3) MHE remission rate; (4) incidence of MHE; (5) DST score; (6) ALT level; (7) MELD score.

Study selection and data extraction

Two researchers independently screened the literature according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. First, preliminary screening was performed by reading the title and abstract of the literature. Full‐text reading was then performed on studies that potentially met the inclusion criteria. When the two researchers had inconsistent opinions, a discussion took place with a third researcher to reach a consensus. After the literature screening was completed, the data were extracted by the two researchers according to a standard data extraction table. The extracted information included literature information, research type, research time, demographic characteristics of the participants, probiotic preparation type, dosage and time, control type and outcome index.

Assessments of quality

A bias risk assessment was conducted on the included studies using the bias risk assessment tool for RCTs recommended by the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews of Interventions (Version 6.2). 23 The elements of the bias risk assessment included random allocation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessors, integrity of outcome data and selective reporting of results. The evaluation results of each item were divided into three levels: low, unclear and high risk, and they were represented by a bias risk map. The two researchers used a standardised data extraction table to independently evaluate the risk of bias in the included studies. Inconsistent results were addressed by a discussion with a third researcher to reach a consensus.

Statistical analysis

RevMan 5.3 software was used for statistical analysis. Mean difference (MD) was used as a statistic for outcome variables (DST reaction time, ALT level and MELD score), calculated by the same standard in continuous variables. Standard mean difference (SMD) was used as a statistic for outcome variables (blood ammonia level and DST score), calculated by different standards in continuous variables. For dichotomous variables (the incidence of OHE and the remission rate of MHE), the risk ratio (RR) was calculated, and the 95% confidence interval (CI) was used to estimate the interval range of the effect size. The I 2 test was used to determine the extent of heterogeneity among studies. If I 2 < 50% or p > .1, the included literature was considered homogeneous, and a fixed‐effects model (Mantel–Haenszel) was used for analysis. If I 2 > 50% or p ≤ .1, the included studies were considered heterogeneous, and a random‐effects model (DerSimonian–Laird) was used for analysis. If the heterogeneity was large, subgroup analysis or sensitivity analysis was used to explore the potential source. A value of p < .05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant.

RESULTS

Literature screening process and results

A total of 361 studies were obtained through preliminary screening. After layer‐by‐layer screening, 18 RCTs were included in the meta‐analysis. The details of the literature screening process and results are shown in Figure 1.

Basic characteristics and bias risk assessment of the included studies

The 18 RCT studies involved 1226 participants. The duration of administration primarily ranged from 1 to 3 months, and the participants were mostly 40–60 years old. In each study, the probiotic and control groups included men and women, and there was no significant difference in the ratio between the two groups. However, a small number of studies did not report this ratio. The controls included in the studies were placebo controls, blank controls and either lactose or conventional treatments. In addition, the included studies had a low risk of bias, and the participants were included randomly in the trial design to evaluate the efficacy of probiotics using objective methods. Meta‐analysis

Effect of probiotics on blood ammonia level

A total of 14 RCTs were included, with I 2 = 98%. The random‐effects model analysis showed that blood ammonia levels in the probiotic treatment group were significantly lower than those in the control group (SMD = −2.68, 95% CI: −3.90 to −1.46, p < .0001) (see Figure 3).

Meta‐analysis of the effect of probiotics on blood ammonia level in patients with MHE.

Effect of probiotics on alanine aminotransferase levels

A total of 8 RCTs were included, with I 2 = 97%. The random‐effects model analysis showed that the ALT level in the probiotic treatment group was significantly lower than that in the control group (MD = −11.10, 95% CI: −16.17 to −6.03, p < .0001) (see Figure 4).

Meta‐analysis of the effect of probiotics on ALT levels in MHE patients.

Effect of probiotics on number connection test reaction time

A total of 9 RCTs were included, with I 2 = 99%. The random‐effects model analysis showed that the NCT reaction time in the probiotic treatment group was significantly lower than that in the control group (MD = −12.54, 95% CI: −21.63 to −3.46, p = .007) (see Figure 5).

Meta‐analysis of the effect of probiotics on NCT reaction time in MHE patients.

Effect of probiotics on the Digit Symbol Test score

A total of 4 RCTs were included, with I 2 = 99%. The random‐effects model analysis showed that there was no significant difference in the DST score between the probiotic treatment group and the control group (SMD = .81, 95% CI: −2.66–4.29, p = .65) (see Figure 6).

Meta‐analysis of the effect of probiotics on DST score in MHE patients.

Effect of probiotics on the Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease score

A total of 3 RCTs were included, with I 2 = 88%. The random‐effects model analysis showed that the MELD score of the probiotic treatment group was significantly lower than that of the control group (MD = −2.55, 95% CI: −3.56 to −1.54, p < .00001) (see Figure 7).

Meta‐analysis of the effect of probiotics on MELD score in MHE patients.

Effect of probiotics on the minimum hepatic encephalopathy remission rate

A total of 7 RCTs were included, with I 2 = 86%. The random‐effects model analysis showed that the reversal rate of MHE in the probiotic treatment group was significantly higher than that in the control group (RR = 2.79, 95% CI: 1.23–6.35, p = .01) (see Figure 8).

Meta‐analysis of the effect of probiotics on MHE remission rate in MHE patients.

Effect of probiotics on the incidence of minimum hepatic encephalopathy

A total of 7 RCTs were included, with I 2 = 0%. The fixed‐effects model analysis showed that the incidence of OHE in the probiotic treatment group was significantly lower than that in the control group (RR = .18, 95% CI: .09–.34, p < .00001) (see Figure 9).

Meta‐analysis of the effect of probiotics on the incidence of OHE in MHE patients.

DISCUSSION

In this study, 18 RCTs reporting the relationship between probiotics and MHE were included in the meta‐analysis, involving 1226 participants. Our results showed that probiotics had a significant positive correlation with the remission of MHE and a negative correlation with the occurrence of OHE. Compared with patients receiving only conventional or placebo treatments, patients receiving probiotic treatment had more significant reductions in blood ammonia levels, ALT levels, NCT reaction times and MELD scores before and after treatment. However, this study did not find a statistically significant positive effect of probiotic treatment on the improvement of DST scores in patients with HE.

The results of this study were consistent with previous research. A meta‐analysis by Pu et al. 36 also showed that probiotics alleviated MHE (RR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.11–1.97, p = .008), reduced blood ammonia levels and shortened NCT reaction times. However, it did not find evidence that probiotics reduced ALT levels in patients. In addition, a meta‐analysis assessing the effect of probiotics on the incidence of MHE demonstrated a 54% reduction in its occurrence (RR = .46, 95% CI: .26–.81, p = .007). This was consistent with the conclusion of this study. 37

Although there are many theories to explain the pathogenesis of HE, it has not yet been fully elucidated. The ammonia poisoning theory was the first to be proposed and remains the most widely accepted hypothesis. 38 The exact pathogenesis of MHE, although complex and not fully understood, shares similarities with HE, wherein elevated blood ammonia levels play a crucial role in its pathophysiological basis. 39 Therefore, treating MHE requires controlling blood ammonia levels. There are many microorganisms in the intestine, and ammonia produced by some microbial functions is the main source of blood ammonia. Studies have found that patients with cirrhosis often have gastrointestinal symptoms. 40 Intestinal barrier dysfunction in patients with cirrhosis is related to intestinal microbial products and microbial disorders. Therefore, intestinal microorganisms may be involved in the pathogenesis of cirrhosis complications. 41

The imbalance of intestinal flora can lead to the destruction of the intestine‐brain axis, affecting the behaviour of patients. The dynamic balance of the intestinal tract and the brain plays an important role in maintaining nerve, hormone and immune levels. The imbalance of intestinal flora can lead to increased intestinal ammonia production, resulting in the occurrence of HE. 42 , 43 Probiotics are a class of microorganisms that are beneficial to the human body. 44 They can regulate intestinal flora and improve intestinal permeability, thereby reducing plasma ammonia levels, improving liver function in patients with MHE, alleviating MHE and preventing its occurrence. 45 The gut‐brain axis partly optimises bidirectional regulatory interaction between the gut and the brain through endocrine, immune and neurological pathways. 46 The mechanism of probiotic supplements can be interpreted mainly through these three routes of communication. In the immune pathway, probiotics are proven to prevent bacterial translocation, facilitate intestinal barrier function, decrease inflammatory cytokine levels and suppress the activation of microglia, thereby attenuating systemic and neural inflammation. 47 Due to peripheral inflammation inducing neuroinflammation, probiotics' function in preventing bacterial translocation and reducing inflammatory cytokine levels could potentially improve both peripheral inflammation and neuroinflammation. In neurological pathways, probiotics also play a role in influencing cognition. Several studies have found that the use of probiotics significantly increased the gene and protein expression of brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and reduced oxidative stress and apoptosis in the hippocampus. 48 Brain‐derived neurotrophic factor is a critical neurotrophin that plays a crucial role in neuronal growth and survival, as well as plasticity, which is important for cognitive processes. 49 Another mechanism might be that most Lactobacillus species and other probiotics excite the vagal nerves that connect with all the neurons involved in behavioural changes (e.g. depression, anxiety, learning and memory). 50 In terms of endocrine pathways, one study indicated that Lactobacillus helveticus NS8 supplementation normalised most cognitive disorders by reducing corticosterone release to modulate the function of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and by recovering BDNF, noradrenaline and 5‐HT levels. 51

This study showed that in comparison with the control group, probiotic treatment significantly reduced blood ammonia and ALT levels in patients with MHE, shortened NCT reaction time, lowered the incidence of MHE and improved its remission rate. Probiotics are more effective than no treatment, conventional treatment or placebo treatment for improving liver and cognitive functions, promoting the remission of MHE and preventing its occurrence. Although this study focuses on the effects of probiotics on MHE, other independent studies, which are not part of this research, have examined the comparative efficacy of probiotics and pharmacological treatments (e.g. lactulose and rifaximin) in managing HE. These studies suggest that probiotics may offer a comparable benefit in reducing blood ammonia levels and improving cognitive function with potentially fewer adverse reactions. 52 , 53

This study has certain limitations. (1) There was significant heterogeneity among the RCTs regarding blood ammonia levels, ALT levels, DST scores, NCT reaction times and MHE remission rates, which may be caused by different dosages, frequency of medication and demographic differences. (2) The study retrieved three databases, and the limited number of included research may have affected the stability of the results. (3) Although most of the included studies mentioned random allocation, they did not report specific random allocation methods, which may have affected the reliability of the results. The conclusion of this study needs to be further verified by high‐quality, large‐sample RCTs. (4) This study includes 12 trials from China, which may restrict the international clinical applicability of the results and affect the widespread adoption of probiotic treatment efficacy. Future research in more countries and regions is necessary to validate the generalisability of these findings.

In summary, this study showed that there is a significant positive correlation between probiotics and the remission rate of MHE. Probiotics can reduce blood ammonia levels in patients with MHE, improve cognitive function, alleviate MHE and prevent its occurrence, thereby providing new evidence for treatment.

CONCLUSION

In summary, this study showed that there is a significant positive correlation between probiotics and remission of MHE. Probiotics effectively reduce blood ammonia levels, enhance cognitive function, alleviate symptoms of MHE and prevent its onset. Probiotics should be further studied in relation to different forms and phases of HE. To further validate the generalisability of these results, larger clinical trials and research in additional countries are needed.
