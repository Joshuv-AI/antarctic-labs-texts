Heavy metals exposure and infertility: a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies
Authors: Saba Goodarzi, Motahareh Hasani, Shirin Djalalinia, Razieh Lotfi, Armita Mahdavi-Gorabi, Mostafa Qorbani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfertility is a major public health concern worldwide, with growing evidence implicating environmental exposures to heavy metals as potential contributors. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the association between heavy metals in biological samples and infertility among men and women.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, a systematic search on PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science was conducted. Observational studies reporting levels of arsenic, cadmium, chromium, mercury, nickel, and lead in infertile versus fertile individuals were included until December 2025. Data screening, data extraction and quality assessment was performed by two researchers independently. Heterogeneity between studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic. Meta-analysis was performed using fixed/random-effects model to pool standardized mean differences (SMD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsA total of 22 studies were included in the systematic review. In the qualitative synthesis, although the association of infertility with different heavy metals was statistically significant in the most of included studies, the findings were heterogeneous among studies. In the quantitative synthesis, seminal levels of cadmium (pooled SMD = 1.84, 95% CI: 0.15, 3.53), blood lead levels (SMD = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.06, 1.32), and blood mercury concentrations (SMD = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.02, 0.30) was significantly higher in infertile individuals compared with fertile counterparts. Other pooled associations between heavy metals and infertility were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).ConclusionThe study findings support a significant association between heavy metals, particularly cadmium, lead and mercury with infertility. These findings underscore the importance of mitigating environmental and occupational exposure to heavy metals to protect reproductive health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41043-026-01397-5.Keywords: Infertility, Heavy metals, Meta-analysis, Reproductive health, Nickel, Chromium, Cadmium, Lead, Mercury, Arsenic, Trace elements

Introduction

Infertility is a medical condition that impacts millions of people and couples worldwide, often leading to profound physical, spiritual, mental, and medical challenges. It is associated with devastating health and societal consequences, including economic hardship, diminished mental and physical well-being, and social stigma [1–3].

Infertility is a condition defined by the inability to achieve a clinical pregnancy after one year of consistent, unprotected sexual activity. [4]. Infertility may happen due to identifiable female or male factors, or unexplained factors [5]. Certain causes of infertility can be avoided. [6]. A systematic review study reported an overall pooled prevalence of 46.25% for infertility and 51.5% for primary infertility [7].

Many lifestyle, environmental, and genetic factors have been implicated in its pathogenesis [8]. Several metals, such as cadmium (Cd), mercury (Hg), and lead (Pb), are considered reproductive toxicants and Potential endocrine-disrupting substances [9–14]. Human exposure to heavy metals typically occurs through consuming contaminated food and water, as well as through contact with polluted soil or air [15]. Numerous clinical investigations and observational studies have examined the relationship between heavy metal exposure and reproductive health outcomes.

For instance, a study has identified significant associations between blood concentrations of Hg, Cd, Pb, and arsenic (As) and an increased risk of endometriosis [12]. Similarly, exposure to Cd in the environment has been associated withdeterioration in semen quality parameters in men [13]. Pb exposure has also been extensively studied, showing that elevated blood Pb levels are associated with infertility and related risk factors [9, 10, 16, 17]. Whereas another study failed to find such a link [18]. As is another heavy metal of concern; environmental exposure to As has been shown to negatively impact sperm quality [19]. Similar associations have also been observed for Hg, including a non-linear relationship between blood Hg levels and infertility. [11].

Although individual studies have explored the relationship between heavy metal exposure and infertility, the results are heterogeneous and sometimes contradictory, due to differences in study design, exposure assessment, population demographics, and biological sampling [20] Some have reported strong associations between specific metals and reduced fertility outcomes, while others have found weak or null results [21].

Given the rising infertility rates and the potential role of heavy metals, this systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate their association with infertility in both men and women. By synthesizing existing evidence, it seeks to clarify the reproductive toxicity of heavy metals, identify research gaps, and inform strategies to mitigate their impact and improve reproductive health globally.

Method

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the association between heavy metal levels and infertility in both men and women. The study followed the guidelines outlined by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [22]. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO with registration number CRD42024618989.

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive literature search on PubMed and NLM Gateway (for MEDLINE), Institute for Scientific Information (ISI), and SCOPUS from inception to December 29th, 2025. The search strategy incorporated Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords related to heavy metals (“heavy metal”, “lead”, “Pb”, “mercury”, “cadmium”, “arsenic”, “nickel”, “chromium”) and reproductive health indicators (“infertility”, “fertility”, “pregnancy”, “menstruation”, “ovulation”, “assisted reproductive technology”, “IVF”, “ART”, “adenomyosis”, “ovulatory disorders”) The search strings were customized for each database to ensure optimal retrieval of relevant articles. The full search strategy is provided in the Supplementary File. Moreover, the references of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify any articles missed during the database search.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) conducted on human participants with documented evidence of infertility; (2) measured at least one of the six heavy metals of interest—Pb, Cd, Cr, Hg, Ni, or As—in biological samples, including urine, semen, blood, or biopsy tissues in infertile and fertile population; (3) employed an observational study design (cross-sectional, case-control, or cohort studies).

Non-human or in vitro studies, case reports, letters, comments, reviews, and meta-analyses were excluded. Studies published before 2000 were excluded because they were considered outdated. In addition, low-quality articles that were not eligible for data extraction were excluded.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Title and abstracts of retrieved articles were screened independently by two reviewers, followed by full-text assessments for potentially eligible studies. The following information was extracted from each study, including study characteristics (e.g., author, year, country), population demographics, type of heavy metal investigated, methods of sampling and measurement, and effect measures. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer when necessary. The quality of included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [23] for observational studies by two reviewers.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 18. Meta-analyses were performed using either fixed-effect or random-effects models, depending on the degree of between-study heterogeneity. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify differences in heavy metal concentrations between infertile individuals and fertile controls, and heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic. Separate meta-analyses were conducted for each heavy metal according to the biological sample in which it was measured. Owing to the limited number of available studies, meta-analyses were performed only when at least two independent studies reported concentrations of the same metal measured in the same biological sample. Studies reporting concentrations of the same metal in different biological matrices (blood, semen, or urine) were analyzed separately to account for biological and methodological differences across sampling matrices. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using a leave-one-out approach, whereby each study was sequentially excluded and the meta-analysis was re-estimated using random-effects models with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation to assess the robustness of the pooled effect estimates. Formal assessment of publication bias, including funnel plots or statistical tests, was not performed due to the limited number of studies included in each meta-analysis.

Result

A total of 4,958 records were identified through the systematic search. After removing duplicates and excluding studies published before 2000, 3,272 articles were screened based on their titles and abstracts. Subsequently, the full texts of 335 articles were reviewed for eligibility, and 22 studies met the inclusion criteria for this systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1). The included studies were published between 2005 and 2021 and involved 5,161 participants (2,084 men and 3,077 women). The included studies were conducted across various regions worldwide, including four in China and three in India. Study designs included arecase-control, and cross-sectional, with sample sizes ranging from 40 to 1796 participants. Heavy metal concentrations have been assessed in various biological samples, including urine (n = 2), blood (n = 11), semen (11), follicular fluid (n = 1) and tissue biopsies, such as endometrial tissue (n = 1). Qualitative synthesis

Three observational studies evaluated the association between blood Hg concentrations and infertility. In a study by Zhu, F. et al., [24], elevated blood Hg levels (> 5.27 µg/L) were associated with a significantly higher odds of women infertility (OR = 2.57; 95% CI: 1.12, 5.87). Similarly, Maeda et al. [25] reported a significant association between blood Hg concentration and infertility (OR = 39.9; 95% CI: 1.91, 836) (Table S1).In contrast, Mendiola et al. [26] found no statistically significant association between Hg concentrations measured in seminal plasma or serum and abnormal semen analysis ( Table S2).

Only one observational study assessed the association between seminal Ni concentrations and infertility and reported no statistically significant association (Table S2) [27].

Across all studies examining the association between Cr exposure and infertility, Cr concentrations measured in follicular fluid [28], seminal samples [29], and blood [30] were not associated with infertility-related outcomes.

Among three studies reporting ORs for the association between As exposure and infertility, findings were heterogeneous across exposure matrices (Table S1). Two [25, 31] studies assessing blood arsenic concentrations reported no statistically significant associations with infertility outcomes, including a non-significant inverse association with fertility (OR = 0.64; 95% CI: 0.38, 1.09). In contrast, one study [32] identified a significant positive association between urinary arsenic exposure and infertility, with higher odds observed in the highest exposure quartile compared with the lowest (OR = 4.3; 95% CI: 1.7,10.9; Q4 vs. Q1). Regarding As measured in reproductive fluids, one study reported significantly lower follicular fluid arsenic concentrations among women with fallopian tube obstruction compared with controls (33.72 ± 9.50 vs. 37.64 ± 9.39 µg/L; p < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed between women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and controls [28]. Pb exposure consistently proved a positive association with infertility, with odds ratios ranging from 2.60 to 2.94 [33, 34]. Cd detected in endometrial samples was significantly associated with an increased risk of unexplained infertility, with an odds ratio of 18.9 (95% CI: 4.5,79.9) [35]. Although, some of the studies revealed no significant association between these three metals and infertility [25, 31, 35].

Quantitative synthesis

A total of seven separate meta-analyses were conducted to examine differences in heavy metal concentrations between infertile individuals and fertile controls across blood and semen samples. In blood samples, As concentrations did not differ significantly between groups based on two studies including 505 participants (SMD = 0.15, 95% CI: -0.45, 0.74) (Fig S1C), although substantial heterogeneity was observed (I² = 86.5%). Blood Cd levels, showed a positive but non-significant pooled effect (SMD = 0.93, 95% CI: -0.04, 1.89) (Fig S1A), with very high heterogeneity (I² = 98.1%). In contrast, blood Pb concentrations were significantly higher among infertile individuals compared with fertile controls (SMD = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.06, 1.32) (Fig S1B), although considerable heterogeneity remained (I² = 96.7%). Blood Hg concentrations were assessed in two studies comprising a total of 1,857 participants. The pooled analysis showed a small but statistically significant increase in mercury levels among infertile individuals compared with fertile controls (SMD = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.02, 0.30). No between-study heterogeneity was observed (I² = 0%), indicating high consistency across the included studies (Table 2).

In semen samples, As concentrations showed no significant difference between infertile individuals and fertile controls (SMD = 6.68, 95% CI: -6.15, 19.50) (Fig S2C), with extreme heterogeneity (I² = 99.9%). Seminal Cd concentrations were significantly higher in infertile individuals compared with fertile controls (SMD = 1.84, 95% CI: 0.15, 3.53) (Fig S2B), although substantial heterogeneity was observed (I² = 99.3%). No significant difference was identified for seminal Pb concentrations across seven studies (SMD = 0.34, 95% CI: -0.16, 0.83) (Fig S2A), despite high between-study heterogeneity (I² = 90.8%) (Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that the pooled association between blood Cd and infertility was unstable, with loss of statistical significance in most iterations. In contrast, the association between seminal Cd levels and infertility remained directionally consistent, showing moderate robustness despite substantial heterogeneity ( Fig. S4).

For blood Pb, the direction of effect remained positive across all iterations, although statistical significance was sensitive to the exclusion of individual studies. The association between seminal Pb levels and infertility remained non-significant throughout sensitivity analyses, indicating a consistently weak association (Fig. S3).

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Overall, the studies demonstrated varying levels of quality,50% classified as high-quality, indicating generally acceptable methodological rigor across the included literature, 31.8% as moderate-quality, and 18.1% as low-quality. Most studies adequately addressed participant selection and exposure/outcome assessment, but some lacked detailed methodological descriptions, limiting their reproducibility. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides a comprehensive evaluation of the association between heavy metals and infertility, pooling data from 22 studies conducted across varied populations. The pooled analysis reveals a significant association between heavy metal exposure and risk of infertility, indicating notably higher levels of heavy metals among infertile individuals compared to their fertile counterparts. This association persisted despite considerable, underscoring the complexity and multifactorial nature of infertility and environmental exposures.

Among the individual heavy metals analyzed, Cd and Pb emerged as the most consistently associated with infertility. However, other metals such as As, Cr, and Ni did not show consistent associations, highlighting that not all heavy metals may contribute equally to infertility risk.

In this study, cadmium exposure was associated with infertility, with the association being more pronounced for cadmium concentrations measured in seminal samples compared with blood. This association may be attributed to cadmium’s endocrine-disrupting properties and its tendency to accumulate in reproductive tissues [36, 37]. In a study conducted by Shen et al. [38] it has been shown that Cd has a significant association with endometriosis, consistent with another study which reported Cd levels in endometrial samples were significantly associated with infertility [35], highlighting tissue-specific accumulation and reinforcing the potential role of Cd in reproductive dysfunction. In addition, the association between Cd and male infertility indices such as the distribution of sperm quality, is shown in several studies [39–41].

Likewise, Pb exposure showed a significant association with infertility, consistent with previous findings of its deleterious effects on sperm quality, hormonal balance, and ovulatory function [34, 42]. These biological disruptions likely contribute to the observed infertility outcomes associated with Pb. Several studies found a significant inverse association between sperm parameters and Pb, which showed a negative correlation with semen volume [43] or sperm concentration [44]. The qualitative synthesis of studies reporting odds ratios further supported these patterns.

The result of this study is consistent with the study conducted by Kaur et al. [45]. This Meta-analysis revealed a significant association between blood Pb with male infertility, with SMD of 0.67 (95%CI: 0.07, 1.28, p = 0.03). In addition, a significant association for semen Cd was also noted.

In the present meta-analysis, blood Hg concentrations were modestly but significantly higher among infertile individuals compared with fertile controls, while the limited number of available studies warrants cautious interpretation of this finding. Mercury’s potential reproductive toxicity is well documented, particularly its interference with oocyte maturation and oxidative stress pathways [46, 47]. However, variability in sample types and exposure duration may partly account for the modest magnitude of the association observed.

For As pooled estimates did not indicate statistically significant associations. In the case of As, the wide confidence interval and non-significant p-value likely reflect high heterogeneity and small sample sizes. Some epidemiological studies have shown a link between As exposure and reproductive dysfunction, but the results remain inconclusive, partly due to differences in As speciation and bioavailability [31, 48]. However, mechanistic studies [49] suggest that As exposure can induce oxidative stress, disrupt hormonal balance, and impair gametogenesis, which may contribute to reproductive dysfunction. The discrepancy between mechanistic evidence and epidemiological findings likely reflects heterogeneity in study populations, exposure levels, As speciation, and sample sizes.

Building upon this pattern of heterogeneous evidence, Cr and Ni similarly exhibited non-significant associations with infertility. Chromium’s overall effect was non-significant, though previous literature has suggested that certain forms of Cr (e.g., hexavalent Cr) may have toxic effects on reproductive organs [50]. Similarly, seminal Ni did not show a significant association with infertility, although limited data preclude definitive conclusions [27], These findings are consistent with a systematic review on Ni exposure and human sperm quality, which reported conflicting results and found no clear evidence of adverse effects in humans, despite mechanistic evidence from animal models [51].

Overall, while Cd, Pb, and Hg consistently show associations with infertility, the non-significant findings for As, Cr, and Ni emphasize the heterogeneity of evidence and suggest that environmental contributions to infertility are complex and multifactorial.

Strength and limitation

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, most included studies were cross-sectional studies limiting causal inference due to the lack of temporal assessment. Although the consistency of associations and biological plausibility may partially support causality, the findings should still be interpreted with caution. Therefore, prospective cohort studies are needed to further clarify the causal relationship. Second, substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed in several pooled analyses, indicating that the pooled SMDs should be interpreted with caution, as they may not represent fully reliable average effects across studies. Although potential sources of heterogeneity may include participant age, exposure duration, geographic variation, assay methods, and differences in infertility etiology, further subgroup analyses were limited by the small number of included studies. Therefore, only subgroup analysis based on sample type was feasible.

Third, the number of available studies was limited for some metals and biological matrices, most notably Hg and As, reducing the precision and robustness of pooled estimates. In addition, sensitivity analyses indicated limited robustness for certain associations, suggesting that some findings were sensitive to the inclusion of individual studies. Variability in exposure assessment across different biological matrices (blood, semen, urine, follicular fluid, and tissue samples) further complicates direct comparisons across studies.

Despite these limitations, this study has important strengths. It represents one of the most comprehensive systematic reviews and meta-analyses to date examining multiple heavy metals and infertility in both men and women. The use of matrix-specific meta-analyses allowed biologically relevant distinctions between systemic and local exposure to be explored.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study underscores the potential reproductive toxicity of specific heavy metals, particularly Cd and Pb. These findings have important public health implications, highlighting the need for regulatory efforts to limit environmental and occupational exposure, and the importance of environmental exposure assessments as a part of infertility workups and suggest the potential utility of screening for heavy metals in high-risk populations, especially in polluted regions or industrial.

Given the substantial heterogeneity future research should adopt longitudinal designs with standardized exposure assessments and account for potential confounders, including age, smoking, and occupational history. Furthermore, studies should explore interactions between multiple toxicants and their cumulative impact on reproductive health.
