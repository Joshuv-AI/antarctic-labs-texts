Construction and property characterization of PNIPAAm-grafted chitosan/sodium alginate composite Thermo-responsive hydrogel films
Authors: Xiaoxue Lu, Jingna Liu, Yuanhong Zhuang, Bingli Wang, Peng Fei, Wenqiang Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A series of thermo-responsive PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite hydrogel films were synthesized via radical polymerization and ionic cross-linking. FTIR, XPS, and NMR confirmed the successful covalent grafting of PNIPAAm onto the chitosan (CS) backbone. XRD and SSNMR analyses revealed that the grafting disrupted the CS crystalline regions, inducing an amorphous structural transition. The PNIPAAm-CS copolymer solutions exhibited LCST values in the range of 30–32 °C. Notably, compared with the corresponding precursor systems, the hydrated films exhibited an apparent thermo-responsive transition onset approximately 1 °C higher in temperature-dependent optical measurements, suggesting that film-network formation influenced the thermal response behavior of the PNIPAAm-containing system. Grafting modification significantly improved optical and mechanical properties: PNIPAAm-CS/SA-4 achieved a peak transmittance of 82.81% (vs. 50.78% for pure CS/SA), while PNIPAAm-CS/SA-2 reached an optimal balance with 22.6 MPa tensile strength and ∼ 100% elongation. Furthermore, antibacterial evaluation of the PNIPAAm-CS copolymers showed that, although the inhibitory activity decreased with increasing PNIPAAm incorporation, measurable antibacterial effects against E. coli and S. aureus were retained. Together with the preliminary package-integrated visual response demonstration, these results suggest that the hydrated films may serve as candidate temperature-responsive indicator materials for further development in intelligent packaging.

Introduction

In recent years, intelligent polymeric materials with environmental responsiveness have garnered significant attention in the fields of biomedicine, food packaging, and sensors. Among these, thermo-responsive hydrogels, which undergo reversible transitions in physical states or chemical properties in response to ambient temperature changes, have emerged as a prominent research focus (Wang et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2026). As an abundant natural alkaline polysaccharide, chitosan (CS) is widely utilized in constructing functional composites due to its exceptional biocompatibility, biodegradability, and antibacterial activity. However, native chitosan lacks inherent temperature sensitivity, and its high crystallinity partially restricts its solubility and application flexibility in specific environments (Zhao et al., 2026). To impart thermo-responsiveness to chitosan, poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAAm), which possesses a lower critical solution temperature (LCST), is commonly employed for modification. PNIPAAm exhibits a unique phase transition behavior in aqueous solutions (LCST ≈ 32 °C); specifically, the macromolecular chains transition from a hydrophilic extended state to a hydrophobic collapsed state when the temperature exceeds this threshold. Previous studies have demonstrated that grafting PNIPAAm onto chitosan can endow the polymer with thermo-responsive phase-transition behavior. For example, PNIPAAm-CS has been investigated as a thermosensitive in situ gel-forming system for ocular drug delivery, exhibiting an LCST close to 32 °C (Cao et al., 2007).

Sodium alginate (SA), an anionic polysaccharide with favorable film-forming and gel-forming properties, can interact with positively charged chitosan through polyelectrolyte complexation and can be further stabilized by Ca2+ cross-linking. Previous studies have constructed CS/oxidized SA/Ca2+ hydrogels to improve network stability and functional performance, such as adsorption capacity (Zhang et al., 2024). However, the role of such a polysaccharide-based network in regulating the phase-transition behavior of grafted thermo-responsive segments in a film state remains insufficiently understood.

In a PNIPAAm-grafted polysaccharide film system, the thermo-responsive transition of PNIPAAm segments is not only governed by the hydration–dehydration balance of the amide groups, but may also be affected by the surrounding network environment. The CS/SA/Ca2+-associated ionic network can provide multiple physical constraints, including electrostatic interactions between CS and SA, Ca2+-mediated alginate junctions, hydrogen bonding, and chain entanglement. These interactions may restrict the conformational mobility, free-volume contraction, and hydrophobic association of grafted PNIPAAm segments during heating. As a result, the transition behavior in hydrated films may differ from that in precursor solutions, appearing as a shifted or weakened macroscopic optical response. Therefore, elucidating how this network environment influences the apparent thermo-responsive transition behavior is important for understanding the structure–property relationship of PNIPAAm-CS/SA hydrogel films.

Chitosan/sodium alginate-based films have been extensively investigated for packaging-related applications. For instance, M. He et al. (2024) developed CS/SA multilayer films containing anthocyanins through layer-by-layer assembly for pH-responsive intelligent packaging applications. In addition, CS/oxidized SA/Ca2+ hydrogels have been constructed to improve network stability and adsorption-related properties (Zhang et al., 2024). Thermo-responsive polysaccharide systems containing PNIPAAm have also been reported. Cao et al. (2007) investigated PNIPAAm-CS as a thermosensitive in situ gel-forming system for ocular drug delivery. However, these studies mainly focused on pH-responsive packaging films, adsorption hydrogels, injectable gels, or drug-delivery systems. The construction of PNIPAAm-grafted CS/SA thermo-responsive hydrogel films, together with the elucidation of grafting-induced solid-state structural evolution and its relationship with film transparency, mechanical behavior, and thermo-responsive performance, has received limited attention.

In contrast, the present study constructs a thermo-responsive hydrogel film by grafting PNIPAAm onto the chitosan backbone and subsequently integrating the graft copolymer into an SA/Ca2+-assisted film-forming network. More importantly, this work focuses on the solid-state structure–property relationship of the resulting films. SSNMR combined with XRD was employed to elucidate the grafting-induced disruption of ordered chitosan domains and the accompanying amorphous structural transition, while the corresponding changes in film-state phase-transition behavior, optical transparency, and mechanical performance were systematically evaluated. Therefore, the scientific contribution of this study lies not simply in preparing another CS/SA-based film, but in clarifying how thermo-responsive chain grafting and polysaccharide network formation collectively regulate the functional behavior of a film-forming material.

Although studies on PNIPAAm-grafted chitosan have been reported, precisely elucidating the impact of the grafting reaction on the microenvironment of the polysaccharide backbone and the complex intermolecular interactions within this ternary system remains a challenge. Notably, traditional liquid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) faces limitations such as restricted solubility and insufficient resolution when processing highly grafted or cross-linked solid membrane materials, making it difficult to deeply reveal their solid-state chemical environments. In this study, a series of PNIPAAm-CS copolymers with varying grafting ratios were synthesized via radical polymerization using CS and NIPAM as primary raw materials, and PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite hydrogel membranes were further constructed by incorporating SA. The chemical structures, grafting sites, and crystalline behaviors of the copolymers were systematically characterized using FTIR, XPS, solid-state NMR (SSNMR), and XRD. Emphasis was placed on analyzing the distribution of PNIPAAm segments on the chitosan backbone and their disruptive effect on the polysaccharide crystalline regions through 13C CP/MAS spectra (Ying Li et al., 2025). The morphological transformation and phase transition patterns of the system at different temperatures were investigated using a super-depth-of-field three-dimensional microscopic system and the cloud point method. Additionally, the effects of the grafting ratio on the mechanical properties, light transmittance, and antibacterial characteristics of the composite membranes were evaluated in detail. This research not only provides an experimental basis for developing intelligent composite membranes with precise temperature-responsive capabilities but also offers a new perspective for the high-value modification of natural polysaccharides. Accordingly, this study investigated whether PNIPAAm grafting and subsequent CS/SA/Ca2+-associated network formation could modulate the structural and functional properties of thermo-responsive hydrogel films. Particular attention was paid to the grafting-induced structural evolution of the chitosan backbone, as revealed by SSNMR and XRD, and to its relationship with the optical transparency, mechanical performance, and apparent thermo-responsive transition behavior of the resulting hydrated films.

Experiment

Materials and instruments

Chitosan (CS, deacetylation degree 95.1%, viscosity 154.0 mPa·s, measured in 1% acetic acid at 20 °C, average molecular weight ∼ 100 kDa) was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Batch No. E2506130). N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAM, 98%) was obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Potassium persulfate (KPS, 99.9%) was supplied by Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sodium alginate (SA, viscosity 300 mPa·s measured in 10 g/L solution at 20 °C, loss on drying 12.1%, residue on ignition 30.0%, average molecular weight ∼ 250 kDa, and G/M ratio 1.39) was obtained from Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd. (Batch No. 241113A2). Anhydrous ethanol, acetic acid, and potassium bromide (KBr) were all purchased from Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd. (Guangdong, China). Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) strains were provided by the China Center of Industrial Culture Collection (CICC, Beijing, China). Luria-Bertani (LB) agar medium and LB broth medium were purchased from Hangzhou Best Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). All other reagents were of analytical grade unless otherwise specified.

Preparation of PNIPAAm-CS copolymers

PNIPAAm-CS copolymers were synthesized via radical polymerization. Initially, 4.0 g of chitosan (CS) was dispersed in 250 mL of 1% (v/v) acetic acid solution and magnetically stirred until complete dissolution. Simultaneously, varying amounts of N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAM) (1.4, 2.8, 5.6, or 11.2 g) were dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water, corresponding to molar ratios of CS to NIPAM of 1:0.5, 1:1, 1:2, and 1:4, respectively. The four CS:NIPAM feed molar ratios were selected to establish a systematic low-to-high NIPAM input gradient for regulating the PNIPAAm content in the chitosan-based copolymers. The lower ratios of 1:0.5 and 1:1 were designed to maintain a chitosan-dominated structure and film-forming ability, whereas the higher ratios of 1:2 and 1:4 were used to enhance the thermo-responsive contribution of PNIPAAm segments. The samples PNIPAAm-CS-1, PNIPAAm-CS-2, PNIPAAm-CS-3, and PNIPAAm-CS-4 correspond to the CS:NIPAM feed molar ratios of 1:0.5, 1:1, 1:2, and 1:4, respectively. These sample codes refer to the monomer feed ratios rather than the absolute grafting degrees. The two solutions were then mixed in a 1000 mL Erlenmeyer flask and stirred continuously for 30 min at room temperature under a nitrogen atmosphere to eliminate dissolved oxygen. Subsequently, 2.16 g of potassium persulfate (KPS) dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water was added to the reaction system, and the mixture was heated to 60 °C for a 6 h isothermal reaction.

Upon completion of the reaction, 300 mL of anhydrous ethanol was added to the solution, and the mixture was centrifuged at 4000 r/min to precipitate the copolymer. The resulting precipitate was washed five times with anhydrous ethanol to remove any residual PNIPAAm homopolymer and finally freeze-dried to obtain a solid powder. The products were designated as PNIPAAm-CS-1, PNIPAAm-CS-2, PNIPAAm-CS-3, and PNIPAAm-CS-4, according to the varying amounts of NIPAM added.

Structural characterization of PNIPAAm-CS copolymers

FTIR analysis

The infrared spectra of the samples were recorded using a Nicolet iS10 Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer. The samples were prepared using the potassium bromide (KBr) pellet method. The spectra were collected over a scanning range of 4000 cm−1 to 400 cm−1 at a resolution of 16 cm−1, with 32 scans performed for each spectrum (Ahadi et al., 2026).

1H NMR analysis

The 1H NMR spectra of the samples were recorded using a Quantum-I Plus 600 MHz liquid-state NMR spectrometer (Zhongke-Niujin, Wuhan, China). An appropriate amount of the sample was completely dissolved in deuterated ultrapure water (D2O) containing 1% DCl. The measurements were conducted at room temperature with an operating frequency of 600 MHz. Chemical shifts were reported in parts per million (ppm) and calibrated using the residual solvent peak (D2O, δ = 4.79 ppm) as an internal standard. The experimental data were processed and integrated using MestReNova software (Zhang et al., 2026).

SSNMR analysis

The solid-state 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance NEO 400 MHz spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). The cross-polarization magic-angle spinning (CP/MAS) technique was employed to enhance signal sensitivity and reduce acquisition time. Powdered samples were packed into 4 mm ZrO2 rotors and tested at room temperature (25 °C) with a spinning frequency of 8 kHz. The experimental parameters were set as follows: a contact time of 2 ms and a recycle delay of 3 s. Chemical shifts were reported in ppm and calibrated using tetramethylsilane (TMS, δ = 0 ppm) as an external standard (Zhang et al., 2026).

XRD analysis

The crystalline structures of the samples were analyzed using an Ultima IV X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan) at room temperature. The scanning angle (2θ) ranged from 5° to 80° to investigate the influence of PNIPAAm on the crystallinity of the chitosan molecules (Ghosh et al., 2024).

XPS analysis

The elemental composition and chemical states of the samples were analyzed using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha+ instrument. The samples were mounted on a holder, and an Al Kα X-ray source (HV = 1486.6 eV) was used for excitation. The C1s spectra were analyzed, and the binding energies were calibrated using the C1s peak at 284.80 eV as a reference (A. Zhang et al., 2025).

Variable-temperature Micro-morphology and Thermo-responsiveness analysis

The morphology and particle size distribution of the products were observed and analyzed using a super-depth-of-field three-dimensional microscope. During the experiment, a small amount of the PNIPAAm-CS copolymer solution was uniformly dispersed on a glass slide, and micro-images were captured in reflection mode at an appropriate magnification (Hao et al., 2024).

Physicochemical properties analysis of PNIPAAm-CS copolymers

Determination of LCST for copolymer solutions

The lower critical solution temperature (LCST) of the copolymer solutions was determined by the cloud point method using a microplate reader. The LCST was defined as the onset temperature at which the absorbance (optical density, OD value) began to increase due to the phase separation of the polymer. Initially, different products (PNIPAAm-CS-1, 2, 3, and 4) were respectively prepared as 0.60% (w/v) solutions in 1% aqueous acetic acid. Simultaneously, a series of PNIPAAm-CS-4 solutions with a mass fraction gradient (0.06%, 0.12%, 0.18%, 0.24%, 0.30%, 0.36%, 0.42%, 0.48%, 0.54%, and 0.60%) were prepared in 1% aqueous acetic acid. A DB70-2 A microplate constant temperature incubator (accuracy: ± 0.1 °C) was employed to control the sample temperature, with a monitoring range from 26 °C to 36 °C and a heating step of 1 °C. The variation in OD values for each sample was monitored within the wavelength range of 400–800 nm. The LCST of each sample was determined by plotting temperature-OD value curves (J. He et al., 2024).

Determination of antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activities of native chitosan and PNIPAAm-CS copolymers against Escherichia coli (CGMCC 1.3657) and Staphylococcus aureus (CGMCC 1.2465) were evaluated using the disk diffusion method. E. coli and S. aureus were selected as representative Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, respectively. These two strains are commonly used model microorganisms for evaluating the antibacterial performance of food packaging-related materials (Liu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024), because they represent bacteria with different cell-envelope structures and different sensitivities to chitosan-based antibacterial systems. Their use therefore allows a preliminary comparison of the antibacterial response of the copolymers toward both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. The cryopreserved strains were inoculated into LB broth and cultured with shaking at 37 °C and 200 r/min for 12 h to obtain activated bacterial suspensions. Subsequently, 100 μL of the bacterial suspension (106–107 CFU/mL) was uniformly spread onto LB agar plates. Blank susceptibility disks were placed on the plates, and 100 μL of 4% sample solution was loaded onto each disk. After pre-diffusion at 4 °C for 2–3 h, the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. The diameter of the inhibition zone was measured to calculate the inhibition rate (Wang et al., 2024):(1)Inhibition rate%=D1−D0D1×100%

Where D0 is the diameter of the blank susceptibility disk (6 mm), and D1 is the diameter of the inhibition zone.

Preparation of PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite hydrogel membranes

Initially, 0.4 g of PNIPAAm-CS was dispersed in 20 mL of 0.1 mol/L acetic acid solution and magnetically stirred at room temperature for 2–4 h to ensure complete dissolution. The undissolved impurities were removed by centrifugation, and the clear supernatant (2.0%, w/v) was collected. Separately, 5.0 g of sodium alginate (SA) powder was dissolved in 500 mL of deionized water to prepare a 1.0% (w/v) SA solution. Subsequently, 18.0 g of the SA solution was placed in a beaker, and 6.0 g of the PNIPAAm-CS solution was added dropwise into the central vortex of the SA solution using a pipette under moderate-speed magnetic stirring. The mixture was thoroughly homogenized using a homogenizer and then allowed to react on a water-bath magnetic stirrer for 3 h. The film-forming process was conducted as follows: A 90 mm Petri dish was pre-rinsed with a 3% (w/v) CaCl2 solution, and the excess liquid was discarded. Then, 5.0 g of the aforementioned mixed solution was added and spread uniformly. The resulting nascent gel film was immersed in a 3% CaCl2 solution for 10 min for cross-linking. After cross-linking, the membrane was repeatedly rinsed with deionized water to remove residual Ca2+ and acid, yielding the PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite hydrogel membranes (Wang et al., 2026). According to the type of PNIPAAm-CS used, the products were designated as PNIPAAm-CS/SA-1, PNIPAAm-CS/SA-2, PNIPAAm-CS/SA-3, and PNIPAAm-CS/SA-4, respectively. The obtained gel membranes were stored in deionized water at 4 °C for further use (Kjesbu et al., 2026).

Characterization of PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite hydrogel membranes

Scanning Electron microscopy

The surface morphology of the composite membranes was observed using a JSM-6010LA scanning electron microscope (JEOL, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. The samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold prior to observation. Micro-images were captured at magnifications of 100× and 1000× to analyze the surface microstructure of the PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite hydrogel membranes (Liu et al., 2025). The approximate sizes of representative nodular microdomains in the high-magnification SEM images were estimated based on the scale bars using ImageJ software. Ten representative nodular microdomains were measured for each sample, and the results were expressed as the size range and mean ± standard deviation.

Texture profile analysis

Dumbbell-shaped specimens (4 mm in width and 50 mm in length) were prepared using a cutting machine according to the GB/T-258 III standard. The mechanical properties of the composite membranes were measured using a CT3-10 K texture analyzer (AMETEK Brookfield, USA) with a tensile speed of 0.5 mm/s. Before tensile testing, the surface water of each hydrated film specimen was gently removed with filter paper, and the thickness was measured using a vernier caliper. Five positions were measured for each specimen, and the average value of the individual specimen was used as the parameter d in the tensile strength calculation. The average thickness of the hydrated film specimens was approximately 0.45 ± 0.08 mm. The maximum load (P), sample width (b), average thickness d, initial length (L0), and breaking length (L) were recorded to calculate the tensile strength (σT) and elongation at break (εB) according to the following equations (Liu et al., 2025):(2)σT=Pb×d(3)εB%=L−L0L0×100%

Determination of the apparent Thermo-responsive transition onset temperature of PNIPAAm-CS/SA precursor systems

The apparent thermo-responsive transition onset temperature of the PNIPAAm-CS/SA precursor systems was evaluated using temperature-dependent optical measurements with a microplate reader. During the measurement, the sample temperature was controlled using a DB70-2 A microplate constant-temperature incubator with an accuracy of ±0.1 °C, covering a range from 28 to 38 °C with a heating step of 1 °C. The OD values were recorded at fixed wavelengths within the range of 400–800 nm. The apparent transition onset temperature of each precursor system was identified from the initial inflection point of the temperature-dependent absorbance curve associated with thermally induced optical changes (J. He et al., 2024).

Determination of the apparent Thermo-responsive transition onset temperature of PNIPAAm-CS/SA hydrogel films

The apparent thermo-responsive transition onset temperature of the PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite hydrogel films was evaluated using the same temperature-dependent optical method. The hydrogel films were cut into circular discs matching the diameter of the microplate wells and placed at the bottom of the wells, with an appropriate amount of deionized water added to maintain hydration. The temperature was controlled using a DB70-2 A microplate constant-temperature incubator with an accuracy of ±0.1 °C, covering a range from 28 to 38 °C with a heating step of 1 °C. A microplate reader was used to monitor the variation in OD values within the wavelength range of 400–800 nm. The onset temperature of the marked increase in OD value associated with thermally induced optical changes was recorded as the apparent thermo-responsive transition onset temperature of the hydrated film (J. He et al., 2024).

Optical property characterization

The PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite hydrogel membranes were cut into uniform squares (3 mm × 3 mm) and placed flat in a microplate. A full-wavelength scan was performed within the range of 200–800 nm using a microplate reader to obtain the light transmittance (T%) spectra. The optical permeability and transparency of the composite hydrogel membranes were evaluated by monitoring the transmittance trends across different wavelengths (Liang et al., 2024).

Proof-of-concept package-integrated Thermo-responsive display test

To preliminarily evaluate the feasibility of the hydrogel films as temperature-responsive visual indicators for intelligent packaging, CS/SA and PNIPAAm-CS/SA-4 hydrogel films were selected as the control and representative thermo-responsive samples, respectively. The hydrated films were cut into circular discs with a diameter of 20 mm and sealed between two transparent polyethylene terephthalate (PET) sheets to maintain their hydrated state during testing. The edges of the PET sheets were sealed to minimize water evaporation, and the sealed film units were then attached to the outer surface of a transparent PET packaging model.

The samples were first equilibrated at 25 °C and photographed under fixed illumination, background, and camera distance conditions. Subsequently, the samples were transferred to 45 °C and maintained for 30 min. This temperature was higher than the apparent thermo-responsive transition onset temperature of the PNIPAAm-CS/SA hydrogel films. After heating, the samples were photographed again under identical conditions. The visual changes of the film units were quantified by analyzing the mean grayscale value of the film region using ImageJ software. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (including technical replicates), and the results were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis of the data was conducted using SPSS 22.0 software. Differences between groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (One-way ANOVA). When significant differences were observed, Duncan's multiple range test was subsequently performed for post-hoc analysis. The level of significance was set at P < 0.05. Error bars in the figures and tables represent the standard error of the mean (SEM).

1H NMR analysis

The chemical structures of CS and the series of PNIPAAm-CS copolymers were further characterized using 1H NMR spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 1(c). In the spectrum of CS, the multiple overlapping peaks observed in the range of δ = 3.30–3.80 ppm are attributed to the proton signals (H3-H6) of the glucopyranose ring (Chang et al., 2026), while the intense peak near δ = 4.80 ppm corresponds to the residual solvent peak of D2O. For the grafted PNIPAAm-CS products, distinct characteristic proton signals of PNIPAAm appeared in the spectra. Specifically, the peak at δ = 3.54 ppm is assigned to the methine proton (-CH-) of the isopropyl group in the PNIPAAm side chain. The prominent signal near δ = 1.22 ppm corresponds to the two methyl groups (-CH(CH3)2) of the isopropyl moiety, and the broad peaks in the range of δ = 1.37–2.0 ppm are attributed to the methylene (-CH2-) and methine (-CH-) protons of the PNIPAAm backbone. As the NIPAM feed ratio increased during the preparation process (from PNIPAAm-CS-1 to PNIPAAm-CS-4), the intensities of these PNIPAAm characteristic peaks significantly enhanced relative to the CS ring proton signals, exhibiting a clear gradient trend. These results are highly consistent with the findings from FTIR and XPS, confirming at the molecular level that PNIPAAm was successfully grafted onto the chitosan backbone. Furthermore, it demonstrates that the composition of the copolymers can be effectively modulated by adjusting the monomer feed ratio. SSNMR analysis

The solid-state chemical structures of chitosan (CS) and its grafted derivatives (PNIPAAm-CS) were further characterized using 13C CP/MAS NMR spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 1(d). In the spectrum of pure CS, typical signals of the glucopyranose ring carbons are observed: the resonance at δ = 105.54 ppm is attributed to the anomeric carbon (C1), δ = 83.38 ppm to the C4 involved in the glycosidic bond, δ = 75.62 ppm to the overlapping resonances of C3 and C5, and the peaks at δ = 61.46 ppm and 57.84 ppm correspond to the C6 and C2 (linked to the amino group) carbon atoms, respectively. Notably, the C1 and C4 signals in the pure CS spectrum exhibit relatively sharp and symmetrical profiles, reflecting the highly ordered crystalline network inherent to native chitosan.

For the grafted products (PNIPAAm-CS-1 to PNIPAAm-CS-4), besides the retained signals of the chitosan backbone, prominent new characteristic peaks appeared in both the downfield and upfield regions. Specifically, the intense signal at δ = 174.94 ppm corresponds to the amide carbonyl carbon (C=O) of PNIPAAm. In the upfield region, the peaks at δ = 41.50 ppm and 22.72 ppm are assigned to the methine carbon (-CH-) and methyl carbon (-CH3) of the isopropyl side chain in PNIPAAm, respectively. With the increase in the monomer feed ratio, the peak intensities of the PNIPAAm segments exhibited a clear gradient-enhancement trend, providing definitive molecular-level evidence that the poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) chains were successfully covalently grafted onto the chitosan backbone.

A detailed comparison of the spectra before and after grafting reveals that as the grafting ratio increases, the characteristic peaks in the chitosan ring region (55–110 ppm), particularly for C1 and C4, undergo significant broadening, and the original fine-splitting signals gradually become blurred. This amorphous broadening of the peaks directly reflects that the introduction of PNIPAAm side chains, through physical entanglement and chemical shielding effects, effectively disrupted the original intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen-bonding networks of chitosan, thereby significantly inhibiting the regular arrangement of its crystalline domains. This finding is not only highly consistent with the subsequent XRD results showing weakened diffraction peak intensities, but also explains the internal mechanism at the molecular level for the substantial increase in light transmittance of the composite membranes due to reduced crystallinity and fewer ordered interfaces. These results confirm that the PNIPAAm-CS graft modification successfully achieved precise regulation of the chitosan backbone microenvironment from a “highly ordered” to a “highly amorphous” state (Azizivahed et al., 2026).

XRD analysis

The crystalline structure variations of CS and the series of PNIPAAm-CS copolymers were further investigated via X-ray diffraction (XRD), and the results are shown in Fig. 1(e). Pure CS exhibits two prominent diffraction peaks at 2θ values of approximately 10.5° and 20.1°, corresponding to the (020) and (110) crystal planes of chitosan, respectively, which indicates its high crystallinity (Šárka et al., 2025). However, for the PNIPAAm-CS copolymers, the intensities of the original crystalline diffraction peaks of CS gradually weaken and tend to become broader and flatter as the PNIPAAm grafting amount increases.

When the NIPAM feed ratio reaches its maximum (PNIPAAm-CS-4), the crystal plane peak at 10.5° nearly disappears, and the diffraction peak at 20.1° transforms into a broad amorphous halo peak. This phenomenon indicates that the successful grafting of PNIPAAm side chains onto the chitosan backbone disrupted the original hydrogen-bonding network and the regular arrangement of CS segments, thereby reducing the crystallinity of the material and inducing a transition from a semi-crystalline to an amorphous state. This structural modification is conducive to enhancing the flexibility and solubility of the copolymers in the aqueous phase, which is consistent with the functional requirements of the material as a hydrogel substrate (Long et al., 2025).

XPS analysis

The elemental composition and chemical states of the surface of CS and the series of PNIPAAm-CS copolymers were further investigated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), as shown in Fig. 2. The survey spectra (Fig. 2a) revealed prominent O1s, N1s, and C1s photoelectron peaks for all samples at approximately 532 eV, 400 eV, and 285 eV, respectively. In the high-resolution N1s spectra (Fig. 2c), pure CS primarily exhibited a characteristic peak near 398.5 eV, which is attributed to the primary amine groups (-NH₂) of chitosan. In contrast, the grafted PNIPAAm-CS products showed a significant new peak near 400.1 eV, corresponding to the amide groups (-NH-C=O) on the PNIPAAm side chains.Fig. 2XPS spectra of chitosan and PNIPAAm-CS copolymers. XPS spectra of chitosan and PNIPAAm-CS copolymers. Note: (a) XPS survey spectra; (b) C1s spectra; (c) N1s spectra; (d) O1s spectra.

As the amount of NIPAM added during preparation increased from PNIPAAm-CS-1 to PNIPAAm-CS-4, the relative contribution of the amide nitrogen signal gradually increased, whereas the primary amine-related signal of the chitosan component showed a relative decrease. Together with the FTIR and NMR results, these changes are consistent with the progressively increased incorporation of PNIPAAm segments into the chitosan-based copolymer system. It should be noted that the XPS results reflect relative changes in the surface nitrogen-containing chemical environment and cannot, by themselves, quantitatively determine the amount or accessibility of residual primary amino groups. Therefore, these data are not interpreted as direct quantitative evidence for amino-group consumption in the antibacterial mechanism (Fadavi et al., 2025).

Comparing Fig. 3(a) with (b), pure CS exhibited no significant change, indicating thermal stability. In contrast, the PNIPAAm-CS copolymers displayed distinct, grafting-dependent behavior. At 25 °C (Figs. 3c, e, g, i), the apparent size of the particles increased with higher NIPAM grafting ratios, accompanied by more blurred boundaries. However, upon heating to 60 °C (Figs. 3d, f, h, j), the particles underwent significant contraction rather than crystal growth. Notably, as the grafting ratio increased from PNIPAAm-CS-1 to PNIPAAm-CS-4, the particles at 60 °C became increasingly compact and smaller in size, accompanied by localized aggregation. This trend provides strong evidence that a higher grafting density enhances the thermo-responsive contraction force, leading to a more efficient expulsion of water molecules from the polymer matrix above the LCST.

Furthermore, while the NIPAM monomer (Fig. 3l) precipitated into large, elongated crystalline structures at 60 °C, the PNIPAAm-CS copolymers maintained a spherical or quasi-spherical contracted morphology. This fundamental difference demonstrates that the covalent attachment of PNIPAAm to the CS backbone effectively modulates the phase transition behavior, preventing macroscopic crystallization and confirming its suitability as a functional unit for smart hydrogels (Yudi Li et al., 2025).

Physicochemical properties analysis

Determination of LCST for synthetic copolymer solutions

The Lower Critical Solution Temperature (LCST) of chitosan (CS) and the series of PNIPAAm-CS copolymer solutions was determined using the cloud point method. Fig. 4(a) illustrates the temperature-dependent absorbance trends for samples with different grafting ratios at the same concentration. The results indicate that the absorbance of the pure CS solution remained constant within the range of 26–36 °C, showing no phase transition behavior. In contrast, the PNIPAAm-CS series products all exhibited distinct thermo-responsive properties. Below the critical temperature, the solutions remained transparent with low absorbance. However, as the temperature rose to approximately 30 °C, the absorbance increased sharply, indicating a conformational transition of the PNIPAAm segments from a hydrophilic, extended state to a hydrophobic, collapsed state, which led to phase separation within the system. As the PNIPAAm grafting amount increased (from PNIPAAm-CS-1 to PNIPAAm-CS-4), the equilibrium absorbance after the phase transition significantly enhanced. This is attributed to the stronger light-scattering effect generated by the higher density of PNIPAAm segments during the phase transition.Fig. 4Determination of LCST for chitosan and PNIPAAm-CS copolymer solutions. Note: (a) Different products; (b) PNIPAAm-CS-4 prepared at a series of mass fraction gradients (0.06%, 0.12%, 0.18%, 0.24%, 0.30%, 0.36%, 0.42%, 0.48%, 0.54%, and 0.60%).Fig. 4

Determination of LCST for chitosan and PNIPAAm-CS copolymer solutions. Note: (a) Different products; (b) PNIPAAm-CS-4 prepared at a series of mass fraction gradients (0.06%, 0.12%, 0.18%, 0.24%, 0.30%, 0.36%, 0.42%, 0.48%, 0.54%, and 0.60%).

Fig. 4(b) further examines the influence of solution concentration on the phase transition behavior of the PNIPAAm-CS system. It can be observed that as the solution concentration increased from 0.06% to 0.60%, the onset temperature of the phase transition (LCST) demonstrated a clear concentration dependence, with the value gradually decreasing from 32 °C to 30 °C. This advance in LCST is primarily due to the increased collision probability between macromolecular chains at higher concentrations. This enhanced probability allows the hydrophobic interactions between segments to overcome thermal motion at a lower temperature, thereby accelerating the aggregation and precipitation of the polymer (Fadavi et al., 2025).

Antimicrobial activity study

The antimicrobial activities of chitosan (CS) and the series of PNIPAAm-CS copolymers were evaluated using E. coli and S. aureus as model organisms, with the results presented in Fig. 5. Experimental data revealed that pure CS exhibited the strongest antimicrobial effect, with inhibition rates against E. coli and S. aureus reaching 67.26% and 70.53%, respectively. This is primarily attributed to the protonation of the abundant primary amine groups on the CS molecular chains in acidic media. The resulting -NH3+ groups can bind to the negative charges on the bacterial cell wall surface through electrostatic attraction, thereby disrupting cell membrane integrity or interfering with normal physiological metabolism.Fig. 5Antibacterial activity of chitosan and PNIPAAm-CS copolymers. Note: (a) Antibacterial activity against E. coli; (b) Antibacterial activity against S. aureus. Different capital letters represent the statistical significance at p < 0.05.Fig. 5

Antibacterial activity of chitosan and PNIPAAm-CS copolymers. Note: (a) Antibacterial activity against E. coli; (b) Antibacterial activity against S. aureus. Different capital letters represent the statistical significance at p < 0.05.

In contrast, after PNIPAAm grafting, the antibacterial activity of the copolymers gradually decreased as the NIPAM feed ratio increased. From PNIPAAm-CS-1 to PNIPAAm-CS-4, the inhibition rate against E. coli decreased from 35.57% to 21.48%, while that against S. aureus decreased from 42.30% to 24.65%. These results indicate that increasing incorporation of PNIPAAm was accompanied by a progressive reduction in the antibacterial performance of the chitosan-based copolymers.

The antibacterial activity of chitosan is generally associated with the interaction between protonated amino groups and negatively charged bacterial cell surfaces under acidic conditions (Sahariah & Másson, 2017). In the present study, the N1s XPS spectra showed that PNIPAAm grafting altered the relative nitrogen-containing chemical environment of the copolymers, with an increased amide-related contribution and a relative decrease in the primary amine-related signal. However, because quantitative determination of accessible amino groups was not performed, the decrease in antibacterial activity cannot be conclusively attributed to the consumption of active amino groups. Instead, the observed reduction may be associated with changes in the accessibility and local chemical environment of protonatable amino sites after PNIPAAm incorporation. The coverage of the chitosan backbone by grafted PNIPAAm side chains may also contribute to reduced contact between the copolymer and bacterial surfaces; nevertheless, this possible shielding effect remains a proposed explanation rather than a directly demonstrated mechanism.

All copolymer samples showed slightly higher inhibitory activity against S. aureus than against E. coli, which may be related to differences between the cell-envelope structures of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Overall, these results reveal a trade-off between the introduction of thermo-responsive PNIPAAm segments and the retention of antibacterial activity in the copolymer system. Further quantitative analysis of accessible amino groups and antibacterial evaluation of the final hydrogel films will be required before application-oriented conclusions can be established (Huang et al., 2025).

From an application perspective, the reduction in antibacterial activity indicates a functional trade-off between thermo-responsive modification and the intrinsic antibacterial property of chitosan. For intelligent packaging applications, this decrease may limit the ability of the material to provide additional antimicrobial protection, especially when direct contact with food surfaces or high microbial loads is involved. Therefore, the present PNIPAAm-CS/SA films should be regarded primarily as temperature-responsive visual indicator candidates rather than validated antimicrobial packaging materials. To mitigate this trade-off, future material design could focus on optimizing the PNIPAAm grafting level, such as selecting a moderate grafting ratio that balances thermo-responsiveness and antibacterial retention, constructing chitosan-rich surface layers to preserve accessible protonatable amino groups, or incorporating food-compatible antibacterial agents into the film matrix. In addition, grafting strategies that reduce the shielding of active amino groups or introduce spacer segments may help maintain antibacterial functionality while retaining thermo-responsive behavior.

Microstructure and physical properties of PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite films

Surface morphology analysis

The micromorphology of the pure chitosan/sodium alginate (CS/SA) film and the series of PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite films was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Fig. 6(a) and 6(b) display the morphology of the pure CS/SA film; under high magnification (1000×), the surface exhibits a rough, fibrous texture with layered wrinkles. This is attributed to the dense and irregular cross-linking network resulting from the strong electrostatic attraction between the CS and SA molecular chains.Fig. 6SEM micrographs of CS/SA and PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite hydrogel films. Note: (a, b) CS/SA; (c, d) PNIPAAm-CS/SA-1; (e, f) PNIPAAm-CS/SA-2; (g, h) PNIPAAm-CS/SA-3; (i, j) PNIPAAm-CS/SA-4.Fig. 6

SEM micrographs of CS/SA and PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite hydrogel films. Note: (a, b) CS/SA; (c, d) PNIPAAm-CS/SA-1; (e, f) PNIPAAm-CS/SA-2; (g, h) PNIPAAm-CS/SA-3; (i, j) PNIPAAm-CS/SA-4.

The introduction of PNIPAAm-CS led to a significant transformation in the surface micro-features of the composite films. In PNIPAAm-CS/SA-1 (Fig. 6d), distinct and relatively large aggregated nodules are distributed across the matrix surface. As the PNIPAAm grafting amount gradually increased from PNIPAAm-CS/SA-2 (Fig. 6f) to PNIPAAm-CS/SA-4 (Fig. 6j), these aggregated nodules exhibited a trend of significant size reduction and tended toward a dense and uniform distribution. The underlying mechanism for this “nodule refinement” phenomenon is that increasing the grafting ratio raises the density of PNIPAAm segments within the system, providing more abundant hydrogen-bonding donor and acceptor sites. This enhances the intermolecular interactions with the SA matrix. Such improved interfacial compatibility effectively suppresses the large-scale self-aggregation of PNIPAAm segments, prompting the thermo-responsive components to anchor uniformly onto the polysaccharide backbone in finer domains (Xia et al., 2024).

This increasingly uniform and fine microscopic distribution significantly reduces the refraction and scattering of light at large-scale aggregate interfaces as it passes through the film. Macroscopically, this is manifested as a linear increase in light transmittance with higher grafting amounts. Meanwhile, the dense distribution of micro-nodules implies stronger interfacial bonding between components, allowing for more effective stress transfer. This explains why the composite films maintain excellent mechanical integrity and toughness while achieving high transparency. In summary, by adjusting the PNIPAAm ratio, the transition from a rough fibrous structure to a uniform micro-nano nodule structure was successfully achieved, laying the microstructural foundation for the film's superior optical and mechanical properties (Y. Zhang et al., 2025).

To provide a more objective comparison of the “nodule refinement” phenomenon, the sizes of representative nodular microdomains were estimated from the high-magnification SEM images using the scale bars. The measured microdomain size range decreased from 5.88 to 9.02 μm for PNIPAAm-CS/SA-1 to 4.27–7.10 μm for PNIPAAm-CS/SA-2, 2.10–4.50 μm for PNIPAAm-CS/SA-3, and 0.73–1.53 μm for PNIPAAm-CS/SA-4. Correspondingly, the average size decreased from 7.00 ± 1.11 μm to 5.78 ± 1.02 μm, 3.17 ± 0.84 μm, and 1.19 ± 0.27 μm, respectively. These semi-quantitative observations support the proposed nodule refinement phenomenon and indicate that increased PNIPAAm incorporation promoted a more homogeneous microstructural distribution within the CS/SA film matrix.

Mechanical properties analysis

The mechanical properties of the pure chitosan/sodium alginate (CS/SA) film and the series of PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite films were characterized using a texture analyzer, with the results shown in Fig. 7. Fig. 7(a) presents the stress-displacement curves of the samples, intuitively reflecting the deformation characteristics of the materials during the stretching process. Fig. 7(b) illustrates the trends in tensile strength and elongation at break calculated from the raw data.Fig. 7Mechanical properties of chitosan and PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite films. Note: (a) Stress-displacement curves; (b) Tensile strength and elongation at break. Different capital letters represent the statistical significance at p < 0.05.Fig. 7

Mechanical properties of chitosan and PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite films. Note: (a) Stress-displacement curves; (b) Tensile strength and elongation at break. Different capital letters represent the statistical significance at p < 0.05.

Experimental data indicate that the pure CS/SA film exhibited high brittleness, with a tensile strength of only 8.8 MPa and an elongation at break of approximately 28.2%. This behavior can be attributed to the relatively rigid polysaccharide network formed by CS/SA electrostatic interactions, in which the lack of sufficient flexible chain segments limits molecular-chain rearrangement during stretching.

After the introduction of PNIPAAm-CS, the elongation at break increased monotonically with increasing PNIPAAm incorporation, reaching 98.6% for PNIPAAm-CS/SA-4. This trend is closely related to the flexible PNIPAAm side chains and the grafting-induced amorphous structural transition revealed by SSNMR and XRD. As discussed above, SSNMR showed progressive broadening of the chitosan ring‑carbon signals, especially C1 and C4, while XRD showed weakened crystalline diffraction peaks and the transformation of the original CS crystalline features into a broader amorphous halo at high NIPAM feed ratios. These structural changes indicate that PNIPAAm grafting disrupted the ordered hydrogen-bonding domains of chitosan and increased the amorphous fraction of the network. The resulting more disordered and flexible structure provided greater free volume and chain mobility, thereby allowing more effective molecular-chain sliding and deformation under tensile stress.

In contrast to the monotonic increase in elongation at break, the tensile strength exhibited an inverted “U-shaped” trend. The tensile strength first increased and reached a maximum of 22.6 MPa for PNIPAAm-CS/SA-2. At this moderate grafting level, the introduced PNIPAAm segments could improve interfacial compatibility with the SA matrix, enhance physical entanglement, and provide additional hydrogen-bonding interactions, while the CS framework still retained sufficient structural integrity. Therefore, PNIPAAm-CS/SA-2 achieved an optimal balance between network reinforcement and chain flexibility (Gu et al., 2025).

However, when the PNIPAAm incorporation was further increased in PNIPAAm-CS/SA-3 and PNIPAAm-CS/SA-4, the tensile strength decreased. This decrease can be explained by excessive disruption of the original ordered CS domains, as supported by the more pronounced SSNMR peak broadening and XRD crystallinity reduction. Although the increased amorphous structure improved deformability, it also weakened the cohesive strength and load-bearing continuity of the polysaccharide network. Consequently, the films became more extensible but less resistant to tensile failure. Therefore, the mechanical results indicate that moderate PNIPAAm grafting strengthens the CS/SA network, whereas excessive grafting promotes amorphization and flexibility at the expense of tensile strength.

Apparent Thermo-responsive transition behavior of PNIPAAm-CS/SA precursor systems and hydrogel films

The apparent thermo-responsive transition behavior of the CS/SA control and the PNIPAAm-CS/SA composite systems was evaluated in both the precursor state and the hydrated film state using temperature-dependent optical measurements. The corresponding absorbance–temperature curves are shown in Fig. 8.Fig. 8Apparent thermo-responsive transition behavior of CS/SA and PNIPAAm-CS/SA precursor systems and hydrated hydrogel films. Note: (a) CS/SA and PNIPAAm-CS/SA precursor systems; (b) CS/SA and PNIPAAm-CS/SA hydrated hydrogel films.Fig. 8

Apparent thermo-responsive transition behavior of CS/SA and PNIPAAm-CS/SA precursor systems and hydrated hydrogel films. Note: (a) CS/SA and PNIPAAm-CS/SA precursor systems; (b) CS/SA and PNIPAAm-CS/SA hydrated hydrogel films.

As shown in Fig. 8(a), the CS/SA precursor system exhibited no obvious temperature-dependent optical transition within the tested range, indicating that the control system lacked detectable thermo-responsive behavior. In contrast, all PNIPAAm-containing precursor systems showed a marked increase in absorbance at approximately 31 °C. Moreover, the magnitude of the absorbance increase became more pronounced as the NIPAM feed ratio increased, particularly in PNIPAAm-CS/SA-4. These results indicate that the introduction of PNIPAAm segments endowed the precursor systems with measurable thermo-responsive behavior and that an increased PNIPAAm content enhanced the macroscopic optical signal associated with thermally induced aggregation.

After film formation, the hydrated PNIPAAm-CS/SA films still exhibited temperature-dependent optical changes, as shown in Fig. 8(b). Compared with the corresponding precursor systems, the apparent thermo-responsive transition onset of the hydrated films shifted slightly toward a higher temperature, from approximately 31 °C to approximately 32 °C. In addition, the amplitude of the absorbance change was reduced after film formation. For example, the equilibrium absorbance of PNIPAAm-CS/SA-4 decreased from approximately 2.3 in the precursor state to approximately 0.6 in the hydrated film state. These observations demonstrate that film-network formation altered the macroscopic thermo-responsive optical behavior of the PNIPAAm-containing system.

It should be emphasized that the optical method used in the present study primarily reflects changes in light scattering associated with thermally induced molecular association and structural rearrangement. Therefore, the approximately 1 °C shift observed after film formation cannot independently demonstrate a microscopic spatial confinement mechanism. Nevertheless, the difference between the precursor systems and the hydrated films indicates that the network environment affected the macroscopic thermo-responsive behavior of the PNIPAAm-containing system.

A possible explanation is that the CS/SA/Ca2+-associated ionic network imposes physical constraints on the grafted PNIPAAm segments. In the precursor systems, PNIPAAm side chains possess relatively higher conformational freedom and can undergo rapid dehydration, collapse, and hydrophobic association once the temperature approaches the transition region. In contrast, after film formation, electrostatic interactions between CS and SA, Ca2+-mediated alginate cross-linking, hydrogen bonding, and chain entanglement may restrict the mobility and free-volume contraction of PNIPAAm segments. These network constraints could increase the cooperative difficulty of PNIPAAm chain collapse and suppress large-scale aggregation, thereby leading to a slightly higher apparent transition onset and a reduced absorbance change in the hydrated films.

Accordingly, the observed optical transition shift is interpreted as being consistent with a network-constrained thermo-responsive rearrangement of grafted PNIPAAm segments, rather than as direct proof of a fully established microscopic confinement mechanism. Further rheological, dynamic light scattering, or molecular-scale characterization would be required to quantitatively clarify the contribution of this network-related restriction effect (Syed et al., 2026).

In addition, transition stability and reversibility were not evaluated in the present study because cyclic heating–cooling and hysteresis measurements were not performed. Therefore, the revised manuscript does not claim enhanced transition stability, and further cyclic response tests will be required to assess the reversible stability of the hydrated films.

Conclusion

In this study, PNIPAAm-grafted chitosan/sodium alginate composite hydrogel films were constructed through radical polymerization and ionic network formation. FTIR, XPS, and liquid-state NMR confirmed the successful incorporation of PNIPAAm segments into the chitosan-based system, while SSNMR provided further insight into the solid-state microenvironment of the polysaccharide backbone. In particular, SSNMR revealed progressive broadening and weakening of the chitosan ring‑carbon signals, especially C1 and C4, indicating that PNIPAAm grafting disrupted the ordered local arrangement of chitosan chains. Combined with XRD evidence of weakened crystalline diffraction peaks and amorphous halo formation, these results demonstrate a grafting-induced amorphous structural transition. Compared with FTIR, which mainly verifies functional-group changes, and XRD, which reflects long-range crystalline order, SSNMR offers molecular-level information on local solid-state structural evolution, thereby deepening the understanding of the structure–property relationship in this grafted polysaccharide film system. The PNIPAAm-CS copolymer solutions exhibited LCST values of 30–32 °C, while the hydrated films showed an apparent thermo-responsive transition onset approximately 1 °C higher than that of the corresponding precursor systems. The composite films also displayed improved transparency and mechanical performance, with PNIPAAm-CS/SA-4 reaching an average transmittance of 82.81% and PNIPAAm-CS/SA-2 achieving the highest tensile strength of 22.6 MPa. Overall, this work clarifies the relationship among PNIPAAm grafting, amorphous structural evolution, and film performance, providing a basis for the further development of thermo-responsive polysaccharide-based films as candidate visual temperature indicators for intelligent packaging.
