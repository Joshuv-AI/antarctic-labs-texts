Tracheal schwannoma presenting as pneumonia: A rare case report in an adolescent
Authors: Wanyan Xiong, Zhixuan Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTracheal schwannoma is a rare benign tumor originating from Schwann cells, and it is even less common in adolescents. The clinical manifestations are usually nonspecific respiratory symptoms such as cough and sputum production, which can easily be mistaken for infectious diseases. Imaging findings often suggest an inflammatory mass, making early diagnosis challenging.Case presentationA 15-year-old female was admitted with a 3-day history of cough and sputum production and was initially diagnosed with community-acquired pneumonia. High-resolution chest CT and 3D reconstruction revealed a well-defined, mildly to moderately enhanced round nodule (approximately 1.1 × 0.7 cm) in the lower trachea, accompanied by bronchopneumonia. Imaging findings suggested an inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor or inflammatory granuloma. The lesion was completely resected under painless bronchoscopy. Histopathology showed spindle cells arranged in bundles, with strong positive staining for S-100 and SOX-10 and a Ki-67 proliferation index of about 5%, confirming the diagnosis of benign tracheal schwannoma. After postoperative anti-infective therapy, the patient's symptoms rapidly resolved.ConclusionAlthough tracheal schwannoma is benign, it may initially present as pneumonia and mimic an inflammatory tumor on imaging, leading to diagnostic confusion. For adolescent patients with recurrent respiratory symptoms and poor response to conventional therapy, early chest CT and bronchoscopy should be considered. Bronchoscopic resection combined with anti-infective therapy can achieve excellent outcomes.

Introduction

Primary tracheal tumors are rare entities, accounting for only 0.1–0.4% of all respiratory tract neoplasms, and approximately two-thirds of them are malignant, such as squamous cell carcinoma and adenoid cystic carcinoma [1,2]. Benign tracheal tumors are less common and include papilloma, hemangioma, hamartoma, and neurogenic tumors such as schwannoma [3,4]. Tracheal schwannoma is a benign neoplasm arising from Schwann cells of the peripheral nerve sheath. Although schwannomas can develop in multiple locations including the head and neck, mediastinum, and gastrointestinal tract [[5], [6], [7]], primary tracheal schwannoma is exceedingly rare, representing less than 0.5% of all tracheal tumors reported worldwide [8,9]. Most cases occur in middle-aged adults, while pediatric or adolescent cases are exceptionally uncommon [10,11].

Clinical manifestations are often nonspecific (e.g., chronic cough, sputum production, or dyspnea) and may be mistaken for bronchitis, asthma, or pneumonia [9,12,13]. When airway obstruction is mild, radiological findings can be subtle; therefore, tracheal schwannoma may be misdiagnosed as community-acquired pneumonia or inflammatory airway disease, delaying diagnosis and management [11,14]. In adolescents, this diagnostic challenge may be greater because symptoms are more often attributed to infection than to neoplastic causes [10,11].

High-resolution computed tomography (CT) with three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction is useful for detecting intratracheal lesions, but imaging features are not specific. CT typically shows a well-circumscribed intraluminal soft-tissue nodule with mild to moderate enhancement, which can overlap with inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors or granulomatous lesions [15,16]. Thus, definitive diagnosis relies on bronchoscopy and histopathological evaluation [10,17]. Bronchoscopically, these tumors often appear as smooth, polypoid or sessile lesions with intact mucosa [14,18].Histology commonly demonstrates Antoni A and Antoni B areas [19,20],and immunohistochemical positivity for S-100 and SOX-10 supports Schwann cell origin, while epithelial and myogenic markers such as epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), cytokeratin (CK), smooth muscle actin (SMA), and desmin (DES) and Desmin are typically negative [20].

For lesions confined to the tracheal lumen with a narrow stalk, bronchoscopic excision has become the preferred therapeutic approach due to its minimally invasive nature and excellent outcomes [18,21,22]. Endoscopic techniques including laser ablation, electrocautery snare, and cryoablation have all been successfully employed to achieve complete resection and airway patency restoration [18,23]. The recurrence rate after complete removal is extremely low, and the overall prognosis is favorable [22,24].

Here, we present a rare case of tracheal schwannoma in an adolescent female, whose initial manifestation mimicked community-acquired pneumonia. The lesion was incidentally detected by CT and successfully resected under painless bronchoscopy. This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of tracheal schwannoma presenting as a pneumonia-like illness and emphasizes the importance of early bronchoscopy and histopathological confirmation in adolescents with atypical or refractory respiratory symptoms.

Case presentation

A 15-year-old female was admitted to our hospital on May 19, 2024, with a 3-day history of recurrent cough and sputum production.Written informed consent for publication of this case report and any accompanying images was obtained from the patient and her parent/legal guardian. The onset was spontaneous without any obvious precipitating factors. The cough was paroxysmal, producing a small amount of yellow, viscous sputum. She denied chills, fever, chest pain, or dyspnea. For 2 days before admission, she had been treated at a local clinic with oral antibiotics for a presumed upper respiratory tract infection, but her symptoms did not improve. One day before admission, the cough worsened and was occasionally accompanied by mild shortness of breath, but no wheezing was noted.

The patient had been previously healthy with no history of asthma, chronic bronchitis, tuberculosis, or other chronic respiratory diseases. She had no history of surgery or trauma, and no family history of tumors or hereditary disorders. Immunizations were up to date, and her growth and development were normal. There was no history of recent exposure to specific medications or toxins.

Discussion

Tracheal schwannoma is an extremely rare benign neurogenic tumor arising from Schwann cells of the peripheral nerve sheath, accounting for only 0.2%–0.5% of all primary tracheal tumors [13,22]. Most previously reported cases have occurred in adults, typically around 40 years of age, with a distinct female predominance. Reports in adolescents are exceedingly uncommon, making this case of a 15-year-old female particularly notable. Such cases are of clinical importance because the tumor's nonspecific manifestations mimic more common respiratory diseases, leading to delayed diagnosis or misdiagnosis.

Diagnostic challenges and clinical presentation

Although tracheal schwannoma is most often reported in adults, pediatric and adolescent cases are rare. The pathogenesis is generally considered sporadic, and no specific precipitating factor for adolescent onset has been established. Early presentation in younger patients may reflect a long-standing, slow-growing intraluminal lesion that becomes clinically apparent once airway narrowing reaches a critical threshold, sometimes in the setting of concomitant airway inflammation or infection that unmasks symptoms. In early-onset cases, syndromic conditions such as neurofibromatosis type 2 or schwannomatosis should be considered, although our patient had no personal or family history suggestive of these disorders. Recent case reports, including Kadriyan et al. (2025), further support that intraluminal tracheal schwannoma can cause significant airway obstruction and may be easily misattributed to more common respiratory conditions [25].

The clinical features of tracheal schwannoma largely depend on the size, location, and degree of airway obstruction [11]. Early-stage lesions tend to produce mild or nonspecific symptoms such as dry cough or slight dyspnea, which can easily be mistaken for upper respiratory tract infection or bronchitis. As the tumor enlarges and causes more than 50% luminal narrowing, patients may develop cough with sputum, dyspnea, or even secondary obstructive pneumonia due to impaired ventilation [22]. In this case, the patient presented with a 1-cm intraluminal mass in the lower trachea and concurrent bronchopneumonia, manifesting as persistent cough and sputum production without typical wheezing. The absence of wheezing or other clinical signs suggestive of central airway obstruction, together with inflammatory findings on imaging, led to an initial misdiagnosis of community-acquired pneumonia, delaying recognition of the underlying airway lesion.Similar diagnostic pitfalls have been described previously, with nearly half of benign tracheal tumor cases initially mistaken for asthma, pneumonia, or other chronic airway diseases [22]. This emphasizes the need for heightened clinical suspicion and timely use of high-resolution CT and bronchoscopy in patients, especially adolescents, presenting with refractory or recurrent respiratory symptoms unresponsive to standard therapy.

In addition, asthma represents one of the most common diagnostic confusions for intrathoracic tracheal and other large-airway tumors, especially in younger patients. Asthma-like symptoms (cough, episodic dyspnea, and wheeze) that are atypical, progressive, or refractory to bronchodilators and inhaled corticosteroids should raise suspicion for central airway obstruction and prompt further evaluation with chest CT and bronchoscopy.

Radiologic-pathologic correlation and diagnostic significance

Radiologic assessment plays a central role in the initial evaluation of airway lesions; however, the imaging appearance of tracheal schwannoma lacks specificity. In this case, CT and 3D reconstruction demonstrated a smooth, round, submucosal mass with sharp demarcation from the tracheal wall, a non-contrast attenuation of approximately 36 HU, and mild-to-moderate homogeneous enhancement on contrast imaging. These findings suggested a benign, slow-growing process, but the differential diagnosis remained broad, including inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) and inflammatory granuloma.

This radiologic ambiguity represents a common diagnostic challenge. IMT, although rare in the trachea, is a low-grade neoplasm composed of spindle-shaped myofibroblasts and inflammatory infiltrates. It frequently presents with enhancing, well-defined masses similar to schwannomas and often occurs in young patients, further compounding confusion [26]. However, IMT can occasionally exhibit invasive margins, heterogeneous enhancement, or calcifications, features less typical of schwannomas. Immunohistochemically, IMT is characterized by ALK gene rearrangement or ALK protein positivity, a marker that is absent in schwannomas. In contrast, tracheal schwannomas are defined histologically by alternating Antoni A and Antoni B regions, nuclear palisading (Verocay bodies), and diffuse positivity for S-100 and SOX-10, with negative staining for epithelial (CK, EMA) and smooth muscle markers (SMA, Desmin).

Thus, radiologic–pathologic correlation is indispensable for accurate diagnosis. Imaging can delineate lesion morphology, extent, and airway involvement, but definitive diagnosis depends on histopathological and immunophenotypic confirmation. In our patient, biopsy revealed typical spindle-cell morphology with Antoni A/B zonation and strong expression of S-100, SOX-10, and Vimentin, along with a low Ki-67 index (∼5%), consistent with a benign schwannoma. Notably, SOX-10, a nuclear transcription factor of neural crest origin, has emerged as a highly specific and sensitive marker for schwannian differentiation, complementing S-100 staining when the latter shows heterogeneous expression. Such combined immunoprofiles help distinguish schwannomas from IMT and other spindle-cell tumors of the airway, including leiomyoma, fibroma, and neurofibroma.

The importance of distinguishing schwannoma from inflammatory mimickers cannot be overstated. Misinterpretation of imaging findings as pneumonia or IMT may lead to prolonged antibiotic use, delayed intervention, or even unnecessary systemic therapy. Conversely, overestimating the malignant potential can result in overly aggressive surgical management. Therefore, awareness of this entity and a multidisciplinary diagnostic approach integrating radiology, bronchoscopy, and pathology are crucial for optimizing patient outcomes.

Therapeutic approach and prognosis

For benign tracheal tumors that are small, well-circumscribed, and confined to the mucosal or submucosal layer, bronchoscopic resection is now widely regarded as the treatment of choice [5,17,18]. Endoscopic techniques—including electrocautery snare excision, laser ablation, cryotherapy, or argon plasma coagulation—allow precise removal with minimal trauma and rapid restoration of airway patency. Studies have shown that complete bronchoscopic resection achieves excellent local control when the lesion diameter is less than 2 cm and the base is narrow [6].

Nevertheless, careful follow-up is essential, as 10–20% of patients may experience local recurrence after endoscopic therapy [27]. For tumors with broad attachment, extraluminal extension, or incomplete endoscopic removal, segmental tracheal resection with primary reconstruction remains the definitive option [7]. In our patient, the tumor was completely excised bronchoscopically, with no recurrence observed at six months. The combination of minimally invasive resection and perioperative anti-infective therapy resulted in complete clinical and radiological resolution.

Clinical implications

This case highlights several important lessons for clinical practice.First, tracheal schwannoma should be included in the differential diagnosis of persistent or recurrent respiratory symptoms, particularly in adolescents unresponsive to conventional therapy.Second, radiologic findings alone are insufficient to differentiate schwannoma from inflammatory or mesenchymal tumors, underscoring the diagnostic value of bronchoscopic evaluation and histopathologic confirmation.Third, for suitable patients, bronchoscopic complete resection provides a safe, minimally invasive, and curative treatment option, avoiding the morbidity associated with open surgery.Finally, long-term surveillance remains essential to detect potential recurrence early and ensure durable remission.

Based on the diagnostic challenges illustrated by this case, we propose a practical diagnostic algorithm for adolescents with persistent or recurrent respiratory symptoms, emphasizing clinical red flags that warrant advanced imaging and timely bronchoscopy (Fig. 4).In summary, tracheal schwannoma, though benign, can mimic pneumonia or other inflammatory lesions both clinically and radiologically.
