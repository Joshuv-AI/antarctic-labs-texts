Lachnospiraceae‐Derived Extracellular Vesicles Mediate the Cardioprotective Effects of Barley Leaf in Myocardial Infarction by Improving Intestinal Stem Cell Function
Authors: Wenjing Chen, Yifan Zhao, Qian Zhao, Yingzhuo Zhou, Chen Ma, Li Dong, Yinghua Luo, Zhong Zhang, Fang Chen, Xiaosong Hu, Daotong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIschaemic cardiovascular diseases, particularly myocardial infarction (MI), remain the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Targeting extracellular vesicles (EVs) from the gut microbiota by diet may provide opportunities to improve cardiovascular health. Barley leaf (BL) has a long history of use in Traditional Chinese medicine and has been found to beneficially influence the gut microbial composition. Herein, we used a murine model of MI to explore the mechanistic role of gut bacteria‐derived EVs in the cardioprotective effects of BL. Dietary supplementation of BL remarkably improved cardiac function and ameliorated adverse remodelling in experimental MI. The cardioprotective effects of BL were linked to enhanced gut epithelial barrier and suppressed transfer of bacterial‐derived lipopolysaccharide. Moreover, BL alleviated MI‐induced gut microbial dysbiosis, with an enrichment of Lachnospiraceae. Gut microbiota depletion by antibiotic treatment abolished the cardioprotective effects of BL. Furthermore, mice receiving microbiota from BL‐fed mice had better cardiac outcomes after MI compared to mice receiving microbiota from mice without BL supplementation. Notably, we identified that BL increased the abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, a commensal member of the family Lachnospiraceae. Supplementing antibiotic‐treated mice with live but not heat‐inactivated Lachnospiraceae ameliorated myocardial injury and cardiac remodelling in MI mice. We isolated EVs from Lachnospiraceae and demonstrated that Lachnospiraceae‐derived EVs (L‐EVs) achieved desirable biosafety, stability and colonic retention effects following oral administration. Mechanistically, estrogen‐like metabolites from L‐EVs modulated the estrogen receptor alpha (ERα)‐solute carrier family 6 member 14 (Slc6a14)‐Hippo signalling pathway to promote intestinal stem cell function and ultimately protected against MI‐induced adverse remodelling. Our study thus provides novel insights into the role of the microbiota–gut–heart axis in the pathophysiology of MI and underscores the great potential of gut bacteria‐derived EVs to reduce pathological outcomes after MI through improving gut health.Keywords: barley leaf, cardiac remodelling, extracellular vesicles, gut microbiota, Lachnospiraceae, myocardial infarction

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are a group of diseases that damage the heart and blood vessels. They are a major public health concern with high morbidity and mortality worldwide. Myocardial infarction (MI), a common presentation for ischaemic heart disease/coronary artery disease, is a leading cause of death among all CVDs (Thygesen et al. 2018). According to the data from the American Heart Association (AHA), about 800,000 people in the United States experience MI (Benjamin et al. 2017). MI can occur due to the formation of plaques in the arteries resulting in reduced blood flow to the heart and injuring cardiomyocytes, which is accompanied by the initiation of inflammatory response and scar formation in the infarct area (Sahoo and Losordo 2014; Lu et al. 2015). Due to the lack of oxygen supply, myocytes undergo adverse structural and functional remodelling leading to compromised cardiac function (Borden et al. 2019). Although the improved clinical management of MI including anticoagulation, antiplatelet, thrombolysis and reperfusion therapies, has resulted in a substantial reduction in mortality, the post‐infarction cardiac remodelling and heart failure still cause a considerable health‐care cost and societal burden (Rienks et al. 2017). Hence, the development of novel and effective approaches to reduce cardiac outcomes following MI is still in urgent need.

The pathophysiology of MI is multifactorial and influenced by complex interactions between genetic and environmental factors. It is now well recognised that the gut microbiota plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of CVDs, including MI (Khuu et al. 2024). Early studies have showed that alteration of the gut microbiome and metabolites derived from the intestinal microbiota has been linked to the severity of MI in rats (Lam et al. 2012; Lam et al. 2016). Compared to healthy controls, patients with MI displayed a higher microbial richness and diversity in the systemic microbiome (Zhou et al. 2018). Moreover, a unique feature of gut microbiota combined with metabolites is observed in patients with MI (Dong et al. 2023). Mechanistically, the disrupted intestinal epithelial barrier following MI leads to increased permeability and the transfer of microbiota‐derived endotoxins lipopolysaccharides (LPS) into systemic circulation, which activate the immune‐inflammatory system and contribute to increased risk of cardiovascular events (Zhou et al. 2018; Carnevale et al. 2020). However, abrogation of gut bacterial translocation by antibiotics effectively alleviates cardiovascular outcomes after MI (Zhou et al. 2018). Furthermore, depletion of short‐chain fatty acid‐producing bacteria has been demonstrated to impair myocardial repair after MI (Tang et al. 2019). These studies suggest that the gut microbiota may be a key target for the prevention and treatment of CVDs.

Oral administration of probiotics has been reported to alleviate myocardial hypertrophy, heart failure and depression‐like behaviour following MI (Gan et al. 2014). Nevertheless, utilising live bacteria may lead to potential risks such as antibiotic resistance and systemic infections in vulnerable populations (Kothari et al. 2019). Recently, emerging evidence has highlighted the significant potential of bacterial extracellular vesicles (EVs) as safe alternatives to live bacteria for disease intervention (Xie et al. 2023). By encapsulating a diverse set of bioactive molecules in membrane‐bound particles, EVs secreted by bacteria serve as crucial mediators of transkingdom communication between microorganisms and their hosts (Xie et al. 2023). However, the mechanisms by which gut bacteria‐derived EVs affect MI pathogenesis, particularly their role in microbiota–gut–heart axis remains poorly understood. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms may open new avenues for the attenuation of post‐MI adverse events.

Poor dietary habits account for 52% of all CVD deaths worldwide according to the Global Burden of Disease Study (Collaborators 2019). Recent research has focused on the impact of diet‐dependent EVs from the gut microbiota on the host health (Teng et al. 2025). Understanding the interplay between diet, gut microbiota and CVDs such as MI is crucial for developing effective strategies to prevent cardiometabolic dysfunction. Barley leaf (BL), the young grass of barley (Hordeum vulgare L.), is historically recorded as an herbal‐based component of Traditional Chinese medicine with potential nutritional value. Animal and human studies have revealed its various health‐promoting properties, such as antioxidative, antidepressant, hypolipidemic and neuroprotective activities (Yu et al. 2004; Yamaura et al. 2012). Our recent studies have demonstrated that BL protects against colitis‐induced gut microbiota dysbiosis (Li et al. 2018; Li et al. 2021). However, it remains unknown whether BL could improve cardiac function and attenuate adverse remodelling post‐MI, and whether this effect is related to the modulation of gut flora.

In this study, we provided evidence that dietary supplementation of BL could protect heart from ischaemic injury and prevent MI‐induced intestinal barrier dysfunction and microbiota dysbiosis. Using antibiotic treatment and faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), we demonstrated that the gut microbiota plays an essential role in the cardioprotective effects of BL. We identified that BL significantly enriched a commensal gut species Lachnospiraceae, which can improve intestinal barrier function, prevent low‐grade endotoxemia, and ultimately ameliorate myocardial injury and improve cardiac function. We uncovered that the beneficial effects of Lachnospiraceae on the gut health and MI are largely due to its secreted nanosized EVs. Mechanistically, the EVs released from Lachnospiraceae could enhance intestinal stem cell‐mediated epithelial barrier integrity via activation of the oestrogen receptor alpha (ERα)‐solute carrier family 6 member 14 (Slc6a14)‐Hippo signalling pathway. This study highlights the potential of BL and gut bacteria‐derived EVs for alleviating post‐infarction cardiovascular events in patients with MI.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of BL Powder

In brief, fresh leaves of H. vulgare L., grown in Hangzhou, China, were cut into pieces and subjected to freeze‐drying in an OE‐950 freeze‐dryer (Labor MIM, Budapest, Hungary) at −60°C for 24 h. After drying, the BL was pulverised for 1 min with a blender (KA‐2610, Jworld Tech, Korea). The powder was then passed through a 300‐mesh sieve to ensure uniform particle size and stored at −20°C until required.

Animals

Male wild‐type C57BL/6J mice aged 8–10 weeks were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). All mice were maintained on specific‐pathogen‐free (SPF) facility with a 12‐h light/dark cycle, an ambient temperature ranging from 20 to 25°C, and 50%–70% humidity. All mice were provided with unrestricted access to food and water for at least 1 week. Animal experiments protocol was in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Committee on the Ethics of China Agriculture University (AW30112202‐4‐1).

Mice were randomly distributed and fed with either a standard chow diet (CD) or an isocaloric diet supplemented with BL at a ratio of 2.5%. The BL dosage used in this study is a human equivalent dose that has been reported without adverse effects (Yu et al. 2004). The two diets were provided by Hfk Biotech Co., Ltd (Beijing, China), and the composition was listed in (Table S1).

In the experiment of antibiotic treatment, a combination of neomycin (100 mg/L), streptomycin (50 mg/L), penicillin (100 mg/L), vancomycin (50 mg/L) and metronidazole (100 mg/L) was administered via the drinking water for 3 weeks.

FMT was performed as described (Huang et al. 2023). Briefly, the faecal samples (100 mg per mice) were collected under sterile conditions and resuspended with pre‐cooled phosphate buffered saline (PBS). The supernatant was centrifuged with 1000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C, and this step was repeated twice. Then the microbiota was stored at −80°C with glycerin (20%). The mice drank antibiotic cocktail for 3 weeks and were then transplanted with faecal microbiota by gavage.

For bacterial colonisation, Lachnospiraceae (DSM 24430, DSMZ) were cultured anaerobically (80% N2, 10% CO2 and 10% H2) at 37°C in brain–heart infusion (BHI) broth supplemented with 5% foetal bovine serum (FBS), 0.01% L‐cysteine, 1% cornstarch and 0.5% konjac flour. Cultures were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min and resuspended in PBS at a final concentration of 5 × 109 CFU/mL. It was placed on ice before administering to each mouse one time per 3 days for 4 weeks by gavage.

For Lachnospiraceae‐derived EVs (L‐EVs) intervention, each mouse was orally administered with L‐EVs (100 µg/mice) per 3 days for 3 weeks.

Mouse Model of MI

The MI mouse model was induced by permanent ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery, as previously described (Schloss et al. 2016). Briefly, mice were lightly anaesthetised with 1.0%–1.5% isoflurane gas in a chamber. The hearts were exposed by left thoracotomy, and the left main descending coronary artery was tied by a 7–0 silk suture. After the hearts were repositioned in the intrathoracic space, rapid evacuation of air was performed and the chest wall was promptly closed. Sham‐operated mice underwent the same surgery except for tying the left anterior descending coronary artery.

16S rRNA Gene Sequencing

At the endpoint of the experiments, the mice faeces (100 mg per mice) were collected under sterile conditions and stored at −80°C. The total genomic DNA was isolated from faeces samples using a QIAamp‐DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), according to manufacturer's instructions. The detection of extracted genomic DNA was examined by electrophoresis using 1% agarose gel. The V3–V4 region of bacterial 16S rRNA genes was amplified by an ABI GeneAmp&reg 9700 PCR thermocycler. PCR products were extracted from 2% agarose gels and purified with AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, USA). PCR amplification products were quantified using a QuantiFluor‐ST fluorescent quantitative system (Promega, USA).

Purified amplicons were constructed, and paired‐end sequencing was performed on an Illumina Miseq platform at Majorbio Bio‐Pharm Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The 16S rRNA gene sequencing data were then clustered at 97% similarity (Uparse, version 7.0.1090), with species classification determined by the sequence composition of operational taxonomic units (OTUs). A representative set of sequences was generated and subsequently assigned taxonomic classification using the SILVA database (Release138 http://www.arb‐silva.de).

Echocardiographic Measurements

Transthoracic echocardiography was conducted using a VEVO2100 system (VisualSonics Inc, Toronto, Ontario, Canada) to determine cardiac function. LVIDs, LVIDd, LVEF and LVFS were obtained from the M‐mode echocardiogram which was recorded at papillary muscle level. Male mice were anaesthetised with isoflurane (1.5%–1% in air) inhalation in a chamber and measured at least three times.

Triphenyl Tetrazolium Chloride (TTC) Staining

To measure the area at risk and infarct size, the hearts were excised and stained with Evans blue and TTC 7 days after MI. Briefly, mice were anaesthetised by 1%–2% isoflurane. 1 mL of 1.0% Evans blue (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was injected into the left ventricular (LV) cavity. The heart was then excised, frozen for 30 min and sliced into 1‐mm thick sections. The sections were then incubated in 1% TTC solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and digitally photographed.

Histological Analysis

Heart and colon tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 µm) were cut. Masson trichrome staining was completed to evaluate cardiac fibrosis and infarct size, and infarct size was expressed as the ratio of fibrosis area (blue staining) to the left ventricle area using 20× objective.

For colon sections, tissues stained with haematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining and alcian blue (AB) staining using commercial kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to standard procedures. The sections were observed using a light microscope (Leica DM500). Histological pathology was scored as previously described: crypt damage (0–4 scale), severity of inflammation (0–3 scale) and depth of injury (0–3 scale) (Rachmilewitz et al. 2002). Six randomly chosen high‐power fields of AB staining were collected and analysed for each section. Quantifications were measured with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

TUNEL Staining

TUNEL staining was performed with the TUNEL kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. Tissue sections were counterstained with DAPI. Image analysis was performed with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

Immunofluorescence and Immunohistochemistry

Tissues were fixed for 48 h in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 5‐mm thickness. Hearts and colonic sections were blocked nonspecific binding with 5% bovine serum albumin for 30 min. Then tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies for CD31 or Collagen III overnight at 4°C and incubated with secondary antibodies for 30 min at 37°C. Afterwards, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h. For colonic organoids, colonic organoids in Matrigel underwent 30‐min fixation using 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature, then isolated from Cell Recovery Solution (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). The organoids were then incubated with primary antibodies to Ki67, ZO‐1, Slc6a14, ERα or YAP/TAZ for 3 h and 1‐h treatment with fluorescent dye‐labelled secondary antibodies.

Immunohistochemistry staining was performed using mouse‐ and rabbit‐specific HRP/DAB detection IHC kits (Abcam; Cambridge, UK). Briefly, paraffin‐embedded sections were incubated with first antibodies overnight at 4°C, and secondary antibody was used on the following day. Antibodies against Ly6G, Claudin‐3 and ZO‐1 were all purchased from Abcam. Quantifications were measured with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

LPS Assay

The concentrations of LPS in plasma were measured using the Mouse LPS ELISA Test Kit (MEIMIAN, MM‐0634M1) according to manufacturer's instructions.

L‐EVs Isolation and Identification

The isolation of EVs from Lachnospiraceae culture supernatant was performed by ultracentrifugation as described (Hu et al. 2021). The bacterial culture solution was pelleted at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C, and the obtained supernatants were passed through a 0.22 µm filter (Millipore, Germany) to remove any bacterial debris from the medium. Subsequently, the supernatant was concentrated using 100 kDa ultrafiltration membranes (Millipore, Germany), ultracentrifuged at 150,000 × g at 4°C for 2 h. For purification, the EV pellets were washed with PBS and then purified by density gradient centrifugation using Optiprep (Sigma–Aldrich; Shanghai, China). Briefly, a discontinuous Optiprep with concentrations ranging from 10% to 55% (w/v) was prepared in advance, and the resuspended EVs were gently layered onto the top of the gradient to minimise interfacial mixing. The ultracentrifugation process was performed at 180,000 × g for 16 h at 4°C. After ultracentrifugation, equal‐volume fractions (1 mL each) were collected from the top to the bottom. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was performed to determine particle numbers, and vesicle‐enriched fractions were pooled. To remove residual Optiprep, EVs‐enriched fractions were diluted in PBS and pelleted by a second round of ultracentrifugation (150,000 × g, 2 h, 4°C). Purified EVs were resuspended in sterile PBS and passed through 0.22 µm filter for sterilisation. The samples were stored at −80°C until use. The reproducibility across multiple preparations were assessed by comparing NTA profiles for particle yield and size distribution together with protein concentration, and were further supported by representative transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging. The extracted EVs were used only when the data of NTA, protein concentration and TEM imaging were consistent.

Electron Microscopy and NTA

Morphological characteristics of Lachnospiraceae and EVs were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with a Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (SU8100, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The morphology of the EVs was ascertained through the utilisation of TEM (HT7700, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), while the size distribution and number were determined by NTA.

Proteomic Analysis of L‐EVs

The EVs samples were reconstituted in 10 µL lysis buffer, centrifuged (14,000 × g, 20 min, 4°C) and 200 ng of the cleared supernatant was subjected to LC‐MS analysis. Using a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system equipped with a C18 pre‐column of 174500 (5 mm × 300 µm, 5 µm, Thermo) maintained at 50°C and an analytical column (ES906 PepMap Neo UHPLC 150 µm × 15 cm, 2 µm, Thermo). Mass spectrometry was performed on a Thermo Orbitrap Astral instrument with Easy‐Spray (ESI) ionisation (2.0 kV spray voltage, 290°C transfer tube). Data‐independent acquisition parameters included: MS1 scan range m/z 380–980 at 240,000 resolution (200 m/z), 500% AGC target, 2 Th parent ionwindow, 300 DIA windows and 25% NCE. MS2 m/z acquisition range 150–2000, Astral mass analyser was set to 80,000. Raw mass spectrometry data were acquired for subsequent analysis.

Database search parameters were configured with 10 ppm precursor ions mass tolerance and 0.02 Da fragment ions. Modifications included cysteine alkylation, methionine oxidation, N‐terminal modifications included acetylation, and loss of methionine, and loss of methionine + acetylation. Up to two missed cleavage sites were permitted. DIA‐NN software filtered results using ≥99% confidence peptide spectrum matches (PSMs), followed by 1% FDR validation at both peptide and protein levels. Analysis involved: (1) GO annotation through interproscan against integrated databases (Pfam, PRINTS, etc.); (2) The protein family and pathway via KEGG. Differentially expressed proteins underwent Volcano plot analysis, hierarchical clustering and multi‐level enrichment assessments (GO, KEGG).

Metabolomic Analysis of L‐EVs

Metabolite extraction was performed by resuspending samples (100 µL) in prechilled 80% methanol, vortexing well and incubating on ice for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C. A portion of the supernatant was diluted to a final concentration of 53% methanol with LC‐MS grade water and centrifuged again at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C. The final supernatant was analysed using a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled with an Orbitrap Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Novogene Co., Ltd.). Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Hypersil Gold column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 µm) using a 12‐min linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min, with 0.1% formic acid in water and methanol as eluents. The mass spectrometer operated in positive/negative polarity modes with a spray voltage of 3.5 kV, capillary temperature of 320°C and sheath gas flow rate of 35 psi. Data files were processed by XCMS for peak alignment and quantitation. Metabolites were identified by comparing features against the NovoMetDB database with a mass deviation of 10 ppm, and compounds with a coefficient of variation (CV) greater than 30% in QC samples were removed. Finally, metabolites were annotated using the KEGG, HMDB and LIPIDMaps databases.

Stability of L‐EVs

Simulated gastric fluid was prepared by combining HCl (18.5% w/v, pH 2.0), pepsin solution (80 mg/mL in 0.1 N HCl, pH 2.0) and pancreatin (4 mg/mL). To prepare simulated intestinal fluid, the pH of the simulated gastric fluid was adjusted to approximately 6.5 using 1 N NaHCO3.

To evaluate the stability of L‐EVs following oral administration, the vesicles were incubated in simulated gastric fluid at 37°C for 2 h, followed by incubation in simulated intestinal fluid at 37°C for an additional 2 h. The stability of L‐EVs was evaluated by TEM using the methods described earlier.

Hemocompatibility of L‐EVs

Haemolysis assay was performed to investigate the haemocompatibility of L‐EVs. Fresh whole‐blood was collected from mice and washed three times with PBS by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 20 min. The resulting red blood cells were incubated with different concentrations of L‐EVs at 37°C for 2 h, followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min. For controls, red blood cells were incubated with ultrapure water (positive control) or PBS (negative control). After incubation, the supernatants were photographed, and the absorbance was measured at 576 nm. The haemolysis rate for each sample group was then calculated.

Biosafety of L‐EVs

C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into three groups (PBS control, low dose‐L‐EVs and high dose‐L‐EVs). The treatment groups received daily oral doses of L‐EVs at 50 and 100 µg/mice, respectively, for 7 consecutive days. Following the intervention, blood serum and major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidneys, were collected for subsequent histological analysis.

Biodistribution of L‐EVs In Vivo

To analyse the in vivo biodistribution of L‐EVs, DiR‐labelled L‐EVs were administered to mice via oral gavage. Whole‐body fluorescence images of mice were captured at different time points (3, 6, 12 and 24 h) using the IVIS Lumina Series III In Vivo Imaging System (Perkin Elmer). Subsequently, mice were euthanised at the corresponding time points, and major organs, including the liver, lung, kidneys, spleen and intestine tissues, were collected to monitor the distribution of L‐EVs by ex vivo imaging analysis.

Fluorescent Labelling Detection of L‐EVs In Vitro

To determine whether colonic organoids uptake L‐EVs, fluorescent labelling detection of EVs was performed with the PKH26 fluorescent dye (Umibio, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. Organoids and EVs were cocultured for 12 h, followed by imaging using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). To evaluate endosomal uptake inhibition, organoids were pre‐treated with the endosomal inhibitor Dynasore (DS) for 2 h. Subsequently, the organoids were incubated with PKH26‐labelled L‐EVs for 24 h and visualised using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus).

Colonic Organoid Culture and Treatment

Crypts were isolated from mice as described previously with some modifications (Huang et al. 2023). Briefly, colon was flushed with ice‐cold PBS, cut longitudinally and gently washed with cold PBS to remove the luminal contents. The washed colon was further cut into 5 mm pieces and incubated in PBS containing 5 mM EDTA for 20 min at 4°C. The mixture was passed through a 70 µm cell strainer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), and the crypt was enriched through centrifugation at 150–200 × g for 3 min. The isolated crypts were mixed with Matrigel (Corning, New York, USA) and seeded on 48‐well plates. After polymerising at 37°C for 20 min, fresh IntestiCult Organoid Growth Medium (Stemcell technology, Toronto, Canada) was added and refreshed every 2 days. The colonic organoids were passaged every 4–6 days. To test the effect of L‐EVs on organoids, 30 µg/mL of L‐EVs was added to the medium for a specific time.

To investigate the role of Slc6a14 in regulating the Hippo signalling pathway and promoting intestinal stem cell function, colonic organoids were divided into three groups: control, L‐EVs and L‐EVs+αMT. Organoids in the L‐EVs + αMT group were pretreated with the Slc6a14 inhibitor α‐methyl‐DL‐tryptophan (αMT, 20 µM) for 4 h. Subsequently, both the L‐EVs and L‐EVs+αMT groups were treated with L‐EVs (30 µg/mL), while the control group received an equal volume of PBS.

To further examine whether ERα activation mediates the Slc6a14‐dependent downregulation of Hippo signalling and enhancement of intestinal stem cell function, a separate set of colonic organoids was assigned to three groups: control, L‐EVs and L‐EVs+fulvestrant. The L‐EVs+fulvestrant group was pretreated with the ERα inhibitor fulvestrant (20 µM) for 4 h, followed by treatment with L‐EVs (30 µg/mL) in both the L‐EVs and L‐EVs+fulvestrant groups; controls were administered an equal volume of PBS. To assess the effects of oestrogen‐like metabolites, colonic organoids were treated separately with estriol (50 µM) or estetrol (50 µM). Additionally, combined treatments were performed in which organoids were exposed to both inhibitors (αMT or fulvestrant) and the respective estrogen‐like metabolites.

RNA‐seq Analysis

RNA quality was evaluated with the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on the Bioanalyser 2100 instrument (Agilent Technologies, California, USA). The library preparations were sequenced on an Illumaseq system, generating 150 bp paired‐end reads. Fastq‐formaina Novt raw reads were initially processed using fastp software. This quality control step removed reads containing adapter, poly‐N reads and low‐quality reads to obtain clean reads. The clean dataset was then evaluated for Q20, Q30 and GC content, and mapped to the reference genome GRCm38 using HISAT2 (v2.0.5). Gene expression quantification used featureCounts v1.5.0‐p3, with differential analysis conducted by DESeq2 R package (1.20.0). We used the clusterProfiler R package to implement GO enrichment analysis and clusterProfiler R package to test the statistical enrichment in KEGG pathways. We then performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the GSEA tool (available at http://www.broadinstitute.org/gsea/index.jsp) to analyse both GO and KEGG datasets.

Quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT‐PCR)

The total RNA was extracted from mouse colonic organoids using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Subsequently, 1 µg of RNA was reversely transcribed into cDNA following the manufacturer's protocol of the reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), which was amplified with SYBR Green Premix ExTaq (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) using the Roche LC480 System. The qPCR reaction conditions were set as follows: predenaturation at 95°C for 30 s, denaturation at 95°C for 10 s, and annealing and extension at 60°C for 30 s, for 40 cycles. Relative gene expression levels were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal reference. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, CA). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normally distributed variables comparisons between two groups were evaluated by Student's t test. Significant differences in multiple groups were evaluated by ANOVA. Non‐normally distributed data were compared by the Mann–Whitney U test (between two groups) or the Kruskal–Wallis test (among multiple groups). p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

BL Attenuates Cardiac Dysfunction and Adverse Remodelling After MI

To determine if dietary BL influences cardiac function and myocardial injury after MI, we fed mice with a standard CD or an isocaloric diet where BL was supplemented at a ratio of 2.5% for 3 weeks. Subsequently, mice underwent sham or permanent left anterior descending coronary artery ligation (Figure 1a). Echocardiography analysis on Day 7 post‐MI showed that BL supplementation improved LV dysfunction by significantly decreasing the LV internal diameter at systole (LVIDs) and LV internal diameter at diastole (LVIDd) while increasing the LV systolic function indexes including LV ejection fraction (LVEF) and LV fractional shortening (LVFS) (Figure 1b). We further determined the effect of BL on cardiac structural remodelling. Studies by 2,3,5‐triphenyltetrazolium chloride staining and area at risk by Evans blue demonstrated that supplementation with BL greatly reduced the infarcted size and risk area (Figure 1c). This was also confirmed by Masson's trichrome staining, which showed that the BL‐fed mice had a significant reduction in fibrotic scar size on Day 7 after MI (Figure 1d). Since excessive collagen deposition leads to LV dilation, cardiac dysfunction and adverse remodelling (Yan et al. 2017), we analysed Collagen III deposition by immunofluorescence and found that BL‐fed MI mice exhibited alleviated Collagen III accumulation compared to control MI mice (Figure 1e). The apoptosis of cardiomyocytes after MI is an important factor affecting the degree of ventricular remodelling (Jia et al. 2019). We performed terminal deoxynucleotidyltransferase‐mediated dUTP‐biotin nick end labelling (TUNEL) staining and found that BL‐fed MI mice showed less occurrence of TUNEL‐positive myocytes and nonmyocyte cells in the infarcted zone compared to control MI animals (Figure S1a). Furthermore, compared to control MI mice, angiogenesis labelled by CD31‐expressing cells in the infarcted site was alleviated in mice supplemented with BL (Figure S1b). The cardiac inflammation was determined by Ly6G staining of neutrophils in the infarcted site from mice, and MI‐induced neutrophil infiltration was significantly improved in mice fed with BL (Figure 1f). Importantly, the cardioprotective effects of BL were in a dose‐ and time‐dependent manner (Figure S2). Collectively, these data consistently demonstrate that BL protects against MI‐induced cardiac dysfunction and myocardial injury.

BL Alleviates Intestinal Barrier Dysfunction and Gut Microbiota Dysbiosis in MI Mice

MI‐induced intestinal barrier dysfunction drives gut bacteria and microbial products translocation into systemic circulation, which activates excessive immune inflammation and further contributes to impaired cardiac function (Videja et al. 2020). Therefore, we next explored the effects of BL on gut barrier function and bacterial‐derived LPS translocation. As shown in HE staining and AB staining, BL‐fed mice had a significant reduction in MI‐induced epithelial damage and increase in the number of mucus‐producing goblet cells compared with the control mice on Day 7 after MI (Figure 2a, b). Furthermore, the protein levels of Claudin‐3 and ZO‐1 were increased in the colonic tissues of BL‐fed MI mice compared to control MI mice (Figure 2c). We also tested serum bacterial LPS, a systemic marker for increased intestinal permeability and bacterial translocation, which was consistently reduced in BL‐fed mice (Figure 2d). These results indicate that BL reverses gut barrier dysfunction and prevents LPS translocation, which may be one potential factor that contributes to the improvement of pathological outcome after MI.

Previous studies have shown that intestinal barrier dysfunction provokes disordered gut microbial community, which is involved in MI pathogenesis (Zhou et al. 2018; Carnevale et al. 2020). To identify whether the cardioprotective effects of BL are associated with the modulation of gut microbiota, we performed high‐throughput 16S rRNA gene sequencing of the faecal samples from Sham and MI mice fed or not with BL for 7 days. After quality control, 910068 sequences were generated from the 20 assayed samples and were used in the following analysis. The alpha diversity of faecal microbiota, as measured by Chao and Shannon index, was not significantly different between the four groups (Figure S3a, b). The overall structure of gut microbiota that was investigated by Bray–Curtis‐based principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) showed that there was a distinct gut microbial community of the four groups (Figure 2e). These data suggest that supplementation with BL can effectively reshape the composition of gut microbiota.

We then further analysed the gut bacterial composition at phylum and genus levels. The faecal microbiota in each group of mice was dominated by the phylum of Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, as previously reported (Mariat et al. 2009). The relative abundance of Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobiota was increased in mice subjected to MI and was reduced with BL supplementation (Figure S3c). Further analysis at the genus level revealed that norank_f_Eubacterium_ coprostanoligenes_group was dominant in Sham + CD group, whereas MI + CD group exhibited a significant increase in the abundance of Faecalibaculum and Blautia (Figure 2f). Surprisingly, Sham + BL and MI + BL groups exhibited a similar microbiome at the genus levels, characterised by a dramatic increase in the abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group (Figure 2g). Moreover, the differential bacterial taxa were investigated by the linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis and a significantly higher abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group was observed in both sham and MI mice fed with BL compared with the corresponding CD‐fed mice (Figure 2h). Collectively, these data suggest that BL could prevent gut microbiota dysbiosis, and the enrichment of Lachnospiraceae may mediate the cardioprotective effect of BL.

BL Attenuates Myocardial Injury Through the Gut Microbiome

To determine whether the gut microbiota plays a role in the BL‐mediated improvement of cardiac repair after MI, we administered antibiotics to CD‐ and BL‐fed mice for 3 weeks to deplete the intestinal bacteria (Tan et al. 2022). Antibiotic‐treated mice were then subjected to permanent coronary artery ligation to induce MI (Figure S4a). Antibiotic treatment greatly abolished the ameliorative effect of BL on gut barrier dysfunction and bacterial‐derived LPS translocation (Figure S4b–d). Notably, BL‐induced reduction in the infarcted size and risk area was not observed when mice were treated with antibiotics (Figure S4e). Although BL still attenuated fibrotic scar size in antibiotic‐treated MI mice, the improvement on collagen accumulation was abolished by antibiotic treatment (Figure S4f, g). Furthermore, BL failed to alleviate MI‐induced myocardial injury, as revealed by the number of TUNEL‐positive cells in the infarcted zone (Figure S4h). The improvements in angiogenesis and neutrophil infiltration were also blunted in the infarcted site of BL‐fed MI mice with the administration of antibiotics (Figure S4i, j). These results reveal that the gut microbiota is involved in the cardioprotective effects of BL.

The Cardiac Protection of BL Is Transferable by Gut Microbiota Transplantation

To verify whether the protective effects of BL are mediated by the changes in gut microbial community, FMT was performed. We transplanted the microbiota transplants from CD or BL donor mice to recipient mice by gavage, followed by the induction of MI (Figure S5a). We conducted 16S rRNA sequencing to examine the effects of FMT on the composition of intestinal bacteria. PCoA analysis revealed that the gut microbiota of mice received microbiota from CD donor mice were separated from those mice received microbiota from BL donor mice (Figure S6a). Importantly, mice that received microbiota from BL‐fed mice displayed an increased abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group compared with control recipient mice (Figure S6b–d). These results suggested that the faecal flora composition of donors was successfully reproduced in recipient mice.

We found that mice receiving microbiota from BL‐fed mice showed a significant decrease in plasma LPS level compared to mice receiving microbiota from control mice (Figure S5b). Further histological analysis of colonic tissues revealed that mice receiving microbiota from BL‐fed mice showed reduced epithelial damage and increased mucus secretion (Figure S5c, d). Immunohistochemistry staining also showed that mice receiving microbiota from BL‐fed mice had elevated level of Claudin‐3 in the colon (Figure S5e). Using echocardiographic analysis, we observed that mice receiving microbiota from BL‐fed mice had better cardiac function as compared to those mice receiving microbiota from CD‐fed mice (Figure S5f). Moreover, mice receiving microbiota from BL‐fed mice showed ameliorated infarcted size, myocardial injury, cardiac fibrosis and neutrophil infiltration compared to mice receiving microbiota from CD‐fed mice (Figure S5g–j); although angiogenesis was not greatly improved (Figure S7). Collectively, these data indicate that BL‐induced changes in gut microbial composition are sufficient to ameliorate the severity of MI and are responsible for the cardioprotective effects of BL.

BL‐Mediated Enrichment of Lachnospiraceae Attenuates MI

As Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group was identified as a key genus enriched under BL supplementation (Figure 2f–h, Figure S6b–d), we thus speculated that Lachnospiraceae could be a key regulator contributing to the cardioprotective effects of BL. To test this hypothesis, we gavaged live or heat‐inactivated Lachnospiraceae (1 × 109 colony‐forming units [CFU]) into antibiotic‐treated mice for 8 weeks followed by the induction of MI through ligation of left anterior descending coronary artery (Figure 3a). Although we found no significant difference in histological injury between Lachnospiraceae‐treated MI mice and saline‐treated MI mice, live Lachnospiraceae significantly improved the impaired gut barrier, as revealed by elevated expression levels of tight junction proteins in the colon and a reduction in serum LPS level (Figure 3b–d). Correspondingly, infarcted size, cardiac fibrosis, angiogenesis, myocardial injury and neutrophil infiltration were significantly attenuated in MI mice administered with live Lachnospiraceae (Figure 3e–i). However, heat‐inactivated Lachnospiraceae failed to have these cardioprotective effects (Figure 3b–i). These results suggest that Lachnospiraceae can alleviate gut barrier dysfunction, prevent LPS translocation and thereby protect mice against cardiac remodelling and deterioration of function after MI.

Preparation and Characterisation of Lachnospiraceae‐Derived Extracellular Vesicles (L‐EVs)

Emerging evidence has highlighted the crucial roles of gut bacteria‐derived EVs as essential mediators that have great influence on health and disease (Toyofuku et al. 2019; Toyofuku et al. 2023). However, their potential role in cardioprotection against MI has not been widely studied. Ultrastructural analysis of the cultured Lachnospiraceae showed that vesicle‐like structures were present from the surface of individual bacterial cells (Figure 4a). Using a series of filtration and centrifugation steps, we obtained the EVs from the culture supernatants of Lachnospiraceae (Figure 4b). TEM and SEM images revealed that the purified EVs from Lachnospiraceae were membrane‐enclosed structures with spherical morphology (Figure 4c, d). NTA showed that the average size of these L‐EVs ranged from 100 to 200 nm in diameter (Figure 4e). These results demonstrate that the Lachnospiraceae isolate can release abundant nano‐sized vesicles.

We next performed proteomic analysis to determine the proteomic profiling characteristics of L‐EVs. A total of 4971 proteins were identified by searching raw files against the Lachnospiraceae database from UniProtKB. Among them, 3265 proteins were shared between the Lachnospiraceae and its secreted EVs. The number of proteins unique to Lachnospiraceae was 1690, whereas L‐EVs had 16 unique proteins (Figure 4f, Figure S8a). Principle component analysis (PCA) of these identified proteins exhibited a clear separation between two groups (Figure 4g). Volcano plot revealed that 462 and 2528 proteins were up‐ and downregulated, respectively, in L‐EVs compared with their parental bacteria (Figure 4h).

We next estimated the functional capabilities of the differentially expressed proteins. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses revealed that most of these proteins were associated with metabolism‐related pathways, including carbon metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, propanoate metabolism, galactose metabolism, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism and glutathione metabolism (Figure 4i). The representative pathways in L‐EVs were plotted by using GSEA (Figure S8b). Notably, several proteins that were previously described to have beneficial effects in the maintenance of gut health and amelioration of inflammatory disorders, such as 60 kDa chaperonin and β‐galactosidase (Figure 4j) (Peen et al. 1996; Li et al. 2021).

Gene Ontology (GO) annotations were used to classify the proteins based on their biological processes, molecular functions and cellular component. The functions related to the GO terms including transmembrane transport, translation, cellular protein metabolic process, and ATPase activity were enriched in L‐EVs (Figure 4k). Specifically, most of them were found to be significantly involved in the modulation of intracellular signal transduction related to phosphatidylinositol‐3‐kinase/Akt signalling pathway, Toll‐like receptor and AMPK pathway (Hinton et al. 2007; Kawai and Akira 2011; Ma et al. 2022). These results indicate a possible contribution of multiple L‐EVs proteins involved in the interactions between Lachnospiraceae and the host.

L‐EVs Exhibit Favourable Stability, Biosafety and Colonic Retention Properties

To assess the stability of L‐EVs, their morphology was measured after incubating L‐EVs in simulated gastric fluid at 37°C for 2 h, followed by simulated intestinal fluid at 37°C for another 2 h. It was observed that the integrity of L‐EVs was well preserved, suggesting that orally delivered L‐EVs can maintain stability during transit through the gastrointestinal tract (Figure 5a). We further studied the blood compatibility of L‐EVs by a haemolysis assay. It was observed that these L‐EVs at concentrations of 25–200 µg/mL did not induce haemolysis, suggesting that L‐EVs possess good blood biocompatibility (Figure 5b). To evaluate the biosafety of L‐EVs, mice were orally administered with 50 and 100 µg/mice L‐EVs for 7 consecutive days. Major organ tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney) and blood serum samples were collected and analysed. There are no notable differences in body weight changes between the L‐EVs‐treated group and the control group (Figure 5c). Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of major organs revealed no obvious abnormalities or tissue damage in the L‐EVs treated groups (Figure 5d). Furthermore, there were no significant changes in blood biochemical indicators including alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), serum creatinine (CREA) and serum urea (UREA) compared with control (Figure 5e).

L‐EVs Promote Intestinal Stem Cell‐Mediated Epithelial Development

Considering the pivotal role played by L‐EVs in cardioprotection against MI, we hypothesised that L‐EVs may act as a key mediator that improves intestinal homeostasis to mitigate MI pathology. To test this hypothesis, we employed gut organoids to assess the development and differentiation of the intestinal epithelium following L‐EVs intervention. We successfully isolated crypts from the large intestines of mice and cultured them in Matrigel. The cellular internalisation of L‐EVs was firstly assessed. After a 12 h co‐incubation with PKH26‐labelled L‐EVs, immunofluorescence assays confirmed the effective cellular uptake of L‐EVs by organoids (Figure 7a). Treatment with L‐EVs significantly increased the budding numbers and surface area of intestinal organoids (Figure 7b). The organoids exposed to L‐EVs had significantly increased organoid‐forming efficiency (Figure 7c). The protein expression of cell proliferation marker Ki67 was significantly increased in L‐EVs‐treated organoids (Figure 7d). Moreover, L‐EVs treatment was able to increase the protein expression of ZO‐1 (Figure 7e). These data provide direct evidence showing the functional role of L‐EVs in promoting intestinal stem cell proliferation and enhancing epithelial barrier function.

To provide mechanistic insights into the effects of L‐EVs on organoid growth. We performed RNA sequencing of organoids with and without L‐EVs treatment (Figure S9). PCA analysis showed that there was a distinct clustering in the mRNA expression profiles (Figure 7f). Volcano plot revealed that a total of 157 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (45 up‐regulated genes and 112 down‐regulated genes) differed significantly between L‐EVs‐treated and control organoids (P value < 0.05 and Fold change > 1) (Figure 7g). Heat map analysis of DEGs showed that L‐EVs treatment resulted in an increased expression of DEGs associated with proliferation and differentiation (Figure 7h). L‐EVs Promote Intestinal Stem Cell Functions via the ERα‐Slc6a14‐Hippo Signalling Pathway

As an evolutionarily conserved signalling network, the Hippo pathway plays a crucial role in regulating the proliferation, differentiation, and survival of stem cells (Kwon et al. 2013). The key components of the Hippo pathway include mammalian STE20‐like kinase 1/2 (MST1/2), large tumour suppressor kinase 1/2 (LATS1/2), Yes‐associated protein 1 (YAP), WW‐domain‐containing transcription regulator 1 (TAZ), and the transcriptional enhanced associated domain (TEAD) family (Fu et al. 2022). Among them, YAP and TAZ (YAP/TAZ) are crucial downstream effectors that can translocate into the nucleus to regulate multiple cell events, including proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation (Fu et al. 2022). Based on these findings, we assessed the effects of L‐EVs on the expression of Mst, Lats, Yap, Taz and Tead in intestinal organoids. The results showed that L‐EVs treatment markedly increased the mRNA expression of Mst, Lats, Yap, Taz and Tead (Figure 8a). EV treatment also resulted in enhanced YAP/TAZ nuclear localisation and increased expression of YAP/TAZ target genes Ctgf and Cyr61 (Figure S10), supporting the notion that activation of nuclear YAP/TAZ activity is responsible for the effect of L‐EVs on organoid growth. We used dynamin‐2 inhibitor DS to treat the organoids and then examined whether L‐EVs can be taken up by the cells as a key step to modulate the Hippo signalling pathway. It was found that the uptake of L‐EVs in organoids, and regulation of the Hippo pathway was effectively blocked by DS (Figure 8b, c). These results suggest that dynamin‐dependent endocytosis contributes to the cellular uptake of L‐EVs to exert biological functions.

To further investigate the upstream components that specifically modulate the Hippo signalling pathway, we reanalysed our sequencing data. As shown in Figure S9, the heatmap of RNA‐seq results indicated that Slc6a14 was upregulated in organoids after treatment with L‐EVs. Slc6a14, a glutamine transporter, has been reported to be an important amino acid transporter that regulates cancer stemness (Ruffin et al. 2020). Notably, potential interactions between Slc6a14 and Hippo signalling were identified using the STRING database (Figure 8d). The qRT‐PCR and immunofluorescence staining results validated the upregulation of Slc6a14 in organoids following treatment with L‐EVs (Figure 8e, f). We wondered if Slc6a14 upregulation could be the mechanism of L‐EVs promoting intestinal stem cell‐mediated epithelial development. To test this, we pre‐treated the organoids with a Slc6a14 inhibitor α‐methyltryptophan (αMT) and then treated with L‐EVs. It was noted treatment with L‐EVs resulted in inhibition of the Hippo signalling pathway, enhanced nuclear YAP/TAZ translocation, as well as increased expression of YAP/TAZ target genes, which were abolished by αMT (Figure 8g, Figure S10). L‐EVs‐mediated enhancement of intestinal stem cell proliferation and differentiation were abrogated after pretreatment with the Slc6a14 inhibitor αMT (Figure 8h, i). These results demonstrate that L‐EVs promote intestinal stem cell‐mediated epithelial development by activating the Slc6a14‐Hippo signalling pathway.

ERα is a ligand‐dependent transcription factor that could enhance Slc6a14 expression (Karunakaran et al. 2011; Hu et al. 2025). Expression of ERα was confirmed by qRT‐PCR and immunofluorescence staining (Figure 8j, k). To determine whether L‐EVs‐mediated Slc6a14 upregulation resulted from the regulation of ERα, we blocked ERα by treating organoids with fulvestrant, a selective estrogen receptor degrader (SERD) before exposure to L‐EVs. The results demonstrated that L‐EVs‐induced Slc6a14 expression could be blocked by SERD, along with abrogated expression of genes in Hippo signalling pathway, decreased nuclear YAP/TAZ activity and reduced organoids growth (Figure 8l–n, Figure S10). These data suggest that L‐EVs upregulate Slc6a14 expression through the activation of ERα.

It has been reported that ERα expression may be modulated by endogenous and exogenous estrogens (Chen et al. 2022). In our study, liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC/MS) analysis confirmed that L‐EVs contain estrogen‐like metabolite estriol and estetrol (Figure S11). Subsequently, we further evaluated the effect of these bioactive molecules on intestinal stem cell functions. As expected, treatment with estriol or estetrol significantly enhanced intestinal stem cell‐mediated epithelial development, as evidenced by increased surface area of colonic organoids (Figure 8o, s). Consequently, the qRT‐PCR results confirmed that estriol or estetrol significantly increased the expression of Esr1, Slc6a14, and genes related to Hippo signalling and intestinal stem cell proliferation (Figure 8p–r, t–v). However, this effect was significantly diminished by pretreatment with ERα inhibitor or Slc6a14 inhibitor (Figure 8p–r, t–v). Overall, these findings suggest that estrogen‐like metabolites encapsulated within L‐EVs serve as the main signalling molecules that contribute to enhanced intestinal stem cell functions through the ERα‐Slc6a14‐Hippo signalling axis.

Discussion

CVDs remain the most health‐threatening diseases and MI continues as the main cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide (Benjamin et al. 2017). There is an urgent need for the development of new and effective interventions. Emerging evidence reveals the role of gut microbiota in regulating the pathogenesis of CVDs, highlighting the potential of microbiota‐based strategy to improve or prevent poor prognosis after MI. Herein, we used a murine model of MI to explore the cardioprotective effects of BL. We observed that BL supplementation improved cardiac dysfunction, decreased infarct area, alleviated myocardial fibrosis and cardiomyocyte apoptosis following MI. The beneficial impacts of BL were gut microbiota‐dependent and a commensal species of the family Lachnospiraceae was identified to contribute to the cardioprotective effects of BL. Mechanistically, we uncovered the roles of EVs secreted by Lachnospiraceae for relieving cardiac modelling and myocardial injury by regulating the ERα‐Slc6a14‐Hippo signalling pathway to improve intestinal stem cell functions. These data indicate that BL, Lachnospiraceae and its derived L‐EVs can be developed as promising agents to reduce MI risk factors and post‐MI poor outcomes.

There is a growing body of evidence that the pathophysiology of CVD is closely linked to microbial‐associated systemic inflammation. In patients with MI, circulating level of LPS is correlated with systemic inflammation and can predict adverse cardiovascular events (Zhou et al. 2018). A higher microbial richness and diversity is observed in the systemic microbiome of MI patients, supporting that the translocation of gut‐derived LPS to systemic circulation contributes to inflammation and cardiomyocyte injury (Zhou et al. 2018). Following MI, the compromised cardiac function drives gut permeability elevation and intestinal mucosal injury, leading to LPS translocation into systemic circulation and stimulating the inflammatory cascade that ultimately leads to the progression of atherosclerosis (Zhou et al. 2018). Moreover, LPS‐dependent low‐grade endotoxemia can enhance artery thrombosis via a Toll‐like receptor 4‐mediated leucocyte cathepsin G activation (Carnevale et al. 2020). Consistent with the results of these findings, our data showed that the gut barrier was disrupted and serum LPS level was elevated in MI mice; whereas these effects were effectively mitigated by BL supplementation.

Emerging evidence from animal and human studies has revealed the potential associations between disturbed gut microbiota composition and MI (Khuu et al. 2024). For instance, the abundances of Synergistetes phylum, Spirochaetes phylum, Syntrophomonadaceae family and Tissierella Soehngenia genus were found to be significantly higher in MI rats compared with control rats on Day 7 post‐operation (Wu et al. 2017). Moreover, the gut microbiota of patients with MI was characterised by higher levels of the genera Megasphaera, Butyricimonas, Acidaminococcus and Desulfovibrio, and lower levels of Tyzzerella 3, Dialister, Pseudobutyrivibrio, and Lachnospiraceae ND3007 group as compared to that in healthy controls (Han et al. 2021). Similarly, our study also showed that MI induced gut microbiota dysbiosis characterised by a significant increase in the abundance of Faecalibaculum. A recent study showed that the serum microbiome of patients with MI had an increase in the alpha diversity and high abundance of Faecalibaculum (Chen et al. 2024). This suggests that Faecalibaculum may be a critical component of the gut microbiota that drives systemic inflammation. Further researches are needed to confirm the causal relationship between this genus and adverse outcomes following MI, which may help pave the way for the development of microbiome‐based personalised interventions.

Numerous animal‐based studies have reported the use of probiotics to reduce myocardial injury and improve cardiac dysfunction after MI. For example, oral administration of combined Lactobacillus helveticus and Bifidobacterium longum effectively restrain apoptosis propensity in the limbic system following MI (Gan et al. 2014). Moreover, supplementation with Lactobacillus plantarum 299v is found to reduce MI size in rats (Lam et al. 2012). Interventions with probiotics L. helveticus R0052 and B. longum R0175 can attenuate post‐MI depression‐like behaviours (Kothari et al. 2019). Administration of probiotic Lactobacillus rhamnosus GR‐1 improves hemodynamic parameters and prevents the progression of heart failure (Xie et al. 2023). However, to our knowledge, there is limited research on the application of dietary strategies to prevent post‐MI events by targeting the gut microbiota. Our previous studies have demonstrated that BL is able to modulate the gut microbiota and increase the abundance of beneficial microbial species (Li et al. 2018; Li et al. 2021). In this study, our results showed that BL supplementation could attenuate gut microbiota dysbiosis. Importantly, we demonstrated that gut microbiota was required for the beneficial effects of BL in cardiac dysfunction post‐MI, since oral administration of antibiotics could abolish the beneficial effects of BL in MI mice. Furthermore, FMT experiments showed that BL‐induced changes in gut flora were sufficient to ameliorate cardiac dysfunction post‑MI, further confirming the role of gut microbiota in regulating the cardioprotective effects of BL. Therefore, our study supports the involvement of a microbiota‐gut‐heart axis in MI and associated CVDs, opening a promising avenue for nutritional intervention by targeting the aberrant gut microbiota.

Lachnospiraceae is a highly abundant member of the gut bacteria able to have positive impacts on host health by fermenting dietary polysaccharides and produce short‐chain fatty acids (Huang et al. 2023). Studies have reported that Lachnospiraceae mediates protection against placental inflammation in gestational diabetes mellitus by enhancing epithelial barrier integrity and inhibiting inflammation (Huang et al. 2023). Colonisation of Lachnospiraceae species could provide protection against infection caused by pathogen Clostridioides difficile (Tejada et al. 2024). A recent study has showed that its abundance is decreased in patients with MI (Frank et al. 2007; Reeves et al. 2012; Chen et al. 2017). In another genome‐wide association study, Lachnospiraceae was found to have potential beneficial effects against CVD (Dai et al. 2024). However, the causal role of Lachnospiraceae in cardiac protection to MI remains unknown. Here, we discovered to our knowledge for the first time that administration of Lachnospiraceae could protect for cardiac dysfunction post‐MI, suggesting that Lachnospiraceae has the potential to be developed as a novel probiotic for relieving MI‐associated adverse outcomes.

EVs are nanosized vesicles released by species across all domains of life. These vesicles are lipid‐shielded structures with a diameter ranging from 50 to 500 nm. All cells including prokaryotes and eukaryotes could release EVs as part of their physiological process (Kalluri and LeBleu 2020). EVs contain a variety of bioactive constituents such as proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and metabolites, which have been demonstrated to be crucial for biological response and intercellular communication (Kalluri and LeBleu 2020). The nanosized and lipid membrane‐encapsulated structures make EVs highly biocompatible for cell uptake and intercellular signalling. These unique characteristics of EVs have advanced their potential biomedical utility as diagnostic tools and therapeutic agents in various human diseases. Although EVs from animals and plants have been widely studied, interest in bacteria‐derived EVs has gradually increased (Toyofuku et al. 2019; Toyofuku et al. 2023). Recent studies have highlighted the crucial roles of EVs produced by the members of gut bacteria in modulating host intestinal homeostasis (Díaz‐Garrido et al. 2021; Shen et al. 2021). For instance, oral administration of Roseburia intestinalis‐derived EVs is found to effectively treat dextran sulphate sodium‐induced colitis, suggesting its great potential as a safe and effective anti‐inflammatory therapy (Han et al. 2024). In another study, the EVs derived from by Lactobacillus johnsonii are shown to have enhanced therapeutic efficacy in mitigating intestinal inflammatory injury (Tao et al. 2024). Here, we successfully isolated EVs from Lachnospiraceae culture medium and evaluated their therapeutic efficacy on heart damage in experimental MI. We found that L‐EVs could produce similar beneficial effects on gut barrier and cardiac function comparable to their parental bacteria. Notably, we did not observe obvious side effects of L‐EVs on cardiac function at steady state. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to describe the roles of gut bacteria‐derived EVs in cardioprotection against MI. Given that the application of isolated EVs would overcome the potential risk of the use of live probiotics in immunocompromised individuals, this study highlights the great potential of L‐EVs as novel, safe, and effective clinical interventions for MI‐associated CVD. Despite these advantages, we acknowledge that one limitation of our study is the use of a murine model of MI to investigate the in vivo impact of L‐EVs. Although it provides valuable insights into the cardioprotective effects but may not fully translate to human MI pathology. Further studies using humanised models or clinical samples could help bridge this gap and increase the translational potential of our research.

Further investigations were conducted to decipher the mechanisms underlying the cardioprotective effect of L‐EVs. The intestinal epithelium undergoes well‐regulated self‐renewal and regeneration programs to maintain the structural and functional integrity of the epithelial barrier, preventing the translocation of microbiota‐derived endotoxins into systemic circulation (Zhou et al. 2018; Carnevale et al. 2020). By using an ex vivo gut organoid system, we determined the biological functions of L‐EVs on intestinal epithelial barrier. We found that L‐EVs could promote intestinal organoid formation by enhancing the proliferation capacity of intestinal stem cells. RNA sequencing analysis showed that the potential role of L‐EVs in promoting intestinal stem cell function was possibly linked to the modulation of Hippo signalling pathway. The Hippo signalling pathway, principally mediated by its downstream effectors YAP and TAZ, plays crucial roles in regulating stem cell fate decisions, cell proliferation and differentiation, which are essential for tissue self‐renewal and regeneration (Moya and Halder 2016; Moya and Halder 2019; Xu et al. 2020; Deng et al. 2022). By inhibiting the Hippo pathway, the activity of YAP/TAZ is enhanced, thereby promoting the proliferation of stem cells, which is of great significance for the development and regeneration of intestinal organoids. Studies have reported that the inhibition of the Hippo signalling pathway can increase the number of Lgr5+ intestinal stem cells, thereby enhancing the self‐renewal capacity of the intestine (Johnson and Halder 2014). Although Slc6a14 has been reported to play a crucial role in promoting the survival and growth of cancer cells, the specific biological role of Slc6a14 in intestinal stem cell functions remains incompletely understood (Ruffin et al. 2020; Sniegowski et al. 2021). In this study, Slc6a14 is identified as an upstream regulator of Hippo signalling to promote L‐EVs‐induced organoid growth. Furthermore, it is worth noting that except for Slc6a14, other regulators including Cyp1a1 and Sgk1 are also identified to be upregulated in the L‐EVs treated organoids in our sequencing results. Further investigations are warranted to address whether these signalling molecules also contribute to L‐EVs‐mediated intestinal epithelium regeneration. Notably, the Slc6a14‐Hippo signalling pathway has been reported to be related to the estrogen receptor activation (Castilla et al. 2014). In line with these findings, our data showed that inhibition of ERα abrogated the effect of L‐EVs on Hippo signalling inhibition and organoid growth. Importantly, metabolomics analysis confirmed the presence of estrogen‐like metabolites such as estriol and estetrol in L‐EVs, which were verified as the key components contributing to L‐EVs‐induced enhancement of intestinal stem cell functions. Therefore, the importance of in‐depth research on the ERα‐Slc6a14‐Hippo signalling pathway lies in its new insights into understanding intestinal self‐renewal and regeneration, and offering potential strategies for the treatment of MI. By regulating this signalling axis, the repair and regeneration of the intestinal epithelium can be promoted, thereby alleviating adverse symptoms in MI patients.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that BL effectively protected against deterioration of cardiac function and adverse remodelling after MI. Mechanistically, BL was shown to reshape the gut microbiota by increasing the abundance of beneficial microbial species from family Lachnospiraceae. Furthermore, EVs secreted by Lachnospiraceae reduced post‐MI inflammation by enhancing the intestinal stem cell‐mediated gut barrier function via the ERα‐Slc6a14‐Hippo signalling pathway, thereby inhibiting the transfer of bacterial‐derived LPS and retaining MI‐induced myocardial ischaemic injury (Figure 9). Our findings provide insights into the crucial role of gut microbiota in the pathophysiology of MI and highlight the great potential of BL to improve or prevent postinfarction cardiac dysfunction via the gut bacteria‐derived EVs.

Lachnospiraceae‐derived extracellular vesicles (L‐EVs) mediate the cardioprotective effects of barley leaf in myocardial infarction (MI) by improving intestinal stem cell function. BL supplementation significantly enriches Lachnospiraceae, which can release extracellular vesicles to modulate estrogen receptor alpha (ERα)‐solute carrier family 6 member 14 (Slc6a14)‐Hippo signalling pathway and promote intestinal stem cell proliferation. This results in enhanced gut epithelial barrier, thereby inhibiting the transfer of bacterial‐derived lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and ultimately mitigating myocardial ischaemic injury following MI. These findings provide new insights into the role of the microbiota–gut–heart axis in the pathophysiology of MI and highlight that BL, Lachnospiraceae and L‐EVs can be developed as promising agents to reduce MI risk factors and post‐MI poor outcomes.
