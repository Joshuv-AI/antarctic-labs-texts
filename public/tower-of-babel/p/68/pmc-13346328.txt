Plasma suPAR and domain-specific cognitive performance in virologically suppressed women with HIV
Authors: Hemil Gonzalez, K Ridgeway, Eran F. Shorer, Raha Dastgheyb, Rohan Rajagopal, Yanxia Cao, Ralph Morack, Eunsil Hahm, Kathleen M. Weber, Leah H. Rubin, Audrey L. French
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cognitive impairment remains common among people living with HIV despite effective antiretroviral therapy, with women disproportionately affected. Identifying stable and scalable inflammatory biomarkers that reflect domain-specific vulnerability is important for improving risk assessment in this population. Soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR) is a circulating marker of immune activation with established analytical stability and prognostic value across multiple inflammatory conditions, including HIV. However, its relationship to cognitive performance in virologically suppressed women living with HIV (WLWH) is not well characterized. We conducted a cross-sectional study of 200 virologically suppressed WLWH enrolled at the Chicago–Cook County site of the MWCCS. Plasma suPAR was measured using the suPARnostic® AUTO Flex ELISA. Cognitive performance was assessed using demographically adjusted T-scores across executive function, psychomotor speed, motor function, memory, attention/working memory, verbal fluency, and learning. Multivariable linear regression models examined associations between log-transformed suPAR and continuous cognitive scores, adjusting for menopause status and suPAR storage duration. Relationships between log-suPAR and odds of impairment (T-score ≤ 40) were examined in adjusted logistic regression models. A same-visit subset was analyzed to account for timing differences between blood collection and cognitive testing. Higher log-suPAR was associated with lower psychomotor speed, lower motor function, and lower global average cognition. Odds of impairment in psychomotor speed, and motor function were higher with increasing log-suPAR. Sensitivity analyses showed consistent effect directions and magnitudes. These findings suggest that elevated suPAR reflects inflammation-related, domain-specific cognitive vulnerability in virologically suppressed WLWH and warrants further evaluation in longitudinal studies; clinical translation will require validation in larger, more demographically diverse cohorts, with concurrent assessment of menopausal and vascular risk profiles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13365-026-01321-x.

Introduction

Combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) has transformed HIV into a chronic condition; however, cognitive impairment remains common among people living with HIV (PLWH) despite sustained viral suppression and effective treatment (Heaton et al. 2011; Hogg et al. 1998). Women living with HIV (WLWH) are disproportionately affected relative to men in several cohorts, with measurable impacts on daily functioning and quality of life (Lam et al. 2023; Maki et al. 2018). Yet reliable, accessible biomarkers that can flag risk for domain-specific cognitive deficits in treated populations remain limited (Li et al. 2023; Maki et al. 2015; Rosenthal and Tyor 2019).

Systemic inflammation remains a leading candidate mechanism underlying persistent cognitive difficulties in the ART era (Jacqueline et al. 2020; Rubin et al. 2018; Tavasoli et al. 2023). Although multiple plasma and cerebrospinal fluid markers of immune activation have been associated with cognitive performance in PLWH, many lack stability, scalability, or domain specificity needed for widespread clinical implementation (Anderson et al. 2020; Mukerji et al. 2024; Prates et al., 2026).

Soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR) is a circulating marker of immune activation produced largely by myeloid cells (Dekkers et al. 2000; Ellis et al. 1991). It is analytically stable in plasma and has been linked to adverse outcomes across kidney, cardiovascular, and infectious diseases, including among PLWH (L. J. H. Rasmussen et al. 2016; Höybye et al. 2019). Conditions known to elevate suPAR include chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, and acute or chronic infections such as sepsis, tuberculosis, hepatitis C (HCV) coinfection, COVID-19, and malaria, as well as malignancy, cigarette smoking, and obesity (Hayek et al. 2015, 2023; Vasbinder et al. 2024). Prior studies suggest that higher suPAR levels are associated with poorer cognitive performance and vascular risk factors linked to cognitive impairment in virally suppressed adults, and that suPAR biology is relevant to HIV neuropathology (Cinque et al. 2004; Gianella et al. 2019; Nebuloni et al. 2009; De Almeida et al. 2022). Together, these data position suPAR as a plausible, clinically tractable biomarker of inflammation-related cognitive vulnerability.

Despite this rationale, sex-specific evidence in WLWH is scarce. Women have historically been underrepresented in neuroHIV research, even though hormonal milieu, vascular risk, and immunologic profiles differ meaningfully from men (Epperson et al. 2013; Rubin et al. 2014). Furthermore, cognitive impairment in treated HIV frequently involves psychomotor speed and motor domains, which are subserved by frontostriatal and subcortical circuits that may be particularly sensitive to inflammation and microvascular injury (Ipser et al. 2015; Nir et al. 2021). Whether suPAR captures this domain-specific vulnerability in virologically suppressed WLWH remains poorly understood. Identifying biomarkers that reflect vulnerability within these domains could improve risk stratification and help identify women most likely to benefit from targeted prevention or intervention strategies.

To address this gap, we conducted a cross-sectional analysis of WLWH with sustained viral suppression enrolled at the Chicago-Cook County site of the MWCCS (formerly WIHS) (D’Souza et al. 2021).

We hypothesized that higher plasma suPAR would be associated with poorer domain-specific cognitive performance, particularly in psychomotor speed and motor domains that are commonly affected in treated HIV and may be especially sensitive to inflammatory processes. By focusing on a well-characterized cohort of WLWH and a feasible, scalable inflammatory biomarker, this study aims to clarify whether suPAR reflects domain-specific cognitive vulnerability in women receiving effective ART and to inform future, sex-dependent risk-stratification strategies.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study included 200 virologically suppressed WLWH enrolled at the Chicago-Cook County site of the MACS/WIHS Combined Cohort Study (MWCCS; formerly the Women’s Interagency HIV Study [WIHS]). WIHS methodology is described in detail elsewhere; briefly, participants were evaluated semiannually with structured interviews, physical examinations, and specimen collection (Bacon et al. 2005; Adimora et al. 2018). Inclusion criteria were: age ≥ 18 years; ≥ 6 months on ART; HIV RNA < 200 copies/mL at the selected visit; no recorded HIV RNA > 5000 copies/mL in the preceding three visits; and CD4+ T-cell count of > 150 cells/mm3 for at least one year. Exclusionary criteria were conditions likely to influence suPAR or cognitive performance, including recent (< 30 days) hospitalization, stroke or intracerebral hemorrhage, major surgery, deep venous thrombosis or venous thromboembolism, liver cirrhosis, end-stage renal disease, active hard drug use (cocaine, crack-cocaine, or heroin), hazardous alcohol use (> 7 drinks/week), or uncontrolled malignancy.

Cognitive assessments

Cognitive performance was assessed every two years using a comprehensive neuropsychological test battery measuring executive function (Stroop-interference trial; Trail Making Test-Part B), psychomotor speed (Stroop-color trial; Symbol Digit ModalitiesTest), motor function (Grooved Pegboard), verbal memory and learning (Hopkins Verbal Learning Test–Revised), verbal fluency (Letter Fluency [F, A, S]; Semantic Fluency [animals]), and attention/working memory (Letter Number Sequencing Test)(Adimora et al. 2018; Maki et al. 2015; Rubin et al. 2017). Demographically corrected T-scores were calculated using established norms that account for age, race/ethnicity, sex, education (years of schooling and educational attainment, as measured by the Wide Range Achievement [WRAT] reading test), and prior testing exposure. Domain-specific impairment was defined as a T-score ≤ 40 (1 SD below the normative mean)(Rubin et al. 2021).

A global cognitive score (“global average”) was calculated as the mean of all available domain-specific T-scores for each participant. Given that not all participants had complete data across all domains, the composite was computed using available-case averaging. The number of available domains per participant ranged from 2 to 7 (median 7); 93% of participants had a T-score for at least 6 of the 7 domains. Global impairment was defined as a global average T-score ≤ 40. This measure reflects overall cognitive performance across domains and differs from Frascati-based definitions of global impairment, which require impairment in at least two cognitive domains. When cognitive testing and plasma collection did not occur at the same visit, cognitive data from the nearest visit were selected, provided assessments occurred within 13 months of plasma collections. Primary outcomes were continuous domain-specific and the global average T-scores. Secondary outcomes were categorical measures of domain-specific and global impairment (impaired vs. unimpaired; T-score ≤ 40).

Blood sample collection and processing

Plasma samples were collected between November 2004 and June 2019. In 48% of cases, plasma and cognitive assessments were obtained at the same visit. Whole blood was collected in EDTA tubes, centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min, and plasma aliquots were stored at -80 °C. Plasma suPAR was quantified using the suPARnostic® AUTO Flex ELISA (Cat# E001, ViroGates A/S, Denmark) per manufacturer instructions (“suPARnostic® AUTO Flex ELISA. User Guide” 2024). One lipemic sample was excluded; no hemolyzed samples were identified. Samples and standards were run in duplicate, with means used for analysis; five samples were re-assayed a third time due to poor repeatability. Absorbance was read at 450 nm (reference 650 nm) on a BioTek Synergy Multi-Mode Reader (Agilent Technologies, Winooski, VT, USA), and concentrations were derived from the supplied standard curve. The assay’s sensitivity range was 1.5–15.8 ng/mL, and no sample dilutions were required. The median storage duration was 6.9 years (IQR 6.9, 9.6). Log transformation was used to stabilize variance and improve linearity of suPAR levels (Lee 2020).

Candidate covariates

Candidate covariates included demographic factors (age, race/ethnicity, years of education), HIV-related factors (current CD4 + count, nadir CD4 + count, cART regimen class), chronic comorbidities (HCV coinfection, diabetes, hypertension, smoking status, and body mass index-BMI), self-reported menopause status, and suPAR storage duration.

Statistical analysis

The primary analysis examined associations between continuous log-transformed suPAR and continuous cognitive T-scores using Pearson correlations and multivariable linear regression. Standardized beta-coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported (Nieminen 2022). To minimize overfitting while addressing potential confounding, candidate covariates (described above) were evaluated as potential confounders in preliminary analyses. Menopause status and suPAR storage duration were the only variables associated with both suPAR concentrations and cognitive performance (p < 0.05) and were therefore included in all multivariable models. A prespecified sensitivity analysis restricted multivariable models to participants with cognitive data and plasma collection occurring at the same visit (48% of participants). Effect directions and magnitudes were compared with those observed in the full cohort to evaluate the influence of timing differences between plasma collection and cognitive assessment. Secondary analyses examined domain-specific and global cognitive impairment, defined as a T-score ≤ 40. Differences in log-transformated suPAR levels by impairment status were examined using Mann-Whitney U tests and multivariable logistic regression adjusted for menopause status and suPAR storage duration. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% CI were reported. A corresponding sensitivity analysis restricted to same-visit assessments was conducted for secondary outcomes. Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of the study, no formal corrections for multiple comparisons were applied. All analyses were performed in R version 5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

All participants provided written informed consent. The present study was approved by the Cook County Health Institutional Review Board.

Results

Cohort characteristics

Among 200 virologically suppressed WLWH, the median age was 52 years (IQR 46, 57). The cohort was 72% non-Hispanic Black, 19% non-Hispanic White, 7% Hispanic, and 2% other ethnicity, with 43% reporting education beyond high school. The median current CD4+ count was 681 cells/mm3 (IQR 439, 948) and the median nadir CD4+ was 198 cells/mm3 (IQR 114, 299). All participants met viral suppression criteria at the selected visit. Current ART regimens included nucleoside analog reverse transcriptase inhibitors (94%), integrase strand transfer inhibitors (47%), protease inhibitors (32%), and non-nucleoside analog reverse transcriptase inhibitors (31%). 59% were postmenopausal (natural or surgical). Primary analysis: associations between suPAR and cognitive T-scores

In unadjusted analyses, log-transformed plasma suPAR was negatively correlated with psychomotor speed (R = -0.23, p = 0.001) and motor function (R = -0.20, p = 0.007), as well as global average cognition (R = -0.20, p = 0.006). No significant correlations were observed for executive function, attention/working memory, learning, verbal fluency, or verbal memory. These bivariate associations are visualized in Fig. 1.

In multivariable linear regression models adjusted for menopause status and suPAR storage duration, higher log-transformed plasma suPAR levels were associated with poorer psychomotor speed (β = -0.21; 95% CI -0.36, -0.06, p = 0.006) and motor function (β = -0.20; 95% CI -0.36, -0.04, p = 0.017) as well as in global average cognition (β = -0.18; 95% CI -0.32, -0.03, p = 0.020). No significant associations were observed in executive function, attention/working memory, verbal learning, verbal memory, or verbal fluency. Full model results are presented in Table 3.

In a same‑visit sensitivity analysis (48% of participants), sociodemographic characteristics were similar to the full analytic sample. Associations between log‑suPAR and psychomotor speed, motor function, and global average cognition were directionally consistent and stronger than those in the full cohort. Higher log-suPAR levels were associated with poorer psychomotor speed (β = −0.28; 95% CI − 0.49, − 0.06, p = 0.011) and motor function (β = −0.40; 95% CI − 0.64, − 0.16, p = 0.001) as well as global average cognition (β = −0.26; 95% CI − 0.47, − 0.04, p = 0.021) in adjusted models. No significant associations were observed for other cognitive domains.

Secondary analysis: associations between suPAR and odds of cognitive impairment

When cognitive performance was dichotomized as impaired (T-score ≤ 40) versus unimpaired, participants with impairment were observed to have higher log-suPAR levels compared to those without impairment in psychomotor speed, motor function, and verbal fluency domains (p < 0.05 for all domains). No significant group differences were observed for the remaining domains (not shown). These domain-specific contrasts are shown in Fig. 2.

In the same-visit sensitivity analyses, higher log-suPAR levels were associated with increased odds of impairment in the psychomotor speed (aOR = 4.70, 95% CI 1.21, 21.5, p = 0.033), verbal fluency (aOR = 6.62, 95% CI 1.32, 41.3, p = 0.029), and motor (aOR = 9.37, 95% CI 1.98, 57.9, p = 0.008) domains, and approached statistical significance for higher odds of impairment on global average cognition.

Discussion

In this cohort of virologically suppressed WLWH, higher plasma suPAR levels were associated with poorer psychomotor speed and motor function performance. Higher suPAR levels were also associated with lower global average cognition, likely reflecting the contribution of these affected domains to overall cognitive performance. Participants with higher suPAR levels also demonstrated higher odds of impairment in psychomotor speed and motor function. Although the observed effect sizes were modest, the consistency between continuous and categorical analyses suggests that suPAR may reflect clinically relevant inflammatory processes associated with domain-specific cognitive vulnerability in women on effective ART. These findings support further investigation of suPAR as a candidate inflammation-related biomarker of cognitive vulnerability in women aging with HIV.

Our findings align with prior work linking systemic immune activation to cognitive outcomes in PLWH and extend this evidence by focusing specifically on WLWH, a population historically underrepresented in neuroHIV research (Anderson et al. 2020; Jacqueline et al. 2020; Mukerji et al. 2024; Rubin et al. 2018; Tavasoli et al. 2023). Psychomotor speed and motor performance depend heavily on frontostriatal and subcortical pathways, which are sensitive to inflammatory and microvascular injury (Anderson et al. 2015; Guha et al. 2023). Prior studies have noted similar domain patterns in chronic treated HIV, and the associations observed here support the hypothesis that suPAR may index inflammatory states relevant to these circuits (Montoya et al. 2019; Robinson-Papp et al. 2008).

Mechanistically, suPAR is released from the surface of myeloid cells, including monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils, as well as activated lymphocytes, and reflects chronic innate immune activation rather than a single disease process (Line Jee Hartmann Rasmussen et al. 2021). In treated HIV, persistent monocyte activation has been described as a durable feature of the systemic inflammatory state and has been linked to vascular dysfunction, microglial activation, and subcortical white matter injury (McKibben et al. 2015; Singh et al. 2024; Toribio et al. 2020; Wilson et al. 2014). Frontostriatal and subcortical circuits subserving psychomotor speed and motor function are particularly vulnerable to microvascular and inflammatory insults, providing a plausible substrate for the domain-specific associations observed here (Kamkwalala et al. 2020; Vera et al. 2016). The absence of associations with verbal learning, memory, attention/working memory, and executive function may reflect differential reliance on cortical and medial-temporal substrates that are less driven by peripheral myeloid activation and more influenced by Alzheimer-pathway or aging-related processes; this is consistent with recent evidence that plasma Alzheimer-related biomarkers may be more strongly associated with memory-domain performance in WLWH, than inflammatory markers (Li et al. 2023).

We adjusted multivariable models for menopause status and suPAR storage duration, the only variables associated with suPAR levels and cognitive performance in preliminary evaluations. The persistence of the primary associations after adjustment suggests that reproductive aging biology and preanalytic assay factors do not solely account for the observed relationships. Menopause may intersect with inflammatory signaling, endothelial dysfunction, mood disturbances, and cognitive aging in WLWH, and warrants further study as a potential modifier of the suPAR-cognition relationship (Looby et al. 2018; Schnall et al. 2020). Similarly, although suPAR is analytically stable, long-term storage may introduce minor variation; adjusting for storage duration improves confidence in the biological interpretation of our findings.

Notably, we did not observe significant associations between suPAR and executive function, verbal learning and memory, or attention/working memory. These null results may reflect domain-specific vulnerability, differences in underlying neural substrates, or limited statistical power. They also underscore that suPAR is unlikely to serve as a global marker of cognitive impairment but may instead highlight selective pathways of neuroinflammatory or vascular involvement.

This study has several strengths, including a well-characterized cohort of WLWH with durable viral suppression, rigorous cognitive phenotyping using demographically adjusted T-scores, and prespecified covariate selection based on empirical criteria. The inclusion of categorical impairment analyses and a same-visit sensitivity analysis further support the robustness of the findings.

Several limitations merit consideration. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and reverse causation, e.g., preclinical neurovascular injury influencing systemic inflammation, cannot be excluded. suPAR reflects generalized immune activation rather than neuroinflammation specifically, and relevant inflammatory or vascular markers (e.g., hs-CRP, endothelial markers) were unavailable for comparison. Sample collection spanned multiple years, and although storage time was adjusted for, additional unmeasured temporal factors could influence results. Finally, the exclusion criteria, while improving internal validity, may limit generalizability to WLWH with higher comorbidity burdens or cerebrovascular disease. The cohort was drawn from a single MWCCS site in Chicago and was predominantly non-Hispanic Black (72%), a deliberate feature that reflects the demographic composition of women with HIV in this region and the historic underrepresentation of Black women in neuroHIV biomarker research; nevertheless, these findings may not generalize to other racial or ethnic populations, to men, or to cohorts at other geographic sites. The modest sample size of 200 participants limits statistical power for multi-domain comparisons and may have reduced sensitivity to detect weaker associations. Hormone therapy use, finer hormonal staging (e.g., follicle-stimulating hormone, estradiol, perimenopausal versus early- and late-postmenopausal categories), and the natural versus surgical distinction of menopause were not systematically captured in the analytic dataset and represent important next steps. Finally, although the chronic comorbidities that retain relevance to suPAR biology (e.g., HCV coinfection, controlled diabetes, hypertension, current smoking) were not exclusionary in order to preserve real-world representativeness, residual confounding by these conditions cannot be excluded.

In summary, elevated suPAR was associated with poorer psychomotor and motor function and lower global average cognition in virologically suppressed WLWH, with consistent findings across continuous and categorical analyses. These results support suPAR as a promising and clinically feasible inflammation-related biomarker of domain-specific cognitive vulnerability in women aging with HIV. A single cross-sectional measurement of a non-specific inflammatory marker is not in itself sufficient for clinical decision-making. Future longitudinal studies incorporating hormonal staging, vascular assessment, and multi-analyte inflammatory profiling will be essential to determine whether suPAR has utility as a candidate inflammation-related biomarker of cognitive vulnerability in women aging with HIV.
