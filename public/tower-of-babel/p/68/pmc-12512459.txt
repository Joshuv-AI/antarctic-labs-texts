Bacillus paranthracis alleviates saline stress in tomato by reprogramming physiology, photosynthesis, and antioxidant activity
Authors: Qurban Ali, Tanveer Alam Khan, Mohsin Ali, Mayank Anand Gururani, Sunil Mundra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Salt stress is a significant environmental constraint that hinders plant growth and poses a threat to global agriculture. This study investigates the potential of the halophilic bacterium Bacillus paranthracis (strain Endo10) to enhance salt stress tolerance in tomato plants. We assessed its effects as a plant growth-promoting rhizobacterium (PGPR) by measuring growth performance, photosynthetic efficiency, spectral reflectance, and antioxidant defense mechanisms under saline conditions. The result showed that the inoculation of tomato plants with B. paranthracis significantly mitigated NaCl stress, enhancing growth and physiological performance. Treated plants exhibited significantly greater overall growth, including increased biomass and root development, compared to the NaCl controls. Chlorophyll-a fluorescence and spectral reflectance analysis confirmed superior photosynthetic efficiency (PSII) in inoculated plants. They also demonstrated stronger antioxidant activity, with elevated SOD, POD, APX, and CAT enzyme levels and reduced electrolyte leakage. These findings demonstrate that B. paranthracis improves tomato plant health under saline stress by enhancing photosynthetic efficiency, stimulating antioxidant enzyme activity, and leveraging key growth-promoting traits. Consequently, B. paranthracis is a promising bioinoculant for sustainable agriculture in salt-affected soils.

Introduction

Climate change is increasingly associated with a range of environmental stressors that adversely affect crop growth and production worldwide, thereby threatening global food security through significant yield reductions [1–3]. Soil salinity is a major stressor that endangers agricultural output by causing soil degradation and impairing plant growth, with salinity levels exceeding 200 dS m − 1 in the Abu Dhabi region and potentially causing yield losses up to 34% [4]. Recent studies estimate that approximately 20% of the world’s arable land is affected by salinization [5], with sodium chloride (NaCl) being the primary contributor to soil salinity [6]. Most crops exhibit limited salt tolerance, and elevated soil salinity disrupts their growth by interfering with nutrient uptake and hormonal balance [7]. Conventional approaches often involve the extensive use of chemical fertilizers and pesticides, which can disrupt nutrient availability, diminish microbial activity and diversity, and ultimately degrade soil health [8, 9]. Furthermore, the excessive use of synthetic agrochemicals has exacerbated soil degradation, as these chemicals introduce high concentrations of chloride (Cl⁻), sulfate (SO₄²⁻), and other harmful ions [10]. Over time, these soluble salts deplete soil fertility and hinder plant development, creating long-term challenges for sustainable agriculture [11].

Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) comprise a diverse group of microorganisms that establish beneficial interactions with plants, enhancing their growth and metabolic functions [12, 13]. Research has shown that PGPR isolated from saline soil, including Enterobacter, Pseudomonas, Arthrobacter, Bacillus, Chryseobacterium, Achromobacter, and Ochrobactrum, can promote plant growth under saline conditions [14, 15]. These PGPR enhance plant growth and improve salt stress tolerance through direct and indirect mechanisms. Direct effects include nitrogen fixation, phosphorus solubilization, and the production of ammonia (NH₃), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) deaminase production, and nitrogen fixation and siderophores [16–18]. Indirectly, PGPB strengthens plants by activating antioxidant defenses, secreting volatile organic compounds (VOCs), producing extracellular polysaccharides (EPS), and regulating osmotic balance [13, 19, 20].

Beyond their roles in nutrient acquisition and phytopathogen resistance, PGPR are crucial for enhancing plant resilience under abiotic stress [17, 21]. This is particularly evident in arid environments, which harbor unique, stress-adapted microbial communities that enable vegetation survival [22]. Research demonstrates that inoculating plants with PGPRs isolated from saline soils is an effective strategy for conferring salt tolerance and improving overall hardiness [8, 23]. Several bacterial strains have demonstrated significant potential in enhancing plant salinity tolerance through various mechanisms. For instance, Bacillus strains All3 and Gb7 improved salt tolerance in tomato plants by boosting antioxidant activity and reducing hydrogen peroxide accumulation in leaves [24]. Similarly, Bacillus strains NMTD17, GBSW22, and FZB42 promoted growth in rice plants under salt stress by enhancing antioxidant enzyme activities and modifying the rhizobacterial community [25]. Furthermore, Bacillus halotolerans KKD1 was shown to improve wheat growth under saline conditions by modulating key plant hormones, including 6-benzylaminopurine and gibberellic acid (GA) [12]. Through these mechanisms, PGPR can induce considerable improvements in physiological, biochemical, and morphological processes. These benefits include enhanced photosynthetic efficiency, increased oxidative stress tolerance, and improved overall growth parameters [26, 27].

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) is a globally significant vegetable crop, grown on millions of hectares with an annual production of around 189 million tons [28] Tomatoes are consumed both fresh and processed, valued for their nutritional richness and bioactive compounds such as lycopene, flavonoids, and essential vitamins, which offer numerous health benefits [28]. Despite its economic and dietary importance, tomato cultivation faces challenges from abiotic stresses, particularly salinity and drought stress [29]. With soil salinization accelerating globally, projections suggest that 50% of arable land could be affected by 2050, posing a significant threat to agricultural sustainability [30]. Consequently, developing strategies to mitigate these stresses is essential for maintaining tomato productivity in challenging environments. The existing body of research underscores the critical role of PGPR in improving salinity tolerance in plants, with a particular emphasis on the globally valuable yet salt-sensitive tomato plant [31–34].

Despite extensive research into a wide range of bacterial genera for mitigating salt stress in tomato plants, the development of effective and biosafe strains continues to pose a significant challenge. Moreover, the specific mechanisms by which PGPR enhance plant defense are poorly characterized, highlighting a critical need for further investigation into the relevant metabolic pathways. This study investigated the potential of B. paranthracis (strain Endo10) to enhance salinity tolerance in tomato plants. Through comprehensive growth analyses, including shoot and root length, biomass (fresh and dry weight), photosynthetic efficiency, and antioxidant enzyme activity, we aimed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying this acquired tolerance. This study intends to lay the foundations for more targeted and effective use of B. paranthracis in tomato cultivation under challenging salinity conditions.

Materials and methods

Bacterial culture and conditions

The Bacillus paranthracis (Endo10) strain used in this study was isolated in our laboratory from endophytic samples obtained from two rows of barley monocropping cultivated at Alfoah Farm, United Arab Emirates University. Previous experiments evaluated this strain for its plant growth-promoting traits and salt tolerance by culturing it on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar supplemented with varying concentrations of sodium chloride (NaCl). For the current study, the selected bacterial strain was retrieved from − 80 °C storage, streaked onto fresh LB agar plates, and incubated at 28 °C for 24 h. A single colony was then inoculated into 20 mL of LB broth contained in a 50 mL conical flask and incubated on a shaker at 200 rpm for 24 h at 37◦C. The cells were then collected via centrifugation and resuspended in ddH2O to achieve a final OD600 of 1.0, corresponding to a concentration of 1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL.

Seedling preparations and greenhouse experiment

The tomato seed cultivar (SV4224TH) was purchased from Bayer Company, Netherlands. The seeds were surface-sterilized with 75% ethanol for 1 min and then with a 2% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) solution for 5 min, followed by four washes with double-distilled water (ddH2O) [28]. The seedlings were prepared in 50-well trays for two weeks, regularly watered. The equal size of the seedlings was transferred to the pots (size 14 cm in width and 13 cm in height). Two seedlings per pot were planted, and each pot was filled with a 1:1 mixture of sand and peat soil. To ensure survival, the transplanted seedlings were watered daily for one week before selecting uniform-sized seedlings for the experiments. 10 mL of a fresh B. paranthracis culture, grown overnight to an OD₆₀₀ of 1.0 (1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL), was added to each pot. After 1 day, 20 mL salt solutions with various concentrations (0, 100, 150, and 200 mM) were applied to inoculate B. paranthracis and uninoculated salt in tomato plants. The simple ddH2O was used for plants grown as a control (CK). Each treatment was performed five times, with two tomato seedlings in each pot. The plants were grown in a greenhouse under controlled conditions (25 °C with a 16-hour light/8-hour dark photoperiod). After 7 days of inoculation, the effect of salt stress was observed for each treatment. The photograph was captured of the plant, and the growth parameters, i.e., fresh/dry weights, number of leaves and nodes, shoot and root length, were measured [25].

Leaf spectrometer measurements for assessment of stress

Spectral data obtained from tomato leaves subjected to inoculated B. paranthracis and uninoculated salt stress were analyzed to evaluate the plants’ physiological tolerance. Measurements were conducted on fully expanded sixth leaves using a SpectraVue CI-710 S leaf spectrometer (CID BioScience, Camas, WA, USA), with consistent sampling areas selected for accuracy. The analysis incorporated multiple vegetation and pigment indices to assess stress responses, including: Soil Plant Analysis Development (SPAD) (relative chlorophyll content), leaf greenness (indicator of photosynthetic activity), chlorophyll content index (CCI), carotenoid reflectance index 1 (CRI1), normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), normalized pigment chlorophyll index (NPCI), water band index (WBI), photochemical reflectance index (PRI) and the Zarco-Tejada and Miller Index (ZMI) (structural and pigment assessment). These indices collectively enabled a non-destructive, comprehensive evaluation of chlorophyll levels, pigment distribution, water status, and overall leaf health under different salinity conditions [31].

Chlorophyll-a fluorescence estimation

The chlorophyll-a fluorescence was measured using a Pocket PEA device (Hansatech Instruments Ltd., King’s Lynn, UK) on fully expanded, sixth-formed tomato leaves that had been dark-adapted. The maximal fluorescence (FM) and minimal fluorescence (FO) values were used to assess the efficiency of photosystem II (PSII). Additional PSII-related parameters were derived from the collected data, following established methodologies [31].

Determination of electrolyte leakage

Electrolyte leakage (EL) was quantified as an indicator of plasma membrane integrity, following the protocol of Sullivan and Ross (1979) with minor modifications. Ten uniform leaf discs were placed in boiling tubes containing 10 mL of deionized water and incubated in a water bath at 45 °C (ECa) and subsequently at 55 °C (ECb) for 30 min each. After each incubation step, electrical conductivity (EC) was measured using a calibrated conductivity meter. Finally, the samples were boiled at 100 °C for 30 min to obtain the maximum conductivity (ECc). The percentage of electrolyte leakage was determined using the formula established by Fariduddin et al. [35]:Electrolyte leakage (%) = (ECb - ECa)/(ECc) × 100.

Assessment of antioxidant enzyme activity

The activity of key antioxidant enzymes, including peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX), was analyzed in tomato plants seven days post-inoculation (dpi) with B. paranthracis and under uninoculated salt stress conditions. Fresh leaf tissue (0.3 g) was homogenized in an ice-cold phosphate buffer solution (PBS; pH 7.8) supplemented with 1 mM EDTA. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected as the enzyme extract for spectrophotometric analysis [36]. Leaf extracts treated with ddH₂O served as the control group. The CAT activity was measured by a CAT assay kit (E-BC-F006, Elabscience, Houston, Texas, USA), and the POD activity was measured by a POD assay kit (E-BC-K227-S, Elabscience, Houston, Texas, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The APX activity was measured by an APX assay kit (E-BC-K353-S, Elabscience, Houston, Texas, USA), and the SOD activity was measured by an SOD assay kit (E-BC-K019-S, Elabscience, Houston, Texas, USA).

Determination of proline contents

The proline concentration in the leaves was determined using a proline kit (E-BC-K177-M, Elabscience, Houston, Texas, USA). Approximately 500 mg of leaf tissue was ground into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen and then homogenized with 10 mL of 3% aqueous sulfosalicylic acid. The homogenate was mixed with an equal volume of a solution containing ninhydrin and glacial acetic acid. The reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature for one hour and then placed on ice to terminate the reaction. The chromogenic phase was extracted with 4 mL of toluene, and the absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 520 nm. Proline concentrations were quantified by comparing the absorbance values against a standard curve prepared with known proline concentrations.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were carried out in a completely randomized design. Data from all treatment groups were statistically analysed by calculating mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance among groups was assessed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The graphical illustrations were made with Origin graphical and analysis software (Version 2023, OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, United States).

Results

B. paranthracis improves plant growth under salt stress

The tomato plant growth-promoting traits exhibited significant enhancement under both normal and saline-stressed conditions following inoculation with B. paranthracis strain (Fig. 1). The overall growth parameters were significantly increased in only the B. paranthracis strain-inoculated plants, as compared to control plants. The shoot fresh and dry weights significantly decreased in NaCl-stressed plants (100, 150, and 200 mM) by 25%, 20%, and 19% (fresh weight) and 15%, 14%, and 13% (dry weight), respectively. In contrast, B. paranthracis-inoculated plants under the same NaCl concentrations (100, 150, and 200 mM) showed significant increases of 32%, 27%, and 25% (fresh weight) and 22%, 20%, and 19% (dry weight), respectively (Fig. 2ab). In addition, shoot and root lengths increased in B. paranthracis inoculated plants under NaCl concentrations of 100, 150, and 200 mM. Compared to uninoculated plants at the same NaCl levels, shoot length increased by 38%, 35%, and 31%, while root length increased by 25%, 22%, and 20%, respectively. In contrast, uninoculated plants showed smaller increases of 24%, 22%, and 20% in shoot length and 14%, 13%, and 11% in root length at the same NaCl concentrations (Fig. 2cd). Under NaCl stress conditions (100, 150, and 200 mM), B. paranthracis-inoculated tomato plants exhibited significant growth improvements, increasing the number of leaves by 69%, 65%, and 55%, and the number of nodes by 18%, 15%, and 13%, respectively. In contrast, non-inoculated plants exposed to the same NaCl concentrations showed marked growth inhibition, with reductions in leaf numbers by 24%, 22%, and 20%, and in node number by 11%, 10%, and 9%, respectively (Fig. 2ef). These results demonstrate that increasing NaCl concentrations harms tomato plant growth. However, B. paranthracis inoculation effectively mitigates NaCl-induced stress, enhancing overall plant development traits even under high salinity.

Leaf spectral analysis

Spectral data were collected from the fully expanded sixth leaves of tomato plants using a Leaf SpectraVue handheld sensor to evaluate key physiological and stress indicators. The measured parameters included SPAD index, leaf greenness, CCI, CRI1, NDVI, NPCI, WBI, and ZMI, each reflecting distinct aspects of plant health and stress response. Tomato plants subjected to varying levels of salinity stress (100, 150, or 200 mM NaCl) exhibited a significant reduction in chlorophyll content (as indicated by SPAD values) and canopy greenness compared to unstressed controls (Fig. 3ab). Among the tested concentrations, 200 mM NaCl was the most detrimental, causing pronounced declines in photosynthetic pigments and overall plant vitality. However, inoculation with B. paranthracis markedly alleviated these adverse effects. Under non-stressed conditions, B. paranthracis-treated plants showed increases of approximately 33% in SPAD and 31% in greenness relative to water-treated controls, indicating enhanced photosynthetic capacity and improved vegetation health. Inoculated plants subjected to salinity stress consistently maintained significantly higher spectral indices than their non-inoculated counterparts (Fig. 3bc).

Furthermore, salinity stress significantly reduced CCI, CRI 1, and NDVI. In contrast, B. paranthracis treatment significantly improved these parameters, increasing CCI by 28.3%, CRI 1 by 26.1%, and NDVI by 25.8% compared to respective controls. Likewise, NPCI and WBI increased by 24% and 30.6%, respectively, following inoculation (Fig. 4abcd). ZMI, which was markedly decreased under salinity stress, was significantly enhanced by B. paranthracis treatment, showing a 29% improvement relative to the control. Moreover, B. paranthracis inoculation increased PRI by 25.11% compared with the control, indicating enhanced photochemical efficiency and pigment stability. In contrast, NaCl stress caused a concentration-dependent decline, reducing PRI by 9.0%, 21.1%, and 33.1% at 100, 150, and 200 mM, respectively. Co-application with B. paranthracis consistently improved PRI relative to NaCl alone, demonstrating its ability to mitigate salinity-induced reductions in light-use efficiency (Fig. 4e). These findings collectively underscore the role of B. paranthracis in sustaining physiological performance and promoting salinity tolerance through enhanced pigment stability, improved water status, and maintained nutrient homeostasis.

Effect of B. paranthracis on photosynthetic performance under salt stress

The OJIP chlorophyll fluorescence transient analysis demonstrated differential responses of tomato plants to salinity stress and B. paranthracis inoculation, emphasizing the impact on photosystem II (PSII) functionality. Under non-stressed conditions, both the water control and B. paranthracis-treated plants exhibited typical O-J-I-P curves with high fluorescence intensities, reflecting intact PSII activity and efficient electron transport. Similar fluorescence patterns were maintained in NaCl + B. paranthracis treatments at 100 and 150 mM, indicating preserved photosynthetic performance (Fig. 5a). In contrast, plants exposed to NaCl alone, especially at 150 and 200 mM, showed markedly suppressed fluorescence across all phases, particularly at the I and P steps, indicating disrupted electron flow and impaired PSI acceptor reduction. The most severe photoinhibition was observed at 200 mM NaCl, where the OJIP curve was significantly flattened. Notably, inoculation with B. paranthracis at this level partially restored the J, I, and P steps, suggesting enhanced PSII stability and protection of the photosynthetic electron transport chain under high salinity stress (Fig. 5a).

The Fv/Fm and performance index (PI) were markedly improved in tomato plants inoculated with B. paranthracis. While control plants displayed lower Fv/Fm values, inoculated plants exhibited values approaching the optimal range typically seen in healthy, non-stressed plants, indicating that B. paranthracis helps preserve PSII functional integrity and photochemical efficiency under saline conditions (Fig. 5b). Similarly, the PI, which reflects the overall efficiency of energy conservation from light absorption to electron transport, was significantly higher in inoculated plants compared to controls. This substantial enhancement of PI highlights the role of B. paranthracis in boosting the performance of the photosynthetic apparatus, thereby supporting greater photosynthetic vitality and more effective energy utilization under salinity stress (Fig. 5b).

Analysis of energy flux parameters revealed the impacts of salinity and B. paranthracis inoculation on PSII activity. Leaves from well-watered plants displayed high active reaction center (RC) density and efficient energy absorption (ABS/CSm), trapping (TR₀/CSm), and electron transport (ET₀/CSm), accompanied by moderate energy dissipation (DI₀/CSm), reflecting optimal photosynthetic performance (Fig. 6a). Similarly, B. paranthracis inoculation under non-stressed conditions maintained high photosynthetic efficiency with strong energy fluxes and dense RCs. Salinity stress alone significantly reduced RC density and ET₀/CSm while increasing DIo/CSm, indicating impaired electron flow and increased energy loss. However, plants inoculated with B. paranthracis under salt stress-maintained ABS/CSm and TRo/CSm near control levels and exhibited reduced DIo/CSm, reflecting improved energy utilization. Additionally, non-saline inoculated plants showed enhanced ET₀/CSm and lower DIo/CSm compared to the control, suggesting that B. paranthracis improves photochemical efficiency even under non-stress conditions by optimizing energy distribution within PSII (Fig. 6b).

Activities of antioxidant enzymes

The application of different levels (100, 150, or 200 mM) of NaCl through soil and/or B. paranthracis induced a significant increase in the activity of these enzymes over the respective control plants. Salinity stress significantly affected antioxidant enzyme activities, while co-inoculation with B. paranthracis markedly enhanced plant tolerance. APX activity increased under 100, 150, and 200 mM NaCl compared to the control, and was further enhanced by the following B. paranthracis inoculation, reaching the highest level under 200 mM NaCl + B. paranthracis (Fig. 7a). Similarly, CAT activity rose under salinity and was further elevated to 34%, 40%, and 45% with bacterial treatment, reflecting enhanced enzymatic ROS detoxification (Fig. 7b). Also, SOD activity increased under salt stress and was further enhanced by B. paranthracis. POD activity exhibited the most pronounced increase under salinity alone, and was further elevated by B. paranthracis to 90% at 150 mM and 74% at 200 mM NaCl (Fig. 7c, d). These results indicate that B. paranthracis inoculation significantly enhances antioxidant defenses, thereby improving salinity tolerance by reducing oxidative damage and maintaining redox homeostasis under stress.

Proline content

Plants exposed to increasing NaCl concentrations (100, 150, and 200 mM) exhibited progressively higher proline content compared to control plants. Inoculation with B. paranthracis alone induced a significant 23.30% increase in proline levels over control. When combined with salt stress, B. paranthracis had an additive effect, further enhancing proline accumulation. Notably, the 200 mM + B. paranthracis treatment resulted in a 63.08% increase in proline content relative to the control (Fig. 7e). These results suggest that B. paranthracis co-inoculation not only alleviates osmotic stress caused by salinity but also potentiates the plant’s adaptive response by significantly boosting proline biosynthesis under high salinity conditions.

Electrolyte leakage

Salt stress (100, 150, or 200 mM NaCl) led to a significant increase in EL, indicating enhanced membrane damage and loss of cellular integrity under saline conditions. Among the tested concentrations, 200 mM NaCl was the most detrimental, resulting in a 42% increase in EL compared to the non-stressed control, highlighting the severity of membrane destabilization at higher salinity. In contrast, inoculation with B. paranthracis significantly mitigated this effect, reducing EL even when compared to the water-treated control plants (Fig. 7f). This reduction suggests that B. paranthracis plays a protective role in maintaining membrane stability and limiting ion-induced oxidative stress. The ability of this rhizobacterium to lower EL under both stressed and non-stressed conditions indicates its potential in enhancing cellular resilience and preserving membrane integrity

Discussion

Plants encounter numerous challenges due to their sessile nature, particularly abiotic and biotic stresses [37, 38]. Among these, salinity stress is a major abiotic factor that significantly impairs crop growth and productivity, including key crops like wheat and tomato. This issue underscores the need for sustainable agricultural practices to mitigate its effects [12, 39]. Elevated salinity levels induce osmotic stress, ionic toxicity, oxidative damage, and nutrient imbalances in plants, ultimately reducing photosynthetic efficiency, stunting growth, and reducing yield and quality [40, 41]. PGPR have demonstrated significant potential in alleviating abiotic stresses, such as salinity and heavy metal toxicity, through various direct and indirect mechanisms, thereby enhancing crop growth and productivity [42, 43]. However, the efficacy of novel PGPR strains derived from intercropping systems in mitigating salt stress remains insufficiently explored.

This study investigated the impact of B. paranthracis inoculation on plant growth and development under both normal and salt-stressed conditions. In addition, we analyzed plant growth traits and found that the bacterial isolate B. paranthracis significantly enhanced the growth parameters of tomato plants cultivated under saline conditions. Specifically, B. paranthracis increased shoot and root length, shoot fresh and dry weight, and the number of leaves and nodes per plant. These findings are consistent with previous studies [21, 31, 44], further supporting the potential of such bacterial strains in mitigating salinity stress. Excessive salt stress disrupts plant nutrient uptake and osmotic balance, leading to stunted growth and reduced photosynthetic efficiency [40, 45, 46]. Indeed, several Bacillus species have been shown to improve tomato tolerance to salt stress. Among them is Bacillus megaterium strain A12 [47], Bacillus aryabhattai strain H19–1, and Bacillus mesonae strain H20–5 [48]. The application of the B. paranthracis strain demonstrated a more effective restoration of normal physiological characteristics in tomato plants under salt stress compared to the control group. These findings align with previous research by Zhang et al. (2018) and AlHosani et al. (2025), which highlighted the role of bacterial inoculation in recovering plant physiological functions [31, 49].

Furthermore, salinity stress caused a drastic reduction in Fv/Fm and PI, indicating extensive PSII damage and diminished quantum efficiency, consistent with reports that salt stress disrupts thylakoid membrane stability and accelerates photoinhibition. In contrast, B. paranthracis inoculation improved Fv/Fm and PI values, suggesting that the bacterium preserves PSII functionality by stabilizing electron transport chains and maintaining efficient energy conversion [31]. These protective effects may result from bacterial modulation of osmotic balance, enhanced accumulation of proline and soluble sugars, and activation of antioxidant defenses, collectively reducing oxidative stress and maintaining chloroplast integrity [31, 50, 51]. In addition, B. paranthracis positively affected key photosynthetic components, including the number of active reaction centers, trapped electron flux per reaction center, and electron transport efficiency [31]. Reaction centers are crucial for converting light energy into chemical energy, while the electron transport chain drives the production of ATP and NADPH, essential molecules for plant growth and metabolic processes [31, 52]. Moreover, heatmap profiling demonstrated that salinity markedly suppressed several PSII-related traits, particularly TR₀/RC and ET₀/RC, indicating impaired reaction center functionality and reduced electron transfer capacity. Inoculation with B. paranthracis, however, reinstated key indices such as PIABS, Fv/Fo, and dVG/dto, underscoring its role in safeguarding both the architecture and efficiency of PSII [31]. These findings align with previous studies showing that Bacillus species promote chlorophyll accumulation, stabilize D1 protein dynamics, and improve energy distribution under limited water availability [53].

In this study, it was also demonstrated that B. paranthracis can sustain or even boost photosynthetic efficiency under stress, thereby enhancing plant resilience and growth. A key focus of this study was the evaluation of multiple spectral indices such as CCI, NDVI, ARI, PSRI, WBI, FRI, PRI, NPCI, greenness, and pigment content. Notably, plants treated with B. paranthracis exhibited significantly higher values across these indices, indicating a positive influence on their physiological state under salinity stress. Elevated levels of indices like NDVI, CCI, and PRI suggest improved photosynthetic activity, chlorophyll concentration, and overall plant vitality. These results are consistent with prior research [31, 54], which linked higher index values to a denser green canopy and greater leaf area, critical factors for sustaining productivity under stress conditions. The increased spectral index values in B. paranthracis-treated plants imply that the bacterium not only bolsters antioxidant defenses but also promotes overall plant health, leading to enhanced canopy greenness and leaf expansion. By improving photosynthetic performance and stress tolerance, B. paranthracis ultimately supports better plant growth and higher yields in saline environments [31].

Abiotic stress disrupts the balance of ROS, damaging plant cells and compromising overall plant health. SOD, a key antioxidant enzyme, helps mitigate this issue by scavenging excess ROS, thereby strengthening plant resilience [55]. Certain PGPR, particularly Bacillus species, are known to enhance stress tolerance by producing SOD, which neutralizes harmful free radicals in the rhizosphere [31, 56]. The current study confirms that plants inoculated with the B. paranthracis strain exhibit significantly higher antioxidant enzyme activity, as well as CAT, SOD, POD, APX, and proline, compared to saline conditions [57, 58]. Studies on tomato, maize, and wheat crops reveal that rising NaCl concentrations correlate with increased activity of antioxidative enzymes such as CAT and SOD [39, 59]. Another study demonstrated that inoculating tomato plants with the salt-tolerant bacterium Sphingobacterium BHU-AV3 effectively enhances their antioxidant systems and energy metabolism during salt stress [60]. Plants employ enzymatic defense mechanisms to respond to salt-induced oxidative stress. The findings indicate that elevated antioxidant activity at higher NaCl concentrations is essential for protecting plants from oxidative damage [61, 62].

As an osmoprotectant, proline can be taken up by plants to regulate cellular osmotic balance, thereby reducing dehydration induced by salt or drought stress [63]. Aquaporins a class of integral membrane proteins, play a crucial role in discharging Na+ from plant cells. This function helps prevent the toxic effects caused by excessive Na+ accumulation [64]. In this study, B. paranthracis-treated plants exhibited increased proline levels, which play a crucial role in osmotic regulation and cellular protection under salt stress. As an osmoprotectant, proline helps maintain protein and membrane stability, enabling plants to withstand high salinity conditions [65]. Liu et al. (2025) reported comparable findings, demonstrating that treatment with Halomonas alkaliantarcticae M23 significantly elevated proline and lysine levels in maize plants under salt stress [59]. The proposed working mechanism of B. paranthracis suggests its potential to enhance plant growth traits under NaCl stress through multiple pathways (Fig. 8).

Conclusion

In summary, the study demonstrates the remarkable efficacy of B. paranthracis in enhancing tomato plant resilience under salt stress conditions. The inoculated bacterial strains were found to significantly improve key physiological and photosynthetic parameters, indicating their role in mitigating the adverse effects of salinity. Furthermore, these bacteria facilitate plant growth by regulating antioxidant enzyme activity, which is crucial for stress tolerance. These findings highlight the promising application of B. paranthracis as a sustainable bio-inoculant to improve crop yield in saline-affected agricultural soils, offering a viable solution for challenging growing environments.
