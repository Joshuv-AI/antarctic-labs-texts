Exploring experts’ views on “Not Parent Expected” discoveries via direct-to-consumer DNA ancestry testing
Authors: Juliette Careau, Roel Feys, Maarten H.D. Larmuseau, Rebekah Drumsta, Rob Whitley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMillions of people have taken Direct-to-Consumer (DTC) DNA ancestry tests to learn more about their ethnic ancestry and family history. A small but growing body of research has noted that these DTC DNA ancestry tests raise challenging ethical, psychosocial, and resource-related issues that have not been adequately considered nor discussed. One key issue concerns a “Not Parent Expected” (NPE) discovery, in which a user suddenly and unexpectedly learns from these tests that an assumed parent (typically the father) is not a biological parent. Such news can negatively affect mental health, self-identity, and family relationships. Given the limited research on this topic, we aimed to explore experts’ views on the ethical, psychosocial and support-related aspects of DTC DNA ancestry testing, particularly for individuals who receive NPE news.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study. This involved semi-structured interviews with ten experts on DTC DNA ancestry testing, who had written about or actively researched these issues. The interviews were analysed using codebook thematic analysis.ResultsThe analysis resulted in three overlapping themes. The first concerned insufficient support for the NPE population, despite the emergence of some relevant resources. The supports discussed included formal resources, such as genetic counselling and therapy, as well as informal resources, including online peer support. Both formal and informal resources were seen as having advantages and disadvantages. The second theme concerned the need for better consideration of informed consent before taking these tests. Many experts reported that standard consent procedures do not fully prepare consumers for the possibility of making an unexpected discovery such as an NPE event. They suggested that this information could be communicated more effectively, including using creative measures before consent is obtained. The third theme related to the testing companies’ role and responsibility in relation to support for individuals learning NPE news. A few experts suggested that these companies bear some moral responsibility, even in the absence of a legal obligation, to provide or direct individuals to supportive resources.ConclusionsTogether, these findings contribute to ongoing debates regarding DTC DNA ancestry testing by highlighting the need for greater support for those affected by NPE discoveries and clearer consideration of the responsibilities of testing companies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01494-4.

Background

During the last decade, approximately 30 million people have taken Direct-to-Consumer (DTC) DNA ancestry tests [1]. These tests are available online for a fee, from companies such as AncestryDNA and 23andMe. Consumers subscribe online, then receive their testing kit by mail, after which they spit into a vial and send it back to the company. Their DNA is analyzed to provide various insights, including ancestry estimates and information about biological relationships with other users in the database. Some companies provide additional information, such as selected trait-related or health-related insights [2, 3]. Interpreting these results, however, often requires substantial work on the part of consumers, who may need to contextualize the information, explore biological matches, and determine for themselves what the findings mean for their identity, family relationships, or possible need for support.

Often labeled as recreational genetics, this process is typically marketed as an enjoyable and exciting way to learn about one’s ancestry. Some DTC genetic tests are also marketed in relation to health-related information. However, the focus of this paper is on ancestry and its implications, particularly in relation to a “Not Parent Expected” (NPE) discovery, in which an individual learns that a presumed parent (typically the father) is not their biological parent [4]. Related terms in the literature include misattributed paternity and misattributed parentage event (MPE). We use NPE throughout, as it has emerged as a commonly used and less stigmatizing term in both academic and community contexts. Estimates indicate that such misattributed paternity affects around 1–2% of the population in Western societies, including extra-pair paternity, undisclosed sperm donor conception, or adoption [5].

There have been very few studies conducted that specifically focus on the NPE population. Our research team recently conducted a qualitative study aiming to address this gap [6]. The overarching aim of that study was to document the psychosocial experience of adults suddenly learning about their NPE status via a DTC DNA ancestry test. The results indicate that learning this news can have a profoundly negative impact on psychosocial well-being, sometimes leading to significant mental health challenges, family rupture, and disruption of self-identity. To address such issues, participants often sought support from a variety of resources including their social circle, online peer support groups, and mental health professionals. However, participants often reported a lack of adequate resources for people in their situation. Indeed, some individuals mentioned that mental health professionals sometimes lacked knowledge about the specific challenges they faced. These findings overlap considerably with other qualitative studies on this population, which also document shock, grief, loss, trauma and issues of self-identity in response to an NPE discovery, as well as a lack of post-discovery support [7–10].

Other studies on DTC genetic testing have raised a series of ethical concerns revolving around issues such as confidentiality, privacy, secondary uses of data, unexpected findings, and the testing of minors [11–14]. In a focus group study, Hazel et al. [15] found that many prospective users had concerns about control of data, especially secondary uses of data which could harm users or their families. These concerns remain highly salient in light of more recent research on DTC genetic testing, which continues to highlight ethical and regulatory challenges, as well as issues related to information provision, consumer expectations, and decision-making in an evolving commercial and data governance landscape [16–19]. Other research also indicates the risk of psychosocial stress after learning information about genetic risk for health outcomes [20, 21]. For example, such information (which may not always be accurate) is typically shared via an online portal, usually without the kind of pre- or post-test counseling that typically accompanies clinical genetic testing [14, 22].

Relatedly, Schuman et al. [10] found that participants who had learnt NPE news from a DTC DNA ancestry test wanted more robust consent procedures from the companies, such as a more prominent warning about the possibility of learning NPE news and the unexpected consequences that can follow. This concern reflects ongoing discussions about how DTC DNA testing companies communicate the possibility of unexpected findings, including NPE discoveries and, in some cases, other unexpected information. For example, the Consumer Federation of America stated that warnings about unexpected results may be difficult to find or sometimes not available, depending on the company [23].

While a growing body of research has examined the lived experiences of individuals confronted with NPE discoveries, less attention has been paid to how experts working at the intersection of genetics, ethics, law, and social science interpret these developments. Expert perspectives constitute a distinct and complementary form of insight: rather than reporting personal experiences, they reflect sustained engagement with recurring cases, normative analysis, and broader structural implications. Exploring how experts conceptualize the ethical and social challenges associated with DTC DNA ancestry testing can help to clarify areas of consensus, identify points of tension, and inform ongoing debates about responsibility, governance, and support in this rapidly evolving field.

Taken together, existing literature suggests that DTC DNA ancestry tests raise a range of challenging ethical, psychosocial and support-related issues that remain insufficiently examined, particularly in relation to NPE discoveries. To explore these important issues further, we conducted an interview study with experts to examine their views on the ethical, psychosocial and support-related aspects of DTC DNA ancestry testing for individuals who receive NPE news.

Methods

We conducted a qualitative study using in-depth interviews with experts to explore how they understood and interpreted the ethical, psychosocial, and support-related issues associated with NPE discoveries through DTC DNA ancestry testing. As an exploratory qualitative study, the project was designed to elicit a range of perspectives rather than to test predefined hypotheses [24–26].

Recruitment and participants

We recruited experts on DTC DNA ancestry testing using a targeted expert-informant approach. The initial list of potential participants was developed primarily through literature searches and the research team’s existing knowledge of the field. Search strategies included Google Scholar and PubMed searches using terms such as “ancestry DNA” and “not parent expected”, consultation of key articles already known by the team, and review of relevant reference lists. Potential participants were also discussed during team meetings. In addition, snowball sampling was used, as interviewees were invited to suggest other individuals with relevant expertise.

Those who expressed interest and met our inclusion criteria were invited to participate in an interview. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) demonstrated expertise in DTC DNA ancestry testing or closely related issues, evidenced through relevant peer-reviewed publications, ongoing research, or professional experience; (ii) holding a relevant Master’s, Doctoral, MD, or Law degree; (iii) being aged 18 or older; (iv) being able to provide informed consent; and (v) being able to participate in English or French. All participants had to meet all inclusion criteria. Exclusion criteria were the inverse of the inclusion criteria.

The recruitment procedures resulted in 10 in-depth semi-structured interviews with academic experts. Participants had expertise in areas including law, ethics, health policy, and genetic counselling. Participants were based in North America and Europe. All participants gave written informed consent, and the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Board of the CIUSSS de l’Ouest-de-l’Île-de-Montréal. A total of 32 individuals were contacted, of whom 10 participated. Some invitees did not respond, while others indicated after initial contact that the topic did not sufficiently match their area of expertise and therefore did not proceed. Others were unable to participate due to factors such as parental leave, time constraints, or retirement. Given the small and specialized nature of this academic field, participant characteristics are reported only in broad terms to protect anonymity.

Procedures

All 10 participants took part in an in-depth semi-structured qualitative interview. This interview was driven by a topic guide, created to include several key components (see supplementary file). The topic guide included questions about participants’ views on: (i) existing policy regarding DTC DNA ancestry tests; (ii) ethical issues raised by those tests; (iii) regulatory frameworks for DTC DNA ancestry tests; (iv) available resources for people affected by NPE news through those tests; (v) the companies providing those tests; and (vi) the future regarding ethics, policy and service-provision related to DTC DNA ancestry tests. These questions were posed neutrally, and we used follow-up probes throughout, for instance asking for examples of assertions made. To encourage open discussion, all participants were assured of anonymity and confidentiality.

All interviews were held on Zoom and led by the first or last author. Participants were given the choice to conduct their interview in French or English; all chose English. The interviews lasted on average around 50 minutes long, ranging between 34 and 80 minutes. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed, and participants were given a $100 gift card as compensation for their time. All data were collected between October 2023 and April 2024.

Analysis

We used a codebook thematic analysis to identify and interpret recurring patterns in the interview data related to NPE, drawing on Braun and Clarke’s conceptualisation of codebook thematic analysis and using an inductive approach to code development [27, 28]. A member of the research team closely read the transcripts and accompanying notes, and developed an initial coding structure to capture key ideas and recurring issues raised in the interviews, with particular attention to material relating to NPE. This coding structure was subsequently reviewed and refined through discussion within the research team.

All interview transcripts were imported into MAXQDA, a qualitative data analysis software [29]. Two members of the research team then independently coded the transcripts under the supervision of the senior author, meeting regularly to compare coding decisions, resolve discrepancies through discussion, and iteratively refine the coding framework. These discussions were used to refine the analytic framework and deepen engagement with the data rather than to establish inter-coder reliability or formal agreement. The codes were then used as the basis for developing broader themes related to NPE through an iterative thematic analysis process [30]. At the end of the coding phase, codes were reviewed in light of their relevance to the study objectives and the patterns they revealed across the dataset. Overlapping codes were then grouped into broader themes, which are presented in the results section below.

The resulting themes provide an interpretive account of the main issues raised by participants in relation to DTC DNA ancestry testing and NPE discoveries. Given the expert-informant design and the relatively small population of scholars working directly on these issues, the aim was depth of insight rather than representativeness. The sample was considered analytically adequate once interviews yielded recurrent insights relevant to the study aim.

The research team is interdisciplinary with backgrounds spanning genetic genealogy, bioethics, psychology, psychiatry, and lived-experience-informed advocacy in the area of NPE and DNA identity disruption. Several authors have prior research or professional engagement with this topic, which may have shaped the framing of the study and interpretation of findings. None of the authors are themselves members of the NPE population. However, one author has personal experience with an NPE discovery in their family, which informed their engagement with the topic and contributed contextual understanding to the research process.

Results

The analysis resulted in three overlapping themes, namely (i) insufficient support for the NPE population, despite emerging resources; (ii) the need for better consideration of issues surrounding informed consent; and (iii) questions surrounding responsibilities to provide resources. Each theme is discussed in turn below. Of note, these themes reflect recurring patterns in the data identified across the interviews. All quotes are presented verbatim, with some identifying details altered to maintain participant anonymity.

Theme one: Insufficient support for the NPE population, despite emerging resources

One of the core themes from the interviews was the insufficient support available for individuals affected by NPE news upon taking DTC DNA ancestry tests, despite some emerging resources. Most experts highlighted the gap in resources in this regard. For example, one stated: “I just feel like we need more resources to help people” (Expert 2), while another said: “there’s not anything to my knowledge for the general public. […] I have been talking to colleagues who I know who are counselors and saying this is an area where- it’s a growing area in that people need support” (Expert 7). Another expert, when asked about their thoughts on the provision of supports for people receiving NPE news, said: “There’s nothing. There’s nothing. […] they’re creating it for themselves, which is an indicator that they want it. The services would be utilized” (Expert 4). On a more positive note, one expert mentioned that while there were still gaps in resource access and provision, there have been some improvements over the years:I have a very positive feeling about it because when I- because I’ve seen- from my perspective, there’s been so much growth from 2013 when there was nothing. The being able to give clients multiple resources, multiple support groups. There’s books, there’s podcasts. […] it’s so much better than it was that I have a really positive perspective of how things are now. I realize that there are still gaps in people finding their way to those resources, but I feel like the first step has been taken and it was a really big step and a really hard step. (Expert 6)

The emerging resources discussed by participants can be divided into two groups: (i) informal resources outside of official health care, such as peer-support, family support and life coaching; and (ii) formal resources provided by accredited health care providers, such as psychotherapy and genetic counseling. These are addressed in turn below.

To begin, most experts discussed the emerging informal resources for people finding out NPE news from a DTC DNA ancestry test. For example, one expert shared: “there’s been a lot of, I don’t know what it’s called, but like pop up communities that are engaged in like coaching for this as opposed to clinicians” (Expert 2), while another stated “Family supports seems to be helpful” (Expert 3). A few experts highlighted some benefits of informal resources, while acknowledging some potential limitations. Specifically, when discussing online peer support, one expert mentioned “what has emerged is a very robust network of peer support, which is great. It’s not the thing for everyone right?” (Expert 3). Similarly, another stated: “It is often amazing. Often really amazing. And also can be really horrible. Yeah. Yeah. Unfortunately” (Expert 7). An extended example of such a nuanced view is given below:I think it can be helpful. It can be validating for people. It’s why those things exist. But- but like the moderators of those pages can be a little, I don’t know, just can be hard to adhere to, like, the group norms, especially if these pages are really large and we’re talking thousands of people in a page. I don’t know how supportive that is to have like thousands of people on a Facebook page supporting each other. So, because I haven’t experienced it, I can’t like, personally, I can’t say if I think it would- would be helpful because they exist for a reason. But it’- it’s probably inadequate. (Expert 4)

Experts typically praised formal resources, but again with some caveats. For example, one reported: “genetic counselors are certainly people that I would recommend be involved in the counseling” (Expert 2) and suggested that “a brief counseling, like a 12-session type, you know- three to 12 sessions would be very helpful” (Expert 2). However, some experts brought up some caveats regarding formal resources. For instance, some mentioned the lack of proper training specifically about NPE scenarios for therapists and counselors, with one stating that “their therapists were unaware [of NPE phenomenon] and that the client did the education for the clinician, which makes the treatment really hard” (Expert 2). An expert highlighted the need for research-informed resources, stating: “more research needs to be done to understand what are the resource gaps for this population and what actually will be helpful specifically for them”, going on to say: “professional mental health counselors are just beginning to understand this phenomenon and how they might help their patients. And so, that research, I believe, would also be useful to them as well” (Expert 3).

Theme two: The need for better consideration of issues surrounding informed consent

The second core theme was the need for better consideration of issues surrounding informed consent, when taking a DTC DNA ancestry test, particularly having information at hand about potential unexpected consequences. Of note, consumers are typically prompted to provide consent to have their DNA stored and analyzed (and sometimes shared) by ticking checkboxes during their online registration, agreeing to the terms and services. Once their results are available in the portal, they are also sometimes prompted to give consent (by ticking a checkbox) to view biological matches, which can sometimes reveal an NPE situation. Many experts stated that these consent procedures are inadequate, as they do not fully prepare consumers for the potentiality of making an unexpected discovery, particularly an NPE discovery in the context of ancestry testing. For example, one expert noted that past practices were insufficient, but that this is changing:The vast- vast majority at that time didn’t say anything to people or give any warning about the potential for these unintended consequences, except maybe the biggest players in the industry. And now, I think, that is starting to shift a bit. (Expert 10)

The vast- vast majority at that time didn’t say anything to people or give any warning about the potential for these unintended consequences, except maybe the biggest players in the industry. And now, I think, that is starting to shift a bit. (Expert 10)

Experts typically stated that consent procedures usually did inform consumers of potential risks, however, many believed that consumers were not thoroughly reading and understanding the online terms and checkboxes. For instance, one expert stated, “we know people are not reading those lengthy consent forms. That’s uncontested and, therefore, it’s not serving the purpose that it’s supposed to serve for the individual taking the test” (Expert 1).

Several experts brought up the complexities of genetic information not being merely “personal information”, but data that implicated family members who did not consent to share their genetic information. This meant that people such as siblings, children and parents may have to bear the consequences of an NPE discovery despite never agreeing to such testing. For example, one expert related, “my information about my DNA, in some sense, belongs to a number of other people with whom I am connected” (Expert 1). Similarly, one expert said, “there is this unresolved tension about whether an individual’s genetic information is their own information versus thinking more from, ‘It’s not just an individual interest, but there are familial and collective interests’” (Expert 8). All this is encapsulated in the quote below:There’s some really knotty ethical questions that come into this kind of thing […] because one family member might want to find something out, but other family members don’t want to know. And yet they’re brought into the process because this individual wants to find something out. (Expert 7)

There’s some really knotty ethical questions that come into this kind of thing […] because one family member might want to find something out, but other family members don’t want to know. And yet they’re brought into the process because this individual wants to find something out. (Expert 7)

Interestingly, some highlighted the fact that learning NPE news can be shocking regardless of the amount of information given prior, with one stating, “if they truly do not suspect anything sort of amiss in their family tree and they make that discovery, no amount of consent is going to prepare them for that” (Expert 3). That said, some experts made the point there is room for improvement in providing prior warnings about unexpected information with one saying: “it’s very hard to prepare people to be like, ‘It’s very possible that the people who raised you are not biologically related to you.’ […] but I think there definitely to be a better job being done.” (Expert 9).

Given all these issues, a few experts depicted better ways in which users could give their informed consent before taking an at-home ancestry DNA test. For example, one suggested that consumers could watch a video followed by a short quiz, which would allow for a more interactive way of giving their informed consent. Another highlighted the importance of the “possibility of finding out something you were not expecting […] Public discussion I think is quite important around this and maybe something a little bit more explicit on the websites might be sensible” (Expert 7). All this is summarized in the extended quote below:We have good evidence, for example, that a ten-minute video that would tell people this is why some people take the test, this is some of the things people find out. Here are ten questions you should ask and answer for yourself before you go forward with this test. I mean, I’m just saying, you know, I think that would be something very positive. Now, would I want to see that legislated? I’m not sure, because I don’t think that we should get into that kind of nitty gritty. (Expert 1)

Theme three: A responsibility to provide resources?

The final theme from the interviews centered on the question of the testing companies’ role and responsibility to provide resources and supports for individuals discovering NPE news. Indeed, a few experts raised this question. For example, one pondered:Is this [counseling] something that the testing companies put in place? But I suspect- I mean, that’s a really, really interesting quite an ethical question. Is the duty of care around this? How would they support that? How would they- they vet everybody? How all that kind of thing that they would have to- have to think about. Or even if they could signpost people to services would be an interesting thing. (Expert 7)

Is this [counseling] something that the testing companies put in place? But I suspect- I mean, that’s a really, really interesting quite an ethical question. Is the duty of care around this? How would they support that? How would they- they vet everybody? How all that kind of thing that they would have to- have to think about. Or even if they could signpost people to services would be an interesting thing. (Expert 7)

A few experts expressed that companies do hold some responsibility in providing better resources and supports for individuals discovering NPE news through one of their tests. For example, one expert said “they [the companies] have a responsibility to- and they’ve got the money to, like, provide more emotional support. And I think that is a responsibility, not a luxury, to have that. It’s their responsibility to provide that” (Expert 4). Another expert highlighted the companies’ financial ease to provide resources, saying “There’s so much money in it and there’s no reason that there can’t be more services and that they’re not more accountable for aftercare” (Expert 2). Similarly, another expert suggested, “I just think for a test that can really impact your health decisions, it’s really should have more regulation. And- and maybe it should be a little less direct-to-consumer. Like you have, like, paired with genetic counselors and stuff” (Expert 9). Although this expert uses broader DTC genetics language, the concern in the present study was how ancestry testing may generate disruptive relational findings that warrant support and clearer signposting.

A few experts expressed that these companies hold some responsibility, although not necessarily a legal obligation, to provide resources, as one expert noted: “I think we can be doing a better job with this. I am reluctant, though, necessarily to impose some sort of legal obligation on the companies to facilitate access to those services” (Expert 3), while another stated, “morally, perhaps. Legally? I don’t think they do” (Expert 5). Similarly, one expert said, “potentially directing them to the resources that they might need if they do discover something unsettling would be probably where I would say the responsibility ended” (Expert 10).

Finally, it is important to note that a few experts did highlight some recent efforts done by some of the companies to provide resources, or to direct individuals to appropriate resources, stating for example, “they’ve gotten a start. They have a page, they have a website with some resources. […] They have a- they’ve gotten a start and it’s better than what they had before, which was nothing. And there’s always room for growth” (Expert 6).

In sum, experts typically noted that these companies could take a more active role in supporting consumers, for example, by directing them to appropriate resources. Several participants viewed this as a moral responsibility, even if not one that should be formalized as a legal obligation.

Discussion

The key findings of this study are that experts noted several issues arising from DTC DNA ancestry tests, notably: insufficient support for those learning unexpected news, despite growing resources; concerns regarding the adequacy of informed consent procedures for those taking these tests, as well as questions surrounding the responsibility to provide resources.

Some of these findings overlap with previous research. For example, in their qualitative study, Schuman et al. [10] found that some participants who had learnt NPE news suggested stronger warnings before learning the results, as well as a need for better support afterwards from the testing companies. Similarly, our previous research study indicated that participants often reported a lack of supports and resources that met their specific needs [6]. This suggests convergence between the views of academic experts and the experiences reported by individuals affected by NPE discoveries regarding the overall lack of well-tailored resources available for this population.

Our second theme relates to the consent procedures, more specifically the need for better consideration of issues surrounding informed consent. For instance, many academic experts highlighted how these consent procedures fail to prepare consumers for unexpected discoveries such as an NPE discovery. This finding is consistent with a 2017 study examining 43 testing companies’ terms of service and policies, noting that only ten highlighted the possibility that unexpected or sensitive results may arise, while only three explicitly mentioned the possibility of learning about misattributed paternity [13]. That said, this study is now somewhat dated, and while a few experts suggested that some company practices may have improved, important concerns remain and the present findings do not allow detailed assessment of current company practices.

When discussing consent issues, some experts highlighted the relational nature of genetic information, noting that NPE discoveries can affect family members beyond the individual who decided to take the test. This aligns with qualitative studies with NPE individuals showing that learning this news typically affects the entire family system [6–10]. Yet, to our knowledge, research on the ethical considerations of DTC DNA ancestry tests has paid little attention to how these family-wide impacts intersect with questions of consent. More specifically, our findings suggest that NPE discoveries challenge strongly individualised models of consent, since the information generated through testing may implicate the interests, relationships, and privacy of biological relatives who did not themselves choose to be tested. This concern is consistent with broader work in genetics and bioethics on the shared and relational character of genetic information [31–33]. Our results suggest that this issue warrants further ethical analysis and discussion.

The present study highlights additional issues. Although some of the concerns identified here resonate with broader debates in DTC genetic testing, our findings relate specifically to ancestry testing and the unexpected discovery of parentage-related information. For instance, the third theme raises the question of the testing companies’ role and responsibility to provide supports for individuals learning NPE news. In this context, that responsibility is best understood not as a single comprehensive duty or legal obligation, but as a more limited expectation that companies should take reasonable steps to mitigate foreseeable harms associated with NPE discoveries. The experts interviewed generally emphasized that DTC DNA ancestry testing companies bear some moral responsibility to provide, or direct individuals to, appropriate resources when NPE discoveries are made through testing. Interestingly, most experts did not suggest there was a need to change existing laws in this regard, but rather that more initiative could be taken by the companies, especially given their financial means to do so. This concern is reflected in broader discussions about the responsibilities of DTC DNA ancestry testing companies, indicating the importance of further discussion about this issue [34]. In practice, this responsibility may relate to several aspects of company conduct, including clearer pretest information about the possibility and implications of NPE discoveries, more careful communication and framing of unexpected results, and the provision or sign-posting of appropriate post-test resources. Indeed, the findings of the present study support the view that companies may need to play a more active role in anticipating and responding to the ethical and psychosocial consequences of NPE discoveries.

There are several limitations to the present study. First, our recruitment strategy relied on identifying and contacting academic experts with visible research engagement in this area based in North America and Europe. This may have introduced some degree of selection bias, as it may have excluded emerging scholars and other relevant voices without a strong public or publication presence. It may also underrepresent perspectives from other regions, particularly where English or French are not spoken, as well as from industry actors and civil society organizations. Second, we elicited the views and perspectives of experts about issues that they have researched, studied or deeply considered; however, the vast majority did not have personal experience of some core issues raised. As several participants themselves noted, this meant that some reflections were necessarily speculative. Third, the sample size is relatively small, even for a qualitative study, although this reflects the limited number of scholars working directly on these issues. Finally, the primary aim of the interviews was to elicit expert perspectives about NPE discoveries, but sometimes the interviews veered into discussion of the direct provision of trait-related or health-related information by the testing companies. This provided interesting content, some of which was related to NPE discoveries and thus integrated into the article. However, some of this content was omitted from the current paper to ensure focus, but should be further explored in future research. Other possibilities for future research could include broadening the range of perspectives included, particularly by triangulating expert views with those of affected communities and other stakeholder groups. In this regard, the present findings may be read alongside recent qualitative work by some of the authors examining the psychosocial and mental health experiences of individuals learning NPE news through DTC DNA ancestry testing [6].

Conclusions

This study documents some key shared perspectives held by academic experts. First, there is a call for improved and more comprehensive support for the NPE population, despite some of the emerging resources. Second, experts emphasize the need for better consideration of issues of informed consent, including providing clearer information about the possibility of NPE discoveries and potentially adopting more interactive consent models. Third, experts raised questions about the role of testing companies in providing resources, with some asserting that these companies bear a moral responsibility to offer or facilitate access to appropriate support. Together, these findings contribute important considerations for ongoing public and policy debates regarding DTC DNA ancestry testing, and the support provided for those receiving unexpected news.
