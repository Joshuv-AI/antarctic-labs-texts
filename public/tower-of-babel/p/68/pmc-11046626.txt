Identifying reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome
Authors: Nikita Thomas, Mathew Abraham
Published: 2024

Introduction

Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) is characterised by reversible segmental and multifocal vasoconstriction of cerebral arteries leading to cytotoxic edema. Patients present with severe headache, with or without associated neurological deficits and seizures. Diagnosis can be hampered by the dynamic nature of clinicoradiological features.

Case report

A 42-year-old woman known to have systemic hypertension presented with a 1-week history of generalised malaise, left sided intermittent acute sudden thunderclap headache, and numbness and pain in the right lower limb. She had a history of a fall 3-4 years previously, which was treated conservatively, and of oophorectomy and hysterectomy 8 previously. Upon presentation, she was alert and conscious; her blood pressures ranged between 150 and 170 systolic and 100 and 110 mmHg diastolic and she was tachycardic up to mid-110s. She was noted to have fundus normal. Reflexes were coming through and decreased sensation to pinprick in the right leg. Upon investigations her ACE levels were elevated (93.4 U/L) and her antinuclear antibody panel was positive; she had low vitamin D3 levels, viral markers were negative, and other routine investigations were normal. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated scattered areas of multifocal stenosis with post-stenotic irregular beading seen predominantly in the distal branches of the posterior cerebral and middle cerebral arteries on both sides. The cortical arterial branches of the posterior and middle cerebral arteries (P3, distal M2 and M3 divisions), and in A1, A2 with pruning of cortical branches of both anterior cerebral arteries present. There was mild focal stenosis in the upper /middle third junction of the basilar artery. She was treated with nimodipine, amlodipine and vitamin D supplements. The treatment was well tolerated.

Discussion and conclusion

The first step to diagnose central nervous system vasculitis is early suspicion, but all the non-vasculitic processes that might be involved should be excluded. In our case, the patient's history, the physical exam, the laboratory findings and the instrumental tests excluded other non-vasculitic causes of infectious diseases. Furthermore, a CNS involvement either in the context of a systemic vasculitis, or in the course of other rheumatic diseases, was considered and excluded through a careful workup.

Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) and RCVS are two increasingly recognised entities that share similar clinical and imaging features. RCVS is characterised by reversible segmental and multifocal vasoconstriction of the cerebral arteries and classically presents with thunderclap headache, with or without associated focal neurological deficits and seizures. PRES is frequently associated with uncontrolled hypertension but can also be seen in the setting of renal failure, exposure to cytotoxic agents, or pre-eclampsia. RCVS is often triggered by exposure to vasoactive agents, postpartum state or immunosuppression.
