Epidemiology of ventilator associated events in intubated patients: a multicenter observational study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVentilator-associated infectious complications are the most prevalent healthcare-acquired infection in intensive care units. The surveillance of ventilator-associated events (VAE) has now supplanted traditional ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) monitoring. However, its use is not common and limited reports have been published. We aimed to describe the epidemiology, etiology and the prognosis of VAE.MethodsThis multicenter observational-descriptive study was conducted at 15 centers with active prospective surveillance of VAE. Their daily basis follow-up was for 90 days. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guideline 2015 update was used for the definition of VAE. VAE subdiagnosis was defined as ventilator-associated condition (VAC), infection-related ventilator-associated complication plus (IVAC-plus), infection-related ventilator-associated complications (IVAC), and possible ventilator-associated pneumonia (PVAP)ResultsA total of 185 VAE episodes developed in 174 of the 1018 patients included in the study. The VAE incidences per 1000 mechanical ventilation day were; VAC 2.33, IVAC-plus 6.0, IVAC 1.3 and PVAP 4.7. Additionally 158 VAP episodes (14.8%, 7.09/1000 MV days) were observed, 85 (45.9%, 4.99/1000 MV days) of them fit the definition of a concurrent VAE criteria.Risk factors for VAE included a Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score greater than 7 on admission (odds ratio [OR]: 1.75; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.23–2.47), the presence of a tracheostomy (OR:1.78; CI:1.19-2.65), and antibiotic use within the previous 90 days (OR:2.41; CI:1.09-4.20) were risk factors for VAE.The mortality rate was 59.6% in ventilated patients. Multivariate analysis identified several risk factors for mortality, as follows: age greater than 63 years (OR: 1.75; CI: 1.26–2.42), a SOFA score greater than 5 on admission (OR: 2.00; CI: 1.47–1.46), a higher mean Charlson Comorbidity Index (OR: 1.08; CI: 1.02–1.13), being a medical-type patient (OR: 1.54; CI: 1.06–2.21), healthcare-associated infections (OR: 2.01; CI: 1.39–2.88), and the occurrence of VAE (OR: 2.21; CI: 1.04–4.70).ConclusionVAE is a common complication in intubated patients and is 2.21 times more likely to occur in intubated patients who die. Patients with a high SOFA score, tracheostomy and antibiotic use in the last 90 days are at increased risk. prevention of VAE in intubated patients is important for patient survival.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11341-3.

Introduction

Mechanical ventilation (MV) remains a critical life-saving invasive procedure. Ventilator-associated infectious complications constitute a significant component of healthcare-associated infection (HAI) surveillance [1]. The diagnosis of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), which was surveyed until recently, is based on new or progressive lung infiltration and signs of an infectious origin (fever, leukocytosis, change in sputum quality, and detection of infectious agent). Differentiating VAP from acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), atelectasis, fluid overload, and non-infectious complications like pulmonary edema and thrombosis is challenging [2, 3]. Previous reports have demonstrated a weak correlation between the clinical diagnosis of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) and histopathologic findings [4, 5]. Therefore, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) introduced the definition of ventilator-associated events (VAE) for surveillance purposes in 2013 [3]. VAE includes the categorical diagnoses of ventilator-associated condition (VAC), infection-related ventilator-associated complications (IVAC), and possible/probable ventilator-associated pneumonia (PsVAP/PrVAP) [6]. These categorical definitions were updated in 2015 to clear up some confusion and the PsVAP/PrVAP definitions were combined as possible VAP (PVAP). Some researchers refer to two main groups as VAC (non-infectious) and infection-related ventilator-associated complication plus (IVAC-plus) (infectious complications) according to 2015-update [7, 8]. There are few studies in the literature with an extensive sample of VAE patients. Risk factors for VAE and factors affecting the prognosis of patients with VAE have been identified in a few number of studies [9–11]. However, there are not enough studies determining the epidemiology and outcomes of VAE.

Here, based on prospectively maintained surveillance records, we aimed to determine the epidemiology of intubated patients and the impact of VAE on prognosis.

Methods

VAE diagnostic algorithm has been started for surveillance of ventilator-associated infectious complications in Türkiye along with the guideline published by the General Directorate of Public Health Ministry of Health in 2017 [12]. Moreover, VAP Prevention Bundle (hand hygiene evaluation of the need for mechanical ventilation daily,daily interruption of sedative infusions, keeping the head of the bed between 30 and 45 degrees, oral care in every day with sterile water) established within the scope of the National Infection Prevention and Control Protocol of the Ministry of Health is implemented as part of the standard nursing care package in tertiary intensive care units [13].

This study was a multicenter, observational-descriptive study conducted prospectively. Fifteen centers actively participating in VAE surveillance were included, with each center including data for at least 50 intubated patients. Patients with incomplete records or those whose records were inaccessible (e.g., due to transfer to another hospital) were excluded from the study.

Study populations

Adult patients (>16 years) who underwent invasive MV for more than 48 hours between July 1 and December 31, 2022, at participating centers were included in the study, provided they exhibited no signs or symptoms of pneumonia upon admission. Data collected included demographics, the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), clinical characteristics, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) Score, and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score at admission. Additionally, information was gathered on the indication for intubation, patient type (medical or surgical), invasive procedures, previous infections, antibiotic usage, and 90-day prognosis.

Daily minimum positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP) and FiO2 values were monitored. Patients meeting the VAE criteria were included in the VAE group. Recurrent episodes occurring at least 14 days after the initial VAE were recorded as new events. To evaluate the prognosis of the VAE group, the SOFA and APACHE II scores on the day of the VAE, as well as the patients’ ages, were recorded. The VAE patients were followed for a maximum of 90 days.

The primary outcome of this study was to determine the incidence, categorical subtypes, and risk factors of VAE, concordance with VAP, as well as the outcomes of IVAC-Plus. The secondary outcomes included identifying factors influencing mortality and survival in intubated patients and assessing the impact of VAE on survival. On day 14 of VAE, patients were categorized as survivors and as non-survivors.

Definitions and data collection

In this study, cases were defined according to the algorithm prepared by some researchers on the basis of CDC 2015 criteria and including the definition of IVAC-plus [7, 8]. In this study, cases were defined according to the 2015 update of the CDC VAE definitions [8]. VAE is diagnosed when there is at least one indication of impaired oxygenation in the patient, such as a ≥0.20 increase in the minimum FiO2 or a ≥3 cmH2O increase in PEEP level, with this elevation persisting for at least 2 days following a period of baseline stability or improvement. In cases of VAE without suspected infection, it is defined as a VAC. If there is suspicion of infection or a newly initiated antibiotic continued for at least 4 days, the diagnosis is infection-related ventilator-associated complication plus (IVAC-Plus). IVAC-Plus encompasses two sub-diagnoses: IVAC, which is diagnosed in the absence of evidence of pulmonary infection, and PVAP, which is diagnosed when at least one diagnostic criterion for pulmonary infection is met (such as significant growth of a respiratory pathogen in cultures, insufficient growth of a pathogenic microorganism combined with purulent sputum, or evidence of signs of pulmonary infection) (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Diagnosis of ventilator associated events

Diagnosis of ventilator associated events

During the study, VAP surveillance according to VAP definitions was also prospectively investigated by the participating centres. VAP is defined as pneumonia occurring 48–72 hours after intubation in intubated patients. The diagnosis of VAP involves new or progressive infiltrations on chest radiography, the presence of systemic infection indicators (such as fever and leukocytosis), changes in sputum quality, and the identification of the infectious agent [14]. Throughout the study, VAP surveillance, in line with these definitions, was prospectively conducted by the participating centers. Inappropriate antibiotic therapy was defined as the administration of an antimicrobial agent to which the microorganism was resistant.

Statistical analysis

The information collected was processed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM, Chicago, United States). Before the study, a power analysis was studied. The results of the power analysis showed that minimum 842 patients were required to achieve 80% power. Significance between groups was determined using Student’s t-test and Mann Whitney U test. In all analyses, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical variables were stated using percentages and numbers, and Chi-square or Fisher’s Exact Test analysis was used for comparisons. All the analyses were performed with the significance level set at P < 0.05. Values with P <0.05 were taken in multivariate logistic regression analysis.

The first analysis was performed between patients VAE and non-VAE. Risk factors for VAE were determined by regression analysis. The second analysis was performed between survivor and non-survivor among all patients who were followed up as intubated. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to determine factors affecting mortality. Another analysis was performed to determine the 14-day prognosis in patients with IVAC-Plus.

Incidence and risk factors of VAE

VAE was observed in 174 patients (17.0%) with an incidence of 8.33 per 1,000 mechanical ventilation (MV) days (Fig. 2). The change in incidence according to cities is given in Fig. 3.Fig. 2A flowchart of included patientsFig. 3Incidence of VAE (1000 MV days) from cities in the study

Upon admission, the median APACHE II score was 24.0 (range: 6.0–45.0) for the VAE group, compared to 21.0 (range: 5.0–45.0) for the non-VAE group (p < 0.001). The median SOFA score was 6 in both groups (p = 0.259). Among patients with an APACHE II score greater than 22; 54% were in the VAE group, while 43% were in the non-VAE group (p = 0.009). For patients with a SOFA score above 7 upon admission, 48.3% were in the VAE group compared to 34.8% in the non-VAE group (p = 0.001). Hypertension was present in 51.1% of the VAE group versus 42.2% in the non-VAE group (p = 0.036). Other comorbidities did not differ significantly between groups. There was no significant difference between the VAE and non-VAE groups regarding intensive care type, patient type (medical-surgical), intubation service, and intubation indication (Appendix 1).

The rate of tracheostomy was higher in patients with VAE (n = 47, 27.0%) compared to those without VAE (n = 143, 16.9%) (p = 0.003). Similarly, the use of central venous catheters before the development of VAE was more common in the VAE group (n = 155, 89.1%) than in the non-VAE group (n = 696, 82.5%) (p = 0.033). However, the median duration (range) of invasive procedure use did not differ significantly between the two groups.

Nine percent of patients in the non-VAE group and 23.0% in the VAE group had used antibiotics for any infection within the 90 days before admission (p < 0.001). In the VAE group, 27 patients (15.5%) had infections with a carbapenem-resistant agent within 90 days before hospitalization, compared to 38 patients (4.5%) in the non-VAE group (p < 0.001). During the study period, HAIs (excluding VAE) were observed in 44.3% of the non-VAE group, whereas 25.7% of the VAE group experienced such infections. Total hospitalization duration and the length of MV were significantly longer in the VAE group (p < 0.001). The 90-day mortality rate from admission was 72.4% in the VAE group compared to 57.0% in the non-VAE group (p < 0.001). Multivariate analysis identified several significant risk factors for VAE: a SOFA score greater than 7 on admission (OR: 1.75; 95% CI: 1.23–2.47, p = 0.002), tracheostomy (OR: 1.78; 95% CI: 1.19–2.65, p = 0.005), and prior antibiotic use for any infection in the 90 days before admission (OR: 2.15; 95% CI: 1.09–4.20, p = 0.026).

Characteristics and outcomes of VAE

A total of 185 episodes were observed in 174 patients. The VAE group had a median of 18.0 (4.0-138.0) days of hospitalization and 11.0 (4.0-137.0) days of mechanical ventilation before developing VAE (p = 0.104 and p = 0.051, respectively). The main component of VAE was IVAC-plus identified in 133 episodes (71.9%, as shown in Fig. 4). Of these episodes, 105 were classified as PVAP, representing 56.8% of all VAE episodes, while 28 were classified as IVAC, comprising 15.1% of all episodes. The incidence of VAE was 8.33 per 1,000 MV days. The sub-diagnoses rates were: VAC at 2.33 per 1,000 MV days, IVAC-Plus at 6.0 per 1,000 MV days, IVAC at 1.3 per 1,000 MV days, and PVAP at 4.7 per 1,000 MV days. Discussion

VAE is still an important complication in intubated patients with a incidence of 8.33/1000 MV days (17%) and the main component of VAE was IVAC-plus in this study. Although there are some differences between researchers regarding the use of sub-diagnoses, the definitions of VAC and PVAP are generally common. In the literature, the incidence of VAE was reported as 6.4-34.9, 1000 MV days has been reported [15–24].

The risk factors of VAE and affecting mortality in intubated patients were evaluated in the multicenter prospective study.

In our study, we identified several risk factors for VAE: a SOFA score greater than 7 upon admission, the presence of a tracheostomy, and prior antibiotic use within the last 90 days before hospitalization. The SOFA score is a well-established prognostic tool for both the development of VAP and poor outcomes in VAP patients [25, 26]. However, research on risk factors specific to VAE remains limited. Patnaik et al. [11], evaluated VAEs in critically ill patients and found that a high median SOFA score was an indicator of poor prognosis in patients with VAEs. In the study of Fang et al. [27], indicated that an increase in the SOFA score by day 3 was predictive of VAE development. According to our results, a SOFA score >7 predicted the development of VAE in intubated patients. Furthermore, for patients diagnosed with IVAC-Plus, a SOFA score greater than 7 on the event day was indicative of a mortality.

Tracheostomy is performed to maintain an open airway for patients unable to protect their airway. This procedure facilitates faster weaning from MV and enables patients to resume daily activities more promptly. It is also anticipated to reduce the incidence of VAE and VAP [28, 29]. However, the presence of a tracheostomy may also predispose to colonization and infection due to loss of function of the upper airway [30, 31]. There remains insufficient data on the relationship between VAE and tracheostomy, leaving it unclear whether tracheostomy contributes to poorer outcomes or merely indicates higher disease severity. In the EUVAE study, the tracheostomy rate was higher among VAE and PVAP patients [32]. According to our data, tracheostomy increased VAE development by 1.78 times.

Previous use of antibiotics for any infection within the past 90-days is identified as a risk factor for VAP caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria [33]. Antibiotics often eliminate commensal or beneficial bacteria, allowing opportunistic or antibiotic-resistant pathogens colonization. This selection pressure is particularly concerning in hospitalized or mechanically ventilated patients. Prior or ongoing antibiotic use is associated with an altered lung microbiome, increasing the risk of infection with multi-drug resistant (MDR) organisms. On the other hand, the lung microbiome interacts closely with the immune system. Antibiotic-induced changes may result in dysregulated immune responses, either hyperinflammation or immunosuppression, which can exacerbate lung injury or infection risk [34]. According to our study, previous antibiotic treatment (within 90 days) before hospitalization (due to healthcare and community-acquired infection) increased the development of VAE by 2.15 times. However, due to the multicenter nature of our study, we could not collect colonization data, as not all centers conducted surveillance for resistant bacteria.

Prolonged MV increases the risk of VAP. A single-center study identified MV for more than 5 days as a risk factor for VAP in intensive care patients. In a single center study, mechanical ventilation for more than five days was found to be a risk factor for VAP in intensive care patients [35]. In a study by Othman et al. [36], the incidence of VAP was 5% in patients receiving a day of mechanical ventilation and 65% in patients receiving 30 days of mechanical ventilation. Fang et al. found that patients in the VAE group had longer ventilation times than those in the non-vae group [27]. In our study, although multiple regression analysis did not yield significant results, patients with VAE had longer ventilation days before the onset of VAE than those without VAE. Early weaning should be considered a protective factor against VAE.

We found that the 90-day mortality rate among intubated patients was 59.6%. Significant predictors for 90-day mortality included a SOFA score greater than 5 on admission, age over 63, a higher CCI, and the development of nosocomial infections. The main finding of the study was that the mortality rate was 2.21 times higher in patients with VAE. But there was no difference in mortality for subdiagnoses. Numerous studies have reported higher mortality rates in the VAE group compared to those without VAE. Nonetheless, data on mortality directly attributable to VAE remain limited. Klompas et al. [37], conducted a study in 2014, revealing that VAE increased mortality 1.98-fold and identified all VAE sub-diagnoses (VAC, VAC-plus, IVAC-Plus) as risk factors for mortality. Meagher et al. [38], and Younan et al. [39], evaluated the impact of VAE on mortality in trauma patients and reported approximately threefold and twofold increases in mortality attributable to VAE compared to non-VAE patients, respectively. In a study by Klein-Klouwenberg et al. [40], the sub-distribution hazards for mortality were found to be 3.9 for VAC, 2.5 for IVAC, and 2.0 for VAE [37]. Intubated patients had a death rate of 60% within 90 days in our study. Excluding patients who were intubated for less than 96 hours may have shown the mortality rate to be relatively high.

Recent literature has criticized the discordance between VAE and VAP definitions. In a meta-analysis, Fan et al. [41], reported that VAE surveillance fails to accurately detect traditional VAP cases in intensive care units. Our results indicate that VAE surveillance missed 73 out of 158 VAP episodes, accounting for 46%. When analyzing VAE for VAP diagnosis, VAE demonstrates higher specificity (89.6%) and NPV (92.0%) than VAP. However, VAE proves less practical for diagnosis, given its lower sensitivity (55.6%) and PPV (48.3%). For patients diagnosed with PVAP but not included in the VAP group, VAE and its subgroups also appear suboptimal as diagnostic methods. Given the definitional overlap, it is unusual for approximately 50% of VAP cases not to meet IVAC criteria. You are right. Further studies detailing compatibility with VAE and VAP are needed. Therefore, improvements in the diagnosis of VAE should be considered. Discussions on definitions based on oxygenation and compliance, among other factors, could provide new insights.

Limitations

This is a multicenter surveillance study that prospectively examined the characteristics of VAE. The impact of VAE on mortality is one of the most important outcomes. The most important limitation is the inability to access data on resistant bacterial colonisation of patients. Furthermore, at least 50 patients were included from each centre over 6 months, which equates to approximately two patients per week. This is a limited study group and studies involving larger numbers of patients are needed.

Another limitation is that sicker patients are more likely to develop VAP/IVAC and have worse outcomes, but it is unclear how much of this is attributable to infection and how much to their baseline condition.

Conclusion

VAE is a common complication in intubated patients and mortality rate is 2.21 times higher in patients with VAE. In intubated and especially tracheostomised patients, it is necessary to prevent the development of VAE. Furthermore, given the high discordance between VAE and VAP, further research to improve diagnostic sensitivity is crucial.
