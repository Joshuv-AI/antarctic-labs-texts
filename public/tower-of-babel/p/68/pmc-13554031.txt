A novel governance framework for transformative science–policy–society interfaces: What can sociocracy offer?
Authors: Eszter Kelemen, Zsófia Kollányi, Kata Fodor, Juliette Young, Karla Locher-Krause, Kármen Czett, Robin Dianoux, Jiska van Dijk, Kaisa Korhonen-Kurki, Marie Vandewalle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent literature on science–policy–society interfaces (SPSI) suggests that transformative change in environmental governance requires solution-focused, inclusive, and co-produced mechanisms in the creation of policy-relevant knowledge. Nonetheless, due to accepted epistemic norms and modes of organisation, most SPSIs fail to address prevailing power disparities and contribute to real economic and political restructuring for sustainability. This paper addresses a rarely considered but critical aspect of transformative knowledge co-production, namely the governance structures and processes that underpin SPSI interactions. Based on a design thinking process and expert interviews, we outline a novel model of SPSI governance that is based on the principles of sociocracy, centring equity, inclusivity, and consent-based decision-making. Finally, using the example of the Science Service for Biodiversity, a newly developed SPSI under the EU-funded BioAgora project: we sketch the strengths and limitations of this model, inviting readers to reimagine and further the discussion on SPSI governance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13280-026-02362-6.Keywords: Governance framework, Inclusive, Science–policy–society interfaces, Sociocracy, Transformative change

Introduction

Global environmental science–policy–society interfaces (SPSIs), such as the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform for Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES), the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), and Future Earth, call for transformative change to combat the intertwined climate, biodiversity and health crises. Effectively pursuing transformative change requires both scientific research and science–policy–society interactions, to shift from exploring and understanding ecological processes to more problem-driven and dialogical ways of working together (Horcea-Milcu et al. 2024). Co-producing knowledge is key to making SPSIs more transformative (Sarkki et al. 2025), including not just scientists from different fields (Belaïd and Unger 2024) but also societal actors (Rozance et al. 2020) and policymakers (McGonigle et al. 2020). Kayhkö et al. (2025) suggest that co-production can either be demand-driven, producing specialised knowledge to tackle societal challenges, or it can emphasize transformative sensemaking, addressing the root causes of environmental issues. Besides identifying best practices and promising pilots that foster transformation, SPSIs should also confront the political, economic and societal processes that cement the status quo (Preiser 2025).

The question is, can SPSIs—in their current organisational form—meet the above expectations? Through a literature review and expert elicitation, Jagannathan et al. (2023) identified the most agreed upon characteristics of transformative SPSIs, comprising participatory and multidisciplinary approaches, collaboration and social learning, and iteration and deliberation to include diverse values and forms of knowledge. The same study, however, also highlighted some misguiding (and strongly debated) claims, e.g. that SPSI processes are linear and scientists act as apolitical actors at the interface. This tension can be traced back to the organisational and funding structures and the formal and informal rules that continue to govern interactions at the SPSI. Through the epistemic analysis of the IPCC special report on the impacts of global warming of 1.5 °C (SR15), Berg and Lidskog (2024) found that the methodological framing chosen (pathway models) centred greenhouse gases (GHGs) as the main epistemic focus of the report. This, in turn, resulted in privileging technological solutions that directly and measurably alter GHG concentrations—consistent with the prevailing growth-oriented mindset—over more transformative options that challenge capitalism, economic growth, or the globalisation of supply chains. They conclude that the IPCC report’s epistemic rules and culture reinforces unsustainable trajectories and creates path-dependency, because more critical standpoints would be considered normative (policy prescriptive) (Berg and Lidskog 2024: 9). Turnhout (2024: 3) goes even further, arguing that “the norms of neutrality and relevance provide strong incentives to refrain from [questioning dominant political and scientific framings] as this would not just risk losing policy relevance, but is also likely to be seen as political, thereby risking loss of authority.” All this suggests that SPSIs in their current form are more part of the status quo than the agents of change. Making them more transformative requires new governance structures and processes which consider pluralism, justice, and the conscious reorganisation of power structures (Subramanian et al. 2025).

This perspective paper aims to generate debate and new ideas on how to reorganise the governance of SPSIs to increase their transformative potential. We first present our analytical framework on SPSI governance structures and processes. We then describe the design-thinking process we used to co-create the Science Service for Biodiversity in Europe (SSBD) within the EU-funded BioAgora Horizon project. Drawing on expert interviews, workshops, and organisation studies, we propose an innovative governance framework that builds on sociocratic principles such as equity, inclusivity, and decentralised, consent-based decision-making (Rau and Koch-Gonzalez 2024). Using the SSBD as an example, we envision how such a model could function in practice and what challenges it might face.

Our case study—BioAgora and its final output, the future SSBD—is an EU level initiative to strengthen the SPSI on biodiversity, cited in the EU Biodiversity Strategy for 2030 (EC 2020). The SSBD’s main functions are (1) answering biodiversity-related policy requests to support evidence-based policymaking; (2) connecting existing networks and maintaining a diverse scientific community for sustained knowledge access; and (3) pursuing transformative change in European policymaking by addressing root causes and societal values. Although focused on biodiversity, our suggestions could apply to any field where science, policy and society (should) work interconnectedly.

Conceptual background

Science–policy–society interfaces (SPSIs) are organised spaces for knowledge brokering and co-production. They go beyond the linear transmission of reliable scientific information, engaging policymakers, researchers and other knowledge holders in the iterative co-creation of policy solutions (Young et al. 2014; Sarkki et al. 2015). Based on recent literature, we identified four critical aspects of SPSIs—what and whose knowledge counts as relevant, how such knowledge is (co-)produced, which governance structures shape knowledge (co-)production, and how (co-produced) knowledge is used—which analytical components guided our empirical analysis.

In SPSIs, relevant knowledge is defined widely, recognising contributions from many different actors (such as civil society, policy actors or indigenous knowledge holders) in solving wicked problems through co-production (Harris et al. 2024). While joint knowledge creation is often messy, time- and resource-intensive, and sometimes criticised for limited generalisability (Sutherland et al. 2017), it is also widely regarded as essential for generating actionable knowledge. Knowledge co-creation can link scientific understanding with practical decision-making, making it more likely that political decisions drive effective behaviour change (Baum and Gross 2017) and promote environmentally sustainable and socially just outcomes (Wyborn et al. 2019).

Formal and informal governance processes shape and facilitate the roles of, and interactions between, SPSI actors, including e.g. methodological decisions on knowledge generation or synthesis, procedural decisions on facilitating knowledge co-production, or more political decisions on the integration of SPSI outcomes into policy (Montana 2019). Governance processes also include ethical measures which ensure that challenges such as conflicts of interest, influence peddling, and discrimination are prevented or adequately managed (Tremblay et al. 2016).

A variety of organisational forms are employed by current SPSIs to structure and formalize knowledge co-production and uptake. They range from loosely coordinated network structures to organised platforms operating according to strict procedural rules (Görg et al. 2016), with many hybrids in between (e.g. network-of-networks or policy labs). Co-producing actionable knowledge requires an open and adaptive organisational frame that transcends disciplinary silos and integrates diverse knowledge to address complex social–ecological systems (Dedeurwaerdere 2024). This, in turn, calls for institutional arrangements where equal actors form flexible and adaptive collaborations, making decentralised governance structures more suitable for SPSIs than traditional hierarchical models.

The types of knowledge integrated at the SPSI, along with co-production processes and structures, shape both immediate policy outcomes and potential societal transformation. Yet, the relationship between these outcomes is often marked by tension. A meta-analysis of 32 co-production cases (Chambers et al. 2021) found that solution-oriented, evidence-based approaches engaging powerful actors tend to achieve high-level policy impact, but rarely empower marginalised groups or challenge dominant paradigms, which are key for system-level change. Efforts to shift power and challenge dominant paradigms may be less effective at achieving near-term policy change yet are critical for long-term transformation. Addressing this dilemma requires rethinking the institutional conditions of co-production itself—a challenge this paper explores through the lens of sociocratic governance.

Sociocracy offers a flexible and inclusive organisational model that supports responsive and reflexive (i.e. agile) functioning while providing organisational actors significant autonomy (Eckstein 2016). Sociocratic organisations can be found among software developers, public and private schools, and grassroots organisations. Their common characteristic is the decentralisation of authority and the replacement of hierarchical relationships by a more horizontal structure (Schell and Bischof 2022). Importantly, such decentralised organisations are guided by their purpose rather than by leaders’ directives, achieved through the implementation of four core principles (Table 1).

Methodology

In the BioAgora project we applied a design thinking approach and invited fellow researchers, practitioners, and policy experts from the European Commission (EC) and its background institutions to co-design the governance model of the EU’s future Science Service for Biodiversity (SSBD). Design thinking incorporates an iterative collaborative process where the applied research methods and facilitation tools stimulate both divergent and convergent thinking (Tschimmel 2012). Iterations help improve both the problem-framing and the solution-seeking phase. Therefore, feedback loops within and across the four steps allow switching between more divergent and more convergent thinking multiple times. The process of co-designing the governance model of the Science Service

This paper builds mainly on the analysis of expert interviews (a full report, explaining the whole process and the detailed analysis of all empirical data, is available on Zenodo—Kelemen et al. 2025). Semi-structured interviews were carried out between June 2023 and February 2024 with 16 key organisations, including international and European SPSIs, e.g. IPBES, Future Earth, ICES, and Biodiversa + from which the SSBD could draw inspiration, as well as potential future SSBD collaborators, such as different Directorate Generals of the EC and its background institutions. The interviews served both to gather information and to build strong partnerships; therefore, almost half of the interviews (n = 7) were run in a group setting. Thus, the interviews with the 16 key organisations included a total of 28 experts. Interviewed organisations were represented by colleagues from middle-management (i.e. heads of units, task forces, or specific programmes) who have a strategic vision of their organisation and an overview of the landscape, but still engage regularly in knowledge co-production. Interviews were carried out online and lasted between 40 and 120 min. Interviews were recorded and transcribed after the interviewees provided their consent (n = 11), while written notes were analysed in case recording was not consented (n = 5). Interview questions and the full list of interviewed organisations can be found in Supplementary Material.

Transcribed interviews were analysed qualitatively. First, a theory-driven coding structure was developed based on conceptual literature, comprising the four analytical dimensions we explained in the previous section. These four analytical dimensions were used as parent codes, each comprising several child codes. Second, this deductive coding structure was tested on three selected excerpts by three coders (EK, JY, KL-K), all coders coding all segments. After the test coding, the three coders convened to compare their individual usage of the codes, based on which the predefined codes were refined, some of them were merged, emerging (inductive) codes were added, and a commonly agreed coding procedure was developed. Third, the three coders coded the interviews (5 to 6 interviews allocated to each), using separate software (NVivo, Atlas.ti and MaxQDA). Coders met twice in this phase to cross-check the coded segments and to ensure intercoder reliability; then, they compiled all coded segments in an MS Excel document. Fourth, a final intercoder reliability check was done, and the mutually agreed coded quotations were integrated into the Atlas.ti software, allowing a joint analysis of all interviews. Fifth, a larger group of collaborators (EK, ZK, KF, KC, JY, KL-K, KK-K) was convened to interpret the results of the coding and identify emerging themes which cut across the four analytical dimensions.

Results of the interview analysis, findings from the desk research, and the outcomes of the workshops were synthesised in a Causal Loop Diagram (CLD) (Barbrook-Johnson and Penn 2022). CLDs are qualitative analytic tools of systems analysis making it possible to understand and visualize a complex system where long causal chains, nonlinear relationships, feedback loops and delays may be present. For this paper, we used the kumu.io software to prepare the CLD.

Organisational strategies as levers of transformation

The interview analysis enabled us to identify four organisational strategies to enhance the transformative potential of SPSIs. While these strategies mirror earlier insights from SPSI literature and are in line with the four principles of transformative change—justice, pluralism, adaptive learning, and reciprocal relations—as identified by IPBES (2024), interviews also shed light on challenges and potential reasons for resistance.

Pluralist and inclusive approaches—which offer recognition and avoid power concentrationSeveral interviewees highlighted the need to question the traditional view of (natural) science as the sole provider of knowledge in the SPSI to reflect the complexity of socio-environmental challenges and the diversity of needs and interests that shape policy outcomes. This claim is well-established in current SPSI literature: transdisciplinary integration of perspectives from both natural and social science, policy, and practice responds to demands for actionable knowledge by ensuring that academic insights are socially robust (Hofmann et al. 2025).Still, most interviewed organisations primarily engage scientific experts and government agencies, and approach businesses, NGOs and citizens less frequently. Interviewed experts trace back this failure to specific challenges such as time and resource constraints, imbalanced representation, missing incentive structures for participants, or low buy-in from policy actors. Critical case study research, though, warns that it is rather ontological and epistemic injustices which lead to a failure of inclusion (see, e.g. Subramanian et al. 2025). Theories of public policy monopolies reinforce that “established” knowledge holders often have a disinterest to diversify the knowledge pool (Baumgartner and Jones 1993). Potential ways forward should include true—and actor-specific—recognition for SPSI participation, as interviews suggested, but also formalised structures and processes against the concentration of power (Turnhout 2024).

Iterative knowledge-generation—which is agile and solution-focusedMany interviewees pointed out the importance of the SPSI process to be flexible, timely, and aiming to create actionable knowledge—which requires the process to operate in an iterative, co-creative manner, based on regular interactions between knowledge holders and policy makers. The advantages of knowledge co-creation is also emphasised in the literature, as being the best way to ensure the responsiveness of the process to the policy makers’ needs (Cockburn 2022).In practice, however, there is significant resistance from both sides towards this deep cooperation. According to our interviews, many policy-makers tend to view SPSIs as service machines, while academic knowledge holders often perceive policy makers’ active involvement as a threat to academic autonomy, independence and rigour. Transforming such tensions into collaboration requires co-productive agility, which includes willingness and competency to understand different viewpoints, responsiveness to frequently adjust goals and practices, as well as flexible but robust leadership, management and resource allocation (Chambers et al. 2022). Literature warns that such agile mechanisms are still rare for knowledge holders and policy-makers to openly exchange on conflicting issues, which prevents a better framing and tailored response to the needs and constraints of both science and policy (Sarkki et al. 2025).

Discussion

Complex system behaviour in the SPSI

To synthesise insights from the literature review, results from the interview analysis, and iterative discussions during the design-thinking workshops, a causal loop diagram was developed which showcases how specific governance interventions influence SPSIs’ (transformative) impact on policies (Fig. 2). The figures’ thematic categories include the outcomes of policy processes (turquoise), and external factors (purple), on top of the key analytical dimensions (indicated with blue, yellow and red colours) used for the interview analysis and presented in Table 1. Only elements explicitly mentioned during the encounters are included in the figure, while the connections between them reflect either direct references made by participants or logically inferred linkages (e.g. SPSI flexibility reduces answering time).

System map of the SPSI. Each node of the figure represents a significant element (phenomena) of the system, and each link a direct, causal connection between two elements, following the direction of the link. Solid lines mean that the two elements are directly proportional or “positively” correlated (they “move” or change in the same direction), and dashed lines mean an indirect proportionality or “negative” correlation between the two (they “move” or change in opposite directions). DM = decision-maker, SPSI = science–policy–society interface

Here, we highlight two examples that illustrate the interconnectedness of SPSI’s internal characteristics and its external context. The first concerns the frequency and quality of “SPSI-dialogues”, which are meaningful, facilitated encounters between SPSI actors. These encounters enable knowledge co-creation through direct exchange by fostering openness to societal perspectives, more equitable access to knowledge, and enhanced capacities to work at the SPSI. These effects can improve the uptake of scientific findings and ultimately lead to better policy outcomes. Removing this element and relegating scientists to the role of external service providers undermines these benefits.

The second example is the policy uptake of scientific results. Higher uptake not only enhances environmental outcomes but also strengthens scientists’ motivation to participate in the SPSI. Uptake depends on the timeliness and accessibility of scientific input as well as its credibility for policy-makers. This, in turn, reflects the SPSI’s quality of work and the commitment and capacity of its actors. Deterioration in flexibility, transparency, and institutional stability—even for external reasons—can considerably constrain the interface’s transformative potential.

These examples illustrate that SPSIs are shaped by numerous causal chains and feedback loops, driving the behaviour of the system in unexpected ways. Altering a single element in the system may generate considerable ripple effects across the interface, while reforming isolated elements may remain ineffective due to institutional rigidities or countervailing contextual factors. Achieving transformative policy outcomes therefore requires coordinated action across multiple dimensions, including processes, structures, and forms of knowledge involvement, among others.

What can sociocracy offer to SPSIs?

The synthesis above suggests that an agile organisation with opportunities for knowledge co-creation and democratic decision-making could suit the ethos of transformative SPSIs. One potential model for such a flexible and inclusive organisational structure is sociocracy (Eckstein 2016), which can accommodate all four organisational strategies identified in the Results section (Fig. 3). Circles—the basic structures of sociocratic organisations—engage actors with diverse knowledge and worldviews as equal partners (Surowieczki 2004), and thus open up to pluralist and inclusive approaches while also mitigating power imbalances. Consent-based decision-making in, and double-linking between, circles encourage experimentation and create structured feedback loops, therefore institutionalize iterative, solution-centred approaches and agile functioning (Romme 1995). Thanks to the rotation of specific roles within circles, and double-linking, positions can be detached from people, which prevents the centralisation of power and enhances trust beyond interpersonal relations (Surowiezcki 2004). Finally, electing the representatives of the circles in a consent-based manner instead of top-down appointment addresses power asymmetries and replaces hierarchies with horizontal partnerships (Schell and Bischof 2022).

The greatest challenge of a sociocratic SPSI governance structure is the same as its greatest advantage: its non-hierarchical, consent-based operation and decision-making process, which ensures the required flexibility and the inclusion of diverse knowledge systems, may be simply too far from what both academia and policymaking is accustomed to.

As some empirical examples show, power inequalities might hijack sociocratic decision-making if norms of equal participation are not institutionalised (King and Griffin 2024) and ethical guarantees are missing. While the close collaboration of policy, societal and scientific actors can increase the relevance and usability of SPSI outputs, it can also lead to simplifying knowledge co-production to a demand-driven, consultancy-style linear process, serving the needs of a few powerful actors.

Another emerging issue is that of time: decision-making even in matured sociocratic organisations may take longer than in a highly hierarchical one. Autocracy is very efficient in terms of timing, however, arguably not so much if we consider the efficacy of the decisions made—which is the main reason to look for an alternative governance framework. Nevertheless, as in an SPSI urgency is always a possibility, the risk of timing has to be considered.

Envisioning a sociocratic SPSI

In the last part of the Discussion section, we use the example of the SSBD to explain how a sociocratic governance model could work in practice (Fig. 4). Following the principles of sociocracy, its main governance bodies are represented as circles. Operative work is carried out by two management circles including paid coordinators who offer day-to-day management support, and unpaid voluntary experts who are recruited from thematic networks. The Request Management Team coordinates the co-production of knowledge outputs responding to policy or societal needs, while the Network Management Team facilitates active connection with relevant actors, including the knowledge holders. Voluntary experts in both circles would include a variety of knowledge holders, who are selected to represent gender, geographical and disciplinary diversity, and serve for a fixed term.

A sociocracy-based governance model for SPSIs. This figure shows a possible governance structure proposed for the SSBD, consisting of three central governing circles which are deeply rooted in existing thematic networks. Each circle is operated by diverse, voluntary members, serving for fixed terms (unfilled dots). The management circles, however, also include two paid co-coordinators who support day-to-day management (filled dots). The three circles are double linked (larger unfilled dots placed at each circle’s boundary). The black icon in the intersection of the circles represents the ethical ambassadors, a cross-cutting team advising the management bodies and the transformation and innovation board. Expert groups function as temporary bodies, emerging from the thematic networks upon request and working together in an agile and iterative way until their task is fulfilled

Each management circle elects one representative from its members to take part in the meetings of the strategic circle (the Transformation and Innovation Board), which ensures policy relevance and contributes to forecasting the future tasks of the SSBD. This strategic circle is composed of the representatives of the management circles, as well as of the representatives of key stakeholders of the SSBD (e.g. relevant Directorate Generals of the EC, key partners like Biodiversa + , other SPSIs like Eklipse, societal actors and NGOs). To ensure double-linking, two members of the strategic circle are elected as representatives in the two management circles. A special role which cuts across all three circles is the ethical ambassador—a small group of voluntary experts serving for a fixed term to ensure ethical compliance and support risk assessment.

Besides the three permanent circles, incoming requests and other tasks (e.g. capacity development or methodological support) are fulfilled by temporary expert groups. Members of expert groups are recruited through open calls and selected to represent diverse knowledge (going beyond academia), geographies, gender and age groups. Expert groups work in an agile and iterative way, i.e. they consult with the relevant policy and societal actors and co-produce the final output to ensure that actionable knowledge is harvested. Additionally, the three permanent circles are deeply embedded in active thematic networks organised around biodiversity-related topics. These networks can be existing scientific societies, clusters of research projects, but also practitioner and NGO networks specialised in certain topical areas. Thematic networks are crucial for the SSBD as they provide direct access to a large pool of relevant experts who can be mobilised to answer requests in the SSBD. In return, volunteering experts can benefit from having a direct say in policy development and including a diversity of societal and marginalised voices. The experience of providing policy relevant knowledge and creating close connections with different actors can be highly empowering. When transparent processes of inclusion are guaranteed and power imbalances are minimised, this form of reward is perceived as a key motivational factor and an enabler to enhance trust across the SPSI.

Decisions in the permanent and temporary bodies can be made in two different ways. Simple operative decisions that require timely reactions are based on established rules of procedure and get implemented automatically (e.g. managing an incoming policy request or recruiting a new expert group). Complex strategic decisions, which might entail ethical issues (e.g. future directions of the SSBD, public deliberation on certain issues, etc.), as well as decisions about leadership positions and representation, are based on consent, i.e. a decision is accepted if nobody in the given circle has a reasonable objection and the proposed solution is considered safe enough to try.

Conclusions

Recent literature on SPSIs and interviews with key actors suggests the need for greater emphasis on justice, pluralism, and rebalancing power relations. Sociocracy applies organisational solutions which align well with these ideas, although to our knowledge it has never been implemented at the boundaries of policy, society, and science. The governance model, suggested in this paper, offers a transparent, accountable and robust approach to manage science–policy–society interactions through established and tested rules of procedures; all the while enabling co-production, addressing power imbalances, and fostering inclusive and agile processes by applying sociocratic principles. Nevertheless, since this model is not yet tested, we only have assumptions on its transformative potential and fitness to context.

Due to geopolitical conflicts, the rise of populist governments, and the associated economic challenges, biodiversity seems to be deprioritised on the political agenda. Budget cuts in research and in the NGO sector might reduce the capacity of knowledge holders to voluntarily take part in the SPSI, while dominant financial interests might derail knowledge co-production processes. Additionally, institutional inertia and established governance practices might limit the capacity and openness of policy actors to participate in knowledge co-production processes.

Despite these challenges, it is worth considering alternative governance mechanisms that address the limitations of current SPSIs. This paper proposes one potential way forward: a governance model that applies sociocratic principles to foster inclusion and democratic decision-making through agile organisations while countering power imbalances. We invite fellow participants of SPSIs to critically reflect on these ideas, experiment with sociocratic principles when possible, and contribute to further debate on how such a governance model could be made functional. We believe that setting up and managing a sociocratic organisation at the boundaries of science, policy and society is possible and has transformative potential. Achieving this, however, requires openness and flexibility from all participating actors, along with a shared commitment to critically examine privileges and address existing power imbalances in both academia and policymaking.
