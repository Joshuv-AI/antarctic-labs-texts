Polysaccharide-functionalized gold, silver, and iron oxide nanoparticles for siRNA delivery: The role of artificial intelligence in design and optimization
Authors: Donya Esmaeilpour, Ponnurengam Malliappan Sivakumar, Arezoo Khosravi, Atefeh Zarepour, Muhammad Babar Taj, Ehsan Nazarzadeh Zare, Mika Sillanpaa, Hassan Karimi-Maleh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanoparticles (MFNPs), including gold (Au), silver (Ag), and iron oxide (Fe3O4), have emerged as promising nanocarriers for small interfering RNA (siRNA) delivery in cancer nanomedicine. Functionalization with polysaccharides enhances their biocompatibility, targeting capability, and therapeutic efficacy. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the design, mechanisms, and applications of polysaccharide-functionalized Au, Ag, and Fe3O4 nanoparticles as siRNA delivery vehicles, emphasizing the integration of Artificial intelligence (AI) for their optimization in cancer therapy. We systematically explore the types of metallic nanoparticles used, their functionalization strategies with various polysaccharides, and the mechanistic pathways involved in siRNA delivery. We further highlight the role of AI and machine learning (ML) in predicting nanoparticle stability, improving siRNA loading and release, enhancing targeting precision, and enabling personalized nanomedicine approaches. Polysaccharide-coated MFNPs demonstrate improved siRNA protection, cellular uptake, endosomal escape, and gene silencing efficiency. AI-driven facilitate the rational design of nanocarriers by predicting physicochemical properties, biological interactions, and patient-specific responses. Preclinical and emerging clinical evidence support their potential as next-generation cancer therapeutics. Polysaccharide-functionalized Au, Ag, and Fe3O4 nanoparticles, empowered by AI-based optimization, represent a cutting-edge strategy for siRNA delivery in cancer nanomedicine. Future research should focus on translating these nanocarriers into clinical applications through multidisciplinary collaboration and advanced computational tools.Keywords: Polysaccharide-functionalized nanoparticles, Cancer nanomedicine, Iron oxide nanoparticles, Gold nanoparticles, Silver nanoparticles, Artificial intelligence

Introduction

Cancer is characterized by uncontrolled cell proliferation in a specific region of the body, with the ability to invade other organs and tissues, ultimately leading to death. Early detection and timely treatment are therefore critical to prevent disease progression and reduce mortality. Among emerging therapeutic strategies, siRNA has attracted significant attention due to its ability to silence genes with high sequence specificity. siRNA is a double-stranded RNA molecule of about 20–25 nucleotides that functions through RNA interference (RNAi), where it binds to complementary messenger RNA (mRNA) sequences and promotes their degradation, thereby blocking translation. This mechanism enables the suppression of oncogenes, inhibition of proteins linked to multidrug resistance, and modulation of tumor-associated immune responses. Extensive research has explored siRNA applications in cancer therapy; however, despite its promise, siRNA faces critical challenges. In other words, following systemic administration, siRNA molecules are susceptible to rapid degradation by nucleases and clearance by the reticuloendothelial system. Their high molecular weight and strong negative charge hinder passive diffusion across cellular membranes, necessitating carrier-mediated delivery. After cellular internalization, siRNA must escape endosomal compartments to avoid lysosomal degradation and enable cytosolic release, where RNA-induced silencing complexes are active. Collectively, these barriers underscore the need for rationally designed delivery systems capable of protecting siRNA, facilitating cellular uptake, enabling endosomal escape, and ensuring controlled intracellular release [1,2].

In this context, nanotechnology-based delivery systems have emerged as a powerful strategy, offering protection against nuclease degradation, improved tumor targeting, and controlled intracellular release. Nanocarriers not only enhance siRNA stability but also promote efficient cellular uptake, facilitate endosomal escape, and minimize off-target effects, thereby maximizing therapeutic efficacy. By leveraging the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, nanoparticles accumulate at tumor sites and enable localized siRNA-mediated suppression of oncogenes or resistance-related pathways. The conjugation of nanocarriers with RNAi molecules further broadens opportunities for precise targeting of cancer mediators and facilitates the rational design of multifunctional delivery vehicles. A wide range of nanoplatforms has been engineered for siRNA delivery, including lipid nanoparticles, polymeric nanoparticles, dendrimers, hybrid systems, liposomes, lipoplexes, protein- and peptide-based carriers, metal–organic frameworks, and inorganic nanoparticles, each contributing distinct physicochemical features to optimize gene silencing [[3], [4], [5]]. In this context, metallic nanoparticles (MNPs), particularly Au, Ag, and Fe3O4 nanoparticles, have garnered significant interest as delivery platforms due to their unique physicochemical properties, ease of surface modification, and intrinsic therapeutic activities [6]. However, the inherent toxicity, aggregation tendencies, and lack of biocompatibility of bare metallic nanoparticles have necessitated innovative surface engineering approaches. Polysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanoparticles represent a next-generation strategy, utilizing the biocompatibility, biodegradability, and functional versatility of polysaccharides to overcome the biological barriers of nanoparticle-mediated siRNA delivery [7]. Natural polysaccharides such as chitosan (CS), hyaluronic acid (HA), dextran (Dex), and alginate (ALG) not only stabilize nanoparticle surfaces but also impart targeting capabilities, immune modulation, and enhanced circulation times (Fig. 1) [8]. Compared to synthetic polymers, polysaccharide coatings provide superior bioactivity and tunable interactions with biological systems, as evidenced by numerous in vitro and in vivo studies [9]. Despite promising results, critical gaps remain in the systematic optimization of polysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanocarriers, particularly regarding siRNA loading efficiency, intracellular trafficking, and tumor microenvironment responsiveness [10].

The advantages of utilizing polysaccharide-functionalized metal nanoparticles for siRNA delivery. Functionalizing nanoparticles with polymers could improve their bioavailability, biocompatibility, cellular uptake, prevent immune activation, and enhance their capability for siRNA loading.

So far, different review articles have been published about various aspects of polysaccharide-coated nanoparticles for drug or gene delivery. For example, Song et al. (2024) focused on polysaccharide-functionalized AuNPs for cancer imaging and therapy [11], while Serrano-Sevilla et al. (2019) provided an overview of polysaccharide-based nanocarriers for siRNA delivery without emphasizing metallic cores [12]. However, existing reviews often lack an integrative discussion of the mechanistic pathways, comparative evaluation across different metallic nanoparticles, and exploration of AI-driven optimization strategies (the application of artificial intelligence or machine learning algorithms to analyze multidimensional experimental datasets and to identify, prioritize, or refine nanoparticle design parameters (such as size, surface chemistry, or polysaccharide characteristics) that are subsequently validated through experimental synthesis and biological testing). Accordingly, this review aims to address these critical gaps by providing a comprehensive, mechanistically grounded, and AI-enhanced perspective on polysaccharide-functionalized Au, Ag, and Fe3O4 nanoparticles for siRNA delivery in cancer nanomedicine. Moreover, it critically examines the current state of AI in nanoparticle engineering and highlights its transformative potential in enhancing siRNA delivery performance and patient-specific therapeutic outcomes. Through a multidisciplinary and comparative approach, this review identifies key challenges and proposes future research directions to advance polysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanoparticles as intelligent nanocarriers for siRNA-based cancer therapy. Polysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanoparticles: mechanisms, interactions, and biomedical applications

Polysaccharide-functionalized MNPs represent a promising frontier in cancer nanomedicine, offering multifunctional platforms that integrate therapeutic delivery, imaging, and diagnostic capabilities [19]. The synergy between metallic cores and polysaccharide shells not only enhances the physicochemical stability and biocompatibility of nanoparticles but also facilitates specific interactions with biological targets through surface functional groups. Mechanistically, polysaccharides serve as both stabilizing and targeting agents, providing steric hindrance to prevent nanoparticle aggregation while simultaneously enabling receptor-mediated endocytosis in tumor cells [20]. These functionalized nanoparticles interact with cellular membranes through electrostatic and ligand-receptor interactions, improving internalization, and intracellular trafficking, ultimately enhancing the delivery of therapeutic payloads such as siRNA. The presence of different functional groups such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, sulfate, and amino groups on polysaccharide chains allows for further chemical modifications, enabling tailored functionalization strategies to modulate drug release profiles, enhance endosomal escape, and reduce off-target effects.

Metallic nanoparticles and cancer nanomedicine

MNPs represent a diverse and powerful class of nanomaterials in cancer nanomedicine, resulted from their unique physicochemical properties, ease of functionalization, and multifunctional capabilities [21]. Their composition, shape, size, and surface characteristics critically influence their biomedical performance. Among the vast array of MNPs, AuNPs, AgNPs, and Fe3O4NPs have emerged as pioneers for therapeutic and diagnostic applications in oncology (Fig. 2).

Applications of MNPs for cancer diagnosis and treatment using different methods. MNPs could provide the capability of cancer theranostic due to their intrinsic properties; such as inducing hyperthermia and MRI by magnetic nanoparticles, production reactive oxygen species (ROS) by AgNPs, and photothermal effect and stimuli drug release by AuNPs. Covering these NPs with polymeric shell could eliminate their limitations and enhance their biocompatibility, bioavailability, and selective accumulation in targeted site.

AuNPs have garnered significant attention due to their chemical inertness, excellent biocompatibility, and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) properties, which enable efficient photothermal conversion and optical imaging [22]. AuNPs can be synthesized in various morphologies, such as spheres, rods, cages, stars, each of them resulting in distinct optical and biological behaviors [23]. Their high surface-to-volume ratio facilitates efficient loading of therapeutic agents, including siRNA, while their tunable SPR allows for precise light-triggered drug release or photothermal ablation tissues. AuNPs, also serve as radiosensitizers, enhancing radiotherapy efficacy through secondary electron generation [24]. Importantly, their relatively low toxicity and FDA approval of certain gold-based formulation support their translational potential.

AgNPs, historically utilized for antimicrobial applications, have demonstrated emerging anticancer potential through multiple mechanisms, including generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondria disruption, and induction of DNA damage [25,26]. Their cytotoxicity towards cancer cells is dose- and size-dependent, offering opportunities for synergistic effects when combined with nucleic acid therapies. AgNPs exhibit inherent fluorescence, supporting imaging-guided delivery [27]. However, concerns regarding their long-term safety and off-target effects necessitate careful control of size, surface coating, and dose. Functionalization with polysaccharides such as CS or Dex improves their colloidal stability, reduces cytotoxicity toward normal cells, and enhances selective accumulation in tumor tissues.

Fe3O4, particularly superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs), provide unique magnetic properties that enable their use in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), magnetic hyperthermia, and magnetically guided drug delivery [28]. Their superparamagnetism ensures rapid magnetic response without residual magnetization, reducing aggregation risks in vivo. Fe3O4 NPs are FDA-approved for clinical imaging, underscoring their safety profile. Coating these NPs with polysaccharides such as Dex or ALG improves their hydrophilicity, reduces opsonization by serum proteins, and prolongs circulation time [29]. Additionally, polysaccharide-coated Fe3O4 NPs can be conjugated with targeting ligands (e.g., HA for cluster of differentiation 44 (CD44) targeting) or loaded with siRNA for magnetically targeted gene silencing in tumors [30].

Beyond these three primary MNPs, other metal-based nanostructures such as platinum, palladium, copper, and zinc oxide nanoparticles have been investigated for anticancer applications, as well. For instance, platinum nanoparticles (PtNPs) offer catalytic properties that mimic enzyme activities (nanozymes), facilitating tumor-selective ROS generation or prodrug activation [31]. Copper and zinc oxide nanoparticles also exhibit intrinsic cytotoxicity but face challenges in biocompatibility and clinical translation [32].

Each type of MNP presents distinct advantages and limitations in the context of cancer nanomedicine. AuNPs are highly effective in photothermal and photodynamic applications; AgNPs exhibit cytotoxic and antimicrobial dual roles; and Fe3O4 NPs integrate imaging, targeting, and hyperthermia functionalities. The selection of metallic core depends on the intended therapeutic mechanism, biodistribution requirements, and safety profile. Functionalization with polysaccharides enhances the biological interface of these nanoparticles, improving their solubility, immune evasion, and tumor specificity [33].

Recent studies have explored hybrid and core-shell architectures, combined different metals or incorporating inorganic and organic layers to integrate multiple functionalities in a single platform. For instance, Au-coated Fe3O4 NPs merge targeting with photothermal therapy, while Ag-Au bimetallic NPs exploit combined plasmonic and cytotoxic properties [34]. Such hybrid systems, when further functionalized with polysaccharides, enable synergistic therapeutic strategies including simultaneous siRNA delivery, photothermal therapy, imaging, and immune modulation.

Polysaccharides for nanoparticle functionalization in siRNA delivery

As mentioned earlier, polysaccharides have emerged as versatile and biocompatible biomacromolecules for the functionalization of metallic nanoparticles, providing critical advantages for the delivery of therapeutic compounds to the cancer cells [35]. Their natural abundance, biodegradability, low immunogenicity, and structural diversity enable tailored physicochemical and biological properties that address the key challenges of siRNA delivery, such as nuclease degradation, poor cellular uptake, and endosomal entrapment [15].

Structurally, polysaccharides offer abundant functional groups including hydroxyl, carboxyl, amino, and sulfate moieties that facilitate covalent or non-covalent interactions with metallic nanoparticle surfaces, enabling stable coating or conjugation [36]. This functionalization provides multiple roles: improving nanoparticle colloidal stability, modulating surface charge, providing steric hindrance against aggregation, reducing protein adsorption (the “stealth” effect), and enabling ligand attachment for active targeting [37].

So far, different polysaccharides have been used for functionalizing MNPs. For instance, CS get attention due to its intrinsic cationic nature at physiological pH, which promotes electrostatic interaction with negatively charged siRNA molecules [12] and so facilitate high loading efficiency while protecting siRNA from enzymatic degradation. This positive surface charge could also enhance cellular uptake via interacting with negatively charge of membrane (Fig. 3). Furthermore, chemical modification of CS (e.g., thiolation, quaternization, PEGylation) allows fine-tuning of solubility transfection efficiency, and pH-responsive release [38].

Producing different types of delivery nanosystem with enhanced properties using polysaccharides (like CS, Dex, and HA) for surface functionalized metal nanoparticles.

Dex, a neutral polysaccharide composed of glucose units, is widely employed for coating iron oxide NPs, especially for MRI application [39,40]. Dex provides a hydrophilic shell that minimizes NP aggregation, prolongs systemic circulation, and reduces recognition by the mononuclear phagocyte system. It could be modified using different strategies to enhance loading and delivery of therapeutic materials, including aldehyde activation, grafting of amine groups, or attachment of targeting ligands (e.g., folate, peptides) for receptor-mediated endocytosis in tumor cells [41]. Dex-coated NPs have also been utilized as scaffolds for multi-layered architectures, enabling co-delivery of different therapeutic agents (Fig. 3).

HA is a negatively charged polysaccharide exhibit active tumor-homing capacity via CD44-mediated endocytosis, improving site-specific delivery of therapeutic compounds while reducing off-target accumulation [42]. Furthermore, HA's enzymatic degradability in the tumor microenvironment (by hyaluronidase) allows stimuli-responsive release of therapeutics, enhancing therapeutic selectivity. In addition to direct surface adsorption or covalent linkage, HA can be chemically modified to introduced thiol or amine groups for stronger anchoring to metal surfaces or to enable cross-linking for improved stability (Fig. 3).

Other polysaccharides such as ALG, heparin, pullulan, and agarose have also been explored for functionalizing MNPs for delivery applications. For instance, ALG provides a pH-sensitive matrix that facilitates drug release in acidic tumor or endosomal environments, while heparin offers binding affinity for growth factors and cationic proteins, potentially modulating NP interactions with the tumor microenvironment [43]. Pullulan's stealth properties help prolong circulation, and sulfate polysaccharides contribute to anti-angiogenic or immunomodulatory effects synergistic with other therapies. Critically, the choice of polysaccharide impacts not only NP physicochemical stability but also biological performance. Parameters such as molecular weight, degree of substitution, branching, and charge density influence binding affinity, NP cellular uptake pathways, endosomal escape capability, and intracellular release kinetics. Therefore, polysaccharide selection and functionalization strategies must be tailored to optimize the balance between protection, targeting, and efficient cytoplasmic delivery of therapeutic materials [44]. Recent advances have combined polysaccharides with additional functional moieties (e.g., cell-penetrating, peptides, pH-responsive polymers, polyethylene glycol) to create hybrid coatings that synergize the advantages of different materials. Moreover, stimuli-responsive polysaccharide derivatives as redox-sensitive, enzyme-cleavable, or thermoresponsive systems are being developed to achieve spatiotemporally controlled release in response to tumor-specific cues [45].

Functionalization strategies of polysaccharide-coated metallic nanoparticles for enhanced therapeutic efficacy

As mentioned in previous section, the functionalization of metallic NPs with polysaccharides represents a key strategy to improve their therapeutic performance in cancer therapeutics. This process involves the strategic modification of the NP surface to enhance stability, biocompatibility, targeting specificity, and controlled release of drugs [46]. Various chemical and physical approaches are employed to achieve functionalization, tailored to the unique properties of both the polysaccharide and the metallic core. Covalent bonding methods, such as carbodiimide-mediated amide formation or thiol-gold linkages, are frequently utilized to ensure stable attachment of polysaccharide chains onto Au, Ag, and Fe3O4 NP surfaces, providing robust coatings that resist desorption under physiological conditions. Alternatively, non-covalent strategies including electrostatic adsorption, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions offer facile functionalization routes while preserving bioactivity, though they may exhibit weaker stability in vivo [47].

The introduction of reactive functional groups onto polysaccharide backbones expands their versatility in NP functionalization [48]. For example, carboxyl, aldehyde, or amine groups can be chemically introduced into CS, HA, or Dex to facilitate further conjugation of ligands, therapeutic agents, or stimuli-responsive moieties [49,50]. This enables the construction of multifunctional nanocarriers capable of simultaneous drug delivery, tumor targeting, and co-delivery of chemotherapeutics or imaging agents. Additionally, grafting synthetic polymers such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) onto polysaccharide-coated NPs imparts “stealth” properties by reducing opsonization and clearance by the reticuloendothelial system, thereby prolonging systemic circulation and enhancing tumor accumulation via the EPR effect [51]. Targeting ligand conjugation represents another critical functionalization strategy to improve the selectivity and efficacy of polysaccharide-functionalized nanoparticle. By coupling folic acid, peptides (e.g., RGD, TAT), antibodies, or aptamers to the polysaccharide shell, active targeting toward overexpressed receptors on cancer cells can be achieved, promoting receptor-mediated endocytosis and increasing intracellular delivery of therapeutic compounds [52].

Stimuli-responsive functionalization is an emerging strategy to trigger release of drug in response to tumor-specific conditions [53]. By incorporating pH-sensitive linkers, redox-sensitive disulfide bonds, enzyme-cleavable motifs, or photothermal agents into the polysaccharide coating, controlled release can be achieved within the acidic, reductive, or enzymatically active tumor microenvironment. For instance, thiolated CS coatings on gold nanoparticles enable glutathione-triggered disassembly and therapeutic release inside cancer cells with elevated intracellular GSH levels [54]. Similarly, hyaluronidase-sensitive HA coatings allow enzymatic degradation-triggered release specifically in CD44-positive tumors. Multilayered functionalization approaches, such as layer-by-layer (LbL) assembly, offer additional opportunities to fine-tune NP properties [55].

Alternative deposition of oppositely charged polysaccharides and polyelectrolytes on MNP cores enables precise control over surface charge, thickness, and loading capacity, while facilitating co-encapsulation of different therapeutic agents. These multilayer systems also improve endosomal escape by creating proton-sponge or osmotic swelling effects during intracellular trafficking. Importantly, the selection of functionalization strategy profoundly influences the physicochemical characteristics of the resulting nanocarrier such as hydrodynamic size, zeta potential, colloidal stability, and surface chemistry which in turn effect biological interactions including protein corona formation, biodistribution, cellular uptake pathways, and immunogenicity [56]. Therefore, functionalization must be optimized not only for therapeutic binding and protection but also for compatibility with downstream biological processes to ensure effective gene silencing with minimal off-target effects.

Application of polysaccharide-functionalized MNPs for siRNA delivery

Nanomaterial properties and siRNA delivery

siRNA has emerged as a highly promising tool in cancer therapy because it enables specific post-transcriptional silencing of genes that drive tumor growth, metastasis, drug resistance, and anti-apoptotic processes. Unlike traditional chemotherapy which can affect both healthy and cancerous cells, siRNA allows targeting of oncogenes or other cancer-associated genes with great precision, minimizing effects on non-diseased tissues. Another key advantage is its ability to work in synergy with existing treatments. siRNA can help reverse chemoresistance, enhance apoptosis, or reduce tumor-promoting pathways when used along with chemotherapy or targeted agents [67,68]. The delivery of siRNA as a therapeutic modality offers significant promise for the treatment of cancer, but its clinical translation is restricted by poor stability in biological fluids, rapid clearance, non-specific uptake, and inefficient cellular internalization and endosomal release. The RNases and phosphatases play a vital role in the degradation of siRNA. Once they reach systemic circulation, endonucleases and exonucleases degrade the siRNA, thereby preventing the siRNA from reaching the intended tissues [69]. siRNA also downregulates irrelevant mRNAs with partially complementary sequences, predominantly in the siRNA seed region, resulting in siRNA-mediated off-target activity [70]. In addition, naked siRNA can induce the Toll-like receptor-3 (TLR-3) activation and cause adverse effects on the blood and lymphatic systems [69]. Due to its anionic and hydrophilic nature, siRNA has difficulties crossing the cell membranes [71]. To address these limitations, metal nanoparticles (like gold, iron oxide, silver) have been explored as robust cores due to their favorable physicochemical properties including tunable size and shape, ease of surface functionalization, and unique optical, magnetic or catalytic features. When these metal nanoparticles are functionalized with polysaccharides such as CS, HA, Dex, or CS derivatives, the resulting hybrid systems combine biocompatibility, enhanced colloidal stability, and the ability to achieve targeted delivery (via receptor recognition), while also facilitating controlled release of siRNA [12,72,73]. These nanoformulations used different mechanisms to enhance cellular uptake of siRNA molecules, among the most prevalent of them are clathrin-mediated endocytosis, which is based on the interaction between the ligand present on the surface of NPs with the receptor present on the cell membrane, and caveolae mediated endocytosis, which involves flask-shaped membrane invaginations called caveolae which are rich in endothelial cells, adipocytes, fibroblasts, etc [74,75]. For instance, A layer-by-layer (LbL) technique was used to prepare CS-coated gold nanoparticles, and the LbL coating was established using signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) siRNA and CS to yield AuNP-CS/siRNA/CS nanoparticles. This nanocarrier was used to deliver siRNA ionophoretically to treat melanoma. The average nanoparticle of the LbL-AuNPs was found to be 150 ± 10 nm, and their cellular uptake was principally based on clathrin mediated endocytosis. The STAT3 siRNA-loaded LbL-AuNPs (AuNP-CS/siRNA/CS) showed decreased cancer cell viability, attributed to apoptotic induction in B16F10 cells as well as reduction of STAT3 protein expression [76].

NPs can also pass through the cell membrane via physical and biochemical processes which are including direct translocation (in which NPs disrupt the cell membrane and reach the cell; this process circumvents endosomal entrapment and energy dependent translocation, entry by lipid fusion (lipid bilayer decorated NPs interact and fuse with cell membrane and deliver the contents directly to the cytoplasm), electroporation (NPs cross cell membrane using pores that are formed through electroporation), and microinjection (using devices called microinjectors [77].

Different properties of nanoparticles, such as shape, size, rigidity, texture, charge, surface functional groups, and lipophilic/hydrophilic characteristics, could influence the specific pathways that is selected for cellular uptake. For instance, NPs with a size in the range of 10–50 nm were found to possess optimal cellular uptake due to their effective interaction with cell surface receptors and overcome rapid clearance by the RES [78]. NPs with a size between 60 and 200 nm undergo clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and particles with size larger than 200 nm undergo caveolae-mediated endocytosis, whereas larger particles with a diameter greater than one μm undergo macropinocytosis [79,80]. It was shown that the distribution of Au NPs inside the cells and tissues mainly occurred through passive uptake due to their smaller particle size, whereas Au NPs coated with ligands target specific endocytosis method [81].

Spherical NPs show higher cellular internalization efficiency than non-symmetrical NPs, such as nanostars and nanorods. This may be due to the enhanced surface area and interaction to the extracellular matrix (ECM) of nanostars and nanorods. On the other hand, spherical NPs are stable in suspension and less likely to aggregate. Thus, the cellular internalization of spherical NPs is high and is more likely to be distributed uniformly inside the cell. In contrast, non-spherical NPs tend to aggregate or have less potential for internalization, leading to uneven distribution inside the cells and also affecting their therapeutic potential [81].

Surface charge plays an important role in the cellular internalization of NPs, as well. In other words, since cell membrane contains anionic phospholipids, cationic NPs could more efficiently interact with it than neutral and anionic NPs. It was shown that the positively charge nanoparticles choose clathrin-mediated endocytosis, while the anionic NPs primarily internalized through caveolin-mediated endocytosis [82,83].

NPs entrapped in endosomal vesicles undergo lysosomal degradation or unproductive exocytosis, which hinders the delivery of siRNA and their therapeutic potential. If endosome disruption does not occur, the components trapped during endocytosis are degraded by the lysosome. Hence, carriers used for siRNA delivery should combat endosomal barriers to release siRNA in the cytoplasm, cellular organelles, and other compartments [77,84]. Endosomolytic agents can disrupt the endosomal membrane, including polymers, peptides, and small molecules. They act through various mechanisms, such as change in pH (pH-dependent change) between the extracellular and endosomal environments, which has been commonly used for potential endosomal disruption and siRNA release. Other endosomolytic mechanisms are based on structural changes and pore formation. In general, cationic polymers can interact with negatively charged phospholipids present in the endosomal membrane, resulting in the disruption of the endosomal membrane structure [85,86]. Different mechanisms are introduced for the endosomal escape of the cargos including:

Chloroquine (an anti-malarial agent), siramesine (a σ-2 receptor agonist), and amitriptyline (a tricyclic antidepressant) are small-molecule membrane-destabilizing compounds classified as cationic amphiphilic destabilizing (CAD) agents, which were used to investigate the enhanced endosomal escape of ligand-conjugated siRNA, specifically cholesterol-siRNA conjugates. The use of the small molecule resulted in enhanced ligand-conjugated siRNA delivery, thereby improving the targeted knockdown by up to 47-fold ([87]. Another CAD agent, namely, Ebastine (an antihistamine), was used to enhance siRNA delivery. These small molecules could also be incorporated to enhance therapeutic siRNA/mRNA delivery, such as with ligand-conjugated siRNA or lipid nanoparticles [88].

Cell penetration peptides are among the materials that could be used for the translocation of cargos through endosomal membranes. TAT (Trans-Activator of transcription) peptide and other arginine-rich cell-penetrating peptides (such as 2KH7-TAT, L17E, and L17ER4) are under this class, which could interact with the anionic bis(monoacylglycerol)phosphate (BMP), the structural isomer of phosphatidylglycerol that are principally present in late endosomes and lysosomes, via an electrostatic interaction that initiates fusion, leading to membrane leakage [88]. On the other hand, lytic peptides are amphipathic in nature and consist of 15-30 amino acids, where the hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues engage alternately. These are obtained naturally from defense toxins, antibacterial peptides, and viral fusion sequences or constructed artificially from the analogous protein. They act as membrane destabilizers by means of the insertion and rearrangement of lipid bilayers or the development of pores through peptide self-assembly [88].

Cationic polymers, such as polylysine (PLL), polyethyleneimine (PEI), and polyamidoamine (PAMAM) dendrimer, could reduce the endosomal membrane integrity and stimulate the formation of membrane holes. These exhibit a proton sponge effect that drives endosomal membrane disruption and leads to an influx of chloride ions and water into the endosomes, resulting in osmotic swelling and ultimately, endosomal membrane disruption [16]. These could be used in combination with polysaccharides during the fabrication of nanocarrier and so provide endosomal escape feature for the nanocarrier from one part, and improve its ability for loading higher amounts of siRNAs (with negatively charge) and promote therapeutic performance from the other side. On the other hand, positively charged polysaccharides like CSs could also induce endosomal escape. This was test in e study in which a layer-by-layer nanoplatform was produced via direct reduction of gold nanoparticles with CS that acted as both reducing agent and covering layer. This core-shell structure was then covered with a layer of siRNA molecules and another layer of CS that protected from the siRNAs from one side and provided positively charge for cell attachment and endosomal escape of fabricated carrier. The fabricated formulation showed high cellular uptake and high endosomal escape even better than Lipofectamine (Fig. 4) [57].

The other strategy is applying ionizable lipids which can protonate at lower pH levels, thus becoming positively charged, whereas they have neutral charge at physiological pH. The alteration of charge based on environmental pH is beneficial in the therapeutic nucleic acid delivery. In general, neutral lipids are less prone to interact with negatively charged membranes of blood cells. The neutral surface charge at pH 7.4 of ionizable cationic lipids (ICLs) enhances the blood circulation time by preventing rapid clearance by RES cells such as splenic macrophages and Kupffer cells [67]. At the endosomal environment due to the acidic pH (between 5.5 and 6.5), ICLs regain the positive charge and interact with negatively charged phospholipids present in the endosomal membrane results in the disruption of the endosomal membrane structure that facilitates the endosomal escape, and release of nucleic acid to the cytosol [85,86,89].

Polysaccharide-coated nanoparticles carrying siRNA are designed to enter cells and deliver their genetic cargo into the cytoplasm, where gene silencing occurs. These nanoparticles interact with the outer surface of cells either via electrostatic interactions between their positively charge and negatice charge of cellular membrane or via interactions with ligands like CD44. After entering the cell, siRNA therapeutics are transported through a series of compartments starting with early endosomes, then late endosomes, and finally lysosomes. During this maturation, the internal pH gradually decreases from near-neutral (around 7.4) to acidic levels (about 6.5in early endosome that reach to below 6.0 and below 5.0 in late endosome and lysosome). Moreover, there are different enzymes inside the lysosome such as nucleases and phosphatases that could degrade siRNA [90]. As mentioned in previous section, carriers which are considered for siRNA delivery are designed so that could escape from endosomes to prevent the effect of pH and enzymes on their sensitive cargo molecules. After reach to the cytoplasm, the important point is the release of siRNA in a controllable manner. Indeed, the release kinetics of siRNA from polysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanoparticles is a crucial factor influencing gene silencing efficiency and therapeutic outcomes. In this context, some strategies could be considered to release siRNAs in a controllable manner. Utilizing stimuli responsive polysaccharides or compounds in the structure of nanocarrier, like redox or temperature responsive compounds, or inducing external stimulation is among the main strategies that could be considered for controlling releasing kinetic of therapeutic materials. For instance, CS's pH sensitivity enables siRNA release in acidic environments such as endosomes or tumor tissue, while HA can degrade enzymatically in the presence of hyaluronidase, an enzyme overexpressed in many cancers. Similarly, redox-responsive linkers like disulfide bonds incorporated into the nanoparticle system break down in the reducing intracellular environment, leading to rapid siRNA release in the cytoplasm [[91], [92], [93]]. These strategies could enhance therapeutic performance of siRNAs through protecting them against enzymes and acidic pH and then released them in a controllable manner. In this context, nanocarrier could be designed so that loading higher amounts of siRNA molecules. For instance, engineering structure of carriers with positively charged compounds could enhanced loading amounts of siRNAs and so better silencing performance achieved [73].

Utilizing polysaccharide-functionalized MNPs for siRNA delivery

Recent studies demonstrate that polysaccharide‐coated or ‐conjugated metal nanoparticles can significantly improve gene silencing efficiency both in vitro and in vivo, reduce off-target toxicity, and allow for co-delivery of siRNA with chemo- or photothermal agents. For instance, CS polymer, grafted with diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) and thiol groups was functionalized on the surface of Au NPs and then siRNA molecules were loaded on it. In here, DIPEA was used to enhance the positive charge capacity of carrier for loading higher amounts of siRNA. The fabricated formulation showed high loading capacity, pH responsiveness, high cellular uptake, and good knockdown capability that appropriate it for further research studies (Fig. 5) [94]. Accordingly, this positively charged system could enhance the therapeutic performance via protecting siRNA molecules (via loading them), enhancing their cellular uptake, and providing the capability of endosomal scape.

The combination of Au NPs and PSP001, a polysaccharide extracted from the fruit rind of Punca granatum (pomegranate), was used to biosynthesis a pH responsive carrier (HsiRNA@PGD NPs) used for the codelivery of a chemotherapeutic compound, doxorubicin, and a gene, HER2 siRNA (human epidermal growth factor 2 siRNA) for breast cancer therapy. The presence of PSP001 not only acted as a part of carrier, but also enhanced the anticancer activity and immunomodulatory potential. Besides, presence of galacturonic acid groups in the structure of this polysaccharide provided the pH-sensitive release of therapeutic compounds from the HsiRNA@PGD NPs, so that it showed better drug release (about 90%) in acidic pH compare with less than 20% in normal pH. This NP could specifically internalize into the cancer cells, not normal cells, and exhibited significant anticancer activity, compared with control group and monotherapy samples, via inducing apoptosis along with the downregulation of the intended gene HER2. The combination of Au NPs, HA, and polyethyleneimine (PEI) were used to produce a targeted nanodrug delivery system (AuPEI-HA-DOX/siRNA) with the ability of multimodal therapy; chemo, photothermal, and gene silencing therapy. In here, Au NPs acted as photosensitizer and led to increase the temperature after utilizing radiation. On the other hand, DOX and siRNAs (produced against C-X-C chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) that is responsible for the invasion of cancer cells via interaction between cancer cells and microenvironment) were respectively applied as chemotherapy and gene therapy compounds. In details, Au-PEI conjugates were produced first and then tailored with HA, DOX, and CXCR4 siRNA. The nanoconjugates played a role in preventing siRNA degradation and enhancing its endosomal escape. Due to the presence of HA polysaccharide, this carrier could directly target the cancer cells with CD44 expression and endocytosed using receptor-mediated method. This led to a significant anticancer activity against breast cancer cells via enhancing drug release, CXCR4 inhibition, and photothermal effect, that was comparable with monotherapy alone that appropriate it for anticancer applications [96].

Matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) is one of the enzymes of extracellular matrix which is overexpressed in malignant tumors, and so could be considered as a target for designing enzyme-responsive drug formulations. This feature was used in a study to produce an enzyme responsive nanoplatform (AGC/HSP-70 siRNA complex) composed of Au nanorods, which were functionalized with the MMP-2 sensitive peptide (GPLGLAG) connected to the CS polymer (AGC). Besides, heat shock protein 70 silencing RNA (HSP-70 siRNA) was loaded into the fabricated formulation. The presence of MMP-2-sensitive peptide linker facilitated the deep tissue penetration of HSP-70 siRNA and accumulation of Au-nanorods in tumors. The enzyme-responsive linker undergoes enzymatic degradation in the presence of MMP-2. Presence of CS significantly enhanced the cellular uptake of the nanoplatform and the perinuclear distribution of HSP-70 siRNA in cancer cells. The fabricated formulation showed significant reduction in the expression level of HSP-70 proteins of 4T1 cells when they exposed with AGC/HSP-70 siRNA complex and MMP-2 enzyme, that was comparable with the results of cells that were just exposed with AGC/HSP-70 siRNA complex. On the other hand, Au nanorods could exhibit photothermal effect under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation. Therefore, the fabricated formulation induced cytotoxicity effect via promoting apoptosis resulted from the combination effects of gene therapy and photothermal therapy. The fabricated formulation showed significant reduction in the size of tumor in the 4T1 tumor ectopic mice mode treated with AGC/HSP-70 siRNA complex and NIR irradiation, while had no effect on the main organs. Hence, these complexes could be a useful vector for treating deep tumour cells through improved photothermal therapy [97].

Survivin, a key protein involved in preventing cell death, is often overexpressed in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), making it a promising therapeutic target. Silencing the survivin gene using siRNA has shown potential to induce cancer cell death. However, delivering siRNA effectively to tumor cells remains a major challenge due to its instability in the body. This led to produce a targetable nanoparticle using Folic acid, as targeting compound, SPIONs, amylose, and siRNA. The folate-functionalized, SPIO-loaded, and siRNA-complexed magnetic cationic amylose nanoparticles could effectively condense with siRNA to form stable nanoparticles, while also demonstrating high transfection efficiency [98].

Utilizing bipolar polymers is another strategy for delivery of siRNA by the nanocarriers. In this context, the combination of Fe3O4 nanoparticles with CS, polycaprolactone (PCL), and polyethylene glycol (PEG)- HA were used to produce nanocapsules for the targeted delivery of therapeutic compounds. It was a pH-responsive delivery capsule which was loaded with paclitaxel (PTX) and siRNA-FAM as therapeutic compounds. Presence of Fe3O4 nanoparticles and HA provided active targeting ability for the nanocarrier and enhance its therapeutic effect via enhancing the accumulation of siRNA and PTX inside the cells. On the other hand, CS could supply mucoadhesive and antibacterial feature along with the polyelectrolyte complexes production with PCL/PEG anionic degradation byproducts. The fabricated formulation showed controlled release behavior along with significant anticancer activity against MCF-7 cancer cells, and superior delivery of siRNA-FAM [99].

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a pivotal role in cancer development, growth and metastasis. TME is a complex, dynamic, and heterogeneous network that contains tumor cells, immune cells and other environmental accessory supports. The environmental accessory support includes non-transformed stromal cells (fibroblasts, adipocytes, pericytes, and endothelial cells), blood vessels, signalling molecules, and extracellular matrix [100,101]. Upon tumor progression, stromal cells are recruited from the surrounding non-tumorous tissues and from the bone marrow to help the developing neoplastic phenotype. The communication between tumor and TME cells plays a crucial role in tumor progression and metastasis. Notably, the chemokines and cytokines secreted by the tumor cells can modify markers on the endothelial cell surface and stimulate tumor tolerance [102]. Apart from tumor progression and metastasis, TME plays other crucial roles in immune evasion, tumor cell migration, therapeutic resistance and angiogenesis. Hence, the removal of tumor cells alone is insufficient to answer the issue. The regulation of TME to convert it into an environment that is inappropriate for tumor cell survival and expansion could be an effective therapeutic strategy and further minimize the chances of recurrence [103].

The TME has characteristic environmental conditions compared to the normal tissue environment. These characteristic differences in the environment could be used in the design/treatment approaches of tumor cells that include, hypoxia (relieve or promote tumor hypoxia), acidic pH (pH sensitive and pH regulatory strategies), high redox potential (redox sensitive and ROS regulatory strategies), high ATP levels (ATP sensitive and ATP regulatory strategies), complex stromal cells (regulation of tumor stromal cells such as T-cells, cancer associated fibroblasts (CAFs), Antigen presenting cells (APCs), tumor associated macrophages (TAMs), and dense extracellular matrix (regulation of collagen and HA) [104,105]. Nanomedicines employ the above strategies for targeted delivery of drugs or gene transcripts to the TME.

Hypoxia is characterized in TME because the tumour cell expansion rate is greater than the angiogenesis rate. The tumour hypoxia also stimulates cells to deliver hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF-1 α), which could also be considered for siRNA therapy in cancer. Two strategies could be used to regulate the hypoxic condition of TME, such as alleviating or elevating tumour hypoxia [103,104]. A recent study demonstrated that CS-HA nanoparticles loaded with HIF-1α siRNA can effectively knock down HIF-1α gene expression and overcome hypoxia-driven treatment resistance in pancreatic cancer. The siRNA-loaded nanoparticles showed therapeutic potential in targeting pancreatic cancer that is characterized by CD44 expression and hypoxic TME [106].

Upon tumour progression, tumour cells mainly rely on anaerobic glycolysis that results in the accumulation of lactic acid in the tumour environment and thus lower pH in TME. The pH of normal tissues is neutral (7.2-7.4), whereas the TME is acidic (6.6-7.0). This acidic pH was hired for the production of smart delivery systems which are sensitive to acidic pH, and so could release therapeutic compounds in response to this acidic condition. For example, hyaluronic nanocapsules were produced in a research, using inverse mini-emulsion method and loaded with doxorubicin. The fabricated formulation showed higher drug release at pH 6.6, that was used to mimic microenvironment of cancer cells, compared with normal pH, along with a significant anticancer activity against MCF7 breast cancer cells [107].

It has been found that the weak acidic pH of tumor microenvironment can enhance tumour metastasis and immunosuppression. Hence, an alteration in the low pH of TME could effectively achieve potential tumour therapy that could be achieved via producing pH sensitive drug delivery systems (ionizable chemical groups such as phosphoric acid, amino group, and carboxylic acid and acid-intolerant chemical bonds such as acetal/ketone bonds, imino bonds, hydrazone bonds, and primoester bonds are used for efficient drug delivery) or by utilizing pH-regulating nanomaterials, like proton pump inhibitors, to modulate the pH of TME [103].

This includes various nanomaterial-based regulations, such as the depletion or reprogramming of CAFs, polarization of TAMs into antitumor M1-type cells, combining nanomaterials with immune checkpoint blockade therapy to regulate T cell activity and enhancing the immune response.

Cancer cells can reprogram inactive fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, which are known as CAFs, thereby facilitating tumor growth. CAF helps in cell proliferation through the secretion of cytokines (chemokines (CXC-12, CXC-14) and, interleukins (IL-6), etc), growth factors (GFs), and ECM, thereby forming a barrier that prevents therapy and immune escape. Regulation of CAF is accomplished by either consuming CAF or reprogramming them into a dormant state [103,108]. For example, a type of siRNA loaded liposome-HA-protamine nanoparticle (LPH NP) was produced via complexing siRNA targeting Wnt16 with HA, followed by condensation using the cationic protein protamine to form negatively charged polyplexes, which were then coated with cationic lipids through electrostatic interactions and PEG. To enhance uptake by fibroblasts and achieve cell-specific silencing of Wnt16, anisamide was attached to the surface of LPH nanoparticles, promoting targeted internalization. The fabricated formulation could significantly knockdown Wnt16 and so prevented growth and invasion of the cancer cells. Here, utilizing cationic protein along with other coating agents improved loading capacity and colloidal stability of siRNA while protecting it against enzymatic degradation. On the other hand, presence of HA improved its cellular uptake and enhanced therapeutic performance [109].

The TAMs are primarily derived from peripheral blood monocytes and are classified into two main types: antitumor M1 (proinflammatory and antitumorigenic) and pro-tumor M2 (involved in metastasis and tumor growth), based on their phenotype and function. The M2 phenotype is displayed in tumor tissues. The repolarization or reprogramming of the M2 phenotype to the M1 phenotype is a potential therapeutic modality in cancer immunotherapy. Inhibitor kappa B kinase β-siRNA (IKKβ-siRNA) was used in the polarization of TAMs in cancer immunotherapy. The IKKβ-siRNA demonstrated efficient polarization of M2 TAMs to M1 TAMs in both in vitro and in vivo and also showed enhanced antitumor activity [110].

T-cells secrete cytokines that can trigger apoptosis and inflammatory reactions, or directly attack the intended cells after processing information obtained from innate immune antigens [111]. The improper functioning of T-cells could result in tumor immune escape, reduction in the immune response, and decrease in the response rate to immunotherapy [112]. Adoptive T cell therapy (CAR-T) and immune checkpoint inhibitors have the potential to modulate T-cell function [112]. The immunosuppressive TME limits the anti-tumor immunity, believed to pose a significant challenge in cancer treatment. One such mechanism includes the upregulation of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) in tumors. The receptor-ligand interactions of PD-1 protein on activated T-cells and PD-L1 on tumor cells control the efficiency of CAR-T cell treatment in tumors that respond poorly [113]. The combination of HA, thiolated chitosan (TC), HIV-1-derived TAT peptide, and trimethyl chitosan (TMC) were used to produce a targeted nanoparticles for delivery of two types of siRNAs applied against PD-L1 and STAT3. The fabricated nanosystem showed significant reduction in melanoma cancer growth due to the reducing level of PD-L1 and STAT3 genes, as well as prevention of angiogenesis and cancer migration [114].

According to the above-mentioned features, polysaccharide-functionalized metal nanoparticles have emerged as versatile and powerful platforms for siRNA-based cancer therapy by combining stability, targeted delivery, and multifunctional therapeutic potential. The discussed studies collectively highlight how rational integration of polysaccharides such as CS, HA, amylose, and PSP001 with gold and iron oxide nanoparticles enables enhanced siRNA loading, protection from degradation, improved endosomal escape, and selective accumulation within tumors. These hybrid nanocarriers not only facilitate efficient gene silencing but also allow synergistic co-delivery of chemotherapeutic agents and the incorporation of photothermal modalities, thereby amplifying anticancer efficacy. Moreover, tailoring these nanostructures to respond to tumor-associated cues, such as acidic pH, redox potential, enzymatic activity, and overexpressed receptors, further enhances specificity and minimizes off-target effects. Beyond direct gene silencing, these systems demonstrate the ability to remodel the tumor microenvironment by targeting cancer-associated fibroblasts, reprogramming tumor-associated macrophages, and regulating immune checkpoints, ultimately overcoming therapeutic resistance. Collectively, these findings establish polysaccharide-coated metal nanoparticles as a promising next-generation nanoplatform for multimodal, precise, and personalized cancer therapy through siRNA delivery. Recent advances in AI and ML offer new avenues to guide the rational design and optimization of functionalization strategies. By integrating predictive modeling with experimental data, AI-driven approaches can identify optimal combinations of polysaccharide types, functional groups, ligand densities, and stimuli-responsive elements to maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing toxicity and immune clearance. Such computationally assisted design frameworks hold promise for accelerating the translation of polysaccharide-functionalized metallic NPs from bench to besides, particularly in the context of personalized nanomedicine.

AI-driven design and optimization of polysaccharide-functionalized MNPs for siRNA delivery

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into the design and development of polysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanoparticles (MNPs) offers a transformative approach for improving siRNA delivery in cancer nanomedicine. Traditional nanoparticle synthesis and optimization strategies largely rely on iterative trial-and-error experimentation, which is time-consuming and resource-intensive. In contrast, AI enables predictive modeling and data-driven optimization by systematically exploring high-dimensional physicochemical parameter spaces that govern nanocarrier efficacy and safety [115]. In this context, AI methodologies can be broadly categorized into predictive, data-driven models and mechanistically informed or physics-guided approaches, each serving distinct yet complementary roles in nanocarrier design. Predictive models leverage experimental and computational datasets to identify statistical relationships between nanoparticle descriptors, such as size, surface charge, polysaccharide composition, and metal core properties, and biological outcomes including siRNA loading efficiency, colloidal stability, cellular uptake, and gene silencing performance. While these approaches are effective for parameter prioritization and rapid formulation screening, they are primarily correlation-based and often provide limited mechanistic interpretability [116].

Mechanistically informed or physics-guided AI approaches, in contrast, incorporate established physical, chemical, or biological principles, such as electrostatic interactions, diffusion-controlled release, nanoparticle aggregation behavior, or intracellular trafficking pathways, into model architectures or constraint definitions. By embedding domain knowledge, these hybrid strategies can enhance model interpretability, improve robustness, and reduce dependence on large datasets, which is particularly advantageous for complex siRNA delivery processes [117]. Together, these AI paradigms enable a more rational and systematic design of polysaccharide-coated Au, Ag, and Fe3O4 nanoparticles for siRNA delivery, integrating design considerations from nanoparticle synthesis and surface functionalization to intracellular delivery dynamics and therapeutic optimization (Fig. 7).

The role of AI in nanocarrier design and functionalization

In polysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanoparticles designed for siRNA delivery, AI-driven design is particularly valuable for managing the complex interdependence between metallic core properties, polysaccharide chemistry, and siRNA condensation efficiency. Designing efficient siRNA nanocarriers beings at the molecular and structural level. AI-driven platforms enable researchers to predict optimal NP parameters such as size, shape, surface charge, and core-shell architecture that influence cellular uptake, biodistribution, and intracellular trafficking [127]. For instance, using supervised ML algorithms, scientists can model how variations in polysaccharide type (e.g., CS, Dex, HA) or molecular weight affect NP surface chemistry and ligand-binding efficiency [128].

Moreover, AI tools such as genetic algorithms and deep neural networks are used to optimize the functionalization strategies of Au, Ag, and Fe3O4 NPs with targeting ligands, PEGylation, and pH-responsive coatings [129]. These models simulate interactions between siRNA and polysaccharide coatings to ensure effective electrostatic or covalent encapsulation without compromising NP integrity. AI further helps in selecting or engineering biodegradable linkers and stimuli-responsive groups that enhance endosomal escape or siRNA release in response to the tumor microenvironment [130,131]. Through iterative learning, these models can adapt based on experimental data, continuously improving design outcomes and reducing reliance on trial-and-error experimentation. For example, the experimentally observed dependence of cellular uptake and endosomal escape on nanoparticle size, shape, and surface charge reported in section 3.1 can be directly incorporated into supervised ML models to identify optimal design windows for polysaccharide-functionalized Au and Fe3O4 nanocarriers.

AI-guided prediction of nanoparticle stability, biocompatibility, and bioavailability

For polysaccharide-functionalized MNPs, colloidal stability and biocompatibility are strongly influenced by polysaccharide molecular weight, degree of substitution, and surface heterogeneity, rendering empirical optimization inefficient without AI-based predictive models. Stability, biocompatibility, and bioavailability are foundational to the success of any siRNA nanocarrier [132,133]. AI offers a predictive framework to anticipate how polysaccharide-functionalized MNPs will behave in physiological environments. By training ML models on databases continuing physicochemical properties, zeta potential, hydrodynamic diameter, and coating thickness, researchers can predict NP aggregation behavior, opsonization potential, and degradation kinetics in serum or tissue fluid [134].

In terms of biocompatibility, AI algorithms, such as support vector machines (SVM), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and random forest (RF) classifiers can be trained on cytotoxicity and hemolysis data to assess the likelihood of adverse immune responses. These models can also identify combinations of polysaccharides and surface coatings that minimize macrophage uptake while maintaining therapeutic efficacy [135].

Bioavailability predictions, incorporating ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion) parameters, are enhanced using physiologically-based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling integrated with ML. These systems simulate in vivo dynamics and allow for early-stage screening of NP candidates that possess favorable circulation half-life, organ distribution, and tumor accumulation. Thus, AI works as a critical tool for optimizing safety and therapeutic performance before in vivo validation [136]. AI-driven stability prediction is therefore discussed here specifically in relation to how polysaccharide coatings modulate serum interactions, aggregation behavior, and siRNA protection in metallic nanocarriers.

Machine learning approaches for siRNA loading, encapsulation, and controlled release

siRNA loading and release in polysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanoparticles depend on non-linear interactions between polysaccharide chemistry, surface charge density, and siRNA physicochemical properties, making these processes well-suited for machine learning–based modeling. Efficient siRNA delivery depends not only on successful loading into the nanocarrier but also on achieving a controlled and timely release within the target site [137]. ML approaches provide data-driven optimization for siRNA encapsulation processes. Regression models and deep learning (DL) techniques analyze variables such as siRNA sequence, secondary structure, polysaccharide charge density, and NP porosity to predict encapsulation efficiency and binding affinity [119]. Experimental findings discussed in section 3.2 such as the enhanced siRNA loading capacity of chitosan and PEI-assisted systems or the pH-responsive release behavior of HA and alginate-based coatings, provide high-quality training data for ML models aimed at optimizing siRNA encapsulation efficiency and controlled intracellular release.

Furthermore, release kinetics especially under physiological or tumor-specific stimuli can modeled using algorithms such as Gaussian process regression or recurrent neural networks (RNNs) [138]. These models predict how environmental factors such as pH, temperature, or enzyme concentration modulate the degradation of the polysaccharide shell or linker molecules. For example, in acidic tumor environments or lysosomal compartments, pH-responsiveness coatings like CS, or ALG can be triggered to disassemble, releasing siRNA [139,140]. AI models simulate this degradation and release behavior, aiding in the formulation of nanocarriers that deliver siRNA at the optimal rate to maximize gene silencing while minimizing off-target effects. Additionally, multi-objective optimization frameworks allow researchers to simultaneously improve loading efficiency, stability, and release profile a balancing act that would be infeasible using conventional methods alone. In this context, AI is not applied generically to nucleic acid delivery, but specifically to predict and optimize siRNA encapsulation efficiency and release kinetics unique to polysaccharide-coated MNP systems.

AI-enhanced targeting strategies for precision siRNA delivery

As demonstrated in several experimental systems reviewed in section 3.2, including HA-mediated CD44 targeting and enzyme-responsive peptide polysaccharide hybrids, targeting efficiency is strongly influenced by ligand density and spatial arrangement. AI-based optimization frameworks can quantitatively model these parameters to maximize receptor-mediated uptake while minimizing off-target interactions. The heterogeneity of tumor tissues and the presence of biological barriers demand that siRNA nanocarriers be precisely targeted to maximize therapeutic benefits [141]. AI contributes significantly to the development of active targeting strategies by integrating omics data, tumor receptor profiles, and imaging datasets [142]. For example, unsupervised ML algorithms can cluster cancer cell lines based on receptor expression, enabling the rational selection of surface ligands such as HA for CD44 or folate for folate receptors on the NP coating [143].

DL models trained on molecular docking data and ligand-receptor interaction datasets can also recommend optimal linker configurations and multivalent binding sites to enhance binding affinity and internalization [144]. Moreover, AI facilitates the use of computer vision tools to analyze in vivo imaging data (e.g., MRI, Positron Emission Tomography (PET), fluorescence imaging) and predict tumor targeting efficiency, off-target accumulation, and clearance profiles [145]. Through these capabilities, AI empowers the design of “smart” polysaccharide-functionalized MNPs that accumulate into specific tumor subtypes, penetrate deep into solid tumors, and adapt to dynamic changes in the tumor microenvironment, thereby enabling more effective siRNA-based gene silencing. AI-based targeting models discussed in this subsection are therefore framed around polysaccharide-mediated ligand density, spatial presentation, and their impact on siRNA delivery efficiency rather than generic targeting paradigms.

Computational models of nanoparticle cell interactions and intracellular dynamics

Understanding how NPs interact with cells and deliver siRNA to the cytoplasm is crucial for therapeutic success [146]. The intracellular trafficking pathways and endosomal escape mechanisms described experimentally in section 3.1, including clathrin-mediated endocytosis, proton-sponge effects, and polysaccharide-assisted membrane destabilization can be incorporated into computational and AI-driven models to simulate nanoparticle cell interactions and predict siRNA bioavailability at the cytoplasmic level. These models integrate data from live-cell imaging, confocal microscopy, and flow cytometry to simulate how polysaccharide-coated Au, Ag, and Fe3O4 NPs navigate through the endo-lysosomal system [147].

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, combined with AI, can model nanoscale interactions between the NP surface and cellular membrane, highlighting potential for fusion, pore formation, or membrane destabilization. Predictive algorithms also assess the probability of endosomal escape on surface chemistry, pH responsiveness, and charge reversal mechanisms [148,149].

Once in the cytoplasm, siRNA must be released in a bioactive form to interact with the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) [150]. AI models simulate this process and predict gene knockdown efficiency based on siRNA sequence, local concentration, and degradation rates [151]. Together, these computational tools allow for iterative optimization of the entire intracellular delivery pathway, reducing experimental time and accelerating clinical translation. Accordingly, AI-based cellular interaction models are discussed here in relation to how polysaccharide coatings influence cellular uptake, endosomal escape, and intracellular siRNA release from metallic nanoparticles.

AI in personalized nanomedicine: predicting patient-specific nanocarrier responses

Personalization of siRNA delivery using polysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanoparticles relies on matching patient- or tumor-specific biological features with formulation-dependent polysaccharide and nanoparticle descriptors, a task well suited to AI-based integration. One of the most promising aspects of AI in siRNA nanomedicine is its potential to drive personalization. Tumor heterogeneity, genetic variations, and differences in immune response necessitate patient-specific strategies. AI enables such customization by integrating multi-omics data gnomic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic with clinical records and imaging data to predict individual responses to siRNA-loaded MNPs [152].

Through ensemble learning and neural network models, researchers can stratify patients based on predicted sensitivity or resistance to siRNA therapies, guide the selection of nanoparticle compositions, and personalize dosing schedules [153]. For example, a patient with high expression of a specific oncogene could be matched with a siRNA sequence and a polysaccharide-coated NP optimized for delivery to cells expressing that marker.

Moreover, digital twins models, which simulate patient physiology and treatment outcomes in silico, are being explored for real-time monitoring and adaptive treatment strategies [154]. AI also facilitates post-treatment analysis by correlating delivery metrics with therapeutic outcomes, enabling continuous refinement of NP design for future use. Despite the growing interest in artificial intelligence for nanomedicine, direct applications of AI or machine learning to polysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanoparticles for siRNA delivery remain scarce. To date, there are no published studies demonstrating AI-guided optimization of polysaccharide grafting density, surface chemistry, or metal core selection in MNP-based siRNA systems, nor comparative analyses in which AI-based predictions are systematically validated against experimental gene silencing outcomes. This absence reflects both the limited availability of structured datasets for polysaccharide-coated MNPs and the complex, multivariate nature of polysaccharide-siRNA-metal interactions, which complicates model training and validation. Therefore, current discussions of AI in this context should be viewed as prospective and conceptual, highlighting future opportunities rather than established capabilities.

Clinical and preclinical advances of polysaccharide-functionalized gold, silver, and iron oxide nanoparticles in siRNA cancer therapy

Preclinical investigations have shown that MNPs functionalized with polysaccharides are highly effective carriers for siRNA delivery in cancer therapy. CS and HA–modified AuNPs have demonstrated strong siRNA binding capacity, protection against nuclease degradation, and efficient cellular uptake, leading to significant gene silencing in tumor models. The polysaccharide coatings not only enhance colloidal stability and biocompatibility but also facilitate tumor-specific targeting through receptor-mediated pathways. These attributes have been validated in vitro and in vivo, where polysaccharide-functionalized AuNP systems exhibited enhanced antitumor efficacy and reduced off-target effects compared to unmodified formulations [13,57].

Polysaccharide-coated AgNPs have been used to efficiently condense and transport siRNA, resulting in potent gene knockdown and apoptosis induction in cancer cells. In parallel, polysaccharide-functionalized Fe3O4 NPs have demonstrated successful siRNA loading and delivery while enabling magnetic guidance and imaging support. These systems have been tested in preclinical tumor models, confirming their ability to enhance siRNA stability, promote tumor accumulation, and achieve effective gene silencing, thus underscoring their translational promise as multifunctional nanocarriers in oncology [30,60,65].

Although significant progress has been made in achieving targeted siRNA delivery both in vitro and in vivo, clinical translation remains a considerable challenge. Recently developed polymer-based delivery systems have demonstrated satisfactory in vivo efficacy; however, their safety profiles still require optimization. Advancing siRNA from laboratory research to clinical use demands robust, reproducible, and scalable manufacturing approaches [155]. In the case of polymeric systems used for siRNA delivery, the first clinical example was CALAA-01, which utilized a cyclodextrin-based delivery system with PEG stabilization and transferrin-mediated targeting for cancer therapy. It delivered siRNA against RRM2, a gene linked to poor cancer prognosis, and showed promising tolerance in preclinical studies. However, phase 1b trials revealed dose-limiting toxicity, likely due to instability of the transferrin-targeting component, leading to trial withdrawal [156,157].

Subsequently, siG12D-LODER represented another polymeric-siRNA formulation that advanced to clinical evaluation. Local Drug EluteR (LODER), developed by Silenseed Ltd., is a biodegradable poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA)-based implant delivering anti-KRASG12D siRNA for treating locally advanced pancreatic cancer. It provided prolonged, localized siRNA release within tumors, protecting it from degradation while avoiding systemic side effects. Having passed phase 1/2a trials, it is now in phase 2, demonstrating safety and efficacy in combination with standard PDAC therapies [158]. NU-0129, a gold nanoparticle–siRNA conjugate designed to silence the GBM oncogene Bcl2L12, with PEG/OEG coatings enhance stability and circulation, showed promising results in a phase 0 trial (NCT03020017). Intravenous administration led to significant reduction in Bcl2L12 expression in glioma cells, confirming effective brain accumulation and target engagement in glioblastoma patients [159].

In the case of siRNA-loaded CS nanoparticles, multiple challenges remain related to delivery efficiency, biological stability, and safety, which must be addressed for successful clinical translation. While CS offers promise as a biocompatible carrier for targeted gene silencing, the tumor microenvironment, characterized by elevated interstitial pressure and a dense extracellular matrix, limits effective nanoparticle penetration and accumulation at the tumor site. Additionally, strict regulatory requirements demand comprehensive characterization and batch-to-batch consistency, complicating large-scale production. Current manufacturing approaches face difficulties in maintaining the uniformity and stability required for clinical-grade formulations, though advanced techniques such as microfluidics and automated synthesis are being explored to improve scalability and quality control. Despite the protective effects of CS on siRNA payloads, systemic delivery remains hindered by rapid immune clearance [160].

Despite encouraging preclinical findings, several barriers continue to limit clinical translation of polysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanoparticles for siRNA therapy. Manufacturing scalability remains a key concern, as the reproducible synthesis of complex nanostructures with uniform surface functionalization is difficult to achieve. Emerging solutions include the use of microfluidic-assisted nanoprecipitation and continuous-flow synthesis for batch consistency and real-time process control. Safety and toxicity profiles also require thoroughly assessment, since surface charge, metal ion, and long-term tissue accumulation may elicit unforeseen biological responses. Incorporating biodegradable coatings, optimizing polysaccharide molecular weights, and performing long-term biodistribution studies are crucial steps toward safety validation. Regulatory hurdles, particularly for AI-designed nanomedicines, add another layer of complexity. Establishing transparent validation frameworks, standardized data reporting, and collaboration with regulatory bodies can facilitate pathway approval. Finally, targeting efficiency in humans often lower than in preclinical models can be improved through patient-specific nanoparticle customization, guided by AI and ML tools that predict receptor profiles and optimize ligand density.

To advance clinical utility, continuous refinement of nanoparticle design and interdisciplinary collaboration will be essential. Innovations in nanotechnology, computational modelin, and tumor biology are expected to support the development of multifunctional delivery systems capable of overcoming biological barriers and precisely targeting tumor cells, ultimately improving the prospects of CS-based siRNA nanocarriers in cancer gene therapy [161].

Challenges and limitations

Despite the growing interest in artificial intelligence as a tool for accelerating nanomedicine development, several critical challenges currently limit its performance and practical deployment in the design of polysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanoparticles for siRNA delivery. Addressing these limitations is essential to avoid overstating the maturity of AI-driven optimization strategies and to provide a realistic assessment of their current readiness [116,162].

A primary constraint is the limited availability of high-quality datasets suitable for training robust AI models. Most published studies rely on small, heterogeneous datasets generated under disparate experimental conditions, using different nanoparticle formulations, synthesis protocols, and biological models. Such variability reduces model generalizability and increases the risk of overfitting, thereby limiting the predictive reliability of AI-based approaches when applied beyond narrowly defined experimental settings [163].

Inconsistent and incomplete reporting of nanoparticle physicochemical properties further complicates AI implementation. Key parameters such as particle size distributions, surface charge, polysaccharide molecular weight, grafting density, and colloidal stability are often reported using non-standardized methods or omitted altogether. This lack of uniform reporting hampers data integration across studies and restricts the construction of interoperable datasets required for effective machine learning [164,165].

Another major challenge is the absence of standardized biological endpoints for evaluating siRNA nanocarrier performance. Cellular uptake, endosomal escape, gene silencing efficiency, cytotoxicity, and biodistribution are frequently assessed using diverse assays, time points, and readouts, making cross-study comparisons difficult. Consequently, AI models trained on such heterogeneous biological outputs may capture assay-specific artifacts rather than generalizable biological trends.

The limited availability of negative or failed experimental results represents an additional and often overlooked constraint. Publication bias toward positive outcomes leads to unbalanced datasets that inadequately represent failure modes such as nanoparticle aggregation, instability, low siRNA loading, or poor biological performance. As a result, AI models may exhibit inflated predictive accuracy while remaining poorly equipped to identify suboptimal or non-viable nanocarrier designs [166].

Collectively, these challenges indicate that AI-driven optimization of siRNA nanocarriers should currently be regarded as an enabling and supportive strategy rather than a fully autonomous design paradigm. At present, AI approaches are most effective when tightly integrated with experimental validation, serving to prioritize design parameters, guide hypothesis generation, and reduce empirical trial-and-error rather than replace conventional experimental workflows.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Polysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanoparticles, including gold, silver, and iron oxide cores, represent a highly versatile and promising platform for siRNA delivery in cancer therapy. By combining the unique physicochemical properties of metallic cores with the biocompatibility, targeting ability, and stimulus-responsive features of polysaccharide coatings, these hybrid nanocarriers overcome several major barriers in RNA-based therapeutics, such as instability in circulation, limited cellular uptake, and off-target effects. Preclinical studies have consistently demonstrated their ability to achieve efficient gene silencing, improve tumor-specific accumulation, and enable synergistic therapeutic outcomes when combined with other modalities, highlighting their potential as multifunctional and customizable drug delivery systems. Looking forward, the next phase of development lies in precision and patient-specific approaches. Integrating AI and ML into nanoparticle design could enable predictive modeling of tumor-specific interactions, guiding the selection of polysaccharide types, surface modifications, and nanoparticle sizes tailored to individual patients. These computational approaches may also analyze tumor receptor profiles to recommend polysaccharide coatings that optimize cellular uptake while minimizing immune recognition. Machine learning models can also simulate biodistribution, endosomal escape, and gene silencing efficiency, allowing rapid optimization of formulations before in vivo validation. Another critical direction is the development of multifunctional hybrid nanocarriers that integrate siRNA delivery with imaging, real-time tracking, and complementary therapeutic modalities. Iron oxide cores can enable MRI-guided monitoring, while gold or silver cores can provide photothermal or photodynamic therapeutic effects. Integrating these functionalities within a single system allows simultaneous therapy and monitoring, supporting adaptive treatment strategies and personalized dosing. In particular, closed loop theranostics systems in which AI not only designs the nanoparticles but also interprets diagnostic data generated from it to dynamically adjust treatment in real time represent an emerging paradigm for intelligent and self-regulating nanomedicine. Furthermore, as AI-designed nanomedicines progress toward clinical translation, addressing the challenges and opportunities of regulatory science becomes increasingly important. Establishing robust frameworks for validation, transparency, and ethical AI deployment will be essential to ensure reproducibility, patient safety, and compliance with evolving standards. Interdisciplinary collaboration among nanotechnologists, computational scientists, pharmacologists, oncologists, and regulatory experts will play a central role in achieving standardized manufacturing, scalable production, safety assessment, and regulatory approval. By combining AI-driven design, multifunctionality, adaptive therapeutic control, and rigorous regulatory oversight, polysaccharide-functionalized metallic nanoparticles have the potential to advance from experimental research toward clinically translatable, precision siRNA therapeutics, representing a significant step in cancer nanomedicine.
