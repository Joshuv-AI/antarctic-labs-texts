Effects of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum on oxidative stress, mitophagy, and NLRP3 inflammasome activation in broiler breast meat
Authors: Junmeng Yuan, Fan Zhao, Yu Liu, Huawei Liu, Kai Zhang, Xinyu Tian, Yuxin Mu, Jinshan Zhao, Yang Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractPoultry meat has a high polyunsaturated fatty acids content, making it vulnerable to oxidative stress. Mitophagy participates in the regulation of oxidative stress and the nucleotide-binding and oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptor family as well as pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation. Lactiplantibacillus plantarum P8 (P8) is a probiotic strain with an antioxidant capacity. In the present study, we investigated the effects of P8 on oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, mitophagy, and NLRP3 inflammasome in the breast meat of oxidatively stressed broilers. Four hundred 1-day-old male broilers were assigned to a 2 × 2 factorial design with 2 P8 levels (0 or 1 × 108 cfu/g), either with or without dexamethasone (DEX) injection, for a 21-day experimental period. DEX was injected intraperitoneally once daily from d 16 to 21. The breast meat was collected on d 21. The results showed that P8 supplementation decreased malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, increased superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and activated the Keap1-Nrf2 pathway in DEX-injected broilers. Moreover, P8 supplementation downregulated mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) copy number and increased the expressions of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1α (PGC-1α), silent information regulator 1 (SIRT1), mitochondrial fusion protein 1 (Mfn1), and optic atrophy protein 1 (OPA1) in DEX-treated broilers. In addition, the decreased mitophagy level in DEX-treated broilers was elevated with P8 supplementation, as reflected by the increased gene expression of autophagy-related gene 5 (ATG5), Bcl-2-interacting protein (Becline-1), Parkin, PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1), light chain 3 II (LC3II)/LC31, and the protein expression of Parkin as well as decreased p62 expression. In addition, P8 supplementation inhibited NLRP3 inflammasome activation by decreasing the transcription of NLRP3, IL-18, cysteinyl aspartate-specific proteinase-1 (Caspase-1), and the expression of NLRP3 and IL-18 in DEX-treated broilers. In conclusion, dietary P8 supplementation alleviates oxidative stress, improves mitophagy, and inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation in the breast meat of oxidatively stressed broilers.Key words: breast meat, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, mitophagy, oxidative stress, NLRP3 inflammasome

INTRODUCTION

Chicken meat is the second-largest source of consumed meat protein and has no religious dietary limitations (Jin et al., 2021). With the development of intensive feeding, broilers have become vulnerable to oxidative stress (Nasr et al., 2021), leading to lipidosis, fibrosis, and overall myodegeneration in broiler meat, ultimately resulting in undesirable meat color, water-holding capacity, low nutritive value, and the formation of toxic compounds that pose a threat to human health (Zhang et al., 2022).

Oxidative stress is caused by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can damage lipids, proteins, and DNA (Schieber and Chandel, 2014). Autophagy is a cellular process that participates in the lysosomal degradation of proteins and organelles, contributing to cell growth maintenance, differentiation, and homeostasis under adverse conditions such as starvation and oxidative stress (Scherz-Shouval and Elazar, 2007). Mitochondria are the main sources of ROS. Dysfunctional and damaged mitochondria can be recognized and degraded by either nonselective autophagy or a selective type of macroautophagy called mitophagy (Yoo and Jung, 2018). Stress conditions such as oxidative stress, starvation, and hypoxia can cause oxidative damage to mitochondria (De Gaetano et al., 2021). There is evidence that oxidative stress is closely associated with mitophagy. Kurihara et al. (2012) have reported excessive ROS production in mitophagy-deficient cells. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, deficiencies in nonselective autophagy and ubiquitin-independent mitophagy cause ROS accumulation upon starvation (Suzuki et al., 2011). Moreover, mitophagy is a balancing regulator of the nucleotide-binding and oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptor family, pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) released by damaged mitochondria are considered activators of the NLRP3 inflammasome (Li et al., 2022). Therefore, the impairment of mitophagy damages the capacity for mtROS and mtDNA clearance, activating NLRP3 inflammasome. Activation of NLRP3 inflammasome promotes the maturation of interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-18, contributing to inflammatory responses that affect muscle performance (Dubuisson et al., 2021).

Recent reports have shown that the impairment of muscle health is associated with autophagy and mitophagy. Song et al. (2021) have reported that mitophagy is essential for maintaining myofiber homeostasis and supporting skeletal muscle plasticity, thereby playing a critical role in maintaining skeletal muscle health. Selsby et al. (2019) found that heat stress leads to apoptosis in skeletal muscle and that apoptosis declines linearly over 6 h, concomitant with markers of increased autophagy and mitophagy. Moreover, reduced mitochondrial dynamics have been observed in broiler chickens with wooden breast syndrome. The expression of mitochondrial dynamic-related genes showed a strong positive correlation with that of autophagy/mitophagy-related genes (Hosotani et al., 2020).

Probiotics are live microorganisms that confer health benefits to the host when adequately administered. Research has shown that probiotics can induce the production of short-chain fatty acids to lower hepatic lipogenesis and inhibit lipogenesis to decrease meat fat content (Weitkunat et al., 2015). They also positively affect meat quality by reducing lipid oxidation (Popova, 2017). Several studies have investigated the antioxidant effects of probiotics. Dietary probiotic supplementation protects broiler meat against oxidative damage under heat-stress conditions (Cramer et al., 2018). Bai et al. (2016) found that the dietary probiotic Bacillus subtilis strain fmbj enhanced the antioxidant capacity of chicken breast meat and also improved the oxidative stability of chicken breast meat during storage by regulating the activity of antioxidant enzymes and mitochondrial functional genes. Recent studies have shown that probiotics regulate oxidative stress through autophagy. Bacillus amyloliquefaciens SC06 can alleviate oxidative stress-induced disorders and apoptosis via p38-mediated autophagy in rats (Wu et al., 2019) and induce AKT-FOXO signaling pathway-mediated autophagy to alleviate oxidative stress in IPEC-J2 cells (Tang et al., 2021). Lactobacillus reuteri ZJ617 can protect against LPS-induced intestinal barrier damage by enhancing antioxidant activity and attenuating autophagy (Cui et al., 2017). Lactobacillus johnsonii L531 restricts inflammasome activation by eliminating damaged mitochondria and accelerating autophagic degradation, thereby ameliorating enteritis caused by Salmonella infantis in weaned piglets (Xia et al., 2020). Lactobacillus rhamnosus GR-1 pretreatment induces PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy and reduces ROS production and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, which subsequently decreases Escherichia coli-induced apoptosis in bovine mastitis (Li et al., 2021).

The hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis is activated when an animal is under stress, and glucocorticoids are secreted from the adrenal gland. Dexamethasone (DEX) is a synthetic glucocorticoid widely used to establish oxidative stress models (Gao et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2022; Fathi et al., 2023). Our previous study also showed that DEX injection significantly increased drip and cooking losses and decreased b*, pH45 min, and pH24 h in broiler breast meat (Liu et al., 2021). Lactiplantibacillus plantarum is an important inhabitant of the gastrointestinal tract of humans and animals and is widely used as a probiotic because of its health-promoting properties (Broderick and Duong, 2016). The administration of the probiotic Lactiplantibacillus plantarum to poultry has been shown to improve broiler health and growth performance (Chang et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023). Lactiplantibacillus plantarum P8 (P8) is a probiotic strain with antioxidant and immunoregulatory properties (Wang et al., 2021). Previously, we found that dietary supplementation with P8 improved the growth performance (Zhao et al., 2023) and breast meat quality of DEX-induced broilers (Liu et al., 2021). Thus, in this study, we aimed to further evaluate the effects of dietary P8 supplementation on the antioxidant capacity and inflammation of breast meat in DEX-induced oxidatively stressed broilers. In addition, we attempted to determine whether mitophagy and the mitophagy-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome play a crucial role in this process.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Probiotic and Reagents

Probiotic P8 was purchased from Beijing SciTop Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). DEX was obtained from the Beian Feilong Animal Pharmaceutical Factory (Heilongjiang, China).

Experimental Design and Diet Composition

A total of 400 one-day-old male Cobb 500 broilers were assigned to a 2 × 2 factorial design with 2 P8 levels (0 or 1 × 108 cfu/g) and 2 DEX levels (0 or 3 mg/kg body weight), resulting in 4 treatment groups with 10 replicates per treatment and 10 broilers per replicate. The 4 treatment groups were control (Con), DEX, P8, and DEX + P8. From d 16 to d 21, 200 μL DEX was injected intraperitoneally once a day to induce oxidative stress (Wang et al., 2021). The experiment lasted for 21 d, and the basal diet was formulated according to broiler NRC standards. The diet and nutrient composition are presented in Table 1. During the experiment, broilers received food and water ad libitum and were routinely immunized. All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Qingdao Agricultural University (protocol number: 20200319035). The ARRIVE guidelines for animal research were followed.

Sample Collection

One chicken was randomly selected from each replicate and euthanized using the cervical dislocation method. Part of the breast meat was collected in 5 mL Eppendorf tubes containing RNA preservation solution for RNA extraction, and the rest was placed in 5 mL Eppendorf tubes for biochemical analysis and protein extraction. Breast meat samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

Determination of Biochemical Parameters

A total of 900 μL prechilled normal saline was added to 0.1 g of breast meat sample and homogenized using a homogenizer. Thereafter, the supernatant was collected by centrifuging at 1,000 × g for 10 min (4°C), and the protein concentration in the supernatant was determined using a BCA Protein Concentration Determination Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Malondialdehyde (MDA, Cat. ML54112), superoxide dismutase (SOD, Cat. ML05820), catalase (CAT, Cat. ML08526), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px, Cat. ML96521) concentrations in the supernatant were determined using an ELISA kit (Shanghai Enzyme-Linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The ELISA methods are presented in Supplemental Material Data S1. The activities of SOD (Cat. YJ44986), CAT (Cat. YJ55698), and GSH-Px (Cat. YJ66985) were determined using commercial kits (Shanghai Enzyme-Linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The experimental procedures were performed in strict accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Determination of Mitochondrial DNA Copy Number and mRNA Expression Levels Using Real-Time Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from broiler breast meat using TRIzol reagent (Tiangen Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The purity and concentration of RNA were measured using a spectrophotometer (NanoDrop 2000c, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and agarose gel electrophoresis, and the purity was measured using the A260/A280 ratio. RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA for mitochondrial copy number determination using the Servicebio RT First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), and RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA for mRNA expression determination using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix kit (TaKaRa, Kusatsu, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. PCR primer sequences for the chicken genes were designed and selected using Primer 5.0 and Oligo 7.0, as shown in Table 2. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (without ROX) (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd.) and the BioRad CFX96 Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA). The DNA content of the NADH dehydrogenase subunit 1 (ND1) gene was normalized to that of the platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule (pecam) gene to calculate the mtDNA copy number. The cycling parameters were 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 5 s denaturation at 95°C and 34 s annealing/extension at 60°C. The mRNA expressions were determined using real-time quantitative PCR using TB Green Premix Ex Taq kit (TaKaRa) and BioRad CFX96 Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as a housekeeping gene to normalize the target gene transcript levels. The cycling parameters were 95°C for 30 s; 95°C for 5 s; 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s, 60°C for 30 s, and 95°C for 10 s; and the final melting curve analysis to monitor the purity of the PCR product. The 2−ΔΔCq method was used to estimate target gene expressions.

Determination of Protein Expression Levels Using Western Blotting

Total protein in the breast meat was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer, and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein concentration determination kit (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Equal amounts of protein from each sample were separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After protein transfer, the membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk powder on a shaker for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies including anti-β-actin (Cat. AF5003; Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), anti-Kelch-1ike ECH-associated protein l (Keap1, Cat. A1820, Abclonal, Woburn, MA), anti-nuclear factor-E2-related factor 2 (Nrf2, Cat. ab31163; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-RBR E3 ubiquitin protein ligase (Parkin, Cat. GB113802; Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd.), antilight chain 3 B (LC3B; Cat. NB100-220; NOVUS, Beijing, China), anti-p62 (Cat. NBP1-48320; NOVUS), anti-NLRP3 (Cat. GB114320, Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd.), and anti-IL-18 (Cat. GB114098, Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd.). The membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Biyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) after washing with Tris-buffered saline Tween. Protein bands are detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and developed and fixed in the CanoScan LiDE 100 scanner (Canon, Tokyo, Japan). Relative protein expression levels were calculated using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

Statistical Data Analysis

The effects of DEX, P8, and their interactions on oxidative stress parameters and mRNA and protein expression were assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the general linear model procedure using IBM SPSS Statistics 18.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Multiple mean comparisons were performed using a univariate ANOVA and Duncan's multiple range test. All means and comparisons were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Dietary P8 Supplementation Ameliorated the Oxidative Stress in Breast Meat of DEX-Treated Broilers

The effects of P8 on oxidative stress status in breast meat are shown in Figure 1. Compared with the Con group, DEX treatment significantly increased the level of MDA (P < 0.01) and decreased the content of SOD (P < 0.01) and activity of GSH-Px (P < 0.05). In contrast, P8 supplementation significantly decreased the level of MDA (P < 0.01) and increased the levels of SOD, GSH-Px (P < 0.01), and SOD activity (P < 0.05). Moreover, the interaction between DEX and P8 (DEX × P8) reversed DEX-induced changes in MDA, SOD, and GSH-Px levels (P < 0.01). As shown in Figure 2, DEX injection significantly increased the protein expression of Keap1 (P < 0.05) and decreased the protein expression of Nrf2 (P < 0.05) compared to the Con group. DEX × P8 reversed the DEX-induced changes in Keap1 and Nrf2 expression (P < 0.05). However, P8 supplementation did not significantly affect the expression of Keap1 and Nrf2 (P > 0.05).

Dietary P8 Supplementation Attenuated the Mitochondrial Dysfunction in Breast Meat of DEX-Treated Broilers

As shown in Figure 3A, DEX injection significantly increased the relative mtDNA copy number (P < 0.01). In contrast, P8 supplementation significantly decreased the relative mtDNA copy number (P < 0.01), and DEX × P8 reversed the DEX-induced increase in mtDNA copy number (P < 0.01). Moreover, as for the mitochondrial biogenesis, the mRNA levels of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1α (PGC-1α), silent information regulator 1 (SIRT1), silent information regulator 3 (SIRT3) (P < 0.01) were significantly decreased in the DEX group compared to that of the Con group. In contrast, the P8 supplementation increased the mRNA levels of PGC-1α (P < 0.01), SIRT1 (P < 0.01), and SIRT3 (P < 0.05). Moreover, DEX × P8 reversed the DEX-induced decreases in the gene expressions of PGC-1α and SIRT1 (P < 0.01). There were no significant differences in the mRNA levels of mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) or nuclear respiratory factor 1 (NRF1) (P > 0.05) among the groups (Figure 3B). As for mitochondrial dynamics, we found that the mRNA levels of mitochondrial fusion-related genes (mitochondrial fusion protein 1 (Mfn1) and optic atrophy protein 1 (Opa1)) (P < 0.01) decreased, while the mRNA levels of mitochondrial fission-related genes (mitochondrial fission factor (MFF)) (P < 0.01) increased in the DEX group compared to that in the Con group. P8 supplementation increased the Mfn1 and decreased Opa1 expression (P < 0.01). In addition, DEX × P8 reversed the DEX-induced decrease in Mfn1 (P < 0.01) and Opa1 (P < 0.01) (Figure 3C).

Dietary P8 Supplementation Induced the Mitophagy in Breast Meat of DEX-Treated Broilers

The effects of P8 on mitophagy are shown in Figure 4A. Compared with the Con group, DEX treatment significantly decreased the mRNA levels of autophagy-related gene 5 (ATG5), Becline-1, Parkin, PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1), and LC3II/I (P < 0.01). P8 supplementation significantly increased ATG5, Becline-1, Parkin, PINK1, and LC3II/I (P < 0.01). Moreover, DEX × P8 reversed the DEX-induced decrease in ATG5, Becline-1, Parkin, PINK1, and LC3II/I (P < 0.01). In addition, Western blotting results indicated that DEX treatment significantly decreased the protein expression of Parkin and LC3B (P < 0.05) compared to the Con treatment. P8 supplementation increased the expression of LC3B (P < 0.05) and decreased the expression of p62 (P < 0.05). Additionally, DEX × P8 reversed the DEX-induced changes in Parkin and p62 expression levels (P < 0.05) (Figure 4B).

Dietary P8 Supplementation Inhibited the Mitophagy-Mediated NLRP3 Inflammasome Activation in Breast Meat of DEX-Treated Broilers

The effects of P8 on the NLRP3 inflammasome are shown in Figure 5A. Compared to the Con group, broilers in the DEX group had higher levels of NLRP3, IL-18, and cysteinyl aspartate-specific proteinase-1 (Caspase-1) (P < 0.01). P8 supplementation decreased the expression of NLRP3, IL-18, and Caspase-1 (P < 0.01). Additionally, DEX × P8 reversed the DEX-induced increase in NLRP3, IL-18, and Caspase-1 expression (P < 0.01). Furthermore, Western blotting results indicated that DEX injection significantly increased the protein expression of NLRP3 (P < 0.05) compared to the Con treatment. P8 supplementation significantly downregulated NLRP3 expression (P < 0.05). Moreover, DEX × P8 reversed the DEX-induced increase in NLRP3 (P < 0.01) and IL-18 (P < 0.05) expression (Figure 5B).

DISCUSSION

There is evidence that probiotics exert antioxidant effects on meat (Bai et al., 2016; Cramer et al., 2018). Consistent with these findings, DEX × P8 decreased the levels of MDA and increased the levels of SOD and GSH-Px in the breast meat of DEX-treated broilers in the present study. The Keap1-Nrf2 system is an intracellular defense mechanism against oxidative stress. The activation of Nrf2 can upregulate a series of antioxidant genes, including SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px (Sun et al., 2020). In agreement with the regulation of SOD and GSH-Px content, DEX × P8 also increased the expression of Nrf2 and decreased the expression of Keap1 in the breast meat of DEX-treated broilers. These findings indicate that dietary P8 supplementation enhances the antioxidant capacity of breast meat of oxidatively stressed broilers.

Mitochondrial function is closely associated with oxidative stress. Increased oxidative stress leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and mtDNA damage and causes an increase in mtROS levels. After mitochondrial damage, mtDNA and mtROS are released from the mitochondria, aggravating oxidative stress and inflammation (Yang et al., 2020). The present study showed that DEX injection significantly increased the mtDNA copy number, whereas DEX × P8 significantly decreased the level of mtDNA. Mitochondrial morphology is critical for normal cellular and mitochondrial functions. Mitochondrial morphology switches between elongated tubules and fragmented globules via fusion and fission (Okamoto and Shaw, 2005). Mitochondrial fusion is mediated by dynamin family GTPases such as mitofusin (Mfn1, Mfn2, and OPA1), and mitochondrial fission can be regulated by MFF (Wang et al., 2017). In this study, we found that DEX significantly increased the expression of mitochondrial fission-related genes (MFF) and decreased the expression of mitochondrial fusion-related genes (Mfn1 and Opa1) in broiler breast meat. Moreover, DEX × P8 treatment significantly increased the expression of Mfn1 and Opa1. We also found that the DEX injection decreased the mitochondrial biogenesis-related genes (PGC-1α, SIRT1, and SIRT3), while DEX × P8 increased the gene expressions of PGC-1α and SIRT1. The PGC-1 family consists of PGC-1α, PGC-1β, and PRC, playing a vital role in a regulatory network governing the transcriptional control of mitochondrial biogenesis and respiratory function (Scarpulla, 2011). PGC-1α mediates the effects of SIRT1 on mitochondrial biogenesis. SIRT3 is also required for PGC-1α-induced mitochondrial-related gene expression and mitochondrial biogenesis (Li et al., 2017). In addition, although the mitochondrial biosynthesis was increased and the mtDNA copy number was decreased with the interaction between DEX × P8, it has been shown that mtDNA replication does not coincide with organelle growth and division. Therefore, mtDNA replication may not be accompanied by mitochondrial biogenesis (Shadel and Clayton, 1997). Reports also show that an increase in mitochondrial biogenesis in the skeletal muscle of patients with mtDNA depletion is not accompanied by an increase in mtDNA copy number (Lee and Wei, 2005). These findings indicate that dietary P8 supplementation attenuates DEX-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in broiler breast meat.

Mitophagy can clear damaged mitochondria; therefore, the mitophagy levels were measured. DEX injection significantly decreased the expression of ATG5, Becline-1, Parkin, PINK1, LC3II/I, PARKIN, and LC3B. In contrast, DEX × P8 significantly increased the expression of ATG5, Becline-1, Parkin, PINK1, LC3II/I, and Parkin and decreased p62. PINK1 is a serine/threonine kinase containing a mitochondria-targeting sequence that enables mitochondrial localization. When mitochondria are damaged, PINK1 phosphorylates membrane proteins to recruit Parkin from the cytoplasm to the damaged mitochondria (Ashrafi and Schwarz, 2013). Parkin is a cytosolic E3-ubiquitin ligase that ubiquitinates several proteins in the outer mitochondrial membrane and recruits p62. P62 is a multifunctional cytoplasmic protein that functions as a selective mitophagy receptor for the degradation of ubiquitinated substrates and binds to the LC3 protein (Gureev et al., 2020). As a lysosome-related protein, LC3 is widely used for autophagic detection (Yang et al., 2018). When the level of autophagy increased, the conversion of LC3I to LC3II was stimulated. Beclin-1 and ATG5 are key regulators of autophagy and are commonly targeted in autophagy gene editing assays (Ye et al., 2018). Thus, the above findings indicate that dietary P8 supplementation increases mitophagy in breast meat of oxidatively stressed broilers. Similar to our results, a previous study showed that the overproduction of ROS can impair autophagy in mice (Wang et al., 2019), and the addition of the probiotic Bacillus amyloliquefaciens SC06 increased the level of autophagy in the intestinal tract of rats by upregulating the expression of LC3 and Beclin-1 and downregulating the expression of p62 (Wu et al., 2019). mtDNA damaged by oxidative stress can be released into the cytoplasm and acts as a binding motif for NLRP3, activating NLRP3 inflammasome (Shimada et al., 2012). NLRP3, a multiprotein complex consisting of an NLRP3 scaffold, an adaptor apoptosis speck-like protein (ASC) and the effector procaspase-1, initiates the formation of the inflammasome by interacting with ASC, which recruits and activates procaspase-1 to generate active Caspase-1 and then converts the cytokine precursors pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into mature and biologically active IL-1β and IL-18, respectively. Once activated, the active IL-1β and IL-18 will trigger a series of inflammatory responses (Wang et al., 2020). Mitophagy can inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation by clearing damaged mitochondria (Zhou et al., 2011). Studies have shown that a lack of autophagy proteins, such as LC3B and Beclin-1, induces the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria and NRLP3/ROS-dependent translocation of mtDNA to the cytosol. Cytosolic mtDNA participates in the secretion of IL-1β/IL-18, thus to the inflammatory response (De Gaetano et al., 2021). The results in the present study showed that broilers in the DEX group had higher gene expression of NLRP3, IL-18, and Caspase-1 and higher protein expression of NLRP3 and IL-18. Treatment with DEX × P8 decreased the expression of these molecules. Similarly, Han et al. (2021) found that oxidative stress can activate inflammasomes and the inflammatory cytokines secreted by inflammasomes, whereas the addition of antioxidants can inhibit mtROS-mediated activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome by promoting mitophagy. Therefore, dietary supplementation with P8 inhibited the DEX-induced activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in broiler breast meat, possibly by increasing mitophagy.

In summary, this study demonstrates that dietary P8 supplementation decreases oxidative stress, improves mitophagy, and inhibits mitophagy-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome activation in broiler breast meat.
