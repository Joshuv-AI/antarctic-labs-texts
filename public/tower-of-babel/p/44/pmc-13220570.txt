Circulating tumor DNA in breast cancer: updates from SABCS 2025
Authors: Iris Zhi, Xianghui Zou, Min Sun, Nancy Chan, Naomi Ko, Neil Vasan, Shridar Ganesan, Cynthia Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has emerged as a transformative biomarker in breast cancer, enabling sensitive assessment of tumor burden, molecular residual disease, and treatment resistance. Data presented at the 2025 San Antonio Breast Cancer Symposium (SABCS) demonstrate that ctDNA has moved beyond prognostication to guide treatment in advanced hormone receptor positive disease, most notably in the phase III SERENA-6 trial, where ctDNA-detected ESR1 mutations prospectively triggered endocrine therapy switch and significantly improved clinical outcomes. In early-stage breast cancer, converging evidence across subtypes shows that ctDNA-defined minimal residual disease (MRD) robustly identifies patients at high risk of recurrence. Collectively, these findings position ctDNA as a potential biomarker for dynamic treatment adaptation, supporting the next phase of precision oncology focused on MRD-guided escalation, de-escalation, and therapeutic interception across the breast cancer continuum.

To the Editor,

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has emerged as a transformative biomarker in breast cancer, enabling sensitive and dynamic assessment of tumor burden, clonal evolution, and treatment resistance across the disease continuum. Data presented at the 2025 San Antonio Breast Cancer Symposium (SABCS) indicate that advances in ctDNA assays sensitivity, together with results from large perspective clinical trials, have moved liquid biopsy from technical feasibility to clinical utility in breast cancer.

Has ctDNA moved beyond prognostication to guide treatment in advanced breast cancer?

Despite growing interest in ctDNA-guided strategies and emergence of prospective biomarker-driven studies enabled by highly sensitive assays, its clinical role has historically been largely confined to prognostic value, however, emerging data are beginning to suggest its predictive utility. In advanced breast cancer (ABC), the most definitive evidence supporting ctDNA as a treatment-guiding tool comes from the phase III SERENA-6 trial (Table 1). In this study, patients with HR+/HER2- ABC receiving first-line aromatase inhibitor (AI) plus CDK4/6 inhibitor underwent serial ctDNA monitoring every 2–3 months using a commercial hybrid-capture ctDNA assay with ~95% sensitivity and tumor fraction detection as low as 0.05%. Detection of ESR1 mutations triggered a switch from AI to camizestrant significantly prolonged progression-free (PFS) survival (16.6 vs. 9.2 months; HR 0.46) and time to subsequent therapy, and rapidly suppressed ESR1 mutant allele frequency [1]. These results demonstrate that serial ctDNA- monitoring can meaningfully alter disease trajectory and represent a paradigm shift toward real-time, biomarker-directed treatment adaptation.

Additional evidence comes from exploratory analyses of CAPItello-291 and INAVO120. Both studies utilizing broad ctDNA profiling to identify actionable PIK3CA/AKT1/PTEN alterations in patients missed by tissue testing alone with ctDNA defined subgroup derived substantial benefits [2, 3]. Notably, the liquid biopsy assay demonstrated high sensitivity: ~95% at ~ 0.4% variant allele frequency for short variants; ~0.37% for rearrangements. Together, these data support broad ctDNA profiling as a clinically actionable tool in ABC, particularly when tissue is unavailable, insufficient, or discordant.

By contrast, EMBER-3 and pooled MONALEESA analyses continue to position ctDNA primarily as a prognostic and response-monitoring biomarker, with early ctDNA reduction and lower baseline burden associated with improved treatment responses [4, 5].

Can ctDNA-defined MRD identify patients with high recurrence risk?

ctDNA assays can sensitively detect molecular relapses and predict recurrence timing; however their role in guiding treatment adaption remains investigational. In the neoadjuvant setting, the PHERGain study showed baseline ctDNA correlated with tumor burden in HER2 positive breast cancer (Table 2). Although it did not predict pathologic complete response (pCR), failure to clear ctDNA was associated with non-pCR and inferior 3-year invasive disease-free survival (HR 4.5) [6]. Similarly, in the NSABP B-59/GeparDouze MRD study in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), ctDNA positivity declined from 96% at baseline to 9% after neoadjuvant therapy. Post-surgical MRD positivity identified an approximately 30-fold higher risk of distant recurrence, whereas nearly all MRD-negative patients remained metastasis-free at three years [7].

In the adjuvant setting, the PALLAS MRD analysis showed that ctDNA positivity was infrequent in HR+/HER2- disease but it strongly predicted distant recurrence (HR 15.1), with 5-year distant recurrence-free (DFS) survival was 93% in ctDNA-negative versus ~ 30% in ctDNA-positive patients [8]. The c-TRAK TN study further demonstrated that a tissue-free, methylation-based ctDNA assay detected relapse earlier and identified more high-risk TNBC patients [9]. In LEADER, among ctDNA-positive HR+/HER2- patients (Signatera, ~ 94% overall sensitivity, 100% in breast cancer), early ctDNA reduction or clearance were associated with longer DFS [10]. Finally, the CATER study suggested that ctDNA clearance with adjuvant capecitabine may serve as an early surrogate of outcome [11].

Challenges and future directions

Despite substantial advances, several critical questions remain. While SERENA-6 trial established as a treatment-guiding biomarker in advanced disease, the optimal cfDNA platforms and testing strategies for different clinical contexts remain undefined. This includes uncertainty regarding the relative roles of tumor-informed MRD assays, tumor-agnostic approaches, and serial broad cfDNA profiling in both the MRD and advanced disease settings. In early-stage breast cancer, prospective trials demonstrating that MRD-guided intervention improves long-term clinical outcomes are still lacking. In parallel, heterogeneity in assay design, analytical performance, and payer acceptance continues to limit widespread adoption, emphasizing the necessity for assay harmonization, prospective validation, and cost-effective implementation to fully realize the promise of ctDNA-driven precision oncology.
