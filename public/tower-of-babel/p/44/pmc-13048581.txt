Harnessing conserved epitopes and multivalent antigen strategies for vaccine design: Lessons learned and opportunities
Authors: Ebenezer Adjei-Gati, Charlotte Naa Odey Quaye, Prince Odartey Lamptey, Lois Dziedzorm Oklu, Jessica Nyarkoa Kwofie, Yussif Abdul Hafiz, Kwadwo Asamoah Kusi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVaccination remains a cornerstone of global disease prevention, yet outcomes differ markedly across pathogens. For antigenically stable viruses such as hepatitis B virus (HBV), vaccines have achieved durable, cross-genotype protection and have substantially reduced disease burden worldwide. In contrast, extensive antigenic diversity in other pathogens enables immune escape, narrows protective responses, and complicates the development of broadly effective vaccines. Lessons from HBV suggest that targeting conserved, immunodominant antigens can underpin durable immunity. It has, however, proven difficult to translate this to highly variable pathogens. Conserved epitopes are often structurally masked or immunologically subdominant, limiting their ability to elicit robust protective responses. In addition, determinants that govern whether conserved epitope responses translate into durable protection remain poorly understood. This narrative review examines current progress, challenges, and opportunities and highlights how conserved epitopes and multivalent vaccine strategies can inform the next generation of broadly protective vaccines against antigenically diverse pathogens.

Introduction

Vaccines have transformed global public health, preventing millions of deaths each year by inducing protective immunity against a wide range of infectious diseases. Their success has been particularly evident for pathogens with relatively stable antigens, such as hepatitis B, where vaccine candidates and antigens are subjects of research.1,2 However, for many of the world’s most burdensome diseases including HIV/AIDS, malaria, dengue, and Lassa fever, vaccine development has proven far more complex.

Host immunity, encompassing T-cell responses and antibody responses, is a frequent focus of research in vaccine development. Various vaccine delivery platforms, such as DNA vaccines, viral vectors (including Vesicular Stomatitis Virus, adenovirus, and vaccinia virus), virus-like particles, and nanoparticles, are explored in vaccine design.3–5 Several factors influence the effectiveness of vaccines, including host immunity, vaccine delivery platforms, adjuvants for formulation, and the biological characteristics of the pathogen itself. Among these factors, antigenic diversity stands out as a major barrier to vaccine success.5,6 Pathogens with high levels of genetic and antigenic variability often evade immune recognition by altering the very targets that vaccines aim to exploit.7–9 This leads to reduced vaccine efficacy and limited cross-protection.10,11

Antigenic diversity significantly impacts immune responses and poses a major challenge to vaccine effectiveness across various pathogens.7,12,13 Although these pathogens differ in their replication strategies, transmission routes, and evolutionary dynamics, they share a common immunological feature: extensive antigenic variability that enables escape from antibody and T-cell recognition.7 Understanding these shared constraints provides a conceptual framework for evaluating vaccine design strategies to overcome overcome antigenic diversity. This diversity refers to the variation in antigens, which are the molecular structures recognized by the immune system, leading to challenges in eliciting broad and sustained protective immunity. Pathogens can evolve rapidly through mutations or recombination, creating new variants that may escape preexisting immune responses or render current vaccines less effective.12–14 Type 1 human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-1) exhibits remarkable diversity, comprising numerous subtypes and circulating recombinant forms (CRFs), with high rates of mutation.7,15 Within an infected individual, the virus rapidly generates “quasispecies” or diverse viral variants due to high replication rates and error-prone reverse transcriptase, enabling it to evade immune responses.15 This extensive diversity is a primary obstacle to developing an effective HIV vaccine.15 Hepatitis C (HCV) also exhibits extensive genetic diversity, with at least seven major genotypes and more than 80 subtypes differing by 15–30% in amino acid sequences.16 This high variability combined with immune selection within infected individuals’ viral mutants that can escape T-cell and antibody responses. A similar case is found in influenza, where the influenza virus undergoes continuous antigenic change through “antigenic drift” (minor mutations) and “antigenic shift” (major genetic reassortment), leading to seasonal new strains annually.13,17 This necessitates annual (seasonal) reformulation and administration of influenza vaccines, as immunity against one strain may not protect against other strains.13,17,18 In contrast, universal influenza vaccine strategies aim to elicit broad, heterosubtypic protection against diverse subtypes and lineages by redirecting the immune response toward highly conserved but subdominant epitopes.19,20 Key targets for these universal approaches include the functionally constrained HA stalk, the M2e extracellular domain, and conserved internal proteins such as NP and M1, which can stimulate cross-reactive cellular and humoral responses.21,22 Antigen diversity can significantly affect both humoral and cellular immune responses.23–25 As part of the immune defense mechanism, immune pressure pushes pathogens to evolve. This causes the immune system to be biased toward less-conserved regions or highly variable regions, as observed in HIV-1. This challenge often necessitates strategies that target highly conserved regions of pathogens, employ multi-epitope or mosaic vaccine designs, or aim to elicit broadly cross-reactive immune responses such as broadly neutralizing antibodies or polyfunctional T-cell responses.24–27

This narrative review synthesizes recent literature on conserved epitope-based and multivalent vaccine strategies aimed at overcoming antigenic diversity. Relevant studies published between 2015 and 2025 were identified through searches of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science using keywords including conserved epitope, antigenic variability, vaccine design, and multivalent vaccines with emphasis on pathogens characterized by high antigenic diversity such as HIV, influenza virus, hepatitis C virus, Plasmodium falciparum, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Lassa virus. In this review, we examine how antigenic diversity shapes vaccine development across multiple high-burden pathogens like HIV, influenza, hepatitis C virus, Plasmodium falciparum, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Lassa virus. Despite differences in their biology, these pathogens share a common challenge: extensive antigenic variation that enables immune escape and undermines durable vaccine-induced protection. We therefore focus on two complementary conceptual strategies aimed at overcoming this barrier: targeting conserved epitopes that remain relatively stable across variants and designing multivalent or mosaic immunogens capable of broadening immune coverage. By synthesizing lessons learned across diverse pathogens, we aim to (1) highlight the immunological basis of antigenic diversity as a barrier to vaccine design, (2) evaluate current approaches that exploit strategies aimed at overcoming antigenic variation, including conserved epitope targeting, multi-antigen formulations, and promising adaptive vaccine platforms that provide optimal vaccine delivery and increase vaccine effectiveness or efficacy.

Antigenic variability and vaccine design strategies for various pathogens

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-1)

During natural HIV-1 infection, the immune system mounts both humoral (antibody) and cellular (T-cell) responses. Early in the infection, low-titer neutralizing antibodies can impede viral replication.28 However, this often leads to the selection of viral variants that escape these antibody responses, limiting their long-term effectiveness.28 CD8+ T-cell specific for conserved, cross-reactive Gag epitopes have been shown to be highly effective at inhibiting HIV-1 replication.29 Studies also suggest that natural protective T-cell responses often target conserved regions of the virus.30,31 The high genetic diversity of HIV-1, characterized by multiple subtypes and circulating recombinant forms globally,9,32,33 presents a formidable challenge to vaccine development.15,33 Viral diversity can lead to immune escape, where the virus mutates its antigens to avoid recognition by vaccine-induced immune responses.27,28,34 This is particularly problematic as the immune response can be biased toward less-conserved regions of the virus, a phenomenon that is partly caused by selective immune pressure, potentially limiting vaccine efficacy.10,35 Consequently, traditional vaccine approaches that rely on natural antigens have often failed to elicit sufficiently broad or durable protection against diverse pathogen strains and/or genotypes.24,25 In many cases, immunodominant but non-protective epitopes tend to dominate the immune system,24,25 often leaving conserved, functionally critical epitopes under-recognized because they may be obscured by structures like the glycan shield on the HIV-1 envelope trimer.36,37 This necessitates approaches like focusing T-cell responses on highly conserved regions 38,39 or structure-based vaccine design to specifically present these vulnerable sites.40–42 In addressing pathogen diversity, a key strategy involves targeting conserved regions of the virus that are less prone to mutation and crucial for viral function. Vaccines designed to elicit T-cell responses against these conserved regions aim for broader and more durable protection.26,38,39 For example, “conserved-region” HIVconsv vaccines have been evaluated in preventive and therapeutic clinical trials, focusing on T-cell responses on highly conserved regions.30,38,39 Efforts are also directed at eliciting broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) that can recognize conserved epitopes across diverse HIV strains.43,44 These bNAbs often target vulnerable, conserved sites on the HIV envelope trimer, such as the V2-apex or other parts of gp120.43–45 Structure-based vaccine design is crucial for engineering immunogens that present these conserved epitopes effectively.12,14,46 Additionally, to account for global HIV-1 diversity, mosaic and polyvalent vaccine designs are being developed.33,47 These vaccines incorporate antigens from multiple HIV strains or computationally designed “mosaic” sequences that maximize coverage of known circulating strains. Mosaic antigens have been shown to prime broader cellular immune responses and induce anti-viral CD8+ T cell functionality.23,48,49 Vaccines designed to display multiple conserved epitopes, sometimes using virus-like particles, aim to induce a comprehensive immune response against immune targets on the virus.26,50,51 The long-term goal is to create a “universal” HIV vaccine that works against every strain that is currently in circulation.10,35 This entails developing immunogens that can elicit wide cross-subtype immunity as well as having an in-depth knowledge of the immunological responses to HIV diversity.52–54

Current approaches often involve including both humoral (antibody-mediated) and cellular (T-cell-mediated) immune response targets.47,55,56 The current approaches include the following: (1) Viral vectors: vectors such as simian adenovirus (ChAdOx1) and MVA have been used to deliver HIV antigens.35,38,57 Vesicular Stomatitis Virus (VSV) vectors are also being explored, including replication-competent ones incorporating HIV envelope trimers.58 In addition to viral vectors, vaccine developers utilize prime-boost immunization strategies, where a primary immunization (e.g., DNA or poxvirus vector) is followed by a booster immunization (e.g., protein, adenovirus vector, or modified vaccinia virus Ankara (MVA)).38,59 This approach aims to enhance the breadth and magnitude of immune responses.53,54 (2) Subunit vaccines: recombinant glycoprotein 120 (gp120) subunit vaccines have been evaluated in clinical trials.60,61 While a phase 3 trial of a recombinant gp120 vaccine showed limited efficacy,60,62 the RV144 trial, which used a prime-boost regimen with ALVAC and AIDSVAX, demonstrated modest efficacy and provided insights into correlates of protection, including antibodies targeting the V2 loop of the gp120.43,63,64 (4) Nanoparticle vaccines: vaccine platforms that are used to elicit neutralizing antibody responses.65,66 (5) Envelope glycoprotein (Env) trimers: HIV-1 Env glycoprotein trimer is a primary target for broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs).67,68 Vaccine design here aims to stabilize these trimers to elicit the desired antibody response.69 Immunization with HIV Env trimer has been shown to generate broadly neutralizing antibodies.44

Prioritizing and effectively presenting conserved viral epitopes is critical. These regions, by their nature, are less mutable and more likely to be essential for viral survivor and infectivity, making them ideal targets for long-lasting, broadly effective immune responses.30,31,57,70 The focus should be on designing immunogens that precisely guide the immune system to produce T-cell responses (specifically CD8+ T cells) and broadly neutralizing antibodies against these conserved sites on the HIV envelope, such as the V2 loop and other critical trimer elements.43,44 In addition, implementing multivalent and mosaic approaches is essential to account for the genetic heterogeneity that cannot be fully captured by targeting only a few conserved epitopes.33,47,55 By including a diverse array of antigens or computationally optimized mosaic sequences, vaccines can prime immune responses that are active against a wider spectrum of circulating HIV strains, thereby mitigating the risk of viral escape.47–49,55 The success of prime-boost regimens in eliciting cross-subtype immunity further underscores the importance of such combinatorial strategies.52–54

Lassa fever virus

Lassa fever (LF), caused by the Lassa virus (LASV), is an acute viral hemorrhagic illness endemic in West Africa, posing a significant public health burden. Nigeria stands out as a prominent high-burden area for Lassa virus activity.71,72 A critical feature of Lassa fever is the significant genetic diversity among Lassa virus strains.71,73 This genetic diversity arises from several evolutionary mechanisms characteristic of arenaviruses. Lassa virus circulates in its natural rodent reservoir (Mastomys natelensis), where continuous viral replication promotes the gradual accumulation of mutations.74 Over time, this process has resulted in multiple geographically distinct viral lineages across West Africa, particularly in Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Liberia, and Guinea.71,73,75 In addition, variation in the viral glycoprotein complex (GPC), which is the primary target of neutralizing antibodies, contributes to antigenic heterogeneity that can influence immune recognition and vaccine effectiveness76 The adaptive genetic diversification of Lassa virus, particularly the association with distinct Nigerian and non-Nigerian lineages, means that a vaccine designed against one strain may not be broadly effective against others, thereby limiting wide applicability in all endemic regions.73,75 This challenge is further complicated by observations that an inactivated Lassa virus can elicit a non-protective immune response, emphasizing the difficulty in inducing effective cross-protection against diverse strains.77 The Lassa virus glycoprotein complex (GPC) contains N-linked glycans that affect its infectivity and immune response, potentially contributing to immune evasion.78 While researchers do not explicitly detail “conserved epitopes” in the same way as for HIV, the concept of achieving “universal” protection implies targeting regions that are common across diverse strains.73,75,79 The success of recombinant VSV-vectored vaccine in protecting NHP against heterologous lethal Lassa fever suggests that it either targets conserved regions or induces a sufficiently broad immune response to overcome strain differences.3,4 Another strategy to address diversity involves the use of reassortment viruses; thus, genetic material from two or more different viral strains is combined through the process of reassortment. For example, an ML29 reassortant virus was shown to protect guinea pigs against a distantly related Nigerian strain of Lassa virus, and could provide sterilizing immunity.71,72

Lassa fever vaccine development has seen considerable progress, with several candidates advancing through preclinical and early clinical stages.80 Key strategies in vaccine development include the following: (1) viral vector in the form of recombinant vaccinia virus expressing LASV glycoprotein (GPC). A recombinant vaccinia virus containing the Lassa virus glycoprotein gene protected Rhesus monkeys from fatal Lassa fever in an early study.81,82 However, inactivated LASV was found to elicit a non-protective immune response in rhesus monkeys.77,83 Recombinant VSV-vectored vaccines, which express the LASV glycoprotein, have shown significant promise. A recombinant VSV-vectored vaccine rapidly protected non-human primates (NHP) against heterologous lethal LF.3,4 This vaccine has undergone phase 1 clinical trials84 and is currently in phase 2 clinical trials in Africa. ChAdOx1-vectored vaccines against Lassa fever have been produced and evaluated. A vaccine of this type produced a strong humoral and cellular immune response and protected guinea pigs from the deadly Lassa virus challenge.81,82 (2) DNA-based Lassa vaccines, such as INO-4500, have showed long-lasting protection against deadly Lassa fever in cynomolgus macaques, especially when delivered by dermal electroporation.85 These have also advanced to clinical trials.86 Successful vaccine candidates typically elicit both cellular and humoral immune responses.81,82

Identification and exploiting conserved antigenic sites that are critical for viral function and less prone to mutation presents the most promising path forward. Continued research should prioritize mapping these conserved regions on the LASV-GPC, including understanding the role of N-linked glycans in immune evasion, to design immunogens that direct the immune system to these stable targets. Additionally, integrating multivalent or broadly protective strategies is essential. The success of reassortant viruses in providing protection against distantly related strains71,72 exemplifies the utility of approaches that encompass diversity.

Hepatitis C virus

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection is still a major global health concern causing chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Despite advances in antiviral treatments, the development of a prophylactic HCV vaccine is crucial but is complicated by its remarkable genetic diversity.87,88 Within an infected individual, HCV exists as a dynamic population of closely related viral variants known as quasi-species. This diversity arises largely from the error-prone RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, which lacks proofreading capability and introduces frequent mutations during viral replication. Combined with strong immune selection pressure from host T-cell and antibody responses, this rapid mutation rate facilitates the emergence of viral variants capable of escaping immune recognition. At the population level, this evolutionary process has resulted in at least seven major HCV genotypes and more than 80 subtypes that differ substantially in antigenicity16 To counter HCV’s high genetic variability, vaccine development needs to focus on genetically conserved regions of the viral genome. HCV possesses relatively conserved nonstructural (NS) proteins such as NS3, NS4, NS5, NS5A, and NS4B. These proteins are more conserved across different strains than the highly variable envelope glycoproteins.89,90 A heterologous prime-boost vaccination using viral vectors (ChAd3 and MVA), also aimed at conserved nonstructural proteins (NS3-NS5B), can be deployed. This approach successfully induced strong and sustained CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses that exhibited polyfunctionality and cross-reactivity across multiple HCV genotypes, with long-term memory maintenance.88,91

Though developing vaccines against HCV is challenging, various strategies have been explored, including (1) a peptide-based vaccine candidate designed to elicit cellular immunogenicity. These often focus on specific viral proteins like HCV non-structural (NS) protein NS5A, NS4B, and core proteins.16,89,90 One of such candidates, VAL-44, engineered from conserved regions of HCV nonstructural proteins (NS5A, NS4B, and core) and incorporating HLA-A2-restricted sequences elicited significantly stronger cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses and a robust antigen-specific IFN-γ production from both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.90 (2) Vector-based vaccine strategy employing chimpanzee adenoviral (ChAd3) and Modified Vaccinia Ankara vectors. This prime-boost approach aims to prime, boost, and sustain functional HCV-specific T cell memory.91 A regimen using ChAd3 and MVA vectors expressing NS3, NS4, NS5A, and NS5B proteins of HCV genotype-1b successfully induced strong, multifunctional T-cell responses against multiple HCV NS epitopes and showed cross-reactivity with genotypes 1a, 3a, and 4a.88,91 On average, the amino acid sequences differ by approximately 30% between genotypes and by about 15% between subtypes within a genotype.87,92

Overcoming the extensive genetic diversity of the hepatitis C virus (HCV) remains a central obstacle to the development of a conserved and multivalent vaccine. Evidence from various strategies, including multi-epitope peptide vaccines such as VAL-4493 and IC41,94 and heterologous prime-boost vector systems utilizing ChAd3 and MVA demonstrates that focusing on conserved nonstructural (NS) proteins can induce broad T-cell responses across multiple genotypes.91 However, clinical trials have shown that strong vaccine-induced T-cell immunogenicity does not necessarily translate to protection against chronic infection, particularly in high-risk populations.88 Although CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells play essential roles in viral control, sterilizing immunity against HCV likely requires the coordinated induction of broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) in addition to cell-mediated responses.95 Furthermore, the rapid emergence of escape mutations within T-cell epitopes due to error-prone polymerase activity can diminish the effectiveness of vaccine elicited responses, while delayed trafficking of effector T-cells to the liver may allow early viral establishment, further highlighting the limitations of exclusively T-cell response-focused vaccine strategies for HCV. To overcome this challenge, future HCV vaccine development should integrate the continued optimization of conserved epitope selection to ensure cross-genotype coverage and also enhance antigen delivery using heterologous vector systems.16,87

Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB)

Natural immune response to Mycobacterium Tuberculosis (Mtb) is a complex and often effective containment strategy, yet it frequently falls short of complete eradication, leading to persistent infection.96 Evidence suggests that some individuals can clear infection even after exposure, and established latent TB infection (LTBI) can confer concomitant immunity against reinfection. This involves lung-resident macrophages as front-line defenders, employing innate immune mediators like vitamin D, macrophage migration-inhibitory factor (MIF), tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin-1 (IL-1), IL-12, IL-18, and IFN-γ from natural killer cells to activate antimicrobial activities.96 However, natural immunity faces significant challenges because T-helper 1 cells (Th1) are insufficient for complete protection, and both IFN-γ-independent mechanisms and a balance of pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators are crucial to prevent immunopathology.97 One of the key challenges in developing effective immunity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis diversity is a result of genetic diversity that exist across phylogenetic lineages of the M. tuberculosis complex (MTBC).98 This diversity arises primarily through the gradual accumulation of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) rather than high mutation rates.99 Although many T-cell epitopes remain highly conserved across strains, lineage-specific genetic variation can influence antigen expression, virulence, and host immune interactions, thereby affecting vaccine performance7,101. The genetic diversity within M. tuberculosis complex (MTBC) strains can have consequences for its biology and impact immune response.96 In addressing the vaccine development gap caused by antigenic diversity, vaccine candidates focus on immunodominant antigens that are known to be conserved across strains.100 The use of adjuvants with subunit vaccines is also critical to enhance the immune response.101 A stronger, more robust immune response could potentially compensate for minor antigenic variations or target multiple epitopes on an antigen, even if some parts vary. Additionally, vaccine developers could explore prime-boost immunization strategies using different vectors (e.g., recombinant adenovirus and MVA) to induce comprehensive and potentially broader immune responses. Significant progress is being made in TB vaccine development with various candidates undergoing clinical evaluation. These efforts span different vaccine platforms, including subunit vaccines, recombinant viral vectors, and improved live-attenuated approaches. (1) Subunit vaccines: these typically utilize specific antigens and often require adjuvants to enhance the immune response and effectively deliver antigens.99 H1/IC31 and related candidates are adjuvanted subunit vaccines that have been evaluated for safety and immunogenicity in TB-endemic areas and in HIV-infected adults with higher CD4+ lymphocyte counts.102 H1:IC31 vaccination has demonstrated safety and induced long-lived TNF-α + IL-2 + CD4 T cell responses in both M. tuberculosis-infected and uninfected adolescents.102,103 Other related candidates, such as the H4:IC31 vaccine, have shown promising safety and immunogenicity in BCG-vaccinated adults, and the H56:IC31 vaccine has been evaluated for safety and immunogenicity in adults successfully treated for drug-susceptible pulmonary tuberculosis.102,103 (2) Live-Attenuated Vaccines: Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) remains the only licensed vaccine against TB, widely used for the prevention of M. tuberculosis infection. However, BCG, vaccine through propagation under varied laboratory conditions and repeated passaging, has evolved into genetically distinct “daughter strains,” which now differ in genomic content, gene expression, and immunological properties.104 This genetic divergence among BCG strains is now recognized as a possible contributor to the variable protective efficacy of the vaccine observed globally.104

Future TB vaccine development must prioritize the identification and harnessing of highly conserved epitopes that are universally present across diverse Mtb lineages and consistently recognized by the human immune system, as suggested by the observed paucity of antigenic variation in most T cell epitopes.99 Additionally, a robust strategy must involve the development of multivalent antigen vaccine platforms. These platforms should intelligently combine multiple, carefully selected conserved antigens to stimulate a comprehensive and synergistic immune response.99,105 For instance, subunit vaccines with multiple antigens have shown promise in clinical trials. Furthermore, employing a heterologous prime-boost immunization strategy with different vaccine vectors can enhance the magnitude and breadth of the immune response. Continued clinical evaluation and correlates of protection through assays like the Mycobacterium Growth Inhibition Assay are vital. This allows for data-driven refinement of vaccine candidates to ensure they elicit the most effective protective responses against the diverse forms of the pathogen found globally.

Plasmodium

Malaria, primarily caused by Plasmodium parasites, remains one of the most devastating infectious diseases globally. Despite years of control efforts, a broadly effective vaccine has been challenging to achieve due to the extensive genetic variation. The extensive diversity of P. falciparum (severe human causing malaria parasite) arises through several biological mechanisms.106 Unlike rapidly mutating RNA virus, parasites diversity is primary driven by balancing selection acting on immunogenic antigens and genetic recombination during the parasite’s sexual stages in the mosquito vector.107 Many leading vaccine antigens, including circumsporozoite protein (CSP), apical membrane protein 1 (AMA1), and merozoite surface protein 1 (MSP1), are under high immune selection pressure, resulting in extensive allelic polymorphisms across parasite populations.27 In addition, the complex life cycle of Plasmodium involves multiple developmental stages in both human and mosquito host, each expressing distinct antigen repertoires.108 This stage-specific antigen expression further increases the range of immune targets and contributes to the difficulty of identifying universally protective vaccine antigens.109 Vaccine design strategies have therefore been hindered by extensive antigenic diversity across parasite populations and life cycle stages. Vaccines targeting the circumsporozoite protein (CSP), like RTS,S, and R21, show allele-specific protection due to CSP polymorphism.110 Blood-stage antigens like Apical membrane antigen 1 (AMA1) and Merozoite surface protein 1 (MSP1) are highly variable, leading to strain-specific immune responses and rapid immune escape. This diversity necessitates unique vaccine design strategies to ensure broad effectiveness.107

There has been a notable advancement in the design of vaccinations against malaria, primarily targeting Plasmodium falciparum.109 Current efforts focus on various vaccine candidates and platforms, aiming to elicit robust immune responses capable of providing protection, for example, RTS,S/AS01, R21/Matrix-M, and PfSPZ Vaccine. Vaccine development efforts concentrate on several Plasmodium falciparum antigens, including CSP, AMA1, and Thrombospondin-related anonymous protein (TRAP). As a prominent target, a full-length CSP administered with adjuvants has been shown to elicit potent antibody and CD4+ T cell immunity and protection in mice.109 Protective antibody and CD8+ T-cell responses to CSP have also been induced by nanoparticle vaccines. IgG2 antibodies against a clinical-grade Plasmodium falciparum CSP vaccine antigen have been associated with protection against transgenic sporozoite challenge in mice. CSP, AMA1, and TRAP are identified as MHC Class I-restricted epitope targeted by DNA prime/chimpanzee adenovirus (ChAd63) boost vaccines, indicating strong potential for cellular immunity targets in malaria vaccine development.106,111

Understanding the global diversity and balancing selection of candidate vaccine antigens, as well as the genetic diversity of Plasmodium falciparum in various regions, will be an effective counterstrategy for informing vaccine design. Additionally, instead of focusing on a single antigen, efforts are directed toward vaccines that incorporate multiple candidate antigens. For example, the DNA prime/ChAd63 boost vaccine targets CSP, AMA1, and TRAP MHC Class I-restricted epitopes simultaneously. Viral vector vaccines and those administered with specific adjuvants (e.g., Polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid poly(I:C) or glucopyranosyl lipid adjuvant-stable emulsion) are designed to elicit high-level T cell and antibody responses.112

To achieve a universal vaccine for malaria, developing vaccines that target multiple antigens or multiple conserved epitopes within key antigens is crucial. By presenting the immune system with a diverse array of P. falciparum components, a vaccine can elicit a broader protective response that is less susceptible to variability in any single antigen.113,114 The continuous identification and characterization of leading candidate vaccine antigens and their genetic diversity are essential for this. In addition, enhancing the immunogenicity of these multivalent vaccines through advanced delivery platforms and potent adjuvants is paramount. In parallel, combining vaccines that target different life stages (e.g., sporozoite + blood stage + transmission-blocking) may offer synergistic protection. Prime-boost regimens, viral vectors, and the use of effective adjuvants can ensure the induction of high-level antibody and T-cell responses, including robust CD8+ T-cell immunity.115,116

Influenza virus

Influenza is a significant viral disease that poses considerable challenges for vaccine development due to its high antigenic diversity. The two major surface antigens of the influenza virus are Hemagglutinin (HA) and Neuraminidase (NA), which serve as crucial targets for neutralizing antibodies.117,118 The HA protein is responsible for viral entry into host cells and it consists of a globular “head” domain and a more conserved “stalk” domain.119 The HA head is the dominant target of neutralizing antibodies but undergoes antigenic drift and shift, leading to different influenza serotypes and subtypes and strain-specific immunity.119 The NA enzyme helps in the release of new viral particles from infected cells.118 Like HA, NA also exhibits subtype diversity and antigenic variation although antibodies targeting NA can contribute to protective immunity by inhibiting viral release from infected cells.118,119

Vaccine developers are pursuing two main strategies to address this diversity: inducing immunity against highly conserved domains that are less subject to antigenic drift. The Hemagglutinin (HA) stalk is significantly more conserved than the globular head and is a prime target for broad-spectrum immunity. A single-shot adenoviral vaccine has been shown to provide hemagglutinin stalk-mediated protection against heterosubtypic influenza challenge in mice.120,121 However, a key lesson learned is that the HA stem often exhibits subdominance and poor intrinsic immunogenicity, which limits the humoral immune response targeting this region. To overcome this, strategies like hyperglycosylation and disulfide stabilization are being investigated to alter the immunogenicity of HA immunogens and improve responses to the stalk.118,122 Neuraminidase is also increasingly recognized as a vital vaccine antigen. Antibodies that inhibit cross-reactive neuraminidase can be produced by immunizing against the pandemic H1N1 influenza A virus and the H5N1 virus using recombinant neuraminidase proteins.123 Another vaccine strategy is employing mosaic or multivalent immunogens conserved among different strains. Integrating multiple conserved antigens, such as NP, M1, and chimeric HA into viral vectors, has been shown to enhance broad protection.122 By shifting focus from highly variable HA head to conserved regions like the HA stalk and neuraminidase, vaccines can induce broader and more enduring immunity.120,121,124 Addressing the inherent challenges of these conserved targets, such as HA stalk’s sub-dominance, through rational immunogen design (e.g., hyperglycosylation and disulfidestabilization) is crucial. A multivalent vaccine platform is essential for effectively presenting these diverse conserved antigens, eliciting a comprehensive immune response. Approaches utilizing chimeric hemagglutinins, virus-like particles, and self-assembling nanoparticles facilitate presentation of multiple epitopes in an immunogenic format, driving robust germinal center activity and memory B cell maturation.125 Unlike seasonal or strain-matched vaccines that require periodic reformulation to track circulating variants, universal vaccine strategies aim to induce durable immunity against conserved antigenic regions shared across diverse pathogen strains. Ultimately, the development of a truly universal influenza vaccine hinges on combining these strategies: designing immunogens that specifically expose and stabilize conserved epitopes, presenting these epitopes through multivalent platforms capable of inducing broad heterologous and heterosubtypic immunity, and optimizing these formulations with effective adjuvants. This comprehensive approach moves beyond simply “chasing” the virus’s evolution and instead aims to build a fundamental, robust defense against a wide array of influenza threats.

Success story of hepatitis B vaccine against other pathogens

Hepatitis B Virus, like Hepatitis C, is a blood-borne pathogen that leads to chronic infection, liver cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). However, HBV has a widely used and highly effective vaccine, but no curative treatment. For three decades, vaccination has played a crucial role in the global control of hepatitis B virus infection. The development and widespread implementation of HBV vaccines have reduced the incidence of severe liver disease.126 Strategies, such as infant vaccination from 3 months of age and, more recently, the administration of a birth dose, have been identified as successful.127,128 Vaccines against many pathogens that undergo significant antigenic variation, such as the frequent antigenic drift in influenza viruses,129 often require regular updates and show limited long-term effectiveness. In contrast, the Hepatitis B vaccine has remained relatively effective despite the global diversity of HBV genotypes.130,131 HBV exists in multiple genotypes, yet the current vaccines provide broad protection.127,131 This ability to protect against various strains is a critical factor in its success, avoiding a major challenge that plagues vaccine development for other highly variable viruses.127,132

The current HBV vaccines are based on the Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg).133 HBsAg comprises the S-domain, whose loop section is found on the surface of infectious virions and contains several important HBsAg protein epitopes (including the “a”-determinant, “d/y,” and “w/r” determinants). The effectiveness of the conserved epitope is rooted in the essential role of HBsAg in the viral life cycle. HBsAg serves as the primary component of the viral envelope and is critical for the virus to attach and enter host hepatocytes through its interaction with the sodium taurocholate co-transporting polypeptide (NTCP) receptor.133,134 The vaccine primarily targets the highly conserved “a” determinant, which is structurally and functionally vital for viral infectivity across all known HBV genotypes. Because this region is so functionally constrained, the virus cannot easily undergo mutations that would allow it to evade immune recognition without compromising its ability to replicate.133,134 Consequently, the neutralizing antibodies elicited by vaccinated individuals effectively block the initial step of infection by preventing the virus from binding to target cells.127,132 The success of this vaccine implies that the critical parts of the HBsAg targeted by the vaccine-induced antibodies are sufficiently conserved across different viral genotypes.128,132 This demonstrates that when a surface antigen is both immunodominant and functionally constrained due to its essential role in viral entry, vaccine-induced antibodies can achieve durable and broadly protective immunity. This mechanism is in stark contrast to that in pathogens like HIV or HCV, where surface glycoproteins are characterized by extreme genetic diversity, glycan shielding, and the ability to frequently select escape mutations that render vaccine-induced response ineffective.16,41 This contrast highlights the relative advantage of the HBV situation.

Anti-HBsAg antibodies are the primary immune correlate of protection after HBsAg-based vaccination.135 Having a straightforward and measurable marker for immunity significantly simplifies vaccine development and evaluation.135,136 The challenges with other pathogens highlight this lesson. For example, a dengue vaccine must induce a balanced and protective response against four different serotypes to avoid the risk of antibody-dependent enhancement.137 A TB vaccine requires a complex T-cell-mediated response to control an intracellular bacterium, which is far more difficult to achieve and measure than an antibody response.138

The enduring success of the Hepatitis B vaccine highlights a fundamental principle in vaccinology: the identification of a stable, conserved, and immunodominant antigen provides the most direct pathway to a broadly protective vaccine.139 HBsAg exemplifies this, functioning like a master key that elicits strong and durable immune responses across diverse viral genotypes, enabling effective global control despite the virus’s genetic variability.140 This achievement stands in sharp contrast to the persistent challenges faced with pathogens such as influenza, HIV, and dengue, where extensive antigenic diversity and immune evasion mechanisms continue to impede the development of universally effective and long-lasting vaccines.141

Comparative constraints on conserved epitope targeting across pathogens

While HBV provides a paradigm in which a conserved, surface-exposed, and functionally constrained antigen enables durable vaccine-mediated protection, the situation differs fundamentally for pathogens such as HIV and influenza. In HBV, the immunodominant “a” determinant of HBsAg is both essential for infectivity and structurally constrained, limiting antigenic drift. In contrast, HIV-1 Env exhibits extensive sequence variability, dense glycan shielding, and conformational masking that obscure conserved receptor-binding sites. Although conserved regions such as the CD4 binding site exist, they are recessed and structurally protected, making them difficult to target through conventional immunization.46 Similarly, influenza hemagglutinin contains conserved stem regions that are functionally constrained, yet natural immune responses preferentially target the highly variable head domain, leading to strain-specific immunity and necessitating annual vaccine reformulation.142 Thus, in HIV and influenza, conserved epitopes often exist but are either immunologically subdominant, structurally inaccessible, or insufficient alone to induce sterilizing immunity. HCV, Lassa virus, Plasmodium falciparum, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis also present challenges through employing rapid mutation, intracellular persistence, or multistage life cycles to evade immune pressure. Unlike HBV, where neutralization of a single conserved surface antigen blocks viral entry, protection against these pathogens may require combinations of broadly neutralizing antibodies, T-cell responses, or multivalent antigen presentation strategies to overcome immune evasion mechanisms.

Developing vaccines against conserved antigens and epitopes presents a formidable challenge due to the evolutionary immune evasion strategies employed by pathogens. While variable regions are often immunodominant, conserved epitopes are frequently sequestered or structurally shielded to evade host immunity. From an immunological standpoint, several intrinsic barriers limit the induction of protective responses against these targets: Immunodominance and Steric Hindrance: The immune system typically mounts a hierarchical response, favoring “immunodominant” variable loops that are highly accessible. Concluding remarks

While vaccine design to achieve broad population coverage has been straightforward due to limited genetic variability in the causative pathogens, including the HBV and yellow fever viruses, the design of vaccines for other pathogens has been primarily impacted by the significant genetic variability that they exhibit. Development of broadly effective and universally applicable vaccines against such pathogens has therefore progressed at a much slower rate. Strategies that have been proposed to overcome these effects of pathogen antigenic variability on vaccine-induced responses include targeting conserved epitopes that are less prone to mutation, constructing multi-antigen formulations to broaden immune coverage, and developing mosaic antigens that maximize representation of circulating strains. Success in applying these strategies varies: development of vaccines based on conserved epitopes has shown promise, exemplified by HIVconsv vaccines focusing T-cell responses on highly conserved regions, and HCV vaccine regimens utilizing conserved NS proteins to induce strong, cross-reactive T-cell responses across multiple genotypes. Multi-antigen formulations have also proven effective, such as those targeting multiple MHC Class I-restricted epitopes (CSP, AMA1, and TRAP) simultaneously in malaria, and the use of polyvalent or mosaic immunogens has successfully primed broader cellular immune responses and induced anti-viral CD8+ T cell functionality against diverse HIV-1 strains. Vaccines based on these immune response broadening approaches are, however, mostly in the experimental stages. Their effectiveness at inducing broadly protective immune responses can be further improved through focusing on rational immunogen design and optimizing multi-antigen approaches through synergistic strategies, such as combining vaccine candidates that target different pathogen developmental stages to provide comprehensive protection. For mosaic and multivalent vaccines, linkers should be optimized to ensure that the native protein conformation remains accessible to the immune system and is efficiently presented by antigen-presenting cells (APCs).
