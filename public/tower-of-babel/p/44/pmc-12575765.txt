Synergistic effects of colistin-based combinations against colistin and carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae isolated from nosocomial bloodstream infections
Authors: Bahman Pourabbas, Shima Sepehrpour, Sadaf Asaei, Amin Abbasian, Parham Pourabbas, Zahra Rezaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emergence of colistin and carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRCR-KP) poses a critical challenge in treating bloodstream infections. As resistance reduces colistin’s efficacy, alternative strategies are urgently needed. Combination therapy may offer improved outcomes, but its effectiveness remains uncertain. This study evaluated in vitro interactions between colistin and 12 commonly used antibiotics against CRCR-KP isolates from nosocomial bloodstream infections. A retrospective analysis identified 25 CRCR-KP isolates (10.8%) among 270 K. pneumoniae bloodstream infections, with 18 isolates included in synergy testing. Checkerboard assays were used to assess colistin-based interactions, quantified using the Fractional Inhibitory Concentration Index (FICI), Loewe Additivity, and Bliss Independence models. Demographic and clinical data were collected, including ward distribution, comorbidities, prior antibiotic exposure, and patient outcomes. The median patient age was 65 years (range: 2–94), and the mortality rate was 77.8% (14/18), underscoring the severity of these infections. Patients were primarily admitted to the ICU (22.2%), emergency department (33.3%), and hematology unit (22.2%), with frequent invasive procedures (94.4%) and hospital stays ranging from 4 to 60 days. 66% of isolates were classified as extensively drug-resistant (XDR). Disk diffusion and MIC testing revealed high resistance across most antibiotic classes, though tigecycline (44.4%), amikacin (50%), and minocycline (34%) demonstrated partial efficacy. Synergy detection rates varied by model, FICI (75%), Loewe (50%), and Bliss (41.6%), highlighting the importance of using multiple analytical approaches. Clindamycin, chloramphenicol, erythromycin, and minocycline exhibited strong synergy with colistin at lower concentrations, suggesting a promising combination strategy for managing CRCR-KP bloodstream infections. Minocycline appears particularly viable due to its achievable serum concentrations and moderate Gram-negative coverage. Further pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and clinical research is essential to validate these findings and guide the development of effective treatment regimens.Keywords: Colistin and carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRCR-KP), Nosocomial bloodstream infections, Antibiotic synergySubject terms: Applied microbiology, Clinical microbiology, Microbiology

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae is an opportunistic bacterial pathogen that causes a range of infections, including pneumonia, urinary tract infections, and bloodstream infections. The emergence of antibiotic-resistant K. pneumoniae, particularly strains resistant to both colistin and carbapenems, has become a major global health concern due to limited treatment options and poor clinical outcomes1.

Colistin is widely regarded as a last-line treatment for Gram-negative infections, including those caused by carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae. However, the increasing use of colistin has contributed to the rise of colistin-resistant, carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRCR-KP), significantly reducing the clinical utility of this critical antibiotic. This trend poses a serious therapeutic challenge and raises the alarming possibility of a return to a pre-antibiotic era1,2.

Colistin-resistant K. pneumoniae (ColR-KP) is associated with high mortality and morbidity, prolonged hospital stays, and increased healthcare costs. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis reported a pooled prevalence of colistin resistance in bloodstream isolates of 12.9% from 2020 onward, compared to 2.89% and 2.95% during 2016–2019, respectively2. Infections caused by ColR-KP strains have shown mortality rates ranging between 30% and 70%, underscoring the urgency of developing new therapeutic strategies3–5.

In patients infected with ColR-KP, combination antibiotic therapy has been associated with better clinical outcomes than monotherapy. A pivotal multicenter case–control study found that 30-day mortality was significantly higher in patients with bloodstream infections caused by ColR-KP (51.4%) compared to those with colistin-susceptible isolates (39.2%; p = 0.03). Additionally, initial treatment failure was more common in the colistin-resistant group (26.7% vs. 14.8%; p = 0.003), where monotherapy was more frequently administered3. Another study reported a 69.2% mortality rate in patients treated with monotherapy for colistin-resistant K. pneumoniae, versus 33.3% in those receiving combination therapy4. These findings underscore the inadequacy of monotherapy in managing colistin-resistant infections and support the use of synergistic antibiotic combinations to improve survival and clinical outcomes6,7.

Retrospective studies have reported high mortality rates, often exceeding 50%, with monotherapy for colistin-resistant strains3,4. In this study, we retrospectively analyzed clinical data from patients with CRCR-KP infections and performed in vitro synergy testing on the corresponding isolates. Although the clinical and laboratory data were not collected concurrently, the patient profiles and outcomes underscore the urgency of identifying effective combination therapies. This integrated approach enhances the translational relevance of our findings by aligning in vitro results with the therapeutic challenges faced in managing high-risk patients.

Prior studies have identified antibiotics such as tigecycline, amikacin, rifampin, and meropenem as potential synergistic partners with colistin against colistin-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (ColR-KP) isolates. However, antagonistic interactions have also been reported, emphasizing the need for systematic evaluation of combination regimens8,9. In addition to previously studied combinations, this study also assessed antibiotics with distinct mechanisms of action, including protein synthesis inhibitors and less commonly used agents, to systematically identify synergistic interactions that could potentially restore colistin efficacy against CRCR-KP.

Mathematical modeling approaches such as the Loewe additivity and Bliss independence models are widely used to quantify drug interactions. The Loewe additivity and Bliss independence models are widely used to evaluate interactions between antibiotic combinations, particularly to determine whether these interactions are synergistic, antagonistic, or additive. The Bliss independence model is a theoretical framework that estimates the expected combined effect of two agents under the assumption that they act independently through distinct mechanisms. In contrast, the Loewe additivity model assumes a shared mechanism of action and evaluates whether the combination effect is equivalent to an increased dose of one of the agents. Bliss represents response additivity, while Loewe reflects dose additivity. Both models have demonstrated reliability and provide complementary insights when applied to pairwise combinations across diverse antibiotic classes10–12.

Given the potential for colonization rather than true infection in isolates from non-sterile sites, only bloodstream isolates were included in this study13. Accordingly, the analysis focused exclusively on CRCR-KP strains isolated from patients with nosocomial bloodstream infections. We aimed to evaluate the in vitro interactions and potential synergy between colistin and 12 commonly used antibiotics. Drug interactions were assessed using checkerboard assays and interpreted through the Fractional Inhibitory Concentration Index (FICI), the Loewe additivity model, and the Bliss independence model.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective study included all cases of K.pneumoniae bloodstream infections (BSIs) identified at Namazi Hospital, affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, between July 2018 and June 2022. Namazi Hospital is an 800-bed tertiary care facility serving as a major referral center in southern Iran.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria:

Received intravenous colistin sulfate treatment;

Received intravenous colistin sulfate treatment;

Of any age;

Of any age;

Diagnosed with a hospital-acquired bloodstream infection; and.

Diagnosed with a hospital-acquired bloodstream infection; and.

Had a confirmed blood culture positive for CRCR-KP.

Had a confirmed blood culture positive for CRCR-KP.

Exclusion criteria were:

Infections caused by mixed Gram-positive or Gram-negative organisms; and.

Infections caused by mixed Gram-positive or Gram-negative organisms; and.

Administration of colistin sulfate or other antimicrobial agents for fewer than 72 h after CRCR-KP isolation.

Administration of colistin sulfate or other antimicrobial agents for fewer than 72 h after CRCR-KP isolation.

Clinical data were extracted from patient medical records and included: age, gender, site of organism isolation, hospital ward, duration of hospitalization at the time of isolation, comorbidities, prior antibiotic exposure (≥ 3 days), history of invasive procedures, and outcomes (including in-hospital and attributable mortality). Eligible cases required documentation of a positive CRCR-KP blood culture and sufficient clinical information to evaluate antimicrobial treatment and outcomes within 30 days following the initial positive culture.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Professor Alborzi Clinical Microbiology Research Center and the Ethics Committees of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (SUMS), Shiraz, Iran. (IR.SUMS.REC.1401.618).

Bacterial strains

During the study period, 270 non-duplicate Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates were collected from blood cultures of hospitalized patients. Blood cultures were incubated using the BACTEC 9240 Automated Blood Culture System (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Identification of K. pneumoniae from blood samples was performed using the API 20E system (bioMérieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France). All strains were minimally passaged, colony purified, and stored at − 80 °C in tryptic soy broth supplemented with 50% glycerol prior to use in the present study.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of meropenem and colistin were determined using the broth microdilution method. According to CLSI M100 (2021) guidelines, an MIC ≥ 4 µg/mL indicates colistin resistance. Colistin-resistant isolates were further tested for antibiotic susceptibility using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller–Hinton agar plates (Scharlau, Spain). The following antibiotics were tested: piperacillin–tazobactam, amikacin, tobramycin, amoxicillin–clavulanic acid, ampicillin–sulbactam, cefotaxime, cefepime, ceftazidime, cefixime, cephalexin, ceftriaxone, chloramphenicol, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, gentamicin, imipenem, tetracycline, tigecycline, and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole.

Additionally, the E-test method was used to determine MICs for trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, ampicillin–sulbactam, imipenem, erythromycin, and clindamycin. Chloramphenicol, ciprofloxacin, colistin, gentamicin, amikacin, rifampin, minocycline, meropenem, and tetracycline were evaluated using the broth microdilution method.

Checkerboard assay

To assess the synergistic effects of colistin in combination with 12 antibiotics, a checkerboard assay was performed according to the protocol described by Bellio et al.13. Antibiotics were obtained in powder form, aliquoted, and stored at − 20 °C. Stock solutions were prepared using either dimethyl sulfoxide (for chloramphenicol, rifampin, tetracycline, erythromycin, and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole) or sterile water (for all other antibiotics). A 5% NaOH solution was added to the levofloxacin stock solution.

Fresh bacterial inocula were prepared by culturing colonies in Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB) at 37 °C for 18 h with shaking until reaching a concentration of approximately 108 CFU/mL. The inoculum was then diluted in sterile saline (0.9% NaCl) to achieve a final concentration of 5 × 105CFU/mL in the assay wells. The assay was performed in 96-well microplates, with each well containing 100 µL of cation-adjusted Mueller–Hinton broth (CAMHB). Antibiotics were serially diluted from 1/8× to 8× the MIC.

Stock solutions of Drug A and Drug B were prepared in 2× CAMHB and concentrated to four times the desired final concentration. Drug A was added to the rows and serially diluted across the row; Drug B was added to the columns and serially diluted down the columns. Each well then received 100 µL of the bacterial suspension, resulting in a final inoculum of 5 × 105 CFU/mL. Microplates were incubated at 35 ± 2 °C for 18 ± 2 h. Following incubation, optical density (OD) was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader (EPOCH, BioTek, USA). Growth inhibition was calculated relative to the drug-free control. The MIC for each combination was defined as the lowest concentration resulting in more than 80% growth inhibition.

Drug interaction models

To evaluate in vitro interactions between colistin and other antibiotics against each colistin-resistant, carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRCR-KP) isolate, three analytical approaches were employed: the fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) index, and two response surface models—Loewe additivity and Bliss independence—using Combenefit software.

Loewe additivity model

The Loewe model assumes that when two drugs share a similar mechanism of action, their combined effect is additive. Synergy is inferred when the observed inhibition exceeds the expected additive effect. Growth inhibition data from the checkerboard assay were analyzed using Combenefit software, which computed deviations from predicted additivity. Synergy scores (SUM_SYN-ANT_LOEWE) were used to classify interactions as synergistic, indifferent, or antagonistic. Visual outputs included response surface plots and heatmaps.

Bliss independence model

The Bliss independence model, also implemented in Combenefit, assumes that two drugs act independently. Deviations from the predicted Bliss effect were used to calculate synergy scores (SUM_SYN-ANT_BLISS). As with the Loewe model, this approach categorized interactions as synergistic, indifferent, or antagonistic, and utilized response surface plots and heatmaps for visualization. Together, the Loewe and Bliss models provided complementary perspectives on drug interaction dynamics.

Rationale and comparative framework

To comprehensively evaluate antibiotic interactions, we employed three established models: FICI, Loewe, and Bliss. The FICI, based on changes in MICs, offers a simple and widely used metric. However, it is limited to single-point concentration comparisons and may overlook synergistic effects that occur at other drug ratios. The Loewe additivity model assumes dose additivity and is sensitive to both concentration-dependent and ratio-specific effects, making it well-suited for agents with overlapping mechanisms of action. However, it may overestimate synergy between drugs that act on independent targets. In contrast, the Bliss independence model assumes that drugs act independently and defines synergy based on deviations from expected additive inhibition. While more conservative, it is particularly appropriate for evaluating combinations of agents with distinct mechanisms.

Given the inherent strengths and limitations of each approach, we applied all three models to capture a broader spectrum of synergistic interactions, reduce model-specific bias, and ensure a robust identification of clinically relevant combinations. This strategy is especially critical when assessing colistin, a membrane-disrupting agent, in combination with antibiotics that act through intracellular mechanisms, such as protein synthesis inhibitors. Moreover, integrating these models enabled cross-framework comparison of interaction patterns and facilitated the prioritization of combinations that consistently demonstrated synergy.

Statistical analysis

To compare synergy scores across antibiotics, summary statistics (medians, ranges, and interquartile ranges) were calculated from 18 isolates per antibiotic for each of the three models (FIC, Loewe, and Bliss). Due to the non-normal distribution of the data, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test was initially performed to assess overall differences among antibiotic groups. When significant differences were observed in the Loewe and Bliss models, pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s post hoc test with appropriate adjustments for multiple comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Plate data were analyzed and visualized using response surface analysis based on the Loewe additivity and Bliss independence models. All calculations were performed using Combenefit software (version 2.021; Cancer Research UK Cambridge Institute, Cambridge, UK)14.

Results

Patients

Among 270 K. pneumoniae isolates obtained from blood cultures of hospitalized patients with nosocomial infections during the study period, 25 (10.8%) were identified as colistin- and carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRCR-KP). Of these, 18 isolates were included in the current analysis. The median patient age was 65 years (range: 2–94), indicating that older adults were predominantly affected. The cohort consisted of 55.6% female (10/18) and 44.4% male (8/18) patients.

Patient ward distribution showed that 22.2% (4/18) were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU), 33.3% (6/18) to the emergency department, and 22.2% (4/18) to the hematology ward. The remaining 22.2% were admitted to surgical and intermediate medical wards.

Hospital stays ranged from 4 to 60 days, with the majority of patients hospitalized for more than 20 days. A high proportion (94.4%, 17/18) underwent invasive procedures, reflecting a significant risk factor for bloodstream infections. Median antibiotic exposure was 20 days (range: 3–150), likely contributing to the emergence of multidrug-resistant pathogens.

Comorbidities were common, with leukemia present in 27.8% (5/18) of patients. Other underlying conditions included surgical complications, pneumonia, and end-stage renal disease. One patient had concurrent leukemia and COVID-19, potentially exacerbating their clinical condition. These findings highlight the increased vulnerability of immunocompromised patients to CRCR-KP infections.

The overall mortality rate was 77.8% (14/18). The four survivors had shorter hospital stays and lower comorbidity burdens. Most patients received multiple broad-spectrum antibiotics, including meropenem, vancomycin, and colistin, often in combinations of four to seven agents, underscoring the complexity of treating multidrug-resistant infections (Table 1).

Antibiotic sensitivity assay

Disk diffusion testing revealed high resistance rates to most antibiotics. All 18 isolates were resistant to amoxicillin–clavulanic acid, ampicillin, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, cefotaxime, cefuroxime, cefepime, tetracycline, trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, and colistin. In addition, 94.4% of isolates were resistant to aztreonam, cefixime, and piperacillin–tazobactam. Resistance to gentamicin and tobramycin was observed in 77.8% and 72.2% of isolates, respectively.

Resistance to tigecycline (44.4%) and amikacin (50%) was lower, suggesting partial efficacy compared to other agents. All isolates exhibited colistin MIC values ≥ 16 µg/mL, well above the CLSI resistance breakpoint (≥4 µg/mL), indicating high-level resistance and reduced clinical utility of colistin.

Carbapenem MICs ranged from 8 µg/mL to ≥ 128 µg/mL, exceeding the CLSI resistance breakpoint (≥ 4 µg/mL), confirming widespread resistance. All isolates met the criteria for multidrug resistance (MDR), defined as resistance to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories. Moreover, 66% were extensively drug-resistant (XDR), showing non-susceptibility to all but one or two antimicrobial categories.

Colistin and carbapenems demonstrated MIC50 and MIC90 values exceeding CLSI breakpoints. For meropenem, MIC50was 64 µg/mL, and MIC90 was ≥ 128 µg/mL. Amikacin exhibited MIC50 and MIC90 values of 16 µg/mL and 64 µg/mL, respectively. Minocycline showed MIC50 and MIC90 values of 2 µg/mL and 16 µg/mL, with a resistance rate of 34%, suggesting retained activity and potential therapeutic value (Table 2).

Interaction of colistin with other antibiotics: a multi-method approach

Three analytical models were employed to evaluate interactions between colistin and 12 antibiotics against 18 CRCR-KP isolates: the Fractional Inhibitory Concentration Index (FICI), the Loewe additivity model, and the Bliss independence model. For Loewe and Bliss models, a synergy score threshold of ≥ 20 was applied to classify combinations as synergistic, based on prior literature and Combenefit guidance. Scores between 0 and 20 were considered indifferent or weakly additive14. These models yielded varying synergy rates: FICI detected synergy in 75% of combinations, Loewe in 50%, and Bliss in 41.6%. These differences highlight the importance of employing multiple approaches for evaluating antibiotic interactions.

FICI: quantifying synergy

FICI analysis showed that 75% of the tested combinations were synergistic (FICI ≤ 0.5). Strong synergy was consistently observed for combinations of colistin with clindamycin, erythromycin, minocycline, levofloxacin, chloramphenicol, and rifampin. Median FICI values ranged from 0.02 (clindamycin) to 0.31 (rifampin). In contrast, combinations with imipenem, meropenem, and amikacin generally produced indifferent effects (FICI > 0.5) (Table 3).

Loewe additivity model: multidimensional assessment

Using the Loewe model, 50% of combinations demonstrated synergy. The highest synergy scores were observed for colistin combined with chloramphenicol (56.9), clindamycin (49.9), erythromycin (37.02), and minocycline (27.7), closely aligning with FICI findings. The Loewe model effectively quantified drug interactions and highlighted combinations with strong synergistic potential (Table 3).

Bliss independence model: alternative interpretation

The Bliss independence model identified synergistic interactions in 41.6% of combinations. Synergy was most pronounced for chloramphenicol (median score: 57.8), clindamycin (34.4), erythromycin (35.4), and minocycline (29.2). These results were generally consistent with those from the FICI and Loewe models. Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole showed synergy by FICI and Loewe, but was indifferent by the Bliss model (Table 3). The Bliss model provided valuable insights, particularly in cases where interaction outcomes deviated from additivity assumptions.

Statistical analysis and visualization

To evaluate differences in synergy detection, a Friedman test comparing synergy scores across the three models (FICI, Loewe, Bliss) revealed a statistically significant difference (χ2 = 10.17, p = 0.006). Pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests showed that FICI scores differed significantly from both Loewe (p = 0.001) and Bliss (p = 0.002), while Loewe and Bliss scores were not significantly different (p = 0.301). Additionally, Kruskal–Wallis analysis showed that while FICI scores did not significantly vary among antibiotics, both Loewe and Bliss models revealed significant differences (p < 0.05), with clindamycin, erythromycin, minocycline, and chloramphenicol showing the highest synergy scores. These findings underscore the importance of using multiple, complementary models to reliably identify synergistic antibiotic combinations. Synergistic interactions between colistin and clindamycin, chloramphenicol, erythromycin, or minocycline were further visualized using heat maps and 3D surface plots. Heat maps revealed strong synergy (deep blue) at lower concentrations of colistin (2–4 µg/mL) combined with clindamycin (16–32 µg/mL), erythromycin (32–64 µg/mL), or chloramphenicol (2–4 µg/mL), and at 1–2 µg/mL of colistin with 4–8 µg/mL of minocycline. Discussion

This study evaluated the clinical and microbiological characteristics of 18 patients with nosocomial bloodstream infections caused by colistin- and carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRCR-KP). Bloodstream isolates were used to ensure diagnostic accuracy, as specimens from non-sterile sites (e.g., sputum, wounds) often represent colonization rather than true infection and may not require treatment. Nonetheless, colonizing bacteria are frequently exposed to antimicrobials via empirical or prophylactic regimens, promoting resistance development.

The median age of patients in our cohort was 65 years, indicating that older adults, who often have waning immune function and higher healthcare exposure, were disproportionately affected. Most patients had multiple comorbidities (e.g., leukemia in 27.8%, plus pneumonia, surgical complications, or renal disease), reflecting an immunocompromised population highly susceptible to severe infection. Nearly all patients (94.4%) underwent invasive procedures and received prolonged antibiotic therapy (median of 20 days) prior to infection. These are well-recognized risk factors for acquiring multidrug-resistant organisms, as they can disrupt normal host defenses and select for resistant strains. Consistent with other reports, many infections occurred in high-risk hospital settings, ICU (22.2% of cases), emergency (33.3%), and hematology (22.2%), where intensive antimicrobial use and invasive devices facilitate the emergence and spread of resistant K. pneumoniae. This convergence of advanced age, comorbid illness, and extensive medical interventions among our patients highlights a perfect storm of vulnerability for CRCR-KP bloodstream infections.

The overall mortality rate in this study was an alarming 77.8%, which approaches the upper end of or even exceeds the 30–70% mortality range reported in prior outbreaks of colistin-resistant K. pneumoniae (2,4–5). Notably, survivors tended to have shorter hospital stays and fewer comorbid conditions, suggesting that patients with less severe underlying illness or more prompt, effective therapy had better outcomes. These findings underscore how critically ill and immunocompromised patients are at extreme risk for poor outcomes once infected with CRCR-KP. Every delay in appropriate treatment or inability to clear the infection can be life-threatening in this population. The high mortality observed, despite aggressive care, emphasizes the urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies and robust preventive measures (Table 1).

A review of antibiotic regimens revealed that all patients received carbapenems, primarily meropenem or imipenem, and/or colistin, often in combination with additional agents such as fluoroquinolones, ampicillin–sulbactam, or vancomycin. Notably, these treatments were prescribed empirically, without guidance from synergy-based testing, and frequently included agents to which the bacterial isolates were resistant. Despite the use of multiple antibiotics, 14 of 18 patients (77.8%) died, suggesting that these combinations lacked sufficient synergy or bactericidal activity. For instance, colistin was administered in at least five cases, typically alongside agents such as ciprofloxacin or vancomycin—neither of which demonstrated strong in vitro synergy in this study. In contrast, minocycline and chloramphenicol, both of which demonstrated robust in vitro synergy with colistin, were not included in any treatment regimen. These findings underscore the limitations of empirically selected multidrug therapies and highlight the clinical relevance of laboratory-confirmed synergistic combinations. Incorporating synergy testing into therapeutic decision-making may improve outcomes for patients with CRCR-KP bloodstream infections.

Phenotypic susceptibility testing revealed extensive resistance across most antibiotic classes. All isolates were resistant to colistin and carbapenems, with high MIC50 and MIC90 values far exceeding CLSI breakpoints. Two-thirds of isolates met the criteria for XDR status, and all were MDR. These resistance patterns eliminate many standard treatment options and emphasize the critical need for alternative therapeutic approaches. While tigecycline demonstrated partial activity (resistance rate 44.4%), its low serum levels limit its efficacy in bloodstream infections. Minocycline and amikacin retained some in vitro activity, with susceptibility observed in 66% and 50% of isolates, respectively, suggesting a potential role as components of combination therapy. Resistance to third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones was widespread, further narrowing viable therapeutic pathways (Table 2). These results reflect the serious threat posed by XDR K. pneumoniae in nosocomial settings and highlight the need for strengthened infection control, antibiotic stewardship, and innovative treatment regimens.

To address this therapeutic gap, colistin was evaluated in combination with 12 commonly used antibiotics using three drug interaction models: the Fractional Inhibitory Concentration Index (FICI), Loewe additivity, and Bliss independence. Each model provided distinct, complementary insights. FICI, a traditional and widely used method, indicated synergy in 75% of combinations tested. The Loewe model, which assumes shared mechanisms of action, detected synergy in 50% of combinations. The Bliss model, which evaluates combinations of independently acting drugs, identified synergy in 41.6% of cases. While FICI provides clear numerical cutoffs (e.g., FICI ≤ 0.5 for synergy), it may oversimplify interactions, as it does not capture dose-dependent shifts between synergy, additivity, and antagonism. The Loewe and Bliss models, in contrast, assess response across multiple concentration levels and can detect context-specific interaction dynamics. Together, these tools enabled a robust evaluation of drug interactions and identified promising combinations that may help overcome resistance10–12,15.

Among the combinations tested, colistin paired with clindamycin, chloramphenicol, erythromycin, and minocycline consistently demonstrated synergy across all three models (p < 0.05). The colistin–clindamycin combination showed the strongest and most consistent synergy, with a median FICI of 0.02 and high synergy scores in both Loewe (median 49.9) and Bliss (median 34.4) models. Heat map and 3D surface plots revealed maximal synergy at colistin concentrations of 2–4 µg/mL combined with 16–32 µg/mL of clindamycin, substantially reducing bacterial growth (Fig. 1). However, at higher concentrations (≥ 32 µg/mL colistin and ≥ 128 µg/mL clindamycin), synergy declined and slight antagonism was observed. These findings emphasize the importance of optimizing dosage, as indiscriminate escalation may reduce efficacy or increase toxicity. Prior studies support this combination’s potential to reduce Enterobacteriaceae loads and delay resistance16.

Minocycline also demonstrated reliable synergy with colistin, with median FICI of 0.27 and moderate Loewe and Bliss scores (27.7 and 29.2, respectively). The most effective combinations occurred at low colistin concentrations (1–2 µg/mL) paired with minocycline at 4–8 µg/mL, as supported by heat map and response surface analysis (Fig. 2). These findings align with previous reports on colistin–minocycline synergy against MDR/XDR K. pneumoniae17. Additionally, this combination may allow for lower colistin dosing, potentially reducing nephrotoxicity.

The colistin–chloramphenicol combination exhibited a median FICI of 0.09, Loewe synergy score of 56.9, and Bliss score of 57.8, indicating strong synergy, particularly at 2–4 µg/mL of both agents (Fig. 4). This synergy likely reflects a pharmacodynamic complementarity between membrane disruption and protein synthesis inhibition. However, antagonism emerged at higher chloramphenicol levels (32–64 µg/mL) and colistin concentrations above 32 µg/mL, again highlighting the importance of dose optimization. Previous studies have reported similar efficacy against NDM-producing K. pneumoniae, though chloramphenicol’s known risk of bone marrow suppression warrants caution18.

Erythromycin also displayed moderate synergy with colistin, with a median FICI of 0.07 and Loewe and Bliss scores of 37.0 and 35.4, respectively. Synergy peaked at 2 µg/mL colistin and 64 µg/mL erythromycin, reducing bacterial growth by up to 80% (Fig. 3). These results suggest erythromycin may act as a colistin potentiator, although its low serum levels limit systemic application. Nonetheless, it could hold value in localized delivery or specific infection sites.

The mechanisms underlying these synergies are likely multifactorial. Colistin compromises membrane integrity, thereby enhancing the intracellular uptake of ribosome-targeting agents. These antibiotics, which bind to distinct sites on the 30–50 S subunits, inhibit translation through diverse conformational mechanisms. Colistin-induced envelope stress may also disrupt efflux systems and metabolic homeostasis, thereby sensitizing bacteria to intracellular antibiotic attacks. The diversity of ribosomal binding sites and their associated conformational effects likely account for the observed variability in synergy strength. For example, clindamycin binds to the 50 S subunit, while minocycline targets the 30 S subunit; nevertheless, both exhibit effective synergy with colistin19. Therefore, the observed reductions in colistin MICs may be attributed to its ability to disrupt outer membrane integrity, thereby enhancing intracellular access of partner drugs such as minocycline and clindamycin and restoring antibacterial activity at clinically relevant concentrations.

The clinical relevance of our in vitro findings depends on whether the synergistic concentrations observed are achievable with standard intravenous dosing used for bloodstream infections. In checkerboard assays, colistin exhibited synergy with several agents at concentrations ranging from 1 to 4 µg/mL. These levels correspond well with steady-state plasma concentrations (approximately 2–4 µg/mL) typically attained with intravenous colistimethate sodium at 2–3 million IU every 8 h. Among the synergistic partners, minocycline demonstrated synergy at 4–8 µg/mL, aligning with serum levels (2–8 µg/mL) achieved after 100–200 mg IV every 12 h. Similarly, chloramphenicol exhibited synergy at 2–4 µg/mL, which falls within its therapeutic serum range (5–20 µg/mL) when administered at 50–100 mg/kg/day. In contrast, clindamycin required 16–32 µg/mL to achieve synergy, exceeding its typical peak plasma levels (~ 10–15 µg/mL) following 600–900 mg IV every 8 h, suggesting limited feasibility for systemic use. Erythromycin required even higher concentrations (32–64 µg/mL), substantially above achievable serum levels (1–4 µg/mL), thereby limiting its clinical applicability in bloodstream infections. Collectively, these findings support the translational potential of colistin–minocycline and colistin–chloramphenicol as pharmacokinetically feasible and potentially effective combination therapies against CRCR-KP, warranting further in vivo and clinical investigation20.

Although this was an in vitro study, the findings have several potential clinical implications. The observed synergy between colistin and agents such as minocycline and clindamycin at clinically achievable concentrations suggests that these combinations may enhance bactericidal activity against CRCR-KP strains. These results support the development of targeted combination regimens as salvage therapy, particularly for patients with limited treatment options. Moreover, the consistency of synergy across multiple analytical models provides a rationale for prioritizing these combinations in future pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) studies and clinical trials. Finally, these findings may inform antimicrobial stewardship initiatives by identifying colistin-based combinations with retained activity against extensively drug-resistant organisms.

In conclusion, this study provides strong in vitro evidence that colistin, in combination with protein synthesis inhibitors—particularly minocycline, clindamycin, chloramphenicol, and erythromycin—exhibits significant synergistic activity against CRCR-KP bloodstream isolates. While minocycline appears to be the most clinically viable partner based on pharmacokinetic considerations, further in vivo studies and clinical trials are essential to validate these findings and support their use in high-risk patient populations.
