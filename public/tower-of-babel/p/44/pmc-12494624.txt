Single-arm meta-analysis on robotic spine instrumentation for young patients
Authors: Paweł Łajczak, Anna Łajczak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRobot-assisted surgery (RAS) is increasingly used among younger patients. However, current literature often focuses on pairwise comparisons, which limits a comprehensive evaluation of robotic effectiveness across diverse conditions. This single-arm meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of RAS among younger patients undergoing posterior spinal fusion.MethodsA systematic search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library from inception to December 2024. Studies were included if they reported outcomes of RAS for pedicle screw placement in patients with a mean age under 25 years. The primary outcome was the accuracy of pedicle screw placement, assessed using the Gertzbein and Robbins (GR) grading system. Secondary outcomes included intraoperative outcomes, radiation exposure, and correction of deformity.ResultsFifteen studies encompassing 13,325 screws and 920 patients were included. The pooled rate of clinically acceptable screws (GR A + B) was 95.66% (95% CI: 94.04–97.28), with 88.54% (95% CI: 83.60–93.48) achieving perfect placement (GR A). The incidence of heavily misplaced screws (GR D + E) was 0.59% (95% CI: 0.18–1.16). Blood loss averaged 687.85 mL (95% CI: 534.27–841.43), and the mean operation time was 306.03 min (95% CI: 273.19–338.88). Curve correction rates and Cobb angle improvements were consistent with existing scoliosis treatment benchmarks. Heterogeneity was observed, likely due to variability in study designs, robotic systems, and patient populations.ConclusionRAS demonstrates high pedicle screw accuracy and safety in pediatric and adolescent spine surgery, with low rates of extreme screw misplacement. Adoption of this technology requires overcoming current limitations.Keywords: Robot, Assisted surgery, Pediatric spine surgery, Pedicle screw accuracy, Scoliosis, Single, Arm meta, Analysis

Introduction

Robot-assisted surgery (RAS) has become increasingly present in the fields of neurological and orthopedic surgery. Robots are used in epilepsy surgery, endovascular applications, and hemorrhage drainage [1–3]. Robotic devices were implemented to minimize physiological human limitations, which include fatigue and hand tremors [4].

These human limitations are especially problematic in pediatric and adolescent spine surgery, as precise placement of pedicle screws is a crucial factor in determining the clinical success of the intervention. Spine surgeons encounter different spine conditions among younger patients, including adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS), juvenile-onset idiopathic scoliosis (JIS), early-onset scoliosis (EOS), neuromuscular scoliosis (NMS), syndromic scoliosis (SS), congenital scoliosis (CS), spondylolisthesis, Scheuermann’s kyphosis, trauma, and more.

Misplacement of the screws carries a risk of major, life-threatening complications, which include nerve root injury, damage to the spinal cord, vascular complications, infections, and pulmonary and cardiac complications [5–7]. Screw deviations not only increase perioperative complications but also increase additional costs for surgery revisions and further treatment. Therefore, to overcome human limitations and minimize human errors, more advanced equipment was implemented, especially in pediatric spine surgery.

Novel technologies were developed to reduce the misplacement rate. Three-dimensional printing, custom spine reference models, preoperative CT scans, computer-assisted 3D infrared navigation, and finally, robotic devices. Robotic models, such as Mazor X Stealth, Renaissance, Cirq, or TianJi, perform precise screw placement with highly maneuverable surgical arms. Surgical planning is meticulously performed to achieve the most accurate placement possible. Recent reports from spine studies on adult populations suggest reduced radiation dosage with RAS compared to conventional freehand [8]. Surgical robots show high accuracy in the literature, often surpassing other modalities [9, 10].

While these advantages over conventional methods are present in the current literature, notably, most of the current meta-analyses focus on pairwise comparison rather than evaluating solely the robotic effectiveness. This not only leads to a focus on specific conditions (i.e., idiopathic scoliosis), excluding other pediatric and adolescent conditions, but also omits single-arm studies, which frequently analyze large numbers of pedicle screws and patients. Therefore, instead of focusing on multi-arm studies, this single-arm meta-analysis aims to fill the gap in the current literature by focusing solely on the effectiveness of robotic surgery among pediatric and adolescent patients. This study aims to observe potential differences between different clinical conditions, compare the effectiveness between studies, and propose future directions.

Methods

This single-arm meta-analysis was performed following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement and the revised edition of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [11, 12].

PICO framework and inclusion criteria

The PICO framework can be described as follows:

Outcomes: At least one of pedicle screw accuracy, blood loss, operation time, length of hospital stay, curve correction rate, number of fluoroscopy scans, and Cobb angle change

Outcomes: At least one of pedicle screw accuracy, blood loss, operation time, length of hospital stay, curve correction rate, number of fluoroscopy scans, and Cobb angle change

This single-arm meta-analysis included studies that (1) reported the effectiveness of RAS for pedicle screw placement; (2) included patients with any spine condition requiring posterior-approach fixation surgery; (3) had a mean age of patients under 25 years; and (4) provided sufficient data to perform a meta-analysis. Non-English studies were translated.

The following records were excluded: (1) studies with overlapping populations (verified by institution affiliation and inclusion date range); (2) abstract-only; (3) reporting non-primary data—including editorials, review papers, or other meta-analyses; and (4) case series with ≤ 5 patients.

b) Database search

Database search

Both MeSH/Emtree terms and keywords were used to create a search strategy for PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library. The search was performed from inception. The full string for each respective database is provided in the Appendix. No filters or other restrictions were used. Extracted database files were imported into the Rayyan screening software.

c) Screening

Screening

Screening was performed by two authors independently. Conflicts were resolved through mutual agreement with all authors. Both title/abstract and full-text screening were performed independently. The PRISMA flow diagram was used to visualize the screening process [12].

d) Data extraction

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed in Google Sheets. Two authors extracted data of interest from each included study. The summary of extracted data is presented in Table 1.

f)Quality assessment

Quality assessment

RoB2 and ROBINS-I tools were applied for quality assessment [18, 19]. Details on specific domains are available elsewhere. Results of the quality assessment were performed with robvis [20]. Publication bias was assessed with Egger’s test and visual inspection of the funnel plot. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for publication bias.

g)Statistical approach

Statistical approach

This single-arm meta-analysis was performed in R software with a meta package [21]. Conversion of medians was performed according to studies conducted by Wan and Luo [22, 23]. Inverse-variance (IV) random-effects meta-analysis was performed. Proportions and means were used to pool binary and continuous endpoints, respectively. 95% confidence intervals were used (95% CI). An assessment of heterogeneity was performed with an I2 index; an I2 value > 50% was considered as high heterogeneity.

Leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity analyses were performed for each outcome. Baujat plots were used to investigate potential outliers in the outcomes. Subgroups based on pathology type were performed; if more than one condition was present in the study, these studies were labeled as “multiple.” Additionally, subgroups on robot models were performed—studies with multiple robotic devices (without exclusive data for each robot) were excluded. A p value < 0.10 was considered significant for subgroup differences.

Meta-regression was performed with a random-effects model on four variables: age, percentage of female patients, year of study publication, and Cobb’s angle. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The search for the articles was performed from inception to December 2024. A systematic search resulted in 262 records from PubMed, 371 from Embase, 22 from Scopus, 171 from Web of Science, and 16 from the Cochrane Library. A total of 262 duplicate records were excluded. Finally, fifteen articles were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis, including two studies found manually [24–38]. The detailed search process is visualized in Fig. 1.

PRISMA flow diagram depicting the search process

Fifteen studies were included in this systematic review. There were two randomized trials, seven pairwise studies, and six single-arm studies. Eight studies assessed RAS on AIS patients, one on CS, and the remaining six had multiple conditions. Five studies used Renaissance, five used TianJi/TINAVI, three used Mazor X, one study used Mazor X and Renaissance, and one used Cirq. A total of 13,325 screws and 920 patients were analyzed in this study. Detailed characteristics are available in Table 3.

a) Risk of bias.

The risk of bias was assessed with two tools: RoB2 and ROBINS-I. Included randomized studies showed a low risk of bias, but the randomization process was not sufficiently described. There was no observational study with a low risk of bias. Most of the concerns were found in the measurement of the outcomes domain and confounding. Concerns were also found in the selection of reported results, missing data, and the selection of participants. A detailed summary of quality assessment from both tools is available in Fig. 2.

b) Meta-analysis

Meta-analysis

GR A screws

GR A screws

The number of GR A screws was reported in 10 studies. The overall proportion of GR A screws was 88.54% [95% CI 83.60; 93.48]. Heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 98%). There was no difference between pathology subgroups (p = 0.51). Figure 3 shows the forest plot for GR A screws. LOO analysis did not reduce heterogeneity. Meta-regression analyses did not provide significant results (all p > 0.05).

Proportion of GR A screws. Subgroups are based on spinal conditions

GR B Screws

GR B Screws

Ten studies reported the number of GRB screws. The overall proportion of GR B screws was 7.66% [95% CI 4.41; 10.91]. Again, high heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 96%). LOO analysis did not reduce heterogeneity. A statistically significant meta-regression results were observed for the percentage of female patients (Beta = -0.0011, p = 0.019).

GR A + B (clinically acceptable)

GR A + B (clinically acceptable)

Twelve studies reported the number of GR A + B screws. Meta-analysis pooled a proportion of 95.66% [95% CI 94.04; 97.28] with high heterogeneity in the analysis (I2 = 93%). Studies with multiple pathologies showed higher accuracy (97.02%) when compared to AIS (93.81%)—p < 0.01. LOO analysis did not influence heterogeneity. All four meta-regression analyses were insignificant.

A sensitivity analysis was performed with studies using different grading systems of pedicles, as long as they provided proportion of clinically acceptable screws. In total, all fifteen studies were eligible for this analysis. Meta-analysis pooled a proportion of 96.18% [95% CI 95.27; 97.10]; however, heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 96%)—Fig. 4. Funnel plot asymmetry was observed (Fig. 5), and publication bias was confirmed with Egger’s test (p < 0.001). LOO did not influence high I2. Again, all meta-regression analyses were insignificant.

Forest plot for the number of satisfactory screws (sensitivity analysis)

Funnel plot for satisfactory (clinically acceptable) screws (sensitivity analysis)

GR C

GR C

GR C was reported in eleven studies. The overall proportion of GR C screws was 2.71% [95% CI 1.61; 3.81]. High heterogeneity was present in the analyses (I2 = 87%). Studies with multiple pathologies had a lower proportion (2.03%) compared to AIS-only studies (4.02%). High heterogeneity remained after sensitivity analyses. Performed meta-regressions did not yield significant results.

GR D + E

GR D + E

The number of heavily misplaced screws was reported in eleven studies—Fig. 6. RAS had an overall 0.59% [95% 0.18; 1.16] incidence for GR D + E screws. However, high heterogeneity was present in the analyses (I2 = 81%). There was no difference between subgroups (p = 0.55). LOO analysis did not yield significant changes in heterogeneity. Similarly, meta-regression did not provide statistically significant outcomes.

Forest plot for GR D + E screws

GR E

GR E

This outcome was not analyzed for studies reporting four-scale GR grading. The overall incidence of GR E screws (breach > 6 mm) was 0.24 [95% CI 0.00; 0.60] from seven studies—Fig. 7. Heterogeneity was present (I2 = 58%). In LOO, after excluding Macke (2016), heterogeneity dropped to 29%.

Forest plot for GR E screws

Blood loss (mL)

Blood loss (mL)

Blood loss was reported in thirteen studies in this meta-analysis—Fig. 8. Mean blood loss was 687.85 mL [95% CI 534.27; 841.43]. Very high heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 99%). Interestingly, a difference between subgroups of pathologies was observed (p < 0.01)—multiple pathology groups had lower blood loss compared to AIS and CS. LOO did not resolve high heterogeneity—in all cases, I2 was above 99%. Meta-regression did not yield any significant results.

Forest plot for blood loss

Operation time (min)

Operation time (min)

Operation time was reported in twelve studies—Fig. 9. The mean operation time was 306.03 min [95% CI 273.19; 338.88]. High heterogeneity was again observed (I2 > 95%). LOO analysis did not resolve heterogeneity.

Forest plot for operation time

Significant meta-regression results were observed: operation time with mean age of patients (Beta = 31.70, p = 0.012) and percentage of female patients in the study (Beta = 2.63, p = 0.0371)—Fig. 10.

Hospital stay (days)

Hospital stay (days)

Hospital stays varied highly between eight studies. The overall pooled mean stay was 7.30 days [95% CI 5.53; 9.08]; however, high heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 99%). Subgroups remained with high I2. LOO did not influence heterogeneity change.

Curve correction

Curve correction

The curve correction rate was reported in five studies. The overall correction rate was 71.16% [95% CI 62.20; 80.12]. High heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 98%). LOO analysis did not change high heterogeneity.

Cobb angle change

Cobb angle change

Mean Cobb angle change was reported in eight studies. The mean Cobb angle change was − 39.09 [95% CI − 44.07; − 34.11] with high I2 (94%). LOO did not change high heterogeneity.

Number of fluoroscopy scans

Number of fluoroscopy scans

The mean number of fluoroscopy scans was 7.31 [95% CI 4.12; 10.50]. Heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 99.6%), which was not resolved through sensitivity analysis.

Comparison of different robotic systems

Comparison of different robotic systems

Regarding the accuracy of pedicle screws, Mazor X achieved the highest proportion of GR A screws (94.73%, 95% CI 88.30; 100.00, p = 0.0516) and GR A + B screws (98.78%, 95% CI 97.03; 100.00, p = 0.001). Additionally, the Mazor X subgroup achieved the lowest proportion of GR D + E screws (0.00%, 95% CI 0.00; 0.43, p = 0.0952) and GR E screws (0.00%, 95% CI 0.00; 0.43, p = 0.0413).

The Cirq subgroup achieved the lowest blood loss (mean 313.96, 95% CI 294.27; 333.65, p < 0.0001). The lowest number of fluoroscopy scans was observed in the Cirq subgroup (mean 1.50, 95% CI 1.48; 1.52, p < 0.0001). The highest Cobb angle change (mean -42.36, 95% CI -48.44; -36.29, p = 0.0156) was observed in the TianJi group. The Mazor X subgroup exhibited the shortest length of hospitalization (mean 4.10, 95% CI 3.74; 4.46, p < 0.0001). There was no significant difference between robotic models in curve correction rate (p = 0.2118) or operation time (p = 0.4367).

Comparison of RAS effectiveness on different spine sections

Comparison of RAS effectiveness on different spine sections

Only two studies analyzed and provided results for different sections of the spine; therefore, this section was synthesized narratively. Hou et al. showed that the proportion of satisfactory screws with RAS was 89.7% in thoracic scoliosis and 90.68% in thoracolumbar/lumbar scoliosis [25]. Morse et al. [36] evaluated T2-T3, T4-T6, T7-T12, and lumbar segments. They found a significant difference in terms of operation time, and T2-T3 vertebral levels required more time compared to T7-T12 vertebrae (5.4 min ± 2.8 vs. 3.5 min ± 2.7; p = 0.014) and when compared to T4-T6 segments (5.4 min ± 2.8 vs. 2.9 min ± 1.3; p = 0.002). No significant difference was observed compared to lumbar segments. The highest trajectory accuracy was achieved in the lumbar segments, while T4-T6 segments yielded the lowest accuracy (92.3% vs. 56.8%; p = 0.001).

Discussion

This single-arm meta-analysis aimed to explore the effectiveness of RAS among younger populations and fill the existing gap in the literature.

The number of clinically acceptable screws achieved by RAS exceeded 95% (GR A + B: 95.66%; sensitivity analysis with other grading systems: 96.18%). High accuracy of the robotic systems can be achieved through robotic arm stabilization, advanced software, and preoperative trajectory planning. The high accuracy of pedicle screws is generally consistent with the current literature.

Recent network meta-analysis evaluated freehand guidance, RAS, and computer navigation among adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis [39]. RAS achieved significantly higher odds for perfect and acceptable pedicle screws compared to conventional freehand guidance. Interestingly, RAS showed no superiority over computer navigation in terms of pedicle screw accuracy. Moreover, improved accuracy did not translate directly into improved radiographic or clinical outcomes compared to freehand or computer navigation. This raises the question about the potential cost-effectiveness of these systems among pediatric patients.

Cui and colleagues compared RAS and conventional freehand among patients with scoliosis [40]. The number of clinically acceptable screws showed a significant increase in the RAS group. The RAS cohort exhibited a longer procedure time compared to the conventional freehand technique. On the other hand, the length of hospitalization was shorter in the RAS group. However, their review included studies with both adolescent and adult patients.

A similar meta-analysis on RAS in scoliosis patients yielded comparable conclusions in terms of improved pedicle screw accuracy over freehand navigation and also navigation-assisted surgery [41]. Authors also confirmed in their results longer operation times with RAS compared to freehand or computer navigation. Similar to the previous review, this study also combined pediatric and adult patients.

De Vega and colleagues performed a systematic review on the accuracy of different navigation techniques for pediatric-only spinal fusion [42]. Their study showed that freehand navigation achieved the lowest accuracy (78.35%), while the modified freehand technique (non-image guidance) achieved the highest accuracy of 95.86%. However, their review had not identified any RAS studies and was limited solely to screw accuracy. Moreover, the modified freehand described by the authors included various navigation techniques, ranging from 3D-printed drill templates to electronic conductivity or electromyography.

While current synthesis and available literature suggest enhanced pedicle screw accuracy, studies show prolonged operation time with the use of RAS. An extended mean operation time (306.03 min) with the use of RAS for spinal fusion was observed. Kang and colleagues showed longer operation time with RAS surgery compared to conventional freehand surgery (MD 31.52, [95% CI 5.70, 57.35], p = 0.02) [43]. Al-Naseem and colleagues, in their meta-analysis of RAS vs. freehand vs. navigation for posterior fixation of scoliosis (which included both adolescent and adult patients), showed that RAS has prolonged operation time compared to navigation (MD 10.74 [95% CI 3.52, 17.97], p = 0.004) and freehand (MD 40.27 [95% CI 20.90, 59.64], p < 0.0001) [41]. The learning curve could highly influence the total operation time and reduce blood loss with prolonged surgery.

Operation time is prolonged due to the need for trajectory planning and setting up surgical equipment for RAS. Longer surgery times may be associated with greater volume of bleeding, higher incidence of complications, including infections, and patients being longer under anesthesia [44]. This aspect is especially important among younger patients. Kang and colleagues in their AIS-only meta-analysis showed no difference in blood loss between RAS and freehand surgery (MD -59.05, [95% CI -212.81, 94.70], p = 0.45) [43]. Further research is needed to explore whether RAS may impact the potential blood loss. Moreover, future trials should study how preoperative planning may influence blood loss.

Furthermore, currently available robotic workstations are unable to perform an entire scan of spinal deformity, requiring multiple scans on a single patient. Multiple fluoroscopy scans lead to increased cumulative radiation doses for both patient and surgical staff. This aspect is especially important, as prolonged radiation exposure may be associated with a higher risk of cancer development; therefore, proper safety steps need to be undertaken to minimize this risk [45].

Study limitations

Study limitations

The major drawback of this analysis is the presence of high heterogeneity across different outcomes. Differences come from various study designs, pediatric populations, spine deformities, robotic systems used, the experience of the surgical staff, the learning curve, differences in reporting of outcomes, and surgical methodology. In most of the analyses, heterogeneity could not be explained; therefore, the aforementioned factors and the surgeon’s experience could highly influence the variations. Sensitivity analyses and meta-regressions, in most cases, did not yield significant results. Standardization of both methods and reporting is needed to provide more homogeneity.

Observed publication bias (confirmed by Egger’s test, p < 0.05) may limit the generalizability of findings from this paper. This leads to the situation where insignificant or small-effect studies are not published or not available in the literature, resulting in an overoptimistic true effect size of the meta-analysis. The full-focus single-arm meta-analysis, while addressing a specific gap, precludes direct comparisons with conventional methods.

Finally, while short-term outcomes, including pedicle screw accuracy, blood loss, and operation time, are well documented, data on long-term outcomes, including quality of life, remain limited or even absent in the included papers. Additionally, cost-effectiveness analysis could not be performed due to a lack of data.

b)Future directions

Future directions

To overcome these limitations, there are several aspects to consider in future trials. Firstly, studies should adopt standardized grading systems (more specifically, Gertzbein and Robbins grading). This will facilitate cross-study comparisons and enhance future analyses between different methodologies. Furthermore, reliance on four-scale grading systems of the GR scale could limit the assessment of pedicle screws. Therefore, a five-scale (A to E) grading could be used in future trials.

Future studies should also provide analyses on the quality of life and cost-effectiveness to justify the purchase of such expensive systems. The integration of novel technology, including extended reality and artificial intelligence, could even enhance preoperative planning and patient outcomes.

Conclusions

RAS demonstrates high pedicle screw accuracy and safety in pediatric and adolescent spine surgery, with low rates of extreme screw misplacement. Adoption of this technology requires overcoming current limitations. More randomized studies with standardized reporting are needed to further support claims.
