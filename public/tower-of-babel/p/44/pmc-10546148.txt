Providing the basis for a patient-centred and effective screening for cancer-related fatigue (MERLIN study): design of a longitudinal observational study
Authors: Patricia Blickle, Alexander Haussmann, Bernhard Holzner, Anne Katrin Berger, Karen Steindorf, Martina E Schmidt
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCancer-related fatigue (CRF) is a frequent and burdensome sequela of cancer and cancer therapies. It can persist from months to years and has a substantial impact on patients’ quality of life and functioning. CRF is often still not adequately diagnosed and insufficiently treated. According to guideline recommendations, patients should be routinely screened for CRF from cancer diagnosis onwards. We will investigate how an effective screening should be designed regarding timing, frequency, screening type and cut-off points.Methods and analysisMERLIN is a longitudinal observational study that will include 300 patients with cancer at the beginning of cancer therapy. The main study centre is the National Center for Tumour Diseases Heidelberg, Germany. Patients answer five items to shortly screen for CRF at high frequency during their therapy and at lower frequency during the post-treatment phase for 18 months. Further, CRF is assessed at wider intervals based on the Cella criteria, the Brief Fatigue Inventory impact scale, the quality of life fatigue questionnaire (QLQ-FA12) and the fatigue and cognitive items of the quality of life core questionnaire (QLQ-C30), both of the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer. Important psychological, socio-demographical or medical factors, which may exacerbate CRF are assessed. All assessments are performed online. Receiver operating curves, areas under the curve, sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values and likelihood ratios will be calculated to determine optimal short screening modalities.Ethics and disseminationThe study was approved by the ethics committee of the Medical Faculty of the Heidelberg University, Germany (approval number: S-336/2022). Written informed consent is obtained from all participants. The study is conducted in full conformance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Results will be published in peer-reviewed scientific journals, presented at conferences and communicated to clinical stakeholders to foster the implementation of an effective CRF management.Trial registration numberClinicalTrials.gov; registration number: NCT05448573.Keywords: fatigue, oncology, clinical trial, patient-centered care, patient reported outcome measures

Introduction

Cancer-related fatigue (CRF) is among the most common and most distressing physical and psychosocial sequelae precipitated by cancer and its treatment and can persist from months to even years. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) defines it as a perpetual feeling of ‘physical, emotional or cognitive tiredness or exhaustion related to cancer or cancer treatment that is not proportional to recent activity and interferes with usual functioning’.1 During chemotherapy or radiotherapy, up to about 85% of patients experience CRF.2–5 About one-third suffer from CRF more than 5 years post-treatment.6 7 As there are over 43.8 million people worldwide who were diagnosed with cancer in the past 5 years, with increasing tendencies,8 a huge number of individuals is estimated to suffer from CRF.

Beside the fact that CRF can lead to significant decrements in patients’ quality of life (QoL),9 it is significantly associated with lower recurrence-free and overall survival.10 CRF is also related to a high socioeconomic burden, hindering reintegration to social, familial and occupational life after treatment or even cure from cancer.10 11

CRF is not directly diagnosable by objective measurement methods such as blood tests, indicating that CRF can only be assessed by self-reports. Unfortunately, patients often do not address symptoms of fatigue to healthcare providers,12 for example, because they assume these symptoms could be an inevitable consequence of cancer disease and its treatment.10 11 Likewise, healthcare professionals (HCPs) sometimes do not notice or disregard signs of fatigue. As a result, CRF often remains undetected and thus untreated.13

According to the guideline recommendations of the NCCN,1 patients with cancer should be screened for the presence and severity of CRF on a regular basis. However, until today, there are no specific recommendations for the optimal timing, frequency and duration of the most appropriate screening items and cut-off points for fatigue. Due to this lack of a gold standard to identify CRF, establishing an effective screening tool is even more challenging. Furthermore, there is only limited research into how to implement a routine fatigue screening in clinical practice. One recent study investigated two CRF assessments in patients with breast cancer undergoing radiotherapy to detect important cut-offs of moderate-to-severe and severe fatigue.14 Here, it still remains unclear if the results are generalisable to other patients with cancer undergoing different treatments and if these assessment tools are feasible in clinical practice. Another study evaluated the ease of use of a Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) and a Fatigue Pictogram in cancer outpatient care.15 Although the results of this study showed that both screening programmes are equally applicable in practice, it remains an open question which time points and time intervals are most appropriate for screening of CRF. The European Society for Medical Oncology-guidelines have no definite, strict recommendation but suggest a quantitative scale, such as an NRS ranging from 0 to 10 with a cut-off ≥4.16 17 Other research yielded that three CRF screening questions based on the A1 criterion from the Cella criteria build another valid form of a brief CRF-measurement.13 Beside that, the Visual Analogue Scale for fatigue or energy level served as a screening tool in other studies.18 19 Based on a review of existing literature, Fisher and colleagues 20 recently evaluated several screening tools for CRF. Although the authors recommended four questionnaires for use in clinical practice, they noted a lack of evidence on their sensitivity as well as on specific cut-off values. Direct comparisons between different CRF questionnaires were also found to be rare. Thus, a comprehensive study investigating several CRF screening tools is still needed.

The MERLIN study (acronym for ‘Müdigkeit und Erschöpfung Rechtzeitig erkennen und LINdern’, the German equivalent of ‘recognizing and alleviating fatigue and exhaustion in good time’) is therefore one of the first studies that will evaluate reasonable screening time points, screening intervals and cut-offs for CRF based on different assessment tools and under consideration of potentially modifying factors such as age or gender. As there is no gold standard or a diagnosis for CRF in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), the MERLIN study will use the Cella criteria as the main reference standard, which have already been proposed for inclusion in the ICD.21 They are indicated to be valid and are expected to detect clinically significant fatigue reliably.18 21 More precisely, previous studies showed that the Cella criteria are well accepted by patients and have good reliability (Cronbach’s alpha ranging between 0.82 and 0.87) and validity, suggesting that they can discriminate between fatigued and non-fatigued patients.13 21 22

The MERLIN study aims to answer how a CRF screening tool should be designed for use in clinical practice to reliably and effectively detect fatigue in patients with cancer. In this regard, the primary objectives of the study are to determine (1) valid screening questions, (2) reasonable screening time points and (3) valid cut-off points dependent on patient characteristics.

Secondary aims are: (1) to explore typical patterns and courses of CRF and their associations to cancer treatment and patient characteristics, and (2) to reveal associations of psychological factors, such as emotional distress or coping, with the course of CRF.

Methods and analysis

Study design and timeline

The MERLIN study is a longitudinal observational study. The study scheme is displayed in figure 1. There are three levels of assessments: Level 1=Short Fatigue Screening (SFS), level 2=Comprehensive Fatigue Assessment (CFA), level 3=Cofactor Measurement (CM). The scheduled assessments are listed in detail in online supplemental file 1. SFS starts on a weekly basis with time intervals progressively extending, finally reaching 6 weeks. Varying SFS intervals were chosen because the beginning of active cancer treatment (narrow screening intervals) is expected to cause greater fluctuations of CRF compared with the later phase of treatment (wider intervals). CFA are first at 6-week intervals (CFA 0 – CFA 4), then at 8-week (CFA 4 – CFA 6) and finally at 12-week intervals (CFA 6 – CFA 9). CFA will help to determine the selection, time points and cut-offs of the final SFS. CM will be taken at baseline (CM 0) and after 3, 6, 12 and 18 months (CM 1 – CM 4). All assessments are performed online.

To provide support for individuals and foster motivation to access the study platform and complete the questionnaires, participants in the MERLIN study will receive informational materials and advice on managing CRF based on scientific evidence (ie, short texts covering education on CRF). In addition, they will be given contacts for further possible supportive care and treatment of CRF.

Study population

The MERLIN study aims to include 300 patients with cancer before or within the first month of their systemic cancer therapy or radiotherapy. Eligibility criteria allows individuals who have received a cancer diagnosis of any entity and any stage, are at least 18 years old, and have the ability to consent to the study to be included in MERLIN. Exclusion criteria comprise a systemic cancer therapy or radiotherapy within the last 6 months, except hormone therapy if unchanged in the last 6 months. In addition, individuals who are unable to understand the study protocol and to complete scheduled assessments during the study period (eg, for language, cognitive, medical or organisational reasons) or unable to complete the survey online via smartphone, personal computer (PC) or tablet will not be included in the study. All participants have to provide informed consent prior to study enrolment.

Recruitment

Patients with cancer scheduled for a systemic cancer therapy or radiotherapy at the National Center for Tumor Diseases Heidelberg or at another cooperating clinic or medical practice are informed verbally and with written materials about the MERLIN study by trained study personnel, and asked to participate. All questionnaires are completed online via access to a study platform. If participants wish support for login and use of the study platform by study personnel, they have the option to fill out the first online questionnaires immediately on-site after signing the informed consent form.

In addition, recruitment is enhanced through information material on the MERLIN study being available online via various institution or patient platforms. Interested individuals are directed to a contact form on the study website and study personnel will contact them by telephone or via email. If interested persons meet the eligibility criteria, they will be informed about the study procedures. On completion of the informed consent form online they will be enrolled in the study.

Sample size calculation

As the primary objective of the study is to determine whether SFS items can be used as a screening tool, it has to be ensured that the considered tool has a sufficiently high degree of sensitivity, whereas a lower degree of specificity might be tolerated. Here, the required sample size depends on the prevalence of CRF, with a larger sample required for lower prevalence.19

The sample size calculation focused on the precision of the estimates, that is, the width of the CI around the sensitivity and the specificity. A sample size of N=300 is considered appropriate to estimate 95% CIs of sensitivity and specificity with a precision of at least ±10% for the majority of anticipated scenarios.23 In our study, with an estimation of CRF prevalence of 70% (as observed during cancer treatment in previous studies4 24), we expect to detect CRF with a sensitivity of 80% and a specificity of 65%. Given by the larger sample size needed to estimate specificity when prevalence is high, a sample size of N=292 with a precision of ±10% is required (see table 1).19 25 Due to potential dropouts or non-response of some patients after enrolment, recruitment will continue until sufficient SFS and CFA data from time points CFA 0 to CFA 1 is available of 300 patients.

Over the course of the study, the prevalence may decrease to 30% during follow-up. Even then, the planned sample size is still sufficient to detect CRF with satisfactory sensitivity and specificity.

Data collection and management

All screening items and questionnaires are implemented in the Electronic Data Capture-System CHES (Computer Health Evaluation System) of the company ESD GmbH in Austria.26 Participants will be individually informed by email about their study enrolment by providing login data. They will subsequently be informed whenever new questionnaires, depending on the study time point, are available in the CHES portal. Participants have the option to answer the questionnaires until the next assessment time point starts (eg, during their first weeks of study participation they have 7 days to answer the items, before the new time interval starts). If they do not answer in the required time interval, the measurement will be counted as missing. Participants are reminded on an automated basis via email (usually shortly before the time interval to answer ends) and/or via phone calls from study personnel (usually before the time interval of a ‘cofactor measurement’ time point ends). All data will be stored in local data-protected servers. Where appropriate, range checks are implemented in the system to avoid implausible data values by programming answer fields in such a way that only meaningful values can be entered. Final data will further be checked for data quality. A data monitoring board is not necessary for this observational study.

Outcomes and assessments

Short fatigue screening items

SFS includes a total of five items: Based on Criterion A1 of the Cella criteria,13 the items ‘significant fatigue’ (A1.1), ‘diminished energy’ (A1.2) and ‘increased need to rest, disproportionate to any recent change in activity level’ (A1.3) are used, as these items showed good properties to discriminate between fatigued and non-fatigued patients.18 Besides, patients will be asked to rate their perceived level of energy in the last 7 days on an NRS from 0=completely exhausted to 10=full of energy.18 19 To address the impact of CRF, patients will be asked to answer how much their fatigue affected their daily life in recent days on a scale ranging from 0=not affected at all to 10=extremely affected. The use of an NRS is in line with the NCCN guideline recommendations and aims to provide a balanced score for CRF screening.22 The exact wording of all five screening items can be found in online supplemental file 2. The data obtained in this way will serve as a major comprehensive database from which the statistical calculations for the screening tool characteristics can be derived.

Comprehensive Fatigue Assessments including Cella criteria

CFA consists of the 10 remaining A-scale Cella criteria (dichotomous items),18 the quality of life fatigue questionnaire by the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) QLQ-FA12,27 28 the impact scale of the Brief Fatigue Inventory (BFI)29 as well as five items of the QoL core questionnaire EORTC QLQ-C30 (three fatigue items and two cognitive items).30 At two time points (CFA 1 and CFA 5), we assess the effects of physical, emotional and cognitive fatigue on restrictions in everyday life, helplessness and worries.

Cofactor Measurements and further outcomes

Patients will be asked to rate their QoL (assessed with the EORTC QLQ-C30),30 emotional distress (Patient Health Questionnaire, the longer PHQ-9 at CM 0 and PHQ-4 at the subsequent assessments),31 32 sleep disturbances (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index),33 distress (Distress Thermometer)34 and pain with two items of the Pain Catastrophising Scale30 35 (besides the two pain items of the EORTC QLQ-C30). Additionally, they will indicate their social support (with the scales for social support during diseases; SSUK-8),36 physical activity levels and coping with fatigue37 and are asked to report their diagnosed pre-existing diseases and socio-demographic data.

As further cofactors, cancer treatments and cancer characteristics will be extracted from the clinical documentation systems, as well as concomitant diseases, therapies and medication ongoing at baseline measurement and during the study. Participants enrolled via online recruitment will receive a questionnaire to self-report the required medical data.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will be performed by using SAS and IBM SPSS Statistics. All tests will be two-sided, using α=0.05 as level of significance. Baseline demographics and characteristics will be reported. Means and SDs will be presented for continuous normally distributed variables and medians and ranges for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables will be presented by frequencies (absolute numbers and percentages).

Definition and calculation of screening indices

The Cella criteria for CRF18 serve as the main reference standard for calculating any screening tool characteristics in this study.

Based on the SFS, different scores will be calculated and investigated (see table 2). Depending on the characteristics of the items, further combinations of short screenings may be investigated in explorative analyses. For each screening index and each time point CFA 0 – CFA 9, a receiver operating curve and an area under the curve (AUC) will be calculated. Screening indices with an AUC>0.7 are known to represent acceptable discrimination and will therefore be considered further in the analysis for sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive value (PPV and NPV) and likelihood ratios.38 39 Missing data will not be imputed.

Determination of appropriate screening indices and cut-offs

There exists no single criterion for determination of the optimal screening tool, as this depends on what is considered more important in a clinical setting, for example, avoiding false negatives (high sensitivity) or rather limiting false positives (high specificity).

As criteria for a good CRF screening tool, it is aimed to reach a sensitivity ≥85% which means that less than 15% CRF cases remain undetected at a screening time point. As there will be multiple screening time points, those cases might also be detected at a later time.40 Furthermore, the positive likelihood ratio (LR+, true positives/false positives) that indicates how much the screening improves the likelihood of correctly diagnosing CRF should be above two which is considered as clinically important.41 To further evaluate the best screening indices, other test characteristics such as specificity, misclassification-rate, LR−, NPV (=true negative/(true+false negative)) and PPV (=true positive/(true+false positive)), and the Youden Index might be taken into account.39 42–44

Validity of the screening indices based on best screening items and cut-offs identified will be further investigated by scales of the CFA. In this regard, physical, emotional, cognitive and total CRF scores (EORTC QLQ-FA12),27 28 fatigue impact (BFI)29 and fatigue score of EORTC QLQ-C30 (considering its threshold of clinical importance45) will be compared between positively versus negatively screened participants. Potential modification by patient characteristics (eg, sex, age, cancer entity, cancer stage) or CRF characteristics (considering physical, emotional and cognitive subdimension) will be explored.

Identification of reasonable screening time points

To identify reasonable fatigue screening time points, all available screening and comprehensive assessment data will be considered.

First, descriptive analysis of CRF severity, dimensions, impact and prevalence (depending on the different criteria) for each available time point (overall and stratified by sex, entity, cancer therapy and potential predisposing or perpetuating factors) will be conducted. The identification of reasonable time points recommended for screening will be determined depending on common CRF onset or persistence.

Analyses of cofactors and secondary endpoints

Secondary endpoints assessed as part of the CM (eg, emotional distress, sleep disturbances, social support, coping) will be analysed descriptively at each time point as well as over their course across all time points. Multiple regression analyses will be performed to investigate determinants and associated factors of secondary endpoints. Depending on the distribution of the considered endpoint, appropriate statistical tests for differences between patient groups may be performed.

Longitudinal analyses will be used to analyse associations of secondary endpoints with the course of CRF as well as to determine interrelations between them across different time points to identify potential mutual influences.

Ethics and dissemination

The study is conducted in full conformance with the principles of the ‘Declaration of Helsinki’. Approval by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty Heidelberg was obtained before recruiting the first patient (S-336/2022). Patients will be informed in writing and verbally about the nature and scope of the planned investigation before the start of the study. The consent to the study can be withdrawn at any time without giving reasons and without any disadvantages for further medical care. All study data are handled confidentially and in case of withdrawal from the study, no further data will be collected.

Results will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed scientific journals and presented at national and international conferences. Recommendations for the use of a screening tool for CRF in clinical practice may be given based on the study results. Findings will further be communicated to relevant stakeholders (eg, HCPs, patient representatives).

Recruitment for MERLIN started in October 2022 and is expected to continue until 2024.

Discussion

CRF is one of the most common side effects in the huge and further increasing population of patients with cancer. Thus, it is important to implement a CRF screening in routine clinical care in order to detect and treat it at an early stage.1

The longitudinal observational MERLIN study will provide new and valuable insights into reasonable screening time points, screening intervals and cut-offs for CRF based on different assessment tools and under consideration of potentially modifying factors. The main goal is the development of an optimal screening tool, that is, both effective and reliable in detecting CRF and takes into account the effort for patients and HCPs. The cofactor assessment of important CRF-related factors in the study, for example, emotional distress, pain and sleep disturbances, can provide a better understanding of the mechanisms contributing to the development and maintenance of CRF in patients with cancer. Thus, MERLIN will help answer other open research questions that consider the role of longitudinal and mutual influences of other (psychological) factors for the course of CRF.

Thanks to an electronic and standardised study scheme that is implemented through CHES, patients can participate in the study regardless of their place of residence. Participants will be informed via email and on a regular basis as soon as new questionnaires are available on the CHES study platform to ensure a high participation rate. Additionally, the study design, which includes a systematic and automated electronic recording of CRF and its related factors, may assure an enhanced data quality compared with an usual paper–pencil assessment of patient-reported outcomes.46 Moreover, this online assessment via smart phone, tablet or PC represents a feasible modality for future wide-spread CRF screening that is patient-friendly and also requires little personnel.

However, due to the complex study design with multiple measurement time points, it is possible that participants will not complete all questionnaires in the required time interval. Further, as the survey period spans 18 months, it is likely that patients will dropout during the course of the study for various reasons, such as disease progression. Additionally, highly fatigued patients may not be able to participate in the survey because they might be too exhausted to complete the required questionnaires. We thoroughly anticipate these barriers and implement countermeasures if necessary, such as regular reminder calls and providing more help to participants in completing the assessments. Notwithstanding these challenges, the MERLIN study will lay the foundation for a regular CRF screening in clinical practice and help ensure that future patients with cancer benefit from early detection of CRF and subsequent treatment. Overall, the MERLIN study will provide important information for the establishment of a patient-oriented fatigue screening. It will also help ensure that patient-reported outcomes during and after cancer treatment are routinely captured electronically and with high data quality. In the long run, our study may thus help healthcare professionals to reliably and easily screen patients with cancer for fatigue during and beyond the course of cancer treatment so that immediate countermeasures can be taken when signs of fatigue are detected. Thus, MERLIN contributes to the early detection and alleviation of this frequent and distressing side effect of cancer treatment.
