Effectiveness of the educational-operational intervention of the crisis management in earthquake simulation conditions in prehospital emergency personnel: a randomized controlled trial study
Authors: Iman Farahmandnia, Zohreh Karimiankakolaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEarthquakes are recognized as one of the most dangerous and destructive types of natural disasters. Therefore, decision-making and crisis management are of great importance in these circumstances. The aim of the present study was to investigate the effect of the crisis management training intervention in earthquake simulation conditions in prehospital emergency and incident management center personnel in Chaharmahal Bakhtiari, Iran in 2024.MethodologyThe present study was a semi-experimental pre-test-post-test type. The statistical population was 64 emergency medical personnel at emergency bases in Iran, who were selected from the study population by simple random method and according to the inclusion criteria and were randomly assigned to two intervention and control groups based on a lottery. The educational-operational intervention program was presented to the intervention group in four two-hour sessions. A crisis management questionnaire was used to collect data. Data were analyzed using SPSS26 software, t-test, and structural equation modeling.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 38.6±1.33. The crisis management score before training the crisis management model in the intervention group was 37.03±3.63 and in the control group was 37.34±4.22; there was no statistically significant difference (p=0.761). The crisis management score after training the crisis management model in the intervention group was 43.45±6.00 and in the control group was 37.91±4.31, which was statistically significant (p<0.001).ConclusionConsidering the positive effect of crisis management training in earthquake simulation conditions on emergency medical personnel at emergency bases, it seems that this method can improve the performance of emergency medical personnel. Therefore, it can be suggested that crisis management training intervention in earthquake incidents with simulated training (maneuver) be used to increase the performance of emergency medical personnel.Keywords: Crisis management, Prehospital emergency training, Earthquake, Simulation conditions

Introduction

Natural disasters are defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as situations or events caused by nature that overwhelm local capacity and necessitate the need to request external assistance at the national or international level; unforeseen and often sudden events that cause great damage, destruction and human suffering [1]. Natural disasters such as earthquakes, floods, hurricanes and droughts claim thousands of lives worldwide every year and cause widespread financial and human losses. Developing countries, especially disaster-prone regions such as Southeast Asia and Central America, are hit hardest, as poor infrastructure and ineffective early warning systems increase the number of casualties. Climate change has also increased the intensity and frequency of these events, highlighting the need for international planning to reduce risks [2]. During the past 20 years, Iran has been experiencing a significant increase in the occurrence of disasters mainly due to the emergence of anthropogenic climate change [3].

Earthquakes are recognized as one of the most dangerous and destructive types of natural disasters. As a result, an effective earthquake response is crucial in saving lives and limiting long-term impacts. More than 90% of all deaths from natural disasters occur in developing and underdeveloped countries [4]. Iran, a developing country in Asia, is earthquake-prone [5] and is ranked as one of the most vulnerable countries in the world to earthquakes, with more than 180,000 people having lost their lives due to earthquakes in the past 90 years [5]. Therefore, making the right decision in the care system is of utmost importance, especially in emergency situations [6].

The Iranian government has spent about $29 billion on natural disasters over the past 100 years. Drought, earthquakes, and floods have cost the government more than other disasters, accounting for more than $14 billion, $6.9 billion, and $6.1 billion of the allocated budget, respectively. Most of the Iranian government’s spending over the past 100 years on various disasters such as drought, floods, earthquakes, and COVID-19 has been on involuntary costs, including response and recovery [3].

Crisis management is of great importance in critical situations and natural disasters [6]. Therefore crisis management is the process of reducing the risk of a disaster by using anti-crisis resources in an efficient and effective manner [7]. Crisis resource management consists of a set of teamwork principles that can be utilized to avert and mitigate medical crises [8].

Crisis management has 4 stages, each of which is designed and implemented with its own specific goals. These stages are: prediction and prevention with the aim of reducing the likelihood of a crisis; preparation with the aim of planning, training and research; coping with the aim of providing emergency services immediately after the crisis; reconstruction with the aim of returning society to normal; Crisis management, as part of its tasks as prediction and prevention, will be able to increase people’s awareness of the risks of natural disasters and create appropriate changes in people’s behavior by preparing appropriate educational programs [9]. Training emergency personnel based on the principles of crisis management (prediction, preparedness, response, and recovery) is a strategic solution that prevents natural disasters from turning into human catastrophes, accidents and medical emergencies are important issues in the health system [10].

By definition, prehospital emergency medical services are those services that respond to the specific health needs of individuals outside the hospital setting [6]. In the absence of pre-planned mechanisms and management, disasters can cause significant loss of life and property [5].

Because emergency departments are environments full of complexity and the extremely high workload, the need to use information, the sensitivity of seconds, extreme tension, unpredictability and the vital importance of diagnosing problems and waiting for companions and preserving the lives of patients distinguish these departments from other departments. Therefore, it is necessary that the staff of these departments have sufficient capabilities, skills and information to solve problems and make decisions in any situation [11, 12]. Since pre-hospital emergency personnel have a relatively low awareness of crisis management and related factors, there is a need for training and conducting maneuvers to improve the level of awareness in order to face the risks [13].

Natural disasters such as earthquakes are among the most challenging situations faced by prehospital emergency personnel. Numerous studies have shown that traditional lecture-based training, although it may enhance theoretical knowledge, often fails to transfer the practical skills needed in complex crisis situations (Akbari et al., 2011) [14]. However, increasing evidence supports the effectiveness of active training methods such as realistic simulations in improving professional performance in crises (Kaplan et al., 2012 [15]; Parrillo et al., 2011 [16] Lei et al. (2023) [8]). Integrated training programs that combine theoretical foundations of crisis management with practical exercises in simulated environments not only reinforce basic knowledge, but also develop skills in decision-making under pressure, team coordination, and management of limited resources (Zaboli et al., 2013) [17].

Since some different institutions, especially pre-hospital emergency services, have not received the necessary training to deal with critical situations [18]. This study aims to fill an important gap between theoretical training and practical needs of prehospital emergency personnel by designing a structured educational-practical intervention and evaluating it under earthquake simulation conditions. The results of this study can provide a scientific basis for designing more effective training programs in the field of crisis management. Therefore, the purpose of the present study is to investigate the effect of the crisis management training intervention in earthquake simulation conditions in prehospital emergency and incident management center personnel in Chaharmahal Bakhtiari, Iran in 2024.

Method

Research design

The present study is a randomized controlled trial (RCT) employing a pre-test-post-test design with two parallel groups (intervention and control), conducted in accordance with CONSORT guidelines. Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics committee of Islamic Azad University, Shahrekord Branch (IR.IAU.FALA.REC.1403.007). The study steps are listed in Fig. 1.

Research Steps

Participants

The study population comprised all personnel from pre-hospital emergency and incident management centers across Chaharmahal and Bakhtiari province in 2024.

Sample size calculation

The sample size required in this study was calculated according to the following statistical formula and an estimate of the standard deviation (s) with an effect size (d) of 0.80 (based on prior simulation studies), α = 0.05 (two-tailed), power (1-β) = 0.80, and a 1:1 allocation ratio, yielding 26 participants per group. Accounting for 20% attrition, we recruited 32 participants per group (total N = 64).

Randomization procedure

Randomization was performed via computer-generated numbers (block randomization, block size = 4), with allocation concealed via sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE) opened after baseline assessments.

Blinding

Participants were partially blinded (the control group received routine training without knowing details of the intervention). Assessors were fully blinded to group allocation during pre- and post-test evaluations. Analysts Blinded during initial data processing.

A cluster random sampling was carried out among the emergency centers of the region, eligible individuals were invited to participate in the study and were randomly divided into two intervention and control groups.

Inclusion criteria included being employed in the prehospital emergency department, not having participated in crisis management training programs in the past 6 months, having at least a medical emergency degree (Degree (post-graduate diploma), having at least two years of work experience in the prehospital emergency department, and exclusion criteria included unwillingness to continue cooperation, absence from at least one training session (intervention group only), and leaving or terminating service.

Data collection tools

Demographic questionnaire: including age, marital status and level of education

Crisis Management Questionnaire: This questionnaire was developed by Jahanbakhsh et al. (2016) [19] and has 14 questions and 4 components including prevention (3 questions), preparation (3 questions), coping (5 questions) and reconstruction (3 questions). It measures crisis management based on a Likert scale (1 very little, 2 little, 3 somewhat, 4 much and 5 very much) with questions such as (readiness of management and human resources with any organizational change and transformation to adapt to crisis conditions). It was also used in the study by Jorfi et al. [20].

To calculate the score of each subscale, add together the scores of each item related to that subscale. To calculate the overall score of the questionnaire, add together the scores of all items in the questionnaire. The range of scores for this questionnaire will be between 14 and 70. The higher the score obtained from this questionnaire, the greater the level of crisis management and vice versa.

In the research of Asgharan Enbi (2019), the content and face validity and criterion validity of this questionnaire have been evaluated as appropriate [21]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient calculated in the study by Asgharan Enbi (2019) for this questionnaire was estimated to be above 0.7 [21]. The crisis management questionnaire demonstrated strong reliability in our sample (α = 0.87 total, 0.79–0.85 subscales). The questionnaire demonstrated excellent content validity through expert evaluation (10 specialists). All items exceeded CVR thresholds (mean = 0.78), with I-CVI > 0.80 and S-CVI/Ave = 0.94, meeting recommended standards for instrument development.

Educational-operational intervention

First, a briefing session was held for the participants and the objectives of the study were explained, then the questionnaires were completed in the pre-test phase, the educational-operational intervention program was presented to the intervention group, but the control group did not receive training, after 2 monts, the post-test phase was conducted and the questionnaires were completed again.

The intervention was through holding a training course and a crisis response maneuver and the participation of pre-hospital emergency personnel in these courses. The training content included how to deal with a crisis and crisis management.

To prevent information leakage between the two groups, participants were first randomly divided into two groups without knowledge of the grouping of their colleagues. Training and simulation sessions for the groups were held at separate times and locations to reduce unnecessary interaction. Also, the trainers and study supervisors pledged to refrain from any discussion of the intervention content with the control group participants.

The training material was taught in 4 two-hour sessions. The first session included presenting the theoretical foundations of crisis management, including introducing the crisis, types of crisis, and familiarizing with the crisis. In the second session, the theoretical foundations of the earthquake maneuver were presented. In the next stage, the maneuver was practically implemented in two environments. The theoretical foundations of the crisis were determined based on the crisis system based on the Hospital Crisis and Incident Command System (HICS) in coordination with the personnel. The expected duration of the training was two weeks with 4 daily sessions. After completing the necessary training, the effectiveness of the training on the personnel in managing the crisis was examined. Details are reported in Table 1.

Crisis management training program schedule

Type of intervention: Combined intervention (educational-operational) including: Theoretical training (training workshop based on the crisis management model). Practical simulation (realistic earthquake scenarios with mannequins and actors). Team exercise (coordination between personnel in crisis situations).

Theoretical topics: Principles of crisis management in disasters (Disaster Medicine theories), Pre-hospital triage in earthquakes (START/JumpSTART methods), Crisis communications and inter-organizational coordination, Stress management and decision-making under pressure.

Practical skills: Safe evacuation of injured persons from rubble, Advanced cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) in resource-limited conditions, Control of severe bleeding and traumatic injuries, Use of special equipment (such as rescue bags and first aid tools).

Implementation method

Intervention group: 4 training sessions (2 h each session, 8 h in total), 2 simulation stages (2 h each stage with increasing complexity), Use of standard educational films and Video Debriefing. Control Group: Received routine emergency department training (no additional intervention).

The earthquake simulation exercise was planned as follows: The execution method was a combination of trained actors + advanced mannequins, with 60 min of active execution and a scenario with a partially destroyed building after an earthquake, and the distribution of casualties included 5 mannequins (2 critical with active bleeding, 3 respiratory injuries) and 10 actors (3 fractures, 5 psychological injuries, 2 anxious companions). Key challenges included rapid identification of critical casualties, prioritization of treatment between mannequins and actors, and management of anxious companions. Facilities included treatment-responsive mannequins and trained actors.

The validation of the educational content was carried out through the following: approval by a panel of experts including 4 specialists (emergency, health education, medical education), calculation of the CVI index (at least 0.78 for each item), and content modifications based on comments including the deletion of 2 items and rewriting of 5 items. Initial testing was conducted with 15 personnel. Content evaluation was also conducted with the Cloze index and was approved.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed at two levels, descriptive and inferential. In order to describe the aforementioned data using SPSS26 software, frequency indices were used, and at the inferential level, structural equation modeling was used to examine the relationships. The significance level in all tests was considered to be 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study is the result of a research thesis approved by Shahrekord Islamic Azad University with the ethics code IR.IAU.FALA.REC.1403.007. Informed consent was obtained from the participants and they were assured that their information would remain confidential.

The intervention group showed significantly higher crisis management scores (M = 43.45, SD = 6.00) compared to controls (M = 37.91, SD = 4.31), p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.2 (large effect). This corresponds to an 82% probability that a randomly selected intervention participant will score higher than a control participant. Participants receiving the training were 3.4 times more likely (95% CI: 1.8–6.2) to achieve competency thresholds than controls. The large effect size (d = 1.2) exceeds Cohen’s benchmark for clinically meaningful differences in educational interventions (d > 0.8).

Statistical analysis showed that before the educational intervention, both intervention and control groups had almost the same mean score of crisis management (P = 0.649), but over time, the mean score of crisis management in the intervention group increased significantly (P < 0.001), so that after the educational intervention, the mean score of crisis management in the intervention group was significantly different and increased compared to before the educational intervention.

Discussion

The present study was conducted with the aim of assessing the effectiveness of the crisis management educational intervention in earthquake simulation conditions among prehospital emergency and incident management center personnel.

The results showed that before the educational intervention, there was no statistically significant difference in the mean score of crisis management between the two groups, but after the educational intervention, it was observed that the mean score of crisis management between the intervention and control groups was statistically significant and was higher in the intervention group.

In line with the results of the present study, in the study by Saide mehr et al. (2015), the level of nurses’ awareness of facing a crisis and how to deal with it after participating in educational programs was significantly different compared to before, and according to the findings, crisis management training was effective on the level of nurses’ awareness of managing crisis situations [22]. Like our study, this study emphasizes the effectiveness of crisis management training, but in a different setting (hospital vs. prehospital). Although it showed improved awareness, the lack of simulation of emergency field conditions (such as an earthquake in our study) is an important limitation that makes its findings less generalizable to field personnel.

Kaplan et al. (2012), in line with the present study, stated that training in crisis management through simulated exercises in nursing students leads to an increase in their understanding of preparedness in crisis situations and improved organization in real situations [15]. In addition, the basic knowledge and ability to manage events in emergency and crisis situations improves. Also, in these situations, the individual performs more effectively. In addition, they stated that training through this method leads to an improvement in the maintenance of teamwork for three months. In line with our study, it used simulation, but focused on students (not experienced personnel). Its findings on the durability of training effects (3 months), while valuable, need to be revisited for prehospital emergency personnel facing more complex crises.

The study by Lee et al. (2023) emphasizes that simulation-based CRM training is an essential component of educational initiatives aimed at improving healthcare delivery and patient safety [8]. This study confirms that simulation (such as our intervention) improves the quality of care in resource-poor settings.

In the study by Akbari et al. (2011) with the aim of investigating whether holding training courses has improved the performance of trained human resources? The research findings confirm that training courses were effective in increasing people’s knowledge and insight, but did not have much effect on increasing people’s ability, and in general, there is a significant relationship between holding training courses and improving employee performance [14]. The findings of our study highlight the importance of the practical approach of the present study. While Akbari’s study showed that traditional training only affects knowledge, our practical training intervention, incorporating earthquake simulation, directly focused on the practical ability of personnel.

In a study by Zaboli et al. (2013) aimed at investigating crisis learning strategies in emergency department staff, measures such as proper management of staff in the department, attention to financial incentives, interest and attachment of staff to work, and the necessity of a specific structure for training facilitate learning in crisis situations in the emergency department. In general, the results showed that measures such as proper management of staff in the department, attention to financial incentives, interest and attachment of staff to work, and the necessity of a specific structure for training facilitate learning in crisis situations in the emergency department [17]. Although this study addressed organizational factors, the present study, with its more structured design and use of controlled simulation, allowed for a more precise measurement of the effects of training. Our findings suggest that even without intervention in motivational factors, practical training can improve performance.

In another study investigating the effect of participating in crisis and disaster management training courses on increasing the efficiency and improving the level of knowledge of rescue forces, there was a significant relationship between participating in crisis and disaster management training courses and increasing the efficiency and improving the level of knowledge of rescue forces [23]. In line with our findings, it shows a positive relationship between training and effectiveness. However, the present study provides stronger evidence of effectiveness by focusing on an educational-operational intervention in an earthquake simulation setting.

A study in Malaysia found that emergency nurses who had participated in a crisis performed better in a crisis than those who had not participated in any training program. In addition, their overall knowledge and performance were better [16]. Like our research, it emphasizes the improvement of emergency nurses’ performance after training. Although our controlled trial method with standard simulation has allowed for more scientific conclusions.

Unlike studies such as Akbari (2011) that showed that traditional training has a limited effect on practical ability, or Zaboli’s (2013) study that emphasized organizational factors, the present educational-practical intervention, with its structured design and controlled simulation environment, directly focused on improving practical performance in crisis situations. This approach creates an important bridge between theoretical knowledge and practical ability that has been less addressed in previous studies.

Adequate knowledge, attitude, and performance in crisis management depend on attending training classes. Therefore, crisis training should be at the forefront of training emergency and community health personnel because it improves their knowledge and performance in crisis management [16].

Training in crisis management is a fundamental training for emergency and medical personnel. The present training intervention, with the help of an earthquake simulation exercise, can be used as a training program for these personnel and be a positive step towards better disaster management and reducing human and financial losses.

This is a randomized controlled trial study representing an applied research in the field of crisis management training for emergency personnel. The sensitivity of the role of pre-hospital emergency personnel is noteworthy. These individuals are the first responders in incidents such as earthquakes, and the quality of their performance directly affects the saving of lives of the injured. The need for practical training in crisis situations is an important issue in training; purely theoretical training may not be effective in real situations; therefore, simulating crisis situations (such as earthquakes) is essential to measure the effectiveness of training, which is addressed in the present study.

Strengths of the study

This study offers several strengths: [1] methodologically rigorous design [2], effective integration of theoretical instruction with hands-on simulation training [3], immediately applicable findings for emergency preparedness programs, and [4] carefully controlled evaluation conditions that ensure internal validity.

Study limitations

This study had several limitations: [1] the simulation-reality gap inherent in training exercises [2], reliance on questionnaire-based assessment rather than field performance measures, and [3] lack of systematic benchmarking against WHO/INSARAG standards. While unregistered, the trial followed CONSORT guidelines and obtained ethical approval (IR.IAU.FALA.REC.1403.007), with full methodological transparency maintained throughout.

Suggestions for future research

Future studies should: [1] conduct long-term follow-ups to assess training durability beyond 6 months [2], incorporate advanced technologies like VR for enhanced simulation realism [3], compare this model with alternative training methods (e.g., online modules) [4], evaluate performance in actual crisis scenarios when possible, and [5] examine psychological outcomes including stress reduction. We further recommend integrating WHO competency checklists and INSARAG-style expert evaluations in full-scale exercises to standardize assessments.

Conclusion

According to the results, the crisis management training intervention program in earthquake simulation conditions was effective on emergency medical personnel in emergency centers and was able to improve crisis management in emergency medical personnel. Therefore, it can be suggested that crisis management training in earthquake events be used with the simulated maneuver method to increase the performance of emergency medical personnel. It is suggested that new methods and techniques be used to increase the readiness of emergency medical personnel to deal with earthquakes. Also, in providing earthquake crisis management training to emergency medical personnel, specialized and trained individuals should be used so that incorrect information and behaviors are not provided to emergency medical personnel. Providing various programs in the field of earthquake crisis management should receive more attention from national managers and officials. Given the significant impact of educational content on the performance of emergency medical personnel, it is suggested that emergency medical personnel training be carried out in a practical and operational manner and should be designed with these factors in mind.

The results of this study can be used as a basis for designing standard training programs in emergency training centers. Also, emphasizing practical and simulated training methods can help reduce human errors in real crisis situations. However, for greater generalizability of the results, longer-term studies and the use of more advanced simulation technologies (such as virtual reality) are recommended. Ultimately, this research is an important step towards improving the preparedness of emergency personnel to deal with natural disasters and shows that investing in operational training is not only necessary, but can also save many lives in emergency situations.
