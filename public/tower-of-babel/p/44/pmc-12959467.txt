Junk Food Consumption Trends, Patterns, and Awareness of Its Health Risks Among University Students in Bangladesh: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Istiaque Bin Islam, Md. Samiul Ali, Md. Rakibul Islam, Sumaia Islam, Nazifa Tabassum, Md. Masud Rana, Arafat Hassan Razon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe increasing consumption of junk food (JF) by university students in low‐ and middle‐income countries like Bangladesh is a developing public health issue. This study examined the prevalence of JF consumption and awareness of its health risks among Bangladeshi university students.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted using a structured interview‐based questionnaire among 516 students. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and χ 2 tests, with p < 0.05 considered significant.ResultsNearly 68% students consumed JF from restaurants, with 51.9% eating it occasionally. Local snacks were preferred by 77.3% of students, while 47.3% consumed packaged snacks. Around 59% ate small portions, and 38% consumed carbonated soft drinks with JF. Awareness of JF's health risks was high (87.6%), with most students recognizing its link to obesity, diabetes, and hypertension. Statistical analysis revealed significant associations between academic level and the consumption of packaged snacks (χ 2 = 7.919, p = 0.02, Cramer's V = 0.124). In contrast, beliefs regarding the increased risk of heart disease with JF consumption (χ 2 = 2.181, p = 0.34, Cramer's V = 0.065) were not statistically significant. Awareness of additives as JF components was also significant (χ 2 = 9.525, p = 0.009, Cramer's V = 0.136), indicating varied understanding across academic levels.ConclusionDespite high awareness, JF consumption remains widespread among university students in Bangladesh. The findings indicate a gap between knowledge and practice and underscore the importance of future studies to identify nutrition‐focused interventions in university settings.

Introduction

Emerging adulthood is a critical period between adolescence and adulthood in the human life cycle, during which most young people pursue tertiary education. This stage is characterized by identity exploration, self‐focus, and lifestyle transitions that often influence dietary habits [1]. Among these lifestyle changes, unhealthy food choices, particularly the increased consumption of junk food (JF), have become a significant concern [2]. JF generally refers to food items high in nonessential nutrients such as saturated fat, added sugars, and preservatives, and low in essential nutrients like dietary fiber, calcium, and iron [3]. Excessive consumption of JF displaces nutrient‐rich foods in the diet, contributing to nutritional deficiencies and health problems such as obesity, atherosclerosis, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and other noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) [4]. Furthermore, unhygienic preparation environments and the use of non‐food‐grade additives, heavy metals, or microplastics in JF may further endanger consumer health [5].

Recent report from the World Health Organization (WHO) and studies published in The Lancet indicate a global rise in the consumption of unhealthy foods among youths in recent decades, particularly in urban and developing regions [6]. Several factors contribute to this pattern, including peer influence, accessibility of fast‐food outlets, aggressive marketing, and branding targeted toward younger consumers [5, 7]. Socioeconomic and family backgrounds also influence dietary behavior; for example, parental occupation, household income, and education levels are associated with eating habits [8]. Gender differences in food consumption have been observed globally, where men tend to consume more JF than women due to higher energy requirements and social influences [9].

Globally, the prevalence of JF consumption among emerging adults varies widely—60.3% in Nepal, 82.8% in Malaysia, and about 20% daily in Great Britain [8]. Similarly, one‐third of young adults in Southeast Asia consume JF 4–7 days per week, with Thailand showing the highest rate at nearly 50% [10]. In developed countries like France, approximately half of adolescents prefer sugar‐sweetened beverages over water [11]. These findings suggest that JF consumption is a global issue influenced by modernization, urbanization, and lifestyle transitions. In developing nations, such as Bangladesh, rapid socioeconomic and dietary changes have been identified as key contributors to increasing rates of obesity and NCDs. Regular intake of calorie‐dense, nutrient‐poor JF is linked to weight gain, insulin resistance, and metabolic complications [12]. Although a few studies in Bangladesh have explored JF consumption among adolescents and young adults [13, 14]. They have primarily focused on consumption patterns rather than awareness of associated health risks or behavioral factors influencing such habits. Moreover, most studies were limited to single institutions, reducing their generalizability. Therefore, the present study was conducted to assess the prevalence, types, and frequency of junk food consumption, explore influencing factors, and evaluate awareness of NCD risk among university students in Bangladesh.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study employed a cross‐sectional descriptive design conducted between January and June 2025, and data collection was conducted between February and April 2025. The research was carried out across multiple higher education institutions in Bangladesh.

Study Population

The study population consisted of undergraduate students enrolled in 16 universities across Bangladesh. To ensure representation, institutions were selected purposively to cover diverse types (public, private, and science & technology universities) and different geographical regions of the country (Dhaka, Rajshahi, Khulna, Chittagong, and Sylhet divisions). The selection of these universities was based on some criteria, such as universities had to be recognized by the University Grants Commission (UGC) of Bangladesh, and there was an availability of administrative permission or cooperation for conducting the survey on their campus premises. From each participating university, a proportional number of students were randomly selected from different academic disciplines to ensure diversity in age, gender, and study background.

Inclusion Criteria of the Respondents

The respondents had to be between the ages of 18–28 and enrolled in a university in Bangladesh to be eligible for participation. Furthermore, students who willingly consented to participate in the survey by completing the questionnaire in full were included to ensure data completeness. Participation in this study was entirely voluntary. All respondents were informed about the study objectives, confidentiality procedures, and their right to refuse or withdraw at any stage without providing any reason or facing negative consequences.

Exclusion Criteria of the Respondents

In contrast, participants were excluded if they were academicians, faculty members, alumni, foreign students, or university staff. In addition, students who submitted incomplete and inconsistent responses were excluded from the study. Individuals with medical conditions requiring special or therapeutic diets were excluded from our study, since such conditions could significantly influence dietary behavior and bias the association between junk food consumption and BMI.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size for the study was calculated using the formula “N = Z 2 pq/d 2,” where Z = 1.96, p = 0.50 (since there is no reasonable estimate of any prevalence rate, 50% was considered), q = 1 − p, and d = 0.05. Although previous Bangladeshi studies on Junk food consumption exist, most reported varying (50%–98%) prevalence across limited university samples; hence, 0.5 was used to maximize precision and yield the largest sample size [15, 16, 17]. Though the baseline sample size was 384 according to this equation, we used 386 as our baseline sample size to improve precision. We also increased our base sample size by 40% to eliminate any nonresponse and potential data error, and as a result, we fixed our sample size at 540 respondents. However, from the 540 respondents, we received around 516 completed questionnaires, which met all the inclusion criteria of our study. So the final analytic sample was N = 516 participants.

Study Tools and Data Collection

This survey was carried out between January to April 2025. A questionnaire was developed before the study was conducted over some related published material [7, 18]. After creating the questionnaire in English, which was then translated into Bengali by two professional translators. A back‐translation was then performed to ensure semantic equivalence and accuracy. Reconciliation was conducted by a panel of two bilingual nutrition experts from a Bangladeshi university. Following that, a pilot study was conducted among 30 students from Rajshahi University to test clarity, timing, and comprehension. Cronbach's α coefficient (α = 0.83) indicated good internal reliability, and minor linguistic adjustments were made for clarity. Participants were selected through a stratified random sampling method, stratified by university type (public, private, and science & technology). Within each stratum, simple random sampling was applied to recruit eligible students proportionately. Furthermore, in our study, data were gathered through in‐person interviews using a printed structured questionnaire. The questionnaire captured JF consumption rate, frequency, and pattern, including specific time frames such as the beginning of the week, middle of the week, weekend, as well as preferred times of day: morning, afternoon, and evening. The demographic information related to age, sex, height, weight, and family incomes were documented. BMI was calculated as weight (Kg)/height (m2). Both height and weight were self‐reported but verified for consistency through interviewer prompts using a standardized measurement guide adapted from WHO anthropometric protocols to verify consistency [19]. A face‐to‐face interview was conducted by the research team members during scheduled university hours. Beside that the process was monitored by the study supervisor to ensure accuracy and completeness of entries.

Validity and Reliability of the Questionnaire

Content validity of the questionnaire was established through translation and cultural adaptation, with expert review. A panel of five experts in nutrition, public health, epidemiology, and behavioral science was used to evaluate each question separately for relevance. We calculated the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) by using the established statistical method, and only the items meeting the minimum acceptable CVR threshold for five experts were retained for the development of our questionnaire [20]. The acceptable Item‐level Content Validity Index (I‐CVI) was I‐CVI ≥ 0.78. A pilot study with 30 undergraduate students was used to assess internal consistency reliability; the results showed a Cronbach's α of 0.83, which is considered good reliability. As our study was a cross‐sectional study, where we used various self‐administered frequency‐based items, that's why we could not apply the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient during our study.

Data Quality Control

All the interviewers had received appropriate training regarding the usage and preparation of data collection tools according to the objective of the study. During the study, proper supervision was maintained by the corresponding author of this study to maintain the completeness and authenticity of the data that were collected during the study period. To minimize any errors, all the collected data were double‐checked and cross‐verified by a second researcher.

Ethical Approval

The study protocol was reviewed by the Ethical Review Committee of the School of Science and Technology, Bangladesh Open University, Gazipur‐1705, Bangladesh. As this was a noninterventional, survey‐based study involving no clinical procedures or biological sampling, the committee determined that a formal written ethical clearance number was not required under institutional policy. However, verbal ethical approval was granted prior to data collection, and a copy of the application form submitted to the committee is available as a supplementary file for reference.

Consent of the Respondents

Appropriate ethical guidelines had been maintained during this study. The respondent's identity was protected in that study, and they were further made aware that their responses would be processed and published. All the respondents had an equal opportunity to ask any question regarding our research, and they were also assured by the interviewers that they could stop or leave the interview at any moment they wished. As a result, they were encouraged to provide as precise an answer as possible to ensure the accuracy of the data.

Statistical Analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0. Descriptive statistics such as frequencies and percentages were used to summarize the data. Associations between categorical variables were examined using the Chi‐square (χ 2) test. The strength of association was assessed using Cramer's V where applicable. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were prespecified, and no exploratory or subgroup analyses were conducted beyond the defined study variables. The reporting and interpretation of statistical results followed the Statistical Analysis and Methods in the Published Literature (SAMPL) guidelines and the recommendations of “Guidelines for Reporting of Statistics for Clinical Research in Urology” to ensure accuracy, clarity, and transparency [21, 22].

Results

Among 516 students, 51.4% were female, and 48.6% male. Most belonged to middle‐class families (48.8%), and 94.8% were unmarried. About 16.7% were financially independent. Nearly one‐fifth (18%) reported hypertension, and 5.8% had diabetes. More than half (54.7%) had a family history of NCDs (Table 1).

JF Consumption Patterns

Most students (67.8%) consumed fast food from restaurants, and 77.3% preferred local snacks. Nearly half (47.3%) ate packaged snacks, while 32% consumed grilled or roasted items. Beverages such as tea or coffee (64.7%) were more common than carbonated soft drinks (38%). JF was mostly eaten randomly during the week, with final‐year students more likely to consume JF at night. Significant associations were observed for the consumption of packaged snacks (χ 2 = 7.919, p = 0.02, Cramer's V = 0.124), energy drink consumption (χ 2 = 6.181, p = 0.05, Cramer's V = 0.109), and time of day of JF consumption (χ 2 = 14.420, p = 0.03, Cramer's V = 0.118) (Table 2).

Attitudes Toward JF

According to the findings of our study, taste (70%) and convenience (11.6%) were the main motivators for JF consumption. Besides that, hygiene and safety (47.1%) were key concerns among respondents. However, no statistically significant associations were found for the main motivational and attitudinal factors across academic levels. Specifically, the reasons for eating JF (χ 2 = 5.726, p = 0.46, Cramer's V = 0.074), concerns about JF (χ 2 = 5.911, p = 0.42, Cramer's V = 0.076), perception of taste (χ 2 = 1.260, p = 0.87, Cramer's V = 0.035), and nutritional value (χ 2 = 7.207, p = 0.30, Cramer's V = 0.084) all showed nonsignificant results (Table 3).

Knowledge and Perception About JF and Its Hazards

From Table 4, it was found that around 87% of our respondents were familiar with the term JF, and the majority of our participants (87.6%) believed that JF consumption is linked to NCDs. In addition to that, 28.3% of our study participants had a perception that high salt is the main content of JF, and only 22.7% respondents believed that additives are one of the main contents of JF. A significant association was found between academic level and awareness that JF contributes to NCDs (χ 2 = 4.706, p = 0.10, Cramer's V = 0.096), and belief that additives are a main JF component (χ 2 = 9.525, p = 0.009, Cramer's V = 0.136).

JF and Health Risks Perception

A majority of our study participants (84.7%) agreed that JF leads to diabetes, and 90.3% linked it to heart disease. On the other hand, most of the respondents (92.4%) viewed JF as harmful, and 73.8% were willing to change their lifestyle. Statistically, the association between academic level and the belief that JF consumption predisposes to diabetes was significant (χ 2 = 8.572, p = 0.01, Cramer's V = 0.129), indicating a small‐to‐moderate effect size. Other perceptions, such as links with heart disease (χ 2 = 2.181, p = 0.34, Cramer's V = 0.065), and hypertension (χ 2 = 0.402, p = 0.82, Cramer's V = 0.028), were not statistically significant (Table 5).

Discussion

The main findings of this study indicate a widespread pattern of junk food consumption despite a high level of awareness about its associated health risks. Specifically, most of our study participants consumed JF weekly on a random basis, and around 88% of them acknowledged its link to NCDs, aligning with the study's objectives to assess prevalence, influencing factors, and awareness among university students. These results suggest that knowledge alone is insufficient to drive behavioral change. The findings emphasize the urgent need for behavioral interventions and university‐based nutrition awareness programs to bridge this knowledge‐behavior gap.

This study found a concerning high prevalence of JF consumption (~68%) regularly from restaurants among Bangladeshi university students, higher than in Nepal (60.3%), and Thailand (50%), but slightly lower than in Malaysia (82.8%) [23]. Factors such as affordability, convenience, and social influence may explain these variations, with the finding in this study. Although a considerable proportion of our respondents considered JF as a socioeconomic marker, limited personal income restricted expenditure, reflecting their student status. According to our study findings, a considerable number of our study participants had reported various existing metabolic conditions and family histories of chronic diseases. While these findings may indicate potential health vulnerabilities, causal relationships cannot be inferred due to the cross‐sectional design of our study. Besides, we assumed that other factors like financial status, as well as more conversion to westernized dietary approaches, may also influence their health‐related issues that have also been mentioned in a previous study [8].

Most respondents preferred local snacks, indicating a strong cultural influence. Packaged and grilled foods were popular due to time constraints and easy availability. These consumption patterns reflect difficulty in meal preparation, cumulative academic stress, time constraints, economic constraints, and easy availability of packaged snacks in local shops. Similar patterns have been observed in other studies, where more lifestyle disruption and dietary irregularities were noticed among the senior students [24].

The high rate of beverage consumption (particularly coffee and tea) alongside JF indicates habitual snacking behavior rather than structured meals. These findings have revealed that students may like to consume beverages and coffee along with junk foods as snacks. So, we can say from our findings that students not only purchase beverages at restaurants but also purchase some snacks, which passively increases their junk food consumption rate. In contrast, we observed that fresh fruit juice and sweetened fruit juice were rarely consumed by our study participants, which is an indication of poor dietary choices by our study participants. Additionally, students in developing countries are particularly vulnerable to a variety of high‐calorie JF, which are generally far less expensive than healthier choices [8].

Furthermore, approximately 83% of our study population of students consumed JF randomly over the week. The result shows that 51.9% of students occasionally eat JF from restaurants, whereas only 34% of Nigerian university students reported consuming fast food [18]. This variation in results may be the lower availability of restaurants within the university premises of the Nigerian community, and their low socioeconomic condition may also be responsible for that.

Student food preferences are massively influenced by various factors such as taste, convenience, low cost, advertising, and peer pressure, where taste is the most important factor. This aligns with the previous research, which showed that young people often prioritize instant pleasure over health while selecting food [25, 26]. Another study published which was published in 2022 illustrates a growing tendency of students from southern India to consume unhealthy foods, including JF products, and lead a sedentary lifestyle. The study also found that taste, the rise in online food delivery, and tempting advertisements encouraged the consumption of unhealthy products [27].

According to our study, hygiene and safety were a concern to nearly half of the students; however, only a small minority of students were cautious about nutritional value. As all of our respondents had a good educational background, that's why they showed good hygiene practices during the consumption of junk food. Besides that, a significant portion of our respondents consider national value during the selection of junk food, which reflects their consciousness to maintain optimum health. Despite 87.6% awareness of JF's health risks, consumption behavior did not align with knowledge in our study. Although this knowledge did not turn into their healthy dietary habits. Most of the participants believe high salt, sugar, saturated fat, and cholesterol contents to be the main contents of JFs, but only around 42.0% of respondents were aware of the other contents like fiber and additives. Besides, a large number of the participants were aware of the health impact of these components. Students cited taste and convenience as the leading drivers of JF intake, consistent with studies from India and Malaysia [28, 29]. Moreover, 47.1% prioritized hygiene over nutritional value, suggesting partial health awareness.

In our study, a significant proportion of our respondents had demonstrated a substantial awareness of the adverse health effects of junk food, including its associations with cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, and weight gain. Excessive levels of sugar, sodium, and cholesterol accumulate within the body, causing weight gain. It has been reported that being overweight or obese increases the risk of developing insulin resistance, hypertension, and atherosclerosis [18]. Overall, 92.4% students think that JF consumption is dangerous to health, among them 73.8% are willing to adopt a healthier lifestyle to reduce health risk, which is an indication of the desire to lead a healthy lifestyle by the young generation of Bangladesh. The study conveys a clear correlation between BMI and portion size; higher BMI often links to larger portion size, amongst the overweight and obese. Frequent consumption of larger portions, especially from restaurants, leads to higher calorie intake and causes weight gain [30, 31]. Students who eat JF less frequently in restaurants or consume smaller portions are more likely to be within among normal weight range [32]. These findings mirror the previous studies suggest that regular consumption of JF can cause weight gain, metabolic disorder, and chronic diseases [33, 34].

Future studies should focus on performing longitudinal or intervention‐based research to explore the causal relationships between junk food consumption and health outcomes among university students in Bangladesh. Moreover, future studies could incorporate qualitative approaches to examine the underlying psychosocial and cultural factors influencing junk food preferences. Additionally, expanding the study to multiple universities across different regions, including both public and private institutions, would enhance the generalizability of findings.

There are several limitations to this study. First, self‐reported data could introduce social desirability bias or recall bias. Second, the cross‐sectional design makes it more difficult to determine causality or look at changes over time. Third, unmeasured lifestyle confounders such as alcohol consumption, smoking habits, and physical inactivity could influence both junk food consumption and BMI, which future longitudinal studies should consider. Despite these drawbacks, the results offer valuable information about the awareness and consumption habits of junk food among Bangladeshi university students.

Conclusion

This study addressed the growing problem of excessive junk food consumption among Bangladeshi university students, which poses significant risks for obesity, diabetes, and hypertension. Despite the majority of respondents being aware of these health risks, most of them still consumed junk food weekly, mainly due to taste, convenience, and affordability. These findings highlight a clear gap between knowledge and behavior. Therefore, university‐based nutrition education, policy‐driven interventions, and awareness campaigns are essential to promote healthier dietary choices and reduce the long‐term burden of NCDs among young adults in Bangladesh.
