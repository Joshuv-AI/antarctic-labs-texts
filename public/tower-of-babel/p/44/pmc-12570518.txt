WGS-based surveillance for Campylobacter spp. in human infections and chicken meat production in Italy (2023)
Authors: Aurora García-Fernández, Irene Artuso, Francesca Marotta, Roberta Di Romualdo, Sergio Arena, Maria Laura De Marchis, Monica Pitti, Sara Primavilla, Maira Napoleoni, Richard Aschbacher, Silvia Bracco, Matteo Gradassi, Anna Janowicz, Giuliano Garofolo, Laura Villa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCampylobacter spp. infections present an urgent and significant global public health challenge. This study offers a genomic characterization of Campylobacter spp. isolates from human samples and chicken-origin food sources in Italy during 2023. Whole-genome sequencing, cluster analysis, genomic comparison of human and food of animal origin isolates, and characterization of antimicrobial resistance mechanisms were performed on 257 isolates.ResultsCampylobacter jejuni revealed considerable variability, with predominant types, including ST21, ST50 (ST21 CC), and ST3335 (ST206 CC), in human isolates, whereas ST50 and ST2116 (ST353 CC) prevailed in food sources. In contrast, C coli isolates were associated primarily with the ST828 CC. Cluster analysis based on core-genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST) revealed that 40.0% of the isolates formed 23 genetic clusters, suggesting potential outbreak scenarios. The isolates presented a high prevalence of ciprofloxacin resistance linked to mutations in GyrA (80.1%) and extensive tetracycline resistance attributed to tet genes (64.6%). Multidrug resistance was predominantly observed in C. coli (12.1%). Notably, the chromosomal erythromycin resistance gene erm(N) in food C. coli isolates was detected for the first time in Italy and for the first time worldwide in a veterinarian or food isolate.ConclusionsThis One Health study, which was based on genomic surveillance, improved our understanding of antimicrobial resistance and the epidemiology of Campylobacter spp. This study highlights the emergence of a novel antimicrobial resistance marker in Italy, erm(N), and reveals the prospective occurrence of multiple nationwide outbreak scenarios that demand immediate public health attention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04272-1.

Background

Campylobacter spp. infections are the most widespread zoonoses affecting humans and represent the leading cause of bacterial food-borne gastroenteritis worldwide [1] (https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/campylobacter) (https://www.cdc.gov/campylobacter/about/index.html) (both accessed on 18/02/2025). Specifically, Campylobacter jejuni and Campylobacter coli are the most frequently implicated species in human campylobacteriosis. The incidence of campylobacteriosis varies considerably among countries globally [1]. This is due to many factors, including underreporting of cases, differences in reporting systems, diagnostic challenges, and outbreak surveillance [2]. In Italy, the notification rate for human cases in 2023 was 2.4 per 100,000 (data from Enter-Net Italia surveillance), which is significantly lower than the EU average of 45.7 cases per 100,000 in the same year [1]. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in Campylobacter species further increases public health concerns, as these bacteria have developed resistance to several antibiotics, including fluoroquinolones (e.g., ciprofloxacin), macrolides (e.g., erythromycin), tetracyclines, and aminoglycosides, complicating treatment options, especially for severe infections. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) surveillance of AMR mechanisms allows the identification and tracking of genetic factors driving resistance in pathogens, often complementing and validating phenotypic methods. Fluoroquinolone resistance is primarily due to mutations in the gyrA gene, which encodes a subunit of DNA gyrase, whereas macrolide resistance often results from mutations in the 23S rRNA gene or acquisition of the erm gene, which methylates the ribosome and prevents drug binding. Tetracycline resistance is mediated mainly by efflux pumps or ribosomal protection mechanisms [3]. These resistance patterns are largely driven by antibiotic use in animal farming [4]. Campylobacter spp. can acquire resistance under selective pressure, with potential subsequent transmission to humans through the consumption of contaminated animal-originated products.

Campylobacter spp. are often found in warm-blooded healthy animals, which are usually considered natural hosts, and in environmental waters [5–7]. Poultry is recognized as the primary reservoir of C. jejuni and the leading source of infection [8, 9]. Poultry is usually colonized in the second or third week after placement in the broiler house, and contamination of chicken meat occurs during slaughtering by self-contamination of the processing plants by equipment and by the handling of contaminated carcasses [10–14].

Data on Campylobacter spp. in the context of Commission Regulation (EC) No 2073/2005, which establishes a regulatory limit of 1000 CFU/g for Campylobacter spp. on the neck skin of chilled broiler carcasses, indicated that approximately one in six samples from broiler chicken carcasses exceeded the 1000 CFU/g limit on the basis of both official control and food business operator food-borne outbreak monitoring data. Furthermore, in 2023, the level of contamination in broiler carcasses within the EU remained comparable to that in 2022, at approximately 40%. In summary, the results from Campylobacter spp. monitoring emphasized poultry as a major contributor to campylobacteriosis in the EU [1].

Campylobacter spp. human infections may be directly or indirectly connected with poultry and are likely to occur when raw poultry is mishandled in home kitchens [15]. Recently, it has become more evident that preventing infections in the home kitchen could be beneficial by preventing dangerous contamination of food normally eaten raw [16, 17].

A One Health-based phylogenetic analysis of the national evolution of Campylobacter spp. is essential for understanding population dynamics and developing more effective control strategies. Using WGS and core-genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST), the origins of infections can be traced, and the relative contributions of different sources can be determined. Accurate clustering analysis of Campylobacter spp. isolates from humans and food of animal origin circulating within the national territory is crucial for developing effective interventions, informing public health policies, empowering policymakers to make informed decisions and reducing the incidence of campylobacteriosis by targeting the most significant reservoirs and pathways of transmission.

This study aims to create a prototype of real-time genomic surveillance in Campylobacter spp. for the year 2023 in Italy. Clinical isolates of Campylobacter spp. reported nationwide from the voluntary network Enter-Net Italia were compared with Campylobacter spp. strains isolated in the framework of the official monitoring of broiler carcasses.

By applying cgMLST, cluster analysis, and WGS AMR surveillance, we identified several genetic clusters among clinical isolates that are indicative of potential outbreak scenarios. The cluster analysis revealed likely connections between clinical and broiler food isolates, providing insights into possible pathways and sources of infection transmission. The results obtained are beneficial for better understanding the epidemiology of Campylobacter spp. infections, the distribution of AMR, the emergence of new AMR mechanisms, and the genetic basis of resistance mechanisms in these pathogenic bacteria.

M & M

Settings and bacterial strains

This study analyzed 257 Campylobacter spp. strains, including 191 C. jejuni strains and 66 C. coli strains, isolated in Italy in 2023 from clinical cases (n = 150) and food samples, specifically neck skin and meat from broiler carcasses collected after chilling at slaughterhouses (n = 107).

Surveillance of Campylobacter spp. from human strains in Italy is based on a systematic voluntary network, Enter-Net Italia, which is coordinated by the Infectious Diseases Department of the Istituto Superiore di Sanità (ISS). The strain selection criteria for this study involved 150 epidemiologically unrelated isolates of the 2,363 reported Campylobacter spp. cases in Italy in 2023. The strains belonged to two main Campylobacter species: 127 C. jejuni strains and 23 C. coli strains (Supplementary Table S1). The samples were collected from 2nd January 2023 to 23 December 2023. The strains isolated from humans were from fecal samples and one from blood (C. coli), primarily from various regions in Italy, North (Lombardia, Piemonte, and Trentino-Alto Adige) and Centre (Marche, Umbria, Toscana, and Lazio).

The food isolates from broiler carcasses, comprising 64 C. jejuni and 43 C. coli strains, were collected from 11 January 2023 to 16 November 2023 from 16 Italian slaughterhouses located in Lombardia (n = 5), Emilia-Romagna (n = 6), Marche (n = 3), Toscana (n = 1) and Calabria (n = 1). These strains were representative of the official controls conducted in Italy in 2023 as part of the Campylobacter spp. process hygiene criterion (PHC) outlined in Commission Regulation (EC) No 2073/2005.

All the strains included in this study were inoculated onto modified charcoal cefoperazone deoxycholate agar (mCCD Agar; Biolife Italiana srl, Milan, Italy) and Columbia blood agar horse plates (Biolife Italiana srl, Milan, Italy). The plates were incubated at 41.5 °C ± 1 °C for 48 ± 2 h in a microaerobic atmosphere and then screened for antibiotic susceptibility and DNA extraction (ISOLATE II Genomic DNA Kit, Meridian Bioscience, London, UK).

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Susceptibility was determined via the reference broth microdilution method and the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion susceptibility test, following the international guideline recommendations of the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST; www.eucast.org). Four antibiotics (Liophilchem srl, Teramo, Italy) were tested via the disk diffusion method, and the antibiotic concentrations used were as follows: ciprofloxacin (Cip, 5 µg), tetracycline (T, 30 µg), erythromycin (E, 15 µg), and gentamicin (Gm, 10 µg). The control strain for antibiotic susceptibility testing was the C. jejuni strain ATCC 33560. The susceptibility results were interpreted via the EUCAST guidelines (https://www.eucast.org) (accessed on 18/02/2025). The broth microdilution method with the Sensititre automated system (TREK Diagnostic Systems, Venice, Italy) was used to determine the antibiotic susceptibility of Campylobacter spp. strains. Cip, T, E, and Gm were tested via previously reported methods [18] via Eucamp3 microtiter plates (TREK Diagnostic Systems, Venice, Italy), which contain known scalar concentrations of the following antibiotics: ciprofloxacin (0.12–32 µg/mL), erythromycin (1–512 µg/mL), gentamicin (0.25–16 µg/mL), and tetracycline (0.5–64 µg/mL). The strains were classified as resistant or susceptible according to minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) breakpoints via Swin v3.3 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the epidemiological cutoff values (ECOFFs) defined by EUCAST (https://www.eucast.org) (accessed on 18/02/2025). The C. jejuni strain NCTC 11351 was used as a control. Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as resistance to three or more classes of antibiotics.

Library preparation and WGS

Seventy-one strains of human origin were sequenced by Eurofins Genomics Germany Gmbh (Ebersberg, Germany) via a standard genomic library performed by NovaSeq 6000. For the remaining human isolates, DNA libraries were created via DNA prep tagmentation, and paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 instrument according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA) to produce 300 bp long reads.

DNA libraries were prepared from food isolates via the Illumina DNA Prep Kit (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) and sequenced on a NextSeq 2000 Sequencing System.

Genome assembly and quality control

A pipeline was implemented following the “Guidelines for Reporting Whole Genome Sequencing-based Typing Data through the EFSA One Health WGS System” to conduct bioinformatic analyses [19]. Specifically, Fastp v0.23.4 was used for quality control, adapter trimming, quality filtering, and per-read quality pruning [20]. Illumina reads were assembled by using the program shovill v1.1.0 (Seeman T, https://github.com/tseemann/shovill) (accessed on 18/02/2025), and genome completeness and contamination were assessed with CheckM v1.1.2 [21]. Genome assembly quality was evaluated with QUAST v5.2.0 [22]. The pipeline uses Kraken v2.1.3 [23] and Kmerfinder v3.0.2 [24–26] to identify potential contamination of both reads and assemblies, respectively. Sequence types (STs) were defined and assigned ex novo via mlst v2.23.0 (Seemann T, mlst https://github.com/tseemann/mlst) (accessed on 18/02/2025), which scans contig files against PubMLST typing schemes https://pubmlst.org (accessed on 18/02/2025) [27].

Resistome analysis

For the detection of chromosomal point mutations associated with AMR and organism-specific acquired resistance genes, the pipeline uses ResFinder v4.4.2 with the ResFinder database v2.2.1 [28] applying a minimum coverage threshold of 60% (“-l 0.6”) and a minimum identity threshold of 90% (“-t 0.9”) and AMRFinderPlus v3.12.8 with database version “2024-07-22.1” [29], with an identity threshold of 90% (“-i 0.9”) [30]. Nucleotide and protein sequence similarity study was performed using the BLASTn and BLASTp tools, respectively (NCBI BLAST suite). BLASTn was used to compare the nucleotide sequence of the erm(N) gene and the erm(N)–CRISPR-Cas9 genomic region against the NCBI nucleotide database. BLASTp was used to analyze the Erm(N) protein sequence to identify homologous proteins across NCBI available genomes. Default parameters were used. Graphic visualization of this region was created via SnapGene v8.0.1.

Core-genome phylogenetic analysis

The Prokka tool was utilized to annotate the genomes [31], and the Roary tool v3.13.0 was used to generate core genome alignments via MAFFT v7.526 [32, 33]. Phylogenetic trees were generated with IQ-TREE v2.3.5 [34], applying the ModelFinder option and 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. The trees were visualized via Microreact [35].

WGS cluster analysis

The cgMLST INNUENDO scheme with 678 loci [36], implemented with chewBBACA software v2.0.12 [37], was used to identify genomic clusters. The clusters of clinical isolates and matches to food isolates were defined on the basis of cgMLST allele differences [38]. Minimum spanning trees (MSTreeV2) were calculated via ReportTree v2.5.3 with a partition threshold for clustering definition equal to 11 (“-thr = 11”) [39] and visualized via GrapeTree v1.5.0 [40]. The clusters identified were labeled via a unique identifier that integrates key information about the cluster’s characteristics: the progressive cluster number (CXX), the sequence type (ST####), the source of isolation (H for human, F for food, or HF for both), and the Campylobacter species (J for C. jejuni or C for C. coli).

Statistics

We estimated the proportion of positive samples via a Bayesian approach with a beta posterior distribution and a uniform noninformative prior. Starting with a Beta (1, 1) prior, which reflects no prior knowledge about the proportion, we combined it with the observed binomial data to obtain a posterior distribution. We then calculated the 95% credible interval by identifying the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of this posterior distribution, providing an interval that contains the true proportion with 95% probability, given the data.

Results

Antibiotic susceptibility

Susceptibility to all the tested antibiotics (Cip, E, Gm, and T) was observed in 21.3% and 17.4% of the C. jejuni and C. coli human strains, respectively. C. jejuni was 20.5% resistant to Cip and 49.6% resistant to Cip combined with T (CipT). When considering Cip resistance globally, alone or in combination with other types of resistance, we observed extremely high resistance levels in C. jejuni (70.1%). No MDR C. jejuni strains were detected. In comparison, 21.7% of the C. coli strains were resistant to only Cip, 8.7% were resistant to T alone, and 34.8% were CipT co-resistant. When Cip resistance was considered globally, C. coli also presented an extremely high resistance level (73.9%). Additionally, four isolates were MDR, three were resistant to Cip combined with T and E (CipTE), and one was resistant to all the antibiotics tested, Cip combined with T, E and Gm (CipTEGm) (Supplementary Table 1).

All the food Campylobacter spp. strains were resistant to at least one tested antibiotic. Among the isolates, 39.1% of the C. jejuni isolates and 16.3% of the C. coli isolates were resistant exclusively to Cip. Most strains were CipT co-resistant, with 59.4% C. jejuni and 65.1% C. coli. Both species presented MDR; one C. jejuni strain and one C. coli strain were CipTE co-resistant, and only two C. coli strains presented co-resistance to CipTEGm, while one presented co-resistance to CipTGm. A total of 9.3% of the C. coli were resistant only to T.

WGS results

The genome sequences of 150 human and 107 food Campylobacter spp. strains were analyzed. KmerFinder analysis confirmed that all strains matched known Campylobacter species, indicating the absence of contamination. The sequence coverage depth averaged 583x (from 40x to 2299x), ensuring robust genome quality. The total length of the assembled genomes ranged from 1.59 to 1.89 Mb. The median contig number per assembly was 34 (ranging from 13 to 133), with a median N50 of 181 kb (ranging from 50 to 335 kb), highlighting the quality and consistency of the genome assemblies across the dataset (Supplementary Table 1).

AMR gene distribution

The in silico analysis of the 257 sequenced Campylobacter spp. strains revealed the presence of several resistance genes (Figs. 1 and 2, Supplementary Table 1). The presence or absence of a known resistance gene(s) and specific mutations was compared to the corresponding interpretations (resistance or susceptible) for each antibiotic susceptibility test result, revealing a concordance of 98.1%.

Common AMR genes identified included tet(O), tet(O/32/O), tet(O/M/O), tet(O/W/32/O), aad9, aadE, aadE-Cc, ant(6)-Ia, aph(3’)-IIIa, aph(2’’)-If, aph(2’’)-Ii, sat4, cmeB and blaOXA variants (e.g., blaOXA−193, blaOXA−489). The transferable erm(B) gene conferring E resistance was absent; instead, erm(N), also known as the erm(D) or erm(53) gene, which is responsible for E resistance, was present in the chromosomes of two MDR C. coli food strains (2023.TE.14153 and 2023.TE.14154). They were isolated from fresh chicken meat at the same slaughterhouse in Lombardia in June 2023 (Supplementary Table 1). The erm(N) gene was inserted between the chromosomal repeats of the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats and the CRISPR-associated (Cas) protein CRISPR-Cas9 operon (Fig. 3). A BLAST search of the erm(N) gene in NCBI resulted in 100% coverage and identity with the erm(N) gene of two C. coli, the 48777 strain (Accession number NG_081668.1) and 2019/0051 strain (Accession number MZ015744). Comparison between the 9,790 bp sequence of the CRISPR-cas9 operon (from the cryptic C4-dicarboxylate transporter dcuD to the molybdopterin synthase moaE gene) of our two erm(N) isolates and the C. coli 48777 strain revealed a perfect match (100% identity and 100% coverage). This C. coli isolate is an E resistant and erm(N)-positive strain that was isolated in Canada and submitted to NCBI under the Biosample number SAMN11959355 (RefSeq NZ_VEWG01000003.1) (Fig. 3) Protein BLAST (BLASTp) analysis of Erm(N) revealed 100% identity and coverage with the amino acid sequence of Erm(N) in three C. coli strains and one Salmonella enterica serovar Derby strain. Two of the C. coli isolates, isolated in the UK, showed particularly high nucleotide similarity in the CRISPR-Cas9 region (9,790 bp): one, isolated in 2019 from an unknown source (BioSample number SAMN13254962), exhibited 100% nucleotide identity and coverage; the other, isolated from a human in 2024 (BioSample number SAMN46430913), showed 99.99% nucleotide identity with 100% coverage.

Point mutations associated with resistance, such as GyrA_T86I, GyrA_T86V, GyrA_D90N, 23S_A2075G, and blaOXA−61_G57T, have also been reported (Supplementary Table 1).

The isolates presented a high prevalence of Cip resistance linked to mutations in GyrA (80.1%) and extensive T resistance attributed to tet genes (64.6%). Notably, 77.0% of the C. jejuni isolates and 80.3% of the C. coli isolates presented the GyrA_T86I mutation. Double mutations in GyrA (T86I, D90N) were also detected in both species. In contrast, the alternative mutation GyrA_T86V appeared exclusive to C. jejuni (3.1%, n = 6). These strains share common resistance mechanisms, such as the GyrA_T86I mutation and the tet(O) gene, indicating potential cross-resistance to fluoroquinolones and tetracyclines. Among the strains with mutations in GyrA, the tet(O) gene was present in 60.8% of the C. jejuni isolates and 73.6% of the C. coli isolates; the tet(O/32/O) gene was present in 24.1% of the C. jejuni isolates and 10.6% of the C. coli isolates; 1.9% (n = 1) of the C. coli isolates carried the mosaic tet(O/W/32/O) gene; and 0.6% (n = 1) of the C. jejuni isolates carried the tet(O/M/O) gene. A total of 6.3% of the C. jejuni strains and 13.6% of the C. coli strains presented the tet(O) gene without GyrA mutations (Supplementary Table 1). The 23S_A2075G mutation or the presence of erm(N), described as conferring E resistance, were detected in four (food and human) and two (food) C. coli strains, respectively. The 23S_A2075G mutation was also present in one food C. jejuni isolate (Supplementary Table 1).

The aph(2”)-If and aph(2”)-Ii genes, which confer Gm resistance, were present in three and one C. coli isolate of food and human origin, respectively (Supplementary Table 1).

Although ampicillin resistance was not tested phenotypically in this study, the presence of the blaOXA gene and its variants was investigated. Humans and food isolates presented a similar prevalence of the main blaOXA gene variants. The most prevalent blaOXA gene among both humans and food isolates of C. jejuni was the blaOXA−193, with frequencies of 61.4% and 56.1%, respectively. In C. coli, blaOXA−193 was also the most prevalent, occurring in 34.8% of human isolates and 37.2% of food isolates (Supplementary Table 1). A significant proportion of the human isolates from both species did not present blaOXA genes, comprising 17.4% of the C. coli isolates and 16.5% of the C. jejuni isolates. In contrast, for the broiler isolates, blaOXA genes were present in all the C. jejuni isolates and absent in 32.6% of the C. coli isolates. The blaOXA−61_G57T mutation in the promoter of the blaOXA gene was detected in 40.7% and 59.8% of Campylobacter spp. human and food isolates, respectively (Supplementary Table 1).

A mutation in the rplV gene, encoding the L22_A103V ribosomal protein, was detected in 43 Campylobacter spp. isolates: 41 susceptible to E and two E-resistant (Supplementary Table 1). Although this point mutation was reported as a cause of macrolide resistance, it was not associated with E-resistance in our strains. Therefore, this study did not consider it a resistance determinant, as previously described [41]. Other genes, such as optrA and fexA, known as conferring florfenicol resistance in Campylobacter spp., have not been found in the strains of this study [42–44].

Phylogenetic structure of Campylobacter spp. populations

A core-genome phylogeny was reconstructed for both the C. jejuni and C. coli populations. For the C. jejuni isolates, the phylogenetic tree was based on the core-genome alignment of 1,199 genes, with the GTR + F + I + R9 model selected as the best fit by ModelFinder (Fig. 1). Instead, for C. coli isolates, the phylogenetic tree was based on the core-genome alignment of 1,263 genes, and the selected model was GTR + F + I + R5 (Fig. 2). The major ST lineages (> 5 isolates) were clearly separated in the C. jejuni and C. coli trees. Food strains were distributed between human strains across most branches of the C. jejuni and C. coli tree. No clear correlation was observed between the clusters and the region of origin in either tree (Figs. 1 and 2).

Genomic surveillance

MLST

Sixty-nine different STs, grouped into 18 CCs (16 strains did not have a determined CC, ND CC), were found in human Campylobacter spp. strains, 13 in C. coli and 56 in C. jejuni, reflecting wide ST diversity. Two novel combinations of existing alleles with previously unassigned STs were discovered and granted by the PubMLST website as ST13890 and ST13891. The distribution of STs in human Campylobacter spp. isolates showed significant variability, with the most common types being ST3335 (ST206 CC, 12 isolates), ST50 (ST21 CC, 10 isolates), and ST21 (10 isolates) in C. jejuni. In C. coli, the most prevalent STs were ST8195 (ST828 CC) and ST10304 (ND CC) (Supplementary Table 1).

The analysis of Campylobacter spp. from broiler carcasses isolated from 16 slaughterhouses revealed 31 different STs belonging to 11 CCs (25 strains were ND CCs). C. jejuni presented a broader spectrum of STs and CCs, including ST21 CC (all ST50) and ST353 CC (mainly ST2116), with 10 and 21 isolates, respectively. In contrast, 69.8% of the C. coli isolates were classified as ST828 CC, mainly belonging to ST962, ST11781, ST8578, and ST8195 (Supplementary Table 1).

Among the 20 CCs identified in C. jejuni, seven CCs (ST21 CC, ST52 CC, ST206 CC, ST257 CC, ST353 CC, ST354 CC, and ST607 CC) were shared between human and food isolates.

ST828 CC is highly prevalent in human and food C. coli isolates, accounting for 73.9% and 69.8%, respectively.

Cluster analysis

A comprehensive analysis of human and foodborne isolates via the cgMLST INNUENDO scheme and minimum spanning tree (MST) cluster analysis revealed that 99 out of 257 isolates (38.2%) in this study clustered with other isolates, specifically 60 out of 150 (40.0%) Campylobacter spp. isolates from humans clustered with other human or food isolates, forming 23 genomic clusters (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1). Notably, 18 human strains (12.0%), comprising 6 C. coli strains and 12 C. jejuni strains, were food related.

Forty-two C. jejuni isolates, accounting for 33.1% of all human-origin C. jejuni isolates, clustered exclusively with other human isolates across 15 distinct clusters (Table 1): two clusters (C09-ST6175-H-J and C14-ST21-H-J) with five strains each, two (C10-ST122-H-J and C13-ST3335-H-J) with four strains, one (C12-ST3335-H-J) with three strains, and ten smaller clusters with only two strains each (Table 1).

Four clusters (C01-ST2116-HF-J, C02/03-ST50-HF-J, and C04-ST2863-HF-J) comprising clinical and food isolates were observed for C. jejuni, and four (C05-ST830-HF-C, C06-ST8195-HF-C, and C07/08-ST10304-HF-C) were observed for C. coli. Estimation of the impact of the campylobacteriosis outbreak on the Italian population

On the basis of the study by Havelaar et al. (2013) [45] and considering that the situation has remained stable in recent years, we assumed that the annual number of human campylobacteriosis cases in Italy was approximately 352,000. In our study, 40% of Campylobacter spp. strains from clinical cases clustered together with those from poultry. Therefore, the estimated projection of the population likely to be involved in outbreaks would be 114,398–168,995 (95% CI:32.5%−48.0%). Specifically, 12.0% of Campylobacter spp. strains from human isolates clustered with poultry isolates, resulting in an estimated 27,275–64,008 cases associated with chicken-associated outbreaks (95% CI: 7.7%−18.2%). Finally, 28.0% of Campylobacter spp. strains from humans clustered with human strains, suggesting a range of 75,458–125,604 (95% CI:21.4%−35.7%) estimated cases from outbreaks linked to sources other than poultry.

Discussion

Campylobacteriosis is a common bacterial infection in humans caused by Campylobacter species and is primarily linked to poultry, particularly chickens. Food contamination can occur at various stages of production, and transmission to humans typically involves handling, consuming or processing contaminated meat. This poses a significant public health risk. Effective tracking and tracing of animals and animal-derived foods in the food chain is crucial.

This study shows the integration of clinical and food sector monitoring through genomic surveillance, with a focus on the Italian population and poultry production. Using isolates from the Enter-Net Italia network (human infections) and poultry monitoring (Commission Regulation (EC) No 2073/2005), this study highlights the value of combining real-time data from distinct monitoring systems to potentially enhance source attribution and public health responses for Campylobacter spp.

The in silico analysis by MLST revealed significant and noteworthy differences in the distribution of CCs between human and food isolates. The prevalence of Campylobacter spp. STs varied among slaughterhouses, with relatively high variability at some slaughterhouses and low variability at others. The high variability observed underscores the genetic diversity of Campylobacter spp. strains in poultry and humans [46, 47].

Several CCs exhibited varied distributions between the two groups, whereas others were exclusive to human or food isolates. Only a few CCs, such as ST206 CC, ST353 CC and ST21 CC, were present in human and food-borne isolates. This finding is particularly noteworthy for ST21 CC, the CC most frequently detected in humans and one of the most virulent CCs in C. jejuni [48]. The presence of ST21 CC in both human and food of chicken origin isolates suggested significant zoonotic potential, indicating that chickens could be a major reservoir for these ST21 CC strains. This CC has been described as one of the most common C. jejuni CCs worldwide [46, 47, 49].

However, MLST characterization alone is insufficient to fully explore the potential clustering of strains circulating in Italy. Comparative studies have suggested that the gene-by-gene (cgMLST) approach is more effective for identifying lineages in this recombining species [50, 51]. Several typing schemes have been developed, including the publicly available INNUENDO and the web-accessible Oxford cgMLST. However, their concordance and ability to distinguish closely related strains have been thoroughly evaluated [52]. In this study, the INNUENDO scheme was used as the reference method for both Campylobacter species, and the robustness of the analysis was supported by the concordance with results obtained from the core-genome phylogeny (Fig. 1). Campylobacter spp. populations exhibit high genetic diversity, which is driven primarily by horizontal genetic exchange and, to a lesser extent, chromosomal mutations [53, 54]. Consequently, SNP analyses often overestimate genetic exchange, diminishing signals of population structure [55]. In our study, the clusters identified by cgMLST were supported by a core-genome phylogeny, visualizing and interpreting genetic diversity within populations and identifying clusters on the basis of their core genomic content [56].

Genomic surveillance via cgMLST cluster analysis revealed that a significant fraction of the clinical isolates were included in clusters with food, indicating the presence of hidden outbreaks in Italy. Notably, C01-ST2116-HF-J identified both human cases and broiler carcasses over more than eight months. These findings suggest the presence of a recurrent source of infection sustaining a polyclonal outbreak and highlight the importance of real-time genomic monitoring to trace outbreaks effectively.

Notably, C. jejuni ST2116, together with ST2863 and ST832, has already been described as the most prevalent and significantly associated STs with Italian poultry [57, 58] and has been previously described in humans in Italy [48].

These findings underscore the significant role of chickens as reservoirs for Campylobacter spp. strains, which are responsible for human infections. The high percentage of genetic linkages between human and food isolates emphasizes the importance of controlling Campylobacter spp. in poultry to reduce the burden of human campylobacteriosis, a key goal in food safety and public health [59]. Broiler carcasses are particularly vulnerable to contamination during slaughter, highlighting the importance of good hygiene practices to prevent cross-contamination in the kitchen [59]. The fluctuations in Campylobacter spp. ST prevalence in slaughterhouses could be influenced by the frequency of sampling, monitoring, and quality control in slaughterhouses; production cycles in chicken farming, with peaks aligning with processing activities; and seasonal environmental changes, such as temperature and humidity, which could also affect bacterial survival and transmission. Variations in biosecurity practices and animal husbandry, including feed and housing management, could also play significant roles in introducing and maintaining specific strains [12, 60–63]. The early detection of genomic clusters in real time would allow competent authorities to trace single poultry batches and permit countermeasures at primary production and slaughterhouses to address the diffusion of active clones at the national level.

The genomic surveillance pinpointed several groups with a large geographical spread with frequent observations in multiple regions. These findings, which reveal significant clustering of human isolates across different regions, strongly suggest the possibility of multiple localized outbreaks, potentially originating from common sources possibly unrelated to chicken meat (Fig. 4B). This is particularly evident in C09-ST6175-H-J, which also demonstrated widespread circulation throughout the country and over time. This distribution highlights the complex epidemiology of Campylobacter spp. in Italy and the need for targeted public health interventions. Our work and other WGS surveillance programs have shown that outbreaks may occur more frequently than previously thought and are often linked to retail chicken meat [49, 59]. The small number of human isolates (12.0%) belonging to HF clusters suggests extending genomic surveillance to a larger number of nonhuman isolates, including those from different sources and production chains. Furthermore, other studies have highlighted the high percentage of cattle and swine populations colonized with C. jejuni and C. coli and implicated them as potential reservoirs for C. jejuni and C. coli infection in humans [64–66]. Future research could include increasing the number of human isolates per region and incorporating other potential infection sources, such as cattle, swine, poultry, surface water contaminated with animal feces, sewage effluent, and agricultural run-off [67].

Although the precise estimation of human campylobacteriosis in Italy is far from known, using a previously validated estimation of campylobacteriosis cases in Italy [45], we projected from 114,398 to 168,995 (95% CI: 32.5–48.0%) annual Campylobacter spp. outbreak-related cases in Italy. Campylobacteriosis normally occurs as single and sporadic cases [1], but the data presented here revealed the presence of several cases potentially linked to outbreaks. Of these, 12.0% were attributed to poultry-associated outbreaks, whereas 28.0% were linked to other possible sources. These findings highlight the substantial burden of hidden outbreaks, challenging the prevailing belief that Campylobacter spp. human outbreaks are rare or widely dispersed globally [47], and highlight the critical need for systematic surveillance to inform public health interventions.

Using WGS, we assessed the molecular mechanisms responsible for the high levels of antibiotic resistance observed in the studied collection, emphasizing the importance of this methodology for monitoring AMR in Campylobacter spp.

Data from the European Union summary report on AMR 2021–2022 [3] and the National Antimicrobial Resistance Monitoring System (NARMS) (https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/national-antimicrobial-resistance-monitoring-system/2021-narms-update-integrated-report-summary) (accessed on 03/03/2025) revealed that Campylobacter species exhibit differences in AMR profiles and prevalence between human and food animal sources. The alarmingly high resistance rates to ciprofloxacin observed in human isolates of C. jejuni (70.0%) and C. coli (73.9%), as well as in food-related isolates of C. jejuni (100%) and C. coli (90.7%), raise significant concerns. These findings align with previously reported high levels of fluoroquinolone resistance [1, 48]. Between 2013 and 2022, more than half of the European countries reported increasing trends in fluoroquinolone resistance among C. jejuni isolates, which are typically linked to poultry. In contrast, one-third of the countries reported declining trends in macrolide resistance in Campylobacter spp. isolates from humans, particularly for C. coli [3] (https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/news/continued-efforts-needed-fight-antimicrobial-resistance-amr-humans-and-animals) (accessed on 18/02/2025). Growing evidence of increasing multidrug resistance was found mainly among isolates associated with poultry [68, 69]. MDR patterns such as CipTE, CipTEGm, and CipTGm were predominantly identified in C. coli from food and human sources (12.1%). In contrast, C. jejuni presented a lower prevalence of these MDR patterns, with only one isolate of ST6360 (ST353 CC) detected from a food source. The primary molecular resistance mechanisms identified in this study, aligning with global findings, included the GyrA (T86I) mutation and the presence of the tet(O) gene, with a smaller proportion of isolates harboring the tet(O/32/O) variant. These genetic markers are the main mechanisms responsible for conferring resistance to quinolones and tetracyclines worldwide [48, 70–74]. The presence of the 23S_A2075G mutation, as previously described, was the leading cause of E resistance among the isolates in this study [48, 58, 73, 75]. In contrast to other studies [76], in our clusters, genotypic resistance profiles were largely related to genotypes, indicating an association between resistance and phylogenetic origin.

The emergence of the erm(N) gene in two MDR (CipTEGm) food C. coli isolates in Italy is concerning, marking its first identification in the country and worldwide in veterinarian or food isolates. This methyltransferase, located within the CRISPR arrays of the CRISPR-Cas9 operon, confers resistance to E, as no other mechanism associated with this resistance was identified in these isolates. Identical genetic contexts have been reported in C. coli strain 48777 (ST9840) from Quebec, Canada 2019 (Biosample number SAMN11959355) (Fig. 3), and clinical C. coli strains in France, with potential link of source attribution to pigs and chickens [77–79]. Other two C. coli, isolated from human and unknown source, have been also detected in the UK (Biosample numbers SAMN13254962 and SAMN46430913). No other erm(N) genetic context has been described nowadays. Although the chromosomal presence of this AMR marker suggests its hypothetical clonal horizontal transmission, in our study, detecting this gene in different STs within the ST 828CC clonal complex suggested the absence of clonal spread. Differences have been described regarding the exogenous sequences within the CRISPR arrays in some erm(N) C. coli strains isolated in France [79]. Alternative mechanisms, such as recombination, bacteriophage-mediated transfer, or natural transformation, could be responsible for gene dissemination. While Cas9 and CRISPR repeats are common in C. jejuni (98.0% positive), they are rare in C. coli (9.6% positive) [80]. Therefore, the presence of this immune system in these two erm(N) C. coli isolates could be considered an unexpected occurrence. Recent studies suggest that CRISPR-Cas9 may play a role in limiting horizontal gene transfer in bacteria, providing immunity against bacteriophages and conjugative plasmids, making its involvement in microbial evolution an intriguing area for further investigation [81, 82]. However, this immune system can be inhibited; prophages and insertion sequences can disrupt the antiviral functions of the CRISPR machinery by integrating into the direct repeats of the CRISPR locus, which may serve as integration hotspot [83]. These prophages and insertion sequences could work as hypothetical mobilization elements for the erm(N) gene through recombination or transposition events. The insertion of the erm(N) gene into the CRISPR array, particularly near the cas genes, can disrupt the transcription and processing of CRISPR RNAs. This interruption may disable the CRISPR-Cas9 immune system, which normally recognizes and degrades invading genetic elements such as bacteriophages and plasmids. As a result, the loss of this defense mechanism could increase the bacterium’s susceptibility to horizontal gene transfer, thereby accelerating the acquisition of additional mobile genetic elements, including other AMR genes, becoming MDR strains a development of significant concern. Additionally, the previously described presence of cas genes in Campylobacter spp. plasmids underscore the importance of CRISPR‒Cas in adaptive immunity and gene transfer [48]. Exploring these functions further could provide valuable insights into microbial evolution and resistance mechanisms. The spread of this resistance mechanism in Campylobacter spp. is a significant public health concern. The increasing prevalence of clones carrying these resistance genes conferring erythromycin resistance poses a serious threat to the effectiveness of current treatments for campylobacteriosis, which often relies on macrolide antibiotics. Therefore, a more comprehensive and systematic analysis of the distribution and evolution of this resistance gene is urgently needed.

All these observed AMR mechanisms align with those of previous studies and highlight the critical role of these genetic markers in AMR in Campylobacter spp. strains [48, 70, 71, 84, 85]. Understanding the prevalence of antibiotic resistance and the underlying resistance mechanisms is essential for guiding effective treatment strategies and shaping targeted public health and veterinary interventions. In Italy, given the results of the present study, the use of fluoroquinolones to treat severe campylobacteriosis may prove ineffective because of the remarkably high levels of resistance observed in Campylobacter spp. strains.

Conclusions

The prototype of WGS-based surveillance in this study offered valuable insights into the broader epidemiology of Campylobacter spp. in Italy, including the dynamics of clusters and AMR mechanisms over the year and the emergence of new AMR mechanisms such as erm(N). This study highlighted that a considerable number of human campylobacteriosis cases in Italy were linked to the consumption of contaminated chicken products, with a proportion of the cases still having an uncertain source. Without comparison to concurrent food isolates, WGS-based surveillance of human infections alone offers limited public health value. WGS-based surveillance should be performed from a One Health perspective to increase its effectiveness. The successful implementation of this approach, along with the development of targeted strategies for infection containment, biosecurity measures, hygiene practices, public health consciousness, and vaccine development, requires collaboration among stakeholders from public health, the private sector, politics, civil society, and agri-food companies [86–88].
