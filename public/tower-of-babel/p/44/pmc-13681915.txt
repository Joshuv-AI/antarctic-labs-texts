Species-specific divergence in Phytophthora infestans resistance between Nicotiana benthamiana and potato mediated by 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid pretreatment
Authors: Yuhan Zhang, Yuxin Li, Ying Shi, Chengui Zhao, Jingye Fu, Qiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLate blight, caused by Phytophthora infestans, is a devastating oomycete disease threatening global potato (Solanum tuberosum) production. 1-Aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC), has also been proposed to act as a bioactive signal in plant stress responses, but its contribution to plant immunity remains elusive.ResultsHere, we show that ACC pretreatment exerts opposite effects on P. infestans resistance in Nicotiana benthamiana and the potato cultivar ‘Favorita’, revealing strong species-specific transcriptional reprogramming. In N. benthamiana, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are enriched in defense-related pathways, including plant-pathogen interactions, hormone signaling, and secondary metabolism. In potato, DEGs mainly involve primary metabolism, cell wall remodeling, and photosynthesis regulation. The jasmonic acid (JA) biosynthesis and signaling pathway emerge as a central hub underlying this divergence. ACC markedly induces JA-related genes in N. benthamiana but fails to activate them in potato. Exogenous MeJA enhances resistance, whereas the JA biosynthesis inhibitor DIECA weakens the ACC-associated resistant phenotype in N. benthamiana and aggravates disease in potato. Antioxidant-enzyme assays and DAB staining support distinct ROS-associated responses between the two species. Transient expression assays of JA- and protease inhibitor-related candidate genes further support their roles as downstream contributors to disease resistance.ConclusionsCollectively, our findings suggest that ACC pretreatment produces species-specific defense outcomes against P. infestans through differential recruitment of a JA-associated regulatory network and downstream defense layers, including protease inhibitors and ROS-associated responses. These results refine our understanding of ACC/ethylene-associated immune regulation and provide candidate targets for sustainable late blight management and resistance breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09608-1.

Introduction

Late blight caused by the oomycete Phytophthora infestans is regarded as one of the most destructive plant diseases in agricultural history [1]. Potato, the world’s third most important food crop, is profoundly affected by late blight. This disease is estimated to cause more than US$9 billion annually in combined yield losses and chemical control costs [2]. The continued threat of P. infestans largely stems from its exceptional ecological adaptability and evolutionary capacity. The pathogen can rapidly expand through asexual reproduction and disperse over long distances by windborne sporangia. In areas where both A1 and A2 mating types coexist, sexually produced oospores are able to persist in soil for long periods and substantially enhance population genetic diversity [3, 4]. Such evolutionary dynamics continuously promote the emergence of highly aggressive and fungicide-resistant strains, thereby placing current control strategies under severe pressure. To counter the continual threat of P. infestans, plants have evolved sophisticated systems for immune recognition and signal transduction [5]. In this network, phytohormones serve as central signaling hubs that shape the outcome of host–pathogen interactions by coordinating defense responses and balancing resource investment between growth and resistance [6]. Among them, salicylic acid (SA) is widely considered a core regulator of plant defense against hemibiotrophic pathogens, contributing to both pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) and race-specific resistance. Previous studies have demonstrated that exogenous treatment with its derivative methyl salicylate (MeSA) significantly improves potato resistance to late blight [7]. At the same time, jasmonic acid (JA) and its bioactive derivatives also contribute substantially to defense against P. infestans, but their regulatory effects show clear host dependence and infection stage specificity. Exogenous application of JA can improve potato resistance to late blight by activating defense-related gene expression and stimulating the production of antimicrobial secondary metabolites [8]. Therefore, understanding the dynamic coordination among distinct hormone pathways is crucial for elucidating the mechanisms of plant defense against P. infestans.

Among diverse phytohormone signaling pathways, ethylene and its precursor 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) have received considerable attention in recent years. Ethylene, an important gaseous hormone that regulates plant growth, development, and environmental responses, is widely involved in responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses, especially during plant–pathogen interactions. The ethylene biosynthetic pathway has been well characterized and primarily involves three key enzymes: S-adenosylmethionine synthetase (SAMS), ACC synthase (ACS), and ACC oxidase (ACO) [9]. Accumulating evidence has shown that ACC is involved in the regulation of plant–pathogen interactions, although its effects on disease development vary depending on the host species and pathogen type. For instance, ACC treatment has been shown to enhance tomato susceptibility to Alternaria solani [10]. In rice, disruption of ethylene signaling markedly increases resistance to Rice dwarf virus (RDV), whereas exogenous ACC treatment exacerbates disease severity [11]. Conversely, research in litchi has demonstrated that ACC pretreatment reduces disease symptoms following leaf inoculation with Peronophythora litchii, indicating that ethylene production may enhance resistance to this pathogen [12]. However, the ACC/ethylene module should not be viewed simply as an intermediate in ethylene biosynthesis, but rather as a regulatory component that may shape plant defense outputs against pathogen invasion through synergistic or antagonistic interactions with pathways such as SA and JA [13, 14].

PIs are widely found in plants, fungi, and microorganisms, frequently accumulate in plant storage tissues and aboveground organs, and are rapidly induced by mechanical damage, insect feeding, or pathogen invasion [15]. In the rice-rice blast pathosystem, the effector AvrPiz-t directly targets the host Bowman-Birk-type protease inhibitor APIP4 and inhibits its activity, thereby facilitating infection; conversely, the rice NLR immune receptor Piz-t interacts with APIP4 and enhances its protein accumulation and inhibitory activity, thereby reinforcing immune output [16]. These findings indicate that PIs are not only defense effector molecules, but also key nodes at which pathogen effector attack and host immune protection converge. As a hemibiotrophic pathogen, successful infection by P. infestans depends not only on breaching the host cell wall barrier, but also on sustained access to host nutritional resources [17]. Therefore, cell wall integrity and carbon allocation status together constitute a fundamental frontline defense against pathogen colonization and spread. XTH affects cell wall extensibility and mechanical stability by regulating remodeling of the xyloglucan–cellulose network [18]. SWEET sugar transporters are frequently “hijacked” by pathogens to mediate sugar efflux from host cells, thereby supplying nutrients to the invading pathogen, increasing the availability of utilizable carbon sources in the apoplast, enhancing pathogen nutrient acquisition, and ultimately promoting the colonization and proliferation of P.infestans [19].

Dynamic ROS homeostasis likewise represents a core immune component restricting the proliferation of hemibiotrophic pathogens [20]. In plant immunity, ROS signaling is primarily mediated by systems such as respiratory burst oxidase homologs (RBOHs), and its defensive output depends on the fine balance between production and scavenging [21]. The apoplastic peroxidases PRX33 and PRX34 mediate pathogen-associated molecular pattern–induced ROS bursts, and reduced expression of these genes impairs ROS production and increases plant susceptibility to multiple bacterial and fungal pathogens [22].

Based on this background, we used Nicotiana benthamiana and potato to compare resistance phenotypes after ACC pretreatment and P. infestans infection. By integrating infection assays, transcriptome analysis, pharmacological JA/ethylene-related treatments, DAB staining, antioxidant-enzyme assays, and transient expression of candidate genes, we examined whether ACC-associated resistance divergence is linked to species-specific recruitment of JA-related defense programs and downstream effector modules.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Potato (Solanum tuberosum) cv. ‘Favorita’ was grown in a substrate consisting of nutrient soil and vermiculite (3:1, v/v) under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod at 18–22 °C for 4–8 weeks. N. benthamiana plants were grown in the same nutrient soil vermiculite substrate (3:1, v/v) under identical conditions as potato for 4–5 weeks, and during cultivation, plants were irrigated with the same nutrient solution (N: P:K = 1:1:1). Detached potato and N. benthamiana leaves were sprayed with 100 µmol L⁻¹ ACC/ methyl jasmonate (MeJA)/ETH/ sodium diethyldithiocarbamate (DIECA)/ 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP) solution or sterile water (control) until the leaf surface was evenly moistened, placed in sealed containers, and inoculated after 1 day of treatment.

Pathogen growth and inoculation assays

The inoculation experiments were conducted with the widely used P. infestans strain 14-3-GFP, generously provided by Professor Yu Du from Northwest A&F University. The pathogen was grown on rye Suc agar plates at 18℃ under dark conditions for 10–14 days before spore suspensions were prepared. For spore suspension preparation, 5 mL of pre-cooled sterile water was added to each P. infestans plate, and the hyphae were disrupted with a spreader to release the spores. The prepared suspension was then kept at 4 °C for at least 2 h to allow zoospore release. The zoospore concentration was counted with a hemocytometer and then adjusted to about 1 × 105 zoospores·mL− 1 using sterile water. On N. benthamiana leaves, two symmetrical wound sites were made on the abaxial surface away from the main vein, and each site was inoculated with 20 µL of spore suspension. For potato leaves, two symmetrical wound sites were also made on the abaxial surface away from the main vein, followed by inoculation with 10 µL of spore suspension per site. Leaf images were captured 4–6 days after inoculation, with N. benthamiana leaves photographed under UV light. Lesion area was measured using the ImageJ software, and all inoculation experiments were conducted on detached leaves with no fewer than eight biological replicates.

Determination of the relative growth of P. infestans

gDNA was extracted from P. infestans-infected N.benthamiana and potato leaves using the CTAB method [23], and pathogen growth was quantified by qPCR using species-specific primers for P.infestans (PiO8) [24], potato (StEF1α), and N.benthamiana (NbActin).

DAB staining and intensity analysis

H2O2 accumulation in leaves was visualized by staining with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB). Inoculated leaves were immersed in a 1 mg·mL− 1 DAB solution (pH3.8) and stained overnight at room temperature in the dark on a horizontal shaker, after staining, the leaves were transferred to a decolorizing solution consisting of ethanol and acetic acid at a volume ratio of 3:1 (v/v) for complete chlorophyll removal. The analysis of DAB-stained images was performed according to the method of Juszczak and Baier with appropriate modifications [25]. Images of all samples were acquired under identical illumination, exposure, background, and imaging-distance conditions. Image analysis was conducted using ImageJ software. The images were converted to 32-bit grayscale images, and the entire leaf area was selected as the region of interest (ROI). Consistent threshold parameters were applied to identify DAB-stained areas within the same batch of experimental samples. The percentage of DAB pixel area relative to the total leaf area was defined as the proportion of DAB-stained area. The mean gray value within the leaf ROI was used to calculate the relative DAB staining intensity based on inverted grayscale values, using the formula: Relative DAB staining intensity = 255 − mean gray value, followed by log10 transformation.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Potato and N. benthamiana leaves were sampled at 1 day after inoculation, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subjected to RNA extraction and sequencing. Raw sequencing data was processed using fastp (v0.23.2) to filter low-quality reads and trim adapter sequences. UMI sequences were identified and extracted from clean reads using umikit (v1.0) based on anchor sequences; paired reads possessing both valid 5’ and 3’ UMI anchors were classified as valid UMI reads. The remaining valid UMI reads were aligned to the reference genome using STAR (version 2.7.6a). UMI-deduplicated reads mapping to exonic regions of genes and transcripts were counted using featureCounts (Subread-1.5.1) to calculate expression levels. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially expressed transcripts (DETs) between groups were identified using edgeR (version 3.40.2) with significance thresholds of |log₂(fold-change)| ≥ 1 (fold-change ≥ 2) and a p-value < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis for the DEGs were conducted using KOBAS software (version 2.1.1) with a significance cutoff of p-value < 0.05.Alternative splicing events were detected by using rMATS (v4.1.2) with a p-value cutoff of 0.05 and an absolute value of Δψ of 0.1. After alignment, the reads are assembled de novo using the StringTie(v2.1.4) software. New transcripts were then obtained by discarding the reads whose class code is ‘=’. Supplementary Tables 1 and Table 2 provide detailed sequencing quality-control and mapping statistics.The bubble plot was performed using the Metware Cloud, a free online platform for data analysis (https://cloud.metware.cn). The TBtools software was used to draw the expression heatmaps [26]. The raw data of RNA-seq were deposited in NGDC (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/) (Accession ID: PRJCA060051).

RNA extraction and expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the Trizol reagent (Coolaber, Beijing, China) and dissolved in RNase-free sterile water. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript II Reverse Transcription Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qRT-PCR was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table S1.

Transient overexpression in N. benthamiana

The full-length coding sequence (CDS) of the target gene was amplified via high-fidelity PCR, then inserted into the pCAMBIA-GFP-2300 vector without the stop codon to generate a C-terminal GFP fusion protein construct. The resulting pCAMBIA-gene-GFP-2300 construct was then introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and infiltrated into the leaves of 4-week-old N. benthamiana plants. The empty vector was used as a negative control. At 48 h after infiltration, the leaves were inoculated with 20 µL of P. infestans spore suspension, and symptom images were captured on 4–5 days for lesion area measurement.

Antioxidant enzyme activity and MDA assays

Antioxidant enzyme activities and lipid peroxidation levels were measured in leaf tissues collected after 1 day of CK or ACC treatment without inoculation and at 1 day after P. infestans inoculation following the corresponding treatments. The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX), as well as the malondialdehyde (MDA) content, were determined as described previously [27]. The H₂O₂ content was determined using a commercial assay kit (Beyotime).

Results

ACC pretreatment differentially regulates resistance to P. infestans and ROS accumulation in potato and N. benthamiana

To examine the effect of ACC pretreatment on host resistance, we sprayed leaves of potato and N. benthamiana with ACC, using sterile water as the control (CK), and subsequently inoculated both species with P. infestans to systematically compare their resistance phenotypes. The results revealed strikingly opposite responses to ACC pretreatment in the two species. In potato, ACC pretreatment markedly promoted disease progression. At 4 days post-inoculation, ACC-treated potato leaves exhibited markedly enlarged necrotic lesions (Fig. 1A), and the lesion area ratio was significantly higher than that of CK leaves (Fig. 1B). Consistent with these observations, pathogen biomass analysis showed significantly increased P. infestans DNA accumulation in ACC-treated potato leaves (Fig. 1C), indicating that ACC pretreatment compromised potato resistance to P. infestans. By contrast, ACC pretreatment markedly enhanced N. benthamiana resistance to P. infestans. At 6 days post-inoculation, ACC-treated N. benthamiana leaves displayed substantially milder disease symptoms than CK (Fig. 1D), together with a markedly lower lesion area ratio (Fig. 1E) and reduced P. infestans biomass (Fig. 1F). indicating that ACC effectively suppressed pathogen colonization and proliferation within N. benthamiana tissues.

To assess whether the ACC-associated susceptibility phenotype in potato was restricted to Favorita, we examined four additional potato genotypes, including the susceptible cultivar Désirée and HuaSongXiangZao, the resistant cultivar Q9 and HongMiLa. ACC pretreatment increased disease severity relative to the corresponding CK control in all tested genotypes (Fig. S1A-B). These findings indicate that ACC-induced susceptibility to P. infestans is not limited to a single potato genotype, but is consistently observed across diverse genetic backgrounds, suggesting that the ACC-mediated reduction in potato late blight resistance is broadly conserved.

To further assess the relationship between ACC-associated phenotypes and ethylene-related responses, we performed additional pharmacological assays using 50 mM ACC to determine whether this concentration could induce a phenotypic response, an ethylene-related treatment (ETH), and the ethylene perception inhibitor 1-MCP (Fig. S2A-B). Specifically, in N.benthamiana, both ACC and ETH treatments significantly reduced lesion development compared with CK, whereas 1-MCP did not confer a comparable protective effect(Fig. S2C). In potato, ACC markedly increased lesion severity, and ETH treatment also enhanced disease symptoms, although the effect was weaker than that induced by ACC(Fig. S2D). Treatment with 1-MCP partially altered this response but did not fully abolish the disease-promoting phenotype observed under ACC or ETH treatment. These pharmacological results indicate that ACC and ETH exert similar effects in both species. Notably, 1-MCP treatment led to increased susceptibility in both species, implying that inhibition of ethylene perception compromises basal defense. This consistent effect of 1-MCP may reflect a mechanism in which ethylene signaling contributes to maintaining immune competence, and its disruption could interfere with downstream defense pathways or hormonal crosstalk, thereby enhancing disease susceptibility.

To gain preliminary insight into the physiological basis of the above differences in resistance, DAB histochemical staining was performed to detect ROS accumulation in leaves during P.infestans infection, followed by quantitative analysis of relative DAB staining intensity and the proportion of DAB-stained area. In potato, DAB staining was conducted at 2 days post-inoculation. Compared with CK, ACC-pretreated leaves showed markedly weaker brown DAB precipitates and a reduced staining area (Fig. 1G). The relative DAB staining intensity and the proportion of DAB-stained area were significantly lower in the ACC-treated group than that in the CK group (Fig. 1H, I). In contrast, in N.benthamiana, ACC-pretreated leaves exhibited stronger and more widely distributed DAB staining signals at 4 days post-inoculation (Fig. 1J). Both the relative DAB staining intensity and the proportion of DAB-stained area were significantly higher than those in the CK group (Fig. 1K-L). These results indicate that ACC pretreatment regulates ROS accumulation during P.infestans infection in a species-specific manner: ACC reduced ROS accumulation during the early stage of infection in potato, whereas it enhanced ROS accumulation in N.benthamiana. This divergent oxidative response may be closely associated with ACC-mediated differentiation between host resistance and susceptibility. Taken together, exogenous ACC pretreatment exhibited pronounced species-specific effects on resistance to P.infestans in solanaceous hosts: it enhanced resistance in N.benthamiana but significantly aggravated host susceptibility in potato (Fig. 1).

Species-specific transcriptional reprogramming in response to P. infestans infection following ACC pretreatment in the two species

To elucidate the molecular basis of the ACC-induced divergence in resistance between N. benthamiana and potato, we conducted a comparative transcriptomic analysis of leaf samples from both species following ACC pretreatment and P. infestans inoculation (Table S2). The correlation coefficients indicated high correlations in gene expression levels among the samples (Figure S3A-B). DEG analysis showed that ACC pretreatment elicited significant transcriptional responses in both species, but the magnitude and patterns of these responses were clearly distinct. A total of 2,425 DEGs were identified in N. benthamiana, of which 795 were upregulated and 1,630 were downregulated (Fig. 2A, Table S3). In potato, 1,395 DEGs were detected, including 465 upregulated and 930 downregulated genes (Fig. 2B, Table S4). Although downregulated genes outnumbered upregulated genes in both species, the absolute number of DEGs was higher in N.benthamiana than in potato. To account for differences in the number of annotated genes between the two reference genomes, we further calculated the proportion of DEGs relative to all annotated genes. The results showed that DEGs accounted for 8.23% of the annotated genes in N.benthamiana and 6.57% in potato (Figure S3C-D, Table S5), indicating that a relatively higher proportion of genes responded to ACC pretreatment in N.benthamiana. Although downregulated genes outnumbered upregulated genes in both species, the total DEG number was markedly higher in N. benthamiana than in potato, indicating that N. benthamiana underwent a more extensive transcriptional reprogramming following ACC pretreatment.

To further characterize the functional divergence of transcriptional responses between the two species, separate GO enrichment analyses were conducted for DEGs (Table S6). In N. benthamiana, the DEGs were primarily enriched in immune defense- and hormone regulation-related categories, including regulation of salicylic acid biosynthesis, jasmonic acid metabolism, and defense response. Significant enrichment was also detected in the endoplasmic reticulum chaperone complex and the apoplast under the cellular component category, as well as in monooxygenase and hydrolase activities under the molecular function category (Fig. 2C). These findings suggest that ACC pretreatment in N. benthamiana mainly triggers transcriptional reprogramming associated with immune signal amplification and defense metabolic activation. By contrast, the DEGs in potato were mainly enriched in processes such as regulation of protein stability, lignin biosynthesis, plant-type cell wall biogenesis, and response to reactive oxygen species (Fig. 2D), indicating that its transcriptional changes are more strongly linked to structural remodeling and basal stress adaptation than to widespread activation of typical immune pathways.

To further define the response differences between the two species at the pathway level, KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted (Table S6). In N. benthamiana, the significantly enriched pathways were primarily associated with defense-related networks, including plant-pathogen interaction, MAPK signaling, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis; in addition, specific metabolic branches such as phenylpropanoid, flavonoid, and terpenoid biosynthesis were also significantly enriched (Fig. 2E), suggesting that ACC pretreatment enhanced immune signaling and defense metabolic reprogramming in N. benthamiana. By contrast, the significantly enriched pathways in potato mainly comprised vitamin B6 metabolism, photosynthesis-antenna proteins, zeatin biosynthesis, and cutin, suberine, and wax biosynthesis (Fig. 2F). This result indicates that the transcriptional response of potato after ACC pretreatment was more strongly biased toward primary metabolic adjustment, hormonal homeostasis, and surface barrier formation. Overall, ACC pretreatment mainly triggered transcriptional reprogramming associated with immune defense and secondary metabolism in N.benthamiana. In contrast, in potato, ACC preferentially affected pathways involved in primary metabolic regulation. This pronounced species-specific transcriptional divergence may provide a molecular basis for the distinct effects of ACC on resistance in the two host species.

ACC pretreatment causes suppression of ethylene signaling but elicits SA pathway in both species

To clarify whether ACC pretreatment mediates the differential resistance of N. benthamiana and potato to P. infestans via the ethylene signaling pathway, we systematically compared the expression patterns of ethylene biosynthesis- and signaling-related genes in both species and accordingly generated an expression map of the core DEGs in this pathway (Fig. S4A, Table S7 and S8). The results showed that both species displayed highly consistent transcriptional response patterns in the ethylene pathway. At the biosynthetic level, ethylene biosynthetic gene families, including SAMS, ACS, and ACO were mainly downregulated in both species, suggesting that the ethylene biosynthetic pathway was globally suppressed. At the signaling level, most members of the ethylene response factor (ERF) family were likewise synchronously downregulated in both N.benthamiana and potato, whereas only a few genes were upregulated. These findings indicate that ACC pretreatment induced conserved transcriptional suppression of the ethylene pathway in both host species, rather than species-specific differences capable of explaining the observed divergence in resistance. Taken together, the opposite resistance phenotypes induced by ACC pretreatment are unlikely to be primarily driven by transcriptional changes in the ethylene pathway itself, but are more likely attributable to its modulation of other defense hormone networks.

Given the extensive crosstalk between ethylene and SA signaling in plant immunity, and the well-established role of SA-mediated defense in resistance to biotrophic pathogens, we further examined the expression patterns of SA biosynthesis- and signaling-related genes in both species to evaluate whether ACC pretreatment participates in resistance divergence through modulation of the SA module. The results showed that N. benthamiana and potato likewise displayed highly consistent transcriptional responses at key nodes of the SA pathway (Fig. S4B, Table S7 and S8). At the biosynthetic level, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) family genes showed similar downregulation trends in both species. Meanwhile, ICS1, a key gene in SA biosynthesis, was not significantly differentially expressed in either species, indicating that ACC treatment imposed an overall consistent mode of transcriptional regulation on SA biosynthesis-related genes. At the signaling level, the core regulator NPR1 was not significantly differentially expressed in either N. benthamiana or potato, and the expression changes of the downstream transcription factor TGA1 and pathogenesis-related (PR) family genes likewise failed to show clear species specificity.

Overall, following ACC pretreatment, N. benthamiana and potato exhibited largely conserved transcriptional responses in the ethylene and SA pathways, without marked differences sufficient to account for the divergence in resistance between the two species. This result indicates that the opposite resistance phenotypes induced by ACC are unlikely to be primarily determined by differential regulation of ethylene biosynthesis, ethylene signaling, or the canonical SA pathway. Rather, the species-specific divergence in resistance more likely arises from differential activation of other defense modules, or from fundamental differences in the remodeling of multi-hormone interaction networks across host species. This observation also provides a rationale for subsequent investigation of the JA pathway and its downstream defense effector modules.

Species-specific regulation of the jasmonic acid pathway underlies the core molecular basis of ACC-mediated resistance divergence

Previous studies have demonstrated that JA and its bioactive derivatives are key hormones governing basal resistance to Phytophthora in plants. Exogenous MeJA application markedly enhances potato resistance to P. infestans infection, and treatment with the JA biosynthesis inhibitor DIECA significantly increases host susceptibility, collectively confirming the positive regulatory role of JA biosynthesis and signaling activation in potato resistance to late blight [28]. In addition, JA, as a major regulatory hormone in plant immunity, frequently interacts synergistically or antagonistically with signaling pathways such as ethylene, thereby shaping the magnitude and direction of defense responses. Based on this, we further focused on the JA biosynthesis and signaling pathways and systematically compared the transcriptional response differences between the two species within this pathway after ACC pretreatment (Fig. 3A, Table S7 and S8).

Transcriptomic analysis showed that N. benthamiana and potato exhibited markedly divergent response patterns in the JA pathway. At the JA biosynthetic level, several genes encoding key enzymes were significantly upregulated in N. benthamiana, including lipoxygenase (LOX), allene oxide synthase (AOS), 12-oxophytodienoate reductase (OPR), and jasmonic acid-amido synthetase (JAR1), suggesting that ACC pretreatment activated JA biosynthesis and potentially promoted the formation of its bioactive conjugate, JA-Ile, in N. benthamiana. In contrast, genes associated with JA biosynthesis in potato showed no obvious overall induction, suggesting that ACC pretreatment failed to effectively initiate the JA biosynthetic process in this species. At the JA signaling level, members of the JAZ family were significantly downregulated in N. benthamiana, whereas the expression level of the core transcription factor MYC2 was increased. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in the expression of JAZ and MYC2 family members between ACC-pretreated and CK-pretreated potato samples after inoculation.

To validate the reliability of the transcriptomic analysis, key JA biosynthesis-related genes were selected for independent examination of their expression patterns in both species using qRT-PCR (Fig. 3B). The results showed that NbLOX, NbAOS, NbOPR, and NbJAR1 in N. benthamiana were all significantly induced after ACC pretreatment and P. infestans inoculation, with transcript levels markedly higher than those in the inoculated control group. By contrast, StLOX, StAOS, StOPR, and StJAR1 in potato showed no significant induction after ACC pretreatment and P. infestans inoculation, and their transcript levels were not significantly different from those of the control group, indicating that the qRT-PCR results were fully consistent with the transcriptomic data.

To test whether JA signaling contributes functionally to the observed disease phenotypes, we performed MeJA and DIECA assays (Fig. 3C-F). In N.benthamiana, both ACC and MeJA reduced lesion area ratio compared with CK, whereas DIECA increased disease severity and the ACC + DIECA treatment partially abolished the protective effect of ACC (Fig. 3C, E). In potato, ACC and DIECA increased lesion area ratio, whereas MeJA reduced disease severity; notably, ACC + MeJA partially rescued the ACC-associated susceptible phenotype (Fig. 3D, F). These pharmacological results support the hypothesis that JA signaling is a major contributor to the divergent disease outcomes. These results indicate that ACC pretreatment effectively activated the JA signaling pathway only in N. benthamiana, but failed to trigger a corresponding transcriptional response in potato.

ACC pretreatment elicits differential transcriptional responses of the protease inhibitor gene family in N. benthamiana and potato

The above results showed that ACC pretreatment significantly activated JA biosynthesis and signaling in N. benthamiana, but did not effectively activate the corresponding pathway in potato. Because JA typically confers disease resistance by regulating the expression of downstream defense effectors, we further focused on its downstream defense output modules to elucidate the effector-level mechanism underlying ACC-mediated resistance divergence. During family-level screening of the DEGs, the PI family showed especially pronounced species-specific differences. PIs are important effector molecules in plant immunity, and their expression is often regulated by JA signaling, enabling them to play critical roles in defense against both herbivores and pathogens [29]. Heatmap analysis showed that, following ACC pretreatment and P. infestans inoculation, the PI family exhibited strikingly opposite transcriptional response patterns in the two species. In N. benthamiana, multiple PI family members, including class I3, I13, and I20 genes, were broadly upregulated, whereas the corresponding homologs in potato were broadly downregulated (Fig. 4A, Table S9). This result suggests that the PI family may constitute an important downstream effector module mediating ACC-induced species-specific resistance divergence.

To validate the reliability of the transcriptomic results, representative genes from the I3, I13, and I20 classes of protease inhibitors (PIs) were selected for independent examination of their expression patterns using qRT-PCR (Fig. 4B). The results showed that, in N. benthamiana, genes from all three PI classes were significantly induced after ACC pretreatment and P. infestans inoculation, with expression levels markedly higher than those in the inoculated control group. By contrast, in potato, the corresponding genes were all significantly downregulated under the same treatment conditions, with transcript levels lower than those in the inoculated control group. The qRT-PCR results were highly consistent with the expression trends revealed by the transcriptomic analysis, further confirming the species-specific regulation of PI family genes by ACC.

ACC pretreatment induces species-specific transcriptional responses in cell wall remodeling, ROS homeostasis, and immune-related pathways

Beyond the classical defense hormone pathways, plant resistance to pathogens depends on multiple layers of regulation, including dynamic cell wall remodeling, carbon metabolism and resource allocation, maintenance of photosynthesis, and ROS homeostasis. These functional modules do not operate independently, but are interconnected during pathogen infection and jointly determine host barrier strength and immune output. To more systematically explain the opposite resistance phenotypes induced by ACC pretreatment in N. benthamiana and potato, we compared DEGs associated with cell wall modification, sugar transport, photosynthesis, and ROS scavenging. The results showed that these modules generally displayed opposite transcriptional responses in the two species (Fig. S5), indicating that ACC affects not only hormone signaling but also species-specific remodeling of defense and metabolic support processes.

Cell-wall-related and sugar-transporter genes showed opposite transcriptional patterns between the two species (Fig. S5A-B). In N. benthamiana, XTH-family genes and several SWEET genes were downregulated after ACC pretreatment during infection, whereas several corresponding genes were upregulated in potato. Because XTH genes are associated with xyloglucan-cellulose network remodeling and SWEET transporters can be exploited by pathogens for sugar acquisition [30], these transcriptional patterns may reflect differences in host structural remodeling and pathogen nutrient acquisition.

Sustained immunity also requires an adequate energy supply and tightly regulated ROS signaling. We therefore examined the expression patterns of DEGs associated with photosynthesis and ROS scavenging. After ACC pretreatment and P. infestans inoculation, photosynthesis-related genes were broadly upregulated in N. benthamiana, whereas most of the corresponding genes were significantly downregulated in potato (Fig. S5C). This pattern is consistent with better maintenance of chloroplast function and carbon assimilation in N. benthamiana, potentially supporting for cell wall reinforcement, secondary metabolism, and redox regulation. By contrast, suppression of photosynthesis-related genes in potato may indicate reduced resource availability for sustained defense. Consistent with the DAB staining results, the ROS-scavenging system showed contrasting transcriptional responses in the two species (Fig. S5D and Table S10). Despite gene-specific variation, ACC pretreatment generally induced a more responsive antioxidant state in N. benthamiana following P. infestans inoculation. This transcriptional pattern was further supported by enzymatic assays. Compared with the infection-only treatment, ACC-pretreated N. benthamiana exhibited markedly higher SOD, CAT, POD, and APX activities after pathogen inoculation (Fig. 5A). These increases indicate that ACC pretreatment enhances ROS processing and scavenging capacity in N. benthamiana. Consistent with the DAB staining results, ACC-pretreated N. benthamiana accumulated more H₂O₂ during P. infestans infection. Meanwhile, MDA content remained significantly lower in ACC-pretreated tissues than in the corresponding control groups, indicating reduced membrane lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage. Collectively, these data suggest that ACC pretreatment does not simply induce excessive ROS accumulation in N. benthamiana, but instead establishes a controlled redox state in which ROS can function as defense signals while oxidative damage is effectively limited.

In contrast, potato exhibited a distinct redox regulatory pattern. Although P. infestans infection increased the activities of several antioxidant enzymes in potato, ACC pretreatment did not further enhance this response. Instead, SOD, CAT, and APX activities were lower in ACC-pretreated infected potato than in the infection-only group, whereas MDA content reached its highest level following combined ACC pretreatment and pathogen inoculation (Fig. 5B). These results indicate that ACC pretreatment weakens antioxidant buffering capacity in potato during infection and aggravates membrane lipid peroxidation. Importantly, the lower H₂O₂ content observed in ACC-pretreated infected potato should not be interpreted as evidence of improved ROS detoxification. When considered together with the reduced antioxidant enzyme activities and elevated MDA accumulation, this pattern more likely reflects disrupted ROS homeostasis, in which ROS signaling and scavenging are poorly coordinated.

Collectively, these results indicate that ACC pretreatment triggers species-specific immune regulation in N. benthamiana and potato. In N. benthamiana, ACC promotes a coordinated defense state characterized by reduced expression of sugar-transporter genes, maintenance of photosynthesis-related transcription, enhanced antioxidant capacity, and reduced lipid peroxidation, thereby contributing to increased resistance to P. infestans. In contrast, ACC pretreatment in potato is associated with the upregulation of sugar-transporter- and cell-wall-related genes, suppression of photosynthesis-related transcription, weakened antioxidant responses, and aggravated oxidative damage. Thus, ACC does not universally enhance plant immunity; rather, its effects depend on how different host species translate ACC signaling into integrated defense responses.

Transient expression assays validate the functions of JA, PI, and SWEET-related candidate genes in resistance to P. infestans

To functionally validate the candidate differentially expressed genes identified by transcriptomic analysis, we selected representative genes from the jasmonic acid (JA) biosynthesis and signaling pathway, the proteinase inhibitor (PI) family, and the SWEET sugar-transporter family for transient expression assays in N. benthamiana. GFP-tagged candidate genes were transiently expressed in leaves, with GFP alone serving as the empty-vector control, followed by inoculation with P. infestans. Disease symptoms and lesion areas were evaluated at 5 days post-inoculation (dpi) (Fig. 6A, B).

Transient expression of the JA-biosynthetic genes NbAOS and NbOPR significantly reduced lesion areas compared with the empty-vector control, indicating that enhanced JA biosynthesis promotes resistance to P. infestans. In contrast, transient expression of NbJAZ, which encodes a negative regulator of JA signaling, markedly increased lesion development, supporting a positive role for JA signaling in host defense. Similarly, overexpression of the proteinase inhibitor gene NbPI20 significantly restricted lesion expansion, suggesting that PI-mediated defense contributes to resistance against P. infestans.

By contrast, transient expression of either the potato sugar-transporter gene StSWEET1 or its N. benthamiana counterpart NbSWEET1 significantly increased lesion areas, indicating that both SWEET proteins promote host susceptibility to P. infestans. Collectively, these transient-expression assays functionally support the transcriptomic results and demonstrate that JA biosynthesis and PI-mediated defense contribute positively to resistance, whereas JAZ-mediated repression of JA signaling and SWEET-associated sugar transport favor P. infestans infection.

Discussion

Host-dependent divergence of ACC-associated resistance responses to P. infestans

ACC pretreatment exerted opposite effects on resistance to P. infestans in N. benthamiana and potato. In N. benthamiana, ACC reduced lesion expansion and pathogen biomass and enhanced ROS accumulation during infection; in contrast, in potato, ACC increased lesion size, elevated pathogen biomass, and suppressed early ROS accumulation. This susceptibility-promoting effect was observed across multiple potato genotypes, indicating that it is not cultivar-specific but may represent a conserved response pattern in potato.

Although ethylene pathway-related genes showed similar transcriptional trends in the two species, ETH and 1-MCP assays demonstrated that ethylene-related signaling still contributes to disease phenotype regulation. ACC reprograms the allocation of immune resources during early infection in a host-dependent manner, leading N. benthamiana to adopt a resistant state characterized by JA activation and enhanced ROS accumulation, while directing potato toward a susceptible state that favors pathogen colonization and spread [31].

Transcriptomic analysis further revealed that this phenotypic divergence is accompanied by coordinated but oppositely directed reprogramming across multiple defense modules, including JA signaling, cell wall remodeling, sugar transport, and ROS homeostasis. These findings suggest that the immune effects of ACC are jointly shaped by host genetic background and the responsiveness of downstream defense pathways. Previous studies have also shown that ACC/ethylene-related regulation can either enhance resistance or promote susceptibility depending on the host–pathogen combination. Such differences may arise from variations in hormone crosstalk, host developmental status, pathogen lifestyle, and the responsiveness of downstream defense modules [32]. Future studies should further examine whether ACC or ethylene directly affects the growth, sporulation, or zoospore behavior of P. infestans in vitro [33].

Divergent JA activation is a key determinant of ACC-associated disease outcomes

ACC treatment caused broadly similar suppression of ethylene biosynthesis- and signaling-related genes in N. benthamiana and potato, while SA-pathway genes also showed largely conserved transcriptional responses. ETH produced effects similar to those of ACC within each species, whereas 1-MCP increased susceptibility in both hosts. These findings indicate that ethylene signaling contributes to basal defense and ACC-associated disease regulation, but differences in ethylene or ethylene-SA transcriptional responses alone cannot explain the opposite phenotypes. In contrast, JA signaling closely paralleled disease resistance. In N. benthamiana, ACC strongly induced JA biosynthesis and activation genes, including LOX, AOS, OPR, and JAR1, increased MYC2 expression, and reduced the expression of JAZ genes. The corresponding JA network was not effectively activated in potato. Pharmacological assays further showed that MeJA reduced disease severity, whereas DIECA enhanced susceptibility and partially impaired the protective effect of ACC. RNAi-mediated silencing of StAOS2 in potato significantly reduced JA biosynthesis and impaired quantitative resistance to late blight, directly demonstrating that StAOS2-mediated JA biosynthesis positively contributes to potato resistance against P. infestans [34]. Transient overexpression of NbAOS and NbOPR significantly restricted lesion development, providing additional functional evidence that JA biosynthesis promotes resistance to P. infestans. Collectively, the relatively conserved ethylene and SA responses, together with the species-specific activation of JA signaling, identify JA as a major regulatory node underlying the opposite effects of ACC in N. benthamiana and potato. Future studies should further quantify JA levels and related metabolites to validate these conclusions.

Species-specific regulation of ROS homeostasis contributes to opposite ACC-associated disease outcomes

ROS-mediated immunity depends on the coordinated balance between ROS production, signaling, and scavenging rather than on ROS abundance alone [35]. DAB staining showed that ACC pretreatment enhanced H₂O₂ accumulation in N. benthamiana during P. infestans infection but reduced early H₂O₂ accumulation in potato. In N. benthamiana, this enhanced oxidative response was accompanied by increased SOD, CAT, POD, and APX activities and reduced MDA accumulation, indicating that ACC promotes a controlled redox state in which ROS signaling is strengthened while oxidative damage is effectively limited. By contrast, ACC-pretreated potato exhibited lower SOD, CAT, and APX activities and the highest MDA content after infection. Therefore, the reduced H₂O₂ level in potato is unlikely to reflect more efficient detoxification, but instead suggests impaired coordination between ROS production and scavenging, resulting in weakened immune signaling and increased membrane damage. Because ACC can influence both ROS-generating systems, such as RBOH and apoplastic peroxidases, and antioxidant buffering pathways [36, 37], these results suggest that host-specific regulation of redox homeostasis constitutes an important physiological basis for the contrasting resistance responses of N. benthamiana and potato.

Multilayered transcriptional divergence of ACC-induced defense networks shapes host resistance to P. infestans

Beyond hormone signaling, ACC pretreatment induced coordinated but contrasting transcriptional responses in multiple defense and metabolic modules, including protease inhibitors, cell wall remodeling, sugar transport, and photosynthesis. PI-family genes were strongly induced in N. benthamiana but broadly repressed in potato. Because PIs can inhibit pathogen-derived proteases and protect apoplastic defense proteins from proteolytic degradation [38, 39], their differential expression may directly affect the capacity of the two hosts to restrict pathogen colonization. Consistent with this interpretation, transient overexpression of NbPI20 significantly reduced lesion development, supporting PIs as independent functional effectors of anti-Phytophthora defense. However, loss-of-function analyses and functional validation in potato are still required to determine whether PI repression contributes directly to ACC-induced susceptibility.

Cell-wall-related, SWEET, and photosynthesis-related genes showed similarly divergent expression patterns. Several XTH-family and SWEET genes were downregulated in ACC-pretreated N. benthamiana but upregulated in potato. These changes may influence cell wall remodeling and pathogen access to host-derived sugars, respectively [18, 40, 41]. In parallel, photosynthesis-related genes were maintained or induced in N. benthamiana but broadly suppressed in potato, suggesting contrasting capacities to sustain the energy and metabolic requirements of defense. Collectively, these transcriptional responses indicate that ACC reorganizes defense effector deployment, structural remodeling, nutrient availability, and metabolic support in a host-dependent manner. Their coordinated divergence provides a plausible explanation for the enhanced resistance of N. benthamiana and increased susceptibility of potato following ACC pretreatment. Nevertheless, conclusions concerning cell wall properties, apoplastic sugar availability, and photosynthetic performance remain primarily transcriptome-based and require direct physiological validation.

Several limitations of this study should be emphasized. First, the RNA-seq comparison was conducted between infected ACC-pretreated samples and infected CK-pretreated samples, without ACC-only controls or non-infected mock-inoculated controls. Therefore, the identified DEGs represent ACC-associated transcriptional differences under infection conditions and may also include effects resulting from differences in disease pressure or disease severity. Second, all infection assays were performed using detached leaves. Although detached leaves are useful for controlled infection assays, their hormone dynamics, senescence processes, source-sink relationships, and systemic signaling may differ from those of intact plants. Whole-plant experiments will therefore be conducted in future studies. Third, endogenous hormone levels and ethylene emission were not directly measured in this study, and these measurements will be included in future work. Fourth, functional validation was primarily based on transient expression in N. benthamiana, and stable loss-of-function mutant analyses have not yet been performed. Future studies will include validation using stable transgenic materials or virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS). Despite these limitations, this study provides evidence that ACC-associated disease regulation is strongly host dependent. Differential recruitment of the jasmonic acid (JA) pathway, together with redox- and metabolism-related processes that support defense responses, contributes to the opposite resistance outcomes against Phytophthora infestans observed in N. benthamiana and potato.

Conclusions

Overall, ACC pretreatment produces opposite P. infestans disease outcomes in N. benthamiana and potato. The revised results suggest that this divergence is associated with differential recruitment of JA-related signaling, PI-family defense output, and ROS-associated physiological responses. Pharmacological MeJA/DIECA assays and transient expression of JA/PI candidate genes support the importance of this network. These findings provide a refined framework for understanding host-dependent ACC/ethylene-associated immune regulation and identify JA- and PI-related genes as candidate targets for improving late blight resistance.
