Unconventional vesicle transport driven by formin-actin-myosin working system during pollen germination
Authors: Tonghui Li, Chang Liu, Tongtong Shen, Bo Li, Qi Wang, Zhe Zhu, Xuna Wu, Ting Wang, Huimin Wang, Haiyun Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Directional transport of massive secretory vesicles to the germination site is a key process for pollen germination, but the molecular mechanism remains unclear. Here, we find that Myo11C proteins, Myo11C1 and Myo11C2, act redundantly. This functional redundancy is evidenced by impaired vesicle tethering efficiency and aberrant F-actin organization in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant. Myo11C1 unexpectedly colocalized with AtFH5-located secretory vesicles (AtFH5-SVs), rather than with actin filaments. Simulated modeling and truncation mutation analyses show that Myo11C binds actin filaments nucleated from AtFH5-SVs and exhibits plus-end–directed motility, leading to its polarized localization at F-actin plus-ends. Upon dissociating from F-actin, Myo11C directly interacts with AtFH5-SVs to promote their aggregation. E447 is identified as a critical residue for Myo11C1 binding to F-actin, while R1377 and R1452 are conserved residues essential for Myo11C1’s interaction with secretory vesicle–located SEC5B. Together, our work uncovers the molecular basis by which formin-actin-myosin working system generates force driving massive secretory vesicles for directional transport during pollen germination.

INTRODUCTION

The propagation of next generation in flowering plants is determined by the successful double-fertilization process. The mature pollens fall on the stigma, germinate, and grow pollen tubes, subsequently, two sperm cells are delivered to the ovule and target to egg cell and central cell, respectively (1). Polarity establishment is a prerequisite during pollen germination. Extensive secretion is indispensable for incorporating pollen wall material, proteins, and membrane components into the pollen germination site via the vesicular delivery system (2, 3). Several regulators involved in exocytosis are identified, especially the vesicle tethering and fusion-related factor like Arabidopsis exocyst complex subunits, are revealed to guide secretory vesicles (SVs) to plasma membrane (PM) during pollen germination process (4–8). However, our understanding of the directional vesicle transport process in pollen remains very limited.

By live imaging the spatiotemporal localization of green fluorescent protein (GFP)–tagged Armadillo Repeat Only 1 (ARO1), the polarization of vesicle-located ARO1-GFP and Lifeact-tagged F-actin was observed in the pollen grain before pollen germination (9), indicating the spatiotemporal regulatory role of actin dynamics in SV trafficking during pollen germination. AtFH5 is a type I formin protein with a transmembrane domain in Arabidopsis and localizes to SVs to promote actin polymerization during pollen germination process (10). The dynamic interaction between SV trafficking and actin polymerization is defined by two different stages: F-actin polymerization generates forces propelling the directional movement of AtFH5-located SVs (AtFH5-SVs) in pollen cytoplasm; then, AtFH5 translocates from SVs to PM, and, correspondingly, a collar-like actin structure is organized at the prospective site. G-actin–binding proteins, PRF4 and PRF5, are revealed to promote AtFH5-mediated actin polymerization and enhance the interaction between AtFH5-SVs and actin filaments (11). Although genetic and pharmacological data confirmed that the disruption of F-actin dynamics affected the AtFH5-SV rotational movement speed, the aggregation state of the AtFH5-SVs is still maintained (10). How to maintain the aggregated massive SVs for directional transport during pollen germination is still unclear.

Actin-based myosin motors have been reported as powerful players during organelle movement, actin organization, and cytoplasmic streaming in plant cells. In angiosperms, there are two groups of myosin proteins: myosin VIII and myosin XI. The Arabidopsis genome encodes four members of myosin VIII proteins and 13 members of myosin XI proteins (12). Both myosin families contain the N-terminal motor domain (MD), the neck domain, the coiled-coil region, and the C-terminal globular tail domain (GTD). In comparison with myosin XI, myosin VIII proteins have a shorter neck domain and a shorter coiled-coil domain. Expression pattern of myosin VIII differs among its four members: ATM1 (Arabidopsis thaliana myosin 1) and VIII-A are ubiquitously expressed, whereas ATM2 and VIII-B exhibit pollen-specific expression (13). Studies on the localization of class VIII myosins have implicated these myosins in diverse processes, including cell-to-cell communication, maintenance of cortical tension, endocytosis, and cell division (14, 15). In addition, myosin VIII plays a role in the microtubule organization at the growing tip of moss cells (16). A recent in vitro assay has revealed that the lipid-binding polybasic region of AtATM2 is necessary for its association with the pollen tube PM (13). On the other hand, studies have revealed that a few class XI myosins are required for trafficking of Golgi stacks, mitochondria, and peroxisomes (14), as well as the flow of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and F-actin organization (17–19). Class XI myosins are also revealed to be involved in root morphogenesis by affecting the polar distribution of auxin responses and cell division (20). Recent studies have found that myosin XI plays a key role in the delivery of cellulose synthase complexes to the PM by interacting with the exocyst complex in plants (21, 22). Arabidopsis myosin XI proteins—Myo11A1 (XIA), Myo11A2 (XID), Myo11B1 (XIB), Myo11C1 (XIC), Myo11C2 (XIE), and Myo11D (XIJ)—are highly expressed in pollen (23), which suggests that these members potentially play important roles during pollen germination and tube growth. The pollen tube growth of the myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant was much slower, and the fertility was significantly reduced. Also, the movements of intracellular organelles, like Golgi stacks and peroxisome, were significantly reduced, and F-actin was less organized in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant (23). Function analysis of Myo11C2 (Myo XIE) revealed that Myo11C2 could rescue the defective pollen tube growth in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant. GFP-tagged Myo11C2 (Myo11C2-GFP) colocalized with mitochondria, peroxisomes, and Golgi stacks. The fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) analysis of AtPRK1-GFP–labeled SVs indicated the involvement of Myo11C1 and Myo11C2 in apical accumulation of SVs in pollen tubes (24). The myosin xi-b/e double mutant showed decreased pollen tube growth and slower peroxisome movement, which are similar to those in myo11c1 myo11c2 (25). Collectively, myosin XI proteins mainly function in plant cells by transporting vesicles and organelles and regulating F-actin organization.

Using the AtFH5-labeled SV trafficking (AtFH5-SVs) as a working system in pollen grains, it was reported that 2,3-butanedione monoxime (BDM) treatment blocking the motor activity did not alter the coordination between F-actin dynamics and AtFH5-SVs during the rotational movement stage, but the velocity of vesicle trafficking was significantly reduced (10). This raises the question how myosin proteins function during pollen germination, particularly in regulating directional vesicle transport. In this study, we reveal that Myo11C proteins coordinate with AtFH5-dependent actin polymerization and form the formin-actin-myosin system that generates the directional forces required for vesicle tethering and transport during pollen germination.

RESULTS

Impaired AtFH5-SV distribution and F-actin organization are observed in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant during pollen germination

To identify the potential myosin proteins involved in pollen germination, a total of seven myosin transferred DNA (T-DNA) insertion lines were screened on the basis of their homology (fig. S1A) to obtain homozygous mutants, after which statistical analysis of pollen germination rates was performed. The myo11d single mutant and atm2 viiib and myo11a1myo11a2 double mutants showed similar germination rates to those of wild type (WT; fig. S1, B to D). Mutations in both Myo11C1 (myo11c1, SALK_129231C) and Myo11C2 (myo11c2, SALK_089338C) (fig. S2A) resulted in a significantly reduced germination rate compared with the WT, although the single mutants (myo11c1 or myo11c2) seemed as normal as WT (Fig. 1, A and B). The results suggested that Myo11C1 and Myo11C2 were functionally redundant during pollen germination. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis revealed 83.1% reduction in Myo11C1 expression in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant, while Myo11C2 transcripts were undetectable (fig. S2B). The myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant exhibited shorter pollen tubes (fig. S2, C and D) and reduced seed set (fig. S2, E and F), consistent with previous reports (23). To further confirm that the pollen-related defects in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant were caused by the loss function of Myo11C1 and Myo11C2, we introduced the Myo11C1 native promoter sequence along with a genomic DNA fragment containing two tandemly linked mNeonGreen tags into the mutant strain. Several independent transgenic lines showed varying degrees of pollen defect rescue. Two complementation lines, Myo11C1-2mNG #7 and Myo11C1-2mNG #14, fully restored the defects in pollen germination, tube growth, and seed production in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant (Fig. 1, A and B; and fig. S2, C to F), demonstrating that functional Myo11C1 is essential for these processes. We also generated the complementation construct using a genomic DNA fragment of Myo11C2 and introduced it into the myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant to assess its ability to rescue the mutant phenotype. Pollen germination and fertility defects in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant were rescued in complementation lines (fig. S3), providing strong evidence for functional redundancy between Myo11C1 and Myo11C2.

Given that myosin XI has been implicated in F-actin organization across various plant species (14, 26, 27) and considering the similarity between the C-terminal GTD of myosin XI and the cargo-binding domain of myosin V in animal and fungi (28, 29), we sought to determine which myosin activities were required for pollen germination. As SV rotational movement coordinates with dynamic actin polymerization to promote pollen germination, we examined the spatiotemporal interactions between Lifeact-mEGFP (monomeric enhanced green fluorescent protein)–labeled F-actin and mCherry-labeled AtFH5-SVs in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant. Spatiotemporal correlation analysis of fluorescent intensity at site 1 and site 2 revealed that Lifeact-mEGFP fluorescence peaks consistently followed those of AtFH5-SVs in both WT and myo11c1 myo11c2 (Fig. 1, C to F, and movies S1 and S2), suggesting that the coordinated rotational pattern of AtFH5-SVs and F-actin remained normal in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant. However, the duration of one complete rotational cycle was significantly prolonged in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant compared with WT (Fig. 1G), consistent with our previous findings from BDM treatment (10).

We observed that the F-actin in WT underwent dynamic structural transitions, progressing from a diffuse state (Fig. 1C, 0 s) to short and compact bundles at specific sites (Fig. 1C, 2475 and 2925 s). The originally dispersed AtFH5-SVs in pollen grain cytoplasm were gradually aggregated into a spherical cluster, underwent regular rotational movement, and ultimately translocated to the prospective germination site via active exocytosis (fig. S4). In contrast, myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant showed a significantly delayed F-actin dynamic transition, tending to form parallel-aligned and overbundled F-actin that was hardly detected in WT (Fig. 1D, 4150 s). These elongated and curved F-actin bundles surrounding AtFH5-SVs frequently disassembled into disordered structures (Fig. 1D, 5325 s). Consequently, AtFH5-SVs in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant maintained a diffuse alignment for substantially longer periods (Fig. 1H, 6459 ± 1362 s versus 3217 ± 414 s, WT) before eventually forming rotating aggregates resembling WT patterns (Fig. 1D, 5325 s). The aggregated rotationally active AtFH5-SVs failed to maintain stability, frequently dispersing and subsequently exhibiting more random rotational motion (fig. S4). Analysis of the overlap between fluorescence density curves of Lifeact-mEGFP and AtFH5-mCherry, along with the observation of disorganized F-actin and impaired vesicle aggregation in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant (Fig. 1, C to H), indicates that Myo11C coordinates both F-actin dynamics and AtFH5-SV transport to polarized secretion site during pollen germination.

Time-lapse imaging and simulation modeling analysis reveals the dynamic interaction among Myo11C proteins, SVs, and actin filaments

To examine the potential dynamic interaction among Myo11C1, F-actin, and AtFH5-SVs, we generated the triple-labeled lines and introduced into myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant. To differentiate the fluorescent signal from Lifeact-mEGFP and AtFH5-mCherry, Myo11C1 with two tandemly linked mTurquoise tags (Myo11C1-2mT) driven by the endogenous promoter was constructed. The transgenic lines fully rescued the pollen germination defect (Fig. 2B), confirming their functionality for subsequent analyses. The spatiotemporal correlation analysis of relative fluorescent density at site 1 revealed that, during the rotational movement, Myo11C1-2mT signal extensively overlapped with AtFH5-SVs, while F-actin fluorescent signal generally lagged behind AtFH5 and Myo11C1 (Fig. 2, A to C, and movie S3). We performed more detailed analysis of colocalization among the three components of formin-actin-Myo11C1. Initially, at the onset of pollen hydration, AtFH5, F-actin, and Myo11C1 displayed diffuse and extensively overlapping colocalization. Subsequently, as the SVs underwent aggregation and the F-actin dynamically rearranged, the strong colocalization was maintained among the three components (Fig. 2A, 0 and 810 s). When the SVs in pollen were maintained in stable aggregation state and exhibited a polar distribution, Myo11C1 showed more specific colocalization with AtFH5-SVs (Fig. 2A, 2790 and 3240 s). However, localized overlap between Myo11C1 and F-actin was observed at specific sites (Fig. 2, A, D, and E). We also performed the analysis of dynamic interaction during pollen germination process. The stable and high colocalization of AtFH5 and Myo11C1 was maintained throughout the pollen germination (fig. S5). These results suggested that, during the process of actin polymerization and subsequent promotion of SV aggregation, Myo11C1 specifically interacts with AtFH5-SVs and F-actin to regulate the polarized AtFH5-SV aggregation. We treated hydrating pollen with various concentrations of brefeldin A (BFA), a specific inhibitor of intracellular vesicle trafficking, to examine its effect on vesicle aggregation (fig. S6). Treatment with 1 μM BFA significantly inhibited pollen germination (fig. S6F). At a concentration of 10 μM, BFA inhibited the aggregation of AtFH5-SVs, causing them to form BFA bodies instead (fig. S6A), which suggested that maintenance of AtFH5-SV aggregation state was essential for pollen germination.

To elucidate how Myo11C proteins maintain polarized AtFH5-SV aggregation, we used Cytosim computational modeling to simulate AtFH5-SVs clustering dynamics in pollen incorporating F-actin, Myo11C, and AtFH5-SVs (30, 31). Our simulation model is based on three key assumptions: (i) AtFH5 proteins localize to SVs and nucleate actin polymerization, with the resulting polymerization force propelling vesicle movement (10, 11); (ii) Myo11C exhibits dual functionality: plus-end–directed motility along F-actin and cargo-binding capability to AtFH5-SVs (14, 28); and (iii) Myo11C functions as a dimer, with the capability to bind one to two cargoes (32, 33). Simulation of Myo11C at WT expression level (indicated by cyan dots) demonstrates that Myo11C drives the gradual aggregation of initially diffuse AtFH5-SVs during pollen germination (movie S4). Myo11C behaves predominantly in the following functional states (Fig. 2F, WT): (i) F-actin bound form: Myo11C binds F-actin, moves toward the plus-end, then dissociates, and subsequently attaches to AtFH5-SVs; (ii) Cargo-transporting form: Myo11C carries SVs or AtFH5-SVs as cargo along F-actin in a plus-end–directed manner; (iii) Vesicle-bound form: Myo11C remains tethered to SVs or AtFH5-SVs and is passively transported by actin polymerization force. For detailed simulation analysis, refer to Materials and Methods. When the WT pollen begins to germinate, vesicles propelled by actin polymerization collide with each other or with F-actin, where Myo11C acts as a molecular adhesive to promote vesicle clustering (Fig. 2F, 6.96 and 14.36 s, WT). These small clusters continue moving and eventually coalesce into larger polarized aggregates for rotational movement (Fig. 2F, 49.96 and 59.96 s). In myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant, where the simulated Myo11C was reduced to 20% of WT levels based on the qRT-PCR result (Fig. 2G, fig. S2B, and movie S5), vesicles still formed the small clusters (Fig. 2G, 71.96 s) and ultimately formed large aggregates (Fig. 2G, 140.76 s). However, the aggregation process was significantly slower and unstable (Fig. 2G, 166.96 s), consistent with our cellular imaging analyses (Fig. 1, D and H). Small vesicle clusters assembled along cross-linked F-actin bundles with misaligned orientations (Fig. 2G, 71.96 and 85.16 s, yellow rectangles), and the simulated F-actin organization closely resembled the long, curved actin bundles observed experimentally in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant (Fig. 1D). Under Myo11C-depleted conditions, actin polymerization alone drove AtFH5-SVs movement, leading to transient vesicle clustering, but the clustered AtFH5-SVs could not be stably maintained and exhibited divergent movement directions, leading to disorganization of the F-actin structures (Fig. 2H, 10.56, 13.36, and 16.76 s; and movie S6). However, these clusters failed to coalesce into larger aggregates (Fig. 2H, 179.96 s). Together, this model recapitulates the actin treadmilling-based AtFH5-SV transport during pollen germination and elucidates the critical role of Myo11C in sustaining polarized vesicle aggregation.

Myo11C’s MD is essential for polarized SV aggregation

Given the strong concordance between theoretical predictions and experimental observations, we used this simulation model to investigate how distinct functional domains of Myo11C proteins regulate vesicle polarized aggregation. We simulated a system where the myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant expressed a defined quantity of Myo11C proteins: 20% functionally intact Myo11C molecules (cyan dots) and 80% MD-deleted, actin-binding-deficient Myo11C-ΔMD molecules (yellow dots, Fig. 3A and movie S7). If AtFH5-SV polarized aggregation state strictly depended on Myo11C-AtFH5-SVs interactions, then the actin-binding–deficient Myo11C proteins should rescue the AtFH5-SV aggregation defect in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant. The simulation revealed that, while dysfunctional Myo11C proteins remained distributed diffusely, the residual functional Myo11C could still initiate vesicle clustering, ultimately forming larger aggregates (Fig. 3A, 155.56 s), albeit with slower kinetics compared with myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant (Fig. 2G, 140.76 s). The simulated F-actin also organized into the cross-linked and curved bundles (Fig. 3A, 74.76 and 125.76 s, yellow rectangles). These results demonstrate that Myo11C’s actin-binding activity and plus-end–directed motility are essential for polarized SV aggregation.

Because myosin XI proteins use their N-terminal MD for actin binding and adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) hydrolysis (34, 35), we generated the truncated Myo11C1 variant lacking the MD (residues 1 to 741) (fig. S7A) and introduced it into myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant. Deletion of the Myo11C1 MD (Myo11C1-ΔMD) failed to rescue the pollen germination defect in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant; instead, an exacerbated pollen germination defect was observed in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant (Fig. 2B). Time-lapse imaging analysis revealed that dynamic F-actin organization in Myo11C1-ΔMD resembled that in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant, characterized by longer and more curved F-actin bundles (Fig. 3, B and C, and movie S8). Both experimental and computational analyses demonstrated that Myo11C-ΔMD in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant prolonged the maintenance of AtFH5-SVs in a diffuse state, with a significantly extended duration compared with myo11c1 myo11c2 alone (Fig. 3, A, B, and D). To further investigate the regulatory role of Myo11C1’s actin-binding capability in AtFH5-SV aggregation, we introduced a point mutation at E447, a residue conserved in myosin V MDs and implicated in inhibiting ATP hydrolysis and actin binding activity (36). We confirmed the presence of this conserved site in Myo11C1 (fig. S7B). The E447A mutation phenocopied Myo11C1-ΔMD mutant, exhibiting severe pollen germination defect (Fig. 2B), elongated and thickened F-actin bundles, and prolonged retention of AtFH5-SVs in a diffuse state (Fig. 3, B to D, and fig. S7C). Collectively, the data from simulation and experiment suggest that Myo11C’s plus-end–directed motility is prerequisite for its polarized distribution and this motility acts as the critical determinant for AtFH5-SVs’ polarized aggregation.

Myo11C1 interacts with SEC5B to tether SVs for aggregation

Our experimental data showed the colocalization of Myo11C1 and AtFH5-SVs during pollen germination, suggesting a potential direct interaction between Myo11C1 and AtFH5-SVs. To explore the potential interaction, we incorporated a fraction of vesicle-binding–deficient Myo11C proteins (Fig. 4A and movie S9) into the simulated model: 20% functionally intact Myo11C molecules (cyan dots) and 80% vesicle-binding–deficient Myo11C-ΔGTD molecules (Fig. 4A, indicated by yellow dots). The simulation showed Myo11C-ΔGTD (deletion of the Myo11C GTD) formed the clusters and transiently overlapped with AtFH5-SVs driven by misaligned F-actin bundles (Fig. 4A, 15.36, 41.56, and 45.76 s). In the presence of the low abundance of functional Myo11C molecules, the polarized AtFH5-SV aggregates ultimately formed with uniform F-actin alignment (Fig. 4A, 154.76 s). The Myo11C-ΔGTD exhibited similar vesicle dynamics as that in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant, suggesting that the interaction of Myo11C GTD and SVs was very important for vesicle aggregation during pollen germination.

To examine whether Myo11C1 interacts with AtFH5-SVs in vivo, we performed coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) coupled with mass spectrometry analysis to identify potential Myo11C1-interacting proteins. Several exocyst subunits were significantly enriched in Myo11C1 Co-IP fractions (fig. S8A). Using yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay, with exocyst subunits as bait and the Myo11C1 GTD as prey, we found that Myo11C1 GTD specifically interacted with SEC5B (Fig. 4B), similar to previously reported interaction between XIK GTD and SEC5B (22). To further examine the potential relation between AtFH5 and SEC5B, quantitative fluorescence colocalization analysis was performed. The results demonstrated strong colocalization between SEC5B and AtFH5 during the stages of pollen hydration, as well as at the initiation and maintenance stages of vesicle aggregation (fig. S9). These results strongly supported our simulated model, which predicts the functional dimeric Myo11C can bind one to two cargoes. Previous studies identified two conserved positively charged residues critical for exocyst binding in both yeast Myo2p and Arabidopsis XIK (22, 37, 38). Similarly, we found that Myo11C1 contained the conserved binding residues R1377 and R1452 (fig. S7B), then we generated R1377A/R1452A point mutant, and tested its interaction with SEC5B using split-luciferase complementation assay and pull-down experiment. Notably, neither the R1377A/R1452A point mutant nor the MD-only construct of Myo11C1 showed detectable binding to SEC5B (Fig. 4, C and D, and fig. S8B). These results demonstrate that Myo11C1 and XIK share evolutionarily conserved mechanisms for SV binding.

To test whether Myo11C1’s capability to bind to SV regulates actin treadmilling-mediated transport of AtFH5-SVs during pollen germination, we generated Myo11C1R1377A/R1452A and expressed it in the myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant. The Myo11C1R1377A/R1452A mutant reduced pollen germination rate and delayed the formation of large AtFH5-SV aggregates (Fig. 4, E to H). These phenotypes closely resembled those observed in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant, indicating that the interaction of Myo11C1 with SVs was also very important for pollen germination. Consistent with the simulation model, time-lapse imaging revealed partial colocalization between Myo11C1R1377A/R1452A and AtFH5-SVs (Fig. 4E and movie S10). Despite the low abundance of functional Myo11C proteins, the combined effects of actin treadmilling forces and residual vesicle–tethering activity from functional Myo11C GTD ultimately promoted the assembly of AtFH5-SVs from small clusters to large aggregates. Notably, the AtFH5-SVs in Myo11C1R1377A/R1452A mutant exhibited rotational movement comparable with that of WT, demonstrating that these point mutations do not affect actin treadmilling velocity (Fig. 4I).

DISCUSSION

During pollen germination, substantial accumulation of cell wall synthesis materials, PM components, and key regulatory proteins at the germination site is required (3–8). To enable rapid and efficient cargo delivery to specific sites for pollen germination, SVs might use coordinated transport strategies through vesicle clustering/aggregation mechanisms. Our integrated experimental and computational analysis reveals a previously unrecognized role of myosin in vesicle trafficking, demonstrating its capacity to mediate vesicle tethering and clustering, thereby markedly improving the efficiency of actin-powered directional vesicle movement.

Our previous work demonstrated that vesicle-localized formin protein AtFH5 in coordination with profilin promotes actin polymerization to generate propulsive forces for vesicle transport (10, 11). Here, this noncanonical actin polymerization–driven mechanism for massive vesicle transport is comprehensively elucidated by integrating computational simulation with experimental data: (A) Upon pollen hydration, AtFH5 localized on the SVs and nucleated actin polymerization, facilitating tight association between vesicles and dynamic F-actin; (B) actin polymerization generated forces to propel vesicle movement, and AtFH5-SVs can stochastically form aggregates with randomly oriented trajectories; (C) The presence of Myo11C proteins promoted the stable aggregation of AtFH5-SVs and alignment of their directional movement through their ability to bind and tether vesicles and the plus-end–directed motility along actin filaments; (D) when AtFH5-SVs became directionally aligned and gradually accumulated at the cell cortex, the trailing F-actin tended to adopt a more ordered arrangement; and (E and F) Myo11C proteins associated with AtFH5-SVs and underwent passive transport by actin polymerization forces (Fig. 5). The formin-actin-myosin system collectively strengthens the directional transport efficiency of massive SVs during pollen germination. This work challenges the established “actin track–based transport” model and proposes an alternative force-generation mechanism in intracellular transport.

Working model of Myo11C-mediated regulation on AtFH5-actin treadmilling-based transport of AtFH5-SVs during pollen germination.

(A) After hydration, AtFH5 localized on SVs initiates actin polymerization. (B) Actin polymerization generates forces to propel vesicle movement (red arrows). Myo11C proteins drive AtFH5-SVs toward F-actin plus-ends, which are capped by AtFH5 bound to vesicles (blue arrows). When vesicle clusters move toward each other from different directions, Myo11C proteins tether them together and facilitate uniform alignment of filaments and AtFH5-SVs (dashed red arrows) through three functional models: (i) directional movement along F-actin without cargo; (ii) SEC5B-mediated vesicle capture and plus-end–directed transport; and (iii) simultaneous binding of two vesicles. (C) Myo11C proteins mediate the directional transport of AtFH5-SVs by coupling vesicle movement to their own plus-end–directed motility along F-actin. (D) AtFH5-SVs, driven by actin polymerization, move unidirectionally and gradually accumulate at the cell cortex. (E) Myo11C proteins dissociate from F-actin upon reaching the F-actin plus-ends, attach to AtFH5-SVs, and promote the gradual assembly of these AtFH5-SVs into clusters. (F) Myo11C proteins associate with AtFH5-SVs and undergo passive transport by actin polymerization forces.

The plant myosin XI proteins are known as the fastest motors, and their function and regulation mechanisms have always been the topic of great interest in the field. Myo11C2 is previously reported to partially colocalize with mitochondria, peroxisomes, and Golgi stack and plays an important role in organelle movement and apical accumulation of SVs in Arabidopsis pollen tubes (24). Compared with Myo11C2, what we know about the function of Myo11C1 during pollen development is limited. This study revealed that Myo11C1 and Myo11C2 function redundantly during pollen germination. Myo11C1 tethered vesicles through interacting with the exocyst subunit SEC5B and facilitated the SV movement in unified direction. Study from Physcomitrium patens reported that myosin XI focuses and directs vesicles at the tip of the cell, and loss of functional myosin XI dissipated tip-localized VAMP (vesicle associated membrane protein)-labeled vesicles (39). Arabidopsis myosin XIK protein was revealed to interact with some exocyst complex components to facilitate the tethering of CESAs (cellulose synthases) to PM (22). The residues R1366 and R1441 in GTD of myosin XIK are key for the interaction and function. It is found that the residues R1366 and R1441 also exist in Myo11C1 and Myo11C2 proteins, and our results showed both residues were essential for Myo11C1’s interaction with SEC5B. Our results are consistent with the work from Zhang et al. (22) and suggest that myosin XI and budding yeast Myo2p share evolutionarily conserved function in direct interaction with exocyst subunits via the conserved amino acid in their GTD. Considering the expanded size of plant myosin XI family, whether different myosin proteins have their specific interaction network for cellular vesicle trafficking remains to be further explored.

Several actin-binding proteins are reported to regulate pollen germination via altering actin organization dynamics (7). It is revealed that, in myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant, diffused F-actin took much longer to be organized to F-actin bundles; also, the level of the F-actin bundling was significantly increased. It is indicated that Myo11C proteins regulate the F-actin polymerization dynamics, which is consistent with previous report for xi3KO mutant (19). Similarly, misshaped and twisted actin bundles were revealed in root hair tip in the xi-k/1/2/i mutant (17). Ueda et al. (18) reported that the twisted F-actin bundles were also observed in the epidermal cells of petioles in xik mya2 mutant (18). From the cellular F-actin dynamic imaging analysis in different myosin XI genetic mutants, it is suggested that myosin XI proteins function in promoting actin turnover, and the regulation might coordinate collectively with other actin-binding proteins. Furthermore, we identified a residue E447, conserved in myosin V MDs and implicated in inhibiting ATP hydrolysis and actin binding activity, as a key site for the regulation of actin assembly and the plus-end transport function by Myo11C1. The biochemical properties of E442A MV, the structural counterpart of the E447A mutation in Myo11C1, have been well characterized via various in vitro assays. Its affinity for F-actin is in the micromolar range, whereas the WT myosin V binds actin with nanomolar affinity (36, 40). In addition, the E447A mutant protein’s inability to hydrolyze ATP severely impaired its motor activity. Together, it is indicated that the E447A mutant protein exhibited phenotypes similar to those observed with Myo11C-ΔMD protein. We tried to isolate and purify the MD of Myo11C1 to perform the in vitro F-actin assembly assay but failed to get the purified Myo11C1 protein for analysis. Therefore, optimization of the Myo11C1 expression and purification system will help elucidate its molecular mechanism in regulating F-actin dynamics. Conservation analysis of the key amino acids in both MD and GTD of Myo11C suggests that myosin XI and myosin V proteins may share notable spatial structural similarities. We will also leverage the existing research on myosin V from animal and yeast cells, particularly the functional analysis of the key amino acid mutations, to further elucidate the working mechanism of plant myosin XI proteins.

In summary, our integrated experimental and computational analyses support that, when AtFH5-mediated actin polymerization provides the force for the mobility of SVs in pollen grains (10), Myo11C proteins provide a previously unrecognized function in tethering adjacent vesicles to form the aggregates through direct interaction with the exocyst subunit SEC5B during pollen germination. Therefore, the formin-actin-myosin system collectively determines massive SV directional transport during pollen germination. In the oocytes, vesicles recruit Spire1/2 and Fmn2 to assemble an actin network enabling myosin Vb–dependent directional transport for vesicle convergence and surface delivery (41). Unlike animal cells, which use microtubules as tracks for long-distance transport, plant cells use predominantly actin-myosin motor system for vesicle trafficking and organelle movement. Continuous synthesis of cell walls during plant cell growth and division implies a heightened demand for directional cargo trafficking, which correlates with the greater stability of plant actin filaments than animal actin filaments (42). During cytokinesis, the cell plate signifying nascent cell wall formation is organized by specialized phragmoplast arrays of actin microfilaments and microtubules. These arrays enable polarized delivery of cell wall synthesis materials. Notably, the accumulation of type I formins (AtFH5 and AtFH8) (43, 44) and myosin XI (45) at the cell plate suggests the potential involvement of the formin-actin-myosin system mediated transport in cell plate assembly. It is reported that both myosin XIa and VAMP72 colocalize at the cortex of moss cells as highly dynamic punctate structures, whose motility depends on actin filaments (46), indicating that myosin XIa coordinates the traffic machinery and the actin network dynamics to maintain polarized growth in moss cells. Together, based on type I formin proteins’ membrane-anchoring capability and actin nucleation activity, coupled with myosin GTD’s specific organelle recognition and binding capacity, the actin-AtFH5-Myo11C working system cooperating with other known or unknown actin-binding proteins and signaling molecules (11, 47, 48) might play a crucial regulatory role in the directional transport of a large amount of substances in both plant cells and animal cells. Further studies in different cellular systems are needed, and such investigations will broaden our understanding of actin-based organelle transport (41, 42). In addition, further biochemical characterization of Myo11C’s different functional domains and critical residues will provide deeper mechanistic insights into its function and regulation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and growth conditions

All Arabidopsis lines are Columbia ecotype (Col-0). The T-DNA insertion mutants—myo11c1 (SALK_129231C), myo11c2 (SALK_089338C), myo11a1 (SALK_086989), myo11a2 (SAIL_607_G06), atm2 (SALK_104355C), viiib (SALK_134493C), myo11d (SALK_026367C), and sec5b (SALK_001525C)—were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center. The myo11a1 myo11a2 and myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutants were shared by A. Nebenführ (Tennessee University) and were described previously (23). Atm2 viiib double mutant was obtained by crossing atm2 and viiib single mutant. Homozygous T-DNA insertion lines of myo11d, myo11c1, myo11c2, and sec5b single mutant and myo11c1 myo11c2, myo11a1 myo11a2, and atm2 viiib double mutants were identified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) genotyping (table S1). The pLat52:Lifeact-mEGFP/pFH5:FH5-mCherry line was described previously (10). The lines of pLat52:Lifeact-mEGFP/pFH5:FH5-mCherry in myo11c1 myo11c2 were generated by crossing, and homozygous myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutants were identified in the F2 population by PCR-based genotyping (table S1). The pSEC5B:SEC5B-2mNeonGreen/pFH5:FH5-mCherry line was generated via crossing. To generate the triple-labeling lines, pLat52:Lifeact-mEGFP/pFH5:FH5-mCherry line was crossed with the FP-labeled proteins such as Myo11C1-2mT, Myo11C1ΔMD-2mT, Myo11C1E447A-2mT, and Myo11C1R1377A/R1452A -2mT. Arabidopsis thaliana and Nicotiana benthamiana plants were grown in a greenhouse at 22°C under a 16-hour light/8-hour dark cycle.

Pollen germination and pollen tube growth measurements

Arabidopsis pollen was collected from newly opened flowers of 4- to 6-week-old plants and cultured on solid pollen germination medium [1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM Ca(NO3)2, 1 mM MgSO4, 0.01% (w/v) H3BO3, 18% (w/v) sucrose, and 0.15% (w/v) agar; pH 6.90 to 6.95] at 28°C, as previously described (49). For pollen germination analysis, pollen grains were incubated for 3 hours and observed using an AXIO Observer D1 (Zeiss) microscope with a 10× objective. More than 500 pollen grains were counted in each experiment and repeated at least three times. Pollen tube growth rates were quantified by capturing initial images (T1) when pollen tubes reached about 100-μm length, followed by reimaging after 30 min (T2). Growth rates were calculated as Δlength/Δt = (Length2 − Length1)/(T2 − T1). More than 20 pollen tubes were measured in each experiment and repeated at least three times. For BFA treatment, pollen grains were placed on a dialysis membrane, resting on the solid pollen germination medium for 30 min. The membrane was then transferred to medium containing various doses of BFA (Selleck Chemicals) for 3 hours. The pollen germination rate was calculated by counting at least 100 pollen grains per experiment, with five experimental replicates.

Last, the Myo11C1 genomic sequence was amplified with primers C1-F/R and cloned into the Xba I site via infusion cloning (Vazyme) to produce the complementation vector pMyo11C1:Myo11C1-2mNeonGreen-UTR. qRT-PCR assay

Pollen was obtained from more than 1000 open flowers at the same developmental stage. Total RNA was then isolated from pollen grains using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (TIANGEN) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The total RNA was subsequently used for cDNA synthesis via reverse transcription with cDNA Synthesis Supermix (TRANSGEN). For qRT-PCR analysis, the expression level of Myo11C1 and Myo11C2 was detected using specific primers qPCR-Myo11C1-F/R and qPCR-Myo11C2-F/R (table S1), respectively. UBQ10 was amplified as an internal control with primers qPCR-UBQ10-F/R (table S1).

Pollen total protein extraction, Co-IP, and on-beads trypsin digestion

More than 1000 open flowers at the same developmental stage were collected from pMyo11C1:Myo11C1-2mNeonGreen plants for total pollen proteins isolation. Flowers were immersed in pollen germination buffer and vigorously shaken for several min. Pollen grains were collected by centrifuging at 4000g for 10 min at 4°C and then lysed on ice for 1 hour in protein extraction buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 0.5% (v/v) NP40, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride and 1% (v/v) cocktail]. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000g for 15 min at 4°C. Pellets were discarded, and supernatants were recentrifuged under identical conditions. Final supernatants were retained for Co-IP assay.

The supernatants were incubated with 25 μl of prewashed mNeonGreen-trap agarose beads on a rotating wheel for 1 hour at 4°C. Beads were collected by centrifugation at 2500g for 5 min at 4°C and washed five times with 500 μl of washing buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), and 1 mM DTT]. On-beads trypsin digestion was performed as described (50). Briefly, beads were mixed with 40 μl of UTU [6 M urea and 2 M thiourea (pH 8.0)] for 20 min at room temperature, predigested with endoproteinase Lys-C (0.5 mg/ml; Wako Chemicals) for 3 hours at room temperature, diluted fourfold with 10 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), and then digested with trypsin (0.5 mg/ml; Promega) overnight at 37°C. Digestion was stopped with 10% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid. Peptides were desalted over C18.

Peptides mixtures were analyzed by nanoflow Easy-nLC 1200 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Orbitrap hybrid mass spectrometer (Orbitrap exploris 480, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Detailed liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry analysis was performed according to previously described methods (50).

Split-luciferase complementation assay

The different nLUC (N-terminal luciferase) and cLUC (C-terminal luciferase) plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium strain GV3101 and coinfiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. After infiltration, plants were maintained under continuous illumination for 48 hours. Leaves were then injected with 1 mM luciferin, dark adapted for 30 min, and imaged using a cooled charge-coupled device camera. p35S:GUS-nLUC and p35S:cLUC-GUS vectors were used as negative controls.

Y2H assays

Bait and prey plasmids were cotransformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain AH109. Transformants were selected on synthetic defined (SD) medium lacking leucine and tryptophan and incubated at 30°C for 3 to 5 days. Positive interactions were screened on SD medium lacking leucine, tryptophan, adenine, and histidine. Cotransformations with pGBKT7 and pGADT7 were used as a negative control.

Protein pull-down assay

The vectors were transformed into Transetta (DE3) Chemically Competent Cells, and fusion protein expression was induced with 0.2 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside at 16°C for 20 hours. Glutathione S-transferase (GST), GST-GTD, and GST-GTDR1377A/R1452A fusion proteins were extracted using BeyoGold GST-tag Purification Resin (Beyotime). His-SEC5B fusion protein was extracted and purified with BeyoGold His-tag Purification Resin (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Freshly prepared GST, GST-GTD, and GST-GTDR1377A/R1452A proteins were separately mixed with 50 μl of glutathione Sepharose 4B beads and incubated at 4°C for 1 hour with shaking. For quantitative pull-down assays of SEC5B with GST, GST-GTD, or GST-GTDR1377A/R1452A, GST-tagged proteins (1 μM) were incubated with 2 μM His-SEC5B in 500 μl of binding buffer [140 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 1.8 mM KH2PO4, and 10 mM Na2HPO4 (pH 7.3)] at 4°C for 1 hour with shaking. Beads were washed with elution buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 10 mM glutathione (reduced form)], boiled in SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) sample buffer for 10 min, and then analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting.

Time-lapse imaging analysis

Time-lapse imaging of actin filaments, AtFH5-mCherry and Myo11C1 dynamics during pollen germination was performed with spinning disk confocal microscope (UltraView VoX, PerkinElmer, Beaconsfield, Buckinghamshire, UK) which equipped with a Yokogawa Nipkow CSU-X1 spinning disk scanner, a Nikon TiE inverted microscope, and a Hamamatsu EMCCD 9100-13. The 488-, 561-, and 440-nm lasers were used for imaging. Z-stack time-series images were collected at maximum speed with the z step set at 0.5 μm. The acquired images were processed by Volocity (PerkinElmer) and ImageJ (https://imagej.net/ij/).

To correct for photobleaching effects, fluorescence intensity was normalized by calculating relative density: the ratio of fluorescence density in each region of interest to total fluorescence density in the entire cell. Skewness was measured to quantify the extent of actin filament bundling as previously described (51, 52). For pollen grains, the z-series stacks of all optical sections were background subtracted using Gaussian blur, skeletonized with the LPX plugin in ImageJ, and then merged into maximum-intensity projections. Skewness values were calculated using hig_skewness plugin. Pollen germination and pollen tube growth rates were measured using ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism 10 (version 10.4.1), and two-tailed Student’s t test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were performed to determine the statistical significance.

Cytosim simulations

We used Cytosim (https://gitlab.com/f-nedelec/cytosim), an open-source cytoskeleton simulator, to model the dynamic interactions of actin filaments, AtFH5-SVs, and Myo11C as Brownian particles in a viscous medium. The simulation used a 2.0-μm-radius sphere, 0.01-s time steps, viscosity of 1 pN·s/μm2, and steric push/pull forces of 10 pN. It ran for a total duration of 18,000 steps (180 s), capturing 5400 frames (53). In Cytosim, fibers model filaments, solids model vesicles, and hands primarily interact with fibers-enabling binding, growth, and movement along filaments via parameter adjustments. We set the fiber filament length to 1.0 μm, with vesicle and myosin radius of 0.09 and 0.03 μm, respectively. These parameters were chosen for visualization and computational efficiency and did not affect our analysis results. Actin filaments were modeled as fibers with bending rigidity of 0.075 pN·μm2/rad and segment length of 0.0055 μm. Treadmilling was simulated with plus-end polymerization at 0.65 μm/s and minus-end depolymerization at 0.60 μm/s, switching to 0.60 μm/s at both ends once filaments reached 1.0 μm (54–57). Vesicles (AtFH5-SVs, radius of 0.09 μm) were modeled as solids with multiple high-stiffness binding sites. We simulated 30 vesicles, each with four binding sites for myosin GTD binding; 20 vesicles also contained AtFH5 for F-actin nucleation. AtFH5 and the four binding sites were modeled as hand types: AtFH5 nucleated F-actin, whereas the binding sites nucleated a 0.01-μm filament for myosin binding.

Myo11C molecules were modeled as 0.03-μm particles with two GTD (capable of binding up to two vesicles) and MDs (binds F-actin). Sixty Myo11C molecules were used in the WT, with motor velocities of 1.5 μm/s (14, 57) (MD/F-actin binding: on-rate of 10 s−1, 0.1-μm range, off-rate of 0.5 s−1; GTD/SEC5B binding: on-rate of 5 s−1, 0.05-μm range, off-rate of 0.01 s−1). In myo11c1 myo11c2 double mutant, functional Myo11C protein numbers were reduced to 12 (an 80% decrease), weakening vesicle transport. In MD deletion mutant, 12 WT Myo11C and 48 Myo11C-ΔMD proteins were introduced to the simulation, and the 48 Myo11C-ΔMD proteins allowed vesicle anchoring without transport. In GTD deletion mutant, 12 WT Myo11C and 48 Myo11C-ΔGTD were introduced to the simulation, and the 48 Myo11C-ΔGTD retained processive movement toward F-actin plus-end for transport but lacked vesicle-anchoring capability.
