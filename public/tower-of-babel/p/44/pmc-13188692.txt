Profiling the transcriptional regulatory network reveals putative shared regulatory elements within homoeologs in polyploid Brassica napus
Authors: Yue Hu, Yupeng Jia, Zhiquan Yang, Wei Lin, Lei Zhang, Feifan Yin, Chuchuan Fan, Yongming Zhou, Qing-Yong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRegulatory elements and their interactions underpin transciptional control and are essential for plant growth and development. Yet, in the allopolyploid crop Brassica napus, the global regulatory landscape and the architecture of its complex regulatory networks remain elusive.ResultsHere we integrate chromatin accessibility and DNA methylation profiles from four representative accessions to generate a comprehensive atlas of 38,068 regulatory elements in Brassica napus. By coupling these regulatory features with expression quantitative trait locus data, we construct a genome-wide regulatory network comprising 24,541 regulatory elements and 791,512 associations between regulatory elements and genes during seed development. Incorporating 3D genome data reveals that spatial proximity promotes co-expression of cross-chromosomal homoeologous gene pairs through putative shared regulatory elements. Moreover, we uncover pronounced subgenome asymmetry, with the regulatory elements on the An subgenome exerting a disproportionately stronger regulatory influence than those on the Cn subgenome.ConclusionsThis study provides valuable resources for studying the intricate regulatory network governed by regulatory elements and sheds new light on exploring the cross-chromosomal regulation in polyploid plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04047-5.Keywords: Regulatory elements (REs), RE-gene associations, Putative shared regulatory elements, Asymmetric regulation

Background

Plant growth and development depends on the accurate regulation of gene expression, which is coordinated by cis-regulatory elements (CREs), transcription factors (TFs), and other regulatory elements (REs) [1, 2]. Epigenetic maps have proven effective in identifying plant CREs, such as accessible chromatin, low DNA methylation, and active histone signals [3–5]. Notably, chromatin accessibility and/or histone modifications are tissue-specific [5, 6], while DNA methylation patterns are relatively stable across tissues [7–9]. Unmethylated regions (UMRs) of a single tissue have been found to be enriched for accessible chromatin from various tissues, representing potential CREs [10]. Given the difficulty of technical and experimental verification, exploring the relationship between REs and target genes in plants has certain limitations. Long-range chromosomal interactions can promote REs to bypass more linearly proximal genes to interact with nonadjacent genes [4–6]. Recent studies have employed chromatin interactions, chromatin loops, and correlation of histone modifications to identify regulatory links between REs and their target genes [5, 6, 11]. These regulatory links form a complex network that controls various phenotypic traits in plants. In rice, the trade-off between grains per panicle and tiller number has been resolved by deleting cis-regulatory regions [12]. Additionally, constructing the wheat integrative gene regulatory network (wGRN) has assisted in identifying key functional genes controlling spike development for wheat breeding [13]. Therefore, the identification of CREs and the construction of regulatory networks are essential for crop genetic improvement.

The regulation of gene expression in the nucleus of polyploid plants becomes more complex following whole-genome duplication (WGD). Polyploid plants exhibit an increase in the number of genes, often two or more times greater than the ancestral genome, and the amplification of homoeologous gene pairs significantly alters gene expression patterns [14–16]. Homoeologous gene pairs in polyploid plants have a tendency to be co-expressed or exhibit expression biases [17–19]. The spatial proximity of subgenomic homoeologous gene pairs in allotetraploid cotton, for instance, has been linked to their co-expression [20]. Epigenetic alterations in the ancestral genome have been found to be the major contributor to homoeologous gene expression bias in allotetraploid Brassica napus (B. napus), with cis-regulatory effects playing a dominant role [18]. Previous studies in wheat have explored the impact of genomic variation on regulating homoeolog expression dosage, further shaping agronomic phenotypes [17]. Notably, the phenomenon of transvection observed in Drosophila revealed that homologous chromosomes rely on spatial proximity for their trans-regulatory interactions, providing a potential explanation for the regulation of homologs [21–24]. However, research on this phenomenon in plants remains limited [25]. Furthermore, asymmetry of subgenomes is a significant characteristic of polyploid plants, such as asymmetry in evolution, expression, and epigenetics [18, 26, 27]. In B. napus, the An subgenome exhibits higher intensities of active marks and lower DNA methylation compared to the Cn subgenome, while the Cn subgenome has a higher transposon density [28]. Similarly, in cotton, the regulatory effect of the D subgenome is more significant than that of the A subgenome [29]. Moreover, owing to WGD events, the crowding of regulatory elements from multiple ancestors into polyploid plants, followed by the newly added inter-subgenomic regulation and interactions, contribute to more complex regulatory relationships in the nucleus [16]. However, the complex transcriptional regulatory relationships in B. napus have not been clearly characterized.

In this study, based on assay for transposase accessible chromatin sequencing (ATAC-seq), bisulfite sequencing (BS-seq), high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C), RNA-seq and eQTL data, we aim to construct a comprehensive genome-wide regulatory network specifically focused on the seed development stage of B. napus and reveal regulatory characteristics. We found the colocalization of RE–gene associations and chromatin compartments and demonstrated the widespread existence of putative shared REs across the genome. Ultimately, our findings highlight the crucial roles that RE-gene associations play in the asymmetric regulation of the subgenome. These results provide valuable insights into the asymmetry and complexity of subgenome regulation and serve as rich resource of regulatory relationships for further exploration of gene expression in allopolyploid B. napus.

Results

Identification of REs at stage of seed development in B. napus

Recent studies have shown that accessible chromatin is enriched with active REs [3–5, 30]. To explore the landscape of REs in B. napus, we first performed ATAC-seq on the seed tissue of four representative B. napus accessions at 20 days after flowering (DAF). A total of 829 million clean sequencing reads were generated (Additional file 1: Table S1), which were then mapped to the high-quality reference genome of ZS11 [31]. High reproducibility was observed between the biological replicates within each accession (Additional file 2: Fig. S1). By merging the accessible chromatin peaks from all the accessions, we obtained a more comprehensive and representative set (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, we identified 41,860 open chromatin regions (OCRs), which account for 2% of the whole genome (Additional file 1: Table S2, Additional file 2: Fig. S2). The observed OCR proportions are within the range previously reported for plant genomes (0.22–6.5%) [4]. Furthermore, we observed an enrichment of OCRs proximal to transcription start sites (TSSs) (Fig. 1B), in line with previous reports [3, 5, 32]. Notably, a smaller peak was also detected downstream of transcription end sites (TESs) (Fig. 1B), a pattern similarly observed in species such as wheat [33], cotton [34], rice [35] and mouse [36]. This signal may reflect the binding of factors engaged in transcription termination or function as enhancers [36–38].

The unmethylated regions (UMRs) in the genome are highly conserved in different tissues during vegetative development [7–9], less than 0.1% of the UMRs in various tissues differ from one another [10]. Crisp et al. showed that the majority (more than 90%) of accessible chromatin regions were captured in UMRs, which could reveal the location of CREs [10]. Next, we combined BS-seq data of the above four accessions to further identify REs in B. napus. Statistically, the average bisulfite conversion rate of all samples was 99.67% (Additional file 1: Table S3). By integrating the identified OCRs, we found that OCRs were associated with lower DNA methylation levels (Fig. 1C), consistent with previous findings by Sun et al. [11]. Using the method proposed by Crisp et al. [10], we identified 614,327 UMRs regions in B. napus (see Method, Additional file 1: Table S4), which encompassed 90.94% of the OCRs (Fig. 1D). Then, the regions that displayed both UMRs and OCRs were designated as candidate REs (Fig. 1A, D). Altogether, we identified 38,068 REs at the stage of seed development in B. napus (Fig. 1D, E). The majority of REs (accounting for 73.44%) located within 2 Kb upstream of the TSS were annotated as promoters, which can span several hundred to several thousand base pairs (bp) (Additional file 2: Fig. S3A). The remaining 26.56% were other REs, including potential enhancers, silencers, and insulators (Additional file 2: Fig. S3A). Intersection with the TE annotation revealed that a large fraction of these REs (6,715; 66.4%) overlap with TE sequences (Additional file 1: Table S5), implying that active or domesticated TEs may be the important sources of these REs. On average, the identified REs had a size of 419 bp (Additional file 2: Fig. S3B). Despite this modest average size, a subset of REs exceeded 1 kb in length (225; 0.91%), consistent with observations in maize [5]. In these large REs, significant enrichment of motifs bound by members of large TF families such as C2C2-Dof, C2H2, MYB, and bHLH was detected (Additional file 2: Fig. S4), which are known to regulate plant growth and development [39, 40]. Furthermore, we observed that genes physically closest to REs exhibited higher expression levels compared to those without nearby REs (P < 2.2e-16, two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Fig. 1F, Additional file 1: Tables S5-6), suggesting genes close to REs tend to exhibit higher expression activity. To determine whether this effect depends on the relative position of REs, we further compared the influence of REs located upstream versus downstream of the TSS. Our analysis revealed that REs positioned downstream of the TSS are associated with higher gene expression (Additional file 2: Fig. S5A), with the strongest positive regulatory effect observed within 1 Kb (Additional file 2: Fig. S5B). These findings support the notion of position-dependent regulation by certain REs, as recently reported by Voichek et al. [41]. Accordingly, these actively expressed genes are also generally associated with lower levels of DNA methylation (Fig. 1G, Additional file 2: Fig. S6).

Construction and characteristics analysis of regulatory network in B. napus

To construct the regulatory network of B. napus, we used recently published population data for B. napus at the seed development stage (20 DAF and 40 DAF) reported by Tang et al. for eQTL analysis [42]. Based on the results of eQTL analysis and the candidate REs mentioned before, we identified a total of 24,541 REs with eQTLs and 791,512 RE-gene associations for subsequent analysis (Additional file 1: Table S7). Then, we constructed a genome-wide RE regulatory network of B. napus at the seed development stage. Following the established approach for detecting hubs in regulatory networks described by Luscombe et al. [43], we selected hub REs and hub genes (see Methods). Using motif enrichment analysis, we found that the hub REs were significantly enriched in specific members of several large transcription factor families, including C2H2, C2C2-Dof, NAC, MADS and bZIP (Additional file 2: Fig. S7).

To investigate the characteristics of the network, we performed topological analysis using maximum clique centrality (MCC), edge percolated component (EPC), and closeness [44]. As depicted in Fig. 2A, the regulatory network consists of a total of 57,290 nodes (REs and their target genes) and 791,512 edges (RE-gene associations). The largest sub-network is presented in Fig. 2B. It is widely known that allopolyploid B. napus has two subgenomes (An and Cn) [28]. Comparative analysis of their regulatory networks revealed that the An subgenome regulatory network contains more nodes (35,898 vs. 31,425) and edges (420,624 vs. 370,888) than the Cn subgenome (Fig. 2A), even though the latter comprises a greater number of genes in the whole genome (52,562 vs. 44,359). This higher connectivity is further reflected in the degree distribution, as both the in-degree (kin, the number of regulatory relationships regulating a target gene) and out-degree (kout, the number of target genes regulated by each RE) of the An subgenome were significantly higher than those of the Cn subgenome (P < 2.2e-16, two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Additional file 2: Fig. S8A, B). Moreover, we observed significant higher MCC, EPC, and closeness in the An subgenome relative to the Cn subgenome (Fig. 2A, Additional file 2: Fig. S8C-E). These findings suggest that the regulatory network of the An subgenome may play a more dominant role in maintaining the genome-wide regulatory network, with more nodes interacting with one another and regulatory relationships being closer and denser. To verify this speculation, we randomly deleted nodes from the An and Cn subgenomes proportionally and recorded the remaining RE-gene associations separately to assess the integrity of the genome-wide network. As more nodes were deleted from the Cn subgenome, the integrity of the genome-wide regulatory network decreased from 94.50% to 51.75%. However, when the same proportion of nodes was deleted from the An subgenome, the integrity of the genome-wide regulatory network dropped dramatically from 85.27% to 2.61% (Fig. 2C). To further test whether this difference arises from network size or random degree distribution, we generated simulated networks with identical node degree distributions as controls (see Method). The results showed no significant difference in network integrity between subgenomes (Additional file 2: Fig. S9), suggesting An network dominance is not due to node count or structural variation, but stems from biological differences in topology. In addition, the An subgenome tends to have more hub genes (6,225; 64.53% vs. 3,442; 35.47%) and hub REs (5,335; 73.11% vs. 1,962; 26.89%) compared to the Cn subgenome (Additional file 2: Fig. S10). These results imply that An subgenome may serve as the structural backbone of the genome-wide network during seed development.

The three-dimensional (3D) chromatin architecture is strongly correlated with gene regulation and gene expression in the nucleus [45, 46]. To further explore the characteristics of regulatory networks at the three-dimensional genome level, we generated a total of 133 million Hi-C valid interactions of B. napus seed development stage (Additional file 1: Table S8). Generally, each chromosome can be subdivided into hierarchical chromatin domains known as A/B compartments: A compartments are enriched in highly transcribed euchromatic regions, while B compartments are located in heterochromatic gene desert regions [47]. Using the principal component analysis at a resolution of 50 kb, we identified A/B compartments (Additional file 1: Table S9) and observed significantly more RE-gene associations located within compartments compared to those across compartments, indicating that RE-gene associations tend to be situated within compartments (P < 2.2e-16, two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Fig. 2D). Moreover, we also observed higher gene expression for RE-gene associations located within compartments compared to those across compartments, suggesting a functional correlation between active compartments and enhanced transcriptional activity (P < 2.2e-16, two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Fig. 2E). A genomic region where the 3D chromatin architecture impacts RE-gene associations is presented in Fig. 2F, G, demonstrating that RE-gene associations tend to be located within compartments and are associated with more highly expressed target genes compared to those located across compartments. Overall, chromatin compartments might constrain intra-chromosomal RE–gene associations.

The regulatory modes of putative shared REs may exist in homoeologous gene pairs

In polyploid plants, homologous gene pairs often exhibit co-expression or biases in expression [17–19]. To investigate the underlying regulatory mechanisms of the homoeologous gene pairs, we identified 73,387 homoeologous gene pairs in B. napus. It was found that homoeologous gene pairs demonstrated a co-expression phenomenon compared to the random gene pairs screened from the whole genome (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, the majority of RE-gene associations (675,000; 85.28%) occurred in homoeologous regions when considering the distribution of RE-gene associations and homoeologous regions (Fig. 3B, Additional file 1: Table S10). A representative case illustrated that there were 4,230 RE-gene associations in cross-subgenomic homoeologous regions, whereas other regions exhibit only 1,678 RE-gene associations (Fig. 3C). Based on the regulatory relationships, where a homoeolog serves as the target genes, we found that a pair of homoeologs can be regulated by one or multiple identical REs (Fig. 3D), suggesting the existence of putative shared REs between homoeologous gene pairs. By comparing the co-expression levels of homoeologous gene pairs with putative shared REs and those without shared REs, we observed that the homoeologous gene pairs with putative shared REs exhibited higher co-expression levels (Fig. 3E), and higher co-expression levels tended to have more homoeologous gene pairs sharing REs (Fig. 3F). These findings suggest that a subset of homoeologous gene pairs may achieve higher co-expression levels through the sharing of REs. Our next step will be to investigate the underlying mechanisms behind this phenomenon.

The findings that spatial proximity may influence RE-gene associations prompted us to explore whether spatial proximity affects the regulation of homoeologous gene pairs. Subsequently, we found that the number of RE-gene associations in high chromatin interaction regions was 8.69-fold greater than that in random regions (Fig. 3G), and across-chromosomal homoeologous gene pairs were preferentially located in high chromatin interaction regions (Fig. 3H). These results indicate that spatial proximity may play a crucial role in both RE-gene associations and the co-expression of homoeologous gene pairs.

Spatial proximity contributes to the co-expression of across-chromosomal homoeologous gene pairs via putative shared REs

To further investigate the coordinated regulation of across-chromosomal homoeologous gene pairs, we categorized regulatory patterns based on the distances between REs and genes and their distributions along the chromosomes (Fig. 4A). Among these, the phenomenon that multiple genes sharing a RE based on their close linear distance or through the constraints of chromatin loops/TADs has been extensively studied [45, 48–55] (Fig. 4A), while the regulation involving across-chromosomal interactions remains largely unexplored. Based on these, we proposed three potential regulatory models for across-chromosomal homoeologous gene pairs sharing REs (Fig. 4A, B). Model 1 involves one gene with cis-eQTL association (within 1 Mb) and the other with trans-eQTL association (across-chromosomal regulation). Model 2 involves both genes with trans-eQTL associations, and one of them is involved in across-chromosomal regulation. Model 3 involves both genes with trans-eQTL associations, and both are subject to across-chromosomal regulation.

To test these hypotheses, we grouped chromatin interactions of homoeologous gene pairs based on whether they potentially share REs. The results showed that across-chromosomal homoeologous gene pairs with putative shared REs exhibited higher chromatin contact frequencies compared to those without shared REs (Fig. 4C; P < 1.0e-4, permutation test, and Fig. 4D; P < 2.2e-16; two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test), suggesting that spatial proximity may facilitate the sharing of REs. Next, we continued to investigate the impact of chromatin interaction strength on regulatory effects. It is found that the regions with higher chromatin contact frequencies tended to involve more homoeologous gene pairs with putative shared REs and more shared REs (Fig. 4E, F). Moreover, homoeologous gene pairs with higher chromatin interaction frequencies tend to share more REs (Fig. 4G; P = 5.7e-13; two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test). To further examine whether the 3D interaction drives the homoeologous gene pairs co-expression and exclude alignment bias caused by high sequence similarity, we divided the homoeologous gene pairs into four groups based on sequence similarity (95–100%, 90–95%, 85–90%, and < 85%) and compared the co-expression correlation between homoeologous gene pairs with shared REs and those without shared REs within each group. The results show that, even in the group with the highest sequence similarity, homoeologous gene pairs with shared REs exhibit significantly higher co-expression correlation (P < 2.2e-16, Wilcoxon rank-sum test; Additional file 2: Fig. S11). This suggests that sequence similarity alone is insufficient to drive homoeologous gene co-expression, with 3D spatial proximity playing an independent and important role. Based on these findings, we propose that the putative shared REs regulatory models presented in Fig. 4B may be a way to regulate the co-expression of across-chromosomal homoeologous gene pairs.

Overall, our analysis revealed that regulatory Model 1 had the highest proportion (48,040; 54.89%), followed by Model 2 (36,491; 41.70%), and Model 3 had the lowest proportion (2,981; 3.41%) (Fig. 4H, Additional file 1: Table S11). Examples of these putative shared REs are shown in Additional file 2: Fig. S12 and Fig. 4I. These examples demonstrate that spatial proximity may be one of the factors promoting co-expression of homologous gene pairs through shared REs. Totally, we identified 13,208 putative shared REs (34.70% of the total) for the 5,390 homoeologous gene pairs (7.34% of all homoeologous gene pairs) in B. napus (Additional file 2: Fig. S13). These findings present a novel and rational explanation for the co-expression of homoeologous gene pairs.

The asymmetric distribution of RE-gene associations shaped the asymmetric regulation of the subgenome

The analysis of the genome-wide RE regulatory network revealed that the An subgenome may serve as the primary backbone of the network (Fig. 2A-C), indicating the presence of asymmetry in the regulatory networks of the two subgenomes. Asymmetry of subgenomes is a common phenomenon in allopolyploid plants due to genetic incompatibility among subgenomes, as evidenced by differences in gene content, gene expression bias, and epigenetic modifications [14, 27]. To further explore the regulatory characteristics of subgenomes in B. napus, we compared the number of REs in the An and Cn subgenomes, observing a slightly higher number in the An subgenome (12,526 vs. 12,015) (Fig. 5A). Moreover, the regulatory effects of the An subgenome REs on target genes (REAn → G) were significantly greater than those of Cn subgenome REs on target genes (RECn → G) (420,624 vs. 370,888) (Fig. 5B), and the regulatory intensity of REAn was higher than that of RECn (P < 2.2e-16, two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Fig. 5C). These findings suggested that compared to the Cn subgenome, the An subgenome possess more REs, more regulatory relationships, and stronger regulatory intensity. The asymmetric distribution of RE-gene associations could play a crucial role in shaping the asymmetric regulation of the subgenome.

Furthermore, we analyzed the inter-subgenomic regulation of B. napus and found that there was more REs involved in inter-subgenomic regulation on the An subgenome compared to the Cn subgenome (10,857; 86.68% vs. 8,063; 67.11%) (Fig. 5D). Additionally, the target gene expression level of REAn → GCn was significantly higher than that of RECn → GAn (Fig. 5E), indicating that REAn may have greater importance in inter-subgenomic regulation. Based on our observation that active compartments are closely associated with gene transcriptional activity (Fig. 2D-G), we compared the distribution of chromatin compartments and RE-gene associations in the two subgenomes. Remarkably, larger 3D chromatin compartments were more enriched in the An subgenome compared to the Cn subgenome (Additional file 2: Fig. S14A), even though the An subgenome occupied a smaller chromosome size than the Cn subgenome [28], and the number of RE-gene associations located within compartments in the An subgenome was greater than that in the Cn subgenome (Additional file 2: Fig. S14B). In addition, we evaluated the regulation distance of RE-gene associations across compartments and found that it was greater in the An subgenome compared to the Cn subgenome (Additional file 2: Fig. S14C).

Moreover, we are intrigued by the possibility that the aforementioned phenomenon of asymmetric regulation extends to homoeologous regions between subgenomes. As anticipated, our investigation revealed several aspects of subgenome asymmetry in homologous regions, including RE content, RE regulatory intensity, expression of target genes, and average compartment size (Additional file 2: Fig. S15). As shown by the representative example in Fig. 5F, the asymmetric distribution of RE-gene associations shaped the asymmetric regulation of subgenomes (in homoeologous regions of A05:6.4–12.5 Mb and C06:20–25.2 Mb, REAn → G was approximately 2.5-fold higher than RECn → G). In summary, our findings suggest that the asymmetric distribution of RE-gene associations potentially contributes to the asymmetric regulation of B. napus subgenomes, with REAn playing a crucial role.

Discussion

Owing to the dearth of well-established experimental verification of RE-gene interactions in both humans and plants, accurately assigning REs to their respective target genes has proven to be challenging [56, 57]. Based on the 791,512 RE-gene associations, we constructed a comprehensive genome-wide RE regulatory network specific to B. napus at the seed development stage, providing a valuable resource for gaining insights into the intricate regulatory mechanisms in polyploid plants.

Spatial proximity may promote the sharing of REs within across-chromosomal homoeologous gene pairs

Previous research has shown that spatial proximity is necessary for enhancers to activate transcription [58]. In the case of polyploid wheat and cotton, studies have demonstrated that homoeologous regions can come into spatial proximity through inter-chromosomal interactions [20, 59]. However, the exact regulatory mechanism behind this phenomenon remains unclear. This study discovered that spatial proximity may promote the sharing of REs between across-chromosomal homoeologous gene pairs. Specifically, stronger chromatin interactions, co-expression and similar chromatin accessibility were observed in homoeologous gene pairs with putative shared REs. These findings further explain one of the possible mechanisms for the co-expression of across-chromosomal homoeologous gene pairs and address a question raised in a previous cotton study regarding the regulation of genes by homoeologous sequences in different subgenomes [29]. A similar model of shared REs has been reported in Drosophila, where promoter-proximal tethering elements facilitate the activation of shared enhancers, leading to coordinated expression of distant paralogues [55]. The general results presented across species further support the reliability of our models. Moreover, the shared RE regulatory model presents a potential mechanism and strategies for long-range genome regulation under limited transcriptional resources.

The identification of shared REs in homoeologous gene pairs may be confounded by the inherent complexity of polyploid genomes, including multi-copy sequences, high sequence homology, and homoeologous exchanges (HEs) [60, 61]. To systematically evaluate these potential biases, we conducted comprehensive analyses. By integrating the HEs identified by Zhang et al. [62], we found that over 95% of homoeologous gene pairs, and specifically those with shared REs, are not located in HE regions (Additional file 2: Fig. S16, Additional file 1: Table S11), indicating limited interference of HEs on co-expression patterns. Furthermore, using the approach of Kuo et al. [63], we simulated RNA-seq data based on 15 published B. napus genomes and observed a low homoeologous mismapping rate (0.38%–3.49%) (Additional file 2: Fig. S17). Together, these analyses indicate that while such confounding factors exist, their impact on the core findings of this study is negligible. Nevertheless, given the challenges in fully resolving homoeologous alignments, genes located in regions with potential misalignment should be interpreted with caution in subsequent functional studies. Moreover, the presence of the same or similar REs with identical transcription factor binding sites (TFBS) in homoeologous gene pairs may also contribute to their co-expression. Our further analysis revealed that only a small proportion (319 pairs, accounting for 5.92%) of homoeologous gene pairs with putative shared REs had the same TFs regulating both genes (Additional file 1: Table S12). This suggests that having both genes regulated by the same or similar REs has limited contribution, and the majority of regulatory patterns involving putative shared REs are more likely attributed to spatial proximity (5,071 pairs, accounting for 94.08%). Additionally, we investigated the impact of spatial proximity on homoeologous gene pairs that are neither TFs nor regulated by TFs interacting with their respective REs (Additional file 1: Table S13). The results still indicated that spatial proximity may promote the sharing of REs in homoeologs (Additional file 2: Fig. S18). However, due to technical limitations and the complexity of the system, further validation is required to fully understand the regulatory models of shared REs in plants. Gene expression regulation is a complex, multi-layered, and multi-dimensional process, and shared REs may represent one potential regulatory mode within the global regulatory network. In addition to transcriptional-level regulation, post-transcriptional regulation (including miRNA and lncRNA activity, RNA modifications) [64, 65], epigenetic mechanisms (such as DNA methylation and histone modifications) [6, 66, 67], as well as protein stability and protein–protein interactions [68], are also likely to contribute substantially to the coordinated expression of homoeologous genes.

The 3D chromatin architecture plays a critical role in coordinating gene expression [69]. Nevertheless, the resolution of Hi-C is constrained by technical and genomic factors—particularly in polyploids, where high sequence redundancy and subgenome homology in rapeseed can obscure fine-scale interaction signals. Currently, chromatin loops have been widely identified in diploid plants such as Arabidopsis [70], rice [71], maize [11], but there are still few reports in polyploid plants (e.g., wheat, cotton) [67, 72]. Integrating high-resolution chromatin conformation technologies (such as HiChIP, single-cell Hi-C and other 3C-derived techniques [71, 73, 74]) with spatiotemporally specific multi-omics data to improve the sensitivity of regulatory interaction detection will be a crucial direction for dissecting the gene regulatory networks of polyploid plants. This advancement will enable to determine the specific regulatory mechanisms between shared REs and target genes, whether they involve proteins with regulatory functions, transcription factors, or are solely based on spatial proximity.

It is worth noting that recent studies have also shown that transcription can affect the 3D chromatin architecture [75]. Apart from 3D structural proteins such as CTCF, cohesin, and RNA polymerases [76–79], few reports exist on how gene expression influences large-scale 3D structural changes. Thus, it is possible that spatial proximity promoting the sharing of REs between across-chromosomal homoeologous gene pairs is a more general phenomenon, but there may be special cases that require further investigation to understand their specific distribution, effects, and mechanisms.

The asymmetric distribution of RE–gene associations contributes to asymmetric regulation of subgenomes

The presence of structurally and functionally asymmetric subgenomes in polyploid plants is crucial for their evolution [14, 27]. B. napus, which originated from a natural hybridization of Brassica rapa (AA, 2n = 20) and Brassica oleracea (CC, 2n = 18), displays an imbalance between the An and Cn subgenomes [28]. Our study suggested that the An subgenome serves as the structural backbone of the genome-wide regulatory network during seed development (Fig. 2A-C). Similarly, from an epigenetic perspective, the An subgenome has been identified as the dominant subgenome in the young leaf, flower bud, silique, and root of B. napus, exhibiting higher intensities of active marks and lower DNA methylation compared to the Cn subgenome [26]. Notably, the contribution of subgenomes may be controversial at different analytical levels and is influenced by a variety of factors. Studies have found that the subgenome that dominates at epigenetic levels does not necessarily exhibit the same dominance at the gene expression level [26, 80]. Tissue type and developmental stage represent additional key variables [81], with a subgenome showing dominant activity in one tissue potentially exhibiting reduced or even reversed contributions in another. Comprehensively considering factors such as tissue type, maternal donor, seed subregions, developmental stage, and environmental conditions, and systematically integrating multi-level evidence will help to better evaluate the contribution of subgenomes.

Furthermore, emerging inter-subgenome transcriptional regulation relative to the diploid ancestor increases the complexity of transcriptional regulation in the nucleus. However, the landscape of inter-subgenome transcriptional regulation remains unclear in B. napus. In this study, asymmetric regulation of subgenomes was observed in terms of the number of REs, regulatory relationships, and regulatory intensity. The REAn has a larger number (12,526 vs.12,015), more regulatory relationships (420,624 REAn → G vs. 370,888 RECn → G), and higher regulatory intensity (P < 2.2e-16, Wilcoxon rank sum test) compared to RECn. Additionally, there were more REs involved in the inter-subgenomic regulation on An compared to Cn (10,857; 86.68% vs. 8,063; 67.11%); and the target gene expression level of REAn → GCn was significantly higher than that of RECn → GAn (P < 2.2e-16, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). These findings suggest the asymmetric distribution of RE-gene associations shapes the asymmetric regulation of subgenomes, with REAn playing a crucial role. Similar asymmetric regulation of subgenomes has been observed in cotton, with the D-subgenome having a regulatory effect on the A-subgenome [29]. Understanding the asymmetry and complexity of subgenomic regulation enhances our knowledge of coordinated regulation among different subgenomes. It also contributes to genome evolution and adaptability to different environments.

While we have focused on the asymmetric distribution of RE–gene associations as a factor in the asymmetric regulation of subgenomes, there are other factors to consider. Previous studies have indicated that transposable element density, homoeologous exchange, and epigenomic modification bias are associated with subgenomic asymmetry [14, 26, 82]. Furthermore, it is worth considering the effects of these REs on other REs and their impact on phenotype in B. napus. Notably, our study focuses on seed development, a key phase for rapeseed yield and oil quality. Regulatory dynamics in other tissues or under stress were not examined, so context-specific REs may be missed. Future integration of multi-omics data across tissues, stages, and conditions will be essential to build a more comprehensive and spatiotemporally resolved regulatory network for polyploid plants.

Conclusions

In summary, our study, combined with the landscape of RE–gene associations and 3D chromatin architecture, provides new insights into the across-chromosomal regulation. We also shed light on the imbalance between subgenomes, dynamic gene expression regulation, and the co-expression of homoeologous genes.

Methods

Plant materials

Four representative semi-winter B. napus accessions were selected from a natural population of 505 accessions [42], including 9Bao22 from Hubei (30° 58′ N and 114° 30′ E), B262 from Henan (34° 75′ N and 113° 62′ E), NY10 from Jiangsu (32° 39′ N and 119° 42′ E) and SWU99 from Chongqing (29° 56′ N and 106° 55′ E) (Additional file 1: Table S1). Seeds harvested 20 days after flowering (DAF) were used for ATAC-seq, BS-seq, Hi-C and RNA-seq experiments. All sequencing reads were mapped to the high-quality reference genome ZS11 [31].

ATAC-seq analysis

Frozen samples were obtained following the ATAC-seq protocol described previously [83, 84]. The Nextera DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina) was used to perform the Tn5 transposition reaction according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The transposed DNA fragments were purified immediately using a MinElute PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen). The library was then amplified by PCR, and ATAC-seq was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 with 50-bp paired-end reads, as previously described [83, 84]. Two biological replicates were generated for each experiment.

For ATAC-seq raw reads, low-quality reads and adapters were filtered out using Trim Galore (v0.6.7) (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) with default parameters (“-q 20 –phred33 –stringency 3 –length 20”). The clean reads were aligned to the ZS11 reference genome using bowtie2 (v2.3.2) [85] with parameters (“-q -p 10 –very-sensitive -X 1000 –fr”). Multi-mapped reads were removed using sambamba (v0.7.1) [86], and then PCR duplicated reads were removed using the default parameters of Picard (v2.16.0) (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). To assess the reproducibility of the replicates, normalized reads coverage of each sample was computed for principal component analysis using the default function of multiBigwigSummary in DeepTools (v3.5.1) [87]. Peaks were identified using MACS2 (v2.1.2) [88] with parameters (“–nomodel -q 0.05 –extsize 200 –shift −100 -g 1.01e9 –keep-dup all -B –call-summit”).

Based on the peaks detected in all accessions, we first retained the peaks with regional intersections in biological replicates and then preserved all the incorporated regions in the four accessions. The OCRs were annotated using the ChIPseeker package in R [89]. Annotation of OCRs was performed based on their nearest genes in genomic features. OCRs located within 2 Kb upstream of the transcriptional start site (TSS) were annotated as promoters, which can span several hundred to several thousand base pairs (bp). OCRs located within gene bodies were annotated as exons or introns, while OCRs within 2 Kb downstream of the transcriptional termination site (TES) were annotated as downstream regions. OCRs located more than 2 Kb away from the TSS or TES were annotated as distal regions.

BS-seq analysis

DNA for BS-seq sequencing was prepared using standard CTAB methods. The library was constructed using 1 μg of genomic DNA, following a previously published protocol [90]. Library quality was assessed using Qubit and gel electrophoresis. Bisulfite-treated DNA libraries were sequenced on the HiSeq X Ten sequencing platform. Three biological replicates were performed for each accession.

To preprocess the BS-seq raw reads, sequencing adapters were first removed using Trimmomatic (v0.35) software with default parameters [91]. The resulting clean reads were then mapped to the reference genome using Bismark (v0.22.1) with default parameters [92]. The conversion rate of bisulfite treatment was estimated by analyzing an unmethylated lambda DNA present in the BS-seq library. The multiple mapping reads and PCR-duplication reads were filtered to obtain a unique mapping BAM file using Bismark affiliated tools ‘deduplicate_bismark’ with default parameters. The program ‘bismark_methylation_extractor’ was used to extract the potential methylation states of all cytosine positions in the reads. This program was run with the following parameters: ‘–multicore 36 –gzip –bedGraph –buffer_size 10G –CX –cytosine_report’. Cytosines in the CpG, CHG, and CHH contexts were retained. Methylation levels were assessed by combining all biological replicates and methylation sites between samples, and counting them using a 100 bp window. Unmethylated regions were identified using the method proposed by Crisp et al. [10], the specific identification criteria are as follows: “CG sites > = 2 & CHG sites > = 2 & CHG < 10% & CG < 10% & CHH < 10%” and “CG sites > = 2 & CHG sites < 2 & CG < 10% & CHH < 10%”.

RNA-seq analysis

The RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit from Illumina, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform with two biological replicates. To ensure high-quality data, sequencing reads with low quality scores were trimmed, and the remaining Illumina adaptor sequences were removed using Trimmomatic (v0.35) [91] with default parameters. The resulting clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.0) [93] with default parameters. Transcript assembly was performed using Stringtie (v2.1.4) [94] with default parameters, and the transcripts per kilobase million reads (TPM) was calculated for each gene.

eQTL analysis

To conduct eQTL analysis, we obtained the genotype data and RNA-seq data of a natural population of B. napus from a published study by Tang et al. [42]. The reads were aligned to the reference genome using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17) with default parameters [95]. To ensure high-quality data, reads with a mapping quality value < 20 (Q20) were removed using SAMtools (v1.9) [96]. SNPs were identified using Sentieon (v201911.01) [97], and high-quality SNPs were selected using Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) (v4.1.5) [98] with the parameters “QUAL < 30.0 || MQ < 50.0 || QD < 2”. SNPs with minor allele frequencies (MAF) < 0.01 and missing > 0.1, as detected by VCFtools (v0.1.17) [99], were discarded.

The remaining SNPs were imputed and phased using Beagle (v5.1) [100], filtering out variants with MAF < 0.05. Genome-wide association study (GWAS) was performed using EMMAX [101], and the cutoff for determining significant associations was set as -log10(1/N), where N represents the total number of SNPs. Finally, the REs overlapped with eQTL were used for subsequent analysis.

Analysis of regulatory network

The topological analysis of the genome-wide regulatory network was conducted using the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape (v3.8.2) [44]. Maximum clique centrality (MCC), edge percolated component (EPC), and closeness were used to characterize the network. Nodes with higher MCC values are generally considered to hold a more important and centralized position in the network [44]. A larger EPC value indicates a larger connected subgraph composed of nodes connected by preserved edges, indicating more interactions among nodes and the formation of a larger set of densely connected networks. Nodes with higher closeness have a smaller average distance to all other nodes, signifying closer and denser connections to other nodes [44]. Hubs in regulatory networks are highly interconnected nodes or those located at central positions, representing potential key regulators or functional core modules [102, 103]. To screen for candidate hub REs and hub genes, we ranked REs by their kout (the number of target genes regulated by each RE) and genes by their kin (the number of regulatory relationships regulating a target gene), following the established approach for detecting hubs in regulatory networks described by Luscombe et al. [43]. The top 30% of ranked REs and genes were selected as candidate hub REs and hub genes, respectively.

To further explore the characteristics of the regulatory network, we systematically deleted nodes (including REs and genes) from both the An and Cn subgenomes in proportion, measuring the ratio of the remaining RE-gene associations to the total number of associations separately to assess the integrity of the genome-wide network. Additionally, following the method described in previously published studies [104, 105], we generated random regulatory networks with the same degree distribution as the real network using a two-step approach that first establishes basal connectivity to prevent isolated nodes and then precisely matches the original network's edge count through random edge assignment.

Hi-C analysis

The Hi-C library was constructed following the manufacturer’s recommendations. DNA was digested with the restriction enzyme DpnII at the restriction site ‘^GATC’ and the ends of the resulting restriction fragments were labeled using biotinylated nucleotides. After crosslinking, ligated DNA was purified, and the ligation junctions were pulled down for Illumina paired-end sequencing. High-throughput sequencing data were generated using the HiSeq X Ten sequencing platform with 2 × 150-bp reads.

The Hi-C sequencing data underwent quality control and processing using Juicer software (v1.5.6) [106]. The BWA-MEM algorithm (v0.7.17) [95] was employed to map the reads to the reference genome. Reads mapped to the same restriction enzyme fragment were removed, while those with a mapping quality score (MAPQ) above 30 were retained. The parameter used in the pipeline was “juicer.sh -d topDir -s DpnII -p chrom.size -y restriction_site.file -z reference.fa -D Juicer_scripts.dir -t 30”. Normalized contact frequency matrices at 500 Kb resolutions were generated using the ICE method in Python3 (v3.9.7) to robustly remove multiplex bias.

Compartment assignment was determined based on the eigenvalue of the principal component at a 50-Kb resolution using an eigenvector program in Juicer [106]. A/B compartments were identified based on gene density and the sign of the first eigenvector (PC1): A compartments had higher gene densities, while B compartments had lower gene densities. Additionally, we merged consecutive compartments with the same chromatin state to investigate the relationships between RE-gene associations and 3D chromatin architecture.

The statistical significance of the strength of homoeologous interactions was assessed by calculating the false discovery rate (FDR) based on the approach described by Xie et al. [107], a method widely used in chromatin interaction analyses [20, 108, 109]. To minimize the influence of linear genomic proximity, inter-chromosomal interactions were included in the analysis. A Poisson-based null model was employed under the assumption that homoeologous interactions occur randomly within the nuclear space. For each observed interaction frequency, a P-value was computed by comparing it against the expected frequency under this random model, and the corresponding FDR was then derived. Significant inter-chromosomal interactions were filtered with the FDR cutoff of 0.05 (Additional file 1: Table S11).

Motif enrichment and identification of TFs

To identify potential binding transcription factors, we utilized the hub RE sequences for motif enrichment analysis using MEME (v5.0.5) [110]. The analysis was performed with the following parameters: “-dna -nostatus -mod anr -nmotifs 15 -revcomp -minw 6 -maxw 10 -objfun classic -markov_order 0”. Subsequently, the corresponding motifs were identified using the Tomtom module of MEME (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/tomtom). The ARABIDOSIS database was then used to search for transcription factors associated with motifs, and a GOMo search was performed on the browser using the Arabidopsis promoter database (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/gomo) to predict the function of motifs.

We collected and integrated the TF genes of B. napus from three different databases: BnIR (https://yanglab.hzau.edu.cn/BnIR), iTAK (http://itak.feilab.net/cgi-bin/itak/index.cgi), and plantTFDB (http://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/). The TF binding motifs of REs were predicted by using the ‘fimo’ module in MEME (v5.0.5).

Identifying homoeologous gene pairs and investigating their co-expression and shared regulatory elements

Homoeologous gene pairs were defined using Jcvi (v1.0.5) (https://github.com/tanghaibao/jcvi) with the parameters “–nochpf –dbtype prot –no_strip_names”. In total, 73,387 homoeologous gene pairs were detected. Additionally, we identified 2,435 homoeologous regions (also known as synteny blocks) using the command “python -m jcvi.compara.synteny screen –minspan = 1”.

To investigate the co-expression correlations of homoeologous gene pairs, the expression profiles from 91 different tissue samples in BnTIR [111] (http://yanglab.hzau.edu.cn/BnTIR) were introduced, each with three biological replicates. For each tissue, gene expression levels were determined as the geometric mean of TPM values across replicates. Using these levels as input, Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) for all homoeologous pairs were calculated in R and served as a measure of their co-expression.

Based on the constructed regulatory network, which comprises 24,541 REs with eQTLs and 791,512 RE–gene associations, if both genes in a homoeologous gene pair exhibit significant eQTL-supported regulatory associations (P < 1e-7) with one or more identical REs, then those REs are identified as shared REs for that homoeologous gene pair.

Visualization tools

ATAC-seq and RNA-seq tracks were visualized by IGVtools (v2.8.13) [112] and pyGenomeTracks (v3.5) [113]. The network of RE-gene associations was constructed using Cytoscape (v3.8.2) with the ‘yFiles Organic Layout’ algorithm [114]. Circos plots were generated using Circos (v0.69–8) [115]. Heatmaps and mataplots of chromatin accessibility signals were presented using Deeptools (v3.5.1) [87].
