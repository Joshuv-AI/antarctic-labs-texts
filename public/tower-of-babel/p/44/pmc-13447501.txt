Stage-Dependent Physiotherapy for Recovery From Supplementary Motor Area Syndrome: A Case Report
Authors: Takeshi Sasaki, Yui Sato, Masato Kobayashi, Yuichi Sasaki, Kazuhiro Sugawara, Yusuke Kimura, Nobuhiro Mikuni, Yasunori Umemoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSupplementary motor area (SMA) syndrome is a postoperative complication following SMA tumor resection, characterized by delayed movement initiation and impaired bimanual coordination. Although spontaneous recovery is common, rehabilitation strategies tailored to recovery stages remain unclear. We report a 38-year-old woman who presented with a right SMA tumor (isocitrate dehydrogenase-mutant oligodendroglioma). Preoperatively, she was independent in activities of daily living (ADLs) with no motor, sensory, or cognitive impairments. She underwent awake craniotomy with intraoperative monitoring confirming preservation of the primary motor cortex and corticospinal tract. On postoperative day (POD) 1, she developed left hemiparesis, delayed movement initiation, and impaired bimanual coordination, consistent with SMA syndrome. The patient received inpatient physical and occupational therapy (2 hours/day, 5 days/week) for 3 weeks. Balance and gait training were combined with progressively introduced bilateral, alternating, and rhythmic tasks emphasizing motor timing, sequencing, and coordination based on functional recovery stages. On POD 1, the Brunnstrom Recovery Stage (BRS) was III for the upper limb and II for the lower limb. By POD 14, BRS improved to VI and V, and she achieved independence in ward-based ADLs. Gait speed increased from 1.1 to 1.2 m/s, hand function improved from 91 to 98, and bimanual Purdue Pegboard Test performance improved from 7.3 to 10.3 pairs. By POD 22, gait speed reached 1.4 m/s and the Berg Balance Scale score was 56. This case demonstrates the stage-dependent evolution of SMA syndrome and suggests the potential utility of rehabilitation approaches emphasizing coordinated and sequential motor tasks aligned with functional recovery.Keywords: Supplementary Motor Area, Physical Therapy, Neuronal Plasticity, Rehabilitation

INTRODUCTION

The supplementary motor area (SMA), located on the medial frontal lobe, is a higher-order motor region involved in movement preparation, initiation, and inhibition, as well as motor sequencing, bimanual coordination, and attentional control [1]. The SMA interacts closely with the primary motor cortex (M1) and premotor cortex (PMA) and, through extensive connections with the cingulate gyrus, prefrontal cortex, basal ganglia, and thalamus, contributes to the temporal organization of voluntary movement [2,3]. Following surgical resection of tumors involving the SMA, patients may develop SMA syndrome, which is characterized by transient paresis, reduced spontaneous movement, and speech disturbance [4]. A defining feature of this syndrome is impaired voluntary motor control despite anatomical preservation of M1, reflecting a temporary disruption of SMA-centered functional motor networks. Consequently, deficits in bimanual coordination and complex motor tasks are frequently more pronounced than simple muscle weakness [5]. SMA syndrome is generally reversible, with gradual recovery over time. Nakajima et al. [6] reported that even severe postoperative motor deficits generally begin to improve within approximately 1 week after SMA resection, although complete recovery may require 2–9 weeks depending on the case. Duffau [7] conceptualized SMA syndrome as a reversible network disorder in which functional reorganization mediated by neuroplasticity supports recovery. Taken together, SMA syndrome can be understood as a time-dependent network disorder. Accordingly, rehabilitation should extend beyond muscle strengthening or simple repetitive exercises and instead incorporate early and progressive training aimed at restoring voluntary motor control, including movement timing, sequencing, and coordination. In this case report, we describe a patient who underwent awake craniotomy for a right SMA tumor and subsequently developed postoperative motor and gait impairments. By implementing an intensive rehabilitation program that progressively increased task complexity and motor coordination according to functional recovery, we explore the potential clinical value of task-specific, higher-order motor training in facilitating recovery from SMA syndrome.

CASE DESCRIPTION

The patient was a 38-year-old right-handed woman, fully independent in all activities of daily living (ADLs) prior to symptom onset. She lived with her husband in an apartment and was employed in a clerical position. Her medical history was unremarkable. Three months before surgery, she experienced a generalized tonic–clonic seizure while outdoors and was transported to the emergency department. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a fluid-attenuated inversion recovery-hyperintense lesion involving the PMA and SMA, raising suspicion of an epileptogenic tumor. Antiepileptic treatment with lacosamide (Vimpat) was initiated. Seven days before surgery, she was transferred to our hospital for a planned awake craniotomy. Written and oral informed consent for publication of this case report was obtained from the patient. Because this report describes a single case and did not constitute interventional or observational research according to the policy of our institution, institutional review board approval was not required.

Preoperative MRI revealed a lesion extending from the right superior frontal gyrus to the SMA, with possible involvement of the precentral gyrus (Fig. 1A). Although the patient reported no subjective motor or sensory symptoms and exhibited no objective neurological deficits, the tumor location raised concern for potential involvement of the motor system. Handgrip strength measured 23.8 kg on the right and 18.3 kg on the left. Manual muscle testing (MMT) revealed grade 5 strength in the left upper and lower limbs. Comfortable gait speed was 1.40 m/s, and the patient was independent in all ADLs. Cognitive function was assessed using the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale–Fourth Edition. The Full-Scale IQ was 111, with all index scores within the normal range (Verbal Comprehension Index, 113; Perceptual Reasoning Index, 112; Working Memory Index, 103; and Processing Speed Index, 105). Performance on Set A of the Standard Verbal Paired-Associate Learning Test was also within normal limits, indicating no apparent cognitive impairment.

An awake tumor resection involving the SMA was performed. A bifrontal craniotomy was conducted, and the cortical surface appeared normal following dural opening. The central sulcus was identified using somatosensory evoked potentials, and M1 was confirmed by motor evoked potential (MEP) recordings. Upper-limb MEPs were monitored at 1-min intervals during tumor removal, with no changes in amplitude observed. After resection of the anterior portion of the tumor, no fluorescence was detected with 5-aminolevulinic acid, and intraoperative frozen-section analysis revealed no abnormalities. Because the posterior portion of the tumor was adjacent to the precentral sulcus, this region was resected under awake conditions. During tumor removal, impaired alternating movements of the left elbow and ankle, along with reduced strength in the left lower limb, were noted, suggesting involvement of the PMA. Consequently, resection was limited to the lesion extending up to the precentral sulcus (Fig. 1B). The final pathological diagnosis was oligodendroglioma, isocitrate dehydrogenase-mutant, World Health Organization grade 2.

On POD 1, the patient exhibited paresis of the left upper and lower limbs, with Brunnstrom Recovery Stage (BRS) of III in the upper limb and II in the lower limb. Immediately after surgery, MMT of the affected limbs was grade 1. Deep tendon reflexes were hyperactive on the left; however, no pathological reflexes were observed. She required assistance for sitting and rolling in bed. Sensory and language functions remained intact. Postoperative MRI confirmed preservation of M1 and the corticospinal tract; therefore, the paresis was attributed to SMA syndrome. Early physical therapy focused on assisted sitting and standing, with gradual progression to assisted and subsequently supervised gait training as function improved. Bed mobility, sit-to-stand practice, and short-distance gait training with assistance were introduced according to motor recovery.

By POD 5, the patient was alert and fully oriented and independent in basic movements, although rightward trunk deviation was noted. BRS improved to stage IV, and MMT improved to grade 4. However, she demonstrated a marked delay in initiating voluntary movements and reported that “the left side is slower.” She could walk independently using a walker, and rehabilitation focused on standing balance and lower-limb strengthening. Step practice and gait training with postural control were gradually introduced.

On POD 8, basic mobility required only supervision. The Berg Balance Scale (BBS) score was 53, and gait speed was 1.1 m/s. Upper-limb function, assessed using the Simple Test for Evaluating Hand Function (STEF), was 91. Purdue Pegboard Test (PPT) scores were 15.3 pegs for the right hand, 11.3 pegs for the left hand, 7.3 pairs for bimanual performance, and 26.3 pieces for the assembly task, indicating reduced left-hand dexterity and impaired bimanual coordination. Accordingly, ADLs training incorporating bimanual tasks was added. Bimanual activities such as object manipulation and peg tasks were gradually introduced according to functional recovery.

By POD 14, the patient was independent in all ward-based ADLs. BRS improved to VI in the upper limb and V in the lower limb, with MMT scores ranging from 4 to 5. Repetitive PPT and coin-manipulation exercises were performed both unilaterally and bilaterally, resulting in improvement of the STEF score to 98 and bimanual PPT to 10.3 pairs. Gait speed increased to 1.2 m/s, although delayed movement initiation and clumsiness of alternating lower-limb movements persisted. Stair climbing, jogging, and ladder-walking exercises were subsequently introduced. These tasks were used to address residual clumsiness during alternating lower-limb movements.

By POD 22, gait speed reached 1.4 m/s, and the BBS score improved to 56, enabling jogging. The patient was discharged home, resumed clerical work remotely, and was accompanied by family members during community outings. These changes reflected not only improvement in motor performance measures, but also recovery of safe mobility, greater independence in ADLs, and early social reintegration. Improvement in bimanual PPT performance was also associated with smoother use of both hands during ADLs and household activities, including object handling and dressing tasks. As shown in Table 1, gait speed, hand dexterity, and bimanual coordination gradually improved during hospitalization. Throughout hospitalization, she received physical and occupational therapy for 2 hours per day, 5 days per week. No specific contraindications to rehabilitation were identified, although impaired balance increased the risk of falls and required close supervision.

DISCUSSION

This case describes a patient who developed SMA syndrome following awake surgery for a right SMA tumor and subsequently received an intensive rehabilitation program emphasizing coordination and increasing task complexity. The rehabilitation program was implemented based on clinical indicators, including motor recovery severity assessed using the BRS, independence in basic movements and gait, and the presence of coordination deficits. Consequently, she achieved substantial functional recovery within 3 weeks, regained independence in ADLs, and was discharged home. Previous studies have shown that inpatients who receive intensive rehabilitation—such as physical and occupational therapy for 2 hours per day, 5 days per week—demonstrate significant improvements in motor and cognitive function, as well as in ADLs [8]. However, the mean duration of SMA syndrome has been reported to be approximately 45 days, with persistent deficits in 20.3% of patients, nearly half of which involve motor impairments [4]. Therefore, the recovery course in the present case can be considered relatively rapid.

In this patient, despite intraoperative confirmation of preserved M1, postoperative findings included left-sided paresis, delayed movement initiation, and impaired bimanual coordination. These deficits suggest that the symptoms were not attributable to structural damage of the final motor output pathway but rather to a transient functional disruption within motor networks responsible for voluntary movement initiation and sequence construction. Electrophysiological studies in primates have demonstrated that the SMA prospectively organizes multiple elements of a motor sequence and plays a critical role in the preparatory phase of voluntary movement [3]. Accordingly, the delayed movement initiation and coordination deficits observed after SMA injury are better conceptualized as impairments in motor planning and sequencing rather than as isolated muscle weakness. Although motor function often recovers within weeks, this improvement is believed to reflect plastic reorganization of motor networks [5], consistent with the characterization of SMA syndrome as a reversible network disorder [7]. Similarly, Rehme et al. [9] demonstrated transient reductions in functional connectivity within motor networks followed by subsequent reorganization in patients with stroke.

In the present case, early improvement in motor paresis was followed by the emergence of more characteristic features of SMA syndrome, including delayed movement initiation and impaired bimanual coordination. This temporal evolution is consistent with previous clinical descriptions of SMA syndrome [5,6]. Nakajima et al. [6] reported that both the severity of motor deficits at POD 7 and the extent of white matter damage involving the cingulate region were key determinants of recovery duration, underscoring the importance of white matter network reorganization. Furthermore, Kimura et al. [10] demonstrated substantial interindividual variability in the strength and responsiveness of networks connecting M1 with the paracentral lobule and SMA, suggesting that such network differences underlie variability in recovery trajectories. These findings support the interpretation that the clinical course observed in this case was shaped by neuroplastic reorganization within distributed motor networks.

Although SMA syndrome often shows spontaneous recovery [4], which may also have contributed to the clinical course in this patient, the rehabilitation strategy itself may also have contributed to functional improvement. Beyond simple repetitive exercises, the progressive incorporation of balance and gait training, as well as bilateral, alternating, and rhythmic tasks—such as the PPT and ladder walking—was associated with marked improvements in dexterity and coordination. Lesions involving the SMA are known to impair alternating movements and disrupt muscle activation patterns [11], whereas recovery depends on reorganization within and between hemispheric motor networks, particularly interhemispheric connections [12]. Motor learning has also been shown to enhance functional connectivity within the sensorimotor network, including the SMA [13]. The rehabilitation program was implemented based on objective clinical indicators, including motor recovery severity assessed using the BRS, independence in basic movements and gait, and the presence of coordination deficits, with a shift in emphasis from basic mobility training to exercises targeting movement initiation, coordination, and bilateral motor tasks. Taken together, these findings indicate that the stepwise rehabilitation program implemented in this case may have facilitated improvements in bimanual coordination and motor sequencing through plastic reorganization of frontal motor networks. Accordingly, when delayed movement initiation and coordination deficits persist after SMA injury, rehabilitation should extend beyond muscle strengthening to include complex, task-specific training that promotes reconstruction of motor sequences.

As this is a single case report, the findings and the specific recovery trajectory observed may not be generalizable to all patients with SMA syndrome. Moreover, because SMA syndrome often shows spontaneous recovery, a causal relationship between the present rehabilitation program and functional improvement cannot be definitively determined. Further studies involving larger numbers of patients are needed to better clarify the clinical significance of stage-dependent rehabilitation in SMA syndrome.
