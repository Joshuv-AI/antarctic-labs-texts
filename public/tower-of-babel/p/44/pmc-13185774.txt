The gut-liver/bile axis: Gut microbiota and bacterial extracellular vesicles remodeling hepatobiliary pathogenesis
Authors: Siyang Hu, Chunhui He, Kunqi Sun, Hongye Zhou, Xueyan Liu, Hao Wang, Sen Li, Haifeng Yu, Ke Xu, Wei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGlobal hepatobiliary disease burden is increasing, prompting gut-liver axis research. Gut microbiota-secreted bacterial extracellular vesicles (BEVs) carry bioactive molecules affecting host immunity, inflammation, and metabolism. Whether BEVs actively or passively cause liver damage is unclear. This review suggests gut-derived BEVs trigger hepatic innate immunity and sterile inflammation. By combining current findings on how gut microbes, their BEVs, and hepatobiliary diseases interact, we improve the “gut-liver/bile axis” idea. Acknowledging BEVs’ active part in disease offers new ways to use them as diagnostic markers and treatment targets, likely better managing hepatobiliary disorders.Subject areas: Hepatology, Pathophysiology, Microbiome

Introduction

Global burden of hepatobiliary diseases is rising.1 The clinical spectrum is broad—from metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), now the most prevalent chronic liver condition globally,2 to gallstone disease, primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), and malignancies such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and cholangiocarcinoma (CCA).3 What unites these diverse conditions, despite their different etiologies, is a shared underpinning of metabolic dysregulation, persistent inflammation, and immune dysfunction. This commonality underscores the urgent need for better diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Trillions of microorganisms in the gut constantly signal to the liver and biliary system via the gut-liver/bile axis.4 When this microbial community is disrupted, the intestinal barrier can weaken, letting bacterial products and metabolites slip into circulation. Over time, that interference can disrupt metabolism and speed up disease. Because gut microbes shape immune and inflammatory responses, they’re now seen as potential targets for therapies that restore balance.

Bacterial extracellular vesicles (BEVs) are how gut microbes talk to the host. Released by gut bacteria, these tiny packages carry proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolites that can tweak immune responses, alter the gut barrier, and reshape the tumor microenvironment. Their relevance to hepatobiliary disease is increasingly recognized, and they may eventually serve as the basis for new diagnostic approaches.5

Over the past decade, the way we think about the gut-liver axis has shifted. Microbial metabolites are now recognized as key players, and BEVs have joined the picture as important signaling components. Here, we use an updated “gut-liver/bile axis” model to ask how the system maintains balance and how pathological processes arise.

The gut, liver, and biliary tract talk to each other through a two-way street of anatomical and functional connections. When gut microbes go out of balance, it can set off a chain of events: bacterial products—BEVs, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), hydrogen sulfide—slip through the gut wall and reach the liver, where they flip on inflammatory pathways like TLR4/NF-κB in Kupffer cells. Dysbiosis also messes with bile acid metabolism. Levels of secondary bile acids such as deoxycholic acid (DCA) go up, a sign that the whole microbiota-bile acid-cholesterol axis is off-kilter. And that disruption can open the door to inflammation, fibrosis, and eventually cancer in the liver and biliary tract.

Several reviews have linked BEVs to liver disease,6 but how these vesicles travel from gut to liver and what they do once they get there is still unclear. Basic questions remain open: do they cross the intestinal barrier directly? Which liver cells do they target? And what happens downstream? Instead of treating BEVs as passive readouts of dysbiosis, we see them as active players in disease. We conceptualize the gut-liver/bile axis as a framework for understanding how gut-derived vesicles engage hepatic innate immune receptors and initiate sterile inflammatory cascades central to hepatobiliary pathology. By pulling current evidence into this model, we hope to reframe how the field thinks about pathophysiology—and in doing so, start a conversation about BEVs as therapeutic targets rather than just biomarkers.

Literature search strategy: we conducted a systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases for articles published up to January 2026, using combinations of keywords including “bacterial extracellular vesicles,” “BEVs,” “outer membrane vesicles,” “gut-liver axis,” “liver diseases,” “biliary diseases,” “hepatocellular carcinoma,” and “cholangiocarcinoma.” Reference lists of relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional studies.

As this is a narrative review, we did not follow the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines, which are typically reserved for systematic reviews. Instead, we conducted a comprehensive and structured literature search as described above, synthesizing findings to provide a broad overview of the field.

The updated gut-liver/bile axis: from dysbiosis to vesicle shedding

Axis architecture: Anatomical and functional connections

The gut, liver, and biliary tree operate as a tightly linked two-way system. Metabolites from the gut travel to the liver through the portal vein, while bile secreted by the liver flows back into the intestines, shaping microbial composition and reinforcing the gut barrier.7,8 This back-and-forth exchange maintains balance along what’s now called the gut-liver/bile axis.

A healthy gut microbiota supports this system—commensal bacteria produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and other metabolites that strengthen tight junctions and preserve barrier integrity.9 Once the microbial balance goes off, things start sliding. Harmful bacteria take over, good ones fade, and the gut barrier begins to crack. That’s when you get a “leaky gut”—LPS and H2S slip through and make their way into portal circulation.10 Portal hypertension only makes it worse: vessels get congested, tissue swells, tight junctions loosen—all of which leaves the barrier more exposed.11

H2S is another metabolite that rides the gut-liver/bile axis and shapes how the liver responds to immune signals. Sulfate-reducing bacteria like Desulfovibrio churn it out in the gut. Once H2S reaches the liver, it hits Kupffer cells, triggering release of IL-6 and IL-8—cytokines that fan biliary inflammation, damage tissue, and nudge fibrosis along.12 On a separate front, H2S also gets involved in cholesterol gallstone formation. It flips on Farnesoid X receptor (FXR), which turns down CYP7A1 and cuts back on bile acid synthesis. That shift in bile chemistry tilts things toward lithogenic bile10. So you get two things happening at once: inflammation brewing in the biliary tract, and bile that’s more likely to form stones.

The role of BEVs in gut-liver/bile axis

Whether BEVs make it to the liver and biliary tract mostly comes down to how intact the gut barrier is. In a healthy gut, tight junctions keep vesicle passage to a minimum—only a tiny amount slips through. When dysbiosis sets in, that barrier breaks down, and BEVs start entering the portal circulation and building up in the liver.13 These 20–400 nm vesicles, constantly shed by gut bacteria, carry proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolites into the bloodstream.5 Their lipid envelope protects this cargo during transit, ensuring delivery in intact form. Once in the liver, BEVs—along with LPS and other bacterial products—engage pattern recognition receptors such as TLR4 on Kupffer cells, initiating inflammatory responses.13 Obesity adds another layer to this process. A high-fat diet doesn’t just ramp up BEV production—it also slows their clearance by hepatic macrophages. The result is a feedback loop: more vesicles mean more inflammation and metabolic problems, which in turn make dysbiosis worse.14,15 So rather than just mirroring what’s happening in the gut, BEVs seem to carry those effects straight to the liver and biliary tract (Figure 1). How this plays out in different diseases is what we turn to next.

Pathogenic mechanisms in hepatobiliary diseases

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease

MASLD reflecting the hepatic consequences of systemic metabolic dysregulation.2 The process often begins with shifts in gut microbiota composition. BEVs serve as a key mechanistic link, carrying signals from the intestinal environment that contribute to hepatobiliary pathology.

Gut dysbiosis: The initiating trigger

MASLD often originates from gut dysbiosis driven by factors such as alcohol use and poor diet. This microbial imbalance compromises intestinal barrier function at two levels—it weakens the epithelial lining, allowing microbial translocation, and disrupts the deeper vascular barrier, enabling bacterial products and LPS to enter hepatic circulation. Once in the liver, these toxins activate TLR4/NF-κB signaling, promoting inflammation and fibrosis.16 Studies have confirmed this process. A high-fat diet quickly damages the gut lining, and transplanting the resulting unhealthy gut bacteria into healthy animals is enough to weaken their gut barrier and cause metabolic problems. Fortunately, a protective mechanism exists. Activating the WNT/β-catenin pathway in blood vessel cells helps keep the gut-vascular barrier strong.17 The drug obeticholic acid (OCA) works by boosting this pathway, helping repair the barrier and showing significant promise in treating NASH.18

BEVs as the mechanistic executors

BEVs contribute to MASLD pathogenesis through multiple interconnected mechanisms. BEVs impair intestinal barrier integrity, facilitating the portal translocation of microbial components like LPS,19 which upon reaching the liver activate TLR4 and other pattern-recognition pathways (e.g., TLR2 and NOD1/2) in Kupffer and stellate cells,20 thereby promoting inflammation and fibrosis21,22(Figure 2).

For instance, outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) from Porphyromonas gingivalis can block the Akt/GSK-3β pathway in liver cells, which hinders glycogen production and contributes to insulin resistance.23 This disruption is compounded through other pathways, including cGAS/STING15,24 and the cytokine release driven by TLR4/MyD88.25 BEVs produced under a high-fat diet, which are often rich in phosphatidylcholine, can worsen hepatic insulin resistance by activating the AhR receptor.14 At the same time, obesity weakens the clearance of BEVs by CRIg+ macrophages, making systemic insulin resistance more severe.15 Different bacteria appear to cause specific metabolic issues. BEVs from Helicobacter pylori can reduce signaling through IRS1/AKT-2/GLUT2,26 while LPS-carrying Pseudomonas BEVs interfere with normal glucose metabolism.27

BEV-mediated amplification of fibrosis and steatosis

As MASLD progresses toward fibrosis, BEVs from different pathogens can turn on pro-fibrotic cellular pathways in several ways. BEVs from H. pylori and Escherichia coli, for example, activate hepatic stellate cells—the former through CagA/VacA proteins28 and the latter via TGF-β signaling.29 These BEVs also encourage macrophages to polarize through the NLRP3 inflammasome.30 When the microbiome is not functioning properly, its BEVs can carry the protein HMGB1 to liver cells, directly promoting fat accumulation (steatosis).31 Other pro-inflammatory effects include the activation of the TLR-4/TRIF/caspase-11 pathway by E. coli BEVs31 and the triggering of granulocyte-related factors in stellate cells by BEVs from patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH).32 On a broader level, levels of LPS-positive extracellular vesicles rise when the intestinal barrier is damaged.33 These vesicles can also contain small RNAs (sRNAs) that modify host gene expression epigenetically.34 Together, these various mechanisms work together with bile acid signaling through FXR/TGR5 receptors,35,36 worsening metabolic issues and immune system activation.

Protective counter-regulatory mechanisms

Probiotic-derived BEVs, unlike those from harmful bacteria, can perform protective roles. For instance, BEVs from Akkermansia muciniphila help by dampening TLR-4 signaling and increasing production of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10.37 BEVs from the probiotic Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) boost the expression of IL-22 and β-defensin-2.38 BEVs from Lactobacillus species show several helpful effects. They can reduce oxidative stress by modulating the Nrf-2/HO-1 pathway39 and encourage immune cells to develop into anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages and regulatory T cells (Tregs).40,41 At the intestinal barrier, probiotic BEVs strengthen tight junctions by upregulating ZO-1, claudin-14, and occludin42,43 and activate AhR/IL-22/Reg3γ signaling to reduce bacterial translocation,44 thereby counteracting the epithelial apoptosis triggered by F. BEVs via RIPK1/RIPK3.45

Biliary diseases

Biliary diseases, including cholecystitis, gallstones, and cholangitis, are closely associated with gut microbial dysbiosis.46 The biliary tree sits adjacent to the gut, with bile constantly cycling through it. That direct exposure to gut contents helps explain why microbial imbalance factors into biliary pathology, from cholesterol gallstones and acute cholecystitis (AC) to PSC and PBC.

Cholesterol gallstones

Emerging evidence implicates specific gut microbes, particularly from the order Desulfovibrionales, in the pathogenesis of cholesterol gallstones. A proposed metabolic cascade involves microbial production of hydrogen sulfide (H2S), which activates hepatic FXR to suppress bile acid synthesis via CYP7A1. Concurrently, these microbes promote the growth of 7α-dehydroxylating bacteria, leading to an increased pool of hydrophobic secondary bile acids such as DCA. This shift in bile acid profile means cholesterol stays put in the liver instead of being flushed out through the gut. When intrahepatic cholesterol accumulates beyond a certain threshold, liver X receptor alpha (LXRα) is activated, leading to increased expression of the cholesterol transporters ABCG5 and G8. Bile becomes cholesterol-supersaturated, setting the stage for gallstones.10 This sets up a loop: dysbiosis throws bile acids off balance, which scrambles cholesterol metabolism, and the cholesterol feeds right back into the system. This places the gut-liver/bile communication—at the forefront of potential therapeutic intervention.

AC

In AC, the gut microbiota takes a different turn. Enterobacteriaceae—particularly Escherichia coli—expand relative to beneficial taxa, while butyrate-producing genera such as Clostridium and Coprococcus decline. Here’s the interesting part: the E. coli and Klebsiella in their bile are often the same strains found in their gut. The more Enterobacteriaceae you find in stool, the higher endotoxin levels tend to be in bile. Supporting this, animal studies show that giving guinea pigs pathogenic E. coli by mouth reproduces typical cholecystitis pathology. Together, these findings suggest that gut dysbiosis, especially overgrowth of Enterobacteriaceae, enables bacteria to move from the gut into the bile system, causing infection and inflammation47 (Figure 2).

Primary sclerosing cholangitis and primary biliary cholangitis

PSC and PBC are prime examples of how microbial imbalance and altered bile acid metabolism can drive chronic biliary inflammation, yet they manifest with distinct microbial signatures. PSC is linked to a sharp decline in gut microbiota diversity, characterized by an overgrowth of Veillonella and Klebsiella pneumoniae and compromised microbial vitamin B6 synthesis.48,49,50 In contrast, the taxonomic shift in PBC is primarily identified by a depletion of C.51 Mechanistically, gut microbiota-derived secondary bile acids such as ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) inhibit cholangiocyte senescence, attenuating bile duct injury and fibrosis in PSC.52 An imbalanced microbiome has the opposite effect, fostering an accumulation of hydrophobic secondary bile acids that amplify injury to the biliary epithelium.

Cholangiopathies haven’t gotten as much BEV-focused attention as liver diseases have, but evidence is starting to build. Vesicles traveling along the gut-liver axis appear to contribute to cholestatic liver injury by flipping on the NLRP3 inflammasome and triggering gasdermin D-dependent pyroptosis in biliary epithelial cells,49 which in turn paves the way for fibrosis.29 In chronic cholestatic conditions such as PSC and PBC—where persistent inflammation and immune dysregulation define the pathology—BEVs may contribute to disease progression. Upon reaching the biliary epithelium, they could initiate or amplify epithelial injury and trigger sclerosing inflammatory responses.

Hepatobiliary carcinogenesis

A dynamic crosstalk occurs between the host and its gut microbes through the gut-liver/bile axis. While essential for health, this intimate dialog can be subverted, transforming the axis into a central pathway that actively contributes to the formation of tumors in the liver and bile ducts.53

Gut microbiota in hepatobiliary carcinogenesis

Hepatocellular carcinoma

HCC progression reflects a convergence of microbial and metabolic shifts. Long-term consumption of high-cholesterol diets alters the gut microbiota. Pro-inflammatory bacteria like D. and Mucispirillum thrive, while beneficial ones such as Bifidobacterium and A. decline.54 These dietary changes also alter bile acid metabolism, leading to elevated levels of taurocholic acid (TCA) and glycocholic acid (GCA). Meanwhile, tryptophan metabolites such as 3-indolepropionic acid drop sharply.

These dysbiosis-driven changes help create a liver environment conducive to tumor development. When the gut barrier springs a leak, bacterial products and inflammatory stimuli get through—and once they reach the liver, they fuel hepatic steatosis, stoke inflammation, and drive abnormal cell growth. All of that goes hand in hand with surging cytokines and oxidative stress.36 At the same time, loss of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus reduces tryptophan metabolites including IAA and IPA, both natural ligands for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor. Without these metabolites, this protective signaling pathway is weakened, favoring HCC development (Figure 3A). This microbial imbalance also takes the brakes off SREBP2, ramping up cholesterol production in the liver and speeding up hepatocarcinogenesis. That model fits with what we see clinically: HCC patients have less IAA in their stool, and that tracks with lower AhR activity in the liver and higher SREBP2 expression55 (Figure 3B).

Gut bacteria influence not only host metabolism but also how HCC responds to treatment. Recent studies have identified a direct mechanism behind this link. Patients with better radiotherapy outcomes tend to have higher levels of Faecalibacterium—not merely as a correlation, but because its metabolite c-di-AMP plays a functional role. c-di-AMP acts together with double-stranded DNA released from radiation-damaged tumor cells. This combination activates the cGAS-STING pathway in dendritic cells, leading to interferon-β production, which in turn enhances CD8+ T cell antitumor activity. In contrast, therapy resistance is associated with enrichment of pro-inflammatory Streptococcus. This protective pathway was disrupted in germ-free mice and after antibiotic treatment, indicating that a balanced microbiome is necessary for effective radiotherapy.56 This study provides evidence that gut bacterial metabolites can directly modulate host immune signals to influence radiotherapy efficacy (Figure 3C).

Biliary tract cancers

Biliary tract cancers are driven by microbial imbalance, metabolic disruption, and chronic inflammation. In patients with extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA), the gut microbiota tends to shift in a predictable way: pro-inflammatory bacteria like K. and Enterobacter flourish, while commensals such as Bifidobacterium fade.57 When the gut barrier springs a leak, bacterial products like LPS slip through and make their way to the liver. Once there, LPS engages Kupffer cells via TLR4/NF-κB signaling, creating a pro-fibrotic and inflammatory microenvironment that contributes to cholangiocarcinogenesis.57

Dysbiosis also contributes to carcinogenesis through shifts in microbial metabolite profiles. Secondary bile acids like deoxycholic acid (DCA) prod tumor cells to invade by switching on receptors such as S1PR2. Meanwhile, protective metabolites such as 4-hydroxyphenylacetone and palmitoylethanolamide dwindle, chipping away at the body’s natural antitumor defenses.57 This metabolite imbalance can become self-perpetuating: bile acids help 7α-dehydroxylating bacteria thrive,58 and those bacteria churn out even more DCA. Once in the liver, DCA activates hepatic stellate cells to release inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6) and can directly induce DNA damage59—both effects that contribute to CCA progression.33

These effects become more pronounced when bile flow is impaired. Taurocholic acid and other primary bile acids, it turns out, can also promote cancer under the right conditions. They activate ERK/AKT and Hippo-YAP signaling, pushing biliary epithelial cells to proliferate uncontrollably.34 At the same time, a leaky gut lets LPS slip into the bloodstream, where it recruits immunosuppressive cells via the TLR4/CXCL1 axis—creating conditions that permit tumor growth.35

This whole system hinges on a delicate balance. When primary bile acids are at physiological levels, they call in NKT cells through the CXCL16/CXCR6 axis to keep tumors in check, while secondary bile acids dampen this protective response.35 So the “gut microbiota-bile acid-immunity/inflammation” network sits right at the core of CCA pathogenesis—and gives us a map for where to look for new therapies (Figure 2).

Gallbladder cancer

The link between specific pathogens and gallbladder cancer is most apparent with chronic infection. Long-term carriage of Salmonella Typhi contributes to gallbladder carcinogenesis, likely through persistent inflammation.60 Clinical data bear this out: gallbladder cancer patients with chronic S. Typhi or S. Paratyphi infection have higher mortality rates.61 How exactly these bacteria promote malignancy remains under investigation, but the association suggests that certain pathogens—not just general dysbiosis—can drive tumor development in susceptible tissues (Figure 2).

BEVs in hepatobiliary carcinogenesis

BEVs have emerged as active participants in carcinogenesis, a function increasingly supported by evidence of their functionally enriched proteome within the tumor microenvironment.62 Their effects are Janus-faced—capable of either promoting or suppressing tumors—depending on the parent bacterium and the cargo they carry (Figure 2).

Tumor-promoting effects of pathogenic BEVs

BEVs from pathogenic bacteria facilitate HCC development through multiple mechanisms. Helicobacter pylori-derived OMVs activate the TGF-β/SMAD signaling pathway in HCC cells, enhancing invasiveness.63 Moreover, H. pylori infection induces encapsulation of miR-362-5p into exosomes, which suppress TLE4 in recipient hepatocytes, promoting liver injury and HCC progression in a non-contact manner (Figure 4B).64 Other pathogens contribute through distinct vesicle-mediated mechanisms. Clostridioides difficile infection, for instance, correlates with liver disease, and its membrane vesicles trigger morphological changes in HepG2 cells (Figure 4A). Beyond these direct effects, BEVs may drive cancer progression by targeting mitochondria, disrupting cellular energy production and triggering malignant transformation pathways.65 The proteomic cargo of tumor-associated BEVs includes factors that promote angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and immune evasion (Figure 2).

Anti-tumor effects of probiotic BEVs

Conversely, BEVs derived from probiotic strains exhibit protective activities against HCC. Lactobacillus rhamnosus-derived EVs (LDEVs) suppress proliferation in HEPG2 cells by reprogramming apoptotic proteins—downregulating Bcl-2 and upregulating Bax.66 Similarly, Bifidobacterium longum-EVs attenuate hepatocarcinogenesis in mice by inhibiting hepatic apoptosis, fibrosis, and oxidative stress via the TGF-β1/Smad3 pathway, potentially through modulation of the gut-liver/bile axis.67

BEVs in biliary tract cancers

Patients with biliary tract cancers show distinct gut microbiota profiles compared to those with non-malignant inflammatory biliary conditions: CCA microbiomes are enriched in Proteobacteria (38.4%) and Clostridia (30.1%), contrasting with inflammatory states dominated by Firmicutes.68 All of these points to a possibility worth chasing: that BEVs could be driving biliary carcinogenesis the same way they do elsewhere—through chronic inflammation, immune tweaks, or direct run-ins with cholangiocytes. The playbook looks familiar if you’ve seen what happens in HCC: TLR4-driven inflammation, miRNA delivery, and metabolic rewiring(Figure 2).

Clinical translation

Diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers

Microbial signatures in metabolic liver diseases

Recent evidence suggests that specific gut microbiota compositions may serve as biomarkers for liver and biliary tract diseases, with potential utility in predicting disease progression and clinical outcomes.69 The diagnostic utility of gut microbiome signatures spans from early metabolic liver disease to advanced hepatobiliary complications. One example is a high-alcohol-producing strain of Klebsiella pneumoniae, which has been found to actively drive MASLD development through its production of ethanol.70 Furthermore, machine learning models that incorporate changes in specific bacterial groups—such as Proteus, Enterobacter, Ruminococcus, and Lactobacillus—alongside shifts in bile acid metabolism linked to Escherichia, Bilophila, and Rhodobacter, can predict the onset of MASLD up to 4.6 years before it is clinically diagnosed, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.8.71,72,73

Microbial signatures in advanced liver diseases

Microbial signatures show even greater diagnostic value in advanced stages of liver disease. For detecting cirrhosis, a signature based on 19 microbial species—marked by higher Veillonella parvula and lower Faecalibacterium prausnitzii—delivers area under the curve (AUC) values between 0.88 and 0.91 in studies involving diverse ethnic groups. Adding the patient’s serum albumin level to this signature further improves accuracy, raising AUCs to 0.91–0.95.71,74,75 In hepatocellular carcinoma, specific microbial patterns are also closely linked to patient prognosis. A heightened predominance was observed for Bacteroides, Lachnospiracea incertae sedis, and C. XIVa, particularly when coupled with elevated serum bile acids (≥16 μmol/L), correlates with suppressed anti-tumor immunity and poorer patient outcomes.76,77,78 A microbial signature defined by enriched Lachnoclostridium and depleted Prevotella 9 serves as a favorable prognostic indicator, predicting both longer survival and better response to immune checkpoint inhibitors in unresectable HCC.79

Microbial signatures in biliary diseases

Biliary diseases are similarly characterized by distinct gut microbial signatures. Patients with PBC and PSC both show reduced gut microbiota diversity, though the taxonomic shifts differ between the two diseases. PBC is characterized by depletion of C., whereas PSC features enrichment of Veillonella and Klebsiella pneumoniae along with impaired microbial vitamin B6 synthesis.48,49,50 In cholangiocarcinoma (CCA), microbial-based models have shown diagnostic promise. One example is the B-F-R panel, which includes Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkholderia, Faecalibacterium, and Ruminococcus, and achieves better discrimination than the conventional serum marker CA19-9 (AUC 0.881).80,81,82 These microbial alterations have been linked to CCA pathogenesis through several mechanisms, including dysregulation of bile acid metabolism, disruption of intestinal barrier integrity, and TLR4/CXCL1-driven immunosuppression.51,83,84,85,86

Therapeutic applications

Microbiota-targeted therapeutic strategies

This section reviews microbiome-based therapeutic strategies targeting the gut-liver/bile axis designed to rebalance the gut ecosystem, reduce inflammation, and strengthen anti-tumor immunity (Figure 3). We will examine the supporting evidence, the molecular mechanisms of action, and the translational challenges involved in bringing these therapies into standard clinical practice.

Antibiotic therapy

Antibiotics can modulate gut microbial composition, thereby affecting metabolite profiles. In obesity-induced HCC, oral administration of an antibiotic cocktail or vancomycin alone (targeting Gram-positive bacteria) suppresses HCC development and reduces senescent hepatic stellate cells by depleting specific intestinal commensals and their metabolites, such as DCA.87 Similar effects have been observed in cholestatic HCC models and MYC-overexpressing HCC mouse models, potentially mediated through enhanced anti-tumor function of hepatic NKT cells.87 Animal studies have demonstrated that antibiotics including ampicillin, vancomycin, neomycin, and metronidazole88—administered individually or in combination—inhibit liver tumor growth.87 Rifaximin prevents alcohol-induced liver injury by modulating small intestinal microbiota89 and reduces complications in cirrhosis.90 Norfloxacin administration to cirrhotic patients markedly reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNFα, IFNγ, and IL-12) and suppresses NF-κB activation.91 Vancomycin targets Gram-positive bacteria, inhibits primary bile acid conversion, and increases CXCL16 to promote NKT cell infiltration, thereby suppressing liver cancer growth in preclinical models.92 Metronidazole or vancomycin ameliorates TH17-mediated hepatic inflammation in PSC.93 Despite these antitumor effects in specific contexts, antibiotic exposure may adversely impact cancer therapy, particularly immunotherapy. Clinical evidence demonstrates that antibiotic exposure correlates with markedly shorter progression-free and overall survival in patients undergoing anti-PD-1/PD-L1 treatment.94 This phenomenon is corroborated by mouse experiments: Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from responders to anti-PD-1 therapy enhances the antitumor efficacy of PD-1 inhibitors in germ-free mice, whereas microbiota from non-responders does not confer this benefit95 (Figure 5).

Probiotics and prebiotics

Probiotics, often supported by prebiotics, produce a wide array of anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory metabolites. They modulate gut microbiota balance by altering intestinal immune responses, interacting with other microorganisms, and generating metabolites such as SCFAs. These actions strengthen intestinal barrier function, produce anti-inflammatory metabolites,87 downregulate receptors associated with hepatic inflammation and tumor angiogenesis,87 reduce liver damage caused by harmful bacteria and their metabolites, and even inhibit tumor growth. Prebiotics further refine gut microbial ecology by promoting the proliferation of advantageous bacteria. Notably, L. rhamnosus GG effectively counteracts high-fat diet-induced intestinal barrier impairment and concurrent fatty liver progression.96,97 Bifidobacterium reduces inflammatory cytokines and enhances intestinal barrier protein expression.98 Clinical trials demonstrate that multi-strain probiotic supplements (containing Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium) administered for 8 weeks reduce hepatic fat deposition and levels of inflammatory cytokines (TNFα, IL-6) in MASLD patients,98 improve liver histology,99 and, when combined with Bacillus strains, modulate bile acid metabolism.89 Furthermore, specific gut microbes—including Bifidobacterium,100 Akkermansia,101 Enterococcus,102 F.,103 and members of the Ruminococcaceae family104—function as immunological adjuvants in immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy(Figure 5).

Mediterranean diet

The Mediterranean diet, in particular, helps improve metabolic health. It significantly reduces liver fat,105 improves insulin sensitivity,106 alters gut microbial composition,107 and lowers circulating LPS levels.108 These changes decrease the risk of MASLD,109 cirrhosis, and liver cancer.110 However, for patients with pre-existing gut dysbiosis, elevated bile acids, or hyperbilirubinemia, the high fiber content of the Mediterranean diet may require modification—for example, by reducing fermentable fibers—to avoid a potential increase in HCC risk110(Figure 5).

Consumption of foods rich in monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) and omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) is linked to lower HCC risk.111 This protective effect likely operates through modulation of the gut microbiome, reduction of intestinal inflammation, and enhancement of intestinal barrier repair.112 Higher dietary fiber intake also provides substrate for microbial fermentation, elevating beneficial SCFAs. Thus, consuming oils rich in MUFAs and PUFAs may support a healthier gut microbiome and help prevent fatty liver disease.113 Beyond these macronutrients, certain plant compounds are metabolized by gut bacteria into active anti-cancer molecules. Foods such as pomegranates, black raspberries, and nuts are rich in ellagitannins, which are converted in the gut to ellagic acid114 and further processed by specific microbes into urolithin A (UA).115 Ursolic acid doesn’t stop at blocking cancer—it also gives immunotherapy a boost, making it more effective.115,116 On top of that, it dials down inflammation, nudges colon cancer cells into senescence,117 ramps up antitumor immunity, and puts the brakes on cancer cell motility. UA-producing bacteria could be developed as novel probiotics.118 Research into the microbial pathways generating UA may lead to new drugs or synthetic analogs targeting key steps in this process. One such synthetic analog, UAS03, exhibits greater acid stability and retains anti-cancer activity.119

FMT

FMT involves transferring intact microbial communities from healthy donors to patients to reconstitute a balanced gut microbiota. FMT has demonstrated potential in treating hepatobiliary diseases and tumors by rectifying microbial dysbiosis, improving intestinal barrier integrity, and reducing inflammatory responses and tissue injury. In animal models, FMT reverses alcohol/high-fat diet-induced dysbiosis and hepatic inflammation, increases butyrate production, and upregulates the tight junction protein ZO-1.120 Clinical applications in severe alcoholic hepatitis have shown reduced disease severity and improved survival rates.121,122 Enrichment of Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae through FMT has also been associated with improvement in cirrhosis patients with hepatic encephalopathy.123 Growing evidence indicates that FMT can synergize with immune checkpoint inhibitors to overcome immunotherapy resistance104(Figure 5).

BEV-based therapeutic strategies

Beyond their pathogenic roles, BEVs are being engineered into multifunctional platforms for cancer therapy. They can be functionalized as targeted delivery vehicles for chemotherapeutic drugs, genetic material, or vaccine antigens. Combining BEV-based delivery with adjunctive modalities such as photothermal therapy can achieve synergistic effects. This highlights the BEV platform’s versatility for tackling multiple oncologic challenges (Figure 6).

Immunomodulation and immunotherapy

BEVs act as immune adjuvants: surface PAMPs engage host PRRs to enhance antitumor immunity.124 They shift macrophage polarization away from immunosuppression,125 promote DC maturation and CTL differentiation,126 and boost NK cell activity.127

BEVs can counteract resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors by either directly interfering with the PD-1/PD-L1 axis (via engineered PD-1 domains) or by delivering synergistic therapeutics like miRNA-34a,128 thus functioning as both immunomodulators and targeted delivery systems (Figure 6).

Targeted drug delivery

BEVs are uniquely positioned as drug delivery platforms, largely because they lend themselves to diverse therapeutic applications. Through genetic or biochemical engineering, they can be customized to carry various therapeutic payloads, from small molecules to nucleic acids and proteins.

For instance, BEVs from Klebsiella pneumoniae have been used to deliver the chemotherapy drug doxorubicin specifically to small cell lung cancer cells.129 Escherichia coli BEVs delivering perhexiline can repolarize tumor-associated macrophages from a pro-tumor (M2) to an anti-tumor (M1) phenotype.130,131 BEVs can also serve as secondary enhancers to augment existing therapeutic platforms: encapsulating drug-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) within BEVs prolongs drug release and reduces systemic toxicity.132 BEV-based platforms enable precise drug delivery, and surface modification can further enhance targeting accuracy. In one strategy, Salmonella Typhimurium-derived BEVs (StEVs) engineered with RGD peptide ligands and loaded with the prodrug tegafur markedly augmented tumor-specific cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells133 (Figure 6).

Nucleic acid delivery for gene therapy

BEVs are highly efficient at delivering functional nucleic acids into target cells, positioning them as promising tools for gene therapy. For example, engineered BEVs loaded with KSP-targeting siRNA reduced HER2 expression and suppressed tumor growth.134 In another approach, E. coli BEVs (EcEVs) were used to transport pre-microRNA, effectively downregulating the cancer-related gene CXCR4135 (Figure 6).

BEVs in combination therapies: vaccines, oncolytic viruses, and photothermal therapy. In vaccine design, they work well as carriers for antigens. For instance, when the HPV16 E7 antigen is integrated into E. coli-derived BEVs (EcEVs), it helps create effective tumor vaccines.126

BEVs can also work together with oncolytic viruses. In one study, BEVs from Fusobacterium nucleatum (FnEVs) were used to package an oncolytic HSV-1 virus. This combination triggered a form of cell death called PANoptosis in tumors, improved the effectiveness of anti-PD-L1 therapy, and helped establish lasting immune memory.136

In photothermal therapy, BEVs can be used to enhance immune responses. For example, BEVs loaded with both a photosensitizer and the drug doxorubicin (DOX) can be taken up by macrophages and then induce pyroptosis in tumor cells.137 In another strategy, photosensitizer-BEV complexes that are also conjugated with PD-L1 antibodies can activate dendritic cells and CD8+ T cells.138

BEVs offer a promising therapeutic platform. They naturally stimulate immune responses, can target tumors, are relatively easy to modify, and have shown favorable safety in preclinical studies.125,139,140 Potential applications include vesicles from healthy donors or engineered strains to address immunotherapy resistance, possibly serving as an alternative to FMT.141,142

Despite the promise of BEV-based therapies in the lab, bringing them into clinical use is no small feat. Several major obstacles stand in the way: difficulties in scaling up production, rapid clearance of BEVs in the body, and the lack of clear regulatory guidelines.143 Overcoming these hurdles is essential if the innovative BEV strategies outlined earlier are to reach their full potential.

Conclusions and future outlook

Conclusions

Our view of the gut-liver axis is evolving. It has long been clear that gut microbes help sustain this communication network—through metabolites and by preserving the intestinal barrier. But we now see that bacterial extracellular vesicles play an equally vital role. These BEVs carry proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids that can directly affect how liver and biliary cells behave. That reframes gut-liver crosstalk as something more integrated—a “gut-liver/bile axis” where BEVs do much of the signaling (Figure 5). Placing microbial signals at the center of this framework gives us a way to think about where hepatobiliary diseases come from.

The biological effects of BEVs, whether protective or harmful, largely reflect their bacterial origin. BEVs derived from pathobionts tend to promote inflammation, disrupt bile acid homeostasis, and compromise intestinal barrier integrity. These processes can drive disease progression. BEVs from beneficial bacteria take a different approach. They help strengthen the mucosal barrier, help resolve inflammation, and help keep metabolism in check. So BEVs can either drive disease or defend against it—depending on where they come from. That duality is what makes them so intriguing as potential therapies or delivery tools.

Future directions

Moving these insights toward clinical use means tackling a few interconnected challenges. Here’s the problem with current microbiome-modulating therapies—antibiotics, probiotics, FMT: they’re not selective. Instead of going after specific disease mechanisms, they just reshuffle the whole microbial community. Antibiotics can disrupt gut ecology and drive resistance. Probiotic effects, meanwhile, are often short-lived and it depends on the strain. And FMT, for all its power, comes with risks: it can transmit pathogens, and we still don’t really understand how it works.

For BEVs to become a viable therapeutic option, they will need to evolve from broad-spectrum interventions toward more targeted applications. This will require strategies on two fronts: engineering parental bacteria to modulate vesicle cargo, and modifying the vesicles themselves to improve tissue targeting, extend circulation time, or reduce immunogenicity. Beyond engineering, there is also a need for molecular markers that can distinguish pathogenic BEVs from protective ones. Such markers could enable diagnostic approaches that provide insight into vesicle function—something conventional sequencing cannot offer.

Several technical hurdles stand in the way of clinical translation. Inconsistent isolation methods contribute to batch-to-batch variability,144,145and residual bacterial components such as LPS raise safety concerns that may necessitate re-engineering of source bacteria.146 BEVs also have a short half-life in circulation, often clearing the bloodstream within 20 min,147 which raises questions about dose equivalence between experimental models and humans. Standardized production protocols148 and clear regulatory frameworks149 are still lacking. Standards for isolation, characterization, and GMP manufacturing are still needed. Freeze-drying with trehalose or spray-drying could improve stability,150,151 and public databases of vesicle composition would help with standardization and reproducibility.152,153,154

Even with reliable manufacturing, a key question remains: do BEVs actually work better than what we already have? Answering this will take head-to-head studies—comparing BEVs to synthetic carriers like liposomes, and figuring out where they might have an edge over conventional probiotics. Data like that would help us figure out where BEVs fit and how to use them. But there’s a bigger question underneath: how do they actually work? Observing that BEVs from healthy donors or engineered strains may help address anti-PD-1 therapy resistance141,142—or that dendritic cells loaded with tumor antigen exosomes could serve as vaccines155—is one thing. Understanding how they actually work is another. This involves using multi-omics tools to map out relevant pathways and creating better, more human-relevant models for testing. Key questions include how BEVs alter the tumor setting or affect autoimmune processes like in PSC, and the details of how BEVs are made, taken up by cells, and enable cell communication.154 Tackling these will require multi-omics profiling—proteomic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic—on well-defined vesicle populations to trace the pathways at play.

Standardization represents a foundational challenge. Meeting it requires, on one hand, consensus protocols for BEV isolation, characterization, and large-scale GMP-compliant manufacturing; on the other, public databases that systematically catalog vesicular composition across bacterial species and growth conditions. Together, these efforts will accelerate innovation and ensure that findings across the field are reproducible and comparable.148,152,153,154

Overcoming these challenges will require sustained collaboration that cuts across microbiology, hepatology, bioengineering, and regulatory science. Success will unlock the full therapeutic potential of the gut-liver/bile axis, delivering safe, effective, and precisely targeted treatments for patients with hepatobiliary diseases.
