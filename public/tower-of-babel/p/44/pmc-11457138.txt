Effects of Crumpling Stage and Porosity of Graphene Electrode on the Performance of Electrochemical Supercapacitor
Authors: Abrar Amin Khan, Sazid Noor Rabi, Tousif Jamee, Musanna Galib, Fazle Elahi, Md. Ashiqur Rahman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The performance characteristics of supercapacitors composed of crumpled graphene electrodes and aqueous NaCl electrolytes are investigated through Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations using a newly developed crumpled graphene-based supercapacitor model. Results suggest that the three-dimensional configuration of crumpled graphene boosts electrolyte-electrode interaction. This improved interaction, which includes a larger ion-accessible zone, increases the specific capacitance of the supercapacitor by roughly 400% (16.4 μF/cm2) compared to planar graphene electrodes. Examining the effect of different stages of crumpling and the inclusion of pores on the electrode surface shows that the stages of crumpling substantially influence the supercapacitor performance. A smaller crumpling radius, meaning fully crumpled stage, improves the performance as increased crumpling leads to better packing efficiency, which aids in more ion separation. Furthermore, adding pores on the surface of crumpled graphene improves the ion accessibility by creating additional adsorption sites. An exceptional capacitance of 19.8 μF/cm2 is obtained for a porosity of 20%. However, the results suggest that the in-plane-porosity of the electrode needs to be optimized as there is a decline in specific capacitance after that point (20% porosity), indicating a suboptimal relationship between the charge distribution, specific surface area (SSA) and the porosity of the electrode.

Introduction

Supercapacitors are among the most promising electrochemical energy storage systems due to their high power density, quick charge–discharge speed, and long cycle lifespan.1 Although their energy density has been a limiting factor in their large-scale commercial use, they are quickly catching up with batteries in recent times2,3·

Recently, several studies have been conducted on improving the energy storage ability of supercapacitors4−6 Researchers have found that multiple parameters such as electrode material and their surface area, electrolyte composition, and electrode–electrolyte interaction are some of the most impactful ones.7,8 Although several electrode materials have been explored, graphene has stood out as the most dominating material for supercapacitors.9 In comparison to carbon nanotubes (50–1315 m2 g−1), their specific surface area (SSA) (2630 m2 g−1) is substantially larger, which enhances the electrode–electrolyte interaction leading to higher capacitance values.10 A molecular dynamics (MD) study on the interaction of graphene and potassium halide ions provided crucial insight into the structuring of electrolytes on graphene electrodes.11 Another study employing ab initio MD simulation of graphene and alkali ions revealed fundamentally novel understandings regarding the physical behavior (effect of charging, adsorption energy, and ion–solvent binding) of supercapacitors.12 However, the mystery behind the molecule-level electrochemical characteristics of graphene and aqueous electrolytes remains unsolved.13

Although 2D materials have shown immense potential, their specific capacitance still needs to be improved due to their limited solvent-accessible surface area (SASA).14 Several modifications have been implemented in materials such as carbon nanotubes (CNT) and graphene to maximize the SASA. It has been reported that the electrochemical double layer (EDL) capacitance increases with electrode surface curvature, attributed to enhanced ion accumulation as the single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT) radius decreases.15 A similar study showed that the curvature of the graphene electrode decelerates the diffusion pathways, allowing higher concentrations of electrolyte ions near the electrode region.16 Stacking graphene sheets in layers is another approach to improve supercapacitor performance by exposing more surface area of the electrodes for the ions.17 However, the strong van der Waals attraction between these layers impedes ion accumulation.18 Graphene sheets are inclined to agglomerate due to intersheet adhesion, known as restacking, which eventually reduces the SSA of the electrodes.19 Cranford et al. reported that converting 2D graphene to a 3D structure effectively circumvents the stacking problem and concurrently elevates the SASA by up to 60%.20 Crumpling a flat graphene sheet enables the ions to store on both sides of the electrode surface. The formation of wrinkles, ridges, voids and edge carbon atoms increases the SSA of the graphene electrode, thus increasing the electrode–electrolyte interaction area.21 Thus, it is evident that crumpled graphene possesses considerable potential as an electrode material. Leveraging this phenomenon, to the best of our knowledge, we are the first to investigate interactions at the atomic level to evaluate the electrochemical performance of crumpled graphene electrodes.

Another segment of our study is focused on surface topology by controlling the porosity. Although a higher SSA is achieved by crumpling the graphene sheets, the energy and power density can be further augmented by controlling surface roughness, creating subnanometer pores, or increasing the porosity of the electrode surface.22 To explore this phenomenon, fine-tuned 3D porous structures in graphene have been used to create a larger surface area.23 Further research has shown that proper pore size may help ions to replace the solvent in the pore and exclude the co-ions from the electrode.24 The increased average pore size can accommodate more desolvated ions, and the increase of desolvated ions can improve the capacitance of EDLCs.25 In addition, the presence of solvation shells affects the free diffusion of ions and warrants a larger pore size.26 Comprehensive physical and numerical techniques, including Machine Learning (ML) and Density Functional Theory (DFT), have enhanced the understanding of the impact of pores and pore size on supercapacitor performance.27 Kondrat et al. studied nanoporous graphene electrodes and suggested that multilayered supercapacitors with porous channels could optimize the electrochemical performance.28 Accordingly, our new crumpled graphene model has a diverse range of porosity, through which we examined the electrochemical parameters of supercapacitors.

In this study, we have developed a novel supercapacitor model via MD simulation utilizing a crumpled graphene electrode. Our goal was to examine the influence of porosity in conjunction with the crumpled structure on the electrochemical characteristics. We have systematically varied the crumpling stage and amount of porosity to study their effect on electrochemical properties such as specific capacitance, ion density, EDL thickness, free energy, and electric potential.

Methods

A square sheet of graphene (250 Å × 250 Å) was converted into crumpled form using atomistic simulation. The crumpling process of the graphene sheet involved the application of a spherical indentation force that exhibited spring-like characteristics. A time step of 0.001 ps incrementally reduced the effective crumpling radius at a rate of 0.75 Å/step, gradually rendering a more crumpled shape. After each step, the radius (r) of the crumpled graphene was calculated using eq 1:1where r0 is the radius of crumpling before the crumpling process began, r is the crumpling radius after the N-th step of the simulation, Ne is the number of steps required for equilibrium, n is the crumpling rate, and t is the size of each time step. The adaptive interatomic reactive empirical bond-order (AIREBO)-morse potential was employed to characterize the interaction between carbon–carbon particles.29 The simulation was conducted in LAMMPS using a microcanonical ensemble (NVE) since the main objective was to minimize the bending energy rather than temperature or pressure. We followed the input script provided in reference30 for this purpose. Figure 1a illustrates the crumpling process of planar graphene. We have modeled four different crumpled forms of graphene as electrodes by varying the value of r, which are 165.4 Å, 110.1 Å, 89.5 and 68.3 Å, respectively. From Figure 1a, we can see that the radius 165.4 Å indicates the beginning of crumpling, which we hereafter refer to as a mildly crumpled state (Stage-I). In contrast, 110.1 Å shows the halfway point to the state of complete crumpling and is referred to as a moderately crumpled state (Stage-II). When the radius is decreased to 89.5 Å, the crumpling process is near its completion and is termed as the highly crumpled state (Stage-III). Finally, it reaches a radius of 68.3 Å, representing the fully crumpled state (Stage-IV) and resembling a crumpled piece of paper.31 The crumpling behavior varies according to the crumpling stages of the graphene sheet as different amounts of potential energy are contained in different stages.32 Stage-I corresponds to edge-bending, Stage-II prompts self-adhesion, and Stage-III evokes further compression. Energy minimization was done for each of the above cases to ensure structural stability. Here, the smallest value of r (68.3 Å) signifies the most crumpled form of graphene, which holds the utmost importance in our research as detailed in this paper.

Aqueous NaCl was chosen as the electrolyte for the study, where H2O acted as the solvent. The concentration of electrolyte was taken as 1 M, which corresponds to 100 Na+ ions and 100 Cl− ions over 30 000 molecules of H2O. The density of water was set at 1000 kg/m3 considering a pressure and temperature of 1 atm and 298 K, respectively. This density was assumed without considering the volume of the graphene electrodes to avoid complexity. The bond lengths for Na–Cl and O–H were taken as 2.386 and 0.9572 Å, respectively; whereas the bond angle of H2O was maintained at 104.52°.33,34

On the final crumpled graphene model with r = 68.3 Å, multiple porosity levels (of random pore sizes) were incorporated to investigate their effect on specific capacitance. LAMMPS was used to generate pores on the surface of graphene (Supporting Information—section 7). Atoms were randomly removed from the surface of the crumpled graphene to create porosity of 5%, 10%, 20%, and 30% (by surface area), as illustrated in Figure 1b.

Figure 2 shows a typical simulation cell of our study for the model of crumpled graphene with r = 68.3 Å. We chose to implement the PPF (Periodic, Periodic, and Fixed) boundary condition in the x-y-z directions, as the electrodes were maintained at a fixed distance of 100 Å in the z-direction, rendering the movement of the electrolyte in the z-direction, the sole focal point of this investigation. The size of the simulation supercell was 135 Å × 140 Å × 400 Å. Considering the low applied potential, the constant surface charge approach was employed in this study where the voltage applied was represented as a uniform distribution of electrical charges on the carbon atoms. Our simulation considered a surface charge of ±0.045e/C, which has been selected following previous studies.35 The TIP3P model was used to simulate water molecules.36 The water molecules were kept rigid using the SHAKE algorithm.37−39 Along with electrostatic interaction forces, the Lennard-Jones (LJ) potential33 was utilized to model Van der Waals interactions among all the particles. Results and Discussion

The fully charged state of the supercapacitor based on the fully crumpled model (r = 68.3 Å) is shown in Figure 3. The per-atom constant charge of the electrodes (±0.045e/C atom) served as the primary driving force, prompting ions to migrate toward the electrodes with the opposite charge. Each electrode had a total charge of ±1542e. Due to the narrow 100 Å gap between the electrodes, this charge also caused a repulsive effect on ions with the same charge, leading them to fully dissociate from their associated solvent (H2O) molecules and accumulate at the opposite ends, penetrating the crumpled electrode. Although the electrolytes rushed through the electrodes, the movement of H2O molecules was slower due to their higher molecular weight and charge neutrality. Moreover, H2O molecules could not diffuse into the crumpled graphene because of the significant energy barrier. Density profiles quantified the visual observation of the assembling of the ions near the oppositely charged electrodes.

Simulation domain after 1.2 ns, showing accumulation of ions and water molecules due to charged electrodes. The cross-section of the positive electrode confirms the penetration of oppositely charged ions inside the crumpled electrode (Drawn using OVITO).41

The density profiles of Na+, Cl−, and H2O molecules are shown in Figure 4 for various crumpling radii. The density peaks of the ions near and inside the electrode indicate that those are adsorbed into the electrodes. As the distance from the electrode increases, the density of ions declines in alignment with the Graham model.49 Helmholtz layers (HLs) have a higher impact in regions between peaks and the electrode surface. In the fully crumpled graphene model, the inner Helmholtz layer (IHL) extends up to 5 Å, with the outer Helmholtz layer (OHL) located approximately 50 Å from the positive electrode. Na+ ions are drawn to the cathode more strongly than Cl– ions, as indicated by the slightly larger density peak observed for Na+ ions. As cations are marginally smaller than anions, their packing is tighter. The curves become flat after 75–100 Å, denoting the diffusion layer. The ionic density in the diffusion layer is inversely proportional to the distance from the electrode. The “Initial water layer” is found at a distance of around 4–5 Å from the electrode surface,44 generated due to the hydrophobic graphene surface disrupting a continuous network of hydrogen bonds (Supporting Information, section 4). Similar trends and values of the density profile of water molecules were found in prior studies.44,50 Near the positive electrode, the density profiles of oxygen atoms in the first water layer are slightly closer to the graphene surface than those of hydrogen atoms, as seen in Figure 5.

Figure 4 also reflects the nature and characteristics of ion density profiles with the variation of crumpling radius. With an increasing crumpling radius, the density peaks near the electrode surface decrease, as depicted in Figure 4a. This relates to the inability of larger-radius crumpled structures to acquire more charge. As mentioned before, the crumpled graphene models with greater radii represent the structures at the earlier stages of the crumpling process. The peak density of Cl– for the crumpled graphene model with a radius of 89.5 Å (highly crumpled state) is detected at Z = 70 Å with a value of 0.000195 Å–3. In contrast, the density peak for the completely crumpled structure (r = 68.3 Å) is approximately 0.0004 Å–3, about a 50% increase at a relatively shorter distance from the anode. At a radius of 165.4 Å, the ion density apex is higher compared to a radius of 110.1 Å. However, the latter exhibits a significantly larger solvent access area because ions can penetrate the electrode, unlike at a radius of 165.4 Å. The 165.4 Å radius resembles a 2D structure initially, transitioning into a 3D structure over time. For the water molecules (Figure 5), as the model approaches a fully crumpled state, the orientation of water molecules becomes more ordered. Post-simulation visualization, as shown in Figure 5a, revealed that water molecules were nearly parallel to the electrode in the fully crumpled model, enhancing its charging capability.

Our model yields a specific capacitance (C) of 16.4 μF/cm2 (Figure 6), about 400% greater than planar graphene electrodes.40 This improvement is attributed to the three-dimensional electrode structure, which enhances the charge accumulation of electrolyte ions and SSA by ensuring the accessibility of ions on both sides of the electrode surfaces, while reducing the EDL thickness. Crumpling leads to larger contact of edge carbon atoms with the electrolyte ions. This technique yields a morphology with high mechanical energy absorption and a large accessible surface area.31 Furthermore, the uniform distribution of free space within and between crumpled particles facilitates efficient charge transport, particularly at elevated current densities and mass loadings, thereby enabling superior specific capacitance and rate capability for both mass and volume.51

Variation of specific capacitance as a function of crumpling radius. The specific capacitance is lowest at the initial “edge bending” stage (Stage-I) at 165.4 Å. Then at the “self-folding” stage (Stage-II) at 110.1 Å, the capacitance is increased by around 15% for a 33% decrease in the radius of the crumpling sphere. If the graphene sheet is crumpled more, it reaches the “further compression” stage (Stage-III) at 89.5 Å, and a relatively higher jump in specific capacitance occurs compared with the shift from Stage-I to Stage-II. Finally, it reaches the maximum value for the fully crumpled state (Stage-IV) at 68.3 Å.

The capacitive characteristics of EDLCs are further influenced by the ion distribution near the electrode surface and the concentration of electrolytes accumulated at the electrode–electrolyte interface.52 An appropriate orientation of counterions around the EDLC-dominant region was calculated using the parameter dcenter in eq 6. Better capacitive performance is derived from a thinner double-layer thickness (dcenter).53 In the fully crumpled model, the EDL thickness dcenter is 38.8 Å (Table 2), corresponding to around 63.5% of the density fluctuation zone (L) length. An earlier study comparable to ours has found the EDL thickness for planar graphene to be 7.2 Å, with the value of dcenter/L equal to 72%44 (Supporting Information, section 5). There is a steady decline of EDL thickness with increased crumpling of the electrode. Planar electrodes allow for more dispersed ion accumulation, resulting in a thicker double layer, whereas crumpling leads to a denser concentration of counterions and a narrower EDL. Figure 6 shows a consistent increase in specific capacitance with increasing radius. Full crumpling promotes superior packing efficiency, facilitating enhanced ion transport toward the electrode and improved electrode–electrolyte contact. Additionally, the higher solvent-accessible area due to three-dimensional construction contributes to enhanced supercapacitor performance.54

Figure 7a illustrates potential curves for EDLC models with crumpled graphene electrodes at varying crumpling stages. The total voltage, Utotal, consists of Uion from ion-electrode interaction and Usolvent from polarized water solvents.47 Impact of Usolvent in our case is almost negligible as the distribution of water molecules is not significantly affected due to the crumpling of electrodes. In every configuration, it is observed that there is a peak electric potential zone near the electrode surface which is primarily due to Uion while a decrease in potential is observed in bulk areas further from the electrode surface. The dense storage of ions in Helmholtz layers causes an increase in Uion near the electrode. This causes the initial linear increase of the Utotal as shown in Figure 7a. The electrode’s nonplanar shapes, however, result in dissimilar values and trends compared to planar graphene electrodes. As flat graphene transforms into crumpled graphene, a steady decrease in electric potential occurs, reaching the lowest value at the complete crumpling stage. For the higher crumpling stages, due to the shape of the electrode, there is a saturation of ions in the diffuse layer, leading to a thicker diffuse layer. As a result, there is an effective loss of electric strength near the electrode surfaces. In addition, saturated ion accumulation can lead to ionic resistance which is predominantly found in the higher crumpling stages.

High points in the free energy profiles suggest significant barriers hindering ion electrosorption to the electrode surface, while the minima indicate optimal locations for ion buildup.46Figure 7b demonstrates the monotonically varying free energy with crumpling stages. The free energy, also known as position-dependent PMF (Potential of Mean Force), changes depending on the ion location relative to the electrode region.55 Overlap in values between crumpling stages I and II, as well as III and IV, is attributed to equivalent ion electrosorption due to the Debye shielding effect. Increased ion aggregation and hydration shielding due to graphene’s crumpling lead to more ions gathering near the electrode surface. This results in heightened interactions between the electrode–electrolytes and a lower energy cost for electrosorption and activation energy in later crumpling stages. Unlike similar graphene-based models, there is a tendency for free energy to remain positive, owing to the increased concentration of cations in the crumpled graphene EDL.

Crumpled Graphene Electrode with In-Plane Pores

In the current study, the fully crumpled model of graphene (r = 68.3 Å), was further modified by introducing various amounts of in-plane porosity. Pores provide adsorptive sites for ions in the electrolyte and consequently, the SSA and capacitance depend on the electrode’s porosity. Nonetheless, a greater SSA is not the sole condition for enhanced capacitance. The distribution and size of pores also play a critical function, as they facilitate charge transfer by reducing resistance through shorter diffusion routes. For high capacitance, the pore size should be greater than the particle size of the electrolyte.56Figure 8 demonstrates the magnified view indicating the incorporation of random-sized pores onto the surface of the crumpled electrode. Previous studies have found that ions and pores must be of similar size for better EDLC performance.56

Magnified viewpoint (approximately 20 times upscaled from the electrode size 135 Å × 140 Å viewed in OVITO) of the random-sized pores incorporated onto the surface of the crumpled electrode, shown for the cases of (a) 5%, (b) 10%, (c) 20% and (d) 30% porosity. All the images show the same magnified portion of the electrode, differing only in the degree of porosity. Similarly, figures (e) 5%, (f) 10%, (g) 20%, and (h) 30% porosity show a portion of the 20x view with an even higher magnification (approximately 120 times compared to the electrode size). These images show a clear trend of growing optimum-sized pores in the 20% porosity (c and g). This is directly correlated to the improvement of electrochemical performance.

Moreover, a pore size of less than 1 nm causes a rapid increase in specific capacitance due to the distortion of the solvation shell.57 This enhancement reaches a peak between 0.5 and 1 nm. Our study did not prioritize pore size or shape but instead focused on the pore size distribution (PSD) and their impact on the electrochemical performance. We classified pores into three categories based on pore size: sub-optimum, optimum, and supra-optimum. Optimum pores were selected based on their ability to adsorb Na+ and Cl- ions, as their sizes are much larger than the ions themselves, facilitating adsorption.

In contrast, sub-optimal pores, smaller than the ideal value, are inefficient at ion adsorption due to insufficient space and steric (volumetric) effect.58 Although suboptimal pores adsorb electrolyte ions, they cannot store multiple ions. Supra-optimum pores, with sizes significantly larger than necessary for ion adsorption, also have a low statistical efficiency of adsorbing electrolyte ions due to fast ion migration due to repulsive forces. Although these sites can store multiple ions together, ions migrate to other sites quickly.

Our findings indicate that optimum pores positively impact capacitance, due to higher SASA. Consequently, electrodes with a higher density of these pores exhibit increased ion adsorption and capacitance values. Conversely, a higher proportion of sub-optimum or supra-optimum pores than optimum ones reduces accessible surface area, as these pores are unreliable for ion adsorption. Figure 8 illustrates the presence of optimum pores across all porosity levels, with varying densities in each case. There is a notable increase in optimum pores from 5% to 20% porosity, and during this randomly generated increased porosity levels, suboptimum pores decrease and optimum pores increase, positively affecting electrochemical performance. The magnified views (20x and 120x) of Figure 8 demonstrate this conversion of sub-optimum pores into optimum pores until a porosity of 20%.

A comparison of 20% porosity to 30% porosity reveals that there is a shift in the number density of pores. More supra-optimum pores are created, taking up the space previously occupied by the optimum pores. These pores leave too much space between effective adsorption sites, disrupting ion adsorption. This increase of ineffective pores causes the ion adsorption to decrease significantly, as shown by the capacitance values, free energy, and potential values in later sections.

The density profiles of Cl−, Na+, and H2O with varying porosity exhibit an identical pattern to the previous ones explained in Figures 4 and 5, generating the highest density peak at 20% porosity, followed by 10%, 5% and 30% (Supporting Information, section 8). Minimizing the energy barrier and developing a rapid-transport channel by the nanoscale pores explains this remarkable phenomenon. The hydration shell must be effectively disrupted to minimize the energy cost of accessing the pores. Once the hydration shell is broken, ions can pass through the sub-nanometer pores.59 Therefore, ions having a size smaller than that of the pores readily cross the energy barrier and access the electrode, increasing peak density when porosity increases from 5% to 20%. However, as mentioned earlier, there is a caveat to increasing porosity over a limit because electrodes’ density and SSA drastically reduce value. In addition, when the number of pores increases, certain pores are bigger than required for the loss of the hydration shell, as seen in Figure 8. Due to this adverse effect, lower-density peaks are evident with 30% porosity. For water molecules, the density profiles do not alter much due to changes in porosity. Water molecules do not overcome the energy barrier to be adsorbed inside the electrode but rather form a hydration shell surrounding the charged ions due to polarity change for each case.

It has been well established that introducing pores into electrodes below the 1 nm level can influence the capacitive performance of EDLCs. The size of these pores depends on the electrolyte being used and the operating voltage.60 In their study, Jackel et al. used TEA+ and BF4− ions and different carbon electrodes to find that 0.4 and 0.6 nm pore sizes, respectively, were the optimum sizes for the electrolytes.61 They also found that pores can adsorb more than one ion if they are large. Moreover, ion adsorption depends on the polarization of ions. In light of this, electrodes should have pore sizes corresponding to the sizes of anions and cations.

Even though we have not analyzed pore sizes explicitly in this study, we have considered the effects of pore sizes on the electrochemical properties through their approximate value ranges and number densities. Figure 9 shows a statistical representation of the different levels of porosity with a varying peak value for certain sizes of pores. The Pauling radius for Na+ ions is 0.95 Å and Cl− is 1.81 Å. The diameter of H2O molecules is 1.38 Å. Considering the hydration shell surrounding the ions, the total diameter would be around 3.7 and 4.57 Å. For 5% porosity, most pores are approximately 3–4 Å, for 10% porosity they are 4–5 Å, for 20% they are 5–6 Å and for 30% they are at 6–7 Å. This indicates that the average pore sizes created through random porosity generation are organized to facilitate electrolyte adsorption. As the hydration shells have been stripped, each optimum pore could store more than one ion. We have shown this in Figure 9 for four different pore sizes (3.5 Å, 4.5 Å, 5.5 Å and 6.5 Å). These pores can adsorb and release ions and until the ions reach an equilibrium state in the simulation, pores are in motion. In that sense, they can be hypothesized as open-ended wells, closing after reaching an equilibrium state. Their migration behavior is influenced by the corrugated nature of the crumpled graphene surface and the chirality of carbon in the graphene sheet.62 It has also been reported that migration occurs preferentially due to the positive curvature of graphene.63 Here, 3.5 Å pores can store one ion, 4.5 Å two ions, and both 5.5 and 6.5 Å pores can store three ions in theory. This is indicated in the number of instances being decreased with increasing pore size, while the number of ions in each pore increased. However, as the porosity increased above 20%, the efficiency, and the surface area of adsorption per ion decreased. Thus, it is evident that sub-nanometer pores in the range of 4–5 Å are ideal for NaCl electrolytes in the crumpled graphene electrode model.

The potential distribution profiles for various porosities, shown in Figure 10b, exhibit a similar trend to those in Figure 7a. In both cases, the same parameters influence the electric potential, with Uion being the most significant factor. As porosity in the electrodes increases from 5% to 20%, a decline in potential is observed, followed by an increase when porosity reaches 30%. The introduction of porosity in the crumpled graphene shortens the ion diffusion path and boosts ion accumulation inside the electrodes. This gradually reduces the electric potential up to a certain threshold (20% porosity). Beyond this point, the ion accumulation sharply decreases due to the loss of SSA. Consequently, this causes an overall increase in the total potential, Utotal, as depicted for the case of 30% porosity in Figure 10b.

The EDL thickness and partition coefficients are illustrated in Figure 11a. A monotonic rise in double-layer thickness is found with increasing porosity levels. Fewer pores give a smaller area to accommodate, producing a thinner double layer. The thinnest double layer is formed at a 5% porosity level, having a magnitude of 39.5 Å; whereas the case of 20% porosity has a magnitude of 41.5 Å. The partition coefficient is another significant parameter to investigate the porosity effect on ion accumulation, defined as the ratio of the ionic concentration in the pore (Cpore) to the ionic concentration in bulk (Cbulk).64 The optimal performance of EDLC is achieved when the pores are neither too large nor too small for the electrolyte ions, which is found at 20% porosity in the current study. As a result of the larger pore size, the lowest value of the partition coefficient is obtained at 30% porosity. A comparison of supercapacitor performance at various porosity levels is presented in Figure 11b. It can be seen that the case with 20% porosity has the most prominent specific capacitance compared to all other models, which also showed the highest value of partition coefficient. It has about 21% higher capacitance compared to the crumpled electrode with no in-plane pores (16.4 μF/cm2) and is the most optimized form of the crumpled EDLC model (19.8 μF/cm2) within the parametric space of this study. Variation in SSA and overall surface charge are the two major contributing factors behind this outcome. First, SSA increases significantly with increasing porosity, but after a certain porosity (>20% for this study) the SASA starts to decrease, especially when the pore size becomes larger than the optimum one (>6 Å). Second, the per-atom constant charge method together with increasing porosity results in lower surface charge due to atom loss, contributing to a loss in capacitance value. Up to 20% porosity, the effect of increasing SSA outweighs the latter one and therefore the specific capacitance exceeds the aforementioned crumpled model (with no porosity); except for the last case (30% porosity). In this case, the adverse effect of SSA and lower surface charge combine, resulting in a substantial decrease in capacitance as indicated in Figure 11b. It is also evident from Figures 6 and 11b that the crumpling effect far outweighs the effect of porosity as it causes a 400% increase in specific capacitance over planar graphene compared to a maximum increase of 21% achieved by increasing the porosity.

Although no physical experiments have been conducted as a part of this work, studies containing an experimental setup similar to our simulation model have been located and analyzed for potential validation and testing. Parviz et al. converted 2D pristine graphene sheets into a crumpled 3D morphology, offering a method applicable for generating crumpled graphene sheets from dispersions.65 Similarly, in our model, we could aim to produce nanometer-scale droplets of dispersion instead of micrometer-sized ones through the sonication-centrifuge-spray drying method described in their article. Tang et al. employed two additional techniques to create porous crumpled graphene (PCG) structure,66,67 with one method focusing on PCG with micropores for supercapacitor design, demonstrating significant utilization of surface area and capacitance which is very similar to our study. Their output of 20.7 μF/cm2 is close to the theoretical value of 20.9 μF/cm2. Following a similar protocol to theirs, we could use an ultrasonic atomizer and heated furnace to produce the crumpled graphene sheet. The next step would be finding a cost-effective method to apply this concept for real-world applications. For that purpose, designing the supercapacitor in a compact way with maximum SASA would be necessary. Although curved graphene has been used for commercial supercapacitors already, the crumpled electrode model with optimum porosity could augment their performance even further.

Finally, the electrochemistry of crumpled graphene supercapacitors can be explained by leveraging the concept of quantum capacitance. Due to the folding of crumpled layers in graphene, there is an enhancement in quantum capacitance value Cq due to graphite-like stacking, which causes the hybridization of π-states between consecutive layers, triggering the quantum hall effect.68 Moreover, it has also been established that increasing graphene layers causes the effect of quantum capacitance on total capacitance to decrease.69 It would be interesting to study quantum capacitance on our crumpled graphene model. Future studies could analyze the model with various electrolyte systems through experiments and DFT calculations to gain a comprehensive insight into their quantum electrochemical properties.

Conclusion

In this study, we have modeled a supercapacitor with crumpled graphene electrodes in an aqueous electrolyte and comprehensively analyzed the performance parameters via MD simulations. We have shown that by crumpling the graphene, a significantly enhanced electrode–electrolyte interaction is achieved due to the higher accessible surface area. Quantitatively, the completely crumpled graphene exhibits a remarkable 400% greater capacitance (16.4 μF/cm2) compared to the conventional planar graphene. We have extended this finding into optimized models by including in-plane pores onto the crumpled graphene electrodes. It was found that the addition of pores can affect the EDLC performance and the inclusion of about 20% porosity in the electrode resulted in specific capacitance as high as 19.8 μF/cm2. This improvement can be attributed to additional adsorption sites leading to improved electrode–electrolyte interaction, which also minimizes the energy penalty for the ions to remove the hydration shell, matching the ion size with the pore size. However, there is an optimum level of porosity beyond which the addition of further pores has a detrimental effect because of the reduced SASA, leading to lower charge accumulation. This entire study can be leveraged in designing new electric vehicles in combination with Lithium-Ion batteries and crumpled graphene electrodes. Combining batteries and supercapacitors based on crumpled graphene electrodes can bring the best of both worlds while overcoming the limitations of poor charging and lower energy capacity.
