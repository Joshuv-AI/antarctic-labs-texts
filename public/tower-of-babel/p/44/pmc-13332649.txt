Frozen Leaflet During Transcatheter Balloon-Expandable Aortic Valve Implantation

ABSTRACT

The occurrence of frozen leaflet, also known as stuck leaflet, after transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is a rare but potentially fatal complication. It consists of hypomobility or immobility of one or more of the 3 prosthesis leaflets, leading to massive intra-aortic regurgitation with possible subsequent hemodynamic instability and cardiogenic shock, often requiring mechanical circulatory support. Its reported incidence ranges between 0.1% and 0.4%.1,2 The first case was reported in 2011 by Al-Attar et al3, describing an immediately post-implantation severe intravalvular leak, observed on transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE), due to an immobile cusp associated with left ventricular distension and cardiovascular collapse after transapical TAVI with a 23-mm Edwards SAPIEN valve. The case resulted in a fatal outcome. Subsequently, in 2012, Agostoni et al4 described a new case of frozen leaflet after TAVI, promptly resolved using intracardiac echocardiography and the additional implantation of a valve-in-valve (ViV) prosthesis. A handful of further cases have been reported in the literature,5, 6, 7 including 2 involving all 3 leaflets after balloon-expandable valve implantation,8,9 and other 2 cases following self-expandable valve implantation.10,11 Timely recognition of this complication is crucial in order to prevent hemodynamic instability and adopt appropriate solutions without any delay. Given its potential to precipitate catastrophic hemodynamic collapse, prompt adoption of appropriate rescue strategies is paramount for the interventional cardiologist. In the present case report, we describe and analyze a case of intraprocedural frozen leaflet occurring during TAVI with a balloon-expandable valve.

Case Presentation

History of presentation and past medical history

An 83-year-old man, symptomatic for exertional dyspnea, was referred to the Cardiology Unit of Santa Maria delle Croci Hospital in Ravenna, Italy, for severe aortic stenosis, for which he was being followed up with echocardiographic monitoring. He was a former smoker and had a history of type 2 diabetes, arterial hypertension, and dyslipidemia. He also suffered from chronic kidney disease and hypothyroidism, treated with supplemental pharmacological therapy. His history was positive for a previous episode of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and multiple myeloma, currently treated with lenalidomide under hematologic follow-up. His weight was 71 kg, height 170 cm, and body mass index 24.6.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a normal left ventricular ejection fraction (65%), severe hypertrophy with a predominantly concentric pattern, mild left atrial dilatation, and normal aortic root dimensions. Severe calcification of the aortic valve apparatus was noted, with an aortic valve area of 0.87 cm2, a peak velocity of 4.2 m/s, and maximum and mean transvalvular gradients of 65 and 43 mm Hg, respectively, with mild aortic regurgitation (Figure 1A). Coronary angiography demonstrated diffuse atheromasia without significant obstructive coronary lesions (Figure 1B). Computed tomography showed an aortic annulus area of 644.6 mm2, a perimeter of 91 mm, and minimum and maximum diameters of 26.1 mm and 32.5 mm, respectively. The coronary heights were 13.7 mm for the left coronary artery and 24.1 mm for the right coronary artery (Figure 2). The EuroSCORE II was 2.48%.

Management

After multidisciplinary discussion within the heart team and considering the patient's age and comorbidities, the decision was made to proceed with TAVI. The procedure was planned via a right transfemoral approach and secondary left transradial approach under deep analgosedation. After placement of a super-stiff wire in the left ventricle, balloon predilation was performed using a 23 × 40-mm balloon (Figure 3A). Following deployment of a 26-mm balloon-expandable Myval Octapro valve under rapid percutaneous pacing (Figure 3B), severe intraprosthetic regurgitation was detected by angiography (Sellers' grade 3) (Figure 3C). TEE revealed reduced motion of the prosthetic leaflet corresponding to the right cusp, consistent with frozen leaflet, associated with severe intraprosthetic regurgitation (Figure 4). Hemodynamic parameters remained stable without the need for circulatory support or inotropic agents. First, manipulation of the implanted valve was attempted using the pigtail in an effort to restore the normal motion of the immobilized leaflet. Second, prompt postdilation with a 25-mm balloon was performed, without improvement (Figure 3D). Therefore, the decision was made to implant a second valve. A ViV procedure was then performed using a second 26-mm Myval valve (Figure 3E). Post–ViV aortography and TEE demonstrated no paravalvular leak or residual aortic regurgitation and a proper leaflet coaptation (Figures 3F and 4F). Hemostasis of the main femoral access site was achieved using one Perclose device and one Angio-Seal, while the secondary left radial access was managed with a pressure bandage. At the end of the procedure, a complete left bundle branch block occurred and persisted, but without significant pauses or the need for pacemaker implantation. Three days after the procedure, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a mean transvalvular gradient of 5 mm Hg, with no evidence of paravalvular leak. The patient was discharged on the ninth postoperative day.

Discussion

The present case reports the occurrence of frozen leaflet during balloon-expandable aortic valve implantation, representing the first case involving the Myval Octapro device documented in the literature. The occurrence of frozen leaflets has been more frequently associated with balloon-expandable valves.3, 4, 5,9 Over the years, multiple mechanisms have been hypothesized as potential contributors to this phenomenon:12

6.Valve postdilation performed on an incompletely expanded or deformed prosthesis, which may result in leaflet crushing.7,9

A careful preprocedural planning strategy could help mitigate this risk, particularly through the identification of severe calcification, which may predispose to this complication, and the adoption of appropriate predilation before valve implantation. From a diagnostic perspective, TEE plays a key role in identifying the precise location of regurgitation while minimizing radiation exposure. Although angiography provides an immediate assessment, it increases radiation dose and fails to reliably distinguish between intravalvular and paravalvular regurgitation.

Several therapeutic approaches have been described. Most cases reported in the literature involved significant hemodynamic compromise, necessitating ViV implantation. An exception was the recent report of a Portico valve case in 2024,10 in which leaflet mobility was restored with two balloon postdilation maneuvers, thus avoiding ViV implantation. Similarly, a second case of frozen leaflet occurring during ViV implantation with a self-expandable Evolut R prosthesis was successfully managed by mobilization using a pigtail catheter.11 A practical management algorithm to cope with this complication during TAVI has been proposed previously,12 and a complete diagram is shown in the Visual summary. If hemodynamics are stable, operators may attempt repositioning of the stiff guidewire, increasing systolic blood pressure, or gently manipulating the leaflet with a pigtail catheter. Balloon postdilation may also be considered. If hemodynamics are unstable, immediate circulatory support should be established, followed by urgent ViV implantation.6

In the case described, the implantation was performed without issues, and an appropriate pre-dilatation had been carried out. Therefore, causes related to the implantation technique are not assumed. The most likely hypothesis is instead related to a manufacturing defect or an incorrect preparation of the valve. One of the most critical phases in TAVI is the crimping process (for balloon-expandable valves), since the leaflets might be damaged during this phase. For this reason, applying an excessive crimping force is strongly discouraged.12

The principal limitation in providing explanations to this phenomenon is the rarity of the condition. The small number of cases described so far prevents any reliable estimation of its true incidence. Conclusions

Frozen or stuck leaflet during TAVI is a rare but potentially life-threatening event. Early recognition and rapid hemodynamic assessment are critical to restoring leaflet mobility and maintaining procedural safety. Incorporating TEE can facilitate intraprocedural detection by accurately identifying the mechanism and location of dysfunction while minimizing radiation exposure. Adoption of a structured intervention algorithm and timely multidisciplinary involvement is essential to optimize outcomes in this high-risk situation.
