Association between clinical learning portfolio recording and medical students’ competencies and entrustable professional activities in Taiwan
Authors: Jen-Feng Liang, Wan-Yu Yeh, Shiau-Shian Huang, Hsiao-Yun Yeh, Hsiao-Chin Shen, Chia-Chang Huang, Yi-Hsuan Lin, Hao-Min Cheng, Ying-Ying Yang, Jiing-Feng Lirng, Chen-Huan Chen, Shuu-Jiun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The clinical reflection-oriented learning portfolio is widely applied in clinical rotation, but it is time-consuming for clinical trainees, and the effect on learning is still questionable. This study aims to provide evidence of the effectiveness of the learning portfolio by examining the association between portfolio appraisal and clinical performance.Methods:From 2020 to 2022, data were evaluated to investigate the relationship between excellent portfolio award status, proficiency in entrustable professional activities (EPAs)–based assessments, and clinical competencies based on the core EPAs for entering residency of the American Association of Medical Colleges (AAMC), as well as eight core competencies evaluated every 2 weeks from each clinical department. In addition, clerks who continued their second-year clerkship at the research hospital (continuous follow-up group) were analyzed to further explore the association between first-year award status and second-year clinical outcomes.Results:A total of 148 first-year clerks were included in the overall group (award recipients and non-recipients). Among them, 86 continued their clerkship at the research hospital in the second year (continuous follow-up group). In the overall group, compared with excellent learning portfolio non-recipients, award recipients demonstrated higher scores in 11 of the 13 EPAs and in all eight core competencies. Moreover, higher-frequency award winners obtained significantly higher scores compared to lower-frequency award winners. Stratified analyses based on gender yielded similar results during clerkship. However, this beneficial association was not observed in the continuous follow-up group.Conclusion:This database study supports that better learning portfolio recording is associated with better clinical performance during the early stage of clerkship. Learning portfolio could be an effective tool to facilitate clinical learning in early clerkship.

1. INTRODUCTION

Recent times have seen the utilization of learning portfolios as pedagogical and evaluative instruments in clinical medical education become increasingly popular. This is primarily due to the trend toward competency-based medical education (CBME),1 which highlights the importance of rigorous trainee evaluation. Learning portfolios, widely used in clinical rotation-based learning, are designed to actively engage students in clinical practice and document their involvement, observations, reflections, and instructional feedback,1,2 thereby providing those students’ clinical instructors and mentors with an opportunity to help conduct a comprehensive evaluation of their professional development.1,3 Exploring the potential benefits of learning portfolios in education, as discussed in past literature, encourages students to critically assess their learning progress and engage in reflective practices. This fosters heightened self-awareness regarding the effectiveness of their learning methods, motivating adjustments for improvement. Additionally, portfolio construction necessitates the synthesis of knowledge, reflective judgment, and articulation of practical applications, promoting core thinking and professional discernment. Mentors’ observations and feedback are crucial in assessing students’ portfolio-based learning gains, allowing educators to offer personalized guidance and support for self-regulated learning trajectories.3–5

However, some scholars consider that the implementation of learning portfolios can potentially yield deleterious ramifications in the clinical setting. Recording and evaluating the learning portfolio impose burdens on both busy clinical trainees and teachers, and the students may respond in a manner aligned with the teacher’s expectations, rather than expressing their authentic perspectives.3 A recent study, utilizing content analysis of focus group interviews with stakeholders in general practitioner specialty training, raised concerns regarding the efficacy of learning portfolios.6 The skepticism stemmed from the belief that the close mentorship inherent in clinical education renders portfolios redundant, and that evaluative emphasis on specific portfolio elements may impede autonomous learning6; this echoes past student criticism of the implementation of learning portfolios.7 Additional studies indicate that reflective learning portfolios have encountered skepticism among both students and educators.5,8 The obligatory nature of reflection in the learning process has been called into question alongside the inadequacy of the instructional and evaluative approach pertaining to the composition of the learning portfolio, and reflection is usually time-consuming, which may weaken learners’ motivation.5,8 Do learning portfolios really help clinical trainees in learning, and do the benefits outweigh the disadvantages? How should we design and apply learning portfolios to facilitate learning, and how can we encourage the recording of such learning portfolios? For educational administrators, the evidence regarding the effect of learning portfolios is critical when it comes to curriculum planning. However, current evidence supporting their efficacy, especially in general practice, remains limited.

This study aims to evaluate the relationship between learning portfolio appraisal and clinical learning performance in order to determine the use of a reflection-oriented learning portfolio as a learning tool in terms of validity evidence. This study investigates disparities in students’ clinical learning performance between recipients and non-recipients of the “Excellent Learning Portfolio Award.” Student data from the 2-year clerkship period were analyzed annually to assess the correlation between learning portfolio appraisal and clinical performance at different stages.

2. METHODS

2.1. Ethics statement

The data files from the Teaching Assessment System (TAS)—which were used during the conducting of the research analyses—contained de-identified data and no personally identifiable information regarding the students. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital (IRB 2022-08-014A and 2023-08-008AC).

2.2. Study design

This is a retrospective cohort study that uses an administrative database, provided by the department of medical education, in a teaching hospital, the TAS. The database records students’ status during their 2-year clerkship in Taiwan’s medical education system, including monthly learning portfolio recordings based on student and clinical unit mentor feedback (the research flowchart is shown in Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of the study participants.

2.3. Setting

In Taiwan, the MD program is mainly a 6-year curriculum that admits high school graduates, with the first 4 years dedicated to core medical courses and the latter 2 years to clinical clerkships (clinical rotation in teaching hospitals). The first year (fifth grade, clerk-1) includes the core, obligatory courses, such as internal medicine, and usually takes place in the main affiliated teaching hospital. The courses in the second year (sixth grade, clerk-2 or subinternship) are more flexible, elective courses and may be completed in different teaching hospitals. Upon completion of the 2-year clerkship, medical students will receive the MD degree and start their 2-year postgraduate training, followed by 3 to 6 years’ specialty training. The research hospital, a national medical center and teaching hospital, offers tertiary patient care, PGY programs, and residency training in all specialties; it is also the main teaching hospital of a public medical school.

In Taiwan, nearly all teaching hospitals utilize learning portfolios to assess medical students’ educational progress. Besides the evaluation during clerkship, the record condition of learning portfolios is also reviewed for the purpose of PGY training matching in many teaching hospitals. However, there is no nationwide “standard” or official learning portfolio form. In the study hospital, the learning portfolio is electronic (e-portfolio) and was established in the hospital’s information system in 2014. Students rotate through clinical departments every 2 weeks, submitting a portfolio upon completion of the course. The framework and contents of the e-portfolio are developed by a task group in the hospital. The portfolio contains several sections: (1) clinical case records, including outpatient department and inpatient care (case log), and summarized experiences and reflection for selected cases; (2) self-evaluation before and after clinical rotation; (3) the record of clinical evaluation, such as mini-CEX, case-based discussion, and direct observation of procedural skills (DOPS); (4) overall feedback as well as suggestions to the clinical department/courses, and (5) the feedback from clinical instructors and the mentor (example provided in Supplementary Table S1, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A353).

Because each medical student will rotate among more than 20 departments during the first year of clerkship, the content within learning portfolios is substantial. Considering the feasibility of the excellent learning portfolios review process, the students’ work is first preliminarily selected by four teaching assistants according to the assigned clinical departments. They each select the top 20% of the learning portfolio works during clinical rotations, with richer content, to enter the second round of selection. The second stage involves reviewing the quality of the content. The content is then reviewed by department-specific program directors, who are in charge of curriculum planning and implementation. Regular discussions among program directors from various departments ensure consensus on the final list of award recipients. Only half (10%) of the first screened portfolios were deemed excellent. The main criteria of an excellent learning portfolio include the quality of reflection (70%) and the abundance of learning journey recording (30%). It has been posited that the award selection process reflects students’ commitment to portfolio completion and reinforces high-quality writing behavior. Additionally, program directors refrain from involvement in student grading during rotations.

2.4. Participants

The data utilized were collected from medical students who had begun their 2-year clerkship. However, some students transferred to other hospitals during their second year and did not receive the same clinical evaluation. Only data from their first year of clerkship at the hospital were included for analysis.

2.5. Study size

Fig. 1 shows the progression of participants throughout the study. Between October 2020 and May 2022, 148 medical students in their clerkship (overall group, including award recipients and non-recipients) were included for 1-year analysis of the association between a learning portfolio award and modified EPAs/clinical competencies. Additionally, to confirm the continuous association, 86 of them were followed until completion of the 2-year clerkship (continuous follow-up group).

2.6. Data sources/measurement

Throughout the 2-year clinical clerkship, students, clinical teachers, and mentors utilized the TAS platform for monthly learning portfolio recordings, the provision of bi-directional feedback, and for rating clinical performance. Developed by the research hospital in 2013, the TAS platform digitizes the learning and teaching processes, enables convenient operation and real-time communication between clinical teachers, mentors, and students, and has detailed features as described in the previous study.9

In efforts to enhance transparency in undergraduate medical education and ensure safe, high-quality patient care, the adoption of entrustable professional activities (EPAs) and core competencies outlined by the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) has gained traction among academics and practitioners.10–13 The clinical performance indicators of this study incorporate the concepts of EPAs and core competences,12,14 to facilitate a multifaceted exploration of the correlation between learning portfolios and students’ learning effectiveness during their clerkship. The 13 EPA items in this study were developed based on the 13 core EPAs for entering residency proposed by the AAMC and on scores of the eight core competencies that were embedded in each of the 13 EPAs.12 According to the Curriculum Developers’ Guide issued by the AAMC,12 the abbreviations and corresponding full names of the 13 EPAs and eight core competences cited in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S2, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A353. The weight scores and summarized scores of each core competency in every EPA among the 13 EPAs are presented in Supplementary Table S3, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A353.

To accommodate busy clinical teachers, a modified AAMC measurement scale was developed and approved by our institution’s educational committee and nationwide experts. This scale comprises 13 EPA-based assessments and a global assessment score. The modified–EPA assessments were graded on a five-point Likert scale: 1 = No improvement despite reminders or guidance; no improvement, 2 = Some improvement, but not ideal, despite reminders or guidance; slight improvement, 3 = Task can be completed with reminders or guidance; completion with guidance, 4 = Can complete task independently and meet expectations; proactive completion meeting expectations, and 5 = Excellent, exceeding the abilities of a UGY medical student. The scores represented the relative performance among colleagues rather than the milestone-like readiness of the students. Compared with the original AAMC EPA assessment, the modified-EPA assessment still divides students’ abilities into five levels, but revises the lower-level levels 1 and 2, and cooperates with mentors to differentiate students based on whether they show improvement after receiving reminders or guidance during the rotation (1: no improvement, 2: some improvement). This makes it possible to be more in line with the actual situation of clinical clerkship guidance. For a more detailed measurement method establishment process, please refer to another study published by the hospital.15

After obtaining student scores from the 13 modified EPA assessments, the weighted scores of the eight core competencies indicated in Supplementary Table S3, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A353, were converted into the evaluation results of each core competency. Additionally, the global assessment score, based on a 0 to 100 scale, assessed students’ overall clinical learning performance throughout their first and second clerkships.

2.7. Variables

The variables used in this study included: students’ gender, age, time of participation in clerkship, unit of clerkship rotation, whether and the number of awards for “Excellent learning portfolio,” evaluation scores at each clerkship unit (global assessment scores, 13 EPA item scores provided by mentors at each clerkship unit, and eight core competences scores converted by the 13 EPAs in this study). Throughout the 2-year clerkship, multiple assessments are administered throughout each clinical rotation, with the average score used for analysis.

2.8. Statistical methods

Data analysis was undertaken using IBM SPSS software version 20. Descriptive statistics (n/% for categorical variables, mean/SD for continuous variables) depicted gender and age distribution during fifth-year clerkship and sixth-year clerkship (age upon entering clerkship). “Excellent learning portfolio” awards during clerkship ranged from 0 to 14. Students were categorized into three award frequency groups—namely, none, ≤3 times, and ≥4 times—to explore dose-effect relationships with EPAs and core competences. The Kruskal-Wallis test, a non-parametric analysis method that does not assume normality, compared award status across variables, while chi-squared tests assessed gender differences and award status. A gender-stratified analysis was also conducted to explore potential gender differences. A p value of ≤0.05 indicated statistical significance.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Participants

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the study population. In the first year of clerkship, there were 148 clerkship medical students (overall group), with an average age of 25.3 years, 62.8% of whom were male. The gender distribution of the subgroup of 86 medical students (continuous follow-up group) followed until the end of the second year of clerkship mirrored that of the overall group. Approximately 40% of the overall group received the excellent learning portfolio award at least once. Award recipients in both groups were categorized by award frequency: less than three times or more than four times. While not statistically significant, a higher proportion of male students received the award more than four times compared to female students, with a higher proportion of female students receiving it <3 times. Comparisons between students staying in our hospital for the second-year clerkship and those leaving revealed no significant differences in gender, age, or mentors’ global assessment scores.

4. DISCUSSION

4.1. Key results

This study provides evidence regarding the effectiveness of learning portfolios by teaching database analysis. The results reveal positive correlations between excellent portfolio awards, EPA scores, and core competencies among medical students during their first year of clerkship. However, these correlations diminish in the second year of clerkship.

4.2. Interpretation/comparison with previous studies

Our research results reveal that the scores of twelve of the 13 EPAs—the exception was EPA1 (gather a history and perform a physical examination)—exhibited a positive association with better learning portfolio recording (award-winning). The results support the role of learning portfolios in clinical rotation and echo the previous literature’s contention that, through clear instructions, appropriate guidance, and feedback, the operation process of learning portfolios can help students grow.1,2,4 The results are also similar to the findings of relatively rare quantitative studies.1 Indeed, throughout the process of completing the learning portfolio, including reflection and recording, clinical trainees might enhance their abilities in empirical translation and practice-based learning and improvement, which is also helpful for professional self-concept development.

The traditional impression indicates that female students are usually more patient and better when it comes to recording-based assignments. Thus, we conducted a gender-stratified analysis to determine whether there is a difference regarding the effects of learning portfolios between genders. The results in female trainees exhibited a trend similar to that seen with males or the overall group, albeit less statistically significant, which might be due to the smaller number of female students after gender classification. In summary, no gender differences were observed based on our data.

In our study, we used the award-winning element as proof of reflection quality in learning portfolios. We must admit that the positive result for learning in this study stems not only from completing the reflection (portfolio) itself, but also from the effect of the award and recognition. Drawing on self-determination theory (SDT), extrinsic motivators such as forced reflection, mentor input, and public recognition for excellent performers inside the learning portfolio system promote positive progress among new clinical learners. According to SDT, the learning environment, psychological needs, and self-determination motivation all impact students’ educational experiences. As learners’ psychological needs are addressed in the learning environment, their self-determination grows, resulting in the internalization of motivation into intrinsic forms. Learning addresses three basic psychological needs: competence, relatedness, and autonomy. Extrinsic motivation refers to extrinsic incentives that induce positive learning behaviors, which, once internalized, turn into intrinsic motivation, whereby learning becomes intrinsically gratifying.16 This study supports the premise that, when using learning portfolios to assist students in developing self-regulation, the complexity of students’ motivation and behavioral changes during the learning process must be recognized.5,6

An interesting effect noticed in this study is that, following the second year of clerkship, the positive relationship between award-winning and clinical advancement could not be observed despite constant mentorship and grading techniques. Of course, this is possibly simply due to the decreased number of students included in the second year. However, there are several possible explanations. First, the reflection activities facilitated by completing the portfolio are truly only beneficial for the very early clinical trainees, or, second, the EPAs we evaluated across the whole clerkship are relatively basic and could not adequately represent the benefit of learning portfolios in the second year. Another explanation is the declining incentive effects of the learning portfolio award, which were only awarded during the first year of clerkship. In summary, our results indicate that learning portfolios are likely more beneficial during the basic stage of clinical rotation. Program directors should mind this effect and adjust the policy or rule regarding the portfolio accordingly.

4.3. Future research

This study has some limitations. First, excellent learning portfolios were determined by a review committee, yet inter-rater agreement among evaluators was not fully assessed despite clear selection criteria. Second, the fact that the data were sourced from a single teaching hospital may have limited generalizability. However, this study utilized a teaching database to minimize selection bias, with student clinical learning data obtained from the teaching department to ensure data integrity. The clinical performance of medical students on the 13 EPAs was averaged based on mentor ratings across different clerkship units, which helped reduce variations in individual raters’ scoring preferences and mitigate potential deviations from students’ actual performance. Additionally, while the results indicate a correlation between learning portfolio performance and clinical outcomes, we cannot assert that the observed benefits are solely attributable to the portfolio itself. Some arguments claim that the “good” students usually tend to complete any assignment (e.g., portfolio, in this study) and the better clinical performance is caused by students themselves rather than the portfolio. Nonetheless, we could not fully confirm this assertion in a clinical setting. Indeed, many remarkable clerks still complained about the paperwork overload related to portfolios and claimed they would rather spend their valuable time undertaking clinical activities. In fact, this is why the current study is designed to examine whether portfolios do help clinical trainees. Future research should combine quantitative and qualitative approaches to explore learning portfolio applications more deeply, bolstering empirical support for widespread adoption in clinical medical education. For example, due to differences in the order of clinical clerkship rotation, students’ learning journeys are quite diverse. Resultantly, their performance in learning portfolio recording may also vary. In addition, this study only represents the averages of overall courses, with no consideration given to individual differences in clinical instructors or learning content. Nevertheless, this study still contributes some knowledge regarding the association between portfolio recording and general competencies development.

In conclusion, this study’s findings support the notion that better learning portfolio recording is associated with better clinical performance in terms of core competencies and EPAs among early stages of clerkship, regardless of the gender of students. The results support the usefulness of the reflection-oriented learning portfolios for the beginner stages of clerkship and lead to the conclusion that the program directors may confidently encourage the medical students to record portfolios during the first year of clinical rotation. However, how long this favorable relationship will last is still questionable. Further study focusing on the effectiveness of learning portfolios is needed to provide more evidence and instruction regarding learning portfolio application.
