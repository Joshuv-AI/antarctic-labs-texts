Negative-sense RNA virus nucleocapsid as a versatile platform for gene delivery, vaccine development, and antiviral screening
Authors: Sheeba Rehman, Pruthviraj M Chavan, Akanksha Chaturvedi, Debasis Nayak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractViruses typically infiltrate host cells via specialized cellular receptors, a pivotal step that is challenging when suitable permissive cell lines or hosts are unavailable. Leveraging the unique internal replication machinery of negative-stranded (ns) RNA viruses, we demonstrate that purified nucleocapsid (NCs), their functional genomic templates, can directly initiate a complete viral life cycle upon intracellular delivery, bypassing conventional cell surface interaction. We successfully purified vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) NCs, including those from a G-deleted pseudotyped variant (rVSV-ΔG-TFP). Exogenous delivery of these NCs into cells stimulated gene expression and generated infectious progeny virions (from full-length NCs). Critically, VSV-ΔG-TFP NCs, though self-amplifying, are inherently non-infectious, offering a safer, potentially more effective alternative for a vaccine development platform compared to live/attenuated viruses or mRNA-based systems. Furthermore, these NCs provide a secure method for transporting components of highly pathogenic nsRNA viruses (e.g., Nipah virus), acting as an inherent self-deactivating feature against accidental exposure. This study establishes NCs as a novel, intrinsically safer, and self-amplifying platform for antiviral screening, vaccine development, gene delivery, and biosafety in pathogen research.Keywords: Rhabdovirus, VSV, Nucleocapsids, Gene delivery, Receptor-independent, Negative-stranded RNA viruses, Ribonucleoprotein complex purification

Introduction

The conventional understanding of viral infection posits a critical role for the interaction between viral particles and specific host cell surface receptors. Thus, making receptor-mediated viral entry notion generally indispensable for many viral infections1. However, investigating the complete viral life cycle can be challenging when appropriate entry points or susceptible cells are unavailable. While most viruses depend on surface interactions for genomic nucleic acid delivery, a unique characteristic of nsRNA viruses lies in their internal replication machinery. Unlike many other viruses that deliver a naked genome, the nsRNA viruses encapsulate their genetic material with an NC complex, also known as a ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex, which is a true functional template for the viral gene transcription and replication. A deeper understanding of viral behaviour and adaptability could emerge from exploring alternate mechanisms of infectious virus recovery, specifically the ability of these viral NCs to initiate gene expression and replication without the need for external cell surface recognition. VSV is a prototypic nsRNA virus that belongs to the Rhabdoviridae family2. Its genome is approximately 11 kilobases (Kb) and includes five genes: nucleocapsid (N), phosphoprotein (P), matrix (M), glycoprotein (G), and large polymerase protein (L)3,4. The low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) is the proven receptor for VSV5. Upon entering host cells, the VSV-RNPs, composed of viral genomic RNA, tightly encased by multiple copies of the N protein along with L protein and its essential cofactor the P protein, initiate viral genome transcription and replication, allowing the virus to complete its life cycle6–9.

Given the fundamental role of the RNPs in the ns-RNA virus life cycle and the desire to investigate viral life cycle bypassing the conventional surface interaction, we examined employing VSV RNPs to recover infectious virus in the absence of a typical point of cellular attachment. Our approach involved transfecting VSV RNPs into cells using a liposomal transfection reagent (Lipofectamine 2000). For this study, we purified RNPs from two VSV variants: one expressing enhanced green fluorescent protein (VSV-eGFP) and another, a pseudotyped VSV variant (rVSV-ΔG-TFP), where the G gene was replaced with teal fluorescent protein (TFP). The use of rVSV-ΔG-TFP was critical, as it lacks the native G (viral envelope protein), it cannot package into infectious viral particles, thereby restricting the viral life cycle to the nucleocapsid stage. The experimental control was necessary to clearly show that the inserted NCs were the sole component that could start the generation and spread of new infectious viruses, without any help from existing envelope proteins.

Using liposome-mediated transfection of NCs, we successfully recovered infectious virus in Vero cells as well as shRNA-mediated LDLR knockdown Vero cells. Delivery of NCs was achieved using a liposomal transfection reagent, an exogenous delivery strategy that bypasses the natural uptake pathway and critically demonstrates their ability to circumvent reliance on specific cell surface receptors. The liposomal reagents, designed to mimic natural cell membranes by acting like a lipid bilayer and even fuse with them, are widely favoured for their ease of use, affordability, and operational simplicity in delivering various biomolecules. However, applying this principle to the protein-based delivery of functional nucleocapsids into mammalian cells is still largely unexplored.

Our findings highlight the capability of NCs to generate infectious viruses by delivering their genomes to permissive cells, even in the absence of conventional receptor interactions. This novel delivery mechanism not only expands our understanding of possible alternate viral entry strategies but could also open a potential avenue for general virology research related to the discovery of antiviral therapies and candidate vaccines. Moreover, we demonstrate the versatility of these subviral complexes as a tool for gene delivery. As a proof of concept, we achieved in vitro eGFP expression initiated through genome transcription and replication. This technology offers an alternate platform for studying virus biology, including highly infectious BSL-3 and BSL-4 pathogens, while paving the way for innovative applications in virology and gene therapy.

Results and discussion

Generation and purification of VSV nucleocapsids: assessing purity and protein composition

The infectious cycle of nsRNA viruses, such as VSV, is fundamentally dependent on the NC complex, which serves as the minimal and sufficient unit for initiating viral genome transcription and replication within the host cells. For our investigation into non-canonical ways for the delivery of viral genomes and initiation of the viral infection cycle, it was critical to obtain highly purified NCs devoid of contaminating viral envelope proteins, particularly the glycoprotein (G), to ensure that any observed infectivity was solely attributable to the delivered NCs and not residual virions or their surface components.

To this end, NCs were isolated from two distinct VSV variants, which are VSV-eGFP and rVSV-ΔG-TFP. Following isolation, these purified NCs were introduced into recipient cells via the liposomal transfection method using Lipofectamine 2000, a reagent that is capable of delivering nucleic acids and protein complexes intracellularly.

The purity and protein composition of the isolated NCs were rigorously assessed by immunoblotting. Both intracellular (IC) and extracellular (EC) released VSV-EGFP virion particles were used to purify the NCs. The western blot was performed using the purified intracellular and extracellular NCs, as well as the VSV-EGFP cell lysate as a control. Our analysis specifically targeted the viral envelope glycoprotein, as its complete absence is crucial for exploring a non-canonical way of delivering viral NCs independent of engagement with cellular surface receptors. Western blot results clearly demonstrate no detectable signal for the 67-kilodalton (kDa) G protein in ultra-centrifuged NC fraction, as shown in Fig. 1C (lane IC and EC), while the positive control sample derived from whole virion lysate showed the desired band (Fig. 1C lane C). Furthermore, Western blot analysis also confirmed the presence of N protein in purified NC samples (Fig. 1C lane IC and EC), validating the purity of NCs. Thus, this process ensures that subsequent experimental design is solely attributable to the purified NCs.

Characterization and quantification of VSV nucleocapsids

Beyond verifying their purity, the morphological and biophysical characterization of purified VSV NCs is crucial to ascertain the structural integrity and homogeneity, factors directly impacting their functions upon intracellular delivery. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) was employed to visually assess the morphology of purified NCs. TEM images reveal distinct, uncondensed, and elongated structure characteristics of VSV NCs, exhibiting an average length of approximately 750 nm and diameters varying from 25 to 30 nm (Fig. 2A). To further characterize the physical properties, Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) was performed to determine the particle size distribution of purified NCs. DLS analysis indicated an average size of NCs to be 900 nm and a narrow polydispersity index (PDI) of − 9.46, which, despite the unusual negative value typically outside the conventional PDI range, in this context strongly suggests an exceptional uniform and monodisperse population of NCs (Fig. 2B,C). This high degree of homogeneity is critical for reproducible experimental outcomes. To precisely evaluate the effectiveness of delivering NCs and their subsequent gene expression, we used a quantitative approach. We started with a 10 ml volume of virus-containing supernatant from which NCs were purified, and the number of functional units was calculated by FACS analysis. We transfected Vero cells with serially diluted NCs using Lipofectamine 2000. By counting the number of green fluorescence-positive cells, we determined the concentration of the functional NCs. Our analysis showed that 6 × 108 functional NCs were recovered from known stock viral supernatant (which has been previously titered in plaque assay), resulting in a recovery rate of 75% from initial viral inputs. Similarly, the transfection efficiency was estimated to be 60% (Fig. 2D). This high transfection efficiency underscores the validity and functional competence of the purified NCs.

Functional assessment of purified VSV NCs in vitro

Having established the purity and structural integrity of the isolated VSV NCs, the next critical step was to confirm their functional competency: their ability to initiate the complete viral life cycle, culminating in the production of infectious progeny virions, upon intracellular delivery. To assess this, purified NCs were introduced into Vero cells using Lipofectamine 2000, bypassing the conventional receptor-mediated virus entry pathway.

Approximately 8 h post-transfection, the robust generation of progeny virions was observed, marked by the appearance and subsequent diffusion of eGFP fluorescence throughout the cell monolayer (Fig. 3). Critically, the control tests affirmed the necessity of the transfection reagent for viral initiation. Cells transfected with NCs without Lipofectamine reagent revealed no detectable eGFP expression. These results definitively confirm that the purified NCs are inherently incapable of initiating the cellular entry and attachment process on their own, underscoring that their functional activity observed herein is solely a consequence of their direct intracellular delivery. Furthermore, NCs derived from both mature, cell-free virions and those extracted from cell-associated viruses demonstrate identical eGFP expression patterns, indicating consistent functional activity.

Impact of LDLR knockdown on VSV nucleocapsid delivery and replication.

To unequivocally demonstrate that NC-mediated infection bypasses conventional entry pathways, we investigated the effect of silencing the known VSV entry receptor, LDLR, on NC delivery and subsequent viral replication.

First, the efficiency of shRNA-mediated knockdown of LDLR in Vero cells was confirmed by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qPCR), which showed a significant reduction in LDLR mRNA levels compared to control cells (Fig. 4C). To validate the functional impact of this knockdown on conventional viral entry, LDLR-knockdown cells were infected with a low multiplicity of infection (MOI) (0.01) of VSV pseudovirus (rVSV-ΔG-TFP, which lacks the essential viral envelope glycoprotein and is inherently unable to produce enveloped virions). This design restricts its replication to primary infected cells, preventing natural spread and allowing precise control over infection initiation. As expected, a markedly lower fluorescence signal was observed in LDLR-knockdown cells compared to control cells (Fig. 4D), confirming that canonical VSV infection is indeed dependent on LDLR. Upon transfection into Vero cells, the pseudotyped rVSV-ΔG-TFP reveals a characteristic punctate pattern of TFP fluorescence, limited to primary cells due to the single-cycle replication competence of this viral vector. However, trans-complementation, via co-transfection with VSV-G protein-expressing plasmid, enabled efficient virion to spread, confirmed by propagation of eGFP-expressing cells (used as a marker for secondary infection by packaged virions) and progressive increase in GFP expression in neighbouring cells (Fig. 4A). Evaluation of antiviral drug screening using nucleocapsids and doxycycline

A robust, NC-mediated viral replication system has the potential to serve as a direct platform for screening antiviral drugs targeting viral replication and transcription inhibitors. Given the prior report indicating doxycycline’s antiviral activity against VSV10, we investigated its inhibitory effect directly on de novo VSV replication initiation by purified NCs in vitro. Our findings demonstrate that doxycycline significantly inhibited VSV replication originating from transfected NCs in a clear dose-dependent manner. This inhibition was quantitatively evidenced by a marked reduction in both viral gene expression, as assessed by fluorescence signal, and the production of infectious progeny virions, as measured by viral titres, following doxycycline treatment (Fig. 5). These results not only confirm the antiviral efficacy of doxycycline in an NC-transfection model but, more importantly, validate our NC-based system as a valuable and efficient tool for screening antiviral compounds that specifically target the intracellular replication machinery of nsRNA viruses. The reproducibility of this effect across multiple concentrations suggests that the doxycycline, though commonly considered inert in mammalian cells without engineered tetracycline-responsive elements, can directly or indirectly interfere with VSV replication.

The above experiments, notably, affirm that the NCs of the rVSV-ΔG-TFP, while capable of self-replication and self-amplification upon exogenous delivery into cells, are inherently unable to produce infectious viral particles due to the deletion of the viral envelope glycoprotein. This characteristic makes them an intrinsically safer alternative compared to attenuated or live viruses for antigen delivery/vaccines. Furthermore, unlike mRNA vaccines, where the duration and expression of protein antigen are limited by shorter mRNA half-life, the self-amplifying nature of ΔG NCs could enable sustained gene expression, leading to higher yields of target antigens (e.g., TFP) or viral mRNA. Therefore, the use of NCs represents a promising and potentially safer approach for vaccine development and gene delivery, offering a smarter strategy with reduced inherent biohazard risks. Beyond vaccine development, this technology also offers a safer approach for handling highly pathogenic viruses of the nsRNA genome. The NCs, being the functional core capable of producing infectious virus particles only upon successful intracellular delivery, are themselves incapable of attaching to or entering cells independently. This provides a crucial biosafety advantage: for highly pathogenic zoonotic viruses like Nipah virus, whose physical transport as live virus poses risk, such as accidental spillage. Transporting their functional NCs significantly mitigates biohazard concerns. In the event of an accidental spillage, the isolated NCs cannot infect and would like to lose function (due to higher environmental temperature), acting as an inherent “kill-switch”. Thus, instead of transporting highly pathogenic live viruses, transport options for their NCs offer a smarter strategy, virtually eliminating accidental biohazard risks.

Our findings also reveal a dose-dependent inhibition of VSV gene expression by doxycycline, consistent with the previous report. Although the mechanism remains unclear, this observation is noteworthy given the frequent use of doxycycline in the mammalian system, where it is typically assumed to be inert without an engineered expression system. Future studies will be required to investigate the underlying mechanism of this drug to study the inhibitory effect on VSV gene expression.

In summary, this study demonstrates a novel approach for isolation and utility of viral NCs of nsRNA viruses, for recovery of infectious virus via the exogenous delivery of functional NCs, offering a promising, intrinsically safer, and self-amplifying platform for vaccine development, gene delivery, and secure handling of highly pathogenic viruses.

Methods

Cells

The Baby Hamster Kidney (Sigma Aldrich, India, BHK-21; clone C1008) and African green monkey epithelial cells (Sigma Aldrich, India, Vero cells; Clone 13) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 units of penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Virus and pseudovirus stock generation

The recombinant viruses were recovered in BHK-21 cells by co-transfecting plasmids expressing pVSV-FL and supporting plasmids4. Trans-complementation with a plasmid-carrying G protein restored the infectivity of rVSV-ΔG TFP, which was lacking the G gene11. The observation of eGFP or TFP fluorescence, as well as cytopathic effects, confirmed the effective recovery. To determine the number of recovered virus particles, the conventional plaque test was used12.

Isolation of NCs

Around 2.2 million Vero cells seeded in a 100 mm dish were infected with VSV-eGFP at 0.25 multiplicity of infection (MOI). Twenty-four hours post-infection, the cells were collected by centrifugation at 200 × g for 5 min at 4 °C and washed with ice-cold PBS. To initiate lysis, the cells were treated with 4 mL of Tris lysis buffer (comprising 50 mM Tris HCl at pH 8.0, 200 mM NaCl, 33% (vol/vol) glycerol, 20 mM imidazole, 0.5% (vol/vol) Tween-20, 1 mM DTT, and 1× complete EDTA-free protease inhibitor mixture) and rotated at 4 °C for 1 h. Soluble and insoluble fractions were then separated by centrifugation at 17,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The soluble fraction was further processed by pelleting using a 15% sucrose cushion in NTE buffer (composed of 50 mM Tris–HCl at pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, and 0.1 mM EDTA) via centrifugation at 90,000 g for 2 h at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 7 mL of nucleocapsid-forming buffer (consisting of 10 mM Tris–HCl at pH 8.0, 0.4 M NaCl, 1.85% Triton X-100, 5% Glycerol, 0.6 mM DTT) and kept on ice for 2 h with intermittent mixing every 15 min. Once again, the NCs in the buffer were cushioned in the NTE buffer and subjected to centrifugation at 90,000 g using a Beckman SW40Ti rotor. The purified NCs pellet was subsequently resuspended in 100 (μL) of resuspension buffer (comprising 10 mM Tris HCl at pH 8.0, 0.8 M NaCl, 5% Glycerol, 0.6 mM DTT). The aliquots were stored at − 20 °C for further use. Similarly, NCs were also purified from the virus-infected 10 ml supernatant using the modified protocol13 and resuspended in 100 μL of buffer.

Transfection assay to assess the functionality of isolated NCs

Purified nucleocapsids (NCs) were tested for transfection ability, functionality, and protein expression using a transfection assay. A six-well plate was seeded with about 0.5 million cells per well. Lipofectamine 2000 in Opti-MEM was used for transfections, following the manufacturer’s instructions. After being separated from 100 mm culture dishes containing both cells and supernatant, NCs were resuspended in 100 µL of resuspension buffer. The Bradford assay was used to measure the amount of protein present. Lipofectamine was combined with 1 µL of NCs (roughly 750 µg) at a ratio of 1:2.5 NCs-to-Lipofectamine (1 µL NCs to 2.5 µL Lipofectamine) for transfection. Cells treated with NCs alone or without Lipofectamine were included in the control groups. Using rabbit polyclonal anti-VSV antibodies produced against UV-inactivated wild-type VSV, Western blotting was used to verify the purity of the NC preparation.

Morphological characterization of purified NCs

Purified NCs were morphologically examined with a Philips CM-200 TEM at 200 kV. Briefly, 5 μL of purified NCs were deposited onto a carbon-coated 400-mesh copper grid for TEM imaging following negative staining with 2% uranyl acetate. In addition, the NCs’ hydrodynamic diameters and polydispersity indexes (PDIs) were measured using dynamic DLS at 25 °C in deionized water with Micromeritics NanoPlus-3 equipment.

Receptor-independent viral genome entry and pathogenesis studies

We employed Sigma’s Missiom Human Genome shRNA library to knock down the LDLR involved in VSV entrance. Vero cells were transiently transfected with plasmids containing LDLR-specific shRNAs or a scrambled plasmid using Lipofectamine 2000. Following 24 h of knockdown, cells were transfected with pure NCs to investigate how LDLR knockdown changes viral interactions and pathogenicity. The efficiency of the LDLR gene knockdown was validated using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), as illustrated in Fig. 4C. For mRNA detection and quantification, total cellular RNA was isolated by Trizol (Invitrogen, USA). Reverse transcription of each RNA sample was performed using the PrimeScript RT reagent kits (Takara, Japan). For the qRT-PCR reaction, LDLR and actin genes specific primers and SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara) were utilized in a final volume of 20 µL. The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles, and elongation at 60 °C for 30 s. The relative gene expressions were analysed using the 2−ΔΔCT method with actin serving as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Antiviral drug screening using NCs

Dox, a tetracycline derivative with broad-spectrum antimicrobial activities, is commonly used as an effector substance in inducible gene expression systems. This drug has been reported to significantly inhibit VSV replication at concentrations of 1.0–2.0 μg/mL10. To demonstrate the use of NCs complexes for drug screening, we transfected NCs into BHK-21 cells at MOI of 0.01. Three hours post-transfection, the media was replaced with 2% DMEM containing various concentrations of Dox as shown in Figure “5”.
