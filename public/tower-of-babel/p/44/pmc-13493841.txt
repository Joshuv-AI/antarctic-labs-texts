Morphogenic colloids
Authors: Florent Fessler, Adam W. Hauser, Hailiang Liu, Zhe Xu, Paul M. Chaikin, Stefano Sacanna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Morphogenesis is a dynamic process central to life, in which structure and compartmentalization arise to produce functional architectures. Replicating such transformations synthetically remains challenging because they emerge from tightly coupled physicochemical processes. Here, we report a minimal platform in which emulsion droplets exposed to amphiphilic triblock copolymers (BCPs) reproduce key features of biological morphogenesis through reversible shape morphing and topology transitions. Droplets undergo topological remodeling that leads to spontaneous and nonselective internalization of the surrounding aqueous phase, including suspended colloidal matter. In equilibrium, interfacial self-assembly of BCPs combined with droplet swelling stabilizes a spectrum of non-spherical morphologies that can be reversibly tuned by BCP concentration or temperature. These transitions can be induced synchronously across large populations of monodisperse droplets and permanently fixed via photopolymerization. This compositionally simple platform provides a tractable model for investigating the physical principles of morphogenesis while advancing colloidal systems toward biologically relevant complexity and life-like adaptability.

Introduction

The ability to maintain complex nonspherical shapes and to remodel topology is essential across living systems, as morphology supports cellular and organ function1,2, whereas topological transitions regulate compartmentalization, growth, transport and development3–6.

During the development of shapes (morphogenesis), cells and tissues continuously remodel their geometry by adjusting both surface area and enclosed volume in response to environmental and biochemical cues7,8. Whether such transformations preserve or alter topology is central to biological organization and function, yet remains poorly understood4,9–12.

In cells, signals arising from the chemical background can alter both the self-organization of surface-adsorbed molecules—governing interfacial mechanics and surface area13,14—and the osmotic conditions that set the enclosed volume8,15. This coupling is the mechanistic core of morphogenesis: a single molecular cue can co-regulate the principal physical determinants of shape, giving rise to the remarkable complexity and diversity of remodeling processes observed in living matter.

While this dynamical interplay underpins biological morphogenesis, existing bottom-up platforms for shape morphing at the micro-scale typically rely on kinetically arrested systems16–22 or involve crystalline or liquid crystalline phases23–25, and therefore fail to capture the responsive, reversible, and chemically driven nature of morphogenesis in living matter.

Establishing how chemical cues can regulate shape and topology in artificial cell-like systems remains a central challenge, both for elucidating the principles underlying morphogenesis and for engineering life-like matter. More broadly, achieving this would demonstrate that biologically relevant functions can emerge from generic physicochemical rules alone, without the complex molecular machinery that drives their biological counterparts. Macropinocytic uptake, the nonselective bulk internalization of the surrounding medium by cells26,27, is a compelling example: a function of broad biological significance that, as we show here, can be reproduced spontaneously in a compositionally minimal system.

Results

The morphogenic colloidal system we present here reproduces shape and topological transitions observed in biological contexts, while involving only two components—oil droplets immersed in a solvent (water) and an amphiphilic triblock copolymer (BCP) (Fig. 1). Monodisperse emulsion microdroplets, prepared by emulsification of 3-(trimethoxysilyl)propyl methacrylate (see Methods), spontaneously undergo shape transitions upon exposure to the amphiphilic triblock copolymer P123 (PEO20-PPO70-PEO20), until reaching the equilibrium morphologies shown in Fig. 1a. By tuning the osmotic conditions, we control the remodeling pathway, defined as the sequence of transient shapes adopted preceding equilibrium. Under hypotonic conditions, when the background contains negligible osmotically active species (background osmotic pressure Πe ≈ 0; see Methods), initially spherical droplets swell as BCP and water enter the oil phase, ultimately forming water-in-oil-in-water (w/o/w) double emulsions (Fig. 1b, top).

In contrast, when the osmotic pressure of the continuous phase is increased (the hypertonic case, see Supplementary Fig. 3), swelling is suppressed, and droplets instead remodel at constant volume through surface-area expansion. The resulting pathway proceeds through prolate dumbbell, oblate discocyte, and oblate stomatocyte shapes, ultimately leading to a topological remodeling that again yields a w/o/w double-emulsion architecture (Fig. 1b, bottom). Crucially, in this case internal aqueous compartments arise from large-scale deformations of the entire droplet, enabling nonselective capture of surrounding fluid and suspended colloids.

We confirm that these morphologies arise from equilibrium partitioning and self-assembly of P123 within the oil phase and at the oil-water interface by diluting the background concentration after stable morphologies are reached (Fig. 2e, f). Droplets rapidly relax to spherical w/o/w droplets over the course of minutes (Fig. 2e), but take about 24 h for the remaining water and P123 molecules to diffuse out of the droplet and return to the original oil droplet.

Combining epifluorescence and confocal microscopy of dyed liquid structures (Supplementary Fig. 4) with total holographic characterization of droplet size and refractive index (Supplementary Fig. 5) allows the molecular arrangements underlying BCP-induced morphologies to be probed. A schematic summary is shown in Fig. 2d. At low concentrations, holographic analysis reveals that both water and P123 diffuse into the droplets in a fixed stoichiometry (hydration number H = [H2O]/[PEO] = 1-228,29), establishing inverted micelles whose osmotic inflation—driven by low-molecular-weight TPM oligomers30 or salt contaminants from BCP synthesis31—is greatly facilitated by the ultra-low interfacial tension and resulting vanishing Laplace pressure.

At higher concentrations, internal water droplets grow and crowd until inner and outer interfaces approach, allowing BCPs to bridge and thin the interface, producing vesicular spherical blebs which can further transition into high-surface-area dendrites. The buckling of blebs and dendrites in polymerized samples (Fig. 2b) further confirms their vesicular architecture. Analysis of thermal undulations of individual dendrites (see Supplementary Fig. 6 and Methods) yields a bending modulus κb = 10.5 ± 2.1kBT, consistent with the coexistence of bridging and looping BCPs within the membrane32. Finally, these nonspherical morphologies arise only when interfacial adsorption of P123 dramatically reduces the oil-water interfacial tension σ (see Methods). Adding a simple surfactant well below its CMC, such as sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), demonstrates that both self-assembly and near-zero σ are required. Figure 2c shows that the addition of SDS stabilizes morphologies with even higher surface-to-volume ratios at fixed CBCP, but does not trigger morphological transitions in the absence of P123.

To assess the generality of this mechanism, we performed experiments varying both the amphiphile (Supplementary Fig. 1) and the oil phase (Supplementary Fig. 2). Across a series of 11 triblock copolymers spanning a wide range of hydrophilic-to-hydrophobic block ratios and molecular weights, morphogenic behavior is not specific to P123 but is instead governed by two key physicochemical parameters: a sufficiently large hydrophobic fraction, enabling partitioning and self-assembly within the oil phase, and a sufficiently large absolute hydrophilic content, allowing water to be transported and accumulated inside the droplet. Together, these properties also promote strong interfacial adsorption of the BCP, driving the substantial reduction in interfacial tension enabling the emergence of non-spherical morphologies33. Comparable morphogenic transitions are observed across chemically distinct oil phases, including a reactive network-forming silane (TPM), a non-network-forming silane (DMDES), and a fluorinated silane (TMFPS), indicating that the specific oil chemistry plays a secondary role provided the oil acts as a sink for the copolymer, allowing sufficient partitioning to drive inverted micelle formation and swelling. Among these oils, TPM offers particular experimental advantages—its density exceeding that of water facilitates sedimentation and optical imaging, and its photopolymerizability allows morphologies to be permanently fixed for electron microscopy—making it the most tractable platform for detailed quantitative investigation.

Together, these results demonstrate that the observed morphology transitions arise from a general interplay between amphiphile partitioning, self-assembly, and interfacial mechanics, rather than from specific chemical details of the components. This framing also delimits where morphogenesis can be expected beyond the silane oils used here. Two conditions must be satisfied together: (1) the copolymer must partition into the oil and assemble into water-swollen inverted micelles, which requires compatibility between its hydrophobic block and the oil; and (2) its interfacial adsorption must lower the oil-water tension into the bending-dominated regime (κb ≳ σΔA; see Methods), where thermal fluctuations and weak osmotic forces can remodel the interface. This ultralow-tension condition (here bounded at σ ≲ 10−9N m−1) is not specific to silane chemistry: strong amphiphile adsorption combined with self-assembly generically drives the effective interfacial tension toward zero, as in the balanced surfactant systems that form middle-phase microemulsions. Non-polar oils such as hexadecane or chloroform are therefore not excluded in principle, but the PEO-PPO copolymers used here are poorly soluble in aliphatic media, so an amphiphile with an oil-matched hydrophobic block would be required to meet the partitioning criterion.

Remodeling pathways

Having established the molecular origin of the equilibrium morphologies, we next show that controlling external osmotic conditions (see Methods) affects the remodeling process. Although the final equilibrium structures remain qualitatively similar, the pathway leading to internal aqueous compartments changes fundamentally. Instead of arising from the osmotic inflation of inverted micelles within the oil phase, they emerge through topological remodeling of the droplet surface.

To directly probe these remodeling pathways, we designed a capillary assay in which droplets sediment onto the bottom surface while a concentrated BCP solution is introduced at one end, generating a time-dependent concentration profile CBCP(x, t) that obeys Fickian diffusion (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Movie 1). As a result, droplets at different positions experience distinct local dynamics. The agreement indicates that slow temporal variation of CBCP expands surface area at constant volume, guiding droplets through energy-minimizing morphologies until pore closure triggers topological remodeling. This pore closure transforms the invagination into a sealed compartment, leading to internalization of the surrounding fluid. We note that all experiments were performed below the CMC of P123 in the aqueous phase, and the observed behavior is well described by osmotic regulation of water uptake alone without signature of more complex mesophase transitions related to effects of osmotic pressure on the degree of hydration and self-assembly.

We directly confirm this topological transition by repeating the experiment in a bath of fluorescent tracer colloids (500 nm negative solid particles; see Methods); after remodeling, tracers become irreversibly captured within droplets (Fig. 3f, g, Supplementary Fig. 8, and Supplementary Movie 2). Photopolymerization locks the final morphology, and SEM imaging reveals closed shells containing encapsulated tracers (Fig. 3h).

Remarkably, morphogenic droplets in hypertonic solution undergo a prolate-oblate transition (Fig. 3d, e and Supplementary Movies 1 and 2), a phenomenon previously reported in lipid vesicles35–37 but not in oil droplets. This transition unfolds continuously through a sequence of non-axisymmetric intermediates. Temperature-driven morphing

Temperature provides another powerful handle over droplet morphology through the LCST of the PPO block of P123 (Fig. 4a)39,40. In hypertonic conditions, heating drives droplets from spheres into tubular morphologies; during cooling, these tubes can undergo local prolate-oblate-stomatocyte transitions that nucleate internal cavities, rendering the thermal cycle irreversible (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Movie 3). When deformations remain moderate, however, the thermal cycle is fully reversible (Fig. 4c, d, left). Note that the projected area Ap, extracted from 2D images (see Supplementary Fig. 3) and used here to monitor shape transitions and assess reversibility, is only a proxy for the deformation. While related to the true surface area A of the droplet accounted for in the reduced volume, it does not directly measure it. Under hypotonic conditions, temperature modulations drive reversible transitions among equilibrium morphologies (Fig. 4e, f, Supplementary Fig. 10, and Supplementary Movie 4). Droplets reliably return to their initial state upon cooling, provided the system is given sufficient time for outward BCP diffusion to occur (Fig. 2c). As a result, repeated heating-cooling cycles generate periodic shape oscillations with amplitudes that depend on CBCP (Fig. 4g).

At moderate heating rates, the heating and cooling pathways overlap with negligible hysteresis. However, at slightly faster rates and over a broader temperature range (18–33 °C), droplets exhibit pronounced hysteresis: upon heating they bypass the flower-like morphologies observed during cooling, elongating directly into dendrites (Fig. 4e), producing a reproducible hysteresis loop in Ap/A0 as a function of temperature (Fig. 4f). In rare cases, dendrites retract through a prolate-oblate folding pathway reminiscent of hypertonic remodeling, suggesting that temperature cycling can occasionally trigger uptake through single dendrites (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Movie 4).

These results establish temperature, like BCP concentration, as a reversible control parameter for droplet morphology, while showing that true irreversibility arises exclusively from topological transitions that trap external fluid and solutes.

Together, our results demonstrate that a biphasic oil-water system with a single amphiphile is sufficient to reproduce hallmarks of biological morphogenesis. By tuning BCP concentration or temperature, as well as osmotic and kinetic parameters, droplets explore a spectrum of stable and unstable morphologies—from dendritic to discocyte and stomatocyte—without requiring the intricate molecular machinery of living cells.

Beyond providing a minimal model to probe the physical principles of morphogenesis, this platform opens avenues for designing synthetic systems capable of environment-responsive remodeling, on-demand encapsulation, or controlled generation of complex architectures. While our system does not capture the biochemical complexity of living systems, it establishes a direct mechanistic link between non-equilibrium shape dynamics and emergent biological-like function and provides a minimal framework to explore how morphology, topology, and volume regulation can give rise to compartmentalization and uptake. Such minimal systems may serve as testbeds for designing adaptive soft materials and for probing the physical limits of morphogenesis-like behavior.

Methods

Emulsion preparation

Emulsion droplets were prepared following a protocol adapted from ref. 41. Briefly, 0.75 mL of a 100 mM NaOH solution was added to 15 mL of Milli-Q (deionized) water, followed by rapid one-shot addition of 0.75 mL of TPM (3-(trimethoxysilyl)propyl methacrylate, 98%, Sigma-Aldrich) under slow rotation of tubes at 20 rpm. In this basic aqueous environment, monodisperse oil droplets nucleated and grew to a mean diameter of ~2.8 μm within 1 h. The reaction was quenched by sequential addition of 0.75 mL of a 5 wt% Pluronic F108 solution and 75 μL of 1 M HCl to neutralize the pH. F108 is introduced at this stage to stabilize droplets against coalescence. Following droplet formation, samples are washed three times by centrifugation and resuspension in deionized water to remove excess monomer and unbound F108. The samples are subsequently diluted prior to use, resulting in a negligible residual concentration of free F108 compared to P123. Control experiments performed in the complete absence of F108 yield identical morphogenic behavior, confirming that F108 does not contribute to the morphological transitions reported here. For fluorescent labeling, the oil phase was selectively dyed by one-shot addition of 150 μL of Rhodamine B isothiocyanate (RITC, emission 595 nm, excitation 570 nm) covalently attached to 3-(aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APS) in ethanol (see ref. 41), performed 5 min before quenching. This covalent labeling confines the fluorescent signal to the TPM oil phase and prevents dye transfer to the aqueous phase, such that internal aqueous compartments appear as non-fluorescent regions in microscopy images. The concentrated stock solution was used within two weeks to minimize aging effects that could alter physical properties, such as viscosity.

Equilibrium morphologies

To examine the equilibrium morphology of TPM droplets in the presence of P123 triblocks in hypotonic conditions, a 10 μL aliquot of a 100× diluted TPM suspension (prepared by mixing 10 μL of TPM stock solution with 1 mL of deionized water) was introduced into 50 μL of aqueous P123 solution in a 2-dram glass vial. The mixture was equilibrated at room temperature for 20 min to allow surfactant diffusion and stabilization of droplet morphology. Note that longer equilibration times (up to several days) were needed when BCP concentration was above the CMC in water, which was never the case for P123 but for some BCPs reported in Supplementary Fig. 1. The equilibrated suspension was then carefully transferred into a capillary (VitroTubes), which was sealed with capillary wax (Hampton Research) or optical adhesive (Norland Optical 68) to prevent evaporation. The sealed capillary was then positioned under an optical microscope for imaging and morphological analysis.

While a fixed TPM droplet size and concentration were used for all quantitative experiments reported in this work, additional (unreported) measurements indicate that the observed morphologies do not depend solely on the absolute bulk concentration of BCP. Instead, the relevant control parameter is the ratio between the amount of BCP and the total available oil-water interfacial area, which governs the effective surface coverage. Accordingly, variations in droplet size or concentration that modify the total accessible interfacial area shift the apparent transition boundaries, with higher or lower effective BCP concentrations required depending on whether the total interfacial area is increased or decreased.

To assess the reversibility of TPM droplet morphology upon dilution, a Petri dish with a central glass coverslip well, allowing direct observation under a 100x oil-immersion microscope, was used. 10 μL of an equilibrated solution of P123 and TPM droplets was deposited into the well and imaged to establish the initial morphology. Subsequently, 90 μL of deionized water was gently added to achieve a tenfold dilution. Droplet morphology was continuously monitored to evaluate structural responses to reduced P123 levels, thereby probing the reversibility of BCP-induced transformations.

Photopolymerization

To fix the equilibrium morphologies for scanning electron microscopy (SEM), TPM droplets were solidified via UV-initiated radical polymerization. Immediately after the desired morphology was achieved, Darocur-1173 photoinitiator (2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone, 97%, Sigma–Aldrich) was added to the suspension at a final concentration of 0.1% (v/v). The sample was then exposed to UV light for 25 min to initiate photopolymerization and solidify the droplets. Following polymerization, the samples were washed three times by centrifugation and resuspension in deionized water. For SEM imaging, the solidified colloids were dried on a clean silicon wafer and coated with a 2.5 nm thick platinum layer.

Remodeling pathways

The temporal evolution of TPM droplet morphologies under non-equilibrium conditions was monitored in a glass capillary (total volume 20μL). Capillaries were prefilled with 15 μL of TPM droplet suspension containing the desired droplet concentration and, when required, osmotically active species such as D-glucose (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥99.5%). The capillary was sealed onto a coverslip and left undisturbed for 15 min to allow droplets to sediment near the bottom surface. A 5 μL aliquot of P123 solution (at four times the target equilibrium concentration) was then introduced at one end of the capillary, taking care to avoid air bubble formation. This procedure generated a time-varying concentration gradient of P123 triblocks by diffusion along the channel, thereby defining the start of the experiment (t = 0). Capillaries were immediately mounted on the microscope to capture droplet morphology transitions under the steepest CBCP variations.

For tracer capture experiments, fluorescent trimethoxy(3,3,3-trifluoropropyl)silane (TMFPS) tracer particles were synthesized through a sol-gel method44. A total of 200 μL of TMFPS was hydrolyzed in 20 mL of deionized water in a 50 mL beaker under stirring at 250 rpm overnight. Subsequently, a 7% ammonia solution was added to catalyze the condensation of the hydrolyzed TMFPS at pH = 10.8 under stirring at 400 rpm, leading to droplet formation within a few minutes. After 1 h, RITC-APS was introduced to label the droplets. The pH was then increased to 11 using the same 7% ammonia solution to promote aging of the silicon-oxygen network for an additional hour. After a total solidification time of 2 h, the resulting particles were collected and washed three times with deionized water by centrifugation at 400 × g for 1 h per cycle. Particles were further characterized by SEM and found to have a diameter of 500 nm.

Tonicity modulation

Remodeling pathways of TPM droplets were modulated by adjusting the osmotic pressure of the background solution. Permeation of P123 into the oil phase generates an internal osmotic pressure Πi that drives water influx and micelle swelling when the external osmotic pressure Πe is low. We refer to this condition as hypotonic, as it produces inward osmotic flow and promotes droplet swelling.

Inward osmotic flow was suppressed, leading to different remodeling pathways, when Πe was increased. This was achieved either by adding osmotically active solutes, such as glucose, to the background solution (Supplementary Fig. 3), or by exploiting residual low-molecular-weight TPM oligomers that diffuse from droplets into the continuous phase. The effect of these residual species is most pronounced at high droplet concentrations, since dilution of the droplets also dilutes the surrounding background and lowers Πe (see Supplementary Fig. 7). To confirm the osmotic activity of these residual species, droplets were diluted into the supernatant of a centrifuged concentrated droplet solution. Under these conditions, suppression of swelling was again observed, consistent with the presence of osmotically active TPM oligomers in the background.

Temperature-controlled assays

Thermal responsiveness of TPM droplet morphologies near the P123 lower critical solution temperature (LCST) was investigated using a custom temperature-controlled stage. Capillary samples were sealed on a coverslip and mounted onto a thermally conducting copper plate connected to two Peltier elements driven by a power supply. Heating was achieved by modulating the applied current and voltage, allowing controlled temperature variation from room temperature up to 50 °C. Temperature was monitored using a thermocouple in direct contact with the copper plate. Similar temperature protocols were applied across experiments to maintain approximately equal heating and cooling rates (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 2{}^{\circ }{{{{\rm{C}}}}}/\min$$\end{document}~2C∘/min). Cooling to ambient temperature was achieved passively by switching off the heating stage, or by placing ice-filled Petri dishes on the copper plate to reach sub-ambient temperatures. This controlled thermal environment enabled systematic evaluation of temperature-dependent morphological transitions.
