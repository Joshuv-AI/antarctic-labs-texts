Bengali cyberbullying detection: A comprehensive dataset for advanced analysis
Authors: Gazi Tahsina Sharmin Jahin, Firoze Maliha, Nuren Nafisa, Mohammad Mahadi Hassan, M Jamshed Alam Patwary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCyberbullying has become a major concern in the digital world, with social media platforms facilitating harmful interactions that target individuals. Although there has been significant research, studies specifically focusing on Bengali cyberbullying remain scarce. This paper addresses this gap by analyzing over 70,000 social media comments in the Bengali language. The dataset is preprocessed for sentiment analysis to classify positive and negative comments, followed by topic modeling for negative comments using Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) to extract features from age, gender, ethnicity, religion-based, and miscellaneous comments. Various models are applied, including Support Vector Machine, XGBoost, CNN+BiLSTM+GRU, mBERT, XLM-R, etc. Among the evaluated models, mBERT demonstrated the highest performance, attaining an accuracy of 92%. While the CNN+BiLSTM+GRU hybrid model outperformed with an accuracy of 91%. Further improvements are done by incorporating BERT embeddings into CNN and ANN models, and achieved an accuracy of 93%. To enhance model transparency and trust, Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations is applied to interpret predictions.Keywords: Cyberbullying, Explainable AI (XAI), BERT embeddings, Topic modeling, Sentiment analysis

Background

Various researchers have applied machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques to combat cyberbullying, often relying on small, domain-specific datasets [1]. LIME has been widely used for model interpretability. For instance, [2] used LIME with classifiers like SVM, RF, Bi-LSTM, and BERT, offering interpretability but falling short in model-specific explanations. Similarly, [3] and [4] incorporated LIME in hate speech detection but encountered limitations in capturing individual model behaviors. In sentiment analysis, particularly for Bengali, [5] existing research has shown promising results but remains heavily dependent on limited datasets. [6] used FastText and Skipgram for feature extraction, highlighting the robustness of BanglaBERT. However, the study emphasized the need for domain adaptation and multi-class learning. [7] proposed a hybrid ensemble model achieving 85% accuracy, yet it lacked transformer-based architectures crucial for capturing deeper language features. Similarly, [8] demonstrated the superiority of transformer-based embeddings such as BERT and GPT-J-6B over traditional methods like TF-IDF and Word2Vec, reaching up to 98% accuracy. Multilingual efforts like those by [9], using WordPiece tokenization with AfriBERTa and RoBERTa, faced challenges of overfitting and data quality, despite achieving 80.88% accuracy. In Bengali-specific settings, [10] applied BERT with 97% accuracy but was limited by a small and imbalanced dataset. Likewise, [11] integrated TF-IDF with BERT, CNN, and MLP, achieving 92.3% accuracy, though the dataset size raised concerns about overfitting. [12] experimented with pre-trained models like BERT, XLM-R, and mBERT, finding BanglaBERT to be the most effective. These studies underline the necessity of fine-tuned transformer models for Bengali, but again highlight the consistent issue of limited, non-diverse datasets. Hybrid and ensemble models are also gaining attention; [13] reached 87.54% using DistilBERT and XLM-R. However, dataset limitations continue to restrict broader applicability and generalization. Despite technological progress, the lack of large, diverse, and representative Bengali datasets remains a significant barrier to the development of reliable and generalizable models. Most existing works depend on narrow-scope datasets, often sourced from single platforms like Facebook or Twitter, which limits cross-platform adaptability and model robustness. Furthermore, the need for domain adaptation, multi-class learning, and model-specific interpretability frameworks remains largely unattended.

To bridge these gaps, we propose an end-to-end system built upon a large-scale, richly diverse Bengali dataset, integrating ML, DL, transformer-based models (TBM), and explainable AI (XAI) techniques. This comprehensive approach aims to enhance model performance, interpretability, and generalizability for effective cyberbullying detection in the Bengali language.

Data Description

This section presents a detailed overview of the dataset used in the study, including its collection sources, preprocessing techniques, and preparation steps. We describe the origin and characteristics of the data, followed by the methods applied to clean and tokenize the Bengali textual content to ensure it is suitable for further analysis.

Data collection

The dataset consists of over 70,000 Bengali comments collected from diverse public domains. This subsection outlines the data sources and collection methods, providing context on how the dataset was compiled to represent real-world social media communication in Bengali.

Data sources

The data set of the present study consists of more than 70,000 comments collected from different public domains, with approximately 50,000 comments sourced from Kaggle and 20,000 comments from other Bengali text resources [Table 1]. Approximately 50,000 comments were obtained from the publicly available Kaggle dataset [14]. These comments were originally scraped from Bangladesh-focused public Facebook posts. An additional 20,000 comments were sourced from a GitHub repository; these were created by other researchers using social media APIs (e.g., Facebook Graph API, Twitter API v2) along with keyword-based filtering and ethical scraping methods. All data were filtered for Bengali language content and manually or semi-automatically labeled for tasks such as sentiment and toxicity detection. All included data were publicly available and appropriately licensed for academic use. These sources offer the raw data of the social media posts and commentaries that include a broad array of practices and activities typical of Facebook, Twitter, and internet forums [Fig. 3b]. The various types of data achievements with this language will provide a good ground for the analysis of communication patterns within the Bengali language.

Data preprocessing

This section outlines the essential steps undertaken to prepare the raw Bengali social media comments for analysis. It covers data cleaning, tokenization, and annotation procedures that ensure the dataset’s quality and relevance for cyberbullying detection.

Data cleaning

The initial phase of our study involves the careful preparation of Bengali social media textual data. During data pre-processing, the text is cleansed of HTML tags, URLs, emojis, special characters, common stop words, and unnecessary white spaces. Entries without ‘text’ values are excluded from further analysis [Fig. 1 a]. The ‘text’ column entries are converted to string format using ‘astype(str)’ to ensure uniform formatting for tokenization. For word-level parsing in Bengali, language-specific tools are used. However, since transformer-based models inherently handle noise and irrelevant tokens, overly aggressive cleaning is avoided.

Comparison of two stages in the data cleaning process.

Tokenization

For accurate representation of Bengali characters, words, and sentence structures, mBERT is used for tokenization. mBERT is trained on multiple languages, including Bengali, enabling it to handle diverse linguistic patterns and provide effective tokenization for Bengali text. This process involves splitting each input text into tokens, truncating it to a maximum length of 128 tokens, and padding the sequences to maintain uniform input vector lengths [Fig. 1 b].

Annotation

The negative dataset is needed to be effective in analyzing various strategies of cyberbullying, which requires manual annotation. The negative sentiments received through the Sentiment Analysis model work as a cyberbullying dataset for the rest of the work of this research. The annotators are native Bengali speakers who also understand online interaction patterns to be noted. Three native Bengali speakers annotated the negative sentiment comments into the defined cyberbullying categories. We calculated pairwise Cohen’s kappa on a 20% random sample of the data, obtaining an average κ approximately equal to 0.83 (substantial agreement). Discrepancies were resolved by consensus discussion. Following is a description of the dataset’s labels [Table 2, Fig. 2].

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Cyberbullying is one of the most serious problems in today’s highly digital environment, with social media being the prime platform for online abuse [15]. It includes hate speech that targets ethnicity, gender, age, religion, and other such bases [16], [17]. Cyberbullying has been studied widely in many languages; however, much work is yet to be done on underrepresented languages like Bengali [18]. Hence, this study proposes to find a way around this gap by studying cyberbullying in Bengali social media, which will provide useful insights for academic research and real-life applications. So, a systematic detection and mitigation of online abuse would be a burning need with more than 250 million speakers and their ever-increasing presence in social media. The work has implemented a systematic approach to the challenges of cyberbullying detection in the Bengali language. A rich dataset of Bengali texts is compiled. The Sentiment Analysis model extracts the positive, negative comments from the dataset [19], [20]. [Fig. 7 a] shows the original sentiment distribution and [Fig. 7 b] the final annotated class distribution The model distinguishes negative sentiments from positive ones, with around 30,000 comments expressing positive emotions and the remaining 40,000 comments reflecting negative feelings on social media platforms [Table 3]. We note this moderate class imbalance (approximately equal to 57% negative vs. 43% positive comments). To mitigate bias, we used stratified train-test splits and applied class weights in the loss function so that misclassifications of the smaller class are penalized more. These steps ensured balanced learning despite the imbalance. The model identifies sentiment as positive or negative using the BERT transformer architecture, which captures efficient context for this text. This is achieved by fine-tuning the model on a labeled dataset, allowing it to adjust its parameters and minimize classification errors, resulting in a more accurate sentiment analysis for Bengali text [Fig. 5]. We have applied LDA methods afterward for latent topic extraction from the negative comments and got useful ideas regarding prevalent themes in cyberbullying speech [21]. [Fig. 4] displays the top words (scores) for each of the five topics. For instance, Topic 1’s top terms relate to age, Topic 2 to gender, etc., confirming that LDA captured coherent thematic groups aligned with our label categories. We use Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) as a feature extraction technique in a supervised learning setting for classifying Bengali cyberbullying text. Once we have trained the LDA model with the topics, we extract the topic distribution for each document; the distribution provides the probability of each document belonging to each of the 5 topics. The document-topic probabilities are useful as features for supervised classification. It creates a list of topic probabilities for each document that shows how much that document is affiliated with each topic. For example, if there are 5 topics, then it would have 5 values corresponding to the likelihood of that document to belong to a particular topic. Later, several ML, TBMs, and DL are adopted for analyzing and classifying the data [22], [23], [24]. The many-faceted approach, indeed, allows us to evaluate several modeling techniques in terms of their performance with respect to the detection of harmful online behavior [25]. We have introduced a hybrid model named Bengali Cyberbullying Detection(BCD), which integrates CNN, BiLSTM, and GRU architectures to effectively capture both local and sequential features for improved text classification performance. We trained all classification models for 5 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0001 and a batch size of 32. Hyperparameters were chosen based on initial tuning experiments. We used 10-fold cross-validation on the training set to evaluate each model’s average performance. Stratified splits ensured proportional class representation in each fold. These settings were chosen because in preliminary tests, accuracy plateaued by epoch 5, and further training yielded diminishing returns. A learning rate of 0.0001 allowed stable convergence without divergence or vanishing gradients, as observed during hyperparameter tuning. To explain better the understanding of how our model has derived at a particular prediction [26], we have integrated LIME into our models [27], [28]. The study also uses BERT embeddings, experimenting a bit more with CNN, ANN, and other hybrid models to enhance the detection. This methodology not only tries to increase the detection accuracy of cyberbullying but also ensures that the inferences drawn would be interpretable and actionable [29], helpful towards the needs of the platform moderators and policymakers [Fig. 3 a]. In this way, the critical aspects addressed by our approach have distinguished it from the existing works and have contributed significantly to low-resource language cyberbullying research, such as Bengali (Algorithm 1).

Model comparison

In the comparative analysis of various model types for cyberbullying detection [Table 4, Table 5, Table 6], transformer-based models, particularly mBERT, achieved the highest accuracy (92%) and F1-score (91%), showcasing their effectiveness in understanding nuanced Bengali text. We attribute mBERT’s superior performance to its extensive pretraining and tokenization strategy. As a multilingual model trained on Bengali text, mBERT effectively captures complex word-level and subword patterns inherent in Bengali. Its WordPiece tokenizer can represent rare words and affixes better than models trained from scratch. Thus, mBERT achieved the highest accuracy (92%) and F1 (91%) in our experiments, outperforming models without such deep pretraining. Following closely, XLM-R also performed well with an accuracy of 91%. Among neural network models, the CNN+BiLSTM+GRU hybrid model outperformed others with 91% accuracy, leveraging the strengths of both convolutional and recurrent layers. In traditional machine learning models, SVM and XGBoost achieved competitive accuracies of 91%, demonstrating strong performance in classification tasks. These results are significantly enabled by the availability of a large and diverse Bengali-language dataset, the volume and linguistic variety in the dataset enhance the model’s ability to generalize and handle real-world user comments. Overall, while transformer models excel in capturing linguistic complexities, hybrid neural networks and robust ML models also deliver impressive results, underscoring the critical role of high-quality, representative datasets in achieving reliable model performance (Fig. 6).

LIME Application with BERT Embeddings

According to [Table 7], BERT+CNN and BERT+ANN arrive at the highest accuracy, 93%, along with impressive precision and recall scores, 92% and 91%, respectively. These results point to how effective it is to integrate BERT’s contextual embeddings with convolutional and feedforward neural networks to capture the complex patterns in Bengali text [11]. The next best competitor, BERT+CNN+BiLSTM, has an accuracy of 92%, which proves that this model surely can make proper utilization of features from the spatial and temporal dimensions of text. Meanwhile, another model called BERT+CNN+ANN has an accuracy of 91% because while hybrid approaches usually tend to improve a lot, sometimes a certain combination of used architectures does make a difference. BERT-enhanced models (BERT+CNN, BERT+ANN) [Fig. 8a,b], showing the ROC curves across different identity labels. explain why their performances are much better, with BERT playing the main role. Across all identity classes (age, gender, ethnicity, religion, etc.), the area under the curve (AUC) values are consistently higher. The models, for instance, achieve near-perfect AUC values of 1.00 for age and religion with the BERT+CNN+BiLSTM model, demonstrating outstanding accuracy in classification with almost no false positives. It is the context-sensitive embeddings from BERT that have allowed the models to grasp the subtle nuances of the text and hence differentiate between classes more succinctly than in the no-BERT versions. With high values of AUC, especially for these identity-sensitive categories, the likelihood of misclassifications drastically decreases in BERT; this makes the ROCs steeper and closer to the top-left corner point, meaning near-ideal model behavior.

Limitations

Our data, while large, comes from particular Bengali social media platforms and periods and therefore can be subject to demographic or topical bias. For example, particular offline situations or some dialects are not represented, potentially hindering generalizability. Also, while annotators were experienced, manual detection of cyberbullying is not objective; certain sarcasm or implicit comments might have been interpreted inconsistently. Finally, pretrained models over general corpora (e.g., mBERT) may still miss nuanced domain-specific vocabulary. We understand these shortcomings and propose future research in diversifying data sources and annotators.

Ethics Statement

The data were sourced from publicly available platforms, including Kaggle and other Bengali text corpora. As the data were publicly accessible, no consent was required. The dataset was anonymized, and the study complies with the redistribution policies of the respective platforms, including Kaggle, Facebook, and Twitter.
