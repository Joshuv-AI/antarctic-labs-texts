Systemic amyloid A amyloidosis in a captive Cape hyrax (Procavia capensis)
Authors: Aya TERAO, Susumu IWAIDE, Natsumi KOBAYASHI, Tomoaki MURAKAMI, Yoshiyuki ITOH, Masakazu NAKADA, Tomoyuki SHIBAHARA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Amyloidosis is a disease caused by amyloid deposition and has been reported in various animal species; however, reports on Afrotherian species, including Cape hyraxes, are extremely limited. In this report, we describe the case of a 4-year-old captive Cape hyrax affected by amyloidosis. Histological examination revealed amyloid deposits in the liver, spleen, kidneys, and heart, which stained orange-red with Congo red and exhibited typical birefringence under polarized light. Mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis and immunohistochemistry identified serum amyloid A in the amyloid deposits. Based on these findings, the case was diagnosed as amyloid A amyloidosis. This is a first case of amyloid A amyloidosis in Cape hyrax and, to our knowledge, the first documented case in any Afrotherian species.

Amyloidosis is a group of diseases caused by the systemic or localized deposition of misfolded fibrillar proteins known as amyloids, leading to progressive tissue damage and organ dysfunction [1, 10]. Amyloidosis is generally classified into disease types based on the precursor proteins of the amyloid deposits [1, 4]. When amyloids are deposited in multiple organs, this condition is called systemic amyloidosis. The most prevalent form of systemic amyloidosis in animals is amyloid A (AA) amyloidosis, characterized by amyloid deposits derived from the serum amyloid A (SAA) protein [4]. However, as patterns of amyloid deposition vary between animal species [20] and several types of amyloidosis other than AA have been reported in recent years, identification of the amyloid precursor protein is essential for accurate diagnosis [4].

Cape hyrax, or rock hyrax (Procavia capensis), is a mammalian species that inhabits the rocky regions of Africa and the Arabian Peninsula [9]. It belongs to the morphologically diverse clade Afrotheria, supported by molecular phylogenetic analyses [22]. Afrotheria consists of two major clades: Paenungulata, which includes hyraxes, elephants, and manatees, and Afroinsectiphilia, which includes aardvarks, sengis, and tenrecs [18]. Hyracoidea and Pricaviidae, to which all hyraxes belong, consist of three genera, with Cape hyrax forming the sole genus Procavia [17]. Although various diseases have been reported in Cape hyraxes, including cryptococcosis [15] and protothecosis [12], only one case of systemic amyloidosis associated with pancreatic lesions has been documented, and its pathological characteristics have not been described in detail [8]. To the best of our knowledge, there have been no reports of amyloidosis in other Afrotherian species. In this report, we diagnosed systemic amyloid A amyloidosis in a captive Cape hyrax and conducted histopathological and immunohistochemical examinations and mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis.

A 4-year-old male Cape hyrax showed decreased activity at a zoo in Japan from October 2006. In December of the same year, the animal developed hypothermia, dehydration, diarrhea, impaired walking, and intermittent convulsions. The animal died the following day, and necropsy was performed by veterinarians at the zoo. At necropsy, the head-to-body length and body weight were 42 cm and 1.98 kg, respectively, and the animal was emaciated. Numerous greyish white spots were found in the liver capsule and parenchyma. No significant gross lesions were observed in the other organs. Several formalin-fixed organs were submitted for detailed examination at the Ishikawa Prefectural Nanbu Livestock Hygiene Service Center, the National Institute of Animal Health, and Tokyo University of Agriculture and Technology.

The liver, spleen, kidneys, heart, and lungs were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, dehydrated through graded alcohols, embedded in paraffin according to standard protocols, sectioned at 3 µm thickness, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin and Congo red. Amyloid deposition was confirmed using Congo red-stained sections under polarized light. Histologically, eosinophilic, amorphous material was deposited in the perisinusoidal spaces of the liver; the vascular walls of the central and brush arteries, and the trabeculae of the spleen; the outer stripe and inner stripe of medullary interstitium, loose connective tissue of renal pelvis, glomerular capillary walls, and cortical interstitium of the kidney; and the myocardial interstitium including capillary walls of the heart. The extent of amyloid deposition varied depending on the organ and site. Amyloid was diffusely and severely deposited in the liver, the spleen, and the renal medulla. In other part, amyloid deposition was diffuse and mild. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), rabbit polyclonal anti-SAA antibody [5], anti-human immunoglobulin kappa chain polyclonal antibody (A80-111A; Bethyl Laboratories, Montgomery, TX, USA), and anti-human immunoglobulin lambda chain polyclonal antibody (20758-1-AP; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA) were used as primary antibodies. The secondary antibodies used were horseradish peroxidase-conjugated polymer anti-rabbit IgG antibody (Dako, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and the Histofine Simple Stain MAX-PO (MULTI) kit (Nichirei Biosciences, Tokyo, Japan). Positive signals were visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Bovine AA amyloidosis was used as positive control for SAA antibody. Primary antibodies were omitted as negative controls. Plasma cells from the spleen were used as internal positive controls for anti-immunoglobulin kappa and anti-lambda antibodies (Supplementary Fig. 1). IHC revealed that the eosinophilic amorphous material deposited in the liver, spleen, kidney, and heart reacted positively with the anti-SAA antibody (Fig. 1). In contrast, all the amyloid deposits were negative for immunoglobulin kappa and lambda light chains (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to characterize amyloid fibrils. Samples were prepared from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) liver sections as previously described [6] and examined using a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400 Flash; JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). Mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis was performed to identify the amyloid precursor protein within the amyloid deposits. For FFPE tissues from the liver, spleen, and kidney, 7 µm sections were cut onto polyethylene naphthalate (PEN) membrane slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and stained with Congo red. Amyloid deposits were then microdissected from the same sections, covering areas ranging from 190,000 to 320,000 µm2, and subjected to liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) as previously described [6]. Regarding cardiac amyloid, microdissection was not performed due to the difficulty in obtaining specific tissue samples.

The resulting LC-MS/MS data were analyzed using Mascot Server (Matrix Science Ltd., London, UK) and a protein dataset published by the National Center for Biotechnology Information. Because protein sequence data specific to Cape hyrax were unavailable, datasets corresponding to other Afrotherian species were used. Theoretical fragmentation patterns of tryptic peptides were used for peptide identification. An error-tolerant search was initially performed, followed by the inclusion of methionine oxidation, which is frequently detected as an in-silico variable modification, to account for possible chemical modifications. Among the identified peptides, those with expectation values <0.05 were considered statistically significant and are presented in the results.

AA amyloidosis is the most common type of systemic amyloidosis in animals and is typically associated with chronic inflammatory or neoplastic conditions, although idiopathic cases have also been reported [20]. In the present case, the histopathological examination of the liver, spleen, kidneys, heart, and lungs revealed no signs of inflammation or neoplasia, and the underlying cause of AA amyloidosis remains unclear.

The gastrointestinal tract is a common site of amyloid deposition in animals [7, 19]. Amyloid accumulation in the gastrointestinal tract in humans, particularly the small intestine, often results in diarrhea and malabsorption [3]. The hyrax presented with diarrhea and dehydration. Therefore, we suspect that amyloid deposition in the digestive tract may have occurred in the present case, potentially contributing to the overall deterioration of the animal. Systemic amyloid deposition may compromise organ function and play a role in the debilitation and the cause of death of the hyrax. However, as histological examinations of the gastrointestinal tract and brain were not performed, the exact pathology and cause of death remain unclear.

In this case, congestive pulmonary edema was suspected given the fibrous thickening of the alveolar septa and hemosiderin accumulation in the lungs. Although severe amyloid deposition was observed in the myocardial interstitium, only mild vacuolar degeneration was observed, with no significant myocardial damage. The cardiac involvement in AA amyloidosis is usually clinically insignificant in humans [16], and it remains unclear whether amyloid deposition contributes to cardiac dysfunction.

The presence of bile duct hyperplasia in the liver suggested the possibility of some kind of biliary tract abnormality, such as biliary obstruction related to historical cholangitis [2]. However, the treatment history was unavailable, so the cause remains unclear. Widespread interstitial amyloid deposition in the kidney severely disrupted the organ architecture, and renal dysfunction was suspected [7, 11].

To date, only one case of systemic amyloidosis has been reported in Cape hyrax [8]. The pancreas was affected in that case; however, the detailed distribution of amyloid and the identity of the amyloid precursor proteins have not been reported. AA is commonly deposited in the pancreas of animals [4, 21]. Amyloid deposits are typically found in the vascular walls and interstitium of the pancreas [4], suggesting that the previously reported hyrax may also have had AA amyloidosis. However, the possibility of rare systemic amyloidosis other than AA amyloidosis, such as AL amyloidosis, cannot be ruled out [4]. Additional case reports are necessary to clarify the precise pathology of amyloidosis in Cape hyraxes.

LC-MS/MS has been widely used to detect amyloid precursor proteins in animal amyloidosis [4]. In the present study, protein sequences from Afrotheria were used as reference data due to the unavailability of sequence data specific to Cape hyrax. The SAA sequence of the lesser hedgehog tenrec (Echinops telfairi) was consistently detected, supporting the diagnosis of AA amyloidosis. This finding underscored the utility of amyloid typing using mass spectrometry, even in Afrotheria species that lack fully sequenced genomes. When analyzing animals with incomplete genomic information, it is essential to use protein databases of closely related species as reference sequences [14]. However, this approach may lead to misdiagnosis if an appropriate amyloid precursor protein is not detected [4]. Therefore, immunohistochemistry targeting candidate proteins is important for a definitive diagnosis.

In conclusion, this report presents the first confirmed case of AA amyloidosis in a Cape hyrax and describes its pathological features. Accurate classification and continued investigation of amyloidosis in Cape hyraxes and other Afrotherian species will contribute to our understanding of the comparative pathology, particularly in understudied animals.
