Study on microstructure evolution in welded lap joint configuration of dissimilar advanced high-strength steels using low-frequency laser wobble welding
Authors: Contuzzi Nicola, Rashkovets Mariia, Mendagaliev Ruslan, Casalino Giuseppe
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractLaser wobble welding (oscillating laser beam) experiments were performed on high-strength micro-alloyed steels (HSLA) in both a low-carbon state (top plate) and carburized state (lower plate) using various process parameters. The welds were characterized regarding microstructure and microhardness. The cross sections of the welds had a predominantly symmetric geometry and were free of defects. Repeatable heating along an oscillating path resulted in a gradual transformation along heat-affected (HAZ) region of the top HC380LA plate from partial recrystallization through acicular and Widmanstätten ferrite formation to bainite. The microstructure of the lower 16MnCr5 plate experienced self-tempering of HAZ martensite along with bainite formation. The microhardness distribution across the entire fusion zone (FZ) of the dissimilar joint exhibited relatively consistent value, with the most significant variation of 80 HV attributed to the carburized layer of the lower plate.Keywords: Laser wobble welding, HSLA steel, Microstructure

Introduction

Laser welding offers numerous advantages over conventional welding processes in the automotive industry due to its high process efficiency, ability to form a deep fusion zone (FZ), and narrow heat-affected zone (HAZ). However, there are several drawbacks of laser beam that can limit its industrial application: the requirement for a minimum gap due to the small spot size, the generation of excessive spattering caused by the high intensity of the laser beam within a small molten pool, and the potential for the formation of pores and solidification centerline cracks due to the large depth-to-width ratio [1,2].

Previous studies have shown that employing of an oscillated laser beam broadens the weld seam due to decreased cooling rates [3,4], which should be approximately 90 % of the thinner sheets thickness to prevent shearing by itself [5]. This effect is achieved through the manipulation of oscillating parameters such as amplitude and frequency, which induces a stirring action of the laser beam within the molten pool. Consequently, the stirring action results in a decrease of temperature gradient, reduction in the width-to-depth ratio of the weld seam, and subsequent elimination of surface voids [6,7]. Furthermore, Berend O. et al. [8] observed that beam oscillation can also help to reduce the surface roughness of the seam. Standfuβ J. et al. [9] applied a common high frequency mode for laser wobble welding and noticed that spatter formation was reduced at an oscillating frequency of 4 kHz. However, Schweier M. et al. [10] later studied spatter formation in the range of oscillating frequency from 200 Hz to 1910 Hz and concluded that the influence of the oscillation frequency is negligible. However, the application of low-frequency mode in wobble welding is scarce overviewed.

Most of the recent research on the precise microstructure studies of wobble welding has been experienced in light alloys [4,[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. However, there is scarced information about the wobble welding microstructure of steels, which are one of the most widely used materials in the automotive industry [6,[16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. For instance, Gerhards et al. [5] were the first to apply laser oscillation welding for Al–Si coated 22MnB5 steel in a lap configuration and found that welding mixed up the surface coating in the bottom sheet, preventing it from precipitating at FZ boundary. Z. Zhang et al. [16] used an oscillating laser beam to join ultra-high strength PHS1500/PHS2000 steel with Al–Si coating and showed that an oscillating laser path is effective in eliminating δ-ferrite and weld defects. J. Li et al. [20] showed that columnar crystals were broken due to the movement and turbulence of the weld pool, while the formation of skeletal ferrite phases was increased in FZ of SUS316L austenite stainless steel. K. Hao et al. [21] carried out comparative analysis of laser welding and laser-arc hybrid welding of a carbon structural steel Q235 with and without beam oscillation. Their results showed that beam oscillation increased side-plate ferrite, broke up blocky ferrite, and reduced proeutectoid ferrite.

High-strength low-alloyed (HSLA) steel has significant potential for lightweight structures, especially drivetrain parts that require high strength, toughness, and wear resistance, providing an alternative to other high-strength materials such as titanium, aluminum, and their composites. However, welding of low-carbon steels is accompanied by uncontrolled tempering of HAZ, which results in grain coarsening (CGHAZ) close to FZ. This grain coarsening reduces toughness and fatigue strength [22,23]. Generally, grain boundary ferrite, Widmanstatten ferrite, acicular ferrite, some martensite together with a minor amount of retained austenite, and pearlite can be formed during the welding of low-carbon steels, depending on the cooling rate and rate of carbon diffusion [24,25]. The impact toughness of steel increases significantly with an increasing amount of acicular ferrite in CGHAZ, while Widmanstatten ferrite can provide easy path for crack, reducing toughness [26].

Furthermore, carburization is often employed to enhance the strength and toughness of HSLA steels. The increased carbon content and subsequent heat treatment result in the formation of a martensitic structure, which is known to be challenging to weld using fusion welding processes due to the short cooling times experienced after welding [27]. Preheat, post-weld heat treatment, or filler metals are usually applied to maintain homogeneous hardness distribution between the weld seam and carburized base metal. Thus, the application of laser beam welding to HSLA steels still poses challenges.

The present study discussed a low-frequency laser wobble welding of HSLA steel plates in a lap configuration with the presence of gap. The weldability, weld microstructure, and microhardness distribution between a low-carbon and carburized HSLA steel plates under oscillating laser beam conditions were analyzed.

Materials and methods

Series of experiments on laser wobble welding were designed with HSLA steel plates. The chemical composition with respect to the technical documentation of the materials is presented in Table 1. The microstructure of the top HC380L plate had a typical ferritic-pearlitic structure with equiaxed ferrite grains (Fig. 1b-с). 16MnCr5 plate was carburized (0.3 mm) with following austenization, quenching, and tempering at 180 °C for 2 h. The initial microstructure of the lower 16MnCr5 plate was martensite-bainite (Fig. 1d and e).

The plates were clamped in a lap configuration as 1.5 mm thick top HC380L and 5 mm thick lower 16MnCr5 plate with a gap. The experiments were held with ytterbium laser of 0.295 mm focused beam diameter. The IPG D50 wobble welding head adaptor was used to enable the oscillation of the laser beam via the laser transmitter. A counterclockwise circular pattern was employed as the wobble path. The focus position was kept at −2 mm for each welding. No shielding gas was employed.

Laser wobble welding parameters were varied in wobbling amplitude, frequency, laser power, and welding speed (Table 2). The process parameters were based on the preliminary experimental study that identified the optimal parameters for creating a keyhole welding seam.

Samples were observed in cross-sections using an optical microscope after undergoing standard metallographic preparation and Nital etching. The precise microstructure, phase evaluation, and misorientation distribution measurements were done by EBSD analysis after vibration polishing of samples.

Microhardness was measured using Vickers testing with a load of 980 mN, and 10 s loading time as traverse across weld seams in the middle of seam in both plates (dot line in Fig. 1a). Measurements were also taken on the lower plate along carburized layer at a depth of 0.1 mm.

Result and discussion

Overall view. Macrostructure

The first step was to examine the weldability of plates in the presence of gaps. The cross sections of the seam welds with varying combinations of process parameters are depicted in Fig. 2. All welds were free from any porosity or cracks (Fig. 2a). The process parameters ensured full penetration of the weld seams (keyhole state) even in the presence of gaps in the workpieces. Each welds had an average penetration depth of 0.9 mm into the second plate, except for sample W-6 which had a penetration depth of 0.35 mm (heat conduction mode). In particular, increase in oscillating amplitude predominantly decreased the seam penetration, while the influence of frequency was low (see the comparation between samples W-2, W-3, and W-6 in Fig. 2). Furthermore, the weld beads were free from spatter.

Weld seam cross sections: polish state (a); overall macrostructure (b); transition area between FZ and BM in the lower plate of samples W-1 (c), W-4 (d), and W-6 (e).

The second step was to analyze morphology of the weld seams, since the profile of seams produced using an oscillating laser beam may exhibit an asymmetric geometry due to the non-equilibrium distribution of laser energy along the wobble path [28]. The experimental cross sections demonstrated predominantly symmetric weld seams due to low oscillating frequency (Fig. 2b). This effect can be primarily attributed to the increased rotation speed of the keyhole across the molten pool [29].

The third stage involved the evaluation of the welds' macrostructure. Welded seams were presented by a classical three distinct zones: FZ, HAZ, and base metal (BM) as depicted in Fig. 2b. The applied range of process parameters had no discernible effect on microstructure changes, The microstructure of all the samples was identical throughout the seam in cross-section (Fig. 2 c-e). The size of HAZ in each sample corresponded to the generated heat input. Namely, a deeper weld seam resulted in a wider HAZ (Table 3). Following to microstructure features all samples exhibited symmetric microhardness distribution traverse across weld seams.

Microstructure

Microstructure of HAZ in top HC380L plate experienced partial recrystallization in the wide region arising from austenite to ferrite transformation (Fig. 3b). Namely, during the welding process, a portion of ferrite retained its initial state, while another portion transformed into austenite. Subsequent austenite decomposition under decreased cooling rate over the repeatable wobble path of the laser beam leads to the formation of a gradual transition in HAZ. It started from ferrite along austenite grains, so-called grain boundary allotriomorphs, in region close to BM and proceed to Widmanstätten ferrite close to coarse grain heat-affected zone (CGHAZ) (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, the rise in carbide content within this region probably coincides with the nucleation of acicular ferrite (Fig. 3b). This is subsequently succeeded by bainite transformation in CGHAZ under a high repeatable temperature (Fig. 3c).

Microstructure of the top HC280LA plate of sample W-2: IPF map bainite in FZ (a); overall view between FZ and BM (b), where All.fer. is allotriomorphic ferrite (3), W.fer. is Widmanstätten ferrite (1–2), and Acic.fer. is acicular ferrite (1–2); IPF map of bainite in CGHAZ (c).

The microstructure along welded region in the lower plate showed a surface relief similar to martensite-bainite microstructure of BM (Fig. 4). The specific condition owing to oscillating laser beam created self-tempering of martensite in HAZ. Notably, the size of laths did not experienced differences along HAZ. However, temperature gradient still remined non-equilibrium and resulted in a higher reheated area closer to FZ. This caused the formation of bainite along with tempered martensite (Fig. 4e). The formation of bainite in HAZ of both plates was corresponded with an increase in cementite content, given its notably low carbon solubility (Fig. 6b) [29].

The microstructure of FZ in both plates was predominantly bainitic structure of lath-like morphology with some content of retained austenite (Fig. 3, Fig. 4b). Increased cooling rate at the bottom of FZ resulted in a less size of surface relief laths in the lower plate then in the top one. The grain misorientation distribution in FZ and CGHAZ of the top plate and FZ and HAZ of carburized depth showed a martensite-bainite transformation with a typical feature of relatively high intensity in the range between 0 and 25 deg. and 50–60 deg. in both plates (Fig. 5) [30,31]. The pronounced peak at 60 deg. in the lower 16MnCr5 plate demonstrated a higher fraction of bainite in HAZ of carburized layer (Fig. 5e) [32]. The formation of constant high angle grain misorientation through FZ-HAZ transition region eliminated micro-cracks. Together with this, misorientation distribution in HAZ of the top plate showed bainite-Widmanstätten ferrite laths angle distribution with high frequency of low boundaries angles (Fig. 5c).

Microhardness

The microhardness profiles were similar for all the samples, indicating that the microhardness is independent of the process parameters used and is mainly dependent on the microstructure features. The microhardness distribution across the weld seam of the top HC280LA plate showed gradual increase from 250 HV to 395 ± 25 HV in HAZ, which is correspond to austenite-ferrite transformation (Fig. 6a). The increase in microhardness of HAZ in the lower 16MnCr5 plate gave rise to self-tempering of the martensite, with a hardness of 443 ± 15HV in core of the plate (Fig. 6b) and 549 ± 10HV in the carburized layer (Fig. 6c). The elevated cementite content within the carburized layer resulted in a uniform increase in microhardness of approximately 80 HV when compared to the weld seam's core.

The average microhardness of FZ in the top plate, carburized layer, and lower plate was 354 ± 20 HV, 410 ± 35 HV, and 364 ± 20 HV, respectively (Fig. 6). Therefore, the microhardness throughout the entire FZ of dissimilar joint was comparable owing to bainite and martensite formation.

Conclusion

The weld seam morphologies, microstructure and microhardness distribution between HSLA steel plates of a low-carbon and carburized states after the welding with circular laser beam oscillation was discussed. The conclusions can be summarized as follows:

1.Laser welding of dissimilar plates with the presence of a gap was done in a keyhole mode using an oscillating laser beam. The cross sections of the weld seams were symmetric and free of defects.

Laser welding of dissimilar plates with the presence of a gap was done in a keyhole mode using an oscillating laser beam. The cross sections of the weld seams were symmetric and free of defects.

2.The dimensions of heat affected zone (HAZ) were proportional to the amount of heat input: wider HAZ corresponded to deeper fusion zone (FZ). The applied range of the process parameters did not have an effect on the microstructure variations observed between different samples.

The dimensions of heat affected zone (HAZ) were proportional to the amount of heat input: wider HAZ corresponded to deeper fusion zone (FZ). The applied range of the process parameters did not have an effect on the microstructure variations observed between different samples.

3.The microstructure throughout the entire FZ was uniform and predominantly presented with bainitic structure. The carburized layer of the lower plate contained a slightly higher cementite content that resulted in variation of microhardness distribution with a difference of 80 HV.

The microstructure throughout the entire FZ was uniform and predominantly presented with bainitic structure. The carburized layer of the lower plate contained a slightly higher cementite content that resulted in variation of microhardness distribution with a difference of 80 HV.

4.The oscillating laser path induced a repeatable heating of the welding materials along the welding direction, leading to self-tempering of HAZ in both plates. As a result, the microstructure of HAZ in the top HC280LA plate experienced Widmanstätten and acicular ferrite formation to bainite transformation, whereas the lower 16MnCr5 plate had a self-tempering of initial martensite structure along with bainite formation.

Microhardness profile across the weld seam in the top HC280LA plate gradually decreased towards base metal (BM) due to an increase in the ferrite content. Whereas the increase of microhardness across HAZ of the lower 16MnCr5 plate owned to tempered martensite and bainite.

In conclusion, while this study has provided valuable observation of microstructure and microhardness distribution of HSLA steel plates welded using an oscillating laser beam, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. The scope of this research did not cover thermal field simulation, and the evaluation of mechanical properties. Future research endeavors will therefore focus on these aspects.
