Understanding online hospital patient reviews: a nationwide cross-sectional analysis of polarity, actionability and themes
Authors: Siu Yu Zoe Lau, Matthew P Tse, Irfan Dhalla, Dhruv Nayyar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient experience data can support hospital quality improvement initiatives. Unsolicited online hospital reviews have emerged as an alternative data source in addition to traditional survey methods. However, the content and actionability of qualitative online reviews for Canadian hospitals are unknown.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide cross-sectional study analysing Google Review text comments of Canadian acute care hospitals from 2017 to 2022. We analysed a random sample of 1000 comments from June 2017 to June 2022. We characterised the polarity, actionability and thematic content of these comments.ResultsPatients were more likely to leave negative reviews (47.9%) than mixed (19.8%) or positive (32.3%) reviews. Most reviews (74.0%) were semiactionable or very actionable. Negative or mixed reviews were generally more actionable than positive reviews (p<0.001). Lastly, patients were most likely to comment on interpersonal interactions with staff and wait times for care.ConclusionsQualitative online patient reviews contain actionable feedback. This can provide a source of real-time data to monitor and improve on patient experience.

Introduction

A good patient experience is associated with better clinical outcomes and patient safety.1 2 Standardised and publicly reported surveys are used in some countries to collect patient experience data, such as the Hospital Consumers Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (HCAHPS) survey in the USA, National Health Service (NHS) Patient Survey Program in England, and previously the Canadian Patient Experiences Survey on Inpatient Care (CPES-IC) from the Canadian Institute of Health Information (CIHI). However, traditional survey methodology has its limitations. For one, surveys use specific questions that may not capture which components of healthcare services are most important to patients and lack responsiveness.3 4 Surveys also require significant resources, are collected and reported infrequently, and have low and declining response rates.3 5 Specific to Canada, the CPES-IC was only available from a subset of hospitals and the results were only partially reported publicly.6 7 Moreover, CIHI recently ceased operations of the Canadian Patient Experiences reporting system in April 2025, which collected the information from the CPES-IC. While CPES-IC remains available for use to individual institutions, there is no longer standardised national patient experience data in Canada.8

Alongside these changes in the patient experience surveying environment, there has been growing interest in using online patient reviews of hospitals to supplement our understanding of patient experiences. By nature of being publicly available and unsolicited, online patient reviews are less resource intensive, more frequently reported as they are not restricted to specific survey administration periods and are driven by patients’ priorities. Online patient reviews across different platforms (eg, NHS website, Yelp, Google, Facebook) have been shown to correlate with standardised patient experience survey measures and some clinical quality metrics.913 For instance, ratings of patient experiences on the NHS website correlated with ratings from a national paper-based survey. Furthermore, clinical outcomes, such as hospital standardised mortality ratio, were at least as strongly associated with patient online comments as traditional national survey measures of patient experience.9 Similar studies have looked at data from commercial review platforms as well. In the USA, Yelp,10 11 Google12 and Facebook13 star ratings have been shown to correlate with HCAHPS ratings. A systematic review showed correlation between online patient reviews and patient experiences, and mixed associations with quality measures.14 However, only one study of online hospital reviews has included Canadian hospitals,15 and most prior studies have focused on quantitative hospital ratings with limited analysis of narrative text comments. No previous studies have characterised the actionability of qualitative online hospital reviews to inform hospitals’ and health systems’ quality improvement activities. In this study, we sought to analyse the content and actionability of a large nationwide sample of Google Review text comments of Canadian acute care hospitals to characterise how they may be used to improve patient care.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional study analysing Google Review text comments of Canadian acute care hospitals. We report this study in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) and Reporting of Studies using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data (RECORD) guidelines.

Data sources and collection

Two publicly accessible data sources were used. First, we used the CIHI List of Beds Staffed and in Operation to identify the name and location of all Canadian acute care hospitals.16 Next, we downloaded Google Review star ratings and text comments for all Canadian acute care hospitals from 2014 to 2022 using Scrapehero.com. Google Reviews are unsolicited and publicly available online. Each Google Review publishes the reviewer’s name and must have a valid associated email address. Google does not allow for more than one review per institution from a single email address.17 Data were extracted between June and July 2022. Detailed methods of how hospitals were identified in Google Reviews and how data were linked between our data sources are published elsewhere.15

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included acute care hospitals, defined as any hospital with staffed acute care beds in the CIHI List of Beds Staffed and in Operation. We excluded hospitals that exclusively provided rehabilitation, psychiatric or long-term care services. Text comments with less than five words were excluded. From 53 637 total reviews extracted from acute care hospitals, 3496 (6.5%) were excluded due to not meeting the minimum word count. Reviews originally written in any language were eligible for inclusion; all reviews were automatically translated by Google into English prior to data extraction.

Data analysis and variables

We analysed a random sample of 1000 comments, with 497 comments from June 2017 to June 2019 and 503 comments from June 2020 to June 2022 to capture potential differences in patient experience related to the COVID-19 pandemic. We used the randomisation function on the Google Sheet to create the random sample. Google reviewers’ names were removed from the dataset to minimise identifiability and reduce bias during data analysis. As the differences between the two time periods were modest (table 1), we analysed the data as a single dataset.

Distribution of polarity, actionability and themes of Google Review text comments across time periods

We defined a coding matrix a priori based on literature review, then refined iteratively. Comments were coded for polarity, actionability and thematic categories. Polarity was coded as positive, negative or mixed.18 19 Actionability was coded as actionable, semiactionable or very actionable using a set of specificity factors.18 20 The specificity factors were who (eg, doctor, nurse, clerk), what (describes specific event), when (describes frequency or timing of event), where (eg, ward, clinic) and how (how event impacted writer’s experience). Not actionable was defined as having 0–1 specificity factors, semiactionable as having 2–3 factors and very actionable as having 4–5 factors. The coding matrix for polarity and actionability was modelled off studies that examined other forms of qualitative feedback in healthcare.1820 Thematic analysis was performed using a hybrid deductive–inductive approach. Thematic categories were first identified using the CPES-IC, then adjusted and refined based on the initial analysis of 50 comments.

Two researchers (SYZL and MPT) independently coded the first 50 comments, then met to discuss the differences and revise the coding matrix as needed. The first 50 comments were recoded using the updated coding matrix. The two researchers (SYZL and MPT) then independently coded the next 50 comments, after which both coders and the senior researcher (DN) met to resolve any differences and finalise the coding matrix. The two researchers (SYZL and MPT) both independently coded the remaining 900 comments for polarity and actionability, and any discrepancies were resolved by the senior researcher (DN). One researcher (SYZL) independently coded the remaining 900 comments for thematic content. The research team recognised that their healthcare, research and personal experiences could influence their analysis and interpretation of the data. The iterative coding process and resolution mechanism for discrepancies were used to promote reflexivity and consistency.

Statistical analysis

We analysed comments’ polarity, actionability and thematic content using general descriptive statistics. We used χ2 test to compare categorical variables with each other, including time period, polarity and actionability. To compare categorical variables with continuous variables (mean Google Star rating, word count or number of themes), we used analysis of variance. All statistical analyses were done using SPSS V.29.0. Results with p value less than 0.05 were interpreted as statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Of 1000 Google Review comments, 47.9% were negative, 19.8% were mixed and 32.3% were positive in polarity (table 1). In terms of actionability, most comments (74.0%) were semiactionable or very actionable. Specifically, 26.0% of comments were not actionable, 46.7% were semiactionable 27.3% were very actionable. There was no significant difference in polarity or actionability of comments between the 2017–2019 and 2020–2022 time periods (p=0.07 and 0.21, respectively), nor was there a difference in their thematic content (table 1). There was a significant association between comments’ polarity and their actionability (p<0.001). The proportion of comments that were semiactionable or very actionable was 80.8% for negative comments, compared with 58.8% for positive comments (table 4). When compared with non-actionable comments, very actionable comments were also longer (150.2±113.1 words vs 21.2±20.2 words, p<0.001) and commented on a greater number of themes (3.0±1.2 themes vs 1.1±0.9 themes, p<0.001). In our descriptive analysis of thematic frequency, negative comments were most likely to comment on wait times, interactions with nurses and perceived competency of staff (53.2%, 27.6% and 24.2% of comments, respectively) whereas positive comments were most likely to comment on interactions with unspecified staff, physicians and nurses (46.8%, 43.7% and 35.6% of comments, respectively) (online supplemental table S1).

Interpretation

Summary

Our study has three main findings. First, we found that most (74%) of Google Reviews were semiactionable or very actionable, suggesting they could be a useful source of data for hospital quality improvement. Second, we found that negative or mixed reviews were generally more actionable than positive reviews. Lastly, in this large sample of unsolicited reviews of Canadian hospitals, patients were most likely to comment on interpersonal interactions with staff as well as wait times for care.

Explanation of findings

To our knowledge, only one other study has looked at the actionability of narrative patient feedback.20 One of the key differences from our study is that they collected narrative comments from a random sample of patients using the Narrative Elicitation Protocol, rather than unsolicited online reviews. Furthermore, their study focused on outpatient care in the USA. Despite these differences, they found 80% of patient narratives contained actionable content,20 similar to our results. This suggests that narrative comments in general can offer actionable feedback for healthcare quality improvement. Negative experiences tended to be more actionable, which is also consistent with our findings, likely because they are inherently more likely to describe specific areas of improvement or events. In comparison, multiple studies have looked at polarity or valence of hospitals’ qualitative online patient reviews with mixed results. Two studies, one of Google Reviews of Portuguese hospitals21 and another of RateMD reviews of US hospitals,22 also found that most comments were negative although at a higher proportion than ours (62%–65% were negative, 26%–32% were positive, remainder were mixed/neutral). The opposite, where positive reviews are most common,23 24 have also been reported. One study had a significantly different distribution than others looking at Twitter (now known as X). Of tweets sent to hospitals in the NHS, 47% were positive, only 6% were negative and the remainder were neutral.24 This may reflect how inherent differences in online platforms and current events can impact results. For example, the most common use of Twitter in that study was related to fundraising, and the majority of these tweets had a positive sentiment. Many tweets also referenced celebrities, either seeking their support or were related to fundraising.24 These factors are relatively unique to Twitter and may explain why studies of other online platforms did not find a large proportion of positive comments. We are cognizant that differences in coding matrices and inclusion criteria limit direct comparability between studies as well.

In terms of thematic content, our findings of interpersonal interactions and wait times being predominant themes align with results from similar studies in the USA. Interactions with providers in hospitals have previously been shown to be the most frequently mentioned domain.23 25 Another study on Facebook reviews identified that communication, waiting time and helpfulness of treatments were highly associated with star ratings.26 Of Google Reviews in the emergency department setting, ‘wait’, ‘physician’ and ‘other staff’ were also the most frequent topics.4 Notably, multiple studies including ours identified themes outside of the domains used in traditional patient experience surveys.420 2527 Wait times are an important example of this. Wait times are covered in only 2 of 32 questions on HCAHPS, 0 of 22 questions on CPES-IC-6M and 4 of 41 questions on CPES-IC-20M, yet have consistently been shown to be a common theme in qualitative feedback. This again suggests that narrative reviews contain insight that is not currently being captured in standardised patient survey programmes. In the Canadian context, prior studies exploring the thematic content of patient experience data have been limited to very specific patient populations, such as patients on or nearing dialysis28 or older adults being discharged home from the emergency department,29 which are less applicable to our findings.

Future directions

Qualitative online patient reviews offer unique, actionable and real-time data. Negative feedback can be used to identify pain points for improvement and positive feedback can be used to reinforce or incentivise effective behaviours. The most common themes likely reflect areas of importance to patients and can serve as targets for quality improvement. Qualitative online patient reviews are thus an exciting area of research that can supplement, but not replace, traditional patient experience data. One of the challenges in this field though is that manual analysis of qualitative comments is resource intensive. Sentiment analysis, natural language processing and machine learning (ML) have emerged as powerful tools that can address this.30 These tools can synthesise large volumes of unstructured free-text data, and indeed researchers have begun using them to understand patient feedback over the past decade.2425 3034 We anticipate healthcare organisations to turn to these tools in the future, as artificial intelligence solutions continue to expand and online patient reviews become increasingly ubiquitous. However, how actionable content relevant to quality of care can be systematically filtered remains to be determined and the ‘best approach’ at deploying ML tools remains unknown.35

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, our study is subject to selection bias. Patients must have the ability to access and navigate the Google Review site in order to have their perspectives included. Patients with lower socioeconomic statuses, lower health literacy or who communicate most effectively in languages other than English were thus likely to have been underrepresented in our sample. Similar factors can also impact response bias. Studies have demonstrated that non-white ethnic groups, non-English native speakers and lower income groups are less likely to respond to patient surveys.36 37 Various examples of how health informatic interventions can introduce inequality have also been described, as they tend to be more accessible, adopted by, adhered to and effective for socioeconomically advantaged groups.38 Second, larger hospitals which have more patient encounters were more likely to be represented in our study. Many smaller Canadian hospitals fell below the threshold of 10 reviews per year to be included in a prior study on Google Reviews.15 Small hospitals may not have enough online reviews for feedback to be helpful. Third, the limit of 1 Google Review per institution from a single email address may limit patients’ ability to describe their experience across multiple visits. Finally, the coding process in our study was inherently somewhat subjective. Although this was mitigated by having multiple reviewers for polarity and specificity, the themes were only reviewed by one author.

Conclusions

Qualitative comments from Google Reviews can provide actionable patient feedback on hospitals. This form of data can supplement more traditional patient experience data and contribute to hospital quality improvement initiatives.
