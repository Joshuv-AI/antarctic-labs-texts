Infected intra-abdominal hematoma post-laparoscopic one-anastomosis mini gastric bypass: case report and literature review

ABSTRACT

Early imaging and timely intervention are essential for successful management and improved patient outcomes.

Introduction

Laparoscopic one-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB), also known as mini-gastric bypass, is a widely performed bariatric procedure routinely done worldwide for the management of morbid obesity and type 2 diabetes[1].

OAGB provides favorable postoperative outcomes, including significant weight loss within the first 2 years and high rates of diabetes remission[2].

Despite favorable outcomes, this procedure carries a risk of complications, most commonly bile reflux. Other less frequent complications include intra-abdominal bleeding, anastomotic leaks, obstruction, stenosis, and marginal ulcers[3].

Although OAGB is generally regarded as a safe bariatric procedure, postoperative bleeding remains an uncommon but recognized complication, with reported incidences ranging from 1.6% to 4.7% in large clinical series[4,5].

Most bleeding episodes are self-limiting or successfully managed conservatively; however, progression to an infected intra-abdominal hematoma is exceptionally rare.

In this context, we present a rare case of an infected peri-gastric hematoma following OAGB, which presented with sepsis in the absence of early abdominal signs, posing a significant diagnostic challenge, and was successfully managed with percutaneous drainage, resulting in good clinical recovery.

This report adheres to the SCARE guidelines to ensure accurate and transparent presentation of clinical findings and outcomes[6].

Case presentation

A 29-year-old female with class II obesity (BMI 36 kg/m2) presented for evaluation of bariatric surgery following unsuccessful attempts at weight loss through dietary and lifestyle modifications. She reported progressive weight gain over 11 months, associated with reduced physical activity. After appropriate pre-operative assessment, she was scheduled for elective laparoscopic mini gastric bypass. On admission, she had no acute complaints, and baseline laboratory investigations were within normal limits.

She underwent an uneventful laparoscopic mini gastric bypass under general anesthesia. Intraoperative findings were normal, with no adhesions or intra-abdominal pathology. A gastric pouch was created over a 38 French bougie, and a loop gastrojejunostomy was constructed approximately 180 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz. The anastomosis was intact on methylene blue leak testing, hemostasis was achieved, and a drain was placed. Blood loss was minimal, and there were no intraoperative complications.

Her immediate post-operative course was stable. On post-operative day 1, she reported no new complaints, with pain well-controlled. She was started on a clear liquid diet, though tolerance was suboptimal due to bloating and reduced appetite. Early mobilization and physiotherapy were encouraged. By post-operative day 2, she was clinically improved, tolerating oral intake, and was discharged home with appropriate medications and follow-up plans. At early follow-up, she remained asymptomatic, with stable vital signs and well-healing wounds.

However, approximately 11 days post-operatively, she presented with a new onset of fever and headache. Initially, she had no abdominal pain or respiratory complaints, and examination revealed fever with otherwise stable vital signs, clean surgical wounds, and no abdominal signs. Laboratory investigations demonstrated significant leukocytosis, neutrophilia, anemia, thrombocytosis, and markedly elevated inflammatory markers, consistent with early sepsis. Despite the initiation of antibiotics, her condition progressed over the next few days, with the development of persistent fever, pleuritic chest pain, shortness of breath, bilious vomiting, epigastric pain, and generalized fatigue.

On re-evaluation, she remained febrile with mild tachycardia and epigastric tenderness, while surgical wounds remained clean and well-healed. Repeat laboratory findings showed persistent leukocytosis and anemia. Given the worsening clinical picture, imaging studies were performed. Abdominal ultrasound revealed a large septated fluid collection in the epigastric region and left upper quadrant. A subsequent CT scan confirmed a large perigastric high-attenuation collection with peripheral rim enhancement and surrounding inflammatory changes, without evidence of an anastomotic leak. Findings were consistent with an infected peri-gastric hematoma (Fig. 1). A diagnosis of delayed post-operative intra-abdominal infected hematoma complicated by sepsis was made. The patient was readmitted and underwent CT-guided percutaneous drainage, achieving definitive source control. Following the intervention, her clinical condition improved significantly, with the resolution of fever, normalization of inflammatory markers, and gradual improvement in hemoglobin levels.

During the recovery phase, she remained clinically stable with improving laboratory parameters. At 1-month follow-up, she was afebrile, pain-free, and had no abdominal symptoms, with normalization of laboratory findings.

Discussion

First performed by Rutledge in 1997, OAGB combined the principles of Collis gastroplasty and an antecolic Billroth II loop gastrojejunostomy and was termed “mini” due to its simplified surgical approach[7].

The mechanism of OAGB is multifactorial, involving non-obstructive restriction, mild malabsorption, and altered fat tolerance, leading to weight loss and improved diabetes control[1].

The American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery (ASMBS) outlines indications for metabolic and bariatric surgery based on BMI, recommending surgery for individuals with a BMI ≥35 kg/m2 regardless of the presence or severity of comorbidities[8].

Our patient, with a BMI of 36 kg/m2, met ASMBS criteria for bariatric surgery, supporting the appropriateness of surgical intervention.

Among the spectrum of bariatric surgical options, OAGB was selected for our patient as it is a safe, simple, and effective alternative to Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), with less technical complexity, shorter operative time, and greater reversibility compared to other procedures, such as sleeve gastrectomy and biliopancreatic diversion[9].

International Federation for the Surgery of Obesity and Metabolic Disorders (IFSO) data shows that OAGB accounts for approximately 1.8% of bariatric procedures worldwide[10].

It involves creating a lesser curvature gastric conduit to or below the “crow’s foot,” with an anastomosis to an antecolic jejunal loop 150–200 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz[1].

An eight-year retrospective study by Helmy et al reported postoperative bleeding in 14 of 300 patients (4.67%) after OAGB, more commonly intra-abdominal than intraluminal, showing it to be a relatively uncommon complication[4].

Postoperative bleeding may arise from several anatomical sites, most commonly the gastrojejunostomy, and less frequently from the gastric remnant, trocar insertion sites, or the jejunojejunostomy suture line[11].

A series study by Zakaria et al reported postoperative bleeding in 5 of 310 patients (1.6%) undergoing OAGB, presenting as intra-abdominal bleeding with signs including tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, abdominal distension, and visible blood from surgical drains[5].

In contrast, our patient did not exhibit any of these signs postoperatively, representing an unusual presentation that posed a significant diagnostic challenge.

Despite this complication, most intra-abdominal bleeding cases resolve spontaneously and are successfully managed conservatively, with only a few requiring invasive interventions such as laparoscopy[4].

Seven-year retrospective analysis by Carbajo et al of 1200 patients undergoing OAGB documented 12 cases of intra-abdominal hematoma, with only one progressing to an infected hematoma that required further intervention[3].

Our case represents one of the rare occurrences of infected intra-abdominal hematoma, as documented in the literature.

Percutaneous drainage is an effective treatment for infected intra-abdominal hematomas, facilitating the evacuation of thick or organized collections; however, it requires close monitoring due to the risk of tube obstruction, may necessitate catheter adjustment, and often involves a prolonged drainage duration compared to other abscesses[12].

Our patient underwent drainage with a 24 Fr catheter, achieved adequate drainage without tube-related complications, and had a favorable outcome within 6 days.

Although rare, postoperative complications such as intra-abdominal bleeding and infected hematoma contribute to surgical morbidity and require evaluation, with the Clavien–Dindo classification providing a standardized framework to assess severity and guide management[13].

Our case was classified as Grade IIIa, as it required percutaneous intervention without general anesthesia, and was further discussed in a hospital morbidity meeting to enhance awareness and promote learning for improved clinical practice.

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare presentation of an infected intra-abdominal hematoma following OAGB, notable for the absence of early clinical signs and the resulting diagnostic difficulty. Prompt imaging and timely percutaneous drainage led to successful management and favorable recovery, emphasizing the need for high clinical suspicion in atypical postoperative presentations.
