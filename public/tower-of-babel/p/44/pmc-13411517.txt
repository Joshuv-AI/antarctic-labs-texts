Background

Although advances in phacoemulsification technology and fluidics have markedly reduced clinically apparent thermal complications, understanding the mechanisms governing intraocular heat generation remains essential for optimizing ultrasound energy delivery.

Longitudinal ultrasound involves forward-and-backward oscillation of the phaco tip and has been associated with frictional heat generation, particularly during continuous activation and under conditions of reduced irrigation flow. Our group previously demonstrated that continuous longitudinal ultrasound induces a rapid rise in anterior chamber temperature, whereas modifications in ultrasound delivery patterns suppress this increase. These findings indicate that intraocular temperature behavior depends not only on nominal power settings but also on the mechanical characteristics of tip motion [1].

Several experimental and clinical studies have compared the thermal profiles of longitudinal and torsional ultrasound modalities. Early comparative investigations demonstrated that torsional ultrasound generates less heat than longitudinal ultrasound at the incision site [2]. Subsequent infrared thermographic analyses in cadaveric and porcine eye models consistently showed lower incision and probe temperatures with torsional ultrasound, particularly under continuous high-power conditions [3, 4]. Similar findings have been reported in porcine eye experiments evaluating different phaco tip and sleeve designs, indicating that both vibration modality and mechanical configuration influence heat generation [5].

In vivo thermal imaging studies have further suggested that temperature rise during phacoemulsification correlates more closely with ultrasound amplitude than with vibration mode alone. Probe shaft and wound temperatures increase proportionally with applied ultrasound amplitude during torsional phacoemulsification, supporting the concept that the magnitude of tip excursion plays a critical role in heat production [5].

In parallel, recent calorimetric investigations have highlighted important limitations of machine-derived energy metrics, such as cumulative dissipated energy (CDE). Although CDE is widely used as a surrogate indicator of ultrasound energy delivery, it is calculated from activation time and power settings and does not directly represent actual thermal energy generation at the phaco tip. Calorimetric measurements have demonstrated discrepancies between CDE values and directly measured thermal output, suggesting that system-reported metrics may not accurately reflect intraocular thermal exposure across different ultrasound modalities [6, 7].

Importantly, most previous studies have primarily evaluated incision or probe surface temperatures. Direct assessment of bulk anterior chamber temperature behavior under strictly controlled and continuous ultrasound activation remains limited. Moreover, few investigations have attempted to examine temperature behavior of tip-excursion magnitude independent of vibration direction under standardized machine-level settings.

Therefore, the present study aimed to compare bulk anterior chamber temperature behavior between longitudinal and torsional ultrasound under controlled experimental conditions in a porcine eye model, including an exploratory comparison using nominally comparable machine-level tip-excursion settings based on manufacturer-reported stroke limits.

Methods

Porcine eye preparation

This experimental study followed the methodology reported in previous articles [1, 6], and was conducted using freshly enucleated porcine eyes obtained from a local abattoir. All eyes were collected after the animals were slaughtered for the food industry and were used within approximately 5–6 h after enucleation. Eyes with corneal opacity, anterior chamber leakage, or visible structural damage were excluded. Ocular hydration was maintained throughout the experiments by continuous irrigation to minimize post-mortem tissue dehydration. Because no live animals were used and no experimental procedures were performed on living subjects, institutional animal care and use committee approval was not required. Each experimental group included five porcine eyes (total n = 20 eyes).

Phacoemulsification system and settings

All experiments were performed using the Infiniti™ Vision System (Alcon Laboratories, Fort Worth, TX, USA) equipped with an OZil™ handpiece. A 0.9-mm phaco tip (Ozil12) and Ultra Sleeve™ were used in all procedures to ensure consistent irrigation conditions.

The torsional ultrasound mode (T group) operates at an oscillation frequency of approximately 32 kHz with a manufacturer-reported maximum effective oscillation amplitude of approximately 45 μm at 100% power. The longitudinal ultrasound mode (L group) operates at approximately 40 kHz with a reported maximum stroke length of approximately 95 μm at 100% power.

To minimize confounding variables, identical machine settings were applied in all experiments. The aspiration flow rate was set to 18 cc/min, vacuum pressure to 50 mmHg, and bottle height to 50 cm based on past articles [1, 6].

The relatively low aspiration pressure was intentionally selected to reduce heat removal by irrigation and thereby accentuate differences in bulk temperature behavior between ultrasound modalities under controlled low-flow experimental conditions. This setting was not intended to reproduce routine clinical cataract surgery, but rather to provide an experimental condition with limited heat dissipation to facilitate comparison between modalities. Similar low-flow experimental conditions have also been employed in previous mechanistic thermal studies evaluating intraocular temperature behavior [1, 6].

Ultrasound power was set to either 50% or 100%, and continuous ultrasound activation was used during each measurement period. These percentages represent the relative power scale of the Infiniti™ Vision System.

To explore the potential contribution of tip-excursion-related parameters independent of vibration mode, additional measurements were performed using 100% torsional power and 50% longitudinal power to create nominally comparable tip-excursion settings based on manufacturer-reported stroke limits. This approach was designed to compare thermal behavior under standardized machine-level settings rather than to assume physical equivalence of motion.

Surgical procedure and temperature measurement (Fig. 1)

Experimental setup for anterior chamber temperature measurement. Schematic illustration of the porcine eye model used for temperature measurement. A digital thermometer probe was positioned approximately 1 mm anterior to the phaco tip within the anterior chamber during ultrasound activation

A 2.4-mm clear corneal incision was created at the 12-o’clock position, followed by a side-port incision at the 3-o’clock position. The phaco tip was inserted and positioned at the center of the anterior chamber.

A digital temperature probe (CENTER 306) was inserted through the side port and positioned approximately 1 mm anterior to the phaco tip. Probe position was kept constant throughout the experiment.

Temperature recording began immediately before ultrasound activation. Because the experiments were performed under room-temperature ex vivo conditions without active warming, baseline anterior chamber temperatures were lower than physiological intraocular temperature. After activation, the anterior chamber temperature was recorded every 2 s for 90 s under identical environmental conditions, following the methodology described in a previous study [1].

Statistical analysis

Baseline temperatures were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Differences in temperature at 90 s were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

The 90-second time point was selected to represent the plateau phase of temperature stabilization, enabling standardized between-group comparison. In addition, early-phase temperature behavior and the rate of temperature increase were analyzed descriptively.

Nonparametric tests were used because of the small sample size and distribution characteristics. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline anterior chamber temperatures did not differ significantly between the longitudinal (L) and torsional (T) groups (Fig. 2).

Baseline anterior chamber temperature before ultrasound activation. Baseline anterior chamber temperature did not differ significantly between the longitudinal and torsional ultrasound groups

Immediately after ultrasound activation, anterior chamber temperature increased rapidly in both groups and reached a plateau at approximately 20 s, with slight variation among groups (Fig. 3). The initial temperature rise during the first 20 s was steeper in the longitudinal group than in the torsional group. After reaching the plateau phase, temperature changes progressed more gradually. Throughout the 90-second observation period, the temperature rise was consistently greater with longitudinal ultrasound than with torsional ultrasound.

Time course of anterior chamber temperature during ultrasound activation. Anterior chamber temperature increased rapidly immediately after ultrasound activation, reaching a plateau at approximately 20 s. Temperature elevation was consistently greater with longitudinal ultrasound than with torsional ultrasound. Values represent mean temperature measurements obtained from five eyes per group

At 100% ultrasound power, the mean anterior chamber temperature at 90 s after activation was 32.48 ± 0.50 °C in the T group and 37.52 ± 1.71 °C in the L group, indicating a significant between-group difference (P = 0.0189) (Fig. 4). At 50% ultrasound power, the mean temperature at 90 s was 28.94 ± 0.45 °C in the T group and 32.72 ± 0.55 °C in the L group, with a similarly significant difference (P = 0.0189) (Fig. 5).

Anterior chamber temperature at 90 s with ultrasound power set to 100%. Anterior chamber temperature at 90 s after ultrasound activation was significantly higher in the longitudinal ultrasound group than in the torsional ultrasound group (*P < 0.05; Mann–Whitney U test)

Anterior chamber temperature at 90 s with ultrasound power set to 50%. Anterior chamber temperature at 90 s after ultrasound activation was significantly higher in the longitudinal ultrasound group than in the torsional ultrasound group

To further explore the potential contribution of tip-excursion-related parameters independent of vibration mode, additional experiments were performed under nominally comparable machine-level tip-excursion settings (100% torsional power and 50% longitudinal power). Under these conditions, no significant difference in anterior chamber temperature at 90 s was observed between the two groups (P = 0.5284) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The present study should be interpreted as a mechanistic extension of our previous investigations into intraocular thermal behavior during phacoemulsification. In earlier porcine eye experiments, we demonstrated that continuous longitudinal ultrasound induces a rapid increase in anterior chamber temperature and that modifications in ultrasound delivery patterns can suppress this increase, highlighting the importance of tip motion characteristics in ultrasonic heat generation [1]. However, because elliptical motion combines longitudinal and lateral oscillations, the isolated contribution of lateral motion could not be determined.

In the present study, we addressed this limitation by using a torsional ultrasound system that produces predominantly lateral oscillation. Under standardized experimental conditions, torsional ultrasound generated a significantly smaller increase in anterior chamber temperature than conventional longitudinal ultrasound. These findings are consistent with previous laboratory studies reporting lower thermal signatures with torsional ultrasound at the incision and probe levels [2, 3]. Importantly, the present results extend previous observations by showing that the temperature difference between modalities was attenuated under nominally comparable machine-level tip-excursion settings based on manufacturer-reported stroke limits.

From a mechanical standpoint, differences in reported oscillation amplitude between longitudinal and torsional ultrasound provide a useful framework for interpreting these findings. Longitudinal phacoemulsification involves forward-and-backward motion with a reported maximum stroke length of approximately 95 μm at 100% power and a frequency of approximately 40 kHz, whereas torsional ultrasound operates with a smaller reported effective oscillation amplitude of approximately 45 μm at 100% power and a frequency of approximately 32 kHz. Both oscillation amplitude and frequency contribute to ultrasonic energy delivery; however, their relative contributions to intraocular temperature behavior may vary depending on experimental conditions.

It should be emphasized that torsional and longitudinal ultrasound represent fundamentally different mechanical motions. Torsional ultrasound produces rotational oscillation around the tip axis, whereas longitudinal ultrasound generates reciprocating linear displacement. Therefore, the manufacturer-reported oscillation amplitudes do not represent physically identical motion patterns. Accordingly, the present study evaluates temperature behavior under nominally comparable machine-level tip-excursion settings rather than assuming true mechanical equivalence of motion.

Our previous experimental studies suggested that tip-motion parameters may influence anterior chamber temperature behavior under certain experimental conditions [1, 6]. In the present study, anterior chamber temperature rise did not differ between torsional and longitudinal ultrasound when nominally comparable tip-excursion settings based on manufacturer-reported stroke limits were used, despite differences in vibration direction and frequency. These findings suggest that tip-motion characteristics, including excursion-related system parameters, may contribute to the smaller temperature rise observed with torsional ultrasound under the tested experimental conditions.

From a physical perspective, smaller effective tip motion may reduce frictional interaction between the phaco tip and surrounding fluid, thereby limiting heat generation. The temporal pattern of temperature change further supports this interpretation. In both ultrasound modes, anterior chamber temperature increased rapidly after activation and reached a plateau at approximately 20 s, suggesting that thermal equilibrium is achieved once heat production is balanced by irrigation and heat dissipation. Notably, torsional ultrasound also demonstrated an early temperature rise, emphasizing the importance of minimizing continuous ultrasound activation time regardless of vibration mode.

Although the absolute temperature range observed in this study remained below levels typically associated with overt thermal tissue injury, the consistent difference between ultrasound modalities under controlled experimental conditions may still provide mechanistic insight into intraocular thermal behavior during ultrasound activation. In our previous experimental study, anterior chamber temperature changes were shown to parallel temperature behavior near the corneal endothelium during phacoemulsification [6]. Therefore, although the present study did not evaluate corneal wound temperature, bulk anterior chamber temperature behavior may still provide information regarding intraocular thermal exposure affecting tissues other than the incision site. In contrast, corneal wound temperature is strongly influenced by direct tip-shaft friction and incision-related mechanical factors, which were outside the scope of the present study. Such differences in intraocular temperature behavior may become more relevant under prolonged ultrasound activation, where cumulative thermal exposure could increase. Accordingly, the present findings contribute to a better understanding of the physical mechanisms underlying intraocular heat generation during phacoemulsification.

Differences in oscillation frequency between longitudinal and torsional ultrasound may influence energy delivery characteristics; however, the absence of a temperature difference under nominally comparable tip-excursion settings suggests that frequency alone may not be the dominant determinant of intraocular temperature rise under the experimental conditions tested.

Recent calorimetric investigations provide additional context for interpreting these findings. Studies that directly quantified ultrasound energy have demonstrated that machine-reported CDE values do not reliably reflect actual thermal energy generation at the phaco tip and that energy output varies according to ultrasound modality and phaco tip design [7–9]. These observations support the concept that mechanical parameters at the tip level play a critical role in thermal behavior. Accordingly, tip-motion characteristics, including excursion-related system parameters, may represent a practical mechanistic contributor to intraocular thermal response under controlled experimental conditions.

Importantly, this study was not designed to evaluate corneal wound temperature or predict the risk of corneal wound burn. The measured temperatures reflect bulk intraocular thermal behavior under controlled experimental conditions rather than localized thermal accumulation at the incision site.

This study has several limitations. First, anterior chamber temperature was measured at a single point located approximately 1 mm anterior to the phaco tip. This approach does not capture spatial temperature gradients within the anterior chamber or localized hotspots near the corneal incision or endothelium. Previous studies have suggested that corneal wound burns may occur when localized temperatures exceed approximately 50 °C at the incision or probe surface, whereas the present measurements reflect bulk intraocular temperature behavior. Second, the experiments were performed using ex vivo porcine eyes, which may not fully replicate in vivo aqueous humor dynamics, wound architecture, or heat dissipation through surrounding tissues. Therefore, the clinical implications of these findings remain limited. In addition, because the eyes were obtained from a commercial abattoir, information regarding the age of the animals was not available, and age matching between specimens could not be performed. Third, the absence of lens material during ultrasound activation may limit direct extrapolation to clinical cataract surgery. However, this design enabled isolation of thermal behavior attributable to phaco tip motion itself without confounding effects from frictional interaction or occlusion caused by lens fragments. Fourth, the sample size was limited and the statistical analysis should be interpreted as exploratory. Finally, actual tip displacement was not independently measured in this study. Therefore, the exploratory comparison based on manufacturer-reported stroke limits should not be interpreted as a direct assessment of oscillation amplitude itself. Moreover, the low-flow experimental setting was intentionally selected to reduce heat dissipation and does not reflect routine clinical phacoemulsification conditions. Nevertheless, even within this physiological temperature range, relative differences in temperature behavior may reflect variations in cumulative thermal load under certain conditions.

Conclusions

This study extends our previous experimental work by examining intraocular thermal behavior during torsional and longitudinal ultrasound under controlled conditions. The findings suggest that tip-motion characteristics, including excursion-related system parameters, may contribute to anterior chamber temperature behavior under the tested experimental conditions. These results provide mechanistic insight into bulk intraocular heat generation during ultrasound activation and may help inform future optimization of ultrasound energy delivery parameters.
