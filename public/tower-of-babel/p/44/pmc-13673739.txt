Comorbidity phenotypes and the incidence and severity of acute kidney injury in older critically ill patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Liang Zhang, Zhi-yi Hao, Man Li, Jian-qi Liu, Meng Su, Zi-hao Zhang, Shuo-ying Yue, Yuan-yuan Liu, Chang-ping Li, Jun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe impact of specific comorbidity combinations on the risk and severity of acute kidney injury (AKI) in older critically ill patients remains unclear. This study aimed to identify comorbidity phenotypes using latent class analysis (LCA) and quantify their associations with hospital-acquired AKI.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study using the MIMIC-IV database on first-time ICU patients (> 65 years) with a length of stay ≥ 48 h. Patients with baseline chronic kidney disease (CKD) or early-onset AKI were excluded. LCA was applied to baseline chronic conditions to identify distinct comorbidity phenotypes. Subsequently, we employed multivariable Cox proportional hazards models to evaluate AKI incidence, and logistic regression to assess AKI severity (KDIGO Stage ≥ 2), adjusting for potential confounders.ResultsOf 5,721 eligible patients, 2,202 (38.5%) developed hospital-acquired AKI. LCA identified five phenotypes: Class 1 (Low Comorbidity), Class 2 (Respiratory & Nicotine), Class 3 (Isolated Metabolic & Atherosclerosis), Class 4 (Atrial Fibrillation & Complex Cardiac), and Class 5 (Ischemic Heart Failure). Compared to Class 1, Class 4 showed the highest AKI risk (HR 1.52, 95% CI 1.29–1.79, P < 0.001), followed by Class 5 (HR 1.41, 95% CI 1.20–1.65, P < 0.001). Class 2 (HR 1.34) and Class 3 (HR 1.14) also had elevated risks. Notably, secondary analysis revealed that Class 4 (OR 1.87) and Class 5 (OR 1.51) were strongly associated with progression to moderate-to-severe AKI, whereas Class 3 was not. Furthermore, in a Fine-Gray competing-risk model treating in-hospital death before AKI occurrence as the competing event, these associations remained generally consistent.ConclusionDistinct comorbidity phenotypes significantly influence the incidence and severity of hospital-acquired AKI. Phenotypes characterized by Atrial Fibrillation and Ischemic Heart Failure represent the highest-risk groups for severe renal outcomes, highlighting the potential value of targeted renal surveillance and individualized hemodynamic management in older critically ill patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07918-5.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, Multimorbidity, Older patients, MIMIC-IV database

Background

With the rapid acceleration of global population aging, older patients have become the predominant demographic in intensive care units (ICUs) [1, 2]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) represents one of the most common and serious complications in the critically ill, with disproportionately high morbidity and mortality rates among older adults [3, 4]. The aging kidney undergoes structural and functional degenerative changes—often termed “renal senescence”—rendering it increasingly susceptible to ischemic insults, hypoxia, and nephrotoxic agents [5–7]. Despite significant advancements in critical care medicine, the prognosis for older patients who develop AKI remains poor, associated with prolonged hospital LOS, increased healthcare costs, and a heightened long-term risk of progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD) [8–10].

Unlike their younger counterparts, critically ill older patients are typically characterized by multimorbidity, defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions [11–13]. While previous studies have established multimorbidity as an independent risk factor for AKI, most have relied on simple disease counts or weighted scoring systems [14, 15].These “variable-centered” approaches effectively quantify the overall disease burden but fail to capture the complex clustering patterns of comorbidities. For instance, the pathophysiological interplay within a cardiovascular-metabolic cluster may differ significantly from that of a respiratory-neurological cluster [16]. Treating multimorbidity as a single cumulative score obscures the specific hemodynamic and metabolic impacts that distinct disease combinations may exert on renal function [17].

To address the limitations of traditional variable-centered scoring systems, Latent Class Analysis (LCA) offers a ‘person-centered’ approach. Unlike traditional variable-centered methods (such as regression models or sum-scores) that evaluate the isolated, additive effect of individual diseases across a homogeneous population, LCA assumes population heterogeneity and classifies individuals into mutually exclusive latent classes based on their comprehensive pattern of disease co-occurrence, thereby capturing the complex, synergistic clinical profiles of the patients. Recent studies have increasingly recognized the utility of this approach in stratifying the clinical heterogeneity of AKI. For instance, Huang et al. and Barrow et al. applied LCA to identify distinct subphenotypes of established AKI for mortality and symptom prediction [18, 19], while Kim et al. utilized it to stratify preoperative risks in high-risk surgical cohorts [20]. However, these applications have primarily focused on phenotyping patients who have already developed renal dysfunction or are restricted to specific perioperative settings. Evidence remains limited regarding the use of LCA to cluster preexisting, baseline comorbidities to predict the subsequent incidence and severity of AKI, particularly in the highly vulnerable geriatric ICU population.

Therefore, utilizing the MIMIC-IV database, this study aimed to employ LCA to characterize latent comorbidity phenotypes at baseline and quantify their associations with renal outcomes.

Methods

Data selection

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the MIMIC-IV database (version 3.1), a publicly available critical care database containing comprehensive clinical records of patients admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (Boston, MA) between 2008 and 2022. Access to the database was granted to author Liang Zhang after the completion of the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) training course. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

The study population focused on older patients (aged > 65 years) admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU). To ensure the independence of observations, only the first ICU admission for each patient was included. Patients with hospital LOS < 48 h were excluded to allow sufficient observation time for the development of hospital-acquired conditions.

To specifically investigate the risk of hospital-acquired AKI, we applied strict exclusion criteria. Patients with a history of CKD were excluded to rule out baseline renal insufficiency. Furthermore, patients who developed AKI within the first 48 h of hospital admission were excluded. This ensured the elimination of community-acquired cases and established a true baseline free of AKI at the time of ICU admission. Ultimately, a final study cohort of 5,721 eligible patients was established. The detailed screening and selection process is illustrated in the study flow chart (Fig. 1).

Data collection

Data extraction was performed using Structured Query Language (SQL) to retrieve a comprehensive set of candidate predictors recorded within the first 24 h of ICU admission. Baseline characteristics included demographics (age, gender), pre-ICU hospital LOS, vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, GCS), and laboratory parameters (blood counts, electrolytes, glucose, BUN, and baseline eGFR), as well as the requirement for early clinical support (mechanical ventilation and vasopressors). Illness severity was assessed using multiple scoring systems, including SOFA, SAPS II, OASIS, APS III, SIRS, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). Additionally, we extracted data on acute conditions upon admission, such as sepsis, and a history of specific procedures, including percutaneous coronary intervention(PCI) and coronary artery bypass grafting(CABG).

Medication exposure was assessed using the MIMIC-IV eMAR table and was defined as at least one documented administration within the first 24 h after ICU admission. Records were restricted to those marked as “Administered” and included NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, vancomycin, and diuretics.

For latent class analysis, 14 prespecified chronic comorbidities common in older adults were identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes. These variables included hypertension, diabetes, obesity, coronary atherosclerosis, neurodegenerative disease, hyperlipidemia, chronic respiratory disease, sleep apnea, osteoporosis, depressive disorder, atrial fibrillation, thyroid disease, nicotine dependence, and heart failure. The detailed ICD-9 and ICD-10 code definitions for these comorbidities are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

To ensure data quality, variables with more than 20% missing values were excluded from the analysis. For the remaining variables, missing values were handled using random forest-based multiple imputation via the ‘mice’ package in R software.

Baseline serum creatinine adjudication

Baseline serum creatinine was derived directly from the final scr_baseline variable in the MIMIC-IV derived creatinine_baseline table, which is generated by the MIMIC-IV baseline creatinine concept query from the MIMIC Code Repository [21, 22]. The query defines baseline serum creatinine at the hospital-admission level according to a predefined hierarchy: the minimum serum creatinine value during the admission is used when it is ≤ 1.1 mg/dL; among patients with chronic kidney disease, the minimum in-hospital serum creatinine value is used regardless of whether it is elevated; and for all remaining patients, baseline serum creatinine is estimated using the simplified MDRD equation, assuming a baseline eGFR of 75 mL/min/1.73 m². Because patients with pre-existing chronic kidney disease were excluded from the present cohort, the CKD-specific branch of this algorithm did not apply to the final analytic population. Baseline eGFR was subsequently calculated from scr_baseline using the CKD-EPI equation and was used for cohort description and covariate adjustment.

Endpoints of interest

The primary endpoint of this study was the incidence of hospital-acquired AKI. AKI was defined and staged strictly in accordance with the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines. Diagnostic criteria included: (1) an increase in serum creatinine (SCr) to ≥ 1.5 times the baseline within the prior 7 days; (2) an increase in SCr of ≥ 0.3 mg/dL within 48 h; or (3) urine volume < 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 h or longer [23]. To specifically capture hospital-acquired events, only AKI episodes occurring more than 48 h after hospital admission were considered as the outcome.

For the time-to-event analysis, the time origin (time-zero) was defined as the time of ICU admission, with pre-ICU hospital LOS adjusted as a covariate to account for variations in transfer timing. The survival time was calculated as the interval from ICU admission to the diagnosis of AKI. Patients who did not develop AKI during their stay were censored at the time of hospital discharge, whereas death prior to AKI development was treated as a competing event.

The secondary endpoint was the severity of AKI. To assess disease progression, we defined a composite outcome of moderate-to-severe AKI (KDIGO Stage ≥ 2), based on the maximum AKI stage reached during the ICU stay.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range (IQR)] based on their normality distribution, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared across the identified latent classes using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

To identify distinct comorbidity patterns, LCA was performed using the ‘poLCA’ package in R, incorporating the 14 identified chronic comorbidities as binary indicator variables. We fitted models with 2 to 6 latent classes and selected the optimal number based on statistical fit indices (AIC, BIC, and entropy) and clinical interpretability. Patients were assigned to the class with the highest posterior membership probability. To further characterize the identified latent classes, a post hoc multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed with latent class membership as the dependent variable and Class 1 as the reference category. Age, sex, SOFA score, and baseline eGFR were included as independent variables to describe factors associated with latent class membership.

To evaluate the association between comorbidity phenotypes and hospital-acquired AKI, multivariable models were adjusted for potential confounders selected based on clinical knowledge, existing literature, and VIF analysis (all VIFs < 5) [24–26]. The adjustment covariates included demographics, pre-ICU hospital stay, baseline physiological and laboratory parameters, illness severity (OASIS score), and initial clinical support (mechanical ventilation and vasopressors). Specific medications administered later in the ICU were excluded to minimize overadjustment for potential mediators.

A multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for AKI incidence. For the competing-risk analysis, the primary event was hospital-acquired AKI, and the competing event was in-hospital death before AKI occurrence; patients discharged alive without AKI were treated as censored. A Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model was used to estimate the cumulative incidence of AKI while accounting for death before AKI as a competing event. For AKI severity, multivariable logistic regression was used to calculate odds ratios (ORs) for progression to moderate-to-severe AKI (KDIGO stage ≥ 2). The severity model included the same prespecified covariates as the primary Cox model.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0), and a two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For across-group evaluations with significant global p-values, post-hoc pairwise comparisons among the five latent classes were conducted with Bonferroni correction, yielding an adjusted significance threshold of P < 0.005.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 5,721 older critically ill patients were included in the final analysis. The mean age of the cohort was 76.3 (± 7.4) years, with an equal distribution of males and females (50.0%). The baseline renal function was well-preserved, with a median eGFR of 94.0 (IQR 87.4–100.2) mL/min/1.73 m².

Based on LCA, patients were classified into five latent classes. As shown in Table 1, baseline characteristics varied significantly across the groups (P < 0.001 for most variables). Class 1 (Low Comorbidity, 41.8%) had the lowest overall disease burden. Class 2 (6.7%) was characterized by high rates of chronic respiratory disease and nicotine dependence. Class 3 (34.4%) was dominated by metabolic traits, including hypertension and diabetes. Class 4 (8.6%) and Class 5 (8.5%) exhibited distinct cardiovascular profiles, driven by atrial fibrillation and ischemic heart failure, respectively.

In the post hoc multinomial logistic regression analysis, male sex, higher SOFA scores, and lower baseline eGFR were associated with membership in the cardiovascular-dominant classes, particularly Classes 4 and 5 (Supplementary Table S2), providing additional descriptive support for the clinical interpretability of the latent class profiles.

Overall, 2,202 of 5,721 patients (38.5%) developed hospital-acquired AKI. During hospitalization, 287 patients died, corresponding to an overall in-hospital mortality rate of 5.0%. In the competing-risk framework, 140 patients died before AKI occurrence and were therefore treated as competing events in the Fine-Gray analysis. The incidence of hospital-acquired AKI differed significantly among the clusters (P < 0.001). The incidence was highest in the cardiovascular-driven groups (Class 4: 55.8% and Class 5: 49.4%) compared to the reference Class 1 (33.2%). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons showed that the elevated AKI risks in Classes 4 and 5 remained statistically significant compared to Class 1 (all Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S3).

Identification of comorbidity patterns

LCA models with 2 to 6 classes were fitted to identify distinct comorbidity patterns. The model fit statistics are presented in Table 2. As the number of classes increased, the AIC and BIC values consistently decreased, indicating improved statistical fit with model complexity. However, the Entropy value, which reflects the certainty of classification, reached a distinct peak at the 5-class model (0.691). Extending the model to 6 classes resulted in a sharp decline in Entropy to 0.606, suggesting that the additional class introduced ambiguity (Fig. 2). Consequently, based on the Entropy peak and clinical interpretability, the 5-class model was selected as the optimal solution.

Association between comorbidity patterns and hospital-acquired AKI

The results of the multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis are presented in Table 3. After adjustment for the 21 prespecified covariates listed in the Table 3 footnote, latent comorbidity classes were independently associated with the risk of hospital-acquired AKI. Compared with Class 1, Class 4 showed the highest risk of hospital-acquired AKI (HR 1.52, 95% CI 1.29–1.79, P < 0.001), followed by Class 5 (HR 1.41, 95% CI 1.20–1.65, P < 0.001). Class 2 (HR 1.34, 95% CI 1.12–1.60, P = 0.001) and Class 3 (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.01–1.27, P = 0.027) were also associated with increased AKI risk.

Association with AKI severity

Further analysis focused on the severity of renal injury (Fig. 6). Full results of the multivariable logistic regression model for moderate-to-severe AKI are provided in Supplementary Table S5. After multivariable adjustment, comorbidity patterns were significantly associated with the risk of progressing to moderate-to-severe AKI (KDIGO Stage ≥ 2). Specifically, Class 4 (AF & Complex Cardiac) and Class 5 (Ischemic Heart Failure) exhibited the highest risk, with adjusted ORs of 1.87 (95% CI 1.44–2.42, P < 0.001) and 1.51 (95% CI 1.18–1.92, P < 0.001), respectively. Class 2 also showed a significant association (OR 1.42, 95% CI 1.09–1.85, P = 0.009). In contrast, Class 3 (Isolated Metabolic & Atherosclerosis) was not independently associated with severe AKI outcomes (OR 1.03, 95% CI 0.88–1.22, P = 0.690), suggesting that while metabolic comorbidities increase AKI susceptibility, they may not drive disease progression to the same extent as cardiac or respiratory phenotypes.

Discussion

This study utilized LCA to identify five distinct comorbidity phenotypes in older critically ill patients and quantified their association with hospital-acquired AKI. A major finding was that Classes 4 and 5, characterized by a high burden of cardiovascular comorbidities and a more severe acute clinical presentation, showed the highest observed risk for both AKI incidence and severity, with Class 4 showing the strongest association with progression to moderate-to-severe injury.

Cardiovascular-acute critical illness phenotypes: classes 4 and 5

The higher AKI risk observed in Classes 4 and 5 may be interpreted in the context of cardiorenal interactions, including impaired renal perfusion, venous congestion, systemic inflammation, and hemodynamic instability [27]. In Class 5 (Ischemic Heart Failure), renal venous congestion may represent one biologically plausible pathway, together with reduced effective arterial perfusion and the broader hemodynamic burden of critical illness. Recent evidence suggests that elevated central venous pressure increases renal interstitial pressure, reduces the arteriovenous pressure gradient across the glomerulus, and impairs filtration [28, 29].

Class 4 (Atrial Fibrillation & Complex Cardiac) showed the strongest association with moderate-to-severe AKI risk (HR 1.52). This association should not be attributed solely to atrial fibrillation, because Class 4 also exhibited features of severe perioperative or cardiovascular critical illness. Nevertheless, AF-related hemodynamic instability represents a biologically plausible contributing pathway. From a pathophysiological perspective, AF is associated with loss of “atrial kick” and irregular ventricular filling, leading to beat-to-beat variability in cardiac output and potentially affecting renal perfusion pressure [30]. Furthermore, older patients with AF often require anticoagulation and rate-control medications, which, combined with the hemodynamic stress of critical illness, may further compromise renal autoregulation [31]. Overall, the elevated AKI risk observed in Class 4 appears to reflect a clinically meaningful phenotype in which atrial fibrillation, chronic cardiovascular vulnerability, and acute critical illness severity overlap.

The lung-kidney axis (Class 2)

Patients in Class 2 (Respiratory & Nicotine Dependence) also showed an increased risk of AKI (HR 1.34), suggesting that lung-kidney interactions may be relevant in this phenotype. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and nicotine dependence contribute to systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [32]. In the ICU, exacerbations of respiratory failure can lead to hypoxia and hypercapnia. Renal vasoconstriction induced by permissive hypercapnia or hypoxemia-driven sympathetic activation can reduce renal blood flow [33]. Additionally, mechanical ventilation strategies, such as high PEEP, may increase intrathoracic pressure and thereby contribute to impaired venous return and renal congestion in susceptible patients. Taken together, these pathways provide a biologically plausible framework for interpreting the elevated AKI risk observed in Class 2.

To assess the robustness of these associations, we performed a Fine-Gray competing-risk analysis, in which in-hospital death before AKI occurrence was treated as the competing event. The associations between latent classes and hospital-acquired AKI remained generally consistent after accounting for this competing event, further supporting the relevance of these phenotypes for AKI risk stratification.

Comparison with traditional comorbidity measures

Traditional variable-centered approaches, such as comorbidity counts or weighted scores, summarize overall disease burden but may overlook clinically meaningful patterns of disease co-occurrence. In this study, LCA provided complementary phenotypic information by distinguishing comorbidity patterns that showed different associations with AKI incidence and severity. For instance, Class 3 (Isolated Metabolic & Atherosclerosis), despite a high burden of hypertension and diabetes, showed only a modest association with AKI risk (HR 1.14) and was not independently associated with moderate-to-severe AKI, although metabolic syndrome has been linked to adverse kidney outcomes in previous studies [34]. In contrast, the stronger association observed for Class 4 (Atrial Fibrillation & Complex Cardiac) may reflect overlapping cardiovascular vulnerability, perioperative stress, organ support requirements, and acute critical illness severity, consistent with the multifactorial nature of AKI in critically ill patients [35]. These findings suggest that LCA provides clinically interpretable phenotypic information beyond traditional comorbidity counts; however, its incremental predictive value over existing scores should be evaluated in future studies.

Our findings support the value of phenotype-based characterization for renal risk assessment in older ICU patients. For patients resembling Classes 4 and 5, closer renal surveillance and assessment of both perfusion and congestion status may be particularly relevant. For patients resembling Class 2, minimizing ventilator-associated hemodynamic compromise and avoiding nephrotoxic exposures remain important considerations. Overall, recognizing these latent patterns upon ICU admission may facilitate targeted renal monitoring before overt functional AKI, such as a rise in serum creatinine, becomes apparent.

Limitations

Our retrospective analysis faces data extraction limitations inherent to MIMIC-IV, specifically the lack of pre-hospital outpatient records and restricted clinical granularity regarding baseline disease severity and duration. Additionally, the observational design introduces temporal bias, making it difficult to definitively sequence chronic exacerbations and acute renal insults. Despite rigorous multivariable adjustments, unmeasured variables leave a risk of residual confounding. Given that the study population consists of ICU patients, extrapolating these findings to non-ICU populations requires caution.

Conclusion

Among older critically ill patients, latent comorbidity phenotypes characterized by atrial fibrillation and ischemic heart failure were independently associated with higher risks of hospital-acquired AKI and moderate-to-severe AKI. These findings indicate that LCA provides complementary, clinically interpretable phenotypic information beyond traditional comorbidity counts, with potential relevance for renal risk assessment in this population.
