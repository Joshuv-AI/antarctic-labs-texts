An Electronic Device Floating in Hemothorax: When POCUS Helps to FOCUS
Authors: Julien Higny, Martin Benoît, Frédéric Clarembeau, Jean‐Philippe Henry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCardiac perforation associated with hemothorax represents an extremely rare complication following pacemaker implantation. We describe a case of right ventricular lead perforation through the pericardium resulting in a massive left‐sided hemothorax. Point‐of‐Care Ultrasound (POCUS) was key to diagnose the lead migration and pleural effusion. We performed emergent percutaneous drainage of the hemothorax, device removal and replacement.

Introduction

Point‐of‐Care Ultrasound (POCUS) has become fundamental for the management of critically ill patients because it allows rapid bedside diagnosis. Point‐of‐care pleural ultrasonography represents a highly reliable tool in the detection, characterization, and management of pleural effusion [1]. Complications in cardiac pacemakers may include pneumothorax, pericardial effusion, hemothorax, lead displacement, and device‐related infection [2]. Right ventricular lead perforation associated with hemothorax represents an extremely rare complication following pacemaker implantation. Data from registries report an overall perforation rate of 0.1%–0.8% [3]. Herein, we aimed to present an extremely rare complication of pacemaker implantation and share with the readers our experience and lessons learned for the diagnosis and management of this complication.

Case History/Examination

A 72‐year‐old woman presented to the emergency department for dyspnea after an accidental fall. She underwent dual chamber pacemaker implantation for sinus node dysfunction 24 days earlier. On admission, her pulse rate was 98 bpm, blood pressure 112/72 mmHg, respiratory rate 22 breaths/min, oxygen saturation 91% in room air, and body temperature 36.8°C.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

The supine chest X‐ray showed a large opacification of the left lung with a blunting and concave line obscuring the lateral costophrenic angle suggestive of pleural effusion (Figure 1). Instead of the expected cardiac structures, POCUS demonstrated in the apical four chamber view a large anechoic collection in the left pleural cavity with heterogeneous echoes consistent with a massive hemothorax (interpleural distance of > 45 mm) and clotted blood responsible for mediastinal displacement (Video 1). The position of the lead tip was discerned in the pleural fluid as a hyperechoic structure visualized in the posterolateral view on thoracic (lung) POCUS (Video 2). The subcostal view focused at the cardiac apex showed the lead tip passing through the right ventricular wall without pericardial effusion (Figure 2, Video 3). Device interrogation revealed undersensing and loss of ventricular capture at maximal threshold in both bipolar and unipolar pacing. The ventricular lead impedance was 514 Ω. The pacing threshold of the atrial lead was 0.4 V at 0.4 ms and the impedance was 559 Ω. Because of the rarity of this complication, the sonographer suggested performing additional CT imaging in order to better characterize the anatomical relationships and confirm the lead displacement. Thoracic CT confirmed ventricular lead migration into the left lung resulting in hemothorax without pneumothorax (Figure 3). Cardiac perforation can be managed by lead repositioning under fluoroscopy or lead extraction and replacement [4, 5]. In our situation, the patient developed severe dyspnea and was transferred to the operating room for emergent percutaneous drainage of the hemothorax. Finally, we considered device removal rather than a minimally invasive repositioning procedure because of the pleural bleeding and the very distal migration of the lead tip. The procedure was not complicated and the patient was discharged from the intensive care unit 2 days later with normal sensing and pacing parameters.

Conclusion and Results

Cardiac perforation associated with hemothorax represents a very uncommon lead‐related complication following pacemaker implantation. POCUS represents an increasingly important skill for the modern emergency physician because it allows a rapid bedside diagnosis. Our experience demonstrates that POCUS can be instrumental in the diagnosis and treatment guidance of this life‐threatening complication. We are convinced that the certification programme, training experience, and interpretation in appropriate contexts are key for the safety of the patients.

Discussion

Emergency ultrasound and POCUS represent a fundamental skill in the current practice of emergency medicine. Recommendations for education, training experience, and quality improvement in the emergency setting are well described in the literature. Accordingly, emergency physicians should recognize the indications for ultrasound applications, be qualified in image acquisition, and competent to integrate the findings suitably into the clinical management of their patients [6]. Regarding risk factors for lead displacement, female sex, older age, lower BMI, medications (steroids, anticoagulants), myocardial infarction, physician expertise, apical position of the lead tip, and active fixation leads may play a predisposing role. In our experience, we think that the traumatic fall also represented a precipitating factor [7]. Finally, the scope of this document is limited by its retrospective design and single‐case format, resulting in a lack of ability to generalize and danger of over‐interpretation.
