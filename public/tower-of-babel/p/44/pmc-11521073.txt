Exploring the potential link between gut microbiota and chronic kidney disease in causality: A 2-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Wen Wen Zhang, Jin Lin Huo, Mei Di Xiao, Ya Jie Xu, Jin Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Increasing evidence indicates a significant correlation between gut microbiota (GM) and susceptibility to chronic kidney disease (CKD). However, causal relationship presence remains uncertain. Mendelian randomization (MR) was applied to evaluate potential causal relation from GM to CKD. Genomic association analysis aggregates publicly online databases, utilizing Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) database focused on GM and CKD. For examination of potential causal connection from GM to CKD, a 2-way, 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) method was applied. Sensitivity analyses were utilized to scrutinize for heterogeneity, horizontal pleiotropy, MR outcomes resilience. Result from inverse variance weighting (IVW) method revealed that 10 microbiotas such as Porphyromonadaceae (OR = 1.351, 95% CI: 1.114–1.638, P = .002), Dorea (OR = 1.236, 95% CI: 1.040–1.468, P = .016), Ruminococcus torques group (OR = 1.290, 95% CI: 1.035–1.608, P = .024) are potential CKD risk factors. Five microbiotas, including the Prevotellaceae (OR = 0.814, 95% CI: 0.719–0.922, P = .001) are potential CKD protective factors. Sensitivity analyses reveal no horizontal pleiotropy or heterogeneity. Additionally, reverse MR results unveiled potential relation between CKD and disorders in 3 microbiotas, including Senegalimassilia. According to the investigation, MR method was employed to delve into reciprocal causal connection from GM to CKD. Our findings identified 15 types of GM causally linked to CKD, as well as CKD demonstrating causal associations with 3 types of GM. Further exploration of these associated GM types is hopeful to raise novel insights, for CKD preventing and early monitoring.

1. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is clinically showed as a persistently low glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/minutes/1.73 m2, or kidney damage, lasting >3 months, stemming from diverse causes.[1] CKD prevalence in China stands at around 8.2%.[2] Globally, CKD’s prevalence was estimated to be around 9.1% in 2017, contributing to approximately 1.2 million annual deaths. Both morbidity and mortality associated with CKD exhibit a concerning upward trend.[3] Unfortunately, CKD is incurable, with available interventions primarily focusing on slowing disease progression. Ultimately, CKD can advance irreversibly into end-stage renal disease (ESRD), necessitating hemodialysis or kidney transplantation for management. CKD poses a substantial economic burden on societies globally, impacting both developing and developed countries and emerging as a significant worldwide public health challenge. Systemic inflammation and oxidative stress are recognized as pivotal factors contributing to CKD progression and accompanied complications.[4] Notably, research indicates GM’s crucial role in regulating systemic inflammations and influencing the development of CKD.[5–8]

Human gut microbiota (GM) stands out as one of the most diverse microbial ecosystems, encompassing over 35,000 bacterial species and a staggering 10 million genes.[9] Extensive research on GM has unveiled its significant implications for various human diseases. The evolving comprehension on gut-kidney axis has spurred a continuous influx of studies delving into the intricate relationship from GM to CKD. Research indicates that the gut microbiota exerts influence over the inflammatory process, as well as CKD pathogenesis and progression through diverse immune, metabolic, endocrine, and neural pathways.[10] Conversely, individuals with CKD exhibit substantial alterations in the composition and function of their GM.[11] These observations underscore a discernible connection from GM to CKD, hinting at its potential as a causative factor for the disease. However, an intriguing counterpoint from the study suggests that modifying the type and abundance of gut microbiota did not yield improvements in renal function and inflammatory response among CKD patients.[12] Hence, a thorough exploration on causal relation from GM to CKD holds the potential to yield novel insights for subsequent research endeavors and clinical monitoring protocols.

Mendelian randomization (MR) represents a new-established genetic statistical methodology, employing genetic variants which exhibit strong relation with exposures (defined as instrumental variables [IVs]) in nonexperimental data. This methodology conducts statistical assessment on the causal relationship from exposure to the outcome.[13] Leveraging intrinsic genetic variants allocated to individuals at birth, unlike conventional observational epidemiological studies, often mitigates the impact of confounders and biases, providing a means to circumvent potential distortions in the results.[14] Therefore, study here endeavors to unveil causal impact of GM on CKD, aiming to offer fresh avenues for research and stimulate novel perspectives in clinical thinking and exploration.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

Study flowchart is depicted within Figure 1A. This study was reported according to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Guideline for Mendelian Randomization.[15] Statistical data related to GM (exposure factors) were obtained through the Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) database. Each bacterial taxon within GM was treated as an individual exposure factor, and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) related to GM were chosen as IVs.[16] Data on the relation with CKD (disease outcome) serving as the outcome variables, were retrieved from FinnGen consortium. The study also performed a reverse MR analysis to explore the abundance of each bacterial group in CKD patients. MR analysis adhered to 3 conditional assumptions[16]: IVs must exhibit robust relation to exposure, IVs shouldn’t be related with confounders, IVs can solely impact outcome via exposure factors (Fig. 1B).

Flow diagram of the study and Mendelian randomization assumptions. (A) Flow diagram of the study process. (B) Three core assumptions of the MR analysis and Reverse MR analysis. MR = Mendelian randomization, MR-PRESSO = Mendelian randomization pleiotropy residual sum and outlier, SNP = single nucleotide polymorphism.

2.2. Data sources

GWAS summary statistics on gut floras were from MiBioGen consortium. This dataset comprises multi-ethnic, genome-wide, large-scale meta-analysis involving 18,340 participants from 24 datasets, with over 78% of them belonging to the European Ancestry Group.[17] Microbiome GWAS underwent adjustments for technical covariates, genetic principal components, sex, age. It yielded a finalized dataset of 122,110 variant loci classified at 5 taxonomic levels: phylum, class, order, family, genus. For data accuracy ensuring, the fifteen bacterial taxa with unknown family or genus information were excluded, ultimately incorporating 196 taxa (9 phyla, 16 classes, 20 orders, 32 families, 119 genera) within our analysis (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801).

Data on CKD were sourced from GWAS pooled data offered by the FinnGen consortium. CKD is defined by International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10-N18 and ICD-9-585 with FinnGen definition code N14_CHRONKIDNEYDIS, contains 11,265 (3817 female and 7448 male) cases and 436,208 controls, and has a median age at first event of 71.55 years. Unadjusted period prevalence was 2.98%. (https://r11.risteys.finregistry.fi/endpoints/N14_CHRONKIDNEYDIS; Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801).

The samples for GM and CKD in this study were from European ancestry, greatly reducing confounding due to genetic heterogeneity while avoiding population stratification bias, and there was no overlap of samples in the exposure and outcome GWAS, minimizing the increase in type I error rates due to the winner’s curse. As the study was based on secondary analyses of publicly available GWAS data, it did not require ethical exemptions or clinical registration, which are described in detail in the original GWAS study, as cited in the original article.

2.3. IV selection

In acquiring IVs for exposure factors, we meticulously chose SNPs from GWAS database of GM, adhering to strict screening conditions. Since SNP quantity with genome-wide significance P < 5 × 10−8 available was limited, after consulting relevant literature, threshold was meticulously recalibrated (P < 1 × 10−5) to achieve dependable and comprehensive outcomes.[18,19] In order to mitigate linkage disequilibrium effect, chosen SNP needed to fulfill allele distance (>10,000 kb) and the linkage disequilibrium parameter threshold (r2 < 0.001).[19] To avoid weak IV bias,[20] the research applied F statistic to determine robustness of instrumental variable estimation, and F statistic got computed by F = R2(n−k−1)/k(1−R2), where R2 denotes variation percentage within intestinal flora explained by SNPs, n denotes intestinal flora database sample size, and k denotes IVs quantity. When SNPs F > 10, it indicates that weak IV bias can be overcome. Alternative SNPs were not used in the dataset. Based on the experience of previous MR studies, this study used LDTrait (a parallel alternative to PhenoScanner) to perform statistically rigorous pruning of SNPs to eliminate SNPs associated with CKD with confounding characteristics (smoking, alcohol consumption, body mass index, lipids, hypertension, diabetes, uric acid, etc; Tables S2 and S3, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801).

2.4. MR analysis

We conducted MR analysis to estimate GM causality on CKD, with inverse variance weighting (IVW) as the first-choice methodology.[21] To assess each SNP heterogeneity, Cochran’s Q test was conducted. If no heterogeneity was detected (P < .05), a fixed-effects model was utilized. Conversely, with heterogeneity presented (P < .05), random effects model would be utilized.

To enhance result precision, corrections were implemented, incorporating Bonferroni correction. Bonferroni correction was applied on bacteria quantity within each category (genus: 0.05/119, family: 0.05/32, order: 0.05/20, class: 0.05/16, phylum: 0.05/9) ensuring a calculated correction tailored to each taxonomic level.

In addition, we additionally supplemented multidimensional validation with a variety of analytical methods, which include MR Egger, weighted median (WM), penalized weighted median (PWM), contamination mixture (ConMix) method, robust adjusted profile score (RAPS), debiased inverse-variance weighted (DIVW) method, constrained maximum likelihood (CML), Bayesian weighted Mendelian randomization (BWMR).[21–27] These methods minimize bias due to heterogeneity and pleiotropy (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801).

2.5. Sensitivity analysis

To ensure IVW results accuracy, sensitivity analyses were applied, encompassing leave-one-out, Mendelian randomization of polytropic residuals and outliers (MR-PRESSO), MR-Egger, Cochran’s Q test. Cochran’s Q statistic was calculated for potential heterogeneity quantification and testing. MR-Egger regression estimated included SNPs’ potential horizontal pleiotropy, with outcomes considered horizontally pleiotropic when P < .05.[28] Given MR-Egger regression’s lower precision and poorer statistics, MR-PRESSO was utilized to assess overall IVs horizontal multidimensionality. In cases where heterogeneity was detected among IVs, the outlier SNP was removed, and MR analysis was rerun.[29] Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was employed to confirm that significant causal associations identified through MR weren’t driven by single IV.[30] An available website tool (https://shiny.cnsgenomics.com/mRnd/) was used to calculate the statistical power of MR analysis, where a power >80% was considered an excellent value.[31] When the P value of the MR Steiger test was lower than .05, the causal direction of exposure-causing the outcome was statistically significant.[32]

2.6. Data processing

All data manipulation and analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.2). R packages used in the study were 2-Sample-MR, BayesMR and MR-PRESSO.

3. Results

3.1. IV selection

Following IV screening criteria, which involved stringent correlation filtering (P < 1 × 10−5) and consolidation (r2 < 0.001, window size = 10,000 kb), we identified those SNPs from 196 GM taxa. Corresponding F statistics for these SNPs all exceeded 10, signifying that the study was not vulnerable to weak instrumental variable bias. After cross-referencing with LDTrait website and excluding confounders related to CKD (Tables S2 and S3, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801), we identified 2101 SNPs. Among these, 103 SNPs were associated with 9 phyla, 182 SNPs with 16 classes, 222 SNPs with 20 orders, 358 SNPs with 32 families, and 1236 SNPs with 119 genera (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801). Similarly, we performed reverse MR analysis with CKD as exposure (P < 5 × 10−6) and GM as outcome (Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801). All the SNPs passed the Steiger test, and all the orientations showed “TRUE,” which strictly implemented the third conditional assumption of MR.

3.2. MR and reverse MR analysis

The detailed results of the MR analysis are shown in Table S7, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801, and the reverse MR analysis results are shown in Table S8, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801. Following the application of the Bonferroni correction of the MR analysis, 15 positive outcomes were identified, of which one demonstrated significant causal association, and 14 suggestive causal evidences were identified. While about the reverse MR analysis, there were 3 suggestive causal evidences were identified (Table S9, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801).

One demonstrated significant causal association is Prevotellaceae. IVW results indicated that Prevotellaceae (OR = 0.814, 95% CI: 0.719–0.922, P = .001) was protective factors for CKD development. Other complementary methods indicated that MR Egger (OR = 0.819, 95% CI: 0.482–1.391, P = .473), WM (OR = 0.806, 95% CI: 0.674–0.964, P = .018), PWM (OR = 0.805, 95% CI: 0.678–0.959, P = .013), ConMix method (OR = 0.740, 95% CI: 0.633–0.864, P = .001), RAPS (OR = 0.788, 95% CI: 0.683–0.910, P = .001), DIVW method (OR = 0.806, 95% CI: 0.697–0.933, P = .004), CML (OR = 0.812, 95% CI: 0.692–0.953, P = .011), and BWMR (OR = 0.804, 95% CI: 0.696–0.930, P = .003; Fig. 2).

Specifically, IVW results indicated that Actinobacteria (OR = 1.168, 95% CI: 1.005–1.357, P = .043), Verrucomicrobiae (OR = 1.157, 95% CI: 1.016–1.317, P = .028), Rhodospirillales (OR = 1.107, 95% CI: 1.006–1.218, P = .038), Verrucomicrobiales (OR = 1.157, 95% CI: 1.016–1.317, P = .028), Bacteroidales (OR = 1.145, 95% CI: 1.014–1.292, P = .028), Porphyromonadaceae (OR = 1.351, 95% CI: 1.114–1.638, P = .002), Verrucomicrobiaceae (OR = 1.157, 95% CI: 1.016–1.317, P = .028), Akkermansia (OR = 1.157, 95% CI: 1.016–1.317, P = .027), Dorea (OR = 1.236, 95% CI: 1.040–1.468, P = .016), and Ruminococcus torques group (OR = 1.290, 95% CI: 1.035–1.608, P = .024) were risk factors for CKD progression. Bacteroidaceae (OR = 0.818, 95% CI: 0.689–0.972, P = .022), Prevotellaceae (OR = 0.814, 95% CI: 0.719–0.922, P = .001), Bacteroides (OR = 0.818, 95% CI: 0.689–0.972, P = .022), Lachnospira (OR = 0.776, 95% CI: 0.634–0.951, P = .015), and Erysipelotrichaceae UCG003 (OR = 0.857, 95% CI: 0.764–0.961, P = .008) were protective factors for CKD development (Fig. 3, Table S10, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801). In addition, other complementary methods (MR Egger, WM, PWM, ConMix method, RAPS, DIVW method, CML, and BWMR) provided consistent results (Table S9, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801).

To further strengthen the evidence of causal relationships, we calculated the statistical power of positive results of the MR analysis. The results indicated that Porphyromonadaceae, Dorea, Ruminococcus torques group, and Prevotellaceae have a strong causal association with CKD. Genetic susceptibility in the Porphyromonadaceae, Dorea, and Ruminococcus torques group was significantly and causally associated with an increased risk of developing CKD, with each standard deviation (SD) increase associated with a 35.1%, 23.6%, and 29.0% increased risk of CKD. While Prevotellaceae was significantly and causally associated with a reduced risk of developing CKD, with each SD increase associated with reductions in CKD risk of 18.6% (Table S10, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801).

Similarly, we conducted a reverse MR analysis and found 3 suggestive causal evidences. Specifically, IVW results indicated that the development of CKD leads to an increase in Peptococcaceae (OR = 1.104, 95% CI: 1.114–1.638, P = .041) and a decrease in NB1n (OR = 0.843, 95% CI: 1.114–1.638, P = .040) and Senegalimassilia (OR = 0.877, 95% CI: 1.114–1.638, P = .037; Tables S9 and S10, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801).

3.3. Sensitivity analysis

In order to verify the accuracy of the results of the MR analyses, sensitivity analyses, including MR-Egger intercept, MR-PRESSO global test, Cochran’s Q, and leave-one-out method, were conducted in this study. The sensitivity analysis is summarized in Table S11, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801. IVW would have shown 17 positive results, but the P value of MR-Egger intercept for Collinsella and Butyrivibrio was <.05, which was considered not significant and was excluded. Of all the positive causal evidence, MR-Egger intercept had a P value of >.05, and no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy was detected, Cochrane’s Q did not detect evidence of heterogeneity, and MR-PRESSO eliminated all outliers to minimize pleiotropic bias (Table S10, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N801). In addition, leave-one-out sensitivity analysis and the funnel plot were detailed in Figures S1 and S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N800. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that the causal associations of positive results for Verrucomicrobiae, Verrucomicrobiales, Verrucomicrobiaceae, Akkermansia, Dorea, Lachnospira and Senegalimassilia were driven by a single SNP, but the others remain robust.

4. Discussion

The human gut harbors over 1000 bacterial taxa involved in substance metabolism and immunomodulatory functions. Intestinal flora generates indoles, phenols, amines, and other compounds through the fermentation of protein substrates in undigested products that reach the colon. The accumulation of these substances in CKD patients is considered harmful.[33] Studies have demonstrated that uremic toxins present in GM and GM-related metabolites (such as trimethylamine oxide, indolephenol/p-cresol sulfate, etc) may impact CKD progression through various mechanisms.[34–38] These mechanisms include inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, immune-mediated responses, and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system activation. In CKD patients, uremic toxins and other metabolites accumulations trigger NF-kB pathway activation, resulting in a systemic inflammatory response,[39,40] on the other hand, inflammatory vesicle-mediated IL-1β production contributes to reactive oxygen species generation in renal epithelial cells.[41] The diminished filtering capacity of the kidneys in CKD patients leads to waste products depositing, accumulating within blood. The products buildup from protein fermentation in the intestines and bloodstream results in changes to the pH in the intestinal lumen, potentially causing dysbiosis of the gut microbiota.[35] As renal function progressively deteriorates, the colon gradually takes over primary site for urea/uric acid excretion, replacing the kidneys.[42] Substantial urea and uric acid release from the intestines disrupts gastrointestinal tract biochemical environment. Long-term substances exposure diminishes GM viability and compromises intestinal epithelial barrier function.[11,43] Consequently, a bidirectional causal link from GM to CKD can be postulated.

CKD onset is linked to various factors, including hypertension, diabetes, and genetic susceptibility. Many existing studies examining the correlation from GM to CKD received impact from confounding factors including those mentioned above. These studies may also be susceptible to reverse causation, rendering them ineffective in elucidating the causal relation from GM to CKD. Hence, we explored bidirectional causal relationship from GM to CKD across 196 microbial species categorized at 5 levels (phylum, class, order, family, genus) using MR method. Findings above revealed robust causal relation between Prevotellaceae and CKD development, along with causal links between 14 other bacterial groups and CKD progression. Furthermore, reverse MR analysis unveiled a causal connection between CKD and 3 additional microbial groups.

Positive MR analysis revealed positive relations of Actinobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, Rhodospirillales, Verrucomicrobiales, Bacteroidales, Porphyromonadaceae, Verrucomicrobiaceae, Akkermansia, Dorea, and Ruminococcus torques group with CKD risk, respectively, while Bacteroidaceae, Prevotellaceae, Bacteroides, Lachnospira, and Erysipelotrichaceae UCG003 were negatively associated with CKD risk. In the inverse MR analysis, it was observed that CKD leads to promotion in Peptococcaceae abundance and a reduction in NB1n and Senegalimassilia abundances.

Among GMs, 4 major phyla dominate: Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, constituting approximately 97% of the total bacterial population.[44] Actinobacteria, a group of gram-positive bacteria, constitutes 1 of the 4 major bacterial phyla. Despite representing a small fraction of the intestinal microbiota, Actinobacteria functions crucially within maintaining intestinal homeostasis. They participate within degradation of resistant starch and linoleic acid’s conversion into conjugated linoleic acid, thereby enhancing immune function.[45,46] Actinomycetes can also modulate immune inflammation by inducing regulatory T cells.[47] The association between the Actinobacteria phylum and CKD has garnered increasing attention in academic discussions. Animal studies have revealed that, in rats with CKD, Actinobacteria phylum abundance was elevated compared to control.[48,49] Clinical studies have reported notable elevation in actinomycetes abundance within CKD cohort in comparison to control cohort.[50] Raised by Yang et al,[51] fecal microbiota samples from stage 3 to 4 CKD patients were sequenced, revealing a substantially superior Actinobacteria phylum abundance in comparison to control. Vaziri et al[52] selected Actinobacteria phylum among the most proliferating microbial families in ESRD patients. Similarly, research affirms robust causal relation from high Actinobacteria abundance to elevated CKD risk. We hypothesize that the mechanism of action may be linked to the entry of uremic toxins to blood, inflammatory mediators release, the activation of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, among other factors. However, the mechanistic pathway remains unclear and requires further investigation.

While certain studies have proposed associations between Rhodospirillales and conditions like cholelithiasis and age-related macular degeneration, research on the role of Rhodospirillales in CKD is notably lacking.[53,54] Study revealed an elevated abundance of Desulfovibrio in CKD individuals in comparison to control, and variations within Desulfovibrio abundance correlated with indicators of CKD disease severity.[38] Another study demonstrated that Porphyromonadaceae, an intestinal bacterium, produces phenolic compounds (a specific uremic toxin), suggesting a potential contribution to CKD progression.[55] A study by Zhang et al,[56] ESRD patients experiencing hemodialysis treating exhibited an increase in Dorea abundance. However, the precise mechanisms through which the aforementioned flora impact CKD still require further investigation.

Five microbiotas, including Lachnospira and Prevotellaceae, showcased negative relation with CKD risk. Meta-analysis identified a decreased abundance of Lachnospira in GMs of diabetic nephropathy patients, with no notable distinction within GM diversity between diabetic and nondiabetic nephropathy individuals.[57] Study observed a notable decrease in Prevotellaceae in the intestinal flora of rats in the chronic renal failure group compared to control group.[52] Reverse MR indicated that CKD could induce changes in the abundance of 3 microbiota, including Senegalimassilia. Studies have shown a strong causal relationship between Senegalimassilia and CKD and bladder cancer.[58,59] This study may offer novel insights for the ongoing monitoring of individuals at risk of CKD.

This study possesses various strengths while showing gaps. One notable strength lies in our utilization of a bidirectional MR analysis method, effectively mitigating the impact of confounding factors and reverse causality on the study outcomes. However, it is important to acknowledge a limitation: the majority of GWAS data utilized in this study originated from European samples. Consequently, the generalizability of our findings to Asian, African, and other populations warrants further investigation.

5. Conclusion

This study employed MR method to evaluate causal relationship from GM to CKD. Utilizing GM as exposure and CKD as outcome, the MR analysis identified a positive causal association with ten specific flora and a negative causal association with 5 floras in relation to CKD. So did reverse MR analysis, which indicated a positive causal association with 1 specific flora and a negative causal association with 2 floras. Further investigation into these relevant floras could offer insights into assessing disease prognosis and detecting trends in flora abundance, providing novel perspectives for CKD prevention and early monitoring.
