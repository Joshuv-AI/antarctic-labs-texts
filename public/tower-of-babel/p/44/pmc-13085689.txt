Diameter-dependent effects of nanopillar arrays on PEEK bioactivity and soft tissue integration
Authors: Xi Liu, Yuqi Zhao, Hui Liu, Haochen Wang, Xiaocheng Li, Yixuan Gong, Jie Zheng, Haohan Yu, Chang Su, Wen Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Poly-Ether-Ether-Ketone (PEEK) is a promising material for bony structure defects reconstruction due to its favorable mechanical properties and radiolucency. However, its clinical application is limited by poor soft tissue integration resulting from surface bio-inertness. In this study, a highly uniform Nanopillar Array PEEK (NAP) was fabricated using an anodic aluminum oxide (AAO) template-based melt-casting method. The resulting nanostructures exhibited precise control over nanopillar diameter (100–300 nm) without detectable chemical alterations. In vitro experiments demonstrated that NAP significantly enhanced fibroblast adhesion, proliferation, and collagen secretion, with efficacy positively correlating to nanopillar diameters. In the in vivo chest wall implantation model, NAP promoted superior soft tissue integration, as evidenced by increased collagen deposition and elevated expression of integrinβ1, fibronectin, COL-1, VEGF, and CTGF. Transcriptome analysis revealed that NAP upregulated Areg and Farp1 genes, which are associated with cytoskeletal reorganization and adhesion signaling, in a diameter-dependent manner. In conclusion, this study establishes a reproducible strategy for tailoring PEEK surface topography to enhance bioactivity and provides insights into the design of advanced implants for soft tissue integration.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04254-z.

Introduction

Reconstructing bony structure defects following tumor resection and trauma presents, including those in chest wall, cranio-maxillofacial and skull, is a significant clinical challenge [1, 2]. This difficulty is primarily due to the lack of an ideal material. Although titanium alloys exhibit favorable biocompatibility, the high elastic modulus may induce restrictive pulmonary complications and produce substantial imaging artifacts [3]. Poly-ether-ether-ketone (PEEK) has emerged as a promising alternative owing to its bone-matching elastic modulus and radiolucency [4–6]. However, the inherent bio-inertness of PEEK compromises interfacial bonding with bone stumps and impedes soft tissue integration, frequently resulting in fibrous encapsulation [7–9]. Although inadequate osseous integration can be partially addressed through intraoperative mechanical fixation, poor soft tissue in growth remains a critical concern. This often prolongs extubation time and elevates the risk of retrograde infection via drainage tracts, thereby adversely affecting surgical outcomes [10]. Consequently, enhancing the soft tissue integration capacity of PEEK constitutes a pivotal research objective [11, 12].

The poor cell adhesion observed on the surface is a direct manifestation of PEEK’s inherent bio-inertness [13]. To address this limitation, surface modification strategies aimed at enhancing cytocompatibility have been widely adopted [14, 15]. Among the various modification approaches, the introduction of micro- or nano-topographical features onto PEEK is particularly attractive due to its favorable biosafety profile [4]. More importantly, surface topography: including roughness and micro/nano-scale architecture—exerts a profound influence on cellular behaviors such as adhesion, proliferation, and migration [16, 17]. For instance, PEEK surfaces with tailored roughness have been shown to promote fibroblast adhesion and proliferation, while finely controlled nanotopographies can guide directional cell migration and stimulate collagen secretion [18, 19]. The underlying mechanism by which surface topography regulates cellular responses is highly complex, involving interrelated factors such as material microstructure and physicochemical properties [20]. Deeper investigation into these factors will provide critical theoretical insights and practical guidance for the rational design and application of PEEK-based biomaterials.

Nevertheless, the practical application of these bioactive morphologies is constrained by their structural complexity and limited reproducibility. For example, Nd: YAG laser treatment has been employed to generate nano-patterns that enhance bioactivity; however, the stochastic nature of the induced topography compromises methodological consistency [21]. Similarly, Accelerated Neutral Atom Beam (ANAB) processing improves biological performance but encounters analogous challenges in morphological uniformity [22]. Combinatorial techniques, such as sulfonation coupled with laser processing, yield multi-scale structures yet remain impeded by insufficient reproducibility [23, 24]. In summary, despite the demonstrated potential of micro-topographical modifications, all these techniques are either complicated or cannot obtain regular pattern, or cost plenty time and money. Future investigations must prioritize the development of reproducible surface fabrication techniques.

In this study, a highly uniform nanopillar array was fabricated on the PEEK surface using a vacuum melt-casting process with a nanoporous AAO template [25]. Comprehensive characterization verified the morphological consistency, reproducibility, and chemical purity of the resulting nanostructure. Both in vitro and in vivo evaluations demonstrated a significant enhancement in soft tissue integration on the nanopillar-modified surfaces (Fig. 1). Furthermore, a positive correlation was observed between nanopillar diameter and the extent of tissue integration, indicating that biological functionality can be effectively modulated by precise control over a single topological parameter. These findings provide valuable insights into the relationship between defined surface nanotopography and bioactivity, offering a robust strategy for the development of advanced PEEK-based implants for chest wall reconstruction.

Results

Fabrication and characterization of highly uniform nanopillar array PEEK

A highly uniform nanopillar array topography was fabricated on the PEEK surface using a vacuum melt-casting process with an AAO template, which was subsequently removed by chemical etching (NaOH and CuCl₂/HCl solutions). The resulting material was designated as Nanopillar Array PEEK (NAP, Fig. 2A illustrates the fabrication process, while Figure S1 showcases the final material.). The materials were randomly sampled, and Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging was performed at randomly selected locations on each material, confirming a homogeneous distribution of nanopillars with controlled diameters of 100 nm, 200 nm, and 300 nm (Fig. 2B and F), precisely corresponding to the pore sizes of the AAO templates. The consistent pore depth and inter-pore distance of the templates yielded nanopillars with uniform height and center-to-center spacing (Fig. 2E and G, S1A). Three-dimensional topography was obtained by atomic force microscopy (AFM) corroborated the high structural uniformity observed by SEM (Fig. 2C). The pillars could be predicted within scanning area by AFM and variations in surface roughness were attributed to the spatial geometry differences arising from the varying nanopillar diameters as Fig. 2I. The spectra from multiple NAP regions was obtained by Scanning Electron Microscopy coupled with Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (SEM-EDS), showed only the expected carbon and oxygen peaks of the polymer, with no detectable aluminum, copper, or chlorine residues from the AAO template or etching process (Fig. S2A-B). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy confirmed that the chemical composition of NAP surfaces was identical to pristine PEEK, indicating no introduction of new chemical groups during modification (Fig. 2H). Water Contact Angle measurements revealed a positive correlation between nanopillar diameter and surface hydrophilicity (Fig. 2D), suggesting a link between topography and this aspect of bioactivity as Fig. 2J. The adsorption ability for fibronectin (FN) also exhibited the same characteristics (Fig. S2C).

Influences of NAP on fibroblast morphologies

The biosafety of NAP was first confirmed, as cultivation of mouse fibroblast cells (L929) on NAP surfaces showed negligible cytotoxicity (Fig. S3A and B). The influences of NAP on fibroblast behavior were systematically evaluated in terms of adhesion, proliferation, and collagen secretion (Fig. 3A). Cell morphology was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). After 12 h of culture, lamellipodia formation was observed on NAP surfaces, while only filopodia were present on pristine PEEK (Fig. 3B). This distinction became more pronounced after 24 h. At this time point, enhanced cell adhesion morphology was observed on all NAP surfaces compared to pristine PEEK, with the best performance seen on NAP300. The most extensive lamellipodia formation was also noted on NAP300 surface. These observations were verified through fluorescence staining. After 12 h, significantly greater cell spreading area was observed on NAP surfaces compared to pristine PEEK (Fig. 3C). The cell aspect ratio (defined as major axis/minor axis) was higher on NAP100-300 than on pristine PEEK at 12 h, though no significant differences were detected among NAP groups. Enhanced cell aspect radio was evident across all NAP groups, consistent with SEM findings on 24 h. Statistical analysis confirmed superior aspect radio on NAP surfaces as shown in Fig. 3D. The absolute cell spreading area at 24 h was significantly larger on NAP surfaces than on pristine PEEK (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, a positive correlation was found between cell spreading area and nanopillar diameter within the NAP groups.

Influences of NAP on fibroblast adhesion

A centrifugal adhesion assay further validated above findings. While increasing centrifugal force reduced adherent cell numbers on all surfaces, significantly more cells remained attached to NAP surfaces than to pristine PEEK, with NAP300 showing the highest retention (Fig. 4A and B). Calculated cell adhesion forces were significantly stronger on NAP surfaces and correlated positively with nanopillar diameter (Fig. 4C). Examination of focal adhesions, using focal adhesion kinase (FAK) as a marker, revealed that cells on NAP surfaces exhibited increased focal adhesion numbers and higher FAK content compared to those on pristine PEEK. These enhancement as Fig. 4D, E and F shown were also positively correlated with nanopillar diameter, providing a molecular-level explanation for the improved adhesion.

Influences of NAP on fibroblast proliferation and section

NAP surfaces significantly promoted the proliferation of L929 fibroblasts compared to pristine PEEK, and this pro-proliferative effect was more substantial on surfaces with larger nanopillar diameters (Fig. 5B). Regarding cellular function, collagen staining indicated that NAP surfaces (Fig. 5A), particularly those with larger diameters, more effectively stimulated collagen secretion by fibroblasts (Fig. 5C).

Influence of NAP dimensions on soft tissue integration in vivo

A rat chest wall soft tissue implantation model was employed to assess soft tissue integration. Materials (pristine PEEK, NAP100-300) were implanted and harvested at 7 and 14 days (Fig. 6A). Hemolysis assays with material extracts and histological examination of organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) showed no significant hemolytic activity or signs of tissue damage/inflammation, confirming the in vivo biosafety of NAP implants (Fig. S3C, D and S4). Masson staining demonstrated that the area of collagen fiber deposition at the implant-tissue interface was larger in all NAP groups compared to the pristine PEEK group at both time points (Fig. 6B-D), with NAP300 being the most effective one. Furthermore, collagen fibers in the NAP300 group exhibited more organized alignment distal to the contact layer. Immunofluorescence staining for Integrinβ1 and Fibronectin showed the highest fluorescence intensity (indicating protein expression) in the NAP300 group (Fig. 6E, F). Immunohistochemical staining further confirmed that expression levels of Collagen I (COL-1, Fig. 7A-C), VEGF (Fig. 7D-F), and CTGF (Fig. 7G-I) in contact layer were higher in NAP groups than pristine PEEK, in which the NAP300 was the highest. Meanwhile, the arrangement of COL-1 co-located fibers in distal layer was consistent with the Masson staining results (Fig. 7A). The fibers containing COL-1 in the NAP group were more organized than those in pristine PEEK. Additionally, the localization of VEGF and CTGF in distal layer of the NAP300 group showed obvious orderliness. While in other groups, VEGF and CTGF were mainly located in the vacuole regions or around the encircling tissue structures. Rather than in the neatly arranged fibrous tissues especially in pristine PEEK (Fig. 7D and G). These results collectively demonstrate that NAP, especially NAP300, significantly enhances soft tissue integration.

Mechanism underlying enhanced integration: NAP topography influences fibroblast membrane-related gene expression

To investigate the mechanism, L929 fibroblasts were cultured on NAP300 (the most effective topology) and pristine PEEK. Based on prior findings, NAP300 was identified as the most effective surface among the NAP groups for promoting soft tissue integration. L929 fibroblasts were cultured on NAP300 and pristine PEEK surfaces for transcriptome sequencing. All differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are shown in Figure S4 A and B (Supporting Information). Significantly differentially expressed genes were selected for further analysis (Fig. 8A). These DEGs were characterized in relation to relevant functional pathways (Fig. 8B). Potential adhesion-related pathways were examined using the NIH database. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed to identify genes involved in biological processes or molecular functions related to cell adhesion (Fig. 8C). KEGG enrichment analysis was used to identify pathways associated with adhesion, with a focus on genes related to environmental information processing and cellular processes (Fig. 8D). Based on these analyses, four candidate genes of interest were selected: Areg, Farp1, Smagp, and Wnt2b. The specificity of these candidate genes was further verified by qRT-PCR (Fig. 8E). No significant differences were observed in Smagp or Wnt2b expression. In contrast, Areg and Farp1 showed significant differences. To examine the expression trends of these two genes, L929 cells were cultured on NAP100 and NAP200 surfaces, in addition to NAP300 and pristine PEEK. As shown in Fig. 8F, the expression levels of Areg and Farp1 were higher in all NAP groups compared to pristine PEEK. Within the NAP groups, the expression of both genes exhibited a positive correlation with nanopillar diameter (Fig. 8G). Farp1 expression appeared more sensitive to changes in nanopillar diameter. To functionally validate the role of Farp1, siRNA was used to knock down its expression in L929 cells (Fig. 8I, Table S1). Cell adhesion was re-evaluated using fluorescence-based observation. Following Farp1 knockdown (denoted as NAP300 + and PEEK+), a marked reduction in residual cells was observed on the NAP300 surface, with an even more pronounced reduction on pristine PEEK (Fig. 8H and K). Calculation of adhesion forces confirmed that Farp1 knockdown significantly decreased cell adhesion strength (Fig. 8J).

Discussion

In reviewing existing surface modification techniques designed to create specific topographies, we identified two common limitations. First, the physical morphologies produced by many methods are difficult to describe with precise parameters. These surfaces often show significant local variation, making it challenging to achieve reproducible local topography [26]. Second, techniques capable of generating regular patterns, such as nanoimprinting or laser engraving, frequently involve high costs and require sophisticated equipment [27–29]. Reflecting on this situation, we sought a strategy that could provide regular morphology, high reproducibility, low cost, and broad applicability. Consequently, we adopted the AAO template-casting method used in this study. The template itself is a key factor. Commercially available AAO templates are produced using mature manufacturing processes with very stable quality control. By using such a well-controlled template for casting, the resulting morphology is inherently stable and uniform. Importantly, the template can be removed via a simple chemical reaction, and complete chemical removal ensures no foreign substances are introduced. Furthermore, our approach is relatively inexpensive and does not demand highly specialized equipment. The vacuum muffle furnace we employed is a piece of equipment commonly found in most materials’ preparation laboratories. Processing a single batch of NAP using this equipment is straightforward, and the required time is acceptable. Our research confirms that the morphology created by this method yields a highly uniform surface: the center-to-center spacing of the nanopillars is highly consistent, and the height of the nanopillars is also very consistent. Comparing the three NAP materials, all parameters are identical except for the nanopillar diameter. This single-variable nanomorphology is advantageous for establishing a clear relationship between topography and biological phenomena. Additionally, the stability of such nanopillar morphology is a critical factor for consideration, as it must withstand both biological shear forces and potential mechanical damage during surgical implantation. Based on insights from reliable prior studies, we designed the morphology with appropriate diameter-to-height ratios and inter-pillar spacing, which theoretically ensures structural stability [30, 31]. However, more specific experimental validation will be required in future research to conclusively verify this stability.

Since the fibroblast is central to the process of soft tissue integration, we selected it as a representative cell type to study the biological functionality of NAP materials. Fibroblasts are multifunctional cell populations in soft tissue. They are responsible for maintaining the dynamic balance of the ECM. When local ECM damage occurs, signaling molecules like TGF-β activate fibroblasts to migrate to the injury site and initiate repair programs [32]. It is well known that cells require a good adhesive state before they can perform their functions effectively. Only in such a state can fibroblasts better execute subsequent tasks. Based on this perspective, our in vitro work first focused on observing the effect of NAP materials on the adhesive state of fibroblasts. Detail images showed that fibroblasts exhibited distinctly different morphological characteristics on the two types of materials. On the pristine PEEK surface, cells primarily formed filopodia. In contrast, cells tended to form lamellipodia and demonstrated a larger spreading area on the NAP surface. We have previously confirmed that the material composition of pristine PEEK and NAP is identical. Therefore, the physical topological structure of the material surface is an independent and critical regulatory signal for cell behavior.

The NAP material surface consists of regular nanopillars. This array topography can provide a large number of adhesion sites for cells. This directly promotes the formation of lamellipodia and cell spreading. Lamellipodia are essentially flat, sheet-like membrane extensions supported by an Actin network [33]. Therefore the expansion requires the establishment of extensive and stable adhesion to the ECM and material surface. When fibroblasts contact the nanopillar surface, adhesion proteins on the cell membrane can form more “anchor points” with the surfaces of the nanopillars. This multi-point, high-density contact provides strong mechanical support for actin polymerization and cytoskeletal reorganization, thereby driving the large-area spreading of lamellipodia. Consequently, as the diameter of the nanopillars on NAP increases, the number of these “anchor points” gradually rises. The cell’s ability to form lamellipodia becomes more proficient. Thus, the cells’ adhesive capability improves progressively. In contrast, the pristine PEEK surface lacks such dense anchoring points. Cells on this surface tend to extend slender filopodia. Filopodia primarily function in environmental sensing, serving to seek out sparse adhesion points in a relatively “barren” environment [34]. This also explains why cells cultured on pristine PEEK surfaces exhibit poor adhesive capability.

Furthermore, we noticed that this enhancement in adhesive capability displays a distinct size-dependent effect. This highlights the precision of interactions at the nanoscale. When the nanopillar diameter falls within a specific range, its spatial arrangement and inter-pillar spacing may be more conducive to integrin cluster assembly and focal adhesion maturation [35, 36]. Larger diameter nanopillars might provide superior mechanical stability, or their curvature radius might be more favorable for the recruitment and conformational changes of specific adhesion proteins [37]. This indicates that cellular perception of nanotopography is not a simple “all-or-nothing” response but rather an ability to precisely distinguish geometric parameter differences in the topography. Such as the diameter in this study, and subsequently mount a graduated biological response. This scale effect is a core feature of the interaction between nanomorphology and biological systems.

Another crucial point is that the nanoscale topological structure of a material can directly regulates the dynamics of cytoskeletal assembly by activating specific intracellular signaling pathways. The formation of filopodia and lamellipodia is precisely regulated by Rho family small GTPases, primarily Cdc42 and Rac1, respectively [38]. Previous research has indicated that specific nanotopographies can influence the activity of these small GTPases [19, 39]. This suggests that nanotopography can promote the formation of a branched actin network at the cell edge by activating Rac1, ultimately inducing lamellipodia generation and cell spreading. Therefore, the ability of a nanotopography to regulate Rac1-related pathways directly correlates with its ability to modulate cell adhesion. We found that the nanopillar topography significantly upregulates the expression of the Farp1 gene. Farp1 is a member of the RhoGEF family which plays an important role of Focal Adhesion signing. And it can activate small GTPases like RhoA, acting upstream of Rac1. Through its GEF activity, Farp1 specifically amplifies Rac1 activity [40]. In turn, leads to the formation of a branched actin network at the cell edge and induces lamellipodia formation and cell spreading. This process directly and positively regulates cell adhesion capability. Moreover, the expression level of Farp1 positively correlates with the nanopillar diameter. Therefore, the increase in NAP diameter also enhances cell adhesion capability.

Above all, our research confirms that the nanopillar topology on the PEEK surface acts as a potent physical signal. It guides fibroblasts to transition from a filopodia-dominated exploration mode to a lamellipodia-dominated spreading mode by activating the Farp1-related signaling pathway. The upregulation of Farp1 expression is a key molecular marker of this morphological transition, and its expression level is positively correlated with the nanopillar diameter. This discovery not only deepens our understanding of the mechanisms underlying cellular contact guidance but also provides an important theoretical basis for designing a new generation of intelligent biomaterials. By precisely controlling the nanoscale topological morphology on material surfaces, we can potentially “program” cell fate and behavior. This has broad application prospects in the field of implant surface modification and regenerative medicine. While our data demonstrate a positive correlation within the 100–300 nm range, it is plausible that an optimal diameter exists beyond which the beneficial effects might plateau or even diminish due to altered integrin clustering efficiency or other physical constraints. This defines an important parameter space for future investigations. Future research will focus on deciphering the fine regulation of signaling pathways by different nanopillar parameters and utilizing gene-editing technology to further validate the core role of Farp1 in this process.

Moreover, it is important to acknowledge a limitation of the present study: a specific investigation into the antibacterial properties of the NAP surfaces was not conducted. This decision was primarily guided by the intended application context of chest wall reconstruction—a clean surgical procedure where infection risk is primarily managed through aseptic techniques and prophylactic antibiotics rather than inherent implant antimicrobiality [41]. Furthermore, the nanopillar dimensions fabricated in this study (diameters of 100–300 nm) fall within a range previously associated in literature with mechano-bactericidal potential, suggesting a surface topology that may inherently discourage bacterial colonization [42]. Finally, the observed moderate increase in surface hydrophilicity is generally recognized to favor mammalian cell spreading and adhesion over bacterial attachment [42, 43]. These factors collectively indicate a potentially favorable profile. Our work focused squarely on establishing the nanotopography’s effect on soft tissue integration. However, it is crucial to balance both antibacterial properties and biological functions for biological materials [44–46]. We will accordingly perform dedicated bacterial adhesion and viability assays to comprehensively evaluate the antimicrobial performance of these surfaces in the future.

Conclusion

Our study successfully fabricated a nanopillar array PEEK (NAP) material characterized by highly uniform surface topography with a single controlled variable. This material combines excellent local nanoscale reproducibility with cost-effective processability. The NAP surface significantly enhances soft tissue integration by promoting fibroblast lamellipodia formation, increasing adhesion strength, and stimulating collagen fiber production. These biological effects are mediated through the activation of focal adhesion (FA) signaling pathways, which is directly influenced by the precise variation in nanopillar diameter. The findings indicate that soft tissue integration can be effectively “programmed” by selectively adjusting the nanopillar dimensions. Thus, the NAP platform provides a viable strategy for elucidating the relationship between nanotopographical cues and soft tissue integration, offering broad potential for the design of advanced implantable materials.

Experimental section

Fabrication of nanopillar arrays on PEEK Plates: Square samples (10 × 10 × 1 mm, Rochling, Germany) were used for in vitro and in vivo studies. The nano-well diameters of the AAO templates were 100, 200, and 300 nm (Wuxi Yiri New Material Technology Co., Ltd., China), and the distance from the center of each nano-well was 450 nm. Ultrasonic cleaning of the pristine PEEK plate and the AAO template was achieved using acetone, absolute ethanol (Tianjin Fuyu Fine Chemical Co., LTD), and deionized water. Next, the AAO template and pristine PEEK plate were placed inside a tube furnace. The AAO template was set at the bottom and the pristine PEEK plate at the top. The AAO template and the pristine PEEK plate were slowly heated from 37 to 375°C under vacuum conditions and then at 375°C for 30 min. Then, the samples were gradually cooled down from 375 to 37°C [25, 47]. Alumina was removed from the NaOH solution, and aluminum was removed from the CuCl2/HCl solution (Tianjin Tianli Chemical Reagent Co., LTD). Finally, the Nano PEEK samples were obtained. Based on the diameters of the nanorods on the PEEK surface, these three Nano PEEK materials were defined as NAP100, NAP200, and NAP300. The PEEK control samples were fabricated following the identical thermal processing protocol as the NAP samples, without AAO template. Subsequently, the PEEK control samples were polished to achieve a smooth surface, ensuring comparable topographical features for accurate comparison.

Characterization of the PEEK nanopillar array samples: The morphology of the Pristine PEEK plate and the NAP samples was assessed using the SEM technique (HITACHI, S-4800) with an energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS). The diameter of the random nanopillar was measured in the SEM image according to the scale. Surface nanotopography was determined by AFM (, 5500, Agilent Corporation, the US). The roughness was calculated using the arithmetic mean deviation (Sa). The water contact angle was measured by EasyDrop Standard system (K100, Kruss GMBH). The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy technique was used to analyze the samples and the spectra were recorded in the wavenumber range of 4000–500 cm −1 (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS10). Seven parallel tests were investigated of each sample for the measurement of distance, diameter and height; three parallel tests were investigated of each sample for the determination of surface nanotopography; three parallel tests were investigated of each sample for water contact angle test. All of these above are biological replicates.

Protein adsorption: The BCA (Bicinchoninic Acid) assay was used to quantitatively compare the amount of protein adsorbed onto pristine PEEK and NAP. The samples were incubated in a fibronectin solution at 37°C for 1 h to facilitate protein adsorption, followed by thorough rinsing to remove unadsorbed proteins. The adsorbed proteins were then eluted, and the total protein concentration was determined using a commercial BCA kit. A standard curve was constructed based on known concentrations of bovine serum albumin (BSA).

L929 cells culture: L929 cells were used to investigate biological effects in vitro (Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences). DMEM (Gibco, Grand Island Biological Company, the US) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Grand Island Biological Company, the US) and 1% Pen Strep (100×, Wuhan Servicebio technology Co., LTD) was used to culture L929 cells at 37°C in 5% CO2. The medium was refreshed every 2 days. PEEK sample was placed onto the bottom of the culture dish.

Live/dead staining: L929 cells were seeded at the density of 1 × 105/mL on samples for 24 h. The live/dead staining working solution was prepared by mixing Calcein AM (10 µL, 100 µM) and PI (750 µM, 10 µL) in 980 µL Calcein AM Assay Buffer (Wuhan Ilerite Biotechnology Co., LTD). The different samples were incubated with 200 µL of the working solution at 37°C for 30 min. The staining was examined by a CLSM (Calcein: Ex/Em=494 nm/517 nm; PI: Ex/Em=535 nm/617 nm). Three parallel tests were investigated of each sample, biological replicates.

Cell morphology and adhesion arrangement: L929 cells were seeded at the density of 2 × 104 cells on samples and cultured for 12 h, 24 h and 48 h. After that, the samples were washed with PBS for 3 times, fixed with 1 mL 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight at 4°C. Then samples were dehydrated in a gradient ethanols, followed by adding 1 mL hexamethyldisilazane (Tianjin Tianli Chemical Reagent Co., LTD) for 30 min and dried in a fume hood. Lastly, the samples were sputter-coated for 240 s and observed by SEM. In a parallel study, the L929 cells were cultured on samples for 12 h, 24 h and 48 h. In a parallel study, the samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Beijing Labgic Technology Co., LTD) at room temperature for 30 min. After rinsing with PBS, the samples were incubated in 300 µL Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated WGA (MedChemExpress, the US) in dark at room temperature for 25 min. Then the samples were mounted in DAPI-containing anti-fade mounting medium and observed by CLSM (phalloidin: Ex/Em=495 nm/518 nm; DAPI: Ex/Em=364 nm/454 nm). Three parallel tests were investigated of each sample, biological replicates.

FAs detection: L929 cells were seeded on samples densities of 2 × 104 cells and were incubated in a cell culture incubator for 24 h. The cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min. After membrane permeating in 0.3% Triton X-100, the samples were sealed in BSA. Primary Fak antibody (1:400) were incubated overnight at 4℃. Dylight 594 goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200) was added and incubated at room temperature for 2 h in dark condition. F-actin was stained by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated phalloidin and the samples were mounted in DAPI-containing anti-fade mounting medium for CLSM observation (goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody: Ex/Em=590 nm/617 nm; phalloidin: Ex/Em=495 nm/518 nm; DAPI: Ex/Em=364 nm/454 nm). Three parallel tests were investigated of each sample, biological replicates.

Cell adhesion force: The cell adhesion force was measured by centrifugation method according to previous report [48]. L929 cells were seeded at the density of 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, the samples were inverted and placed in the bottom of a 15 mL centrifuge tube pre-filled with 3 mL PBS. The centrifugation was performed for 5 min at the speed of 600 or 1300 r/min respectively. After centrifugation, the residual cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min. After washing with PBS for 3 times, the nuclei were stained with DAPI. Samples were observed under fluorescence microscope (DAPI: Ex/Em=364 nm/454 nm). Cell counting was performed using ImageJ software. The force exerted on the cells during centrifugation could be calculated using the formula: f = (ρcell - ρPBS) × Vcell × ω2 × r. The distribution of cell adhesion () follows a normal distribution: ~ (, 2). was the average adhesion of L929 cells; was the standard deviation. The average adhesion of L929 cells’ could be obtained from the standard normal distribution: (–)/~ (0, 1). Three parallel tests were investigated of each sample, biological replicates.

Cell proliferation: L929 cells were seeded at the density of 5 × 104/mL on samples and cultured for 1 d, 3 d and 5 d respectively. After washing by PBS, 500 µL CCK-8 working solution was added (1:10 dilution in DMEM, Biosharp, Beijing Labgic Technology Co. Ltd) and samples were incubated in the cell culture incubator for 1 h. Then the supernatants were transferred into 96-well plate to read the absorbance at 450 nm by plate reader (Tecan, Switzerland). Three parallel tests were investigated of each sample, biological replicates.

Sirius red staining: L929s were seeded at the density of 1 × 105 on PEEK samples and cultured for 3 d. The cells were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Beijing Solarbio Technology Co., LTD) at 4℃ overnight. Then the samples were stained by Sirius red (Beijing leagene biotech.co.,ltd) for 2 h and washed with 0.1 M acetic acid solution for 3 times to examine the stains by stereomicroscope. Afterwards, the stains were dissolved by 0.2 M NaOH to measure the absorbance at 540 nm by plate reader. Three parallel tests were investigated of each sample, biological replicates.

Animal surgery: The animal surgery in this study was implemented in accordance with guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Experiment Committee of the School of Stamotology, The Fourth Military Medical University (IACUC-20250163). The male SD rats (150 ~ 200 g body weight) were anesthetized with 3% pentobarbital sodium (0.1 mL/100 g). The samples were implanted in the chest wall below the lateral superficial muscles of the chest wall (including 5 rats as parallel tests in 4 groups respectively). The surgical site was located in the penultimate 2–3 intercostals of rats. After healing for 7 days and 14 days, rats were anesthetized and sacrificed. The samples were collected and fixed in the 4% paraformaldehyde solution for further experiments. Three parallel animals were investigated of each group, biological replicates.

Section analysis: After implants removal, the soft tissues were dehydrated and embedded into paraffin for sectioning. These sections were observed after MASSON/Immunofluorescence/Immunohistochemistry (Fibronectin antibody 1:250; ITGβ1 antibody 1:100, Dylight 594 goat anti-rabbit second antibody 1:200; COL-1 antibody 1:1700, VEGF antibody 1:250, CTGF antibody 1:100, HRP goat anti-rabbit second antibody 1:200) staining (goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody: Ex/Em=590 nm/617 nm). Three parallel tests were investigated of each sample, biological replicates.

Transcriptome sequencing: The bioanalyzer was used to detect RNA integrity and degradation. All samples’ RNA integrity number (Rins) was ensured ≥ 8.0 and 28 S/18S RNA ≥ 0.7. After the RNA library was constructed, double-ended sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform. Differential genes were screened. GO function and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were performed on genes with significant differences. Three parallel tests were investigated of each sample, biological replicates.

qRT-PCR: L929 cells were seeded on samples densities of 1 × 105 cells and were incubated in a cell culture incubator for 1 d. Five parallel tests were investigated of each sample. Total RNA in L929 cells were extracted and cDNA was reversely transcribed (Evo M-MLV kit). The qPCR reaction system was prepared using SYBR Green Pro taq HS qPCR kit (primer sequence in supporting materials). The qRT-PCR reaction system was performed in CFX96 real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR apparatus. The obtained data were output processed by Bio-Rad CFX Manager software. With GAPDH as the internal reference gene, the relative expression levels of target genes were quantitatively analyzed by 2-ΔΔCt method. Differential expression of each target gene was obtained. The primers information was listed in Table S1. Three parallel tests were investigated of each sample, biological replicates.

Target gene knocking down: SiRNAs were resuspended in SiRNA buffer to create a 20 µM stock solution. L929 cells were seeded in 24 -well densities of 2 × 104 cells. At a density of 30–50% confluency using antibiotic-free medium 12 h before transfection. For transfection, the SiRNA stock was diluted in Opti-MEM I Reduced Serum Medium (Gibco, Grand Island Biological Company, the US). And Lipofectamine 2000 was diluted separately in the same medium according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The diluted SiRNA and transfection reagent were then combined, gently mixed, and incubated at room temperature for 20 min to allow the formation of SiRNA-lipid complexes (50 nM). Then complex was added dropwise to the cells, and the plate was gently rocked to ensure even distribution. Cells were incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 48 h. Optionally, the transfection mixture was replaced with fresh complete DMEM medium containing serum after 6 h to minimize cytotoxicity. Knockdown efficiency was assessed at the mRNA level by quantitative RT-PCR 48 h post-transfection. Using housekeeping genes like GAPDH or β-actin for normalization. The SiRNA information was listed in Table S2. Three parallel tests were investigated of each sample, biological replicates.

Statistical analysis: Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS19.0. One-way ANOVA was used to compare the overall difference between groups. SNK-q post hoc test was performed for further comparison. The detection level was bilateral α = 0.05.
