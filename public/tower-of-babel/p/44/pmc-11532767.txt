Dietary supplement of veratric acid alleviates liver steatosis and reduces abdominal fat deposition in broilers
Authors: Tingli Pan, Siqi Liu, Qichao Liao, Yu Li, Yang Xiao, Yu Sun, Lei Zhou, Yixing Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and obesity are nutritional metabolic diseases that are prevalent in the poultry industry, and have a negative impact on its functioning. Veratric acid (VA) is a phenolic acid compound extracted from the Chinese herbal medicine Trollius chinensis Bunge, known for its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. In this study, we used chicken hepatocytes (Leghorn male hepatoma cells) and treated with a mixture of oleic acid and palmitic acid as well as Yellow-feathered broilers fed a high-fat diet to examine the impact of VA on liver-lipid metabolism and deposition of abdominal fat. The results showed that VA (1μM) reduced the triglyceride and total cholesterol levels in the chicken hepatocytes (p < 0.05). In the broiler NAFLD model, VA significantly reduced liver TG levels (p < 0.05) without affecting growth performance. Dietary supplementation with 0.05% or 0.1% VA supplementation also significantly reduced the mRNA expression levels of key genes involved in the synthesis of fatty acids such as sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c, fatty acid synthase, and acetyl-CoA carboxylase in broiler livers. In addition, 0.1% VA reduced abdominal fat accumulation and improved blood biochemical indexes in broilers. Network pharmacology analysis suggested that VA may participate in regulating fat metabolism in broilers via the proliferator-activated receptor signaling pathway. Taken together, the study results support VA as a candidate feed additive to provide a novel strategy for preventing NAFLD and excessive fat deposition in chickens.

Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a metabolic disorder caused by dysregulation of lipid metabolism in the liver, and characterized by excess accumulation of fat within the hepatic tissue (Wang et al., 2020a; Zhang et al., 2023). As poultry farming practices have intensified, modern broilers are now raised in environments that promote rapid growth through the use of high-energy diets and limited physical activity. While these practices increase the growth rate, they also increase the obesity rate and incidence of NAFLD. These conditions decrease not only broiler survival but also carcass yield, which significantly impacts the economic efficiency of the poultry industry (Wang et al., 2020a).

The liver plays a crucial role as the primary metabolic organ of the body. Its functions include regulating the uptake, synthesis, breakdown, and transport of lipids through various enzymatic actions, thus ensuring lipid homeostasis is maintained (Nguyen et al., 2008). Unlike mammals, de novo lipogenesis (DNL) occurs primarily in the liver, making chickens an ideal animal model for studying hepatic lipid metabolism (Gao et al., 2021). Research shows that chickens derive the substrates for lipid synthesis primarily from fatty acids produced by lipolysis, transport of dietary lipids, and de novo synthesis of fatty acids in the liver (Nematbakhsh et al., 2021). The hepatic fatty acids that are not stored as triglycerides, are secreted into the plasma in the form of very low-density lipoproteins and transported to target tissues such as adipose tissue, helping to maintain the balance between lipid synthesis and degradation in the body (Jia et al., 2022; Nematbakhsh et al., 2021). Chickens with NAFLD tend to have increased visceral and subcutaneous fat deposits. The excess fat is discarded during meat processing, thereby reducing meat yield and feed conversion efficiency (Lu et al., 2019b). The diagnosis of NAFLD in chickens is difficult because specific clinical features and laboratory diagnostic methods are lacking. Furthermore, effective treatments are currently not available (Tan et al., 2020).

In animal production, scientific management of feeding and nutrition is effective for reducing the risk of fatty liver disease. Nutritional interventions such as the use of feed additives and adjusting to the energy-to-protein ratio in diets can help to prevent and ameliorate fatty liver (Zhang et al., 2022b). Natural plant extracts, have attracted significant attention because of their environmentally friendly properties and lack of drug resistance (Mahfuz et al., 2021). Resveratrol (Wang et al., 2020a) and salidroside (Cui et al., 2022), for example, have been shown to have lipid-lowering effects and can be used to prevent NAFLD.

Veratric acid (VA), one of the primary active components of the traditional Chinese medicinal herb Trollius chinensis (Qin et al., 2015; Yu et al., 2021), is a phenolic acid with demonstrated anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities. It has been shown to significantly improve levels of both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants in rats with diet-induced atherosclerosis by inhibiting lipopolysaccharide induced inflammatory responses mediated by the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B/nuclear factor-kappa B (PI3K/Akt/NF-κB) signaling pathways (Raja et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2016). VA can be extracted from vegetables and fruits or synthesized (Wang et al., 2010), and presents promising opportunities for further development and application (Qin et al., 2015). However, the effects of VA on fat deposition and hepatic lipid metabolism in broilers remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

Therefore, we examined the effects of VA on hepatic lipid metabolism and abdominal fat deposition using chicken hepatocytes (Leghorn male hepatoma cells, LMH) treated with a mixture of oleic and palmitic acids, as well as yellow-feathered broilers fed to a high-fat diet. And this study aimed to establish a chicken NAFLD model to explore the impact of VA on hepatic lipid metabolism, and its effects on growth performance, meat quality, and body fat accumulation in broilers.

Material and methods

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Review Committee of Guangxi University (Gxu-2022-250).

Cell culture and processing

Leghorn male hepatoma (LMH) cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Beijing, China) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The culture medium was changed every 2 days. The cells were fused at approximately 80% for passaging and inoculated into 24-well plates or 96-well plates. We use oleic acid/palmitic acid (OA/PA) medium (RPMI 1640 medium, 200 µM OA, 100 µM PA, 1 g/50 mL bovine serum albumin and 1% penicillin/streptomycin) to mimic in vitro high-fat diet (HFD) treatment. At the same time, cells were cultured in OA/PA medium either with or without veratric acid for 24 h.

Cell viability assay

Veratric acid (VA, 99 % pure) was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biological Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The VA power was dissolved in dimethylsulfoxide to make a mother liquor of 50 mM and then diluted in culture medium to different concentrations (0-8μM). The LMH cells were inoculated in 96-well plates and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (with different concentrations of VA) for 24 h. Cell viability was measured with a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Biosharp, Anhui, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Oil Red O staining

After dissolving the prepared Oil Red O stock solution by shaking, the working solution for Oil Red O staining was prepared in a 3:2 ratio of Oil Red O stock to ddH2O. The staining procedure was as follows: Remove the medium from the wells and wash the cells three times with PBS. Add an appropriate volume of Oil Red O working solution to each well, and incubate at 37°C for 30 minutes. After staining, remove the Oil Red O solution, rinse the cells with 60% isopropanol, and then wash off any remaining isopropanol with PBS. Leave a PBS in the wells, and observe the cells under a microscope to capture images.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

The collected liver and adipose tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin blocks were sectioned into 4 µm thick slices using a microtome. The sections were then floated in warm water at 48°C, deparaffinized, stained with hematoxylin for nuclei and eosin for cytoplasm, and sealed with neutral gum. The hematoxylin and eosin staining (H&E) pathological sections were examined under a light microscope, and to quantify fat size in H&E-stained sections using image J analysis.

Animals

One-day-old male yellow-Feathered Broilers were purchased from Guangxi Fufeng Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd, (Nanning, China) kept in iron cages with free access to food and water. The ambient temperature was maintained between 32 and 35°C for the initial 6 days and then dropped by 2°C per week. The humidity was controlled at 60 % to 65 % and the light was provided by incandescent lamps. After 6 days of adaptation, they were randomly assigned to four groups of 12 each: a normal diet (ND) group; an HFD group; an HFD supplemented with 0.05% VA (HFD-0.05% VA) group; and an HFD supplemented with 0.1% VA (HFD-0.10% VA) group. The broilers were reared until 21 days of age and then slaughtered to observe adipose distribution and liver color, with photographs taken afterward. Liver, adipose, and muscle tissues were subsequently collected.

Diet ingredient composition

The care and use of broilers followed the guidelines published by the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People's Republic of China (approval number: 2006e398). The ND group was fed a basal ration and the HFD group was fed with the basal ration containing 14% lard, 6% cholesterol and 5% sucrose (Zhang et al., 2022a). The lard was purchased from Yueyang Xiangshui Agricultural Development Co. Ltd (Hunan, China), cholesterol was purchased from Guangxi Yulong Pharmaceuticals (Guangxi, China), and sucrose was purchased from Baijie Brand Management Co. Ltd (Yunnan, China). The ration composition and nutrient content are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Growth performance

During the experimental period, body weight (BW) was measured after a 12 h fasting at 7 and 21 days of age. Feed consumption was recorded daily and used to calculate the average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed-to-weight ratio (F/G) of the broilers.

Slaughter performance and sample collection

At the end of the experiment, blood samples were collected from the wing veins of broilers into vacuum tubes. After standing overnight at 4°C, the serum was separated by centrifuging for 10 min at 1500 g and stored at -80°C. The broilers were slaughtered immediately after blood collection. The thickness of the subcutaneous fat and the width of the intermuscular fat were measured, and the abdominal fat and liver were weighed to calculate their relative weights. The butchered weight, full eviscerated weight, and dressing percentage were measured and calculated following the Poultry Performance Standards and Measurement criteria (NY/T823-2020). (Yan et al., 2022). The butchered weight was the weight of the broiler after bleeding and removal of feathers. The full eviscerated weight was the weight of the carcass after removal of the head, feet, trachea, esophagus, crop, heart, liver, glandular stomach, muscular stomach, lungs, abdominal blubber, intestines, spleen, pancreas, bile, reproductive organs, gastric contents, and horny membranes. The dressing percentage was the ratio of the weight of the carcass after slaughter to the live weight of the animal. The tissue samples were placed in a 2 mL centrifuge tube, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C for further analysis.

Meat quality

Measurements of meat quality were performed in the right pectoralis and thigh muscles of broilers as previously described (Zhang et al., 2021). The pH of was measured within 45 min after slaughter and 24 h after storage at 4°C using a portable pH meter (pH-STAR, Matthaus, Germany). The electrical conductivity and color of the muscle flesh were measured with a ketone body muscle conductivity meter (LF-STAR, Matthaus, Germany) and flesh color meter respectively (OPTO-STAR, Matthaus, Germany). All measurements were repeated 5 times in each sample and the mean value was calculated.

Triglycerides (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) assays

Cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China) containing 1% phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride and the samples were collected and heated to 70°C for 10 min to lyse intracellular triglycerides. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 mins, the supernatant was collected and used in subsequent analyses. Tissue samples were cleaved with the same lysis buffer and ground in a tissue crusher (Tissue Lyser II, Qiagen, Germany) after adding steel beads. After the samples were shaken and lysed overnight at 4°C, they were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to obtain supernatants used in the assays. Serum samples were assayed directly. TG and TC content were measured using a tissue/cell assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's protocol, and calibrated with sample protein content. Protein concentration of the samples was measured with the BCA protein assay kits (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).

Serum biochemical parameters

Serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were assayed with respective commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from broiler liver samples using TRIGene reagent (Genstar, Beijing, China), the concentration of RNA was measured with a Tecan Infinite M200 Pro Instrument (Tecan, Switzerland). And cDNA was reverse transcribed using 1 μg RNA. RT-PCR was performed using Real Star Green Fast Mixture (GenStar, Beijing, China) and a qTOWER (Analytik Jena, Germany) (Huang et al., 2022). The primers are listed in Table S2, and the relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method.

Target identification and network construction

The potential targets of VA were obtained from the Swiss Target Prediction(http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) and TargetNet (http://targetnet.scbdd.com/) databases. The chicken targets corresponding to the screened targets were normalized using the UniPort database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Disease targets were obtained by searching the GeneCards database(https://www.genecards.org/) for “NAFLD” and “obesity”. Screening of drug-disease crossover genes was performed, and the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) was used to analyze crossover protein-protein interactions (PPI) and protein interaction networks were visualized with Cytoscape 3.9.1 (https://cytoscape.org/).

Gene ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis

GO annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/home.jsp) “analysis of Gallus gallus”. Mapping was performed using the Bioinformatics free online platform (www.biography.com.cn).

Statistical analysis

The results were reported as ± standard error of mean (SEM). Student's t tests were used to analyze comparisons between the two groups. One-way ANOVA was used for multiple group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Effect of VA on lipid deposition in hepatocytes

The CCK-8 assay assessed results (Fig. 1A) showed that 0-1µM VA was not cytotoxic to LMH cells (p > 0.05). Subsequently, we used OA/PA-treated LMH cells as an in vitro model of fatty liver, to determine whether VA reduced hepatocyte lipid deposition. We exposed LMH cells to varying concentrations of VA for 24 h and found a significant reduction in intracellular TG content in those treated with 1µM VA (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1B). We thus chose 1µM VA for subsequent experiments. The Oil Red O staining and intracellular TG, and TC results showed that lipid content of cells given VA was significantly decreased (p < 0.05) compared with untreated cells (Fig. 1C, D, F). However, VA had no impact on extracellular TG and total TC levels (p > 0.05) (Fig. 1E, G). Effect of VA on growth and slaughter performance in broilers

We investigated the effect of VA on fat metabolism in vivo in four groups of broilers. The ND group was fed a basic diet. The broilers with NAFLD that had been induced by the HFD were divided into three groups: One group was not given VA. The others were given the HFD supplemented with either 0.05% VA or 0.10% VA. We first observed the effect of VA supplementation on important economic indicators, and Table 1 shows the growth and slaughter performance results. Broiler body weight was significantly greater in the HFD than in the ND group (p < 0.05). However, 0.05% VA supplementation resulted in a tendency to decrease HFD-induced body weight gain (p = 0.19) and 0.01% VA supplementation significantly decreased body weight gain (p < 0.05). In addition, the ADG was greatly increased in the HFD group compared with the ND group. VA supplementation diminished the increase but the differences were not statistically significant (HFD vs. HFD-0.05% VA, p= 0.60; HFD vs. HFD-0.10% VA, p= 0.30). Consistent with the trend in body weight increase, the HFD significantly increased the dressed weight of broilers compared with that of broilers in the ND group. However, the dressed weight of broilers in HFD-0.05% VA and HFD-0.10% VA groups had a decreasing trend compared to the HFD group (HFD vs. HFD-0.05% VA, p= 0.47; HFD vs. HFD-0.10% VA, p= 0.42). Differences in the average daily feed, feed-to-weight ratio, eviscerated weight, dressing percentage, and eviscerated yield of broilers among all groups were not significant (p > 0.05). Effects of VA on liver lipid accumulation in broilers

The liver has an important, highly dynamic role in lipid metabolism, and the effects of VA on lipid metabolism were investigated in, livers that were dissected from broilers at the end of the experiment. As shown in Fig. 2 A, compared with the ND group, the livers of the HFD group were yellow and were accompanied by significant increase in abdominal fat. The addition of 0.05% and 0.10% VA to the HFD restored the normal color of the broiler and reduced abdominal fat deposition (Fig. 2A). No significant differences in the appearance of liver tissue from the study groups were seen following hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 2B), but the HFD significantly increased the accumulation of TG in the liver (p < 0.05). Dietary VA supplementation restored the liver TG to the level of ND group (Fig. 2C). but the HFD increased the total TC levels in broiler livers or without VA supplementation (Fig. 2D). Analysis of the expression of lipid metabolism-related genes in the broiler livers revealed that VA dose-dependently decreased the expression of sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (Srebp1) and fatty acid synthase (Fas) mRNA and the low VA dose downregulated the expression of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (Acc) mRNA (Fig. 2E). Biochemical markers present in the circulating blood are indexes of liver health, lipid metabolism, and the maintenance of lipid homeostasis. In particular, serum ALT and AST levels are reflective of hepatocyte injury, with hepatic steatosis being the most common cause of their increase. (Lu et al., 2019a). Serum LDL-C and HDL-C reflect the level of blood lipid metabolism. We found that there were no significant differences in the serum ALT levels of the study groups. However, AST and LDL-C levels were significantly higher in the HFD group than in the ND group, and supplementation with 0.05% VA reduced the levels (Fig. 2F–H). Compared with the ND group, an HFD significantly increased serum HDL-C, and the addition of different doses of VA to the HFD increased serum HDL-C levels to varying degrees (Fig. 2I).

Effects of VA on abdominal fat deposition in broilers

Anatomy clearly showed that HFD significantly increased the accumulation of abdominal fat, and that 0.05% and 0.10% VA supplementation decreased abdominal fat deposition, consistent with the differences in abdominal fat weight and abdominal fat yield (Fig. 2A, Fig. 3A-B). In addition, the H&E staining of adipose tissues clearly showed that the adipocytes in broilers with both levels of VA supplementation were tighter and smaller than those in the HFD group (Fig. 3C–E). Functional enrichment analysis for VA

The potential targets of VA were predicted using the Swiss Target Prediction and TargetNet database. A total of 284 potential targets were obtained after collection and collation. To identify VA targets related to NAFLD and obesity, 1132 NAFLD-related targets and 9275 obesity-related targets were identified in the GeneCards database. Venn diagram analysis identified 60 possible targets of VA that interact with NAFLD and obesity (Fig. 4A). The PPI network analysis showed that HSP90AB1, CASP3, and PPARG had a higher degree value among the potential targets, indicating their major role in the network (Fig. 4B). Subsequent GO annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed and the top 10 results of each category were presented (Fig. 4C). The analysis suggested that VA may regulate various biological processes cholesterol storage, transcription from RNA polymerase II promoter, peptidyl-serine phosphorylation, rhythmic process, and lipid metabolic process. Discussion

The poultry industry is increasingly moving toward large-scale, intensive production to meet consumer demand, but the shift has been accompanied by problems such as metabolic diseases. Commercial caged production has been associated with disturbances in liver lipid metabolism that lead to excess accumulation of abdominal fat and fatty liver in broilers, which result in significant economic losses (Mei et al., 2020). Addressing these metabolic issues is essential for reducing unnecessary economic burdens and promoting the sustainable development of the poultry industry.

In this study, we established in vivo and in vitro models of NAFLD using OA/PA-treated chicken hepatocytes and broilers fed an HFD. We found that 1 µM VA reduced TG and total TC levels in OA/PA-treated LMH cells. We then fed an HFD to yellow-feathered broilers for 14 days, which resulted in significant increases in body weight and dressed weight, liver discoloration, and increased hepatic TG and TC levels, all of which are characteristic of NAFLD in chickens (Zietek et al., 2021). It should be noted that the changes of serum markers may differ depending on the breed of chicken used in the NAFLD model. For example, in our study, the changes in serum ALT in the broilers fed the ND and HFD were not significantly different, which is consistent with previous findings in laying hens and broilers subjected to different NAFLD-inducing methods (Mei et al., 2020; Qiu et al., 2021). The levels of serum LDL and HDL in fatty liver broilers induced by corticosterone were higher than those in the control group, the same as our results (Mei et al., 2020). Dietary supplementation with VA can reduce serum AST and LDL levels and increase HDL levels in broilers. However, VA supplementation reduced AST and LDL-C levels while increasing HDL-C, suggesting that VA may have mitigated liver injury and improved lipid metabolism in these broilers.

The association of increased hepatic DNL and NAFLD is well established (Filali-Mouncef et al., 2022). Key enzymes in fatty acid synthesis, such as acetyl-CoA carboxylase (Acc) (Li et al., 2022) and fatty acid synthase (Fas) (Nematbakhsh et al., 2021), are regulated by sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (Srebp1), a transcription factor that controls DNL (Filali-Mouncef et al., 2022; Galbraith et al., 2018; Kohjima et al., 2007). In this study, VA downregulated the hepatic expression of Srebp1, Acc, and Fas, suggesting that VA may have alleviated NAFLD by inhibiting DNL in the liver.

We used network pharmacology, which connects drugs, diseases, and biological systems (Li et al., 2014; Fan et al., 2022) explore the mechanisms underlying the effects of VA supplementation on NAFLD and obesity. Among the 60 potential targets identified, HSP90AB1, PPARG, and CASP3 emerged as key candidates. HSP90AB1 has anti-inflammatory properties (Sun et al., 2022) and CASP3 is involved in apoptosis and inflammation (Shiffman et al., 2019). GO annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis suggested that VA may have therapeutic effects on NAFLD and obesity that are mediated by regulation of cholesterol storage, lipid metabolism, and the PPAR signaling pathway. Key genes in the PPAR pathway, including NR1H3, PPARG, PLIN1, PPARA, and PPARD, were identified as participating these processes. NR1H3, also known as LXRα, is involved in maintaining hepatic cholesterol homeostasis and plays a protective role in diverse inflammatory diseases (Zhao et al., 2021). PLIN1 primarily localizes to the surface of LDs and is an important regulator of lipolysis (Maurizi et al., 2018). PPARG is a regulator of adipocyte differentiation, PPARA and PPARD are involved in lipid oxidation and metabolism (Lv et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2020b). Our results showed that VA supplementation reduced the expression of Srebp1 and tended to increase Pparα expression in broiler liver, indicating a potential mechanism for the protection of against NAFLD and obesity.

In summary, this study investigated the effect of dietary VA supplementation on liver lipid deposition in an NAFLD model induced in broilers by an HFD. The addition of 0.05% or 0.10% VA did not affect the growth performance and meat quality of the broilers and significantly improved TG accumulation and abdominal fat deposition in the HFD-induced model broilers. The results of this study show that VA can be used as a potential feed additive to alleviate disorders of lipid metabolism in chicken liver and reduce fat deposition to solve a difficult problem in the development of the industry.
