Epidemiology of multimorbidity among indigenous adults: Insights from a large-scale population survey of 53 different indigenous groups in East India
Authors: Kavitha A.K., Jaya Singh Kshatri, Marjan van den Akker, Mohammad Akhtar Hussain, Haimanti Bhattacharya, Sanghamitra Pati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeIndigenous populations in India face a growing burden of multimorbidity, yet data on its prevalence and patterns remain scarce. This study estimated the prevalence, determinants, and co-occurrence of multimorbidity among Indigenous adults using data from the Odisha Tribal Family Health Survey (OTFHS).MethodsThe analysis included 19,430 adults (≥18 years) from 9711 households across 14 districts, representing 53 Indigenous groups, including 13 Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs). Multimorbidity—defined as having ≥2 chronic conditions—was assessed through self-reports and laboratory results. Prevalence estimates used descriptive statistics; associated factors were identified via ordinal logistic regression, and co-occurrence patterns of chronic conditions were visualized using a matrix-based plot that displays the frequency and overlap of conditions (UpSet plot).ResultsThe weighted prevalence of multimorbidity was 21.7 % (95 % CI: 20.5 %, 22.9 %), increasing from 3.8 % in those aged 18–25 to 48.2 % in those 60 and older. Regression analysis showed that older age, male sex, and tobacco and alcohol use were significantly associated with higher odds of multimorbidity. Hypertension was the most prevalent condition, often co-occurring with kidney disease, liver disease, or back pain.ConclusionThese findings highlight the urgent need for age- and sex-sensitive, integrated health interventions tailored to Indigenous communities.

Introduction

Multimorbidity, defined as the co-occurrence of two or more chronic conditions in an individual, has emerged as a global public health challenge, both in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like India [1,2]. As populations age and the burden of chronic diseases increases, and thus multimorbidity which poses a significant strain on healthcare systems [[3], [4], [5]]. While multimorbidity has traditionally been associated with older adults, it is now also affecting younger age groups [1,6,7]. Individuals with multimorbidity face poorer health outcomes, including increased disability, reduced quality of life, and higher healthcare costs [[8], [9], [10], [11]].

In India, shifting health patterns and lifestyle changes are contributing to a rising burden of chronic diseases, and consequently multimorbidity [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Nationally representative evidence from the WHO-SAGE study reported that 28.5 % of adults had at least one NCD and 8.9 % experienced multimorbidity, with prevalence rising from 1.3 % among individuals aged 18–29 years to 30.6 % in those aged 70 years and above [16]. Limited healthcare infrastructure and disparities in access to health services, especially in rural and marginalized areas amplify the effects of multimorbidity, which disproportionately affects vulnerable populations [17]. One such population is indigenous people, officially known as ‘scheduled tribes’ in India.

Odisha, a state in eastern India, is one of the most diverse states for Indigenous populations, with 64 different tribes (tribal groups) and almost a quarter (22.8 %) of its population belongs to scheduled tribes totalling over 10 million individuals [18]. Scheduled Tribes (ST) are officially recognized indigenous communities in India that have historically faced social and economic marginalization. Among them, Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTG) represent the most disadvantaged subgroups, living in remote and scattered habitats with limited access to resources, facing social and economic deprivation and limited access to resources [19]. Odisha's Indigenous population, has among the lowest health indicators in the country, with high rates of malnutrition, limited access to quality healthcare, and high levels of poverty [20]. Recent studies highlight the growing burden of multimorbidity in Odisha, with prevalence patterns varying across populations [[20], [21], [22]]. Multimorbidity in the region exhibits distinct patterns across populations, with prevalence ranging from 20 % among the rural elderly to 28.3 % in primary care settings [22,23].

The rising burden of multimorbidity necessitates a deeper understanding of its prevalence, determinants, and patterns, particularly among indigenous populations, who often experience poorer health outcomes. Despite evidence of significant health disparities, there is a lack of comprehensive studies on multimorbidity among tribal communities in India. This gap limits the development of targeted and culturally appropriate interventions. Therefore, this study aims to address this gap by examining the epidemiology (prevalence, determinants, and patterns) of multimorbidity among Odisha's tribal populations, −generating critical insights to inform targeted interventions.

Material and methods

Study setting, design and participants

Data were drawn from the Odisha Tribal Family Health Survey (OTFHS), covering 53 indigenous groups, including 13 PVTGs. This was a population-based cross-sectional survey with a multistage cluster sampling design carried out in the state of Odisha, India in 2022–2023. There are 30 administrative districts in the state, among which 14 districts (119 sub-district level blocks) are tribal-dominated and included in the survey. To assess the detailed health profile of the tribe, detailed interview data, measurements, and laboratory investigations were collected. Individuals were excluded from the analysis if they refused consent for data or sample collection, were severely ill, had severe cognitive impairment, or lacked age confirmation from a government document. We surveyed 389 clusters, covered 9711 households, and interviewed 30,292 individuals. The detailed methodology of the OTFHS has been previously published. Of the 30,292 participants, 19,430were adults (≥ 18 years), and this subset is used for the current analysis [21,22].

Measurements

Multimorbidity measurement

Multimorbidity was defined using a combination of self-reported conditions and conditions identified through laboratory testing. The study considered nineteen- chronic conditions [23], identified either through self-reports or tests. Self-reported conditions included diabetes, hypertension, chronic kidney disease and chronic liver disease (alcoholic), arthritis, acid peptic disease, asthma, heart disease, stroke, chronic backache, tuberculosis, filariasis, visual difficulty, deafness, cancer, dementia, epilepsy, thyroid disease, sickle cell disorder. Additionally, laboratory testing data were used to complement self-reports for the following conditions: diabetes, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease (alcoholic). Diabetes was defined as random blood sugar levels ≥200 mg/dL, and hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg.

Socio-demographic and health-related variables

In this analysis, different socio-demographic and health-related characteristics were considered to explore their association with multimorbidity among Indigenous adults. These include sex, categorized as male and female, and age, which is grouped into four intervals: 18–25, 26–44, 45–59, and 60+. Education levels were classified as no education, primary (class 1–5), secondary (class 6–10), and higher secondary and above. Occupation was categorized as no occupation, agriculture and others (Skilled, Unskilled workers, Professionals, Sales and Service Workers, etc.,). Socioeconomic status was assessed using a household wealth index constructed through principal component analysis (PCA). The analysis incorporated variables related to housing characteristics, household amenities, and ownership of durable assets (Suppl. Table 1). Variables with less than 1 % prevalence were excluded. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin value of 0.62 indicated sufficient suitability for factor anualysis. Wealth scores were derived from the first principal component and subsequently divided into quintiles: lowest, second, middle, fourth, and highest [24]. Additionally, health-related behaviours such as current use of tobacco (yes/no) and alcohol (yes/no) were included in the analysis.

Laboratory and field measurements

Blood pressure measurements were obtained for adolescents and adults using automated digital blood pressure monitors (OMRON automated blood pressure monitor), with the average taken from three readings to detect high blood pressure [25]. High Blood Sugar: Random blood glucose levels were measured using the Accu-Chek digital glucometer [21]. Anthropometric measures such as height and weight were taken using standardized equipment. Serum samples were analyzed for various biochemical parameters using an automated biochemistry analyser, EM-360 (ERBA Diagnostics Mannheim GmbH) and reagents provided by Transasia Biochemical Ltd). These tests included liver function markers Serum glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT), and Serum glutamate pyruvate transaminase (SGPT)) and kidney function marker (creatinine) [26].

Statistical analysis

The analysis focused on identifying patterns in chronic conditions and the socio-economic, behavioral, and demographic determinants of multimorbidity using the following methods.

Descriptive analysis, and weighted analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the socio-demographic and health-related characteristics of the study sample. Given the complex sampling design and the over-sampling of certain groups in the study, sampling weights were applied to account for unequal probabilities of selection and to ensure the results are representative of the population. Weighted prevalence for Multimorbidity, stratified by sex and age groups, were calculated with 95 % confidence intervals (CI).

Ordinal logistic regression

Ordinal logistic regression was used to model the association between various socio-demographic, behavioral, and health-related factors and the risk of multimorbidity [27]. The dependent variable in the regression models was categorized into three groups: [1] individuals with no chronic conditions, [2] individuals with single morbidity, and [3] individuals with multimorbidity. The independent variables included sex, age, wealth, ethnic group, education, occupation, tobacco use, and alcohol use. Both adjusted and unadjusted models were run. For all models, odds ratios (OR) and 95 % confidence intervals (CI) were computed and the models were checked for collinearity using Generalized Variance Inflation Factor (GVIF) [28].

Distribution of chronic conditions

To examine the co-occurrence patterns of chronic conditions, we used an UpSet plot, a specialized visualization technique designed to display intersecting sets and their relationships [29]. Unlike traditional Venn diagrams, which become cluttered with more than three or four sets, UpSet plots efficiently display complex overlaps. In this method, each chronic condition is represented as a set, and the intersections (co-occurrence of two or more conditions) are depicted as connected dots in a matrix layout. The vertical bars above the matrix indicate the size (frequency) of each intersection, while the horizontal bars show the overall prevalence of each individual condition. This approach enables us to identify the most common and rare multimorbidity patterns in the population. For this analysis, only conditions with a prevalence greater than 0.5 % were included.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1) [30]. the “survey” package was employed to adjust for the complex survey design and apply appropriate weights [31]. The “polr” package in R was used to run the ordinal logistic regression models [27], and upsetR package was used for finding the patters of chronic conditions [32].

Fig. 2 shows the proportion of chronic conditions across different age groups for males and females. It shows a clear trend of increasing multimorbidity with advancing age. Among females aged 18–20 years, 88.5 % had no chronic conditions, while 1 % had three or more conditions. In males of the same age group, the proportion without chronic conditions was slightly lower at 81.8 %, with 1 % having two or more conditions. As age increased, the proportion of individuals with multimorbidity also rose. In the 45–49 age group, 40.3 % of females and 38.1 % of males had no chronic conditions, while 4.2 % of males and 2.3 % of females had four or more. This trend continued in older age groups, with 26.1 % of females aged 60–64 having no chronic conditions, and 10.5 % having four or more. Similarly, in males of the same age group, only 22.8 % had no chronic conditions, while 9.2 % had four or more. Fig. 3 depicts the district-wise multimorbidity prevalence, with the highest burden observed in Kandhamal (69.1 %) and Keonjhar (52.3 %). Ordinal logistic regression showed that the probability of moving from no morbidity to single morbidity, and from single to multimorbidity, increased markedly with age. Compared with adults aged 18–25 years, those aged 26–44 had 2.44 times higher odds of being in a higher morbidity category (95 % CI: 2.21–2.69), those aged 45–59 had 6.27 times higher odds (95 % CI: 5.61–7.01), and those aged ≥60 had 13.1 times higher odds (95 % CI: 11.6–14.9). Males consistently had higher odds of being in a higher morbidity category compared with females. In the unadjusted model, males had 34 % higher odds (OR: 1.34, 95 % CI: 1.27–1.42), which attenuated slightly after adjusting for age (OR: 1.28, 95 % CI: 1.21–1.35) and further in the fully adjusted model (OR: 1.17, 95 % CI: 1.09–1.25). Wealth gradients were weak and inconsistent; for example, individuals in the middle wealth quintile had slightly lower odds than the richest (OR: 0.88, 95 % CI: 0.81–0.96), while other quintiles were close to unity. Educational differences observed in crude models (e.g., higher odds among those with no schooling) diminished after adjustment, suggesting confounding by age and socioeconomic position (adjusted OR: 1.08, 95 % CI: 0.95–1.24). Occupation showed modest effects: agricultural work was similar to not working (OR: 0.99, 95 % CI: 0.92–1.06), while those in “other” occupations had slightly higher odds (OR: 1.12, 95 % CI: 1.00–1.26). Ethnic differences were small, with PVTG individuals showing nearly identical odds to other ST groups (OR: 1.05, 95 % CI: 0.98–1.14). Behavioral risk factors were more influential: tobacco users had 34 % higher odds of being in a higher morbidity category (OR: 1.34, 95 % CI: 1.25–1.43), and alcohol users had about 9 % higher odds (OR: 1.09, 95 % CI: 1.01–1.17) compared with non-users. Hypertension is the most common condition across all groups, with kidney disease emerging as its most frequent co-occurring condition; multimorbidity patterns further vary by sex, age, ethnicity, and wealth. In males, kidney and liver diseases are more prevalent, whereas females more often experience back pain and arthritis. Older adults show a stronger clustering of hypertension with kidney disease, while younger adults commonly report liver disease and back pain. Among ST populations, hypertension with kidney disease predominates, whereas PVTG populations experience more back pain and liver disease. Across wealth quintiles, kidney disease is relatively more frequent, followed by back pain in the lowest and highest groups, and liver disease in the middle quintiles. Tuberculosis and asthma remain rare and do not co-occur with other conditions (Suppl. Figs. 1–4).

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the prevalence, distribution, and determinants of multimorbidity among indigenous adults in India, using data from the Odisha Tribal Family Health Survey (OTFHS). Our study is the first to comprehensively assess multimorbidity patterns in Scheduled Tribes (ST) and Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTG), providing crucial insights into the health burden within these marginalized populations.

Ethnic disparities were evident, with PVTG individuals showing a significantly higher risk of multimorbidity. The findings also highlight a strong age-related increase in multimorbidity, with individuals aged 60 and above experiencing the highest burden. Sex differences were modest, with males having only slightly higher odds of multimorbidity, and the association was attenuated compared with unadjusted estimates after adjustment for other factors. Socioeconomic factors played a role, as individuals in the lowest wealth quintile had slightly higher odds of multimorbidity compared to the highest quintile. Education showed an inverse association, with those having no formal education experiencing -higher odds of multimorbidity compared to those with higher - education. Tobacco use remained a strong and consistent predictor of higher morbidity burden, while alcohol use was also associated with elevated odds, though the effect was weaker in magnitude. Importantly, the observed clustering of conditions appears to mirror contextual risk factors—for instance, the co-occurrence of hypertension and liver disease among males is plausibly linked to higher prevalence of alcohol and tobacco use, while musculoskeletal conditions among females may reflect workload, and differential health-seeking behavior. Such contextualized interpretations provide mechanistic clarity to the patterns identified and highlight opportunities for targeted, culturally informed interventions. Occupational differences were modest, with agricultural workers showing no deviation from non-workers, while individuals in other occupations had slightly higher odds of multimorbidity.

Previous national surveys, such as the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) and the Longitudinal Aging Study in India (LASI), have also reported an increasing burden of multimorbidity with age, particularly in older adults [33,34]. Similarly, Pati et al. (2014), using nationally representative WHO-SAGE data, documented that multimorbidity prevalence increased from 1.3 % among individuals aged 18–29 years to 30.6 % among those aged 70 years and above. This mirrors the strong age gradient observed in our tribal sample, suggesting that indigenous populations follow the expected trajectory of multimorbidity accumulation with age, though their overall burden in older age groups appears comparatively higher [16]. However, our study specifically highlights a higher prevalence of multimorbidity in indigenous communities compared to general populations reported in these surveys. This finding aligns with previous research, including Sinha et al. (2023), which documented a significant burden of non-communicable disease multimorbidity among tribal older adults in India. Similarly, Puri and Pati (2022) reported a high prevalence of multimorbidity among indigenous populations, further supporting our findings. International evidence, such as Keramat et al. (2024), also demonstrates a high prevalence of multimorbidity among Indigenous Australians, reinforcing the global relevance of this issue. Additionally, a recent systematic review by Shahunja et al. (2024) provides further evidence of the disproportionately high multimorbidity burden among indigenous populations worldwide. Furthermore, recent evidence from the Kshatri J et al. (2025) has also underscored the burden of multimorbidity among indigenous populations in India, which further enriches and supports our findings [22]. The differences may be attributed to socio-economic disparities, lower access to healthcare, higher levels of occupational and environmental risks, and unique lifestyle factors within indigenous populations. Notably, districts such as Kalahandi and Nabarangapur, part of the historically deprived KBK region [35], and Kendujhar, a mining-dominated yet Backward Region Grant Fund (BRGF) -listed backward district [36], stood out with elevated multimorbidity prevalence, highlighting the importance of examining district-specific socio-economic and structural determinants.

By highlighting the unique patterns of multimorbidity among ST and PVTG populations, this study provides valuable data for targeted interventions. Our findings support the Tribal Health Action Plan (THAP)’s call to strengthen primary healthcare delivery in tribal areas, increasing health workforce and ensuring community-centric culturally sensitive services in tribal areas ([37]). The presence of multimorbidity clusters among tribal adults underscore the need for targeted interventions: community-based screening, tailored health check-ups, and improved referral systems. Improving health literacy through culturally-appropriate health education materials and Accredited Social Health Activist [ASHA] led outreach can further empower tribal communities to engage more actively in the prevention and management of chronic diseases. Integrating our evidence into the THAP framework, particularly through district-level action plans and strengthened data systems, would help translate these findings into sustainable and equitable improvements in tribal health. The use of a large, representative dataset ensures robust findings that can inform policies addressing health disparities in tribal communities. A major strength of this study is its focus on multimorbidity among indigenous populations in Odisha, an understudied group with unique health challenges. The large sample size and robust statistical methods enhance the reliability of our findings. Additionally, unlike most previous studies that relied solely on self-reported health data, our study combines both self-reported and laboratory-tested data for key conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, chronic liver disease, and chronic kidney disease, ensuring greater diagnostic accuracy. This comprehensive approach minimizes recall bias and enhances the validity of our prevalence estimates. However, the cross-sectional nature of the study prevents causal inferences about risk factors and disease progression. This design also raises the possibility of temporal biases, as it is not possible to determine whether exposures preceded the onset of multimorbidity. Additionally, while major conditions were confirmed through objective measures, some chronic conditions might still be underdiagnosed, particularly in tribal areas with limited healthcare access. There is also the possibility of under-reporting influenced by sex, educational background, or social desirability bias, where participants may under-report certain conditions due to stigma or cultural norms. Furthermore, diagnostic misclassification may have occurred between self-reported morbidity and clinically or laboratory-confirmed diagnoses. Future longitudinal studies are needed to address these temporal limitations, explore the long-term impact of multimorbidity, and evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions in indigenous communities.

The high burden of multimorbidity in indigenous populations underscores the need for integrated healthcare approaches tailored to their specific needs. Public health initiatives should focus on early screening and management of chronic conditions, particularly among older adults and those with lower education levels. Community-based interventions addressing lifestyle factors such as tobacco and alcohol use, along with improved access to healthcare services, are essential for reducing the multimorbidity burden. Additionally, culturally sensitive health programs should be developed to enhance awareness and healthcare utilization in tribal communities.

Conclusion

This study highlights the substantial burden of multimorbidity among indigenous adults in India, with notable variations by age, wealth, ethnic group, and lifestyle factors. Beyond recognizing this burden, the findings point to actionable strategies—such as community-based screenings, tailored health check-ups, improved referral pathways, and culturally appropriate health education—that can directly support tribal populations. Embedding these approaches within existing frameworks like the Tribal Health Action Plan, alongside efforts to strengthen primary healthcare, workforce capacity, and district-level action plans, can help reduce health disparities. Addressing the interplay of healthcare access gaps, traditional practices, and socio-economic vulnerabilities will be crucial for sustainable improvements in multimorbidity prevention and management among tribal communities.
