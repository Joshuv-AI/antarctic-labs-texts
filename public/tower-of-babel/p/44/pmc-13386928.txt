Comparison of systolic and diastolic CT-FFR for myocardial ischemia diagnosis
Authors: Weifeng Guo, Aizhu Sheng, Yuan Wang, Yige Lu, Shihai Zhao, Lekang Yin, Yingjie Zhao, Haijia Xu, Hanbo Zhang, Guanyu Qiao, Li Shen, Yang Pang, Jiasheng Yin, Zhifeng Yao, Chenguang Li, Shan Yang, Cheng Yan, Wei He, Furong He, Mengsu Zeng

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCT-derived fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR) is a powerful tool for identifying hemodynamic ischemia. Coronary CT angiography (CCTA) images with the best quality in a cardiac cycle are conventionally reconstructed in clinical practice. To compare the diagnostic performance of machine learning (ML)-based CT-FFR between systolic and diastolic phases in identifying myocardial ischemia, using invasive fractional flow reserve (FFR) as a reference standard.MethodsFrom December 2020 to October 2021, consecutive coronary artery disease (CAD) patients who underwent coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) and invasive FFR were prospectively enrolled. Prospective electrocardiographic (ECG)-triggered scan from 30% to 80% of R-R interval was applied on image extraction of all the patients. CT-FFR was implemented in systolic and diastolic phases for each target vessel.ResultsNinety-six patients (mean age 62 ± 8, 31.3% female) with 98 vessels were successfully simulated in both systolic and diastolic phases. The area under the curve (AUC) was significantly higher in diastolic CT-FFR (0.885 vs. 0.790, p < 0.001). The limits of agreement (LoA) range was narrower in diastolic CT-FFR (-0.134 to 0.148), compared with systolic CT-FFR (-0.234 to 0.273). Diastolic CT-FFR performed better than systolic CT-FFR in sensitivity (81.5% vs. 66.7%), specificity (83.1% vs. 71.8%), and accuracy (82.7% vs. 70.4%, all p < 0.05). The LoA range was narrower for diastolic CT-FFR across all stenosis degree groups, with diagnostic performance better in sensitivity (84.0% vs. 68.0%) and accuracy (81.4% vs. 58.1%, p < 0.001) among severe stenosis group.ConclusionsIn ML-based CT-FFR methods, diastolic CT-FFR showed better diagnostic performance and narrower LoA than systolic CT-FFR.Trial RegistrationThe study has been registered through the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, with the clinical trial number B2020-088R. Background

Nowadays, computed tomography-derived fractional flow reserve (CT-FFR) is considered a powerful tool for identifying hemodynamically significant coronary artery lesions in coronary artery disease (CAD), compared to invasive fractional flow reserve (FFR) with defects of invasiveness, high cost and drug-induced hyperemia [1–3]. ML-based CT-FFR has been validated for its accuracy and clinical utility in many studies, becoming an important alternative to CFD-based CT-FFR [4–6]. Compared with the requirement for off-site supercomputers to satisfy the vast computation in the CFD method, the computation of ML-based CT-FFR is less computationally intensive and time-consuming with comparable diagnostic performance, using on-site, physician-driving workstations [7–9]. In ML-based CT-FFR computation, Itu et al. used a deep neural network to integrate the relationship between CFD-based CT-FFR values and various anatomic features including radiuses of vessels, with a database of 12,000 synthetic coronary geometrics [3, 10, 11].

Image quality of CT is crucial in both CFD and ML simulation approaches [12]. In different cardiac phases, the coronary artery diameter changes, which potentially influences the measured severity of stenoses in coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) images [13]. Based on the ML algorithm, those inconsistent features of coronary arteries presented on CCTA images in systole and diastole can lead to the difference in image reconstruction and further influence CT-FFR simulation. However, whether in previous studies or clinical applications, the possible difference between systolic and diastolic CT-FFR was rarely studied. Instead, high-quality CCTA images with minimal artifacts were implemented to achieve the optimal image quality [14]. Although Gao et al. have demonstrated that the diagnostic performance of diastolic CT-FFR was superior to systolic CT-FFR, we presumed that CT-FFR was not only related to cardiac phases but also to the severity of stenosis itself [15]. The diagnostic performance of systolic and diastolic CT-FFR may also vary in plaques with different stenosis degrees [16–18].

Despite widespread adoption of CT-FFR, the optimal cardiac phase for analysis remains contested. While diastole dominates coronary perfusion, systole provides complementary anatomical information. Resolving this dichotomy is critical because: (1) Dual-phase acquisition doubles radiation exposure, raising ethical concerns; (2) ML models trained on single-phase data may yield suboptimal results if phase selection biases exist.

Therefore, in this study, we attempted to investigate the difference of CT-FFR based on the ML algorithm between systolic and diastolic phases, and to compare diagnostic performance for different stenosis degrees, using FFR as a reference standard.

Methods

Study population

All study procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (clinical trial number: B2020-088R). All study subjects provided written informed consent. From December 2020 to October 2021, consecutive symptomatic patients with clinically suspected or known CAD in the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine underwent CCTA examination, ICA and FFR. Exclusion criteria included previous coronary intervention or coronary bypass surgery; contraindications to β-blocking agents, nitroglycerin, or adenosine; suspected acute coronary syndrome; previous myocardial infarction < 30 days before CCTA or between CCTA and ICA; congenital heart disease; Serum creatinine greater than 115 µmol/L or glomerular filtration rate less than 60 ml/(min·1.73m2); and inadequate image quality for CT-FFR or without invasive FFR. For CCTA inclusion, patients’ heart rate should be equal to or less than 60 beats per minute (bpm). Screened patients were included in the study upon providing consent to undergo a research-indicated CCTA and invasive FFR who had between 30% and 90% stenosis in at least 1 major epicardial vessel measuring ≥ 2 mm in diameter by CCTA; and who had catheter-based FFR measured in that vessel within 2 weeks of CCTA.

Image acquisition for CCTA

CCTA was performed with a 320-row detector CT scanner (Aquilion One Vision; Canon Medical Systems Corporation) with 320 × 0.5 mm detector collimation and 275 msec gantry rotation time. Tube voltage was set by an experienced cardiac CT investigator according to the patient’s body mass index (BMI), with 80 kV for those ≤ 20, 100 kV for those between 20 and 25, and 120 kV for those > 25 [19, 20]. Considering the chosen tube voltage and the patient’s build, tube current was selected with the standard deviation (SD) value set to 20 Hounsfield Units (HU) [20]. Sublingual nitroglycerine (0.5 mg; Yimin Pharma) was administered to all patients and an additional β-blocker was given to patients with heart rates exceeding 60 bpm to achieve a pre-scan heart rate according to the protocol adopted from with Society of Cardiovascular Computed Tomography guidelines [21]. Additionally, intravenous injection of 50 to 70 mL of iodinated contrast (iopamidol, 370 mg iodine/mL, Bracco Sine Pharmaceutical Corp. Ltd.) was administrated at a rate of 4 to 5 mL/s. Prospective electrocardiographic (ECG)-gated scan from 30% to 80% of R-R interval was used to extract CCTA images of systolic and diastolic phases.

Reconstruction and analysis of CCTA in systolic and diastolic phases

All the CCTA data was processed using a workstation with dedicated plaque analysis software (SUREPlaque™, Canon Medical Systems) and standard kernel FC09 iterative reconstruction (AIDR 3D; Canon Medical Systems) by two cardiac radiologists, who had over 10 years of experience in cardiovascular radiology and were blinded to other analyses. Inter-observer agreement for image quality scoring was assessed (κ = 0.85), with discrepancies resolved by consensus. Manual corrections to centerlines and lumen boundaries showed high reproducibility (interclass correlation coefficient > 0.90). The image quality was further scored considering contrast opacification and artifacts in all segments and the worst score was set as the patient’s score [9]:

All the CCTA images were reconstructed at 5% intervals and those with image quality ≥ 2 were selected for subsequent evaluation. Images with the best quality in systolic and diastolic phases were reconstructed for CT-FFR simulation. According to the SCCT guideline, vessel stenosis were classified as minimal (1–24%), milde (25–49%), moderate (50% − 69%) and severe (70–99%) based on the percentage of diameter stenosis [23].

ICA and FFR measurement

ICA was conducted in the standard technique by experienced interventional cardiologists blinded to CCTA results. The coronary artery segments were divided by software (Syngo fastView, VX57I33; Siemens Healthineers) and Quantitative coronary analysis (QCA) was performed using the same software program. FFR was performed during ICA in vessels with diameter ≥ 2 mm and visual stenosis from 30% to 90%, using the FFR pressure guidewire system (PressureWire Certus, St Jude Medical Systems) [24]. Intravenous adenosine disodium triphosphate 125 (140 µg/ (kg per min)) was administrated to induce hyperemia status. Lesions with FFR ≤ 0.8 were defined to be hemodynamically significant [25].

ML-based CT-FFR computation

CT-FFR computation was carried out on the cFFR workstation (version 3.2.0, Siemens) and performed with systolic and diastolic CCTA images [3]. The centerline, lumen and stenosis in CT-FFR were generated automatically and were manually corrected by two experienced cardiologists blinded to other analyses. Thereafter, CT-FFR was calculated along the centerline and the values at 2 cm downstream of the lesions were adopted as lesion-specific values for subsequent analyses. Lesions were considered functionally significant when CT-FFR ≤ 0.8 [26]. Systolic and diastolic CT-FFR were defined as the CT-FFR values obtained using systolic images and diastolic images, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS, version 26.0 (Armonk: IBM Corp). Continuous variables were displayed as mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pm\:$$\end{document} SD or median (inter-quartile range) for normally and non-normally distributed data, respectively. Categorical variables were presented as quantities (percentages). Consistency was tested by Bland-Altman and paired t-test. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to determine the correlation. ROC and AUC were adopted to assess the ability of systolic and diastolic CT-FFR to identify hemodynamically significant ischemia, with AUCs compared by DeLong et al.’s method [27]. Diagnostic performance on a per-vessel basis was calculated by sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV) and accuracy [28]. The comparison of diagnostic performance indexes was calculated by chi-square test. The aforementioned analyses were also implemented after grouping. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Within a total of 180 patients undergoing study screening, 18 patients (10%) were excluded due to uncontrolled heart rate, 17 patients (9.4%) were excluded due to unsuitable stenosis degree, 13 patients (7.2%) were rejected due to inadequate image quality, 4 patients (2.2%) were excluded by unstable heart rate and 3 patients (1.7%) were rejected by high BMI. Figure 1 demonstrated the reasons for exclusion. Eleven patients (4.1%) were further excluded after CCTA acceptance. Thereafter, 7 patients (3.9%) were excluded due to failure of CT-FFR analysis within 6 in systolic and 1 in diastolic phases.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated ML-based CT-FFR in systolic and diastolic phases among the same group of patients and then compared the consistency and diagnostic performance. For identifying hemodynamical ischemia, diastolic CT-FFR performed better than systolic CT-FFR and showed narrower LoA. Meanwhile, in severe stenoses, diastolic CT-FFR showed better diagnostic performance compared with systolic CT-FFR.

Gao et al. found that diastolic CT-FFR was superior to systolic CT-FFR in coronary ischemic lesions, which was consistent with our findings [15, 29]. The vascular anatomy and measured stenosis severity varied in different cardiac phases, which were reflected in CCTA images and feature extraction in CT-FFR computation [3, 13]. These led to the difference between systolic and diastolic CT-FFR values, which was consistent with our findings. Furthermore, with the heart rate controlled, the diastolic phase accounted for the majority of the cardiac cycle and served as the main stage of blood supply in the myocardium. Coronary compliance varies between systole (reduced lumen diameter due to myocardial contraction) and diastole (maximal vasodilation), potentially explaining the narrower LoA in diastole. Diastolic-phase dominance in coronary perfusion may better reflect hyperemic conditions during FFR measurement, aligning with the physiological state of blood flow in invasive angiography. FFR was defined as the ratio of mean distal coronary artery pressure to the aortic pressure during maximal microcirculation hyperemia status, which was similar to the cardiac status of diastolic phase [30, 31]. Theoretically, diastolic CT-FFR was better correlated to FFR and had a better diagnostic performance, which was in accordance with our findings [32].

The different results that might be presented in stenosis with different degrees were not considered well by Gao et al. As to the discoveries in our study, no significant difference was found in the diagnostic performance of CT-FFR between the two phases in vessels with stenosis degree < 70%, which might be attributed to the insignificant flow restriction in mild-moderate stenoses and thus the insignificant difference in CT-FFR values between systole and diastole. In contrast, in severe plaques with a stenosis degree ≥ 70%, there was an increase in sensitivity and accuracy in diastolic CT-FFR. Gould et al. reported that with relatively severe stenoses, a small increase in percent diameter narrowing produces marked hemodynamic effects, accounting for the better diagnostic performance of diastolic CT-FFR in severe stenosis group in our study [33]. The assumption that the anatomical state of stenosis in vessels in diastolic period was more similar to the physiological state of blood vessels in the DSA state might explain the superiority of diastolic CT-FFR.

The narrower LoA for diastolic CT-FFR reduces diagnostic uncertainty, enhancing clinical utility in ruling out ischemia. The observed 46% narrower limits of agreement (LoA) for diastolic CT-FFR (-0.134 to 0.148) compared to systolic measurements (-0.234 to 0.273) carries significant clinical implications, as it translates to a 90% increase in confidence when interpreting diastolic values relative to invasive FFR. This enhanced precision is particularly critical for borderline cases, substantially reducing diagnostic uncertainty in therapeutic decision-making. Mechanistically, this advantage likely stems from diastolic-phase hemodynamic dominance better approximating FFR’s hyperemic conditions, combined with reduced motion artifacts during ventricular relaxation that improve lumen measurement accuracy.

Currently, CT-FFR has been widely used in clinically identifying hemodynamically significant ischemia, with a relatively good diagnostic performance [34, 35]. In clinical practice, CCTA images with the fewest artifacts and highest image quality were mostly adopted, regardless of the cardiac phase [14]. Our study has revealed the advantage of diastolic CT-FFR, which is expected to enhance its potential utility as a clinical application for higher diagnostic accuracy.

A few limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First of all, to pursue image quality from both systolic and diastolic phases, the exposure window was widened, which inevitably increased the radiation dose. While our protocol minimized radiation via BMI-adjusted tube voltage, routine dual-phase acquisition warrants ethical evaluation. Future studies should prospectively assess whether diagnostic gains justify the increased exposure, particularly in low-risk populations. Second, with controlled low and stable heart rates, the analysis for different heart rates was not conducted. What’s more, due to the assurance of adequate image quality in both systole and diastole, a small proportion of enrolled patients were excluded for failure analysis of diastolic or systolic CT-FFR, which led to selection bias. Therefore, further studies with larger samples are needed to validate our findings in the future.

Conclusions

In conclusion, ML-based diastolic CT-FFR showed better diagnostic performance and narrower limits of agreement compared to systolic CT-FFR.
