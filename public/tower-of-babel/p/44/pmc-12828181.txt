Does fluid irrigation in arthroscopic shoulder biopsy affect sensitivity of Cutibacterium acnes cultures for prosthetic shoulder joint infections?
Authors: Thomas Stanila, Amanda Harrington, Andrew L Chen, Samuel E Mircoff, Dane H Salazar, Nickolas G Garbis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCutibacterium acnes is the most common infectious agent in shoulder periprosthetic joint infection. Culture-based detection from tissue biopsy is the current gold standard for diagnosis, but the methods used to obtain it may affect accuracy of biopsies. The objective of this study is to investigate the impact of shoulder arthroscopy irrigation on culture-based detection from tissue biopsy samples for suspected C. acnes colonization in the setting of a periprosthetic total shoulder infection.MethodsAn in vitro model was designed to hold Gelfoam inoculated with a standard inoculum of C. acnes. Sterile sampling was performed in simulation of arthroscopic practice, both with and without saline irrigation being applied. All biopsies were subsequently incubated under anaerobic conditions in broth media and monitored for visible growth over the course of 14 days.ResultsSixty samples were collected for the no-irrigation group and 60 samples were collected for the irrigation group. A total of 19 biopsies (32%) in the no-irrigation group resulted in positive C. acnes growth within two weeks of initial culturing, compared to 14 positive biopsies (23%) in the irrigation group (P = .310). No significant differences resulted between the no-irrigation and irrigation groups at each evaluated time point (0-2 days, 3-7 days, 8-14 days).ConclusionIn the context of our model, irrigation during shoulder arthroscopy did not significantly impact recovery of C. acnes using culture-based methods in the setting of shoulder periprosthetic joint infections.Keywords: Cutibacterium acnes, Shoulder arthroscopy, Irrigation, Periprosthetic joint infection, Biopsy

Postoperative shoulder infections are relatively infrequent but can have devastating consequences, resulting in a significant increase in both length of stay and incurred expenses in the short-term, and a doubled all-cause mortality rate in the long-term.3,10,21,29 In shoulder periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs), Cutibacterium acnes represents the most common infectious agent, accounting for 38.9% of all reported infections.20

C. acnes, a gram-positive facultative anaerobic bacillus found on healthy adult skin, also colonizes the sebaceous glands surrounding hair follicles at increased rates in the dermis of the shoulder.7,23 This creates multiple challenges for preventing infection following shoulder arthroplasty. For one, conventional procedural sterilization applied to the epidermis—even including hair removal—cannot remove C. acnes in deep tissue.17 The mechanism for prosthetic seeding also appears enigmatic with some authors suggesting that only open surgeries lead to C. acnes contamination of the shoulder joint, while others have identified C. acnes as a commensal bacteria naturally residing within the shoulder articulation.14,18 Finally, when C. acnes induces prosthetic infection, its low virulence often presents without identifiable clinical symptoms, leading to a subacute manifestation similar to aseptic prosthetic failure.16 As such, understanding the propensity of C. acnes for PJIs remains a key clinical inquiry.

Arthroscopic evaluation and culture-based detection of biopsy samples remains the gold standard for diagnosing shoulder PJI. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Tat et al revealed that arthroscopic tissue cultures have a sensitivity of 76% and specificity of 91%, far above other diagnostic tests such as fluid aspiration and serum inflammatory markers.29 A key advantage to arthroscopy involves direct visualization of a potentially infected joint, allowing for precise and targeted biopsy compared to less invasive approaches. In order to visualize the joint space, fluid irrigation is commonly used in arthroscopy as it helps distend the joint space, clear blood, and create a tamponade effect.11 However, this may inadvertently reduce the quantity of C. acnes and limit the possibility of identifying the pathogen through subsequent biopsy.13

As a result, this study seeks to analyze the impact of shoulder arthroscopic irrigation on recovery of bacteria from tissue biopsy using culture methods in patients with suspected C. acnes infection of a total shoulder prosthesis. To pursue this aim, a novel glenohumeral joint model and laboratory protocol will serve as the investigatory method for C. acnes colonization and biopsy.

Materials and methods

Following institutional review board exemption, the experimental model was designed as follows. A 3D-printed base was produced using acrylonitrile butadiene styrene with 4 holes supporting 6.35 mm (0.25 inch) stainless steel screws per the details outlined in Fig. 1. This base was ensured to fit in a similarly dimensioned acrylic plastic box with 2 standard 6 mm × 2 cm arthroscopy passports placed on the top and side of the model. The side port was connected via sterile tubing to gravity-based continuous irrigation consisting of 0.9% normal saline as detailed in Fig. 2. Experimentation was performed in a sterile fashion, with preparation of the model using chlorhexidine and isopropyl alcohol immediately prior to each round of testing and the use of sterile gloves throughout sampling. All materials were disinfected with 10% bleach, followed by rinsing with isopropyl alcohol and being allowed to air-dry at the end of testing before being sealed for future trials.

A deidentified isolate of C. acnes obtained from a previous biopsy culture was used to inoculate the model tissue. The isolate was suspended in saline, standardized to 0.5 McFarland using optical density, and diluted 1:100 to a concentration of approximately 1.5 x 106 colony-forming units/mL prior to each round of testing. In addition, the final suspension was inoculated on blood agar. These plates were independently incubated under anaerobic conditions as an additional positive control for growth. All tested samples included in the final data had growth of C. acnes visibly detected from these purity culture plates, with the identification further confirmed using Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization-Time of Flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry (Bruker BioTyper, Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA).

For the model tissue, approximately 1.5 cm × 1.5 cm squares of sterile Gelfoam (Pfizer, New York, NY, USA) were cut and soaked in the suspension of C. acnes. These pieces were then placed and secured within the prepared models. For each trial, a negative control of saline immediately prior to usage was obtained. In addition, one of the inoculated Gelfoam pieces was reserved as a positive control. During experimentation, one version of the design served as the control with approximately 5 mL of saline dispensed in a thin film across the top of the base. This represented the synovial fluid otherwise encountered in a nonirrigated PJI. The other version had continuous irrigation only introduced once attention was turned to sampling that tissue. For both arrangements, a standard arthroscopic straight punch forceps was inserted through the top port to obtain biopsies from each of the 4 corners of the exposed tissue and 1 from the middle. The number and distribution of the sampling mirrors our institution's protocol in such arthroscopic procedures. Finally, any fluid left on the field of each model was sampled to account for potential displacement of C. acnes during introduction of saline.

Each biopsy and control was placed in sterile containers for transport to the laboratory. Using aseptic technique, each vial's contents were transferred in a biological safety cabinet to an individual tube of thioglycollate broth with appropriate vortexing to ensure integration. These inoculated broths were capped and placed in an anaerobic incubator. Daily visual inspection of each tube was performed over the course of 14 days, consistent with the clinical laboratory's standard culture protocol for samples from periprosthetic joint samples. In this period, the day that qualitative evaluation of a tube was visibly positive for growth was recorded under the following 3 categories: less than 3 days, 3-7 days, and 8-14 days. Confirmation of growth was performed by plating the sample on a blood agar, and organism identification was confirmed using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry.

A trial was considered successful and included in the dataset if positive and negative controls yielded expected levels of growth and if growth under experimental conditions was confirmed by plating thereafter. Proportions of positive growth between irrigated and nonirrigated groups, as well as their distribution across time categories were analyzed using chi-square testing, and effect size was determined with Cohen's h. Statistical significance was set for P values < .05.

Results

A total of 12 trials were completed with confirmed sample growth by culturing and appropriate controls. Sixty samples were collected for each experimental group, resulting in 120 biopsies studied. A total of 19 biopsies (32%) in the no-irrigation group resulted with positive C. acnes growth within two weeks of initial culturing compared to 14 positive biopsies (23%) in the irrigation group (P = .310, Cohen's h = 0.202). When these samples were analyzed by the time point in which they yielded positive growth, no significant differences resulted between the no-irrigation and irrigation groups at any time point. In addition, no growth was obtained from samples from post-testing fluid, even with corresponding positive experimental growth.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate the impact of continuous irrigation on recovery of C. acnes using culture-based methods in the simulated setting of shoulder PJI. In pursuing this aim, a novel laboratory design and protocol was formulated. This methodology, with replicable, controlled results, revealed that irrigation used at the time of biopsy does not significantly hinder the rate of culture-positive growth or the time to positivity.

The challenges of optimizing diagnosis of PJIs have long been evaluated in the literature. Historically, culturing aspirated synovial fluid marks the key initial diagnostic step, but oftentimes this is not possible because of the likelihood of encountering a “dry tap.” In these cases, principles of joint aspiration maintain that saline solution lavage with reaspiration allows for sensitive and specific diagnoses of PJI.15,22 However, this technique does not always correlate with intraoperative culture findings, requiring additional investigation into how irrigation potentially affects culture results in the setting of infection.5,12 This becomes especially important in the setting of wet arthroscopy biopsy procedures, a gold-standard technique in identifying periprosthetic shoulder infections, as has been documented.2,6,29 Our findings suggest that the diagnostic accuracy of arthroscopy biopsy may not be significantly dependent on the presence of irrigation fluid used to help distend and visualize the joint space.

Our results also support the principle that the growth characteristics of C. acnes themselves pose the greatest impediment to accurate diagnosis and treatment. In clinical practice, the bacterium is known to present with delayed and more subtle findings, often resulting in subclinical signs of local infection on physical examination and laboratory evaluation.8,28 These inconsistencies extend from their biological properties, with the species' slow-growing and low-virulence nature necessitating extended, anaerobic culturing beyond the parameters of other bacterial etiologies.27 Our testing encountered similar obstacles, with limited culture-confirmed growth in certain experimental trials even despite positive control growth. This was especially compelling considering the lack of positive growth from post-testing fluid, potentially suggesting disruption of C. acnes in its growth cycle from irrigation fluid or the lack of meaningful displacement of the bacterium into recovered fluid for subsequent culture growth. Regardless, this shows gaps still remain in the current clinical workflow for consistently addressing C. acnes' role in periprosthetic infections, though continuing advances in polymerase chain reaction assays and next-generation sequencing have been shown to help close these gaps.25,26

Our model as representative of PJIs demonstrates an important first step. To our knowledge, it is the first in vitro design attempting to simulate an arthroscopic approach to an infected shoulder arthroplasty. Previous approaches in the literature have relied on rat models to translationally investigate the manifestations of PJIs, though these have largely targeted Staphylococcus aureus.16 More modern techniques have even documented weight-bearing rat implants for targeted study of biofilm development.4,30 The advantages of these designs are innumerable, and they remain the gold standard in the research of infection within in vivo joint spaces. In the context of emulating an arthroscopic approach to a shoulder infection, it was considered that an in vitro design would best allow the controlled sampling of tissue under the experimental conditions. This is especially relevant as the aforementioned microbiologic tendencies of C. acnes have limited the bacterium's study in the in vivo setting.27 The method detailed by Achermann et al provides a laudable in vivo approach through intramedullary metaphyseal injection of dextran beads coated with C. acnes biofilm; however, this was completed in native tibias without the option for joint manipulation.1 Thus, our methodology provides a useful alternative to in vivo models without sacrificing reproducibility and necessary controls. Further advantages include relative cost-effectiveness and availability of the technique, eliminating testing on live animals or recovered patient tissue. In addition, production and reuse of the design is comparatively fast, relying on only basic clinical resources and standard laboratory pathways for culturing. The nature of the model also offers accessible implementation and analysis of other salient factors during operative irrigation. These may include the type of fluid used for lavage, such as normal saline, povidone-iodine, or chlorhexidine gluconate; the role of antibiotic-infused irrigation; the use of antibiotic-loaded spacers; and the study of pulse versus continuous irrigation.9,19,24

There are several limitations to be noted with this study. For one, the in vitro design necessarily forfeits the biochemical milieu of the glenohumeral joint that may otherwise be represented in an in vivo model. This represents an important component of fully understanding the development of periprosthetic infection that is not present in our design. In addition, while serial titration in the dilution of C. acnes was performed in the development of the study to identify an inoculum concentration that would reliably be identified in our technique, this cannot be said to fully represent the bacterial load present in a C. acnes PJI. This is further relevant when considering the presence of biofilms, which is crucial for the bacterium to develop around a prosthesis. Furthermore, while in vitro models of C. acnes biofilms have been previously described,31 our study represents the first use of Gelfoam as a soft tissue substrate to our knowledge. Thus, it remains unclear whether our chosen methodology accurately simulates the microbiologic behavior of C. acnes in an in vivo environment. Other factors, such as the use of gravity-based irrigation as opposed to standard surgical equipment, similarly prevent complete translational application of the findings. Finally, the absence of an exact time to positivity with quantifiable culture data must be addressed.

It is relevant to discuss the large discrepancy between the reported sensitivity rate of arthroscopic tissue culture in clinical practice (76%)29 and those found in our experimental model (23% with irrigation, 32% without irrigation). While the absolute difference is substantial, it is expected given the simplified conditions of our in vitro model. Importantly, the relative difference between experimental cohorts in our study highlights a strength of the testing model to detect differences between controlled variables. Though the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .310), a small effect size was found (Cohen's h = 0.202), suggesting that larger differences may be noted in larger sample sizes. Additional testing expanding the number of trials available to analyze as well as more quantitative time to positivity is necessary not only to further explore the mentioned relationship between irrigation and positive culture growth, but also to continue validating the novel design.

Conclusion

Within the context of our model, irrigation during shoulder arthroscopy does not significantly impact C. acnes culture in PJI. The proposed in vitro technique, with reproducible and accessible options in testing, offers a new avenue for further testing in this field of inquiry.
