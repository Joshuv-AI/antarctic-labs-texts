A computational model for retinal haemodynamics under gravitational and postural variations
Authors: Michele Nigro, Andrea Montanino, Eduardo Soudah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpaceflight‐associated neuro‐ocular syndrome presents a significant risk during long‐duration spaceflight, yet the retinal mechanisms underlying these visual changes remain poorly characterized. We developed a physiologically detailed lumped‐parameter model coupling a five‐compartment retinal vascular network with a dynamic intraocular pressure module responsive to hydrostatic shifts. Head‐down tilt experiments were reproduced to emulate cephalad fluid shifts in microgravity. Model outputs are closely aligned with experimental benchmarks. Intraocular pressure increased from 16.3 mmHg (upright) to 29.5 mmHg at −30° (81% increase), while ocular perfusion pressure remained within 2 mmHg, reflecting haemodynamic responses. Retinal blood‐flow predictions fell within the interquartile range of in vivo Doppler measurements, and peak systolic velocity trends in the central retinal artery agreed within 1% of ultrasound data after incorporating a microgravity‐induced 4% central retinal artery constriction. These findings validate the model's ability to capture the coupled influence of systemic arterial pressure, venous drainage and intraocular pressure on retinal perfusion across gravitational states. Simulations revealed that posture‐induced elevations in arterial pressure and intraocular pressure propagate non‐linearly through the retinal tree, imposing the greatest mechanical stress on the central retinal artery and arteriolar segments, critical sites for flow dysregulation and potential optic nerve damage. By integrating retinal and ocular biomechanics, the model developed in this work offers mechanistic insight into the pathogenesis of neuro‐ocular syndrome, establishes quantitative thresholds to inform countermeasure development, and provides a transferable tool for terrestrial disorders involving intraocular pressure and vascular dysregulation, such as glaucoma and hypertensive retinopathy. Key points Microgravity and postural changes alter ocular circulation, contributing to spaceflight‐associated neuro‐ocular syndrome (SANS), yet their impact on retinal haemodynamicsremains poorly characterized.We present a lumped‐parameter model that dynamically couples intraocular pressure (IOP) with retinal blood flow through a five‐compartment vascular network.The model simulates posture‐induced hydrostatic changes and accurately reproduces trends in IOP, ocular perfusion pressure and retinal flow observed in head‐down tilt experiments.Simulations reveal that arterial compartments (especially the central retinal artery) are the most sensitive to gravitational stress and vascular constriction, identifying key sites of haemodynamic vulnerability.This framework provides novel mechanistic insights into the retinal contributions to SANS and offers a validated platform for evaluating countermeasures and studying ocular pathologies.

Introduction

Spaceflight‐associated neuro‐ocular syndrome (SANS) is a major physiological challenge in human spaceflight [National Aeronautics & Space Administration (NASA), 2020]. SANS is characterized by a set of ocular and neuro‐ophthalmic alterations observed in astronauts after prolonged exposure to microgravity, including optic disc oedema, choroidal folds, globe flattening and hyperopic shifts (Galdamez et al. 2025; Lee et al. 2020; Mader et al. 2011; Nelson et al. 2014; Taibbi et al. 2013). While initially attributed to intracranial pressure (ICP) elevation and alterations in the translaminar pressure gradient, recent evidence suggests that SANS is a multifactorial condition driven by cephalad fluid shifts, cerebral venous congestion, compartmentalized cerebrospinal fluid dynamics and impaired glymphatic clearance (Barisano et al. 2022; Killer et al. 2006; Nelson et al. 2014; Wostyn et al. 2020; Zhang & Hargens, 2018). These interconnected pathophysiological mechanisms alter ocular perfusion, choroidal circulation and retinal vascular autoregulation, leading to both structural and functional visual changes that may persist long after re‐entry to Earth (Laurie et al. 2019; Mader et al. 2017).

Limitations of the current models

Despite significant advances in space medicine and neuro‐ophthalmic imaging, the precise pathophysiology of SANS remains only partially understood (Lee et al. 2016; Mader et al. 2011; Ong, Tarver, et al., 2023; Whittle & Diaz‐Artiles, 2023). Since in‐flight ICP and ocular circulation measurements are very difficult to obtain (Galdamez et al. 2025; Hiles et al. 2015; Lerner et al. 2019), researchers rely on head‐down tilt (HDT) bed rest studies as terrestrial analogues to simulate cephalad fluid shifts and investigate SANS‐related mechanisms (Barkaszi et al. 2022; Hargens & Vico, 2016; Laurie et al. 2019; Linder et al. 1988; Ong et al. 2021; Van Akin et al. 2022; Watenpaugh, 2016). However, to better quantify the complex interactions between gravitational changes, intracranial and ocular haemodynamics, computational models have been developed as complementary tools (Caddy et al. 2024; Fois et al. 2022; Heldt, 2004; Heldt et al. 2002; Nelson et al. 2020; Scarsoglio et al. 2023). Computational models allow more detailed and refined exploration of ocular circulation under varying gravitational states (Fois et al. 2024; Nelson et al. 2017; Salerni et al. 2019; Wilson et al. 2024). Combining HDT experiments with numerical modelling has proven particularly effective for advancing our understanding of conditions such as SANS (Fois et al. 2024; Petersen et al. 2022). Nevertheless, current ocular models still fail to capture the complexity and interactions between different ocular compartments fully. In Salerni et al. (2019), a model consisting of the brain, eye and aqueous humour circuit was presented, in which microgravity conditions were simulated by implementing an HDT and microgravity scenarios acting on the value of the filtration coefficient between the capillaries in the brain and the brain tissue, as well as the reflection coefficient. However, this model does not account for more recent studies, from which additional experimental data and mathematical modelling approaches could be taken into account. Conversely, in a more recent study from Fois et al. (2024), along with a multiscale model of the cardiovascular system, an HDT manoeuvre was simulated alongside an experimental campaign, including an ocular model, for ocular volumes and intraocular pressure (IOP) changes, but without considering any interaction with the retinal vasculature.

Motivation for this study

Given its high metabolic demand and sensitivity to fluctuations in oxygenation and perfusion, the retina plays a central role in spaceflight‐induced visual impairment (Grigoryan, 2023; Iftime et al. 2024; Navasiolava et al. 2020). As the neural interface between the eye and brain, its function depends on a delicate balance between intraocular and systemic circulatory factors (Baden, 2024; Masland, 2001). Recent findings by Iftime et al. (2024) further highlight the susceptibility of the perivascular retina to gravitational shifts, showing that acute exposure to a microgravity analogue significantly alters visual performance, particularly by increasing reaction times.

Overview of the proposed model and its novelty

To address these limitations, we developed a computational model to simulate the effects of gravitational and postural changes on retinal circulation by coupling fluid redistribution dynamics with variations in IOP due to changes in body inclination. A notable innovation of our model is its integration of two distinct yet interconnected models: a retinal vascular network model from Guidoboni et al. (2014) and an IOP model responsive to gravitational orientation defined in Nelson et al. (2017). By coupling these components, the model provides a physiologically accurate representation of ocular circulation, reflecting realistic interactions between IOP fluctuations and retinal haemodynamics. This integrative approach addresses a critical gap in the literature by enabling the prediction of retinal haemodynamic alterations induced by microgravity, key contributors to the onset and progression of SANS. Ultimately, this study offers a physiologically robust framework to investigate spaceflight‐induced ocular alterations, supporting the development of targeted countermeasures to safeguard astronaut ocular health. To our knowledge, while several computational studies have examined ocular pressure dynamics or eye–brain coupling under postural change or simulated microgravity, including lumped‐parameter and 3D arterial models, a framework that explicitly resolves the retinal arterial–capillary–venous network and couples it to posture hydrostatic shifts with head‐down tilt validation has not yet been reported. This work focuses on retinal haemodynamics under microgravity‐analogue conditions and does not explicitly simulate the choroidal and ciliary circulation. Shared couplings are retained via ophthalmic input pressure, episcleral venous pressure (EVP) and IOP; implications and planned extensions are detailed in the ‘Limitations and future developments’ section. This paper is organized as follows. First, we introduce the computational model for retinal haemodynamics, providing an overview of its general framework before detailing the mathematical formulation of the ocular vascular network, the dynamic interaction between retinal circulation and IOP, and the effects of gravitational and postural changes. Next, we explain how the microgravity conditions are incorporated into the computational model, considering hydrostatic pressure redistribution and systemic circulatory adaptations. The Results section then presents the simulated haemodynamic responses under different gravitational conditions, analysing variations in blood pressure, retinal blood flow and vascular haemodynamic responses across multiple tilt angles, followed by a critical discussion of the results.

Materials and methods

Physiological rationale for the circuit model

The eye is supplied by the central retinal artery (CRA) and drained by the central retinal vein (CRV), traversing the lamina cribrosa (Fig. 1); inside the eye, they branch into arterioles, capillaries and venules that perfuse the retina, while the choroid and ciliary body serve adjacent tissues (Bertelli et al. 2019). Blood (and aqueous humour) is driven by intravascular pressure gradients and is opposed by Poiseuille‐type hydraulic resistance. Where wall distensibility is non‐zero, small pressure changes are stored as volume (vascular compliance). To translate these principles into a compact mathematical form, we use the hydraulic–electrical analogy, relying on the lumped‐parameter modelling. Within this computational framework, vascular segments are represented as elements of an electrical circuit, where pressure corresponds to voltage, flow corresponds to current, and vascular characteristics are modelled using resistors and capacitors. Moreover, lumped models are computationally efficient, striking a balance between accuracy and complexity compared to 1D and 3D models, while still providing meaningful insights (Brown et al. 2012; Garber et al. 2022; Kokalari et al. 2013).

Model structure

The retinal vasculature is divided into five compartments: CRA, arterioles, capillaries, venules and CRV. These can be modelled as an interconnected network (Fig. 1). Such a lumped approach is standard in cardiovascular modelling, capturing coupled pressure–flow–volume dynamics with a lower computational cost and allowing systematic parameter studies and posture/gravitation scenarios (Diaz‐Artiles et al. 2019; Garber et al. 2022; Ghitti et al. 2022; Heldt, 2004; Westerhof et al. 1969). Active regulatory mechanisms (myogenic tone, flow‐mediated dilatation) and neurovascular coupling are not modelled. Any pressure dependence currently represents passive transmural loading rather than active control.

Each vascular segment is exposed to different external pressures depending on its position within the network. Intraocular segments are subjected to IOP, retrobulbar segments experience the retrolaminar tissue pressure (RLTp), which is linked to ICP (Morgan et al. 1998), while translaminar segments are influenced by an external pressure that depends on the internal stress distribution within the lamina cribrosa. These pressure interactions play a crucial role in determining the mechanical and haemodynamic behaviour of the ocular system. Unlike previous models that considered IOP as a fixed boundary condition, our approach models the IOP as a dynamic state variable. This advance is based on the initial mathematical model proposed by Nelson et al. (2017), and subsequently refined by Petersen et al. (2022) and Fois et al. (2024). Our approach enables a dynamic coupling between IOP and ocular haemodynamics, allowing the model to capture feedback mechanisms essential for understanding transient effects and haemodynamic responses under altered gravitational conditions, such as microgravity and HDT manoeuvres. By integrating the IOP variation intrinsically within the system, the model provides a biomechanically justified representation of ocular blood flow dynamics and their interaction with systemic circulatory changes. The mathematical formulation of our model, including all governing equations, parameter definitions and detailed derivations, is comprehensively presented in the Appendix.

Boundary conditions and posture effects

As the primary input parameter for the numerical model, the arterial blood pressure upstream of the eye (P in) was selected. In the lumped modelling framework, this pressure acts as the driving force for haemodynamic flow through the retinal vasculature, analogous to a voltage source in an electrical circuit. The relationship between pressure, flow and resistance follows Ohm's law in haemodynamics (Table 1).

Regarding P out, that is the venous pressure downstream of the eye, we adopted the condition proposed in the model by Petersen et al. (2022). To maintain physiological venous outflow, the model ensures that P out remains above the central venous pressure (CVP), set at 7 mmHg. When EVP is greater than CVP, P out follows EVP; otherwise, it is equal to CVP. This relationship is expressed as a piecewise function: (1) Pout=CVP,ifEVP<CVP,EVP,ifEVP≥CVP.

Equation (1) ensures that venous outflow remains physiologically viable under different gravitational conditions, as EVP varies with tilt angle and systemic circulatory changes. The dynamic interaction between EVP and CVP is particularly relevant in conditions such as microgravity and HDT, where venous return and episcleral venous drainage are altered. To simulate the HDT manoeuvre, we considered multiple tilt angles relative to the horizontal axis. For clarity, Fig. 2 defines the tilt angle θ and illustrates the tilt angles considered in the simulations (90°, 0°, −6°, −15°, −30°), extending beyond the commonly used −6 HDT condition to compare with broader experimental datasets. The P in that in our model was assumed to correspond to a standing condition (90° inclination) is the one calculated and used in Guidoboni et al. (2014). Guidoboni et al. (2014) estimated patient‐specific values for the P in and P out profiles using an inverse problem approach. This estimation was based on blood flow velocity measurements acquired via bidirectional laser Doppler velocimetry in the CRA and CRV. These velocity profiles, when combined with the governing equations of the model, including Poiseuille's law for resistance and vascular compliance equations (Table 1), allow for a physiologically accurate reconstruction of ocular haemodynamics, ensuring that the model captures the key pressure–flow interactions regulating retinal perfusion. To assess the pressure variations associated with different tilt angles, the hydrostatic pressure component (P h) was computed as: (2) Ph=ρgh·sinθ, where ρ is the blood density, g is the gravitational acceleration, h is the vertical distance of the compartment from the reference level and θ is the tilt angle. By incorporating these gravitational effects into the governing equations, the model accounts for tilt‐induced changes in ocular perfusion and venous drainage, providing insights into the impact of altered gravitational conditions on retinal haemodynamics.

Changes in body position significantly impact the distribution of P h throughout the cardiovascular system (Diaz‐Artiles et al. 2019; Heldt, 2004; Heldt et al. 2002). This effect is particularly relevant in ocular haemodynamics, where hydrostatic shifts influence both P in and EVP. To account for these variations, the arterial ocular pressure at a given tilt angle P in ,θ is computed by adding the hydrostatic pressure component to the standing condition (90º) input pressure: (3) Pin,θ=Pin+Ph, where P in ,θ represents the arterial input pressure at different tilt angles. This formulation ensures that the model correctly incorporates tilt‐induced pressure changes affecting retinal perfusion. Furthermore, the EVP also varies with tilt angle, influencing venous outflow conditions. As per Nelson et al. (2017), EVP follows the empirical relationship (4) EVP=9.0mmHg−αsinθ, where the coefficient α depends on the inclination regime, as α = 22.1 mmHg for θ ≤ 0 (e.g. HDT and inversion),α = 2.23 mmHg for θ ≥ 0 (e.g. upright posture).

By incorporating these pressure‐dependent boundary conditions, the model dynamically adjusts arterial and venous pressures in response to body position, improving its physiological accuracy in simulating gravitational effects on ocular haemodynamics. For the CRA, blood velocity is governed by (5) vCRAt=8πDCRA2Pint−P1tRin+R1a, where P 1(t) denotes the arterial downstream pressure to P in(t), D CRA is the diameter of the vessel, and R in and R 1 a represent the vascular resistances of the corresponding segment. This equation is derived from Poiseuille's law (Table 1).

By utilizing pressure profiles corresponding to different tilt angles, key haemodynamic parameters such as peak systolic velocity (PSV), end‐diastolic velocity (EDV) and mean velocity can be determined. These parameters are essential for evaluating blood flow regulation and vascular autoregulation under varying gravitational conditions, providing insights into the effects of microgravity, HDT and other posture‐induced changes on ocular circulation.

Discretization and solution

All simulations were carried out using an in‐house developed MATLAB® (R2023a, MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) code, on a standard desktop computer. The code implements a lumped‐parameter network, resulting in an ordinary differential equation (ODE) system. After discretizing, through an implicit Euler time scheme, and a fixed time step ∆t, the ODE system is recast into a system of linear equations for each step, which is solved by adopting the built‐in MATLAB linear solver operator.

Results

Accuracy of the numerical code

The numerical model introduced in the previous section has been run adopting different time steps, to evaluate the accuracy of the results for each of them, and the corresponding computational time. In particular, a convergence study has been performed on the peak systolic pressure (PSP), end‐diastolic pressure (EDP) and MAP. More specifically, the time steps ∆t = 0.01 s, ∆t = 0.005 s and ∆t = 0.001 s have been considered, and the results show excellent agreement, with a relative error below 2% for all haemodynamic parameters between the coarsest and the most refined time discretization. Accordingly, the time step of ∆t = 0.01 s has been chosen as the most suitable in terms of accuracy and computational cost.

Peak systolic velocity

The peak PSV in the CRA was also analysed under different tilt angles. Figure 5 shows the numerical predictions alongside experimental data obtained from the reference study by Sirek et al. (2014). Their dataset includes six non‐astronaut subjects exposed to HDT at various inclinations, as well as 17 astronauts with pre‐, in‐ and post‐flight data from long‐duration space missions aboard the International Space Station. Additionally, a second set of simulations was performed, incorporating a 4% reduction in CRA diameter at θ = −6° and θ = −30°, following the parabolic flight experiments by Mader et al. (1993). As shown in Fig. 5, our numerical model closely follows the experimentally observed trend of increasing PSV with tilt angle up to θ = −6°, beyond which a slight decline is observed. The model's predictions for normal CRA diameter (red markers) align well with experimental data. From a quantitative perspective, at the upright angle (90°), the simulated PSV (9.79 cm/s) is 8.01% higher than experimental values (9.06 cm/s); for a supine position (0°), the difference is 0.98%, with a simulated value of 11.43 cm/s versus an experimental value of 11.32 cm/s; at slight HDT (−6°), the model underpredicts by 5.83% (simulated value of 11.28 cm/s versus an experimental value of 11.98 cm/s), and at −30° the underestimation is 9.13% (simulation: 10.75 cm/s, experimental: 11.83 cm/s). Using the above metrics, agreement for PSV is quantified via RMSE, nRMSE, angle‐wise (∆ a ) and z‐scores with ±1 SD coverage. Hence, pooling the four angles (90°, 0°, −6°, −30°), the model–data misfit is RMSE = 0.74 cm/s (nRMSE = 6.7%). Standardized residuals (z‐scores relative to the experimental mean ±SD from Sirek et al. 2014) are [+2.40, +0.30, −1.23, −2.31], yielding a ±1 SD coverage of 1/4 (25%). For negative tilt angles (−6° and −30°), introducing a 4% diameter reduction (blue markers) results in a noticeable increase in PSV compared to the baseline simulation and improves agreement with experimental values. At −6°, the PSV difference is reduced to 0.41% (simulation: 11.93 cm/s, experimental: 11.98 cm/s). At −30°, the underestimation is reduced to 3.80% (simulation: 11.38 cm/s, experimental: 11.83 cm/s). Focusing on the negative tilts (−6° and −30°), introducing a 4% CRA diameter reduction lowers the misfit from 0.91 cm/s (baseline) to 0.32 cm/s, that is a relative decrease of 64.8%. Additional haemodynamics quantities

Figure 6 presents the evolution of the retinal blood flow rate, the EDV and resistivity index (RI) as functions of tilt angle θ. As the body transitions from a −30° HDT to a supine posture (0°), both retinal flow rate and EDV increase progressively. Specifically, flow rate rises from 0.76 µl/s at −30° to 0.84 µl/s at 0°. A similar trend is observed for EDV, which grows from 3.46 cm/s at −30°to 4.13 cm/s at 0°. Upon assuming an upright posture (90°), both flow rate and EDV drop to 0.65 µl/s and 2.49 cm/s, respectively. Concurrently, RI decreases from 0.679 at −30° to 0.64 at 0°, then increases to 0.75 at 90°.

Discussion

Overall, the model accurately reproduced both the direction and magnitude of physiological trends observed experimentally, highlighting its robustness and physiological reliability across various gravitational regimes. We next interpret these findings in physiological terms, focusing on CRA pressure and the implications for long‐term SANS risk. Our simulations indicate that increased proximal CRA pressure (P 1) and posture‐dependent OPP shifts are consistent with HDT physiology, where cephalad fluid shift elevates MAPeye and alters ocular compartment pressures.

In addition to reproducing these global haemodynamic trends, the model predicts a clearly pulsatile CRA pressure waveform. This behaviour is consistent with experimental and clinical evidence reporting marked systolic–diastolic oscillations in the CRA and supports the physiological plausibility of the simulated pulse pressure, as confirmed by direct and indirect measurements under physiological conditions. Indeed, intravascular recordings have revealed systolic–diastolic oscillations synchronized with the cardiac cycle (Glucksberg & Dunn, 1993), while laser doppler flowmetry studies have consistently reported periodic variations in flow velocity and resistance index in phase with the aortic pulse (Feke et al., 1978; Harris et al., 1998; Mahmoud, 2018; Michelson & Harazny, 1997; Petrig et al., 1999; Riva et al., 1985). These findings are consistent with the CRA's anatomical origin from the ophthalmic artery (which in turn is a direct branch of the internal carotid artery) allowing the transmission of systemic arterial pressure oscillations with minimal damping. Pulse‐wave attenuation occurs predominantly downstream within the arteriolar and capillary networks rather than in the CRA itself. As a result, the CRA behaves as a central artery, and the associated pressure fluctuations can reasonably be expected to fall within the physiological range typical of the ophthalmic circulation (Pournaras et al., 2008; Schmetterer & Ismail, 2024).

It is important to note that this physiological pulsatility should be distinguished from pathological pulsations, which occur under abnormal ICP or IOP conditions and are characterized by irregular oscillations (Berrett et al., 2021). The pulsatile behaviour predicted by our model therefore reflects normal arterial haemodynamics, rather than a pathological response such as that observed during central retinal artery occlusion.

Human tilt and in‐flight studies report posture‐dependent changes in pressures and CRA Doppler velocities, supporting a haemodynamic substrate for SANS (Petersen et al. 2022; Sirek et al. 2014). In parallel, strict HDT and International Space Station missions have shown optic disc changes, and a proposed peri‐arterial cerebrospinal fluid influx along the CRA may raise extravascular pressure, potentially compromising capillary perfusion despite higher arterial inflow (Barisano et al. 2022; Laurie et al. 2019; Mader et al. 2011; Ong, Mader, et al., 2023; Wostyn et al. 2020). Importantly, post‐flight imaging has revealed decreased retinal vascular patterning, suggesting microvascular remodelling after prolonged exposure (Sirek et al. 2014; Vyas et al. 2020). By analogy with terrestrial conditions, glaucoma mechanotransduction and hypertensive microangiopathy, chronic low‐grade pressure and tissue strain can trigger neuro‐inflammatory and vascular remodelling cascades (Dziedziak et al. 2022; Taibbi et al. 2021). Collectively, these observations frame our model's CRA–pressure findings within a plausible pathway from altered ocular haemodynamics to subclinical retinal risk during long‐duration missions. The relative stability of the OPP observed in our model, despite a substantial increase in IOP with postural changes, suggests effective retinal haemodynamic responses. These mechanisms typically maintain blood flow even when facing significant reductions in OPP, as demonstrated in experimental studies such as those by Puchner et al. (2020).

Such compensation usually holds until a critical threshold (around a 21–30 mmHg decrease) is reached, after which autoregulation fails, causing a sharp drop in retinal blood flow. This protective compensatory response is essential in safeguarding the optic nerve head from ischaemic injury under acute gravitational challenges. However, it may become insufficient during extended or extreme gravitational exposures, or in individuals with compromised vascular autoregulation. This vulnerability is probably a contributing factor in the development of SANS, as prolonged gravitational alterations may surpass vascular compensatory limits, leading to neuro‐ophthalmic damage.

The attenuation of pulsatility from the CRA to downstream compartments (see Fig. 4) reflects passive damping of the transmitted pressure wave by vascular resistance and compliance. The posture‐dependent increase in baseline pressures is consistent with cranial hydrostatic pressure redistribution under HDT, which in turn can affect IOP. Given the retina's high vascularization, sustained increases in pressure may compromise perfusion and contribute to hypoperfusion, oxidative stress and inflammation (Di Marco et al. 2022; Modi & Arsiwalla, 2023). Compartmental pressure escalations in the CRA and arteriolar segments indicate increased mechanical stress on retinal microvessels, potentially impairing perfusion. Such insights may inform threshold levels for countermeasure deployment during long‐term missions. By quantitatively replicating ocular responses observed in microgravity analogues, the present model provides a framework to reason about countermeasures against spaceflight‐induced ocular complications. For velocity, the improved agreement obtained by incorporating a 4% CRA diameter reduction at negative tilt angles supports the hypothesis that microgravity‐induced vascular constriction contributes to the observed PSV trends (Mader et al. 2011). Integrating geometrical and physiological changes identified in microgravity studies and, where appropriate, accounting for individual variability (e.g., sex‐specific differences), could further enhance predictive accuracy and facilitate patient‐specific modelling. In our model, both P in(t) and IOP evolved dynamically in response to changes in body inclination, capturing interactions between systemic and ocular pressures under gravitational modulation. While the model by Guidoboni et al. (2014) offered valuable contributions in the context of glaucoma, focusing primarily on elevated IOP, our approach complements that framework by incorporating posture‐dependent variations in systemic pressure, thereby adding physiological insight relevant to altered gravity environments such as spaceflight. Finally, the joint behaviour of EDV and RI reinforces the interpretation that horizontal and HDT conditions favour retinal circulation through higher diastolic velocities and lower effective vascular resistance, whereas upright posture imposes a haemodynamic burden that may exacerbate microvascular stress under sustained gravitational loading.

Limitations and future developments

While the current model provides valuable insights into retinal haemodynamics under altered gravity, it has several limitations. First, the model cannot fully replicate the complex physiological environment of spaceflight, where chronic fluid redistribution, cardiovascular remodelling and prolonged microgravity exposure interact in ways not captured by our current framework (Barkaszi et al. 2022; Galdamez et al. 2025; Oluwafemi & Neduncheran, 2022; Watenpaugh, 2016). While HDT provides a useful terrestrial analogue, it remains an approximation that cannot reproduce the sustained interplay between intracranial pressure and retinal circulation observed during long‐duration missions. Second, the model currently lacks inter‐individual physiological variability, including sex‐based differences, pre‐existing conditions and personalized haemodynamic responses that could significantly influence ocular adaptation to gravitational changes (Evans et al. 2018; Kordi et al. 2012; Strock et al. 2023). Additionally, radiation‐induced vascular effects, a critical concern for deep space exploration, are not incorporated, potentially underestimating the full spectrum of ocular risks (Cialdai et al. 2021; Mao et al. 2022; Waisberg et al. 2024). From a methodological perspective, our application of Poiseuille's law assumes rigid, cylindrical vessels with Newtonian fluid behaviour, which, though standard in haemodynamic modelling, may oversimplify the complex rheological properties of retinal microcirculation (Garber et al. 2022).

The present framework does not explicitly resolve the choroidal circulation nor active retinal autoregulatory feedback (myogenic tone, flow‐mediated dilatation). This constrains interpretability for outcomes driven primarily by choroidal haemodynamics or arteriolar tone adaptation; accordingly, inferences are restricted to retinal pressures and flows under passive vascular properties and posture‐dependent conditions. To address these omissions, future developments will include extending the framework to a whole‐eye model by adding ciliary and choroidal segments and the main aqueous pathways, and by introducing a simple arteriolar autoregulation mechanism. These additions complement the planned cranio‐ocular coupling and venous‐architecture refinements and enable mechanistic testing and extension to choroidal endpoints relevant to SANS and terrestrial disease. Looking forward, for broader applications, the framework could be integrated with established models of cerebrospinal fluid dynamics and cerebral haemodynamics (Holmlund et al. 2022; Scarsoglio et al. 2023; Ursino & Giannessi, 2010), creating a comprehensive platform for studying cranio‐ocular interactions. Incorporating detailed venous architecture would facilitate investigations of thrombosis risk and vascular compliance under gravitational stress (Elahi et al. 2024; Kim et al. 2021; Lan et al. 2021; Whittle & Diaz‐Artiles, 2023; Yu et al. 2023). The model's clinical utility could be expanded by adapting it to terrestrial pathologies such as hypertensive retinopathy and glaucoma, where altered pressure dynamics play a central role (Guidoboni et al. 2014). While our mechanistic predictions align with several SANS observations, extrapolation to long‐term risk remains tentative without follow‐up across missions and analogues. Future work should integrate in‐flight vascular imaging and inflammatory markers to test the remodelling and neuro‐inflammation hypotheses raised here. These developments will strengthen the model's dual role in advancing space medicine and improving our understanding of pressure‐related ocular pathologies on Earth.

Conclusion

This study presents a physiologically grounded computational model that captures retinal haemodynamic responses under varying gravitational and postural conditions, addressing a critical gap in the modelling of SANS. By dynamically coupling IOP with vascular circulation, the model advances beyond previous static frameworks, enabling the prediction of how posture‐induced hydrostatic shifts influence both retinal perfusion and ocular pressure regulation. The results demonstrate that postural changes lead to notable redistributions of pressure across retinal vascular compartments, with arterial segments such as the CRA and arterioles exhibiting the greatest sensitivity. For instance, a 4% constriction of the CRA under HDT conditions leads to a marked increase in PSV, consistent with Doppler ultrasound observations in microgravity analogue experiments (Sirek et al. 2014). Additionally, the model captures a substantial increase in IOP with decreasing tilt angle, reflecting an approximately 81% rise from upright to −30°, in close agreement with experimental measurements by Petersen et al. (2022). These compartmental alterations mirror experimentally observed patterns and reveal plausible mechanisms for mechanical stress accumulation and impaired perfusion under HDT. Furthermore, the model reproduces key features of ocular haemodynamic responses, notably the relative stability of OPP despite pronounced increases in IOP, consistent with physiological compensation reported in studies such as Puchner et al. (2020). This ability to reflect homeostatic regulation supports the validity of the model under altered gravitational stress. In comparing these simulations with prior investigations, the model provides novel insight into the synergistic effect of IOP and systemic pressure changes on retinal haemodynamics. The inclusion of microgravity‐induced geometric adaptations, such as mild arterial constriction, further enhances physiological fidelity and accurately reproduces measured spaceflight responses. Ultimately, this work offers a powerful computational platform to investigate gravitational stress on retinal circulation, improve risk stratification for SANS and guide the development of targeted countermeasures. Its predictive capabilities also hold relevance for terrestrial conditions involving dysregulated ocular perfusion, such as glaucoma and hypertensive retinopathy.
