Synthetic communities and metabolite-driven enrichment of keystone rhizosphere microbial taxa suppress bacterial wilt disease in peanut
Authors: Taobing Yu, Ling Xu, Ruoqi Yang, Xiangyang Fang, Yangkang Huang, Yicong Zhang, Yige Lei, Shang Wang, Ying Jiang, Zhaohai Zeng, Yadong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResistant cultivars play important roles in controlling pathogen invasion in peanut. However, it remains unclear whether and how the metabolites secreted by resistant cultivars and the keystone microbes enriched in their rhizosphere can compensate for the lack of pathogen resistance in susceptible cultivars. In this study, we investigated the roles of metabolites and rhizosphere microbes from resistant peanut cultivars in controlling bacterial wilt disease caused by Ralstonia solanacearum using pot experiments coupled with multi-omics analyses and cultivation assays. R. solanacearum inoculation significantly decreased microbial diversity and network complexity in susceptible cultivars, whereas it increased network complexity in resistant cultivars. Under R. solanacearum inoculation, the rhizosphere of resistant cultivars exhibited significant enrichment of several metabolic pathways related to plant health, including streptomycin, phenylpropanoid, and isoflavonoid biosynthesis. In addition, inoculation induced the recruitment of three putative keystone microbial taxa (Bacillus, Pseudomonas, and Talaromyces), which were significantly correlated with four key metabolites (citrulline, L-phenylalanine, kaempferol, and isopimpinellin) that promoted the growth of these taxa in vitro. Combined application of a synthetic community (SynCom4) and metabolites was more effective than SynCom4 alone in suppressing bacterial wilt in peanut and enriched additional beneficial microbes, including Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Talaromyces, Glutamicibacter, Penicillium, Microbacterium, Curtobacterium, and Stenotrophomonas. These microbes further enhanced peanut resistance against R. solanacearum by activating the host immune system and inducing the synthesis of lignin and antimicrobial compounds (quercetin, dihydroquercetin, and cyanidin). In summary, our work provides a mechanistic understanding of how resistant cultivars modulate their rhizosphere microbiota through metabolite-mediated regulation to combat R. solanacearum invasion in peanut and highlights the importance of interactions between keystone microbes and key metabolites in disease suppression.Key words: peanut, bacterial wilt, Ralstonia solanacearum, rhizosphere microbiota, metabolites, synthetic community

Introduction

Bacterial wilt disease, caused by the soilborne pathogen Ralstonia solanacearum, is one of the greatest threats to global crop production (Mansfield et al., 2012; Mamphogoro et al., 2020). Peanut (Arachis hypogaea) is an important oil crop cultivated worldwide (Zhuang et al., 2019). However, peanut production is severely affected by bacterial wilt disease (Jiang et al., 2017; Qi et al., 2022), which threatens its industrial development. Traditional chemical control strategies may raise sustainability concerns and pose potential environmental risks (Wen et al., 2023), particularly for long-term or large-scale applications. Therefore, host resistance represents the most cost-effective and sustainable strategy to control bacterial wilt disease in peanut (Luo et al., 2020; Qi et al., 2022). Upon pathogen invasion, plant resistance genes facilitate the recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns or damage-associated molecular patterns, thereby triggering pattern recognition receptor-mediated pattern-triggered immunity (Jones and Dangl, 2006; Kong et al., 2021), including the production of defense-related proteins to counter pathogen infection (Ratnaparkhe et al., 1998). Notably, recent studies have shown that rapid activation of plant defenses is associated with the recruitment of beneficial microbes in the rhizosphere (Liu et al., 2021a; Gupta et al., 2022). Consequently, beneficial microbes in the rhizosphere of resistant plants have great potential for the prevention and control of soilborne diseases.

A growing body of evidence indicates that plant-associated microbes substantially influence host plant growth, development, and pathogen resistance (Mendes et al., 2011; Kwak et al., 2018). When threatened by pathogens, resistant plants actively reduce their susceptibility to pathogen invasion through selective recruitment of beneficial rhizosphere microbes (Yin et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023). For instance, the resistant tomato cultivar Hawaii 7996 can recruit Flavobacterium sp. TRM1 to alleviate bacterial wilt disease caused by R. solanacearum (Kwak et al., 2018), and a resistant banana cultivar can recruit Trichoderma and Penicillium to suppress Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense tropical race 4 (Liu et al., 2023). These beneficial microbial taxa can inhibit the growth or spread of pathogens and induce systemic resistance in plants (Pieterse et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2021a). The synergy between resistant crop cultivars and rhizosphere microbes in defending against soilborne diseases has been extensively studied in tomato (Kwak et al., 2018), banana (Liu et al., 2023), and soybean (Mendes et al., 2018), but the responses and underlying mechanisms in peanut cultivars are poorly documented.

In recent years, synthetic communities (SynComs) of microbes have been increasingly used to study the role of microbes in helping plants resist external stresses (Li et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022; Luo et al., 2024). For example, a SynCom composed of Microbacterium sp., Stenotrophomonas sp., and Xanthomonas sp. can help Arabidopsis thaliana control downy mildew (Berendsen et al., 2018). Another study found that a SynCom constructed from three high-abundance bacteria and one low-abundance bacterium substantially suppressed root rot in Astragalus mongholicus (Li et al., 2021). Therefore, SynComs are a viable approach to investigate the functions of the microbiome and to study its interactions with host plants (Durán et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2021). However, the mechanism by which SynComs help susceptible peanut cultivars resist bacterial wilt disease remains poorly understood.

Root exudates are sources of both nutrients and signaling molecules and are thought to play important roles in plant growth, stress resistance, and environmental adaptation (Yuan et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2021). Resistant cultivars can secrete specialized root metabolites that influence the assembly of specific microbial taxa in the rhizosphere (Wen et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2023). Studies have found that fatty acids and amino acids promote the enrichment of beneficial Pseudomonas species (Wen et al., 2021), whereas cucurbitacin B enriches Enterobacter and Bacillus to suppress F. oxysporum in the soil (Zhong et al., 2022). Moreover, the NIR mutant tomato is reported to secrete large amounts of riboflavin and 3-hydroxyflavone to recruit Actinobacteria to inhibit soilborne bacterial wilt disease (Yang et al., 2023). Overall, these metabolites strongly modulate rhizosphere microbial communities and contribute to disease suppression (Yuan et al., 2018; Wen et al., 2021; Hong et al., 2022). However, how the metabolites secreted by resistant cultivars regulate microbial community assembly in the rhizosphere and mediate peanut resistance to bacterial wilt disease remains unclear.

In the present study, we compared two peanut cultivars (resistant and susceptible to bacterial wilt disease) to investigate how the soil microbial community and metabolite profiles respond to bacterial wilt disease (Figure 1). To identify metabolites that help resistant cultivars recruit beneficial microbes and defend against bacterial wilt disease, we performed an untargeted metabolomics analysis. We then verified the interactions between the enriched microbes and the metabolites from resistant cultivars and evaluated their roles in resistance to bacterial wilt disease using strain isolation, plate suppression assays, microbe–metabolite co-culture, microbe–microbe co-culture, and SynCom construction. Specifically, we aimed to (1) identify putative keystone microbes (both bacteria and fungi) and key metabolites in resistant cultivars that contribute to peanut resistance to bacterial wilt disease, (2) determine the effects of key metabolites on putative keystone microbes, (3) evaluate the effects of enriched putative keystone microbes and key metabolites on R. solanacearum, and (4) verify the roles and mechanisms of SynCom4 and key metabolites in resistance to bacterial wilt disease.

Results

Rhizosphere microbiota of resistant cultivars shift to a resistant state upon R. solanacearum invasion

R. solanacearum inoculation had a greater effect on the alpha diversity of rhizosphere microbial taxa in susceptible cultivars than in resistant cultivars. Specifically, the susceptible cultivars inoculated with R. solanacearum (SP) exhibited significantly reduced bacterial Chao1 and Shannon indices and increased fungal Chao1 and Shannon indices. In contrast, R. solanacearum inoculation did not affect bacterial or fungal alpha diversity in resistant cultivars (Supplemental Figure 1A–1D), indicating the stability of the rhizosphere microbiota in these cultivars. Cultivar type and pathogen infection significantly altered bacterial and fungal community structure (permutational analysis of variance [PERMANOVA], p < 0.05; Supplemental Figure 1E and 1F). The microbial network was more complex and robust in resistant cultivars with R. solanacearum inoculation (RP) than in those without inoculation (RCK), whereas the microbial network in susceptible cultivars showed the opposite trend (Supplemental Figure 1G–1J). Specifically, in RP, the number of positive correlations increased from 2066 to 3176, the number of nodes from 307 to 357, and the average degree from 13.456 to 17.793, whereas the average path length decreased from 4.885 to 3.796 (Supplemental Table 1). In contrast, R. solanacearum inoculation in susceptible cultivars decreased the number of positive correlations from 4268 to 3252, the number of nodes from 457 to 371, and the average degree from 18.674 to 14.782, whereas the average path length increased from 3.645 to 4.354 (Supplemental Table 1). Functional prediction based on amplicon sequencing revealed that the metabolic pathways for streptomycin biosynthesis, sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis, and isoflavonoid biosynthesis were significantly enriched only in RP (Supplemental Figure 2A). However, many signaling-related metabolic pathways (e.g., the Wnt and Notch signaling pathways) were significantly inhibited in SP (Supplemental Figure 2B). In summary, the relatively stable microbial communities and significantly enriched metabolic pathways observed in the resistant cultivars may constitute a crucial defense mechanism against bacterial wilt disease.

Identification of keystone taxa that potentially confer resistance to bacterial wilt disease

Given that the microbiota shaped by the resistant cultivars upon pathogen infection may contribute to resistance to bacterial wilt disease in peanut, we further analyzed RP to identify putative keystone taxa capable of conferring resistance. RP exhibited significant enrichment of 336 bacterial amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and 65 fungal ASVs compared with RCK, whereas SP exhibited significant enrichment of 223 bacterial ASVs and 68 fungal ASVs compared with susceptible cultivars without inoculation (SCK) (Supplemental Figure 3A and 3B; Supplemental Tables 2 and 3). RCK exhibited significant enrichment of 186 bacterial ASVs and 70 fungal ASVs compared with SCK, whereas RP exhibited significant enrichment of 312 bacterial ASVs and 77 fungal ASVs compared with SP (Supplemental Figure 4A and 4B; Supplemental Table 4). Finally, Venn diagram analysis showed that 88 bacterial ASVs and 44 fungal ASVs were unique to RP (Supplemental Figure 4C and 4D). In addition, 102 bacterial ASVs and 56 fungal ASVs with specificity and occupancy values greater than or equal to 0.7 were defined as specialist ASVs (Figure 2A and 2B; Supplemental Table 5). In the microbial network of RP, 3 network hubs, 8 module hubs, and 280 connectors were classified as keystone nodes (Figure 2C; Supplemental Table 6). Further analysis identified 64 bacterial and fungal ASVs shared between specialist ASVs and keystone nodes in the network; these were considered to represent putative keystone taxa (Figure 2D). Among these ASVs, 32 were specifically enriched in RP (Supplemental Table 7). Two-way ANOVA performed on these 32 ASVs showed that cultivar type, pathogen infection, and their interaction significantly affected two bacterial ASVs, BASV2 (Bacillus) and BASV334 (Pseudomonas), and two fungal ASVs, FASV6 (Talaromyces) and FASV376 (Talaromyces) (Figure 2D; Supplemental Table 8). In addition, these four ASVs were significantly enriched in RP compared with SP in rhizosphere soil inoculated with R. solanacearum (Figure 2E) and were identified as biomarkers unique to resistant cultivars (Supplemental Figure 5). Therefore, BASV2, BASV334, FASV6, and FASV376 were identified as representing putative keystone taxa that may contribute to disease suppression in resistant cultivars.

Correlation of rhizosphere metabolite levels with the growth of keystone microbes

A total of 381 metabolites were detected across all rhizosphere soil samples. Principal component analysis (PCA) and PERMANOVA showed that cultivar type and pathogen infection significantly affected soil metabolite composition (Figure 3A). RP had 15 significantly enriched metabolites compared with RCK, and SP had 40 significantly enriched metabolites compared with SCK (Figures 3B and 3C). Furthermore, RP had 10 and 4 significantly enriched metabolites compared with RCK and SP, respectively (Supplemental Figure 6; Supplemental Tables 9 and 10). These 14 metabolites may be associated with the recruitment of microbes in resistant cultivars and may contribute to bacterial wilt disease resistance. Therefore, to identify metabolites associated with putative keystone taxa, we further analyzed the relationships between metabolites significantly enriched in RP and 24 ASVs with genus-level taxonomic assignments. Citrulline, dobutamine, isopimpinellin, kaempferol, L-phenylalanine, maltotetraose, and verbascose were significantly associated with several ASVs (Figure 3D). Metabolites with variable importance in projection (VIP) values greater than 2 and that were significantly associated with the putative keystone taxa (BASV2, BASV334, FASV6, and FASV376) were defined as key metabolites enriched in RP; these notably included citrulline, L-phenylalanine, kaempferol, and isopimpinellin (Figure 3D; Supplemental Table 11). We further analyzed the metabolic pathways associated with these four metabolites using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. The metabolites were primarily involved in biosynthesis of amino acids, flavonoid biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and biosynthesis of phenylpropanoids (Supplemental Figure 7A). Furthermore, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that biosynthesis of phenylpropanoids and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathways were significantly enriched in RP compared with RCK (Supplemental Figure 7B). In contrast, pathways related to ABC transporters, galactose metabolism, amino acid synthesis and metabolism, and riboflavin metabolism were significantly enriched in SP compared with SCK (Supplemental Figure 7C). Collectively, these results suggest that in resistant cultivars, changes in key metabolites (such as citrulline, L-phenylalanine, kaempferol, and isopimpinellin) may promote the enrichment of putative keystone taxa and contribute to resistance against bacterial wilt.

Effects of exogenous metabolites on keystone taxa and R. solanacearum and the disease suppression ability of isolates

A total of 82 bacterial and 46 fungal isolates were recovered from rhizosphere soil associated with RP. Based on sequence analysis, the isolates that were most similar to BASV2, BASV334, and FASV6 (FASV376) were B62 (Bacillus_62), B13 (Pseudomonas_13), and F39 (Talaromyces_39), respectively (Supplemental Figure 8; Supplemental Table 12). These three strains were subsequently used in plate assays to evaluate R. solanacearum inhibition. Bacillus_62, Pseudomonas_13, and Talaromyces_39 inhibited R. solanacearum (Figure 4A), indicating that the putative keystone taxa enriched in resistant cultivars may contribute to host defense against pathogen invasion. We then performed 96-well plate co-culture assays to determine the effects of the key metabolites on R. solanacearum and the putative keystone taxa. The four metabolites had no significant effect on R. solanacearum (Figure 4B; Supplemental Table 13), whereas they promoted the growth of the putative keystone taxa to varying degrees. Specifically, L-phenylalanine significantly promoted the growth of Bacillus_62, whereas citrulline and L-phenylalanine significantly promoted the growth of Pseudomonas_13 (Figure 4C and 4D; Supplemental Table 13). Notably, all four metabolites significantly promoted the growth of Talaromyces_39 (Figure 4E). In addition, qPCR results corroborated the growth-promoting effects observed in the co-culture assays: L-phenylalanine significantly increased the abundance of Bacillus_62 (Figure 4F), whereas citrulline and L-phenylalanine significantly increased the abundance of Pseudomonas_13 (Figure 4G). Moreover, all four metabolites (citrulline, L-phenylalanine, kaempferol, and isopimpinellin) significantly increased the abundance of Talaromyces_39 (Figure 4H). In summary, these results demonstrate that the key metabolites enriched in resistant cultivars promote the growth of putative keystone microbial taxa.

Effects of SynComs and exogenous metabolites on peanut resistance to bacterial wilt disease

In vitro bacterial–bacterial and fungal–bacterial interaction assays showed that individual strains, two-member SynComs, and three-member SynComs significantly inhibited the growth of R. solanacearum (Supplemental Figure 9). Among them, SynCom4 (Bacillus_62, Pseudomonas_13, and Talaromyces_39) performed the best, significantly decreasing the abundance of R. solanacearum by 92.1% compared with the control. Therefore, a pot experiment was conducted to investigate the effects of SynCom4 and four exogenous metabolites (citrulline, L-phenylalanine, kaempferol, and isopimpinellin) on disease resistance in peanut plants. The results indicated that R. solanacearum and SynCom4 (RS) and R. solanacearum SynCom4, and metabolites (RSM) significantly enhanced disease resistance in peanut plants throughout the growth period compared with R. solanacearum alone (R). Plant height and biomass in RS and RSM, especially RSM, were significantly higher than those in R (Figures 5A–5C). Similarly, RS and RSM exhibited significantly lower abundance of R. solanacearum and higher abundance of Bacillus_62, Pseudomonas_13, and Talaromyces_39 compared with R (Figure 5D). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and PERMANOVA of metatranscriptomic sequencing data revealed significant differences in microbial composition among treatments (Supplemental Figure 10A; Supplemental Table 14). A total of 632 (114 upregulated and 518 downregulated) and 1055 (609 upregulated and 446 downregulated) significantly different genera were identified in the RS vs. R and RSM vs. R comparisons, respectively (Supplemental Figure 10C). Among these, 56 and 550 significantly increased genera were unique to the RS vs. R and RSM vs. R comparisons, respectively, whereas 59 significantly increased genera were shared by both groups. Further analysis of these genera revealed that both RS and RSM (especially RSM) significantly increased the abundance of Glutamicibacter and Penicillium, and that RSM also significantly increased the abundance of Microbacterium, Curtobacterium, and Stenotrophomonas (Figure 5D). Correlation analysis showed that the three SynCom4 isolates and four significantly enriched genera were significantly negatively correlated with R. solanacearum (Supplemental Figure 11).

We next assessed the functional properties of the rhizosphere microbial community triggered by SynCom4 and the key metabolites. PCoA and PERMANOVA revealed significant differences in functional composition among treatments (Supplemental Figure 10B; Supplemental Table 15). A total of 36 688 (25 679 upregulated and 11 099 downregulated) and 91 516 (57 314 upregulated and 34 175 downregulated) differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in the RS vs. R and RSM vs. R comparisons, respectively (Supplemental Figure 12). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that DEGs from both comparisons were enriched for genes in the MAPK signaling pathway, the Rap1 signaling pathway, plant-pathogen interaction, the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, and the streptomycin biosynthesis pathway (Figure 5E). Notably, the number of DEGs involved in these pathways was greater in the RSM vs. R comparison than in the RS vs. R comparison (Figure 5E).

We also performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to evaluate the transcriptional responses of peanut plants to SynCom4 alone or in combination with the metabolites. PCA and PERMANOVA showed that the transcriptome profiles differed significantly among treatments (Supplemental Figure 13A; Supplemental Table 16). A total of 8909 (2592 upregulated and 6312 downregulated) and 3463 (1241 upregulated and 2222 downregulated) DEGs were identified in the RS vs. R and RSM vs. R comparisons, respectively. Among these, 750 DEGs were shared by both comparisons, representing 28.9% and 60.4% of the total DEGs, respectively (Supplemental Figure 13B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed enrichment of 41 and 35 pathways among the significantly upregulated DEGs in the RS and RSM comparisons, respectively, with 21 pathways shared. Among the top 10 pathways, we focused on tropane, piperidine, and pyridine alkaloid biosynthesis; flavonoid biosynthesis; phenylpropanoid biosynthesis; and isoflavonoid biosynthesis (Figure 6A). In these four pathways, genes related to phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL), 4-coumarate:CoA ligase (4CL), cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (CCR), cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD), hydroxycinnamoyl transferase (HCT), ferulic acid 5-hydroxylase (F5H), chalcone synthase (CHS), flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H), flavonol synthase (FLS), vestitone reductase (VR), isoflavone O-methyltransferase (IOMT), and anthocyanidin synthase (ANS) were significantly upregulated in both comparisons (Figure 6B; Supplemental Table 17). Furthermore, the expression levels of most of these genes were positively correlated with the abundance of genera enriched in the RS and RSM treatments (Supplemental Table 18). This suggests that the enriched rhizosphere microbiota may actively regulate the expression of key host defense-related biosynthetic genes.

We validated the expression patterns of these genes using RT-qPCR, and the results were consistent with those obtained from RNA-seq (Supplemental Figures 14A and 15). Notably, these genes are involved in the synthesis of flavonols (e.g., quercetin, dihydrokaempferol, and dihydroquercetin), isoflavones (e.g., prunetin, isoformononetin, and medicarpin), anthocyanidins (e.g., cyanidin, pelargonidin, and delphinidin), and lignin (Figure 6B). Therefore, we performed plate assays to further evaluate the effects of six compounds (quercetin, dihydrokaempferol, dihydroquercetin, prunetin, isoformononetin, and cyanidin) on the growth of R. solanacearum, and found that quercetin, dihydroquercetin, and cyanidin significantly inhibited the growth of R. solanacearum (Figure 6C). In addition, given the marked upregulation of lignin biosynthesis genes in RS and RSM, we determined the lignin content in roots. The results showed that the lignin content of peanut roots in RS and RSM was 29.6% and 50.4% higher, respectively, than that in R (Figure 6D), and that lignin content was significantly negatively correlated with the abundance of R. solanacearum (Supplemental Figure 14B). Collectively, these results suggest that both SynCom4 alone and the combination of SynCom4 with the metabolites can activate plant immune responses to resist R. solanacearum invasion.

Although the key metabolites associated with resistant cultivars showed no direct inhibition of R. solanacearum, we conducted a separate pot experiment to isolate their potential contribution to wilt resistance in RSM, given their established roles in promoting plant growth. Amplicon sequencing revealed a significantly higher relative abundance of bacterial genera, such as Steroidobacter, Pontibacter, and Flavobacterium, in the group inoculated with metabolites (M) compared with the group inoculated with sterile water (CK). In contrast, the group inoculated with R. solanacearum and metabolites (RM) showed enrichment of genera such as Planococcus, Rheinheimera, and Steroidobacter compared with R (Supplemental Figure 16A). For fungi, the relative abundance of Metarhizium and Monosporascus was significantly higher in M than in CK, whereas only Botryotrichum showed a significant increase in RM compared with R (Supplemental Figure 16B). In addition, RNA-seq analysis revealed that metabolite addition had no significant effect on gene expression in peanut roots, regardless of R. solanacearum inoculation (Supplemental Figure 17A). Volcano plot analysis showed that 46 DEGs (18 upregulated and 28 downregulated) were identified in the M vs. CK comparison, whereas 172 DEGs (68 upregulated and 104 downregulated) were identified in the RM vs. R comparison (Supplemental Figure 17B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the significantly upregulated DEGs showed that no pathway was significantly enriched in the M vs. CK comparison, whereas phenylpropanoid, diterpenoid, and isoflavonoid biosynthesis were significantly enriched in the RM vs. R comparison (Supplemental Figure 17C). Notably, the number of DEGs mapped to each of these pathways ranged from two to five genes (Supplemental Figure 17C). Furthermore, metabolite addition had no significant effect on plant height, biomass, or root lignin content, regardless of R. solanacearum inoculation (Supplemental Figure 18). Overall, these findings demonstrate that the metabolites alone do not alter peanut growth or enrich beneficial taxa and support the conclusion that the metabolites primarily function as resources or signals for SynCom4 (Bacillus_62, Pseudomonas_13, and Talaromyces_39), thereby synergistically enhancing the efficacy of SynCom4 in strengthening host defense against pathogen invasion.

Discussion

Growing evidence indicates that plants actively shape specific microbial communities to influence their phenotype, enhance growth, and even prevent disease (Trivedi et al., 2020; Ku et al., 2024). Rhizosphere microbiota play important roles in plant stress resistance and pathogen inhibition and are the primary drivers of plant defense responses (Zhalnina et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2021a). In this study, we investigated the extent to which resistant plant cultivars achieve disease resistance by secreting key metabolites that enrich keystone microbial taxa to suppress pathogens. We found that the network complexity associated with a resistant cultivar increased following R. solanacearum inoculation, whereas that associated with a susceptible cultivar decreased. Complex microbial interactions can regulate community stability, thereby reducing pathogen propagation (Downing et al., 2014; Tardy et al., 2014). Increased community complexity may reduce the success of pathogen invasion by fostering more efficient competition for resources and niche occupancy (van Elsas et al., 2012; Wei et al., 2015). Furthermore, the lower modularity and shorter average path length observed in resistant cultivars indicate that their rhizosphere microbial communities respond more rapidly to environmental perturbations compared with susceptible cultivars (Faust and Raes, 2012). Overall, these results suggest that the rhizosphere microbial community of resistant cultivars exhibits greater resistance to pathogen invasion than that of susceptible cultivars (Li et al., 2021; Yin et al., 2022).

The rhizosphere metabolite composition differed substantially between resistant and susceptible peanut cultivars, consistent with previous studies on rhizosphere metabolites across different rice and banana accessions (Li et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023). Rhizosphere metabolite composition has been shown to be closely related to the composition and function of rhizosphere microbial communities (Sasse et al., 2018; Upadhyay et al., 2022). We found that peanut disease resistance was associated with the differential secretion of key rhizosphere metabolites that enrich Bacillus, Pseudomonas, and Talaromyces, which are capable of inhibiting R. solanacearum. Populations of Bacillus (Xia et al., 2024), Pseudomonas (Ping et al., 2024), and Talaromyces (Li et al., 2025) have regularly been shown to contribute to the suppression of plant pathogens. In our study, the abundance of key metabolites differed between resistant and susceptible cultivars and correlated significantly with keystone microbial taxa, which further confirmed that differences in rhizosphere metabolite abundance may represent a mechanism for enriching functionally distinct microorganisms among plant cultivars (Liu et al., 2023). In addition, the resistant cultivars showed significant enrichment of genes associated with the flavonoid and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathways, which are involved in defense against pathogen invasion in many plants (Xie et al., 2018; Ha et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022a). Accordingly, we established that key metabolites secreted by resistant cultivars have a direct role in the assembly of disease-suppressive rhizosphere microbial communities and revealed the mechanism by which the combination of SynCom4 with key metabolites enhances the resistance of susceptible cultivars.

The rhizosphere of disease-resistant peanut cultivars is specifically enriched in Bacillus, Pseudomonas, and Talaromyces. Based on previous potting and co-culture experiments, we hypothesize that metabolites secreted by disease-resistant peanut cultivars could prevent R. solanacearum from colonizing the rhizosphere by promoting the proliferation of biocontrol bacteria and fungi, thereby improving plant health (Liu et al., 2021a, 2023; Yang et al., 2023). The lower abundance of R. solanacearum and higher abundance of Bacillus_62, Pseudomonas_13, and Talaromyces_39 in the rhizosphere microbial community under the combined application of metabolites and SynCom4 (RSM) further supports our hypothesis (Figure 5D). RSM also recruited further microbial taxa, including Glutamicibacter, Penicillium, Microbacterium, Curtobacterium, and Stenotrophomonas. These taxa play roles in protecting host plants from infection by Erwinia tracheiphila, F. oxysporum, Fusarium verticillioides, Verticillium dahliae, Hyaloperonospora arabidopsidis, and R. solanacearum (Larena et al., 2003; Berendsen et al., 2018; Fu et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021, 2022; Goossens et al., 2023) through various mechanisms, such as production of antimicrobial compounds, competition for nutrients and ecological niches, biofilm formation, and activation of plant defense responses (Poudel et al., 2016; Feng et al., 2024; Qiao et al., 2024).

Our results also revealed significant changes in the functional composition of the rhizosphere microbial community following SynCom4 inoculation, particularly increases in the expression of genes associated with MAPK signaling, plant–pathogen interaction, and streptomycin biosynthesis. The functional activity of these three pathways was further amplified when metabolites were also applied, as indicated by a greater number of genes mapped to these pathways. Notably, these pathways play important roles in defense against pathogens in many plant species (Zhang et al., 2021; Liao et al., 2023). The MAPK pathway regulates both pattern-triggered immunity and effector-triggered immunity by mediating the phosphorylation of downstream targets (Lanubile et al., 2017). Streptomycin is a commonly used aminoglycoside antibiotic that targets the protein translation machinery, thereby inhibiting microbial growth (Sharma et al., 2020), and may thus substantially suppress soilborne pathogens. Collectively, our results highlight the crucial role of the enrichment of beneficial microbes and functional pathways in enhancing peanut disease resistance.

SynCom4 inoculation significantly upregulated the expression of genes involved in the lignin, isoflavonoid, and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways, with a more pronounced effect in RSM. This response may be related to the colonization of beneficial root-associated taxa, such as Pseudomonas, Bacillus, Penicillium, and Stenotrophomonas, which can activate the host immune response, thereby improving plant disease resistance (Poudel et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2021a; Zhou et al., 2022). The upregulation of lignin biosynthesis genes significantly increased lignin content in peanut roots, with a greater effect observed in RSM. Previous studies have shown that increased lignin accumulation is associated with enhanced resistance of plants to vascular pathogens (Novo et al., 2017). Lignin accumulation results in the lignification of plant cell walls, which impedes the movement of R. solanacearum within the root xylem and reduces its virulence, thereby helping plants resist pathogen invasion (Wang et al., 2025). The enrichment of rhizosphere microbes can also induce the host plant to produce metabolites such as phenylpropanoids, flavonoids, and isoflavonoids, which contribute to pathogen resistance (Zaynab et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2022b). Our results showed that SynCom4 and its combination with key metabolites significantly upregulated the expression of genes involved in the synthesis of quercetin, dihydroquercetin, and cyanidin, and that these three compounds inhibited the growth of R. solanacearum. Importantly, our findings demonstrated that rhizosphere metabolites from the resistant cultivars did not directly enhance resistance or growth or enrich beneficial taxa in the susceptible cultivars. This suggests that the metabolites do not confer resistance by directly affecting plants but rather by promoting the proliferation and activity of SynCom4. Together, these findings highlight the important role of beneficial microorganisms enriched by resistant cultivars through rhizosphere metabolites in suppressing R. solanacearum-induced disease. These microorganisms act through multiple mechanisms, including direct pathogen antagonism, activation of host immune responses, and induction of lignin, quercetin, dihydroquercetin, and cyanidin synthesis in the host (Figure 7).

Schematic diagram of peanut resistance to bacterial wilt.

Disease-suppressive microbiota enriched by resistant cultivars through rhizosphere metabolites help susceptible cultivars resist R. solanacearum invasion. The combined application of keystone taxa and key metabolites recruits additional beneficial taxa, reduces pathogen colonization in the rhizosphere, and activates the host immune system to protect peanut plants from pathogen invasion.

In conclusion, this study found that R. solanacearum inoculation differentially shaped the dynamics of rhizosphere microbial communities and metabolites in peanut cultivars with different levels of resistance to bacterial wilt disease. Disease resistance in resistant cultivars may be attributed to the higher robustness and network complexity of rhizosphere microbial communities and to the enrichment of specific beneficial taxa (i.e., Bacillus, Pseudomonas, and Talaromyces) by key metabolites (i.e., citrulline, L-phenylalanine, kaempferol, and isopimpinellin). SynCom4 alone or in combination with the metabolites enhanced the resistance of susceptible peanut cultivars to R. solanacearum, with the metabolites further increasing resistance compared with SynCom4 alone. This effect was supported by multiple molecular mechanisms, including plant immune activation by beneficial rhizosphere microbiota and interactions among them. Overall, these findings improve our understanding of how beneficial microbial taxa enriched by resistant cultivars through secretions produced in response to R. solanacearum can serve as core microbiota in susceptible cultivars, thereby laying the foundation for preventing and controlling R. solanacearum-induced disease.

Methods

Soil and plant materials

Experimental soils were collected from the 0–20 cm soil layer in a field with no history of peanut cultivation in Jiangxi Province, China. The soil was classified as laterite, and its basic properties were: pH 5.4, soil organic carbon 6.4 g kg−1, total nitrogen 1.5 g kg−1, and available phosphorus 1.58 mg kg−1. The soil was passed through a 2-mm sieve to remove stones and plant roots before use. Two peanut (A. hypogaea L.) cultivars that were resistant (Rihua 3) or susceptible (Rihua 99) to bacterial wilt disease were selected for this study. R. solanacearum GMI1000 was selected as the pathogen. Peanut seeds and R. solanacearum were provided by the Shandong Academy of Agricultural Sciences and the Institute of Agricultural Resources and Regional Planning of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, respectively.

Pot experiment 1: Experimental design and soil sampling

A pot experiment was conducted in a randomized block design with four replicates per treatment in a greenhouse at China Agricultural University (39°99′N, 116°34′E) in Beijing, China. A flowchart of the pot experiment and the sampling strategy is shown in Figure 1A. The experiment included four treatments: (1) RCK, (2) RP, (3) SCK, and (4) SP. Peanut seeds were soaked in 50 ml of sodium hypochlorite solution (NaClO, 0.75%, v/v) for 10 min, washed three times with sterile water, and then pre-germinated in a nutrient soil substrate at 25°C for 10 days. Healthy seedlings at the three-leaf stage were transplanted into pots, with one seedling per pot.

One week after transplanting, 40 ml of an R. solanacearum suspension diluted in ddH2O to an optical density (OD600) of 1.0 (∼108 CFU ml−1) was poured around the base of each peanut plant. The same volume of sterile water was added to pots without R. solanacearum inoculation. Peanuts were grown under controlled conditions with a 14-h light/10-h dark photoperiod and a diurnal temperature regime of 30°C (day) and 20°C (night). The disease status of the peanuts was monitored and recorded beginning 5 days after R. solanacearum inoculation. Rhizosphere soil samples were collected 40 days after inoculation by gently shaking the roots to dislodge adhering soil particles, which were immediately transferred to 50-ml sterile centrifuge tubes containing phosphate-buffered saline (0.1 M, pH 7.4). Samples were vortexed for 5 min at medium intensity (2000 rpm), followed by centrifugation at 10 000 g for 5 min at 4°C. The resulting soil samples were stored at −80°C prior to soil DNA extraction, metabolite measurement, and microbial isolation. Detailed information on soil DNA extraction, amplicon sequencing, bioinformatic analysis, and soil metabolite measurement is provided in the supplemental information.

Identification of putative keystone taxa

We used four different approaches to identify putative keystone taxa: (1) volcano plot analysis to identify ASVs significantly enriched in RP, (2) identification of RP network keystone nodes based on within-module connectivity (Zi) and among-module connectivity (Pi) values, (3) identification of specialist ASVs with specificity and occupancy greater than or equal to 0.7 in RP, and (4) identification of differential biomarkers in RP. In brief, we calculated the Zi and Pi values for each node in the RP network. Nodes with Zi ≥ 2.5 and Pi < 0.62 were defined as module hubs, those with Zi < 2.5 and Pi ≥ 0.62 were defined as connectors, and those with Zi ≥ 2.5 and Pi ≥ 0.62 were defined as network hubs; these were collectively referred to as keystone nodes (Deng et al., 2012). All other nodes were categorized as peripherals. Furthermore, the 3000 ASVs with the highest relative abundance were selected from each treatment, and specificity and occupancy were calculated as described by Dufrene and Legendre (1997). ASVs with specificity and occupancy greater than or equal to 0.7 were considered to represent putative keystone taxa (Gweon et al., 2020). Differentially abundant ASVs were identified in R v.4.2.2 using the DESeq function from the “DESeq2” package (Love et al., 2014) (n = 4; false discovery rate <5% under the Benjamini–Hochberg correction). Volcano plots were generated using the “ggpubr” package (Kassambara, 2023), and LEfSe analysis for biomarker identification was performed using the “microeco” package (Liu et al., 2021b).

Soil microbe isolation and identification

To investigate the potential mechanisms by which microorganisms recruited by resistant peanut cultivars protect against bacterial wilt disease, we isolated microbial strains from the rhizosphere soil of resistant peanut plants. A flowchart of rhizosphere microbial isolation is shown in Figure 1B. In brief, 2.0 g of rhizosphere soil was placed in a centrifuge tube containing 10 ml of Luria-Bertani (LB) medium (for bacteria) or Tiger Red medium (for fungi) and shaken for 30 min at 25°C and 170 rpm to generate a soil suspension. Subsequently, 200 μl of LB medium or Tiger Red medium and 2 μl of soil suspension were added to 96-well plates and incubated for 2 days at 37°C for bacteria and 5 days at 28°C for fungi. Microbes from each well were transferred to plates for purification. A total of 500 bacterial colonies and 350 fungal colonies were picked from the plates and subjected to two rounds of purification. For taxonomic identification of each isolate, genomic DNA was extracted from purified cultures, followed by PCR amplification and sequencing of target gene regions (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered and demultiplexed, and sequences were taxonomically classified using 16S rRNA and ITS reference sequences from NCBI. Then, phylogenetic trees were generated in MEGA11 using sequences from recovered bacterial and fungal isolates together with key ASVs. Finally, Bacillus_62 (B62; bacterial isolate no. 62), Pseudomonas_13 (B13; bacterial isolate no. 13), and Talaromyces_39 (F39; fungal isolate no. 39), which showed the highest similarity to bacterial ASV2 and ASV334 and fungal ASV6 (ASV376), respectively, were selected for subsequent experiments.

Effects of isolated strains on R. solanacearum growth

We selected the strains most similar to bacterial ASV2 and ASV334 and fungal ASV6 (ASV376), including two bacterial strains and one fungal strain, for pathogen suppression experiments. First, the bacterial strains (Bacillus_62 and Pseudomonas_13) and R. solanacearum were inoculated onto LB agar medium and incubated at 37°C for 2 days; the fungal strain (Talaromyces_39) was inoculated onto potato dextrose agar medium and incubated at 28°C for 5 days. Blocks containing R. solanacearum (5 mm in diameter) were placed in the center of LB agar plates, then blocks of the same size containing bacterial isolates were placed 2 cm from the R. solanacearum blocks, and the plates were incubated at 37°C for 6 days. Considering the slower growth rate of fungi, we first inoculated the fungal isolates onto LB agar medium and incubated them at 28°C for 1 day and then inoculated R. solanacearum onto the center of each plate. Plates inoculated with R. solanacearum alone were used as a negative control. All suppression experiments were performed with eight replicates.

Previous studies have demonstrated that SynComs are more effective than single strains in suppressing disease (Compant et al., 2019; Luo et al., 2024). Therefore, we constructed four SynComs: SynCom1 (Pseudomonas_13 and Bacillus_62), SynCom2 (Pseudomonas_13 and Talaromyces_39), SynCom3 (Bacillus_62 and Talaromyces_39), and SynCom4 (Pseudomonas_13, Bacillus_62, and Talaromyces_39). In brief, bacterial strains were cultured in LB broth at 37°C with shaking until the OD600 reached 0.7 (∼48 h), harvested by centrifugation (4000 g, 5 min), and resuspended in sterile distilled water, adjusted to an OD600 of 0.5. Fungal strains were cultured in 1/10-strength potato dextrose broth at 28°C with shaking for 4 days, and the conidial concentration was adjusted to 1 × 106 conidia ml−1 using a hemocytometer. Bacterial and fungal strains were mixed in equal volumes to establish the SynComs (Zhou et al., 2022). In this study, we used in vitro bacterial–bacterial and fungal–bacterial interaction assays to determine the effects of individual strains and SynComs on the growth of R. solanacearum (Durán et al., 2018; Luo et al., 2024). R. solanacearum was aliquoted into 96-well plates containing tryptic soy broth medium with or without an individual strain or SynCom. The plates were incubated at 25°C for 48 h, and the growth of R. solanacearum was assessed using qPCR. Detailed information on the qPCR experiment is provided in the supplemental information.

Effects of metabolites on the growth of R. solanacearum and isolated strains

The key metabolites significantly enriched in RP were tested for their effects on the growth of R. solanacearum and isolated strains according to the method described by Wen et al. (2023). R. solanacearum and isolated strains were cultured in nutrient broth and LB medium, respectively. The inoculated media were shaken at 180 rpm and 28°C for 24 h, and then cell density was adjusted to an OD600 of 0.05 with sterile medium. The bacterial suspension (16 μl) was then added to each well of a sterile 96-well plate containing 200 μl inorganic salt medium: (NH4)2SO4 2.0 g l−1, MgSO4·7H2O 0.2 g l−1, CaCl2·2H2O 0.01 g l−1, FeSO4·7H2O 0.001 g l−1, Na2HPO4·12H2O 1.5 g l−1, and KH2PO4 1.5 g l−1. The four metabolites (8 μl each, final concentration of 10 mM) were added to the appropriate wells as carbon sources for the bacterial cultures. The same volume of bacterial suspension mixed with an equal volume of sterile water served as a control. The plates were incubated at 28°C for 24 h, and the OD600 was measured every 2 h using a Multiskan SkyHigh Microplate Spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, Waltham, MA, USA). For fungi, the effects of key metabolites on the growth of isolates were tested according to the method described by Liu et al. (2023). The fungal suspension (16 μl, final concentration of 1 × 104 spores ml–1) was added to each well of a sterile 96-well plate containing 200 μl potato dextrose broth medium. The four metabolites (8 μl each, final concentration of 5 mM) were added to the appropriate wells as carbon sources for the fungal cultures. The same volume of fungal suspension mixed with an equal volume of sterile water served as a control. The plates were incubated at 25°C for 24 h, and fungal growth was assessed by measuring OD750 using a Multiskan SkyHigh Microplate Spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, Waltham, MA, USA). Furthermore, the abundance of Bacillus_62, Pseudomonas_13, and Talaromyces_39 in metabolite–microbe co-culture samples was quantified by qPCR using the primers listed in Supplemental Table 19. Detailed information on the qPCR experiment is provided in the supplemental information.

Pot experiment 2: Experimental design and soil sampling

Because SynCom4 demonstrated superior efficacy in suppressing R. solanacearum growth compared with other SynComs and individual strains, its effect on peanut health was further investigated. Based on the results of the microbe–metabolite co-culture experiments, the final metabolite concentrations used in the pot experiment were 10 mM for citrulline and L-phenylalanine and 5 mM for kaempferol and isopimpinellin. Topsoil (0–20 cm) with no prior history of peanut cultivation was collected from Jiangxi Province, China, for the pot experiment. The soil was homogenized by passing it through a 2-mm sieve to remove coarse debris (stones and root fragments). In total, 2.5 kg of soil was placed in plastic pots 17 cm in diameter and 16 cm in height. Seeds from susceptible peanut cultivars were surface-sterilized with NaClO for 10 min, washed three times with sterile water, and then planted in substrate. After incubation for 10 days, the seedlings were transplanted into the plastic pots, with one peanut plant per pot. To investigate the effects of SynCom4 alone or in combination with the metabolites on peanut resistance to bacterial wilt, a pot experiment was conducted with four treatments: R, RS, RSM, and CK. Each treatment included four replicates (Figure 1C).

One week after peanut plant transplantation, 15 ml of SynCom4 was applied to the soil either alone or in combination with 20 ml of metabolites (5 ml of each) weekly for 4 weeks. Peanut plants treated with sterile water served as the negative control. The pathogen (R. solanacearum, ∼108 CFU ml−1) was inoculated into the soil after the first weekly treatment (Wen et al., 2023). Pots were maintained in a controlled greenhouse environment with a diurnal temperature regime of 30°C (day) and 20°C (night). Disease incidence, plant height, and plant biomass were recorded after 40 days. Subsequently, rhizosphere soil and peanut root samples were collected. Rhizosphere soil was used for metatranscriptome sequencing, whereas root samples were used for transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) and lignin content determination. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until analysis. Detailed information on transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq), metatranscriptome sequencing, bioinformatic analysis, reverse transcription qPCR, qPCR experiments, and lignin determination is provided in the supplemental information.

Effects of compounds synthesized by peanut roots on R. solanacearum growth

Based on the transcriptomics results, we purchased six compounds for which biosynthetic genes were significantly expressed in RS and RSM to investigate their effects on R. solanacearum. In brief, LB agar plates containing each compound (quercetin, dihydroquercetin, cyanidin, dihydrokaempferol, prunetin, and isoformononetin) at 10 mM were prepared, and plates containing sterile water were used as a control. Agar disks containing fresh R. solanacearum cultures were placed at the center of each plate (eight replicates per treatment), and the plates were incubated at 28°C for 72 h (Xia et al., 2024).

Pot experiment 3: Experimental design and soil sampling

To investigate the effects of metabolite application on peanut resistance to bacterial wilt, we conducted a separate pot experiment. Four treatments were used: R, M, RM, and CK. Each treatment was replicated four times (Figure 1D). The soil, seeds, inoculation method, and pot management were the same as those used in pot experiment 2. Rhizosphere soil and root samples were collected 40 days after pathogen inoculation. The soil samples were used for microbial community analysis via amplicon sequencing, whereas the root samples were used for transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) and lignin content determination. Detailed information on amplicon sequencing, transcriptome sequencing, bioinformatic analysis, and lignin determination is provided in the supplemental information.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Dunn’s multiple comparison test were used to identify significant differences in bacterial and fungal alpha diversity, relative abundance of putative keystone taxa, plant height, plant biomass, lignin content, and DEG levels among treatments. Two-way ANOVA was used to evaluate the effects of cultivar, pathogen infection, and their interaction on putative keystone taxa. Unpaired t-tests were performed to assess differences between two groups, and p values were adjusted using the false discovery rate correction. Boxplots and bar charts were generated using the OmicStudio platform. Partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), PCoA, and PCA were performed using the functions in the “ropls,” “vegan,” and “stats” packages, respectively (Ai et al., 2021; Oksanen et al., 2022). The effects of different factors on community structure, metabolite composition, transcriptome profiles, and metatranscriptomic gene composition were quantified by PERMANOVA using the “vegan” package. The DESeq function in the “DESeq2” package (Love et al., 2014) was used to test for differentially abundant metabolites, genera, and genes (n = 4; false discovery rate <5% under the Benjamini–Hochberg correction). Microbial co-occurrence networks were constructed using the bacterial and fungal ASVs with an average relative abundance greater than 0.05% (Yu et al., 2024). In these networks, the selected bacterial ASVs accounted for 56.0%–62.6% of the total abundance, whereas the selected fungal ASVs accounted for 89.8%–94.8% of the total abundance. To construct a robust co-occurrence network, only correlations meeting the thresholds of |r| > 0.8 and p < 0.05 were retained, followed by visualization using Gephi v.0.9.5 (Yu et al., 2024). Venn diagrams were generated using the “ggvenn” package to show the numbers of unique and shared ASVs, metabolites, biomarkers, differentially abundant genera, and DEGs among treatments. Metabolic features that differentiated the two treatments were identified as those with a VIP value greater than 1.0 and p < 0.05. Scatter plots, heat maps, and pathway enrichment analyses were produced using the “ggplot2,” “stats,” and “clusterProfiler” packages, respectively (Xu et al., 2024). The statistical analyses were performed in R v.4.2.2.
