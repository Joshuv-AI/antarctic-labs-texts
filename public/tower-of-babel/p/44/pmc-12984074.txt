Traditional Chinese Medicine and Ferroptosis in Alzheimer’s Disease: A Potential Therapeutic Approach
Authors: Annan Liu, Liping Xing, Jianhui Li, Mingyuan Yao, Jing Song, Wang Guo, Peihan Duan, Honglin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) represents a prevalent neurodegenerative disorder associated with considerable morbidity and mortality. Currently, no therapeutic agents exist that can achieve a fundamental reversal or complete cure for this condition. Consequently, the identification of novel molecular targets and the development of innovative treatment modalities aimed at slowing progression and alleviating symptoms represent pressing priorities within AD clinical research. Ferroptosis, a regulated cell death process driven by intracellular iron dysregulation and excessive lipid peroxidation, is now recognized as a critical contributor to AD pathogenesis. Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has demonstrated beneficial outcomes in managing AD, and emerging evidence suggests its regulatory effects may extend to modulating ferroptotic pathways. This review summarizes and analyzes the therapeutic efficacy of various TCM strategies against AD, including herbal extracts, monomers (eg, alkaloids, terpenoids, glycosides, phenolic derivatives, quinones), compound formulas, and acupuncture. It highlights how these interventions target key ferroptosis-related axes—such as iron homeostasis, the system Xc−/GSH/GPX4 antioxidant system, and the Keap1/Nrf2/ARE pathway—to collectively address the pathological foundation of the disease. However, current evidence is predominantly preclinical, and the translational potential of TCM is constrained by challenges including blood-brain barrier penetration, pharmacokinetic profiles, standardization, and safety assessments. In conclusion, TCM exhibits substantial potential for both research and clinical application in AD by targeting and attenuating the ferroptosis pathway, offering promising avenues for disease modification and symptomatic relief.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, ferroptosis, traditional Chinese medicine, cell death, Chinese herbal compounds

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the predominant form of dementia in the elderly, progressively impairs cognitive function.1 Its clinical hallmarks encompass memory loss, language deficits, impaired judgment, and eventual loss of self-care capacity. Globally, AD affects over 50 million individuals, with an age-standardized prevalence of 5–7% in populations over 60, rendering it a leading cause of mortality and economic burden.2 China harbors the world’s largest AD patient population, estimated at 9.83 million among those aged over 60.3 The disease’s etiology, while not fully elucidated, involves genetic factors, abnormal protein aggregation, neurotransmitter dysfunction, and neuronal damage.4–6 Currently, the pharmacological management of Alzheimer’s disease in China primarily involves agents approved for cognitive enhancement, namely cholinesterase inhibitors—including donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine—alongside memantine, an N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonist. Standard treatment protocols often employ a combination of these medications to address core cognitive deficits.7 Concurrently, behavioral and psychological symptoms associated with the disease are frequently managed with adjunctive pharmacotherapy, such as atypical antipsychotics or selective serotonin receptor agonists.8 The integration of artificial intelligence and data mining, particularly graph-based complex network analysis, is advancing clinical research methodologies.9 However, existing treatments largely fail to alter disease progression and are often limited by modest efficacy or adverse effects.10,11 Moreover, a significant challenge in developing effective central nervous system (CNS) therapeutics, including for AD, is the blood-brain barrier (BBB), which restricts the passage of many potentially neuroprotective compounds. Therefore, continued research into AD pathogenesis and novel therapeutic avenues for disease modification remains imperative.

Ferroptosis represents a unique mode of regulated cell death, characterized by its dependence on iron and the accumulation of lipid peroxides. This process plays a significant regulatory role in various fundamental biological contexts, such as ontogeny, senescence, immune response modulation, and oncogenesis.12,13 Research indicates that neuronal demise in AD is associated with conditions of oxidative stress and excessive iron accumulation.14,15 Iron, an indispensable trace element, participates in a wide range of physiological activities, encompassing the synthesis of red blood cells, metabolic energy production, muscular operation, and the regulation of cellular division cycles.16 Empirical observations have confirmed increased iron concentrations within the gray matter of AD patients.17 Concurrent dysregulation of iron homeostasis and elevated lipid peroxidation—both established hallmarks of ferroptosis—have been further linked to the pathogenesis of AD.18 Consequently, interventional approaches aimed at modulating ferroptosis to avert or reduce tissue injury have emerged as a notable focus of scientific inquiry. From a drug development perspective, ferroptosis presents an “actionable” target due to its reliance on specific metabolic pathways that can be pharmacologically modulated.19 However, translating such interventions into clinically viable therapies faces notable translational constraints, including the need for compounds with adequate BBB penetration, favorable pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) profiles, and standardized, safe formulations—challenges that are particularly relevant when considering multi-component herbal preparations.20 Targeting ferroptosis represents a promising shift toward developing disease-modifying therapies, moving beyond the symptomatic relief offered by current cholinergic or glutamatergic strategies, with the potential to slow progression and preserve cognitive function.21 It is important to distinguish between ferroptosis-specific markers and general oxidative stress or inflammation markers to avoid overinterpretation of mechanistic data. Clinically, alterations in metabolic and redox pathways relevant to ferroptosis, such as reduced glutathione levels and dysregulated cysteine metabolism, have been observed in the brains and plasma of AD patients, providing a human context that anchors the preclinical mechanistic narrative to disease-relevant biology.22

Derived from natural sources with established therapeutic profiles, Chinese herbal compounds have been globally utilized for centuries to promote human health. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) operates through a paradigm of multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway interventions, which may confer unique advantages in the prevention and management of AD.23 The multi-constituent nature of TCM formulations may also offer innovative strategies to enhance bioavailability and circumvent the BBB challenge.24 The neuroprotective mechanisms of TCM against AD encompass the mitigation of oxidative stress, modulation of autophagic processes, exertion of anti-inflammatory actions, and reduction of mitochondrial dysfunction. Notably, the regulation of ferroptosis constitutes a pivotal mechanism underlying TCM’s therapeutic efficacy.25 This article provides a systematic examination of the ferroptosis pathway and its involvement in AD pathogenesis, synthesizes contemporary evidence regarding TCM’s anti-AD effects via ferroptosis modulation, and discusses the prospective development of TCM-based formulations for AD therapy. This is a narrative review; the literature search was conducted using PubMed, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang Data from inception to December 2025. Keywords included combinations of “Alzheimer’s disease”, “ferroptosis”, “traditional Chinese medicine”, “herbal medicine”, “acupuncture”, and related terms. Only articles published in English or Chinese were considered. No formal quality assessment or meta-analysis was performed, consistent with the narrative approach.

The preclinical evidence summarized in this review is derived primarily from in vitro models (eg, HT22, PC12, SH-SY5Y cells treated with erastin or Aβ) and in vivo rodent models (eg, APP/PS1, SAMP8, ICV-STZ, and Aβ-infused rats). While these models recapitulate certain aspects of AD pathology (amyloid plaques, tau hyperphosphorylation, oxidative stress, cognitive deficits), they do not fully mirror the complex, age-dependent, and multifactorial nature of human sporadic AD. Notably, most studies use young animals or acute insult models, which may overestimate the efficacy of interventions. Furthermore, few studies have employed aged or comorbid models that better represent the typical AD population. Therefore, the translational relevance of the findings should be interpreted with caution, and validation in more clinically relevant models (eg, aged knock-in, humanized APP models) is warranted before advancing to clinical trials.

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is characterized by dysregulated iron homeostasis, lipid peroxidation, and diminished glutathione (GSH) levels or inactivation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4).26 Morphologically, ferroptotic cells display shrunken mitochondria with disorganized, fragmented cristae and membrane disruption, in contrast to the enlarged mitochondria observed in non-ferroptotic states. Agents such as vitamin E, ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), and liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1) specifically suppress ferroptotic cell death without influencing alternative cell death pathways.27 In this review, we consider the following as ferroptosis-specific or highly indicative readouts: (i) pharmacological inhibition by Fer-1/Lip-1; (ii) genetic manipulation of core regulators (GPX4, SLC7A11, ACSL4); (iii) direct measurement of lipid peroxidation products (eg, PUFA-OOH, MDA, 4-HNE) in combination with iron chelation; and (iv) characteristic mitochondrial morphology (shrunken mitochondria with cristae disruption). Markers such as total ROS, SOD activity, GSH/GSSG ratio, and inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) are considered non-specific as they are shared by multiple cell death pathways and oxidative stress conditions. This distinction is critical to avoid overinterpretation of mechanistic data.

The molecular basis of ferroptosis principally encompasses metabolic pathways related to lipids, iron, and amino acids (Figure 1). A central driver of ferroptosis is lipid peroxidation, which arises from the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) and subsequent accumulation of lipid peroxides.28 Enzymes involved in lipid metabolism play a pivotal role in this peroxidation process. For instance, acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4, a crucial enzyme in phospholipid metabolism, catalyzes the esterification of PUFAs—including arachidonic and adrenic acids—into PUFA-CoA.29 GSH, the major cellular antioxidant in mammalian systems, is synthesized from glutamate, cysteine, and glycine through the sequential actions of glutamine-cysteine ligase and glutathione synthetase. Cellular GSH synthesis relies significantly on the cystine/glutamate antiporter (system xc−) antiporter, which mediates the exchange of extracellular cystine for intracellular glutamate.30 Cysteine acts as the rate-limiting substrate for GSH production due to its constrained intracellular availability. The system Xc− complex, composed of solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) and SLC3A2 subunits, exports glutamate under conditions of excessive GSH consumption.31 Perturbations in iron metabolism contribute to pathogenic outcomes. The process initiates when transferrin binds extracellular Fe3⁺ at the cell membrane, forming a transferrin-Fe3⁺ complex that is internalized via transferrin receptor 1.32 Subsequent release of Fe3⁺ from the endosomal compartment into the cytosol is facilitated by divalent metal transporter 1, aided by six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of prostate 3.33 Ferroportin 1 mediates the export of Fe2⁺ released from ferritin. In ferroptosis, labile Fe2⁺ reacts with hydrogen peroxide via the Fenton reaction, producing highly reactive lipid peroxides—a consequence of disrupted balance in iron uptake, storage, and efflux.34

Molecular mechanisms of ferroptosis primarily involve lipid, iron and amino acid metabolism.

Ferroptosis and AD Pathology

Interplay Between Ferroptosis and Canonical AD Pathologies

Aβ plaque deposition and neurofibrillary tangle (NFT) formation constitute the histopathological hallmarks of AD, and accumulating evidence supports a bidirectional relationship between these lesions and ferroptosis.35,36 Intracellular iron overload disrupts the iron regulatory protein/iron-responsive element (IRP/IRE) system, leading to upregulated translation of APP and consequent overproduction of Aβ.37 In parallel, ferrous iron Fe2⁺ directly engages Aβ peptides, stabilizing their β-sheet conformation and accelerating oligomerization and aggregation.38 Iron also facilitates Tau hyperphosphorylation through activation of cyclin-dependent kinase 5 (CDK5) and glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β); additionally, Fe3⁺ binds to histidine residues on Tau, promoting the assembly of NFTs.39 This iron-driven feed-forward loop involving Aβ and Tau exacerbates both proteotoxic stress and cellular vulnerability to ferroptosis.40 Moreover, ferroptotic neuronal death releases damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that potentiate microglial activation, thereby mechanistically linking ferroptosis to neuroinflammation.41,42 Elevated neuronal iron further reinforces Tau pathology via CDK5/P25 and GSK-3β signaling, while Fe3⁺-histidine interactions perpetuate NFT formation.43

The translational relevance of ferroptosis-related metabolic perturbations to human AD is supported by clinical metabolomic investigations. Reduced GSH levels and an altered cysteine/glutamate ratio have been consistently reported in the plasma and cerebrospinal fluid of individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early-stage AD.44 In a targeted amino acid profiling study, Socha et al demonstrated significantly lower circulating concentrations of cysteine and glycine—the rate-limiting substrates for GSH synthesis—in MCI subjects relative to cognitively normal elderly controls45 Likewise, studies have shown that identified a distinct serum amino acid signature in AD patients, marked by diminished branched-chain amino acids and perturbed glutamate metabolism, changes that likely reflect systemic redox imbalance.46 Although these studies did not directly assess ferroptosis, they provide human-relevant metabolic context that grounds the mechanistic framework in disease-associated disturbances, reinforcing the therapeutic rationale for targeting the GSH/GPX4 axis in AD.

Microglia Activation

Microglia are pivotal CNS components, regulating energy metabolism, synaptic plasticity, and ion balance.47 As resident immune cells, they execute immune responses with memory-like features and maintain brain homeostasis. Their activation is a hallmark of neurodegeneration, where iron overload and dysregulated iron homeostasis are implicated in AD pathogenesis.48,49 Upon infection or injury, microglia activate, yielding context-dependent beneficial or detrimental outcomes. Elevated microglial iron promotes phagocytosis and a harmful M1 phenotype, releasing pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, NO) that damage neurons.50 Increased neuronal iron disrupts neuromelanin-iron complexes, releasing toxic free iron and causing neuromelanin leakage, which further drives microglial M1 polarization and neurotoxin release.51 Iron accumulation in activated microglia also contributes to CNS iron deposition.52 In the M1 state, iNOS upregulation leads to NO production, exacerbating glutamate-induced neurotoxicity and neuronal ferroptosis.53

Oxidative Stress and Neuronal Loss

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death, distinct from apoptosis and necrosis, which is primarily induced by oxidative stress—a key pathological process in AD.54 The accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), a hallmark of oxidative stress, plays a central role in initiating ferroptosis. This oxidative imbalance disrupts multiple pathways, including inhibition of the system xc−, downregulation of GPX4, iron homeostasis dysregulation, and lipid peroxidation, the latter being a major driver of ferroptosis.55 A key feature of ferroptosis is the excessive accumulation of lipid peroxides, generated via radical chain reactions wherein oxygen radicals attack PUFAs, forming lipid hydroperoxides and further elevating ROS levels.56 Malondialdehyde (MDA), a lipid peroxidation product, serves as a biomarker for both ferroptosis and oxidative stress.57 Oxidative stress also impairs the antioxidant defense system by reducing levels of key enzymes such as GSH, catalase, superoxide dismutase, and GPX, thereby accelerating ferroptosis.58,59 Neuronal loss, a defining feature of AD-related cognitive decline, is promoted by ferroptosis.60 Elevated iron fosters ROS production, depletes GSH, and exacerbates lipid peroxidation, ultimately leading to neuronal death.61 In studies using Fpnfl/fl/NEXcre and APPswe/PS1dE9 AD mouse models, decreased expression of the ferroptosis regulator GPX4 was observed alongside upregulation of iron metabolism regulator IREB2 and citrate synthase (CS). ACSF2 was upregulated only in APPswe/PS1dE9 mice, suggesting hippocampal ferroptosis activation in both models.62

Aging: The Common Ground for Ferroptosis and AD

Aging is the paramount risk factor for AD, and the pathophysiological processes underlying senescence create a permissive environment for both AD pathogenesis and ferroptosis.63 At the cellular level, aging is characterized by a progressive decline in homeostatic mechanisms, including impaired proteostasis, mitochondrial dysfunction, dysregulated nutrient sensing, and chronic, low-grade inflammation. These hallmarks of aging directly intersect with the drivers of ferroptosis.

Cellular senescence and the aging process are associated with a progressive dysregulation of iron metabolism, leading to increased labile iron pools.64 Simultaneously, aging compromises antioxidant defense systems and enhances susceptibility to oxidative stress.65 The age-related decline in cellular repair mechanisms allows for the accumulation of oxidative damage, particularly to lipid membranes rich in PUFAs. This confluence of increased iron availability, elevated oxidative stress, and diminished antioxidant capacity establishes a perfect storm that lowers the threshold for ferroptosis initiation.66 Notably, senescent cells themselves exhibit features of lipid peroxidation and can promote a pro-oxidant, pro-inflammatory microenvironment, further exacerbating neuronal vulnerability.67 Therefore, ferroptosis can be viewed not only as a specific cell death pathway in AD but also as a critical executor of age-associated cellular decline within the nervous system.

TCM has a long history and a rich theoretical foundation in promoting health, delaying aging, and treating age-related disorders, often conceptualized as tonifying “Qi” (vital energy) and “Kidney Essence”, which are considered the root of vitality and longevity. From a modern pharmacological perspective, many TCM compounds and herbs classified as “tonics” or “anti-aging” agents possess documented antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and mitochondrial-protective properties.68,69 These biological activities directly counteract the core pillars of aging that predispose to ferroptosis. For instance, herbs like Ginseng Radix et Rhizoma (Ren Shen), Astragali Radix (Huang Qi), and Lycii Fructus (Gou Qi Zi) are traditionally used to combat fatigue and weakness associated with aging and have been shown to modulate Nrf2 signaling, improve mitochondrial function, and reduce oxidative stress.70,71 By mitigating the systemic and cellular hallmarks of aging, TCM may create a less favorable environment for ferroptosis initiation, thereby exerting a protective effect against age-dependent neurodegenerative processes like AD. Consequently, the therapeutic potential of TCM in AD via ferroptosis regulation should be appreciated within this broader context of its multi-targeted anti-aging properties.

Pathways Regulating Ferroptosis in AD

Kelch‑like ECH‑associated Protein 1 Keap1/Nrf2 Antioxidant Response Element Pathway

The Keap1/Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway is a critical regulatory system for cellular defense against oxidative damage, coordinating the induction of numerous detoxifying and antioxidant proteins, which underscores its relevance as a therapeutic target for AD.72 Nrf2, a transcription factor activated under oxidative stress, serves as a key inhibitor of lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis.73 Under basal conditions, Keap1 represses Nrf2 activity by facilitating its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. However, oxidative stress triggers the dissociation of Nrf2 from Keap1, allowing Nrf2 to translocate into the nucleus.74 There, it dimerizes with small Maf proteins, and this complex binds to Antioxidant Response Elements (ARE) within DNA, initiating the transcription of a network of cytoprotective genes.75 Functioning as a master regulator of anti-ferroptotic defense, Nrf2 modulates central events in lipid peroxidation and cell death. Its nuclear activity enhances the expression of genes that mitigate ferroptosis by regulating iron metabolism and bolstering antioxidant capacity.76,77 This includes upregulating proteins such as ferritin heavy/light chains, ferroportin, transferrin receptor, and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), while also promoting the production of NADPH, GSH, and coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) to counteract peroxidative damage.78 Additionally, the disruption of the DLG motif-mediated interaction between Nrf2 and Keap1 stabilizes Nrf2 by preventing its ubiquitination, thereby amplifying the cellular antioxidant response and further suppressing ferroptosis.79,80

p53/SLC7A11 Pathway

The catalytic peroxidation of unsaturated fatty acids within cellular membranes, driven by divalent iron or esteroxygenases, marks the initiation of a process culminating in cell death.81,82 As a principal tumor suppressor in humans, the p53 protein modulates oncogene expression and downstream signaling cascades, thereby mediating significant biological outcomes.83 In addition to its well-characterized role in oncology, p53 is abundantly expressed in cerebral tissues, where it modulates dendritic development, responses to oxidative stress, as well as apoptotic and autophagic pathways.84 Consequently, dysregulation of p53 and its associated signaling networks is considered relevant to the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease.85 SLC7A11, a central regulator of ferroptosis, functions as a transmembrane component of system Xc-, which facilitates cellular cystine uptake for the synthesis of cysteine and the production of GSH.86 Reduced SLC7A11 expression perturbs cysteine metabolism, diminishing intracellular cystine and GSH levels. This impairment inhibits GPX4 activity, resulting in the accumulation of lipid peroxides and the induction of ferroptosis.87 Research demonstrates that p53 binds to the promoter region of SLC7A11, repressing its transcription and restricting GSH synthesis, which in turn promotes ferroptotic cell death.88,89 Mediated through p53, aristolochic acid may suppress ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma, thereby potentially facilitating tumor progression. The p53 (3KR) mutant—in which three lysine residues are substituted with arginines, abolishing acetylation—specifically reduces SLC7A11 expression without altering other p53 targets such as CDKN1A/p21 (a cell-cycle regulator) or BAX (an apoptosis-related protein). In contrast, the p53 (4KR98) mutant, containing an additional substitution at lysine 98, fails to downregulate SLC7A11.90

GSH/GPX4 Pathway

In AD, aberrant buildup of lipid peroxides acts as a critical trigger for ferroptosis, evidenced by increased levels of lipid peroxidation markers within neuronal cells.91 The accumulation of iron facilitates the Fenton and Haber-Weiss reactions, yielding ROS, which subsequently initiate lipid peroxidation and cause oxidative impairment to subcellular components.92 Ferroptosis is principally modulated by GPX4 and GSH. GSH, which incorporates a thiol group obtained from cysteine, functions as an essential antioxidant by scavenging ROS and reactive nitrogen species, thereby preserving cellular redox homeostasis and facilitating the detoxification of exogenous compounds.93 GPX4 is a selenium-dependent enzyme that requires a selenocysteine residue to perform its reduction activity.94 Its role involves the conversion of lipid hydroperoxides into less reactive lipid alcohols, thereby mitigating oxidative injury.95 The catalytic core of GPX4 contains selenocysteine, which cycles between reduced and oxidized states to maintain enzymatic function.96 Upon exposure to peroxides, the selenolate moiety of GPX4 is oxidized to selenic acid; this intermediate is then restored to its reduced state by two molecules of GSH, resulting in the reduction of lipid hydroperoxides to nontoxic lipid alcohols and the concomitant production of oxidized glutathione.97 Thus, the biosynthesis of GPX4 and its related metabolic routes play a central role in the control of ferroptosis.

Traditional Chinese Medicine Treats AD by Intervening in Ferroptosis

TCM offers distinctive clinical value in the prevention and management of AD, which is attributed to its holistic therapeutic philosophy, principle of syndrome differentiation, and the multi-constituent, multi-target nature of its formulations. Existing research indicates that TCM exerts neuroprotective properties by modulating neuronal autophagy, mitigating inflammatory responses, and reducing oxidative stress.98 More recently, the specific pathways and molecular mechanisms through which TCM compounds inhibit ferroptosis have garnered increasing attention. Mechanisms of Traditional Chinese Medicine Extracts and Monomers in Regulating Ferroptosis for the Treatment of Alzheimer’s Disease

AD falls within the categories of “dementia”, “forgetfulness”, and “stupor syndrome” in traditional Chinese medicine. It is attributed to multiple internal organs such as the brain, heart, liver, and kidneys.100 It is often caused by factors like old-age frailty, emotional injuries, and long-term illness, which lead to insufficient qi and blood, depletion of the marrow sea, and lack of nourishment for the primordial spirit. The location of AD is in the brain.101 As mentioned in “Lei Zheng Zhi Cai, Volume Three”, “The brain is the residence of the primordial spirit and the sea of marrow, which is the basis for memory”. This indicates that memory is related to the fullness of the brain marrow, and the fullness of the brain marrow is a prerequisite for the brain to perform its physiological functions.102 “Huangdi Neijing” states that

The qi and spirits of all yang meridians converge at the head, and the essence of all marrows gathers in the brain.103 Therefore, the head is the residence of marrow and spirit.

It emphasizes that the brain, as the core of spiritual convergence, is the center of mental activities.104 When a person reaches old age, with insufficient kidney essence and kidney qi deficiency, the marrow sea fails to be filled, the brain loses nourishment, and the mental functions are impaired, resulting in dementia symptoms such as dullness, stupidity, and foolishness.105 Contemporary research indicates that TCM possesses antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic properties, exerting preventive effects against AD via modulation of diverse cellular signaling pathways.106 Clinical investigations have further demonstrated that integrative therapeutic regimens combining TCM with conventional Western pharmaceuticals yield superior outcomes compared to monotherapies in AD management,107 Such combined protocols are associated with significant amelioration of neurological deficits, lowered disability incidence, and enhanced quality of life for patients. Notably, these treatment strategies exhibit a favorable safety profile, characterized by minimal adverse effects and elevated tolerability.108,109 This aligns with a therapeutic goal of managing a chronic, age-related condition by slowing its progression and improving the patient’s functional state.

Chinese Herbal Extract

Pigeonweed (belonging to the Penthoraceae family) is a traditional Miao medicine commonly used in the treatment of diseases such as cholecystitis, alcoholic liver disease, and infectious hepatitis.110 Its ethyl acetate fraction extract (PEF) has recently been found to have the effect of inhibiting ferroptosis. Yong et al94 screened the natural medicine library in China and confirmed that PEF can significantly reduce the level of ROS in PC12 cells, improve the mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial morphology, and exert the inhibitory effect on ferroptosis by regulating intracellular iron metabolism and the expression of related proteins, such as reducing the levels of transferrin receptor 1, ferritin heavy chain, and light chain, while up - regulating the expression of glutathione peroxidase 4. In addition, in the Caenorhabditis elegans model, PEF can reduce the levels of ROS and malondialdehyde, inhibit the generation of lipid peroxidase, alleviate iron accumulation and oxidative damage, and thereby protect neurons from ferroptosis-related damage and improve cognitive and behavioral disorders.

On the other hand, musk, a traditional Chinese medicine with various pharmacological effects such as anti-inflammation, anti-oxidation, and neuroprotection,111 shows certain therapeutic potential for stroke, coma, cerebrovascular, and nervous system diseases in modern medical research.112 Studies have shown that the aqueous extract of musk can significantly increase the survival rate of HT22 cells treated with the ferroptosis inducer erastin.113 Its mechanism of action is related to the activation of the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway, manifested by a decrease in the accumulation of malondialdehyde, ROS, and lipid hydroperoxides in cells, an increase in the level of glutathione, and an enhancement in the expression of glutathione peroxidase 4 and solute carrier family 7 member 11. Meanwhile, the expression of iron - metabolism - related proteins such as transferrin receptor, ferroportin, and ferritin heavy chain is also regulated.

These results suggest that the aqueous extract of musk may exert a neuroprotective effect by alleviating the ferroptosis process. In conclusion, both the ethyl acetate fraction extract of pigeonweed and the aqueous extract of musk can play a role in the protection of the central nervous system by regulating the key pathways of ferroptosis and related biomarkers. This provides a potential mechanism basis for the development of traditional Chinese medicine for neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease. It should be noted that the evidence for these extracts remains largely preclinical (in vitro and in vivo animal models). Positive outcomes in these controlled settings may not directly translate to human patients due to complex pharmacokinetics, potential herb-herb or herb-drug interactions, and the multifactorial nature of human AD. Furthermore, while these extracts show modulation of ferroptosis-associated markers, the contributions of other parallel neuroprotective pathways (eg, general anti-inflammation, autophagy enhancement) are often concurrent and difficult to disentangle.

Chinese Herbal Monomers

Terpenoids

Paeoniflorin (Pae) is a water-soluble monoterpene glycoside isolated from peony, which exhibits a broad spectrum of pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, neuroprotective, antitumor, and immunomodulatory activities.114 Research by Zhai et al115 demonstrated that Pae can suppress the expression of tumor protein p53, thereby restoring the transcriptional level of SLC7A11 and upregulating the protein levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and GPX4, leading to the inhibition of ferroptosis. Concurrently, Pae blocks p53-mediated oxidative stress, reduces ROS and iron ion levels in brain tissue, and ameliorates neuronal damage in AD mice. In PC12 cells, Pae also inhibits erastin-induced ferroptosis, alleviates oxidative damage, and lowers ROS levels. Collectively, these findings indicate that binding to p53 suppresses ferroptosis, improves neurological impairment, and enhances cognitive and behavioral functions in AD mice, highlighting its potential as a novel small-molecule drug for AD treatment.

Pachymic acid (PA) is a major active constituent of Poria cocos, possessing various biological effects such as hypoglycemic, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, sedative, and anti-ischemia/reperfusion injury properties.116 Meng Yun et al117 found that PA activates the Nrf2/SLC7A11/GPX4 signaling pathway, upregulates the expression levels of Nrf2, SLC7A11, and GPX4 genes in the hippocampus of AD rats, thereby reducing MDA and ferrous ion (Fe2⁺) levels, inhibiting ferroptosis, and improving cognitive dysfunction.

These studies demonstrate promising in vivo effects in rodent AD models. However, the field would benefit from more consistent use of specific ferroptosis inhibitors as controls to definitively link the observed benefits to ferroptosis inhibition perse, rather than to broader antioxidant or anti-apoptotic effects. Discrepancies in effective dosages across different studies also highlight the need for standardized pharmacokinetic assessments.

Glycosides

Forsythoside A (FA), a principal bioactive constituent derived from Forsythia suspensa, exhibits a range of pharmacological properties including anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antioxidative, and neuroprotective activities. Research by Wang et al118 demonstrates that FA administration mitigates amyloid-beta plaque deposition and lowers phosphorylated tau protein levels in the brains of APP/PS1 transgenic mice, concomitant with observable improvements in memory and cognitive function. The underlying mechanism involves the suppression of the IKK/IκB/NF-κB signaling cascade, leading to a reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokine release and an increase in anti-inflammatory mediators. Additionally, FA promotes the activation of the Nrf2/GPX4 signaling axis while further inhibiting NF-κB, resulting in decreased intracellular iron overload and lipid peroxidation. Collectively, these findings indicate that FA can counteract erastin-induced ferroptosis and ameliorate neuroinflammation primarily through modulation of the Nrf2/GPX4 pathway, highlighting its potential as a candidate therapeutic for Alzheimer’s disease.

Salidroside (Sal), a bioactive glycoside compound derived from Rhodiola rosea, demonstrates neuroprotective properties in experimental models of AD. According to findings by Yang et al119 Sal administration mitigates cognitive impairment induced by Aβ1₋42 in AD model mice, primarily through activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 cascade. Further mechanistic studies reveal that Sal modulates the expression of key ferroptosis-related markers, including downregulating TFR1 and acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), while upregulating SLC7A11 and GPX4. This coordinated regulation promotes the Nrf2/GPX4 signaling axis, reduces cerebral iron accumulation, and consequently exerts its neuroprotective effects. These results collectively indicate that Salidroside can attenuate ferroptosis, supporting its potential therapeutic relevance for AD.120

Tenuifolin (Ten) is one of the main active components of Polygala tenuifolia and has been shown to improve memory impairment in AD mice. Li et al121 reported that Ten inhibits the decrease in SOD, catalase (CAT), and GSH activity levels, while reducing MDA and intracellular ROS levels in HT22 cells, indicating its antioxidative stress capacity. Additionally, Ten downregulates ACSL4 expression, upregulates SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression, decreases lipid ROS generation, inhibits lipid peroxidation, and protects neurons from ferroptosis, thereby slowing AD progression.

Isoforsythiaside (IFY) is a phenylethanoid glycoside isolated from the dried fruits of Forsythia suspensa, possessing antibacterial, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant activities.122 Zhang et al123 discovered that IFY reduces the expression levels of inflammatory factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and nitric oxide (NO) in APP/PS1 mice, erastin-induced HT22 cells, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated BV2 microglial cells, thereby attenuating neuroinflammatory responses. Moreover, by modulating the Nrf2 signaling pathway, IFY upregulates the protein expression of HO-1, NADPH quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), and GPX4, inhibiting ferroptosis. This suggests that IFY exerts a dual regulatory effect on neuroinflammation and ferroptosis in AD.

Curculigoside (Cur) is the primary active ingredient of Curculigo orchioides and exhibits multiple pharmacological activities.123 Studies have shown that Cur treatment can target the System Xc− pathway and reduce oxidative stress, upregulate GPX4 levels, restore SLC7A11 function in the brains of AD mice, lower Aβ and p-tau levels, and inhibit ferroptosis to protect neurons.124

Phenolic Derivatives

Silibinin is the major flavonolignan component of silymarin extract from milk thistle seeds, accounting for 50–70% of the extract.125 Liu et al126 found that silibinin significantly inhibits p53 protein expression, upregulates SLC7A11 and GPX4 levels without affecting p21 protein expression, thereby preventing streptozotocin (STZ)-induced ferroptosis damage. It also blocks the stimulator of interferon genes (STING)-dependent DNA sensing pathway, downregulates ferroptosis and related neuroinflammatory factors (such as NF-κB, TNF-α, NLRP3, Caspase-1, IL-1β) in STZ-induced sporadic AD models, exerting neuroprotective effects.

Icariin (ICA) is a flavonoid extracted from Epimedium species and possesses pharmacological activities including antidepressant effects, improvement of cerebral ischemic injury, anti-dementia, and anti-aging properties.127,128 Yang et al129 reported that ICA reduces MDA levels, increases SOD activity, decreases brain iron content, and ameliorates oxidative stress damage, suggesting that it improves neuronal damage, memory, and motor function in AD mice by inhibiting ferroptosis in nerve cells.

Eriodictyol is a natural flavonoid compound with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anticancer, and neuroprotective effects. Li et al130 demonstrated that eriodictyol significantly improves cognitive deficits in APP/PS1 mice, inhibits Aβ aggregation and tau protein hyperphosphorylation in the brain, and activates the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway to promote Nrf2 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, thereby inhibiting neuronal ferroptosis and alleviating memory impairment associated with AD.

Quinones

Sennoside A (SA) is a major anthraquinone active ingredient in rhubarb, with anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antiviral, and anti-neurodegenerative properties.130 Li et al131 found that SA reduces hippocampal iron ion (Fe2⁺) levels, increases GSH content, decreases MDA levels, and downregulates the protein and mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in AD mice, inhibiting ferroptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses. This suggests SA may be a potential drug candidate for AD treatment.

Chrysophanol (CHR) is one of the important anthraquinone components in Rheum species.131 Luo et al132 experimentally confirmed that CHR enhances spatial memory in AD rats, reduces hippocampal neuronal damage, increases the survival rate of PC12 cells induced by Aβ25₋35, significantly elevates GPX4 activity, GPX activity, and GSH levels in AD model rats and Aβ-injured PC12 cells, lowers ROS and lipid peroxide (LPO) levels, improves mitochondrial pathological damage, and protects neuronal ultrastructure. These results indicate that CHR may protect hippocampal neurons in AD rats by suppressing ferroptosis, offering a potential avenue for AD drug development.

Alkaloids

Berberine (BBR), also known as berberine, is a natural isoquinoline alkaloid extracted from Coptis chinensis and has protective effects on the central nervous system.133 Li et al134 showed that BBR improves cognitive impairment in AD mice, alleviates neuronal damage, Aβ deposition, and tau protein hyperphosphorylation. Its mechanisms include regulating Nrf2 transcriptional levels, upregulating the expression of SOD, GSH, GPX4, FPN1, and SLC7A11, downregulating MDA, ACSL4, and ROS levels, effectively reducing abnormal iron accumulation in the brain, and ultimately decreasing Aβ deposition. BBR also mitigates oxidative stress by downregulating cyclooxygenase-2(COX-2) and reducing 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE). Furthermore, BBR enhances resistance to ferroptosis by activating the AMPK/GSK3β/Nrf2 pathway.

Chinese Herbal Compound

Suanzaoren Decoction (SZRD), originating from the Treatise on Cold Damage and Miscellaneous Diseases, has been used for over a thousand years and is commonly employed in the treatment of insomnia and depression. Modern research also indicates its ameliorative effects on AD.135 Long et al136 found that in APP/PS1 model mice, SZRD could inhibit Aβ deposition in the hippocampus, reduce neuronal loss and degeneration, and improve synaptic damage. Its mechanism may be related to the regulation of iron metabolism-related protein expression: SZRD upregulates the expression of FPN1, DJ-1, Nrf2, GPX4, and SLC7A11 in hippocampal tissue, while downregulating the expression of TfR1, FTH1, FTL, and ACSL4, thereby reducing iron accumulation in the hippocampus and lowering oxidative stress levels. These findings suggest that SZRD may alleviate AD-related neuronal damage, synaptic dysfunction, and ferroptosis by activating the DJ-1/Nrf2 signaling pathway. Research on compound formulas faces additional complexity. While formulas like SZRD and KXS offer the theoretical advantage of multi-target synergy, it is challenging to identify the exact active constituents responsible for the observed anti-ferroptotic effects, to standardize their preparation, and to attribute outcomes specifically to ferroptosis modulation amidst their broad pharmacological profiles. Negative or neutral results with TCM formulas in AD models are less frequently published, potentially creating a publication bias that skews the narrative toward efficacy.

Kaixinsan (KXS) is a classical formula first recorded in Sun Simiao’s Essential Formulas for Emergencies Worth a Thousand Gold. It consists of Ginseng, Poria, Polygala, and Acorus tatarinowii in a ratio of 3:3:2:2. It has been used since the Tang Dynasty for the treatment of dementia-like disorders due to its properties of replenishing qi and resolving phlegm.137 Yan et al138 found that KXS activates the SIRT1/FSP1 signaling pathway, significantly increasing the protein expression of FSP1, FTH, and SIRT1, while markedly reducing ACSL4 expression levels. This inhibits neuronal ferroptosis, ameliorates neuronal damage in AD mice, and exerts neuroprotective effects in AD.

Acupuncture

Chen et al139 demonstrated that electroacupuncture can activate the p62/Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway, upregulate GPX4 and FPN1, and reduce MDA and Ptgs2 levels in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice, concomitant with improved cognitive performance. In another study, Li et al140 reported similar effects using “Tongdu Qishen” acupuncture. Although these changes are consistent with ferroptosis inhibition, the studies did not employ ferroptosis-specific pharmacological inhibitors (eg, Fer-1, Lip-1) or genetic tools (eg, GPX4 knockdown) to establish causality. Moreover, the observed markers (MDA, SOD, GPX4 mRNA) are not exclusive to ferroptosis. Therefore, the current evidence should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Decisive experiments to confirm anti-ferroptotic effects of acupuncture would include: (i) demonstration that ferroptosis inhibitors occlude the protective effects of acupuncture; (ii) measurement of bona fide ferroptosis markers (eg, lipid peroxidation by C11-BODIPY, mitochondrial morphology by electron microscopy); and (iii) use of conditional knockout models (eg, neuron-specific GPX4 deficiency) to test whether acupuncture’s benefits are lost when ferroptosis defense is impaired. Translational Challenges: Pharmacokinetics, BBB Penetration, Standardization, and Safety

Despite the promising anti-ferroptotic and neuroprotective effects of numerous TCM extracts and monomers observed in preclinical models, their translational advancement is frequently impeded by several interrelated limitations.

Oral Bioavailability and Metabolic Stability

Many bioactive constituents derived from TCM, particularly glycosides (eg, paeoniflorin, forsythoside A) and alkaloids (eg, berberine), exhibit poor oral bioavailability attributable to limited gastrointestinal stability, efflux transporter involvement, and extensive Phase II metabolism including glucuronidation and sulfation.141 For instance, paeoniflorin undergoes rapid hydrolysis in the intestinal lumen and systemic circulation, resulting in absolute systemic exposure below 5% of the administered dose.142 Berberine, a well-characterized P-gp substrate, displays minimal intestinal absorption; nonetheless, its brain accumulation may be facilitated via active transport mechanisms or metabolic conversion.143 Although prodrug approaches, nanoparticle-based formulations, and co-administration with absorption enhancers are currently under investigation, these strategies remain predominantly confined to preclinical development.

Blood–Brain Barrier Penetration

Achieving therapeutically relevant CNS concentrations is imperative for effective AD intervention. However, quantitative data regarding BBB permeability for the majority of TCM-derived compounds remain scarce. Although certain agents, such as salidroside and icariin, have demonstrated neuroactivity following systemic administration, their unbound brain-to-plasma concentration ratios (Kp,uu) have rarely been rigorously determined. Recent investigations employing in situ brain perfusion or MDCK-MDR1 transporter assays suggest that selected flavonoids and terpenoids may traverse the BBB via passive diffusion or carrier-mediated transport; nevertheless, absolute brain concentrations are generally low.144 Advanced CNS-targeted delivery systems—including liposomes, nanoemulsions, and intranasal administration routes—represent promising avenues for enhancing brain bioavailability.145

Standardization and Quality Control

TCM extracts and multi-herb formulations are characterized by pronounced chemical complexity and batch-to-batch variability arising from differences in raw material sourcing, extraction techniques, and manufacturing protocols. The prevalent use of non-standardized extracts or poorly defined fractions in preclinical studies substantially impedes cross-study comparability and obscures identification of the bioactive principle(s). To enable successful clinical translation, it is imperative to establish chemically standardized extracts characterized by well-defined marker compounds and validated analytical methodologies (eg, HPLC fingerprinting). The current absence of such standardization constitutes a critical barrier to regulatory approval and clinical reproducibility.

Safety, Herb–Drug Interactions, and Geriatric Polypharmacy

Patients with AD are typically of advanced age and frequently receive concomitant pharmacotherapy for comorbid conditions—including antihypertensives, anticoagulants, and antidepressants. TCM-based interventions may thus pose clinically significant herb–drug interaction risks, particularly when they inhibit or induce cytochrome P450 enzymes or drug transporters. Berberine, for example, is known to inhibit CYP2D6 and CYP3A4, potentially elevating plasma concentrations of co-administered donepezil or memantine.146 Despite these concerns, systematic drug-interaction studies are conspicuously absent for the majority of compounds discussed in this review. Moreover, long-term safety data in aging populations remain critically lacking. Future investigations should integrate comprehensive toxicological assessments—including hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and genotoxicity—as well as dedicated interaction studies in relevant preclinical models and, ultimately, in early-phase clinical trials.

In summary, advancing TCM-derived ferroptosis modulators toward clinical application in AD necessitates a fundamental paradigm shift: moving from descriptive efficacy-based investigations toward hypothesis-driven, PK/PD-oriented research. The early integration of ADME profiling, rigorous BBB permeability assessment, and stringent extract standardization is essential to this endeavor. Conclusion and Prospects

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-driven modality of regulated cell death, has increasingly been recognized as a pivotal mechanism underlying neuronal degeneration in Alzheimer’s disease. This review systematically synthesizes the expanding body of evidence demonstrating that diverse TCM approaches—including herbal extracts, purified monomers, compound formulas, and acupuncture—exert neuroprotective effects in AD models through modulation of ferroptosis-related signaling cascades, notably the Keap1/Nrf2/ARE axis, the system Xc−/GSH/GPX4 pathway, and iron homeostasis regulatory networks.

Despite these encouraging findings, several substantive limitations must be candidly acknowledged. First, the overwhelming majority of studies remain preclinical in nature, relying on immortalized cell lines and transgenic rodent models that inadequately recapitulate the complex etiology and progression of sporadic human AD. Second, evidence implicating ferroptosis is frequently indirect; many investigations infer ferroptosis inhibition solely from alterations in non-specific oxidative stress markers (eg, MDA, ROS, GSH) without incorporating ferroptosis-specific validation tools such as ferrostatin-1 or liproxstatin-1 controls, direct GPX4 activity assays, or quantitative lipid peroxidation imaging. Third, the pronounced heterogeneity of TCM preparations—spanning chemically defined pure compounds, crude extracts, and multi-herb decoctions—poses substantial challenges for cross-study comparability and definitive mechanistic attribution. Fourth, critical pharmacokinetic hurdles, including low oral bioavailability, extensive first-pass metabolism, and insufficient blood–brain barrier penetration, remain inadequately addressed. Fifth, safety data, particularly regarding chronic administration and potential herb–drug interactions in elderly, poly-medicated AD populations, are virtually absent.

To facilitate the translational advancement of this field, we propose the following concrete research priorities:

Adoption of standardized, ferroptosis-specific outcome measures. Future investigations should incorporate at least one validated ferroptosis-specific endpoint—such as C11-BODIPY581/592 staining, quantitative mitochondrial morphometry, or pharmacological rescue by ferroptosis inhibitors—to definitively establish causality between observed neuroprotection and ferroptosis suppression.

Rigorous preclinical safety and drug-interaction studies. Such evaluations should be conducted in aged, comorbid animal models that better approximate the human geriatric condition and, where justified, extended to Phase 0/I clinical trials involving healthy elderly volunteers.

Based on currently available evidence and the translational readiness assessment summarized in Table 3, the purified compounds berberine and paeoniflorin emerge as the most viable candidates for further drug development. Their defined chemical structures, reproducible pharmacological profiles, and extant (albeit incomplete) pharmacokinetic datasets confer distinct advantages. Nevertheless, formidable obstacles remain: both compounds exhibit poor oral bioavailability, and berberine, in particular, poses clinically relevant risks of CYP-mediated herb–drug interactions. These limitations must be addressed through advanced formulation strategies (eg, nanoparticle encapsulation, prodrug design) or structural optimization. Conversely, standardized extracts with well-documented histories of traditional use—such as Suanzaoren Decoction—may be suitable candidates for pragmatic clinical trials, provided that rigorous quality control protocols and batch-to-batch consistency can be ensured.

Finally, it is imperative to draw a clear distinction between conclusions robustly supported by ferroptosis-specific experimental evidence and those inferred indirectly from non-specific oxidative or inflammatory biomarkers. For instance, the anti-ferroptotic effects of paeoniflorin, salidroside, and eriodictyol are substantiated by studies incorporating ferroptosis inhibitor controls or direct measurements of GPX4 enzymatic activity, whereas the evidence base for many extracts and formulas remains predominantly reliant on non-specific readouts. This evidentiary distinction should inform the design of future confirmatory studies and temper overly optimistic translational claims. In summary, TCM constitutes a rich and underexploited reservoir of ferroptosis-modulating agents with potential applicability to AD therapeutics. However, the realization of this potential will require a sustained commitment to rigorous, mechanism-guided, and translation-oriented research paradigms.
