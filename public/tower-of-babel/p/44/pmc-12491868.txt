A case report on acute cystoid macular edema days after YAG laser capsulotomy
Authors: Amera Gamela Khalaff, Barry Emara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceWe report a unique case of cystoid macular edema occurring immediately after Neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd: YAG) laser capsulotomy.Case presentationA 62-year-old gentleman began seeing wavy lines and a black dot located in his central visual field two days post-YAG. Diagnosis of cystoid macular edema was confirmed by Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) and was treated with prednisolone acetate eye drops four times a day and Bromfenac eye drops once daily. One month post-treatment, the vision improved to a corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) of 20/70, with the macula appearing normal and the cystoid macular edema slowly improving. There was some macular sheen present. The medication was changed to Difluprednate twice daily along with previously prescribed Bromfenac once daily, as a stronger steroid was required. On the two-month follow-up, CDVA improved to 20/25, and the CME had resolved. The patient was advised to continue prescribed medications until finished and to repeat OCT in a month. OCT was done three months post-operatively and was essentially normal with some residual perifoveal edema.Clinical discussionThis case highlights the possibility of CME occurring immediately after YAG laser capsulotomy, resulting in sudden decrease of vision. CME is more common after cataract surgery than after YAG, and this appears to be a unique case of immediate CME post-YAG laser capsulotomy.ConclusionOur experience suggests that colleagues should consider NSAIDS pre- and post- YAG laser capsulotomy to prevent CME. This case highlights the importance of following appropriate management for CME regardless of timeframe of presentation to ensure results.

Introduction

After cataract surgery, the natural lens of the eye is replaced with an artificial intraocular lens (IOL) to restore vision. Occasionally, the capsule that houses the IOL may develop cloudiness or thickening, resulting in reduced vision or sensitivity to glare commonly referred to as posterior capsule opacification (PCO) [1]. Neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd: YAG) laser capsulotomy is an intervention used to treat post-cataract cloudiness by creating a small opening in the cloudy capsule. This opening allows light to pass through the lens, restoring clear vision [2]. Despite its success rate of 95%, reported complications of Nd:YAG include a rise in Intra-ocular Pressure (IOP), retinal detachment, and cystoid macular edema (CME) [3,4].

Cystoid macular edema (CME) following eye surgery refers to the accumulation of excess fluid in cyst-like structures within the neurosensory retina [5]. It is also broadly characterized as the buildup of fluid in the outer plexiform layer of the retina [5]. Intraocular inflammation associated with CME is thought to arise from the release of various substances such as prostaglandins (PGE), nitric oxide (NO), interleukin 6 (IL-6), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [5]. The primary clinical indicators of macular edema typically include reduced central visual acuity, micropsia, and metamorphopsia [5]. Treatment options may involve nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroids, anti-VEGF agents, and systemic immunomodulatory therapies [5,6].

In the context of pseudophakic cystoid macular edema (PCME), various ocular prostaglandin receptors may be activated depending on the location of damage as well as the release of cyclooxygenase enzymes, particularly COX-1 or COX-2 [7]. PCME may result due to significant blood-aqueous barrier compromise, which tends to develop around four to six weeks after surgery. The risk is higher in individuals under 65, males, and those with retinal comorbidities [8]. Prophylactically, applying NSAIDs for two days before surgery is suggested in low-risk patients and for one week preoperatively in high-risk individuals for PCME [5].

In a similar sense to PCME, Nd:YAG may induce inflammatory changes through prostaglandin signaling via laser-induced mechanical stress. Studies highlighted such inflammation may occur due to inappropriately high laser energy settings [9,10]. Furthermore, adverse events such as CME, elevated IOP, and retinal detachment following Nd:YAG are rate; however, pre-existing conditions such as diabetes may increase susceptibility [11].

This article reports a case of a 62-year-old male who successfully underwent bilateral cataract extraction. YAG laser capsulotomy was followed up in the right eye two months later, and the patient presented with CME two days post-YAG. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist [12].

Presentation of case

A 62-year-old white male with no significant medical or family history of diabetes or hypertension presented from their optometrist with blurred vision and difficulty seeing at night, impacting his overall quality of life. His refractive errors of right eye (OD) spherical correction (SC) +2.5 and Cylinder correction (CC) -0.75 at an axis of 175 degrees and the left eye (OS) SC of +1.25 and CC -1.00. The IOP was 17 mmHg OS and 13 mmHg OD. Ophthalmological consultation revealed OS development of a significant degree of astigmatism (plano −1.00 × 180) with CC 20/50. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) (Cirrus OCT, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA) was used for macular thickness measurements, with 299 μm for thickness central subfield in the right eye [Fig. 1]. The patient declared a decline in vision, dry eye, glare, and difficulty with reading. SC, CC, and pinhole visual acuity (pH) for OD were 20/60, 20/30, and 20/30. OS SC, CC, and PH were 20/60, 20/80, and 20/50, respectively. Anterior segment showed nuclear sclerosis OS and OD +2, ultimately leading to a recommendation of bilateral cataract surgery. Both eyes underwent dropless cataract surgery in June 2023, with each eye having a single-piece monofocal standard hydrophobic intra-ocular lens (IOL) (Bausch & Lomb, Vaughn, ON, Canada). The IOLs were placed in the capsular bag without intraoperative complications.Fig. 1Optical Coherence Tomography of patient's OD Horizontal B-Scan during initial consultation three months before procedure.Fig. 1

The patient was seen for a one-day post-operative follow-up, with OD IOP of 22 mmHg with mild edema. OS presented with an IOP of 24 mmHg and corneal microcystic edema. Both eyes presented with round pupil shapes and well-positioned IOLs. One week post-operatively, the patient presented with an IOP of 18 mmHg OU. OD showed mild corneal swelling and fluid haze, but the cornea was clear at some point following the procedure. Two weeks post-operatively, the macula of OS appeared normal on OCT imaging, but there was occasional redness and discomfort in the eye as symptoms of dry eye rather than true inflammation, as evidenced by normal OCT findings and absence of anterior chamber cells or flare during slit-lamp examination. The thickness in the central subfields were 325 μm and 286 μm for OD and OS, respectively [Fig. 2]. However, there was no evidence of retinal pathology. Additionally, the right eye did not show irritation, inflammation, and discharge, leading to a referral for YAG capsulotomy two months later, which coincides with the minimum recommended time before YAG capsulotomy for formation of the blood aqueous barrier.Fig. 2Patient's OD Horizontal B-Scan post-cataract extractions.Fig. 2

The Nd:YAG was performed by a single surgeon in a single session with an energy setting of 2.4 mJ, with 52 pulses applied OD using a Tango Reflex Neo laser (ELLEX Medical Pty. Ltd., Mawson Lakes, South Australia, Australia). The setting was chosen to minimize total energy delivered while appropriately creating an opening in the posterior capsule. During and post-procedure, the patient did not develop any complications; However, two days later, the patient began seeing wavy and watery lines and seeing black centrally located in his vision OD, with a CDVA of 20/200, an IOP of 22 mmHg, and CME confirmed by OCT [Fig. 3]. The thickness central subfield for the right eye measured 582 μm, while the left eye measured 314 μm. The patient was treated with prednisolone acetate eye drops four times a day and Bromfenac eye drops once daily. One month post-treatment, the vision improved to CDVA 20/70, with the macula appearing normal and the CME slowly improving. To enhance inflammation management, diflueprednate was added twice daily, supplementing the existing regimen of bromfenac once daily, as a stronger steroid was required. At two months, CDVA improved to 20/25, and the CME resolved. The patient was advised to continue prescribed medications and to repeat OCT in a month. The OCT was essentially normal, with OD thickness of the central subfield measuring 367 μm with some residual perifoveal edema [Fig. 4]. Discussion

With regards to a time frame specific with developing CME post Nd:YAG, common side effects are also suggested to be associated with the total energy required to create the capsulotomy, with previous examples of complications associated with higher or incorrect laser levels [8,10].

Conventionally CME shows initial improvement in visual acuity one week after surgery, followed by a decline at the one-month mark, and then a gradual improvement by the six-month point, stabilizing with appropriate interventions thereafter [9]. Prophylactically, applying NSAIDs for two days before surgery is suggested in low-risk patients and for one week preoperatively in high-risk individuals for PCME [11].

Strengths of this case include the detailed documentation of the patient's course and the use of OCT for objective assessment. A potential limitation could point toward the lack of NSAID use for CME prophylaxis. This case raises the question of whether similar prophylaxis should be considered for Nd:YAG regardless of risk factors.

The rapid development of CME in this case suggests that the inflammatory cascade triggered by Nd:YAG lead to immediate macular changes. This highlights the importance of considering CME as a potential early complication following the procedure.

The patient reported initial anxiety due to sudden vision loss; However, the patient expressed satisfaction with the outcome and the care received.

Conclusion

Our experience suggests the possibility of CME occurring immediately after YAG, resulting in sudden decrease of vision. Clinicians should consider this possibility and prompt OCT evaluation in patients reporting visual changes post-procedure. Further research is needed to determine if prophylactic measures could prevent acute complications.
