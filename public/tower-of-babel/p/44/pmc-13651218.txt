Nutritional and physiological severity scores for predicting 30-day mortality in geriatric intensive care unit patients: a multicenter prospective study
Authors: Muhammed Mert Can, Akkan Avci, Saliha Dilek Oztoprak Hacioglu, Ahmet Burak Urfalioglu, Erdem Aksay, Yeliz Simsek, Sadiye Yolcu Seven, Begum Seyda Avci, Ali Ayvaz, Ecem Basak Kaya, Aysegul Sancaktar, Adnan Kuvvetli, Yusuf Yurumez, Furkan Bostanci, Ozgur Sogut, Adem Az, Muhammed Ikbal Sasmaz, Cemil Yildiz, Ferhat Icme, Melih Yuksel, Cemal Ilker Cander, Songul Dogan Arac, Mehmet Akif Karamercan, Sinan Karacabey, Mehmet Birkan Korgan, Huseyin Cahit Halhalli, Sezer Avci, Huseyin Acar, Muhammed Semih Gedik, Muhammed Ali Guler, Metin Yadigaroğlu, Mehmet Emir Akcay, Taha Yasar Kiraz, Deniz Kilic, Ramazan Guven, Zeynep Yilmaz, Suna Eraybar, Evren Dal, Huseyin Avni Demir, Satuk Bugra Yapici

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to assess nutritional risk in older adults admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) from the emergency department (ED) and to examine its relationship with 30-day mortality by comparing nutritional assessment tools with physiological severity scores.MethodThe study was conducted simultaneously across 20 EDs. Patients aged 65 years and older who presented to the ED and were subsequently admitted to the ICU were included. Nutritional risk scores including the Nutritional Risk Screening-2002 (NRS-2002), Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI), Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST), and modified Nutrition Risk in the Critically Ill (mNUTRIC) and clinical severity scores, including the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) were calculated using parameters obtained at ED presentation. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of 30-day mortality.ResultsA total of 150 patients were included, with a 30-day mortality rate of 21.3%. In multivariate logistic regression analysis, SOFA score independently predicted 30-day mortality (OR: 1.30 per point increase, 95% CI: 1.03–1.63; p = 0.028). ROC analysis showed excellent discriminative ability for SOFA (AUC = 0.87), APACHE II (AUC = 0.85), and mNUTRIC (AUC = 0.83), while GNRI demonstrated good predictive performance (AUC = 0.79). NRS-2002 (AUC = 0.67) and MUST (AUC = 0.66) showed poor discriminative ability. DeLong comparisons showed no significant differences among SOFA, APACHE II, mNUTRIC, and GNRI (all p > 0.05), while these scores performed significantly better than NRS-2002 and MUST (p < 0.01).ConclusionAmong the nutritional assessment tools evaluated, the mNUTRIC score demonstrated good discriminative ability for predicting 30-day mortality in geriatric ICU patients admitted from the ED. However, among all evaluated parameters, the SOFA score was the only independent predictor of 30-day mortality.Keywords: Malnutrition, Geriatric, Mortality

Introduction

Malnutrition is associated with higher morbidity and mortality, longer hospital stays, more complications, and poorer quality of life [1]. Older adults are particularly vulnerable to malnutrition due to age-related physiological decline, reduced muscle mass, limited metabolic reserves, chronic comorbidities, inadequate nutritional intake, and difficulties in accessing food [2]. Therefore, early and systematic nutritional assessment in older adults is essential for appropriate management and prevention of malnutrition.

Several studies have demonstrated the prognostic value of nutritional screening tools in critically ill and older adults populations. Higher nutritional risk assessed by Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI), Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002), Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST), and Modified Nutrition Risk in the Critically Ill (mNUTRIC) has been associated with increased mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and adverse clinical outcomes [1, 3–7].

Because nutritional risk alone may not fully reflect disease severity, physiological severity scores are commonly used to estimate prognosis in critically ill patients. The Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score assesses disease severity based on clinical and physiological parameters [8], while the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score evaluates organ dysfunction and has been associated with mortality in intensive care unit (ICU) patients [9].

Studies conducted in emergency department settings have reported that older adults with poor nutritional status are at higher risk of short- and long-term mortality and prolonged hospitalization [10]. These findings suggest that early risk assessment at the emergency department (ED) admission may provide valuable prognostic information and facilitate timely interventions for high-risk older patients. However, evidence comparing nutritional screening tools with established physiological severity scores in older adults admitted to the ICU from the ED remains limited.

In recent years, increasing patient volumes in EDs have led to delays in inpatient ward and ICU admissions, in some cases extending for days or even weeks. Older adults at risk of malnutrition may therefore experience prolonged stays in the ED, underscoring the need for early nutritional risk assessment in this setting. The aim of this study was to evaluate nutritional risk at ED admission among patients subsequently admitted to the ICU from the ED and to investigate its association with mortality by comparing nutritional risk assessment tools with established physiological severity scores.

Methods

This study was designed as a multicenter, prospective cohort study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of XXX City Training and Research Hospital (Approval No. 288, dated January 2, 2025). The study was conducted across 20 EDs in Türkiye, including 7 university hospitals and 13 city training and research hospitals. Patient enrollment was initiated simultaneously at all participating centers on February 15, 2025, at 00:00 and concluded on the same day at 23:59:59.

Patients aged 65 years and older who presented to the ED and were subsequently admitted to the ICU were included in the study. Patients younger than 65 years, those presenting with trauma-related conditions, and those not admitted to the ICU were excluded. For all included patients, demographic characteristics, vital signs, laboratory parameters, diagnoses, treatments, and outcomes were recorded at the time of ED presentation. Nutritional and clinical assessment scores were calculated using data obtained during the ED evaluation.

Nutritional risk was assessed using four screening tools: the Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002) [11], the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) [12], the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST) [7], and the modified Nutrition Risk in the Critically Ill (mNUTRIC) score [4]. The NRS-2002 evaluates nutritional risk by considering both nutritional status and disease severity. A total score of ≥ 3 points indicates the presence of nutritional risk. The GNRI is a nutritional assessment index calculated from serum albumin concentration and the ratio of actual body weight to ideal body weight. Lower GNRI values indicate a higher risk of malnutrition and poorer nutritional status. The MUST assesses malnutrition risk using three components: body mass index (BMI), unintentional weight loss, and the effect of acute disease associated with reduced nutritional intake. Based on the total score, patients are classified as having low, medium, or high nutritional risk. The mNUTRIC score is a nutritional risk assessment tool specifically developed for critically ill patients. It incorporates age, number of comorbidities, hospital length of stay before ICU admission, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score [8], and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score [13]. Higher mNUTRIC scores indicate greater nutritional risk and a higher likelihood of benefiting from aggressive nutritional support. Disease severity and organ dysfunction were evaluated using the APACHE II and SOFA scoring systems, respectively. The APACHE II score assesses illness severity based on acute physiological variables, age, and chronic health conditions, whereas the SOFA score quantifies the extent of organ dysfunction across multiple organ systems. For both scales, higher scores reflect greater disease severity and worse clinical status.

Anthropometric and nutritional data (weight, height, recent weight loss, and oral intake status) required for the calculation of NRS-2002, MUST, GNRI, and mNUTRIC scores were obtained from electronic medical records, direct patient interviews when possible, and information provided by relatives or caregivers.

The primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality. Mortality data were obtained from electronic medical records and through telephone contact with patients or their relatives to confirm survival status. Written informed consent was obtained from patients or their legally authorized representatives prior to inclusion in the study.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). There were no missing data for the variables included in the analyses, therefore, no imputation methods were applied. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. Comparisons between groups were conducted using the Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables. For post hoc analyses, Scheffé or Games–Howell tests were applied for normally distributed data depending on variance homogeneity, whereas Bonferroni-adjusted Mann–Whitney U tests were used for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

Variables found to be significant in univariate analyses and those considered clinically relevant based on previous literature were included in the multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of 30-day mortality. Given the limited number of events, a single multivariable model including all clinically relevant variables was constructed, and results were interpreted cautiously. The model included age, nutritional risk scores (NRS-2002, GNRI, MUST, mNUTRIC), and severity scores (SOFA and APACHE II). To reduce model overfitting due to the limited number of outcome events, the number of predictors was restricted based on clinical relevance and sample size considerations.

Multicollinearity was assessed among all variables included in the multivariable model using correlation analysis and variance inflation factors (VIF), with particular attention to the expected collinearity between mNUTRIC, SOFA, and APACHE II scores. Additionally, pairwise comparisons of correlated ROC curves were performed using the DeLong method in Jamovi (Version 2.7) with the snowCluster module.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Our study revealed that the 30-day mortality rate was 21.3%. Low albumin levels and a history of malignancy were associated with increased mortality. In addition, elevated inflammatory markers, including CRP and procalcitonin, were significantly higher among non-survivors. Nutritional risk scores (NRS-2002, GNRI, MUST, and mNUTRIC) demonstrated significant associations with 30-day mortality in the ED population.

Malnutrition in older adults is closely associated with increased functional dependency, higher hospitalization rates, morbidity, and mortality [2, 10]. The 2023 Geriatric Guideline of the ESPEN recommends that malnutrition screening be performed promptly in all older patients, particularly during ED visits [14]. However, the number of prospective, multicenter studies investigating the relationship between malnutrition and mortality specifically in the ED setting remains limited. Most existing research conducts nutritional risk assessments only after admission to inpatient wards or ICUs [14]. In our study, one of the biochemical markers most strongly associated with mortality was serum albumin level. Low albumin values not only indicate inadequate protein intake but also reflect systemic inflammation. Similarly, in a study by Schuetz et al., low albumin levels were reported to increase the 30-day mortality risk by 2.4-fold in patients aged ≥ 65 years presenting to the ED [15]. Malnutrition in older adults is a complex condition that develops through the synergistic effects of inflammatory processes, reduced oral intake, polypharmacy, sarcopenia, and multiple comorbidities [14, 16]. Low albumin levels and elevated CRP reflect a catabolic inflammatory response. Inflammation increases anabolic resistance and accelerates muscle proteolysis, which together with sarcopenia, increases mortality risk [16, 17]. In the ED setting, prolonged waiting times and delays in oral intake can exacerbate protein–energy deficits [18]. In line with the hypothesis of our study, identifying nutritional risk in the ED and initiating early nutritional support may be beneficial to breaking this vicious cycle. There is also evidence in the literature indicating that brief nutritional interventions administered in the ED can reduce CRP and procalcitonin levels within 72 h and shorten the length of hospital stay [19].

Numerous studies investigating the prognostic value of the GNRI have reported findings consistent with our results. Woo et al. demonstrated that older adults with a GNRI < 82 exhibited significantly higher 30-day and 6-month mortality rates [20]. In line with these observations, in our study, patients with lower GNRI scores showed higher mortality, suggesting that nutritional status may have a prognostic role in the geriatric population. However, it should be noted that GNRI includes serum albumin, which is influenced not only by nutritional status but also by systemic inflammation, disease severity, capillary leakage, and organ dysfunction in acutely ill patients. Therefore, the association between GNRI and mortality may partly reflect the overall inflammatory and physiological burden rather than nutritional status alone.

The NRS-2002 and MUST scores are the screening tools recommended by ESPEN [11]. In our study, higher NRS-2002 scores were associated with increased mortality, suggesting that this tool may have clinical utility in the ED setting, although its discriminative performance was lower compared to other severity scores [1, 21].

The EFFORT randomized controlled trial demonstrated that early and individualized nutritional support in hospitalized patients reduced mortality by 21% [15]. This finding supports our hypothesis emphasizing the importance of initiating nutritional therapy without delay. Similarly, the ESPEN guidelines recommend starting nutritional support within the first 24 h whenever possible, noting that delays may lead to adverse outcomes [17].

In a study conducted by Kollu et al., the SOFA score was reported to have the highest predictive value for mortality among ICU patients, followed by the mNUTRIC and APACHE II scores, respectively [22]. In our study, SOFA and APACHE II were included as severity-of-illness benchmarks. The findings of our study are broadly consistent with these results. In our analysis, SOFA, APACHE II, mNUTRIC, and GNRI showed comparable discriminative ability for mortality prediction, whereas NRS-2002 and MUST demonstrated lower performance. In addition, univariate analyses showed that GNRI scores were significantly lower among non-survivors, while SOFA, mNUTRIC, MUST, NRS-2002, and APACHE II scores were significantly higher in this group.

Several studies evaluating the association between malnutrition risk and mortality have highlighted the prognostic value of the mNUTRIC score. Rahman et al. reported that the mNUTRIC score strongly predicted mortality among intensive care patients [23]. It should also be acknowledged that the mNUTRIC score incorporates components of both the APACHE II and SOFA scores. Therefore, although multicollinearity was not statistically significant in our analyses, comparisons among these scoring systems should be interpreted with awareness of their inherent conceptual overlap. In our study, mNUTRIC, MUST, and NRS-2002 scores were significantly higher in the mortality group, supporting an association between nutritional risk and short-term mortality. However, in the multivariable analysis, only the SOFA score remained statistically significant. This finding suggests that, after adjustment for other variables, physiological severity may have a stronger independent association with short-term mortality in the ED setting. Nevertheless, the observed associations between nutritional scores and mortality in univariate analyses indicate that nutritional status also contributes to risk stratification.

Limitations

The strength of this study lies in its multicenter and prospective design, which enhances external validity. In addition, the simultaneous use of multiple nutritional assessment tools (NRS-2002, GNRI, MUST, and mNUTRIC) enabled a comprehensive evaluation of different dimensions of malnutrition. However, several limitations should be acknowledged.

Long-term mortality outcomes (3-month, 6-month, and 1-year) could not be assessed. In addition, as nutritional and clinical measurements were obtained at a single time point, the dynamic course of malnutrition could not be evaluated. Among patients receiving nutritional support, the duration and content of the interventions were not recorded. Furthermore, frailty and functional status measures were not assessed.

The study cohort included all eligible patients admitted from the emergency department to the intensive care unit during the predefined study period; however, no formal sample size or power calculation was performed a priori. The relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power of multivariable analyses and model comparisons.

Patient recruitment over a single 24-hour period may introduce temporal selection bias and limit generalizability due to potential seasonal, weekday/weekend, and inter-centre variability.

Although no significant multicollinearity was detected based on VIF and tolerance analyses, mNUTRIC, APACHE II, and SOFA scores share overlapping physiological components; therefore, some degree of conceptual redundancy cannot be excluded.

Finally, the relatively low number of mortality events relative to the number of predictors may have increased the risk of overfitting, and no internal validation procedure was performed. Therefore, the predictive performance and stability of the models should be interpreted with caution and confirmed in larger external validation cohorts.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the importance of early assessment of nutritional risk in older adults presenting to the emergency department. Low albumin, elevated CRP, higher mNUTRIC scores, and higher SOFA scores were associated with increased short-term mortality. These findings suggest that combining nutritional and severity scores may have prognostic value for risk stratification in geriatric ED patients. Further adequately powered prospective studies are needed to validate these findings.
