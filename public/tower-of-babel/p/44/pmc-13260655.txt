Population Size Differences Can Lead to Biases in Phylogenetic Inference and Introgression Detection in the Presence of Purifying Selection
Authors: Chong He, Meng-Yun Chen, Hao Zhu, Siavash Mirarab
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAssumptions about the probability distribution of gene tree topologies provide a basis for phylogenetic reconstruction and introgression detection. Initial evidence has suggested that in the presence of purifying selection, population size differences can affect the probability distribution of gene tree topologies. Nevertheless, the impact of this phenomenon on phylogenetic reconstruction and introgression detection remains to be explored. Additionally, a theoretical understanding of this phenomenon remains absent. Here, using the population genetic simulation software SLiM, we provide evidence that in the presence of purifying selection, population size differences can cause biases in phylogenetic inference. We also provide evidence that in the presence of purifying selection, population size differences can cause statistics used for introgression detection to exhibit patterns resembling those caused by introgression. Additionally, we present a theoretical analysis showing that using a random gene tree labeling process to model the gene tree formation process in the occurrence of deep coalescence lacks a biological basis under purifying selection, and gene tree distributions under purifying selection are expected to exhibit population size dependence. This work underscores the importance of considering the potential confounding impact of purifying selection on phylogenetic inference and introgression detection.Keywords: Gene tree distribution, species tree, purifying selection, phylogeny, introgression

A fundamental question in evolutionary biology is the relationship between gene trees and species trees. Given a species tree, the topology of gene trees is not necessarily concordant with the species tree topology. “Deep coalescence,” also known as “incomplete lineage sorting” (ILS), is the most common cause of discordant gene trees (Takahata 1989; Maddison 1997; Degnan and Rosenberg 2009). If there is a pair of gene copies drawn from different species/populations, and their most recent common ancestor (MRCA) is not in the most recent common ancestral species of their source species, but instead in an earlier ancestral species, this situation is referred to as deep coalescence (Fig. 1). If neutral evolution is assumed and deep coalescence is the sole cause of discordant gene trees, the multispecies coalescent (MSC) model can be used to describe the random process that generates gene trees. The MSC model relies on a property that exists in ancestor–descendant relationships under neutral evolution: When a gene copy traces its ancestry back to an earlier generation, its ancestry is equally likely to be traced back to any ancestral gene copy in that earlier generation (Takahata 1989; Maddison 1997; Degnan and Rosenberg 2009). Essentially, gene trees can be viewed as compound genealogical trees composed of multiple single-population genealogies in different species (Fig. 1). In the MSC model, Kingman’s coalescent process is used to model the time of the MRCA (Kingman 1982; Tajima 1983), thereby determining the shapes of single-population genealogies constituting gene trees, in particular, whether deep coalescence occurs (Takahata 1989; Maddison 1997; Degnan and Rosenberg 2009). The Kingman’s coalescent process is based on the aforementioned property in ancestor–descendant relationships under neutral evolution. Not only that, in the MSC model, the gene tree formation process in the occurrence of deep coalescence is modeled by a process where species labels occur with equal probability at each tip of gene trees (Takahata 1989; Degnan and Rosenberg 2006; Degnan and Rosenberg 2009). This gene tree labeling process essentially assumes that single-population genealogies are completely randomly connected together to form gene trees, which is also based on the aforementioned property in ancestor–descendant relationships under neutral evolution (Fig. 1). Overall, in the MSC model, not only the occurrence of deep coalescence but also the gene tree formation process in the occurrence of deep coalescence stems from the assumption of neutral evolution.

Illustration of the probability distribution of three-taxon gene tree topologies under the MSC model. In the case shown here, if the MRCA of g1 and g2 is not in the most recent common ancestral species of S1 and S2 (S12), but instead in an earlier ancestral species (S123), deep coalescence occurs. In the occurrence of deep coalescence, the ancestries of g1, g2, and g3 can be traced back to three ancestral gene copies in S123. This process can be viewed as connecting g1, g2, and g3 to a three-tip genealogical tree in S123. In the MSC model, because neutral evolution is assumed, g1, g2, and g3 are completely randomly connected to the tips of this genealogical tree in S123. This random connecting process can be viewed as a process where labels g1, g2, and g3 occur with equal probability at each tip of gene trees. Because of this gene tree labeling process, the probability distribution of three-taxon gene tree topologies is always symmetric under the MSC model.

Under the MSC model, the probability distribution of three-taxon gene tree topologies is always symmetric: The two discordant gene tree topologies always have equal probabilities, and their probabilities never surpass the probability of the concordant gene tree topology (Fig. 1). Symmetry in three-taxon gene tree distributions is fundamental for many evolutionary analyses. First, in phylogenetic studies, it is common to interpret the most frequent gene tree topology as evidence of the true species tree topology (Nesi et al. 2021; Knyshov et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2025). Although it has been shown that the gene tree topology with the highest probability can be discordant with the true species tree topology, under the MSC model, this phenomenon cannot occur in the three-taxon case (Degnan and Rosenberg 2006; Degnan and Rosenberg 2009). Therefore, in the three-taxon case, the most frequent gene tree topology is still an important line of evidence of the true species tree topology. Many phylogenetic inference methods depend on this fact (Liu et al. 2010; Chifman and Kubatko 2014; Mirarab et al. 2014). Second, the MSC model assumes that deep coalescence is the sole cause of discordant gene trees (Takahata 1989; Degnan and Rosenberg 2009; Solís-Lemus et al. 2016). The presence of introgression violates this assumption and can cause the two discordant gene tree topologies to occur with different probabilities. This provides a reason to employ the difference between the frequencies of the two discordant gene tree topologies to investigate evidence of introgression (Green et al. 2010; Durand et al. 2011; Hibbins and Hahn 2022).

The practices mentioned above fundamentally share a common assumption: If deep coalescence is the sole cause of discordant gene trees, the probability distribution of gene tree topologies should be consistent with the expectations under the MSC model. This assumption necessitates that the probabilities of three-taxon gene tree topologies are always equal in the occurrence of deep coalescence. In the presence of selection, different types of alleles can have different probabilities of leaving descendants in the next generation (Neuhauser and Krone 1997; Barton and Etheridge 2004). This property in ancestor–descendant relationships is in contrast to the aforementioned property that exists in ancestor–descendant relationships under neutral evolution. Therefore, it is questionable whether three-taxon gene tree topologies under purifying selection necessarily have an equal probability in the occurrence of deep coalescence.

The most common type of selection is purifying selection (Ohta 1992; Eyre-Walker and Keightley 2007). However, purifying selection is rarely considered a potential confounding factor in evolutionary analyses. A previous study has provided simulation evidence that under purifying selection, population size differences between species can cause asymmetric gene tree distributions, which is unexpected under the MSC model (He et al. 2020). Nevertheless, the understanding of the effect of purifying selection on gene tree distributions is still rudimentary. First, the previous study of He et al. (2020) focused on true gene trees. However, true gene trees are not directly used in practice. More direct evidence is required to shed light on the impact of purifying selection on evolutionary biology practice. Second, the replicability of the phenomenon observed by He et al. (2020) has been greeted with skepticism. In another study, Vanderpool and the co-authors stated that using the population genetics simulation software, SLiM (Haller and Messer 2023), with parameters comparable to those used by He et al. (2020), they could not replicate the patterns observed by He et al. (Vanderpool et al. 2020). This gives the impression that the phenomenon observed by He et al. (2020) is a model-specific phenomenon or an artifact caused by programming errors. Third, all simulations depend on a population genetics model. By comparison, a theoretical analysis independent of any specific population genetics model does not have this problem and therefore can provide a more fundamental understanding of the effect of purifying selection on gene tree distributions. No such theoretical analysis has been proposed. Additionally, theoretical efforts are required to elucidate the pattern of change of gene tree distributions under purifying selection.

In the present work, we used SLiM to simulate gene trees under purifying selection. Our simulations aimed to reveal the potential impact of the phenomenon observed by He et al. (2020) on phylogenetic inference and introgression detection. In comparison with the previous work of He et al. (2020), the present SLiM simulations were performed with settings where both slightly and strongly deleterious mutations, as well as neutral mutations, can occur, instead of settings where only one type of mutation can occur. Furthermore, the present simulations generated nucleotide sequences and genetic variant data for phylogenetic inference and introgression detection instead of merely true gene trees. Therefore, the present SLiM simulations can provide more direct practical implications than the previous work of He et al. (2020). Additionally, we show that unlike what was claimed by Vanderpool et al. (2020), forward-in-time simulations based on SLiM produce results consistent with the simulations of He et al. (2020), which are based on a backward-in-time model, the ancestral selection graph (ASG) model (Neuhauser and Krone 1997). Vanderpool et al. (2020) could not observe a phenomenon similar to He et al. (2020) because their simulations were performed under a combination of settings that is not comparable to any of those used by He et al. (2020). The simulations mentioned above provide evidence that the phenomenon reported by He et al. (2020) is unlikely to be an artifact, and this phenomenon should not be overlooked in evolutionary studies. In addition to simulations, we present a theoretical analysis showing that the gene tree labeling process assumed in the MSC model rests on a premise that is biologically untenable under purifying selection. This conclusion does not rely on any specific population genetics model. We thereby elucidate why gene tree distributions inconsistent with the expectations under the MSC model can occur in the presence of purifying selection. Finally, we theoretically determine the pattern of change of three-taxon gene tree distributions under purifying selection. The theoretically deduced pattern agrees well with our simulation results, supporting the correctness of both our simulation and theoretical analyses.

Materials and Methods

Species Tree and Population Sizes Used in the Simulations

For demonstration, we used the simplest four-taxon species tree in our simulations, which contains three ingroup species and one outgroup species (Fig. 2). The three ingroup species are denoted by S1, S2, and S3, and the outgroup species is denoted by Sout. The gene copies sampled from S1, S2, S3, and Sout are denoted by g1, g2, g3, and gout, respectively. The topology of the species tree excluding the outgroup species is ((S1, S2), S3). In this species tree, the common ancestral species of S1 and S2 is denoted by S12, and the common ancestral species of S1, S2, and S3 is denoted by S123 (Fig. 2). The population sizes of S1, S2, S3, S12, and S123 are denoted by N1, N2, N3, N12, and N123, respectively. The three possible gene tree topologies—((g1, g2), g3), ((g1, g3), g2), and ((g2, g3), g1)—are denoted by T1, T2, and T3, respectively. Among these three gene tree topologies, T1 is concordant with the species tree topology, T2 and T3 are discordant with the species tree topology (Fig. 2). The branch lengths of the species tree were set up as shown in Figure 2. As deep coalescence is still the sole cause of discordant gene trees in the presence of purifying selection, a high probability of deep coalescence was set in our simulations to demonstrate the potential confounding impact of purifying selection on phylogenetic inference. In the real world, such a situation can occur in the presence of rapid radiations (Nesi et al. 2021; Stiller et al. 2024). Furthermore, as will be seen later, under neutral evolution, the true species tree can still be correctly recovered by commonly used phylogenetic reconstruction methods, indicating that the species tree set in our simulations is not an extreme case (Fig. 3).

The impact of purifying selection on gene tree distributions. The gene tree distributions generated under neutral evolution are similar across different population size combinations. These gene tree distributions are consistent with the expectations under the MSC model. By comparison, the gene tree distributions generated under purifying selection are affected by population size differences. In particular, under population size combinations B–E, the gene tree distributions generated under purifying selection are distinct from the expectations under the MSC model.

The impact of purifying selection on phylogenetic inference. The bar graphs show the number of inferred phylogenetic trees that support each possible topology. The mean bootstrap value of each possible topology is shown in parentheses. In the presence of purifying selection, under population size combinations B–E, a false topology is supported by most of the inferred phylogenetic trees, and such a false topology exhibits a mean bootstrap value higher than the true species tree topology.

The population sizes of ancestral species S123 and S12 were set to N123 = N12 = 400. We examined five population size combinations of S1, S2, and S3 in our simulations (Fig. 2). In population size combination A, the population sizes of all the species were identical. In population size combinations B–E, N1, N2, and N3 were 3-/5-fold larger or smaller in comparison with N123 and N12 (Fig. 2). The detailed proportions of N1, N2, N3, N12, and N123 are shown in Figure 2. Generally, the changes in population size of combinations B and D are opposite to those of combination C and E, and the population size differences of combination D and E are greater than those of combinations B and C (Fig. 2). 3-/5-fold increases/decreases in population size are not rare in reality (Hobolth et al. 2007; Schrago 2014). Therefore, the proportions of N123, N12, N1, N2, and N3 set in our simulations are within the realms of possibility. Admittedly, the population sizes set in our simulations (e.g., N123 = N12 = 400) are relatively small in comparison with real populations. Nevertheless, it does not mean that our simulations are unrealistic. Theoretically, our simulations are comparable to those with n-fold larger population sizes, n-fold lower mutation rates, and n-fold lower selection coefficients, because the relative densities of coalescence, mutation, and advantage-disadvantage replacement events are comparable before and after this parameter scaling (Neuhauser and Krone 1997). As described below, the scaled mutation rate and the scaled selection coefficients set in our simulations are within the realms of possibility. If the population sizes used in our simulations increase 100–1000-fold, and the mutation rate and the selection coefficients decrease 100–1000-fold, they become more realistic (Bergeron et al. 2021; Bergeron et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2024). Therefore, our simulation scenarios can be rescaled into more realistic scenarios. Admittedly, scaled simulations may somewhat deviate from unscaled simulations (Dabi and Schrider 2025). Nonetheless, the objective of the simulations described here is to demonstrate whether changes in phylogenetic inference and introgression detection results can occur under purifying selection scenarios where the fates of deleterious mutations are biologically plausible. Scaled simulations still share similarity with unscaled simulations, and the fates of deleterious mutations in scaled simulations remain biologically plausible (Dabi and Schrider 2025). Therefore, in spite of the potential impact of scaling, our simulation design remains aligned with its objective (in the theoretical part, we explain why the fates of deleterious mutations are related to gene tree distributions). Furthermore, in Supplementary Figure S1, we demonstrate a comparison of results of different levels of scaling. The gene tree topology frequencies under different levels of scaling are similar, indicating that scaling has little impact on our result interpretation. The reason why we used relatively small population sizes is that the use of relatively small population sizes can reduce the computational resources and time required for simulations. With our settings, it took about one month on a 72-core machine to produce the results presented in the main text (the result presented in Supplementary Figure S1 took about three months, which is just one of the many combinations of settings shown in the main text). The scripts used in this study can be found in the Dryad data repository described in the “Data Availability.”

Examining the Impact of Purifying Selection on Phylogenetic Inference

Our simulations were carried out using SLiM (Haller and Messer 2023, version 4.0.1). Two sets of simulations were performed: one with all mutations being strictly neutral and the other with a fraction of mutations subject to purifying selection. In the simulations under neutral evolution, the selection coefficients of mutations were all zero. In the simulations under purifying selection, 2/3 of mutations had negative selection coefficients, that is, they were deleterious mutations, and the remaining 1/3 were strictly neutral mutations. In our simulations, the proportion of deleterious mutations was set to 2/3 because nonsynonymous mutations usually occur at the first and second codon positions in the coding sequences (CDS) of protein-coding genes, and they are often deleterious. The selection coefficients of deleterious mutations followed a gamma distribution with a shape parameter α = 0.25 and a mean . The dominant coefficient was set to h = 0.5. Accordingly, the mean scaled selection coefficient of deleterious mutations in ancestral species S123 was in our simulations. In a previous empirical study, the mean scaled selection coefficient of human nonsynonymous mutations was estimated to be 113 or 425 depending on the assumed demographic model (Eyre-Walker et al. 2006). Between these two estimates, 425 is a more plausible estimate than 113 because it is the result accounting for demographic changes. In our simulations, the mean scaled selection coefficient of deleterious mutations was arbitrarily set to a value smaller than 425 but greater than 113, that is, 220. Therefore, the strength of purifying selection exerted on deleterious mutations in our simulations should be weaker than that exerted on human nonsynonymous mutations, yet it still falls within the realms of possibility. Under the aforementioned gamma distribution set for deleterious mutations, ∼20% of deleterious mutations have an absolute value of the scaled selection coefficient | σ123 | < 1, that is to say, ∼20% of deleterious mutations in our simulations were very slightly deleterious mutations, which is comparable to the empirical estimate of humans (Eyre-Walker et al. 2006). In our simulations, the occurrence of mutations was assumed to follow the JC69 model, and the mutation rate was μ = 2.4 × 10–6 per generation per nucleotide. Programs for gene tree estimation often assume that no recombination occurs within each gene (Stamatakis 2014; Minh et al. 2020). Model misspecification in gene tree estimation is not the topic of this paper. Therefore, in the simulations described in this section, the recombination rate was set to 0 within genes. In our simulations, deleterious and strictly neutral mutations correspond roughly to nonsynonymous and synonymous mutations, respectively. Synonymous mutations are not all strictly neutral mutations. Therefore, the strength of purifying selection assumed in our simulations should be comparable to or somewhat weaker than that in the real world.

Twenty replicated simulations were performed for each combination of settings. Each replicate contained 9000 independent genes of length 1000 bp. Using SLiM, the nucleotide sequences of the four sampled gene copies were generated for each gene. Concurrent with the generation of nucleotide sequences, a “tree-sequence recording” file was also generated for each gene. The true gene tree of each gene was extracted from this “tree-sequence recording” file. For each gene, the nucleotide sequences generated by SLiM were used as input for the phylogenetic program IQTREE to generate a reconstructed gene tree. The substitution model assumed in IQTREE was the JC69 model, which was consistent with what was assumed in the SLiM simulations described above. Those reconstructed gene trees whose topology was supported by a bootstrap value > 50% were used as input for ASTRAL to carry out coalescent-based phylogenetic inference. Additionally, in each simulation replicate, the nucleotide sequences of the 9000 simulated genes were concatenated into a supermatrix. This supermatrix was then used as input for IQTREE to carry out concatenation-based phylogenetic inference. The substitution model assumed in concatenation-based phylogenetic inference was also the JC69 model.

Examining the Impact of Purifying Selection on Introgression Detection

Two statistics used for introgression detection—the D-statistic and the Δ-statistic—were considered (Green et al. 2010; Durand et al. 2011; Hibbins and Hahn 2022). D-statistics were calculated from the VCF files simulated by SLiM. In the SLiM simulations that generated VCF files, the species tree and the mutation rate were the same as in the SLiM simulations described in the previous section. Five individuals were sampled from each of the four extant species. Similar to the simulations described in the previous section, there were five population size combinations and two sets of simulations: one under neutral evolution and the other under purifying selection. In each combination of settings, the simulations were performed with 20 replicates, each containing 75 genomic segments of length ∼1 Mbp. Three types of mutations were allowed to occur in the simulations under purifying selection: strictly neutral mutations, exon deleterious mutations, and nonexon deleterious mutations. The selection coefficients of strictly neutral mutations were 0. The selection coefficients of exon deleterious mutations followed a gamma distribution with a shape parameter α = 0.25 and a mean , which is identical to the gamma distribution described in the previous section. The selection coefficients of nonexon deleterious mutations followed a gamma distribution with a shape parameter α = 0.25 and a mean . There were three types of regions in the simulations under purifying selection: exons, introns, and intergenic regions. The average lengths of exons, introns, and intergenic regions were set to 170, 5500, and 12,000 bp, respectively, which were based on previous studies on the human genome (Shabalina 2001; Sakharkar et al. 2004). The number of exons followed a Poisson distribution with a mean of 9. In exons, 2/3 of mutations were exon deleterious mutations, and the remaining 1/3 were strictly neutral mutations. In introns, 20% of mutations were nonexon deleterious mutations, and the remaining 80% were strictly neutral mutations. In intergenic regions, 5% of mutations were nonexon deleterious mutations, and the remaining 95% were strictly neutral mutations. In the simulations described in this section, the recombination rate was set to r = 2.4 × 10–6 per generation per nucleotide.

For each simulated genomic segment, the VCF files of the four species were merged into a single VCF file using the “merge” command in bcftools. The merged VCF files of the 75 simulated genomic segments were used as input for the “DtriosParallel” command in the Dsuite package to calculate the D-statistic (Malinsky et al. 2021). The reference tree used in Dsuite was the true species tree set in our SLiM simulations. The D-statistic was defined as negative when S1 was in the “P2” position and S3 was in the “P3” position in the result of Dsuite. Conversely, the D-statistic was defined as positive when S2 was in the “P2” position and S3 was in the “P3” position in the result of Dsuite. A negative D-statistic is usually interpreted as evidence of introgression between S1 and S3, whereas a positive D-statistic is usually interpreted as evidence of introgression between S2 and S3.

We also performed an analysis to examine the impact of purifying selection on phylogenetic-based introgression detection. We calculated the Δ-statistic using the reconstructed gene trees generated in the simulations described in the previous section. Those reconstructed gene trees whose topology was supported by a bootstrap value > 50% were used in the calculation of the Δ-statistic. The Δ-statistic was calculated as , where k3 denotes the number of gene trees with topology ((g2, g3), g1), and k2 denotes the number of gene trees with topology ((g1, g3), g2). A negative Δ-statistic indicates an excess of topology ((g1, g3), g2), which is usually interpreted as evidence of introgression between S1 and S3. Conversely, a positive Δ-statistic value indicates an excess of topology ((g2, g3), g1), which is usually interpreted as evidence of introgression between S2 and S3. To examine whether the frequencies of the two discordant gene tree topologies significantly deviate from 0.5:0.5, we performed a χ2 goodness-of-fit test. This test was implemented by the “chisquare” function in the SciPy package.

Examining the Replicability of the Results of He et al. (2020)

As mentioned in the Introduction, Vanderpool et al. (2020) claimed that the phenomenon reported by He et al. (2020) is not replicable when using SLiM. Here, we performed SLiM simulations with settings comparable to those used by He et al. (2020) to examine whether Vanderpool et al.’s claim is true. The simulations of He et al. (2020) were performed in a biallelic setting. Accordingly, the present SLiM simulations were also performed in a biallelic setting. This was done by following a recipe described in the manual of SLiM. The recipe we used was the second recipe described in the section “Modeling biallelic loci with a mutation() callback.”

In the simulations of He et al. (2020), the mutation rate was μ = 3 × 10–8 per generation per gene, and the population sizes of ancestral species S123 and S12 were N123 = 2 × 105 and N12 = 2 × 105, respectively. Additionally, individuals were haploid in the simulations of He et al. (2020). Hence, in the simulations of He et al. (2020), the scaled mutation parameter in S123 was θHe = μN123 = 6 × 10–3. In the present SLiM simulations, the mutation rate was set to μ = 1.5 × 10–5 per generation per nucleotide, the length of genes was set to 1 bp, and the population sizes of S123 and S12 were set to N123 = 200 and N12 = 200, respectively. Additionally, individuals were diploid in the present SLiM simulations. Hence, in the present SLiM simulations, the scaled mutation parameter in S123 was θSLiM = 2μN123 = 6 × 10–3, which was equal to θHe.

We compared our simulation results with the results generated with the selection coefficient s = −7.5 × 10–6 in the work of He et al. (2020). In the simulations of He et al. (2020), because individuals were haploid, when s = −7.5 × 10–6, the scaled selection coefficient in S123 was σHe = sN123 = −1.5. In the present SLiM simulations, because individuals were diploid, the scaled selection coefficient in S123 was σSLiM = 2hsN123, where h denotes the dominance coefficient, and it was set to h = 0.5. We set the selection coefficient to s = −0.0075 in the present SLiM simulations. Hence, in the present SLiM simulations, the scaled selection coefficient was σSLiM = −1.5, which was equal to σHe. Overall, in the present SLiM simulations, we made θSLiM and σSLiM equal to θHe and σHe, respectively. Therefore, the relative densities of coalescence, mutation, and advantage-disadvantage replacement events are comparable in the present SLiM simulations and those of He et al. (2020). The population sizes of S123, S12, S1, S2, and S3 in the present SLiM simulations were set in the same proportions as the six population size combinations used by He et al. (2020). Among these six population size combinations, the population size combination F, where N123: N12: N1: N2: N3 = 5:5:1:25:25, is the population size combination that Vanderpool et al. (2020) aimed to compare with. (However, as will be explained later, the population sizes used by Vanderpool et al. are not in the same ratios as this population size combination.) We also performed simulations under N123: N12: N1: N2: N3 = 5:5:1:25:25 using a different level of scaling. The results of different levels of scaling and that of He et al. (2020) are very similar, indicating that the use of scaling is suitable for our analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1).

In the previous simulations of He et al. (2020), if all the tips of a gene tree were in an identical allelic state, such a gene tree would be excluded from subsequent analyses. To completely copy the previous simulations of He et al. (2020), this gene tree exclusion process was also adopted in the present SLiM simulations. This gene tree exclusion process essentially mimics the process of excluding identical sites in practice, as the “gene” in the simulations of He et al. (2020) is roughly equivalent to the “site” in practice (because each “gene” only has two possible allelic states, and the mutation rate per “gene” is close to the mutation rate per site in practice). Additionally, the difference between gene tree distributions under purifying selection and those under neutral evolution cannot be attributed to this gene tree exclusion process because it was both incorporated in the simulations under purifying selection and those under neutral evolution. The above reasons collectively indicate that the SLiM simulations described here can be used to examine the replicability of the phenomenon reported by He et al. (2020).

Results

Population Size Differences Can Lead to Biases in Phylogenetic Inference in the Presence of Purifying Selection

In the present study, we performed SLiM simulations with a species tree containing three in-group species (S1, S2, and S3). Five combinations of the population sizes of S1, S2, and S3 (N1, N2, and N3) were set in our simulations (Fig. 2). In population size combination A, the population sizes of all the extant and ancestral species were identical (Fig. 2). On the other hand, in population size combinations B–E, there was a 3-/5-fold increase or decrease in N1, N2, and N3 in comparison with N123 (Fig. 2). We first demonstrate whether there exist some purifying selection scenarios that can cause gene tree distributions deviating from the expectations under the MSC model and subsequently cause biases in phylogenetic inference.

We began by comparing the true gene trees simulated under neutral evolution and those under purifying selection. In the true gene trees simulated under neutral evolution, the frequencies of the three gene tree topologies exhibited similar patterns across different population size combinations (Fig. 2a–e, first row). Specifically, under all five population size combinations, the concordant topology—T1—was the most frequent gene tree topology, and the two discordant topologies—T2 and T3—exhibited similar frequencies (Fig. 2a–e, first row). These observations are consistent with the expectations under the MSC model (Fig. 1). Furthermore, under neutral evolution, the observed frequencies of these gene tree topologies were close to what would be expected under the MSC model (Fig. 2a–e, first row). By comparison, in the true gene trees simulated under purifying selection, the three gene tree topologies exhibited distinctly different frequencies under different population size combinations (Fig. 2a–e, third row). The gene tree frequencies under purifying selection combined with population size combination A were still similar to those under neutral evolution (Fig. 2a, third row). However, purifying selection combined with population size combinations B–E led to gene tree frequencies distinct from those under neutral evolution (Fig. 2b–e, third row). In the presence of purifying selection, under population size combinations B and D, where N2 decreased whereas N1 and N3 increased in comparison with N123, gene tree topology T2, that is, ((g1, g3), g2), exhibited the highest frequency (Fig. 2b and d, third row); on the other hand, under population size combinations C and E, where N1, N2, and N3 changed in an opposite direction, gene tree topology T3, that is, ((g2, g3), g1), exhibited the highest frequency (Fig. 2c and e, third row). Additionally, the two discordant topologies exhibited different frequencies under these four purifying selection scenarios (Fig. 2b–e, third row). These phenomena are unexpected under the MSC model (Takahata 1989; Degnan and Salter 2005; Degnan and Rosenberg 2006). Furthermore, in the presence of purifying selection, population size combinations D and E led to greater deviations from the expectations under the MSC model in comparison with population size combinations B and C, as the population size differences are greater in the former two scenarios (Fig. 2b–e, third row). This phenomenon is also unexpected under the MSC model. The above results collectively demonstrate that in the presence of purifying selection, population size differences can cause changes in gene tree distributions.

We next compared the phylogenetic inference results under neutral evolution and those under purifying selection. First, the nucleotide sequences simulated by SLiM were used as input to generate reconstructed gene trees. We analyzed those reconstructed gene trees whose topology was supported by a bootstrap value >50%. More than 80% of these reconstructed gene trees correctly recovered the topologies of their true gene trees (Fig. 2a–e, second and fourth rows). The gene tree distributions observed in reconstructed gene trees were similar to those observed in true gene trees (Fig. 2a–e, second and fourth rows). Then, coalescent-based phylogenetic reconstruction was carried out using these reconstructed gene trees as input for ASTRAL (Mirarab and Warnow 2015). Under neutral evolution, the phylogenetic trees inferred by ASTRAL exhibited similar patterns across different population size combinations, and the most frequent topology in the inferred phylogenetic trees under all neutral evolution scenarios was the true species tree topology—((S1, S2), S3) (Fig. 3a–e, first row). By comparison, in the presence of purifying selection, the phylogenetic trees inferred by ASTRAL exhibited different patterns under different population size combinations (Fig. 3a–e, third row). In the presence of purifying selection, the most frequent topology in the inferred phylogenetic trees under population size combination A was still the true species tree topology (Fig. 3a, third row); however, the most frequent topology in the inferred phylogenetic trees under population size combinations B–E was a false topology (Fig. 3b–e, third row). Specifically, in the presence of purifying selection, topology ((S1, S3), S2) was supported by most of the inferred phylogenetic trees under population size combinations B and D (Fig. 3b and d, third row), and topology ((S2, S3), S1) was supported by most of the inferred phylogenetic trees under combinations C and E (Fig. 3c and e, third row). In addition to coalescent-based phylogenetic inference, we also performed concatenation-based phylogenetic inference for each simulation replicate. The phylogenetic trees inferred by the concatenation-based method exhibited patterns similar to those inferred by ASTRAL (Fig. 3, second and fourth rows). In particular, in the presence of purifying selection, under population size combinations B–E, a false topology was supported by most of the phylogenetic trees inferred by the concatenation-based method (Fig. 3b–e, fourth row). Furthermore, in the presence of purifying selection, regardless of using the coalescent-based or concatenation-based method, topology ((S1, S3), S2) under population size combinations B and D and topology ((S2, S3), S1) under population size combinations C and E exhibited mean bootstrap values higher than that of the true species tree topology (Fig. 3b–e, third row). These results demonstrate that in the presence of purifying selection, population size differences can cause biases in phylogenetic inference.

Population Size Differences Can Lead to Biases in Introgression Detection in the Presence of Purifying Selection

We also compared the introgression detection results under neutral evolution and those under purifying selection. The D-statistic is one of the most widely used statistics for introgression detection. This statistic is essentially an indicator of the difference between the probabilities of the two discordant gene tree topologies (Green et al. 2010; Durand et al. 2011; Hibbins and Hahn 2022). To calculate D-statistics, we simulated VCF files using SLiM. Then, D-statistics were calculated from the simulated VCF files using the program Dsuite (Malinsky et al. 2021). Ideally, in the absence of introgression, D-statistics should not be biased toward negative or positive values. Consistent with this expectation, under neutral evolution, D-statistics exhibited similar patterns across different population size combinations, and they did not exhibit any bias toward positive or negative values (Fig. 4a–e, first row). By comparison, in the presence of purifying selection, D-statistics exhibited different patterns across the five population size combinations. In the presence of purifying selection, the D-statistics under population size combination A did not exhibit a bias toward negative or positive values (Fig. 4a, third row). However, under purifying selection combined with population size combinations B–E, the D-statistics exhibited patterns distinct from those under neutral evolution (Fig. 4b–e, third row). For example, the D-statistics under purifying selection combined with population size combination B were smaller than zero in 19 out of the 20 replicates (Fig. 4b, third row). It is unlikely to observe such a result if D-statistics only have a probability of 0.5 of being smaller than zero (P = 2 × 10–5, one-tailed binomial test). Additionally, the P-values reported by Dsuite were smaller than 0.05 in 7 out of the 20 replicates under this scenario. It is unlikely to observe such a result if D-statistics only have a probability of 0.05 of reaching the threshold of statistical significance (P = 3.4 × 10–5, one-tailed binomial test). These results indicate that the D-statistics under purifying selection combined with population size combination B were biased toward negative values (Fig. 4b, third row). By comparison, the D-statistics under purifying selection combined with population size combination C exhibited a bias toward positive values (Fig. 4c, third row). Furthermore, in the presence of purifying selection, as the population size differences became greater, the D-statistics under population size combinations D and E exhibited greater tendencies toward negative and positive values, respectively, in comparison with those under population size combinations B and C (Fig. 4d and e, third row).

The impact of purifying selection on introgression detection. An asterisk in the results of the Δ-statistic indicates that the observed ratio between the two discordant gene tree topologies significantly deviates from 0.5:0.5 (P < 0.05, χ2 goodness-of-fit test). Under neutral evolution, regardless of the population sizes set in the simulations, there is no tendency in the D-statistic or the Δ-statistic toward negative or positive values. By comparison, under purifying selection combined with population size differences, the D-statistic and the Δ-statistic are biased toward negative or positive values, which are often interpretated as evidence of introgression.

Researchers also use phylogenetic data for introgression detection (Vanderpool et al. 2020; Hibbins and Hahn 2022; Suvorov et al. 2022). Phylogenetic-based introgression detection is based on the Δ-statistic, which is also an indicator of the difference between the probabilities of the two discordant gene tree topologies (Vanderpool et al. 2020; Hibbins and Hahn 2022; Suvorov et al. 2022). We calculated Δ-statistics for the reconstructed gene trees produced in the analysis described in the previous section. Similar to D-statistics, Δ-statistics did not exhibit any bias toward negative or positive values under neutral evolution (Fig. 4a–e, second row). In the presence of purifying selection, the Δ-statistics under population size combinations B and D were biased toward negative values (Fig. 4b and d, fourth row), and the Δ-statistics under population size combinations C and E were biased toward positive values (Fig. 4c and e, fourth row). Furthermore, as the population size differences became greater, the Δ-statistics under population size combinations D and E exhibited more pronounced biases than those under population size combinations B and C (Fig. 4d and e, fourth row). In practice, biases in D-statistics and Δ-statistics are often interpreted as evidence of introgression (Green et al. 2010; Durand et al. 2011; Vanderpool et al. 2020; Hibbins and Hahn 2022). However, in the presence of purifying selection, population size differences can also lead to biases in D-statistics and Δ-statistics. Collectively, the above results indicate that in the presence of purifying selection, population size differences can cause false positive results in introgression detection.

The Observation of the Effect of Purifying Selection on Gene Tree Distributions Is Robust to Different Population Genetics Models

The previous simulations of He et al. (2020) have provided initial evidence showing that purifying selection combined with population size differences can affect gene tree distributions. These simulations were based on the ASG model (Neuhauser and Krone 1997). By comparison, SLiM implements the Wright–Fisher model. Ideally, these two different simulation methods should produce consistent results. However, in another study, Vanderpool et al. (2020) claimed that they simulated gene trees using SLiM with a combination of settings comparable to one of those adopted by He et al. (2020), but no asymmetric gene tree distribution was observed. Vanderpool et al.’s claim gives the impression that the results of He et al. (2020) are unreplicable when using SLiM, which seems to further imply that purifying selection has no effect on gene tree distributions. Here, we show that with settings comparable to those used by He et al. (2020), SLiM can produce simulation results similar to those based on the ASG model.

We performed SLiM simulations strictly according to the original settings adopted by He et al. (2020) (Materials and Methods). As observed in the work of He et al. (2020), in the present SLiM simulations, the gene tree distributions generated under purifying selection exhibited different patterns across different population size combinations (Fig. 5). Furthermore, the results produced by the present SLiM simulations were almost identical to those produced by the simulations of He et al. (2020). Additionally, when our SLiM simulation settings were rescaled into 5 times larger population sizes, the result remained similar, verifying that theoretically comparable settings yield consistent results (Supplementary Fig. S1). Collectively, unlike what was claimed by Vanderpool et al., the simulation results of He et al. (2020) are well-replicable when using SLiM.

SLiM simulation results generated under settings comparable to those used by He et al. (2020). SLiM implements the Wright–Fisher model, whereas the simulations of He et al. (2020) are based on the ASC model. The SLiM simulation results shown here are very similar to those of He et al. (2020).

Now, the question is why Vanderpool et al. (2020) could not produce a result similar to that of He et al. (2020). The answer is that the combination of settings used by Vanderpool et al. was not comparable to any of those used by He et al. (2020). We found that there were multiple differences between the settings used by He et al. (2020) and those used by Vanderpool et al. (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Particularly, the population sizes used by Vanderpool et al. are in the ratio N123: N12: N1: N2: N3 = 25:25:1:25:25 (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Among the population size combinations used by He et al. (2020), the only one that shares some similarity with that used by Vanderpool et al. is the population size combination F. N1, N2, and N3 in this population size combination are also in the ratio 1:25:25 (Fig. 5f). However, N123, N12, N1, N2, and N3 in this population size combination are in the ratio N123: N12: N1: N2: N3 = 5:5:1:25:25 instead of N123: N12: N1: N2: N3 = 25:25:1:25:25. Consequently, the settings of N123 and N12 used by Vanderpool et al. (2020) are effectively 5 times larger than those used by He et al. (2020). Additionally, there are other differences between the settings used by Vanderpool et al. and those used by He et al. (2020), for example, different branch lengths (Supplementary Fig. S2A). As Vanderpool et al.’s settings are not comparable to any of those used by He et al. (2020), Vanderpool et al.’s simulations cannot be used to verify or falsify the replicability of the results of He et al. (2020). Furthermore, we simulated gene trees with settings comparable to those used by Vanderpool et al., except that the population sizes were set in the ratio N123: N12: N1: N2: N3 = 5:5:1:25:25 (the simulation settings are still not comparable to any of those adopted by He et al. (2020) because there are other differences, for example, differences in branch lengths). This change in population size produced a gene tree distribution deviating significantly from what would be expected under the MSC model (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Such a phenomenon is in contrast to Vanderpool et al.’s claim that “there should be no effect of negative selection on the distribution of tree topologies” because under the MSC model, changing N123 cannot cause a change in the distribution of gene tree topologies. In Vanderpool et al.’s simulations, using a larger N123 made deleterious mutations much less likely to be present in extant gene copies in comparsion with the simulations of He et al. (2020) ( σ123 was changed from 1.5 to 7.5). In line with this setting, Vanderpool et al. reported that fewer than 1 mutation per locus was observed at the end of their simulations. By comparison, in the SLiM simulations described in the previous sections, ∼20% of deleterious mutations were very slightly deleterious mutations (Materials and Methods). This setting is more realistic than Vanderpool et al.’s ( σ123 | < 1 for ∼20% of deleterious mutations versus σ123 | = 7.5 for all deleterious mutations). The observation that almost none of the deleterious mutations were present in extant gene copies was used by Vanderpool et al. to show that the simulations of He et al. (2020) are unrealistic. Nonetheless, this observation only indicates that Vanderpool et al.’s simulations are unrealistic. Overall, Vanderpool et al.’s simulation result merely indicates that their simulation settings cause a less pronounced change in gene tree distributions in comparison with the settings of He et al. (2020). It gives no support for the claim that purifying selection has no effect on gene tree distributions.

The ASG model is a backward-in-time model, whereas the Wright–Fisher model is a forward-in-time model. The results presented above show that simulations based on these two different population genetics models produce consistent results. This consistency indicates that the results of He et al. (2020) are unlikely to be caused by programming errors or a specific population genetics model.

An Analysis of the Effect of Purifying Selection on the Gene Tree Formation Process in the Occurrence of Deep Coalescence

In the MSC model, regardless of the population sizes in the given species tree, gene trees in the occurrence of deep coalescence are always modeled by a process where species labels occur with equal probability at each tip of gene trees (Fig. 1). In the following analysis, we show that this gene tree labeling process comes into conflict with biological knowledge under purifying selection. Additionally, we deduce the pattern of change of gene tree distributions under purifying selection. A comparison of our simulation results with the theoretically deduced pattern is present to verify their consistency.

Question Decomposition and Overview

Hereafter, t123 denotes the time at which S123 is about to split into S12 and S3 (Fig. 6a). In the occurrence of deep coalescence, the ancestries of g1, g2, and g3 are traced back to three ancestral gene copies at t123 (denoted by a1, a2, and a3, respectively), and therefore g1, g2, and g3 are connected to a three-tip genealogical tree in ancestral species S123 (denoted by G123). Ancestral gene copies at t123 can be viewed as mediators in connecting g1, g2, and g3 to G123 (Fig. 6a). From this viewpoint, if all mediators at t123 have an equal probability of being connected to g1, g2, or g3, they have completely random input (looking backward in time); if all mediators at t123 have an equal probability of occupying each tip of G123, they have completely random output (Fig. 6b). Evolutionary events later than t123 and those earlier than t123 determine the process of connecting g1, g2, and g3 to G123 through mediators’ input and output, respectively. Therefore, the question about the gene tree formation process in the occurrence of deep coalescence can be decomposed into questions about mediators’ input and output (Fig. 6b).

a) Illustration of the process of connecting g1, g2, and g3 to a genealogical tree in S123 (G123). The time at which S123 is about to split into S12 and S3 is denoted by t123. Fundamentally, gene copies at t123 can be viewed as mediators in connecting g1, g2, and g3 to G123. The theoretical analysis presented in this paper is based on this viewpoint. b) The question about the gene tree formation process in the occurrence of deep coalescence can be decomposed into questions about mediators’ input and output.

The above question decomposition gives rise to the following questions: Is it an input-related or an output-related property that underlies the MSC gene tree labeling process? Does such a property remain unchanged under purifying selection? In the following analysis, we address these questions by considering the simplest situation where one mutation is assumed in S123 (Supplementary Section S1 shows that our main conclusion remains valid when multiple mutations are assumed in S123). Hereafter, M denotes a mutation occurring in S123; gene copies that carry mutation M and those that do not are referred to as “carriers” and “noncarriers,” respectively. We first derive the general functional forms for the probabilities of three-taxon gene tree topologies. Then, we use the input-related and output-related parameters of the derived functional forms to illustrate why the MSC gene tree labeling process is biologically justified under neutral evolution (when mutation M is assumed to be neutral) and what change is brought about by purifying selection (when mutation M is assumed to be deleterious). Throughout the analysis, we focus on the effect of a neutral/deleterious mutation on gene trees that experience deep coalescence. Therefore, all probabilities described below are conditional on the presence of mutation M in S123 and the occurrence of deep coalescence. For convenience, these background assumptions are not written explicitly, hereafter.

General Functional Forms for the Probabilities of Three-Taxon Gene Tree Topologies

The first step in our analysis is deriving general functional forms for the probabilities of the three possible gene tree topologies, P(T1), P(T2), and P(T3). This derivation involves considering how g1, g2, and g3 are connected to mediators and then how mediators are placed onto genealogical tree G123 (Fig. 6a). The first point to consider is how g1, g2, and g3 are connected to mediators, or in other words, mediators’ input (Fig. 6). The allelic states of a1, a2, and a3 (i.e., the mediators connected by g1, g2, and g3) can show 23 = 8 possible patterns (denoted by A1–A8, Fig. 7a). These eight patterns are essentially eight possible ways of connecting g1, g2, and g3 to mediators. The second point to consider is how mediators are placed onto a three-tip genealogical tree, or in other words, mediators’ output (Fig. 6). We refer to three-tip genealogical trees with three identical gene copies as type U genealogical trees (Fig. 7a). As all the gene copies of a type U genealogical tree are identical, the gene copies of a type U genealogical tree have an equal probability of occupying each of the three tips. By comparison, if a genealogical tree contains different types of gene copies, the gene copies of such a genealogical tree do not necessarily have an equal probability of occupying each of the three tips (Fig. 7a). We refer to three-tip genealogical trees with one “carrier” and two “noncarriers” as type V genealogical trees and those with one “noncarrier” and two “carriers” as type W genealogical trees (Fig. 7a). The first diverging branch of a type V genealogical tree is either occupied by the “carrier” or by one of the two “noncarriers.” We refer to type V genealogical trees whose “carrier” occupies the first diverging branch as type V1 genealogical trees (Fig. 7a). P(V1 | V) accordingly is the conditional probability that a three-tip genealogical tree is a type V1 genealogical tree given that this genealogical tree is a type V genealogical tree (Tabel 1). Similarly, we refer to type W genealogical trees whose “noncarrier” occupies the first diverging branch as type W1 genealogical trees (Fig. 7a). P(W1 | W) accordingly is the conditional probability that a three-tip genealogical tree is a type W1 genealogical tree given that this genealogical tree is a type W genealogical tree (Table 1).

Now, let us consider the probability of each gene tree topology under each of the aforementioned eight patterns. P(Ti | Ax) (i = 1, 2, 3; x = 1, 2, . . ., 8) is the probability that gene tree topology Ti occurs given that pattern Ax occurs. Under patterns A1 and A2, g1, g2, and g3 are connected to a type U genealogical tree, that is, G123 is a type U genealogical tree (Fig. 7a). As gene copies of a type U genealogical tree have an equal probability of occupying each of the three tips, under patterns A1 and A2, each of the three possible gene tree topologies has a 1/3 probability of occurring, that is, P(Ti | Ax) = 1/3 (i = 1, 2, 3; x = 1, 2; see Table 1). By comparison, under patterns A3–A5, G123 is a type V genealogical tree (Fig. 7a). Furthermore, if and only if G123 is a type V1 genealogical tree, the gene tree topologies under patterns A3, A4, and A5 are T1, T2, and T3, respectively (see an example in Fig. 7b). The Gene Tree Labeling Process Assumed in the MSC Model Becomes Biologically Untenable under Purifying Selection

In this part, by analyzing pi (i = 1, 2, 3), P(V1 | V), and P(W1 | W), we illustrate the difference between gene tree distributions under purifying selection and those under neutral evolution. We begin by considering pi. First, recall that pi is defined as the probability that ai—the ancestor of gi in S123—is a “carrier.” That is to say, pi is the probability that a descendant of a “carrier” is sampled from Si (Fig. 8b). Let fi be the frequency of descendants of “carriers” in Si when gi is sampled from Si. E(fi) accordingly is the expected value of fi (Fig. 8b). The higher the expected frequency of descendants of “carriers” in Si, the more likely a descendant of a “carrier” is to be sampled from Si. Therefore, pi and E(fi) are essentially equivalent (see a rigorous proof in Supplementary Appendix S2). From this, it can be seen that pi inherently depends on the expected fate of mutation M in species Si.

Although the frequency of descendants of “carriers” can fluctuate through time, if mutation M is a neutral mutation (i.e., under neutral evolution), the expected frequency of descendants of “carriers” is an invariant constant and independent of population size (Kimura 1983; Ewens 2004). Therefore, if mutation M is a neutral mutation, E(fi), namely, pi, is invariant among the three routes—S123–S1, S123–S2, and S123–S3 (Fig. 8b). Because the allelic states of a1, a2, and a3 are the outcomes of three identical processes under neutral evolution, regardless of changes in population size, p1, p2, and p3 are always equal under neutral evolution. Mediators’ input, therefore, is always completely random under neutral evolution. By comparison, it is inherent in the definition of purifying selection that purifying selection acts to depress the expected frequencies of deleterious mutations. Furthermore, the degree of this effect, that is, selection effectiveness, depends on population size (Ohta and Kimura 1971; Ohta 1992; Lanfear et al. 2010). As explained above, pi is equivalent to E(fi). Therefore, if mutation M is a deleterious mutation, p1, p2, and p3 depend on the population size differences between the three routes—S123–S1, S123–S2, and S123–S3. Unlike under neutral evolution, if mutation M is a deleterious mutation, the allelic states of a1, a2, and a3 are not always the outcomes of three identical processes (Fig. 8b). Consequently, p1, p2, and p3 are not always equal under purifying selection.

As a particular case, in evolutionary studies, a sufficiently long time interval is often assumed between when mutation M appears in ancestral species S123 and when gi is sampled from extant species Si. In such a long-timescale situation, mutation M is either fixed or lost in Si, that is, the distribution of fi has converged to a Bernoulli distribution when gi is sampled from Si. If the distribution of fi has converged to a Bernoulli distribution, the expected value of fi, that is, E(fi), is equal to the probability that fi takes the value 1, that is, the probability that all gene copies in Si are descended from “carriers.” In this long-timescale case, E(fi) is equal to the probability of fixation of mutation M in Si. The larger the population size, the less likely that a deleterious mutation is fixed in the population (Ohta and Kimura 1971; Ohta 1992). Accordingly, if mutation M is a deleterious mutation, the larger the population size in Si, the less likely that mutation M is fixed in Si, and the less likely that a descendant of a “carrier” is sampled from Si, that is, the smaller pi. Therefore, under purifying selection, population size differences can lead to unequal values of p1, p2, and p3, or in other words, nonrandom input of mediators.

Above, we have shown that mediators’ input under purifying selection can differ from that under neutral evolution. Nevertheless, it is also necessary to answer whether this difference can lead to changes in gene tree distributions. Specifically, if P(V1 | V) = 1/3 and P(W1 | W) = 1/3 (i.e., mediators’ output is completely random, Fig. 9a), P(T1), P(T2), and P(T3) are equal and unaffected by changes in pi. Therefore, we also need to analyze P(V1 | V) and P(W1 | W). First, it is expected by biological intuition that a pair of “noncarriers” or a pair of “carriers” tends to be more closely related than a pair of gene copies in different allelic states. That is to say, the “carrier” of a type V genealogical tree is expected to be separated from the two “noncarriers” and occupy the first diverging branch, and the “noncarrier” of a type W genealogical tree is expected to be separated from the two “carriers” and occupy the first diverging branch. Hence, it can be inferred by biological intuition that regardless of whether neutral evolution or purifying selection is assumed, P(V1 | V) > 1/3 and P(W1 | W) > 1/3, instead of P(V1 | V) = 1/3 and P(W1 | W) = 1/3.

A more precise understanding of the values of P(V1 | V) and P(W1 | W) can be obtained by considering the position of mutation M on genealogical tree G123. We first consider P(V1 | V). If mutation M occurs on the first diverging branch of G123, G123 is a type V genealogical tree and at the same time a type V1 genealogical tree (Fig. 9b). By comparison, if mutation M occurs on one of the two nested branches of a three-tip genealogical tree, this genealogical tree is a type V genealogical tree but not a type V1 genealogical tree (Fig. 9b). Given that a three-tip genealogical tree is a type V genealogical tree, only either of these two situations can occur. The condition P(V1 | V) = 1/3 is satisfied if and only if mutation M occurs on each of the three terminal branches with equal probability. However, this condition conflicts with the biological fact that the first diverging branch is longer than the two nested branches. Furthermore, it can be shown that under an assumption of neutral evolution combined with a constant population size and a constant mutation rate, P(V1 | V) = 2/3 (Supplementary Appendix S3). We also empirically examined the value of P(V1 | V) under neutral evolution by SLiM simulations (Supplementary Methods). Our SLiM simulations supported that under neutral evolution, P(V1 | V) = 2/3 (Supplementary Fig. S3). Additionally, we examined the value of P(V1 | V) under purifying selection. Our SLiM simulations showed that the value of P(V1 | V) under purifying selection is similar to but slightly greater than that under neutral evolution (Supplementary Fig. S3; P = 5.2 × 10–5, Mann–Whitney U test). Accordingly, we have P(V1 | V) ≥ 2/3, instead of P(V1 | V) = 1/3. Next, we consider P(W1 | W). Given a three-tip genealogical tree, if a mutation occurs on the internal branch, the genealogical tree is a type W genealogical tree, and at the same time, it is a type W1 genealogical tree (Fig. 9c). This is the only situation that leads to a type W genealogical tree. Therefore, regardless of whether neutral evolution or purifying selection is assumed, P(W1 | W) = 1/3 is not satisfied. Instead, P(W1 | W) = 1. Collectively, as long as there is a nonzero probability that an internal branch exists in G123, mediators’ output is not completely random. Therefore, unless in some particular situations (e.g., a short internal branch in G123 caused by population expansion combined with a small probability of occurrence of type W genealogical trees), changes in mediators’ input would be expected to lead to changes in gene tree distributions.

In summary, under neutral evolution, mediators’ input is invariant regardless of changes in population size. This input-related property underlies the gene tree labeling process assumed in the MSC model. Whereas, under purifying selection, changes in population size can lead to changes in mediators’ input. And there is no biological reason to claim that changes in mediators’ input cannot lead to changes in gene tree distributions. Hence, under purifying selection, the gene tree labeling process assumed in the MSC model becomes biologically untenable, and changes in population size can lead to changes in gene tree distributions. Noteworthily, the above conclusions do not rely on any specific population genetics model. Therefore, the population size dependence of gene tree distributions is not a model-specific phenomenon but instead an inherent consequence of purifying selection.

Discussion

No Well-Accepted Theory Suggests That Purifying Selection Has No Effect on Gene Tree Distributions

As explained above, the simulation of Vanderpool et al. (2020) was performed with a combination of settings not comparable to any of those used by He et al. (2020). Therefore, the simulation result of Vanderpool et al. (2020) should not be regarded as evidence rejecting the phenomenon reported by He et al. (2020). In Vanderpool et al.’s paper, the authors also claimed, “previous theoretical results predict that there should be no effect of negative selection on the distribution of tree topologies.” This claim gives an impression that the phenomenon reported by He et al. (2020) contradicts some well-accepted theories. Here, we show that the studies cited by Vanderpool et al. provide no support for their claim.

The studies cited by Vanderpool et al. consider the ancestor–descendant relationship of gene copies drawn from a single population. These studies suggest that the shapes of single-population genealogical trees under purifying selection follow distributions similar to those under neutral evolution (Golding 1997; Przeworski et al. 1999; Slade 2000; Williamson and Orive 2002). Both multispecies gene trees and single-population genealogical trees are often referred to as “genealogical trees.” However, multispecies gene trees and single-population genealogical trees are not the same things. Multispecies gene trees are labeled trees. Each label in multispecies gene trees describes the source species of each gene copy. By comparison, single-population genealogical trees do not have such labels. How species labels occur in gene trees determines the topologies of gene trees, whereas it is irrelevant to single-population genealogical trees. Particularly, because there are three possible ways for the occurrence of species labels in three-taxon gene trees, there are three possible topologies for three-taxon gene trees, whereas there is only one possible topology for three-tip unlabeled trees.

From the studies cited by Vanderpool et al., one cannot conclude that the way in which species labels occur in gene trees remains unchanged under purifying selection. First, mediators’ input is related to differences between multiple species/populations (Fig. 8). However, the studies cited by Vanderpool et al. consider properties within a single population. Therefore, these studies have little relevance to mediators’ input. Mediators’ output is related to the shape of genealogical tree G123 (Fig. 9) and therefore is related to the studies cited by Vanderpool et al. However, these studies only suggest that single-population genealogies under purifying selection are similar to those under neutral evolution. They do not suggest that mediators’ output has a property that makes gene tree distributions unaffected by changes in mediators’ input. In fact, mediators’ output does not have such a property even when neutral evolution is assumed (Fig. 9). Therefore, Vanderpool et al. did not provide relevant evidence for their claim. There is neither empirical nor theoretical evidence suggesting that purifying selection has no effect on gene tree distributions.

Implications for Evolutionary Biology

The use of the MSC model is so far the best approach to take into account gene tree discordance in the practice of evolutionary biology. Understandings of factors that cause gene tree distributions inconsistent with the expectations under the MSC model are important for the rigor of evolutionary studies. Previously, the work of He et al. (2020) has revealed that purifying selection combined with population size differences can cause asymmetric gene tree distributions. In the present work, we provide more direct evidence demonstrating that this phenomenon can lead to biases in phylogenetic inference and introgression detection.

The gene tree labeling process assumed in the MSC model is very simple. Perhaps because of its simplicity, it has rarely been questioned what this gene tree labeling process rests on. As elucidated by our theoretical analysis, the gene tree labeling process assumed in the MSC model rests on a premise that is biologically untenable under purifying selection (mediators’ input is always completely random). This underlying premise of the MSC model should not be overlooked in studying the relationship between gene trees and species trees. Both the assumption that mediators’ input is completely random and the molecular clock assumption stem from the invariance of gene fates (Ohta and Kimura 1971; Lanfear et al. 2010). From this viewpoint, violations of symmetry in gene tree distributions may be closely correlated to violations of the molecular clock assumption. Accordingly, selecting genes that exhibit less pronounced differences in dN/dS ratios or less pronounced differences in substitution rates may be a simple method that helps mitigate the impact of purifying selection on evolutionary analyses (Doyle et al. 2015). Additionally, selecting noncoding genomic regions instead of protein-coding genes or selecting genes with low dN/dS ratios may also help mitigate the impact of purifying selection on evolutionary analyses. The accumulation of genomic data has paved the way for resolving many phylogenetic questions involving rapid radiations (Nesi et al. 2021; Scherz et al. 2022; Stiller et al. 2024). As rapid radiations cause high probabilities of deep coalescence, in such cases, only a small fraction of gene trees are formed without experiencing deep coalescence, and they cause only a small increase in the frequency of the concordant topology. Purifying selection combined with population size differences may easily cause a larger increase in the frequency of a discordant topology than that of the concordant topology, thereby compromising phylogenetic inference. Hence, the aforementioned methods for mitigating the impact of purifying selection may be helpful for improving the rigor of phylogenetic studies involving rapid radiations. Purifying selection can have a broader impact on introgression detection than on phylogenetic inference because introgression detection does not depend on the frequency of the concordant topology but on the difference between the frequencies of discordant topologies. In addition to methods for mitigating the impact of purifying selection, combining other signatures of introgression, for example, changes in the distribution of branch lengths (Lohse and Frantz 2014; Edelman et al. 2019), may help improve the rigor of introgression detection. Finally, nonrandom output of mediators can result from factors other than ancestral mutations in S123, for example, ancestral population subdivisions in S123 (Slatkin and Pollack 2008); nonrandom input of mediators can result from factors other than purifying selection combined with population size differences, for example, different contributions of different subpopulations in S123 to descendant species (Slatkin and Pollack 2008), or changes in selection coefficients due to lineage-specific selection (Adams et al. 2018). Therefore, the question decomposition employed in our theoretical analysis can also be adapted to compare various factors that can cause asymmetric gene tree distributions.
