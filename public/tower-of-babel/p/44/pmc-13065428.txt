Relationship of Serum Tricellulin, MarvelD3, and Tumor Necrosis Factor-Alpha Levels With Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder Severity in Children and Adolescents
Authors: Kübranur Ünal, Yasemin Taş Torun, Mehmet Emre Erol, Zeynep Kübra Kurt, Cansu Özbaş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesObsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a neuropsychiatric disorder characterized by obsessions and compulsions, with a prevalence of 1%–3%. Tight junction (TJ) proteins contribute to maintaining the epithelial barrier. This study investigated the association of serum TJ-associated MARVEL protein family and inflammatory markers with OCD severity in children and adolescents.MethodsA total of 43 drug-naive children with “pure” OCD and 37 age-matched healthy controls were included in the study. Serum levels of occludin, tricellulin, MarvelD3, which belong to the TJ-associated MARVEL protein family, along with inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, tumor necrosis factor-alpha [TNF-α], interleukin-6) were assessed. The severity of OCD was ascertained using the Maudsley Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory (MOCI). Biochemical data of patients and control groups were compared against clinical features such as OCD severity and time since symptom onset.ResultsBoth groups showed similar sociodemographic factors, except that psychiatric disorders and family histories of OCD were significantly higher in the OCD group. Although serum TJ levels were not significantly different between groups, serum tricellulin (r= 0.427, p=0.004), MarvelD3 (r=0.489, p<0.001), and TNF-α (r=0.495, p<0.001) levels had a positive correlation with the severity of OCD based on MOCI.ConclusionThe findings suggest that tricellulin, MarvelD3, and TNF-α may be associated with OCD severity. Further comprehensive research is required to determine the role of TJ proteins belonging to the MARVEL protein family in the pathophysiology of OCD.Keywords: Tricellulin; MARVELD3 protein, human; Tumor necrosis factor-alpha; Obsessive-compulsive disorder; Severity of disease

INTRODUCTION

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a chronic neuropsychiatric disorder characterized by obsessions and compulsions, with a prevalence of 1%–3% [1]. Although the core features remain consistent across age groups, the clinical presentation of OCD in children and adolescents may differ from that in adults. The disorder profoundly affects aspects of life such as family relationships and social interactions [2]. Obsessions are defined as recurrent undesirable thoughts, urges, or images that forcefully enter consciousness and cause distress or anxiety [3]. In contrast, compulsions are repetitive overt or covert acts performed to counteract obsessions, reduce distress, or prevent a feared outcome [4]. The symptoms of OCD usually appear during childhood and adolescence. Approximately 80% of patients report that OCD symptoms emerge during these critical developmental periods [2]. Despite significant research in the field, the exact etiopathogenesis of OCD remains unclear. Studies suggest a multifaceted etiology resulting from the interaction between genetic and environmental factors [5-7].

Tight junctions (TJs), which regulate intercellular permeability in the epithelial and endothelial layers, are among the elements responsible for maintaining the integrity of the barrier functions in epithelial cells [8]. TJs are formed and regulated by intercellular junctions containing integral transmembrane proteins, primarily the claudin family, as well as members of the TJ Associated MARVEL Protein (TAMP) family, including occludin, tricellulin, and MarvelD3 [8,9]. Occludin, an important component of TJs, is vital for maintaining their integrity, regulating paracellular permeability, and supporting the barrier functions of the intestinal epithelium and blood–brain barrier (BBB) [10]. Tricellulin, another member of the TAMP family, regulates the structure and function of TJs, particularly intestinal integrity [11]. MarvelD3 is another intestinal TJ protein that regulates epithelial cell stability and maintains intestinal barrier functions [12]. Its role in strengthening intestinal TJs suggests that it may have broader effects on gut–brain axis (GBA) and its related physiological processes.

OCD is a neuropsychiatric disorder with complex and multifactorial origins [13]. Although both genetic predispositions and environmental factors such as early life trauma have been implicated in its onset, recent studies have increasingly illuminated the potential role of disruptions in epithelial barrier integrity in the pathophysiology of psychiatric diseases [14]. This emerging perspective suggests that compromised intestinal TJs can lead to increased permeability (“leaky gut”), allowing the translocation of luminal antigens and microbial metabolites into systemic circulation, which may contribute to neuroinflammation and subsequently influence the central nervous system functions implicated in OCD’s intricate etiology [15,16].

Among the biological frameworks proposed to explain the pathophysiology of OCD, increasing attention has been paid to neuroinflammatory mechanisms that regulate the interaction between the central nervous system and peripheral systems, such as the GBA [17]. Disruption of the epithelial and endothelial barriers may enable microbial and immune-mediated molecules to activate systemic inflammatory responses, which in turn affects neuronal signaling and synaptic function [18,19]. Among proinflammatory mediators, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) has received particular attention due to its role in regulating glial activation, neurotransmitter metabolism, and synaptic plasticity [19]. Elevated levels of TNF-α and related cytokines have been reported in various psychiatric conditions, such as OCD. This finding supports the hypothesis that immune dysregulation and altered cytokine signaling may underlie symptom manifestation and neurocircuit dysfunction under these conditions [20]. This growing evidence suggests that neuroinflammation is a possible mechanistic bridge connecting peripheral immune activity to neurobiological changes in OCD.

The study aimed to examine the association of serum tight TJ proteins (occludin, tricellulin, MarvelD3) and inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein [CRP], TNF-α, interleukin-6 [IL-6]) with the severity of OCD in drug-naive children and adolescents. We chose to focus on tricellulin and MarvelD3 because of their critical but less-studied roles in intestinal barrier function and their developing roles in neuropsychiatric disease. TNF-α was included as a verified proinflammatory cytokine frequently implicated in immunemediated mechanisms of neurological and psychiatric disorders. Furthermore, the present work also attempted to provide evidence to explain the biological mechanisms of OCD and to better understand its pathogenesis by examining the correlations between these proteins and clinical characteristics.

METHODS

Study participants

This study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was obtained from the Ankara City Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Board (dated 18/09/2024 and numbered E2-24-8428). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were informed of the details of the study.

This study was conducted in the Faculty of Medicine (GUFM). Participants were selected from the GUFM hospital’s Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic, and samples were analyzed at the Department of Medical Biochemistry of the Gazi University Hospital. For the sample size, a case-control study conducted by

Zengil and Laloğlu [21], which examined serum TJ levels in individuals with OCD, was considered. Based on the effect size value suggested by Cohen [22] (effect size f=0.6), an alpha error (p-value) of 0.05, and a 1-beta error (power) of 0.80, a total of 72 participants were determined to be sufficient to test the null hypothesis. Considering non-responses and missing data, an additional 10% was added, resulting in a total of 80 participants. The G Power Statistical Program version 3.1.9.4 (Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) was used for the analyses [23]. A total of 43 children with OCD and 37 age-matched healthy controls were included. Focusing on children and adolescents enables the investigation of early neuroimmune changes in OCD, which may differ from those in adult-onset cases due to ongoing developmental and hormonal processes.

The researcher conducted semi-structured psychiatric interviews with the patients to ensure compliance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Children without psychiatric disorders “except OCD” according to the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia-Present and Lifetime Version-DSM-5 (K-SADS-PL-DSM-5) were enrolled, following the exclusion criteria. The primary diagnosis of OCD in the patient group was rigorously established using the KSADS-PL-DSM-5, the gold standard structured interview for this age group, to ensure a confirmed clinical diagnosis.

The healthy control group included individuals aged 8–18 years who visited the GUFM outpatient clinic. Children without psychiatric disorders according to the K-SADS-PL-DSM-5 were enrolled as healthy controls, following the exclusion criteria.

Exclusion criteria for all participants were: 1) a diagnosis of neurological disorders (e.g., cerebral palsy, epilepsy), metabolic disorders (e.g., phenylketonuria), diabetes mellitus, cancer, and allergic, rheumatologic, inflammatory, or autoimmune diseases; 2) history of an infectious disease within the last 4 weeks; 3) use of anti-inflammatory medications within the previous 4 weeks; 4) immunosuppressive treatment in the past 6 months; 5) history of substance use in the last 3 months; 6) a clinical suspicion of Pediatric Autoimmune Neuropsychiatric Disorders Associated with Streptococci (PANDAS); 7) meeting obesity criteria (body mass index [BMI] ≥95th percentile); 8) a diagnosis of any comorbid psychiatric disorder, as assessed by K-SADS-PL-DSM-5; and 9) a history of psychotropic medication use.

Clinical evaluation

All clinical assessments and scale scoring were performed by a board-certified child and adolescent psychiatrist with specialized training in these instruments, thereby ensuring consistency and eliminating interrater variability. The researchers created a sociodemographic data form to determine sociodemographic characteristics, including sex, education level, number of siblings, family structure, age, education level of the parents, and economic status. All patients were evaluated using the Turkish version of the K-SADS-PLDSM-5. This structured diagnostic interview, administered by a trained child and adolescent psychiatrist, has been designed to assess current and lifetime psychiatric disorders in individuals aged 6–18 years and has been updated according to the DSM-5 criteria in 2016 [24]. It covers five major diagnostic domains—mood, anxiety, psychotic, behavioral, and substance use disorders—along with additional appendices and a general assessment scale. To ensure a homogeneous sample and eliminate the potential confounding effects of comorbid psychiatric conditions, only participants with a primary diagnosis of OCD were included in the study. Individuals with any psychiatric comorbidity other than OCD, as identified through the K-SADS-PL-DSM-5, were excluded.

The Turkish version’s validity and reliability study was conducted by Ünal et al. [25] in 2019. The Revised Child Anxiety and Depression Scale (RCADS-CV-PV) is a 47-item scale developed to screen for and measure anxiety and depression in children and adolescents [26]. Both parent and child forms are available. The RCADS assesses total anxiety scores together with anxiety subscale scores for separation anxiety, generalized anxiety, panic disorder, social phobia, and major depressive disorder. The validity and reliability of the Turkish version were established by Gormez et al. [27] in 2017.

The Children’s Yale–Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (CY-BOCS) is a standardized instrument that assesses the severity and characteristics of obsessive-compulsive symptoms in children and adolescents aged 8–17 years [28]. The validity and reliability of the Turkish version were established by Yucelen et al. [29] in 2006.

The Maudsley Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory (MOCI) is a self-report measure used to evaluate various symptom dimensions of OCD, including control, cleanliness, slowness, suspiciousness, and rumination in both healthy individuals and clinical populations [30]. The primary objective of MOCI is to gather detailed symptom information rather than to serve as a diagnostic tool. It consists of 4 subscales with 37 items and is suitable for children and adolescents. The validity and reliability of the Turkish version were evaluated by Erol and Savaşır [31] in 1988.

Biochemical variables

Venous blood samples were obtained in the morning after an overnight fast, ensuring consistency in sampling time and fasting status to reduce the potential confounding effects of circadian variation and food intake on biomarker levels. Blood samples were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min to separate the serum. The serum samples obtained were stored at -80°C until biochemical analysis. All samples were thawed only once, immediately prior to the assays, and processed on the same day. After analysis, the remaining serum was discarded to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles, which can lead to protein degradation and compromise biomarker stability. Serum samples from patients with hemolytic or icteric conditions were excluded from the study. The serum levels of occludin (Cat. No. E3658Hu), tricellulin (Cat. No. E7292Hu), and MarvelD3 (Cat. No. E7726Hu) were measured using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (BT LAB). TNF-α levels were measured using commercial ELISA kits (DIAsource ImmunoAssays S.A). IL-6 serum levels were calculated using an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Elecsys; Roche Diagnostics). An IMAGE 800 nephelometric analyzer (Beckman Coulter Inc.) was used to measure the CRP concentrations. The sensivity of serum occludin, tricellulin, MarvelD3, TNF-α, IL-6, and CRP were 0.27 ng/mL, 0.14 ng/mL, 0.28 ng/mL, 0.7 pg/mL, 1.5 pg/mL, and 0.8 mg/dL, respectively.

The reference ranges for serum TNF-α, IL-6, and CRP were 4.6–12.4 pg/mL, <7 pg/mL, and 0–5 mg/dL, respectively, as provided by the assay manufacturers. Kit measurement ranges for occludin, tricellulin, and MarvelD3 were 0.5–150 ng/mL, 0.3–90 ng/mL, and 0.5–32 ng/mL, respectively. The intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) was 6%, and the inter-assay CV was 10% for occludin. For tricellulin and MarvelD3, the intra-and inter-assay CVs were 8% and 10%, respectively. Serum levels of occludin, tricellulin, and MarvelD3 were analyzed for research purposes only because no clinically accepted reference intervals exist for these TJ proteins.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp.). Descriptive statistics are presented as frequencies (n, %) for categorical data and as means±standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for numerical data. The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables. The normality of the numerical variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Student’s t-test was used to compare two independent groups with normally distributed data, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to non-normally distributed data. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to assess relationships between non-normally distributed variables. Correlation coefficients (r) were interpreted as follows: 0.0–0.39 (no relationship), 0.40–0.69 (moderate), 0.70–0.89 (strong), and 0.90–0.99 (very strong). Statistical significance was considered at a p-value <0.05, with results evaluated at a 95% confidence interval.

Additionally, multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine whether the associations between serum biomarker levels and MOCI scores were independent of clinical covariates. TNF-α, MarvelD3, and tricellulin levels were entered as predictors based on theoretical relevance to intestinal barrier and inflammatory pathways, while illness duration was included as a covariate. The enter method was used. Model assumptions (linearity, normal distribution of residuals, homoscedasticity, and absence of multicollinearity) were evaluated and found to be adequately met. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF). VIF values below 10 were considered acceptable, and a tolerance value >0.10 taken to indicate the absence of problematic multicollinearity.

RESULTS

Sociodemographic and clinical features

This study included 43 drug-naïve participants with “pure” OCD (21 females and 22 males) and 37 healthy controls (20 females and 17 males). The median age was 14 years (range: 10–16 years) in the OCD group and 13 years (range: 10–15 years) in the control group. There were no significant differences between the groups in terms of age (p=0.372) or gender distribution (p=0.642). No significant differences were detected between the patient and control groups regarding BMI percentile and family history of chronic illnesses. The median [IQR] time since symptom onset among OCD patients was 18 [6–24] months. The RCADS-CV scores for all subscales were higher in the OCD group than in the control group. The overall regression model significantly predicted MOCI scores, F (4,37)=3.61, p=0.014, accounting for approximately 28% of the variance (adjusted R2=0.203). Among the predictors, TNF-α remained a significant independent predictor of higher MOCI scores (β=0.412, p=0.009, 95% CI=0.202–1.318), whereas MarvelD3, tricellulin, and illness duration did not show significant independent associations (p>0.05). Collinearity diagnostics indicated moderate multicollinearity between tricellulin and MarvelD3 (VIF approximately 5), while TNF-α and illness duration showed acceptable levels of multicollinearity (VIF<2).

DISCUSSION

This study explored the relationship between serum TJ proteins (occludin, tricellulin, MarvelD3), inflammatory markers (CRP, TNF-α, IL-6), and OCD severity in drug-naive children and adolescents. Although no significant differences were observed in serum levels of these markers between the OCD and healthy control groups, significant positive correlations emerged between tricellulin, MarvelD3, TNF-α, and OCD symptom severity, as measured by the MOCI. These findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis generating rather than confirmatory. Although moderate correlations were observed, the markers did not differentiate between the OCD and control groups at the diagnostic level. Therefore, rather than representing the stable biological features of OCD, they may reflect the state-dependent physiological changes associated with symptom burden.

These correlations between disease severity and biomarkers highlight the potential role of the MARVEL protein family in the pathophysiology of OCD. Previous research has shown that inflammatory and barrier-related markers, such as TNF-α and IL-6, frequently fluctuate in response to symptom severity in psychiatric conditions [14,32,33]. Similarly, our results suggest that tricellulin and MarvelD3 may mirror the dynamic neuroimmune states that vary with symptom intensity.

Although participants with comorbid psychiatric diagnoses were excluded according to the K-SADS-PL-DSM-5, dimensional measures, such as the RCADS-CV, revealed subclinical anxiety and depression. Importantly, significant correlations were also observed between TJ proteins (occludin and tricellulin) and RCADS subscales. The primary OCD diagnosis in the patient group was rigorously determined using the K-SADS to ensure diagnostic homogeneity within the sample. MOCI was applied not for comparison with the control group but to identify distinct phenomenological features and subtypes of obsessive-compulsive symptomatology in clinically confirmed OCD participants, which formed the focus of our biochemical correlation analyses. These findings highlight the importance of combining categorical diagnostic instruments with dimensional scales in neurobiological studies of psychiatric disorders. This pattern suggests that TJ alterations may accompany broader internalizing symptoms and reduce the specificity of the observed findings to OCD pathology alone.

The roles of inflammatory markers such as CRP and IL-6, which have been shown to play a role in depression and other neuropsychiatric disorders, in the pathophysiology of OCD have not yet been definitively established [34]. In the current study, no statistically significant differences were observed between drug-naive OCD patients and healthy controls regarding CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6. Nevertheless, TNF-α showed a robust positive association with MOCI scores, remaining an independent predictor of obsessive-compulsive symptom severity even after adjustment for anxiety and depression subscales of the RCADS-CV. This suggests that TNF-α may contribute to the obsessive-compulsive symptom burden beyond comorbid affective dimensions and supports the notion of state-dependent inflammatory activation during symptom exacerbation.

In the pathophysiology of psychiatric diseases, inflammation can exacerbate symptoms, especially those caused by neurotransmitter imbalances [35]. As a pro-inflammatory cytokine, TNF-α modulates serotonergic and dopaminergic pathways and may alter synaptic transmission and neuroplasticity, mechanisms that underlie its association with obsessive-compulsive phenomena. Previous studies exploring TNF-α levels in OCD have yielded inconsistent results: while some have reported elevated plasma TNF-α compared with controls [36], others have found reduced or unchanged levels [37]. Genetic studies have also suggested potential associations between TNF-α gene polymorphisms (e.g., -238 G/A) and susceptibility to OCD, although these findings have been inconclusive [38]. In their meta-analysis including four studies, Jiang et al. [38] reported no significant overall association between the TNF-α 238 G/A variant and OCD risk, highlighting the heterogeneity and limited sample sizes across available studies. These heterogeneous results likely reflect the variability in sample characteristics, medication status, and matrix type (serum vs. plasma). Taken together, our findings strengthen the notion of state-related inflammatory activation during heightened symptom expression rather than a stable trait abnormality in OCD.

In contrast, the peripheral cytokine levels vary among children with OCD. For example, some reports have shown that salivary IL-6 levels are associated with OCD diagnosis and severity, whereas others have emphasized that cytokine reactivity in children with internalizing disorders depends on the sample characteristics and the measurement matrix. The lack of group differences in IL-6 and CRP levels in our study could be explained by factors such as mild systemic inflammation in the early stage/medication-free pediatric group or peripheral matrix measurement of the biomarker (serum). Even large longitudinal studies on the association between inflammation and psychiatric phenotypes in youth have described effect sizes as modest and symptom specific. Cumulatively, these findings imply that, although generalized inflammation may not distinguish OCD from controls in our study, TNF-α may serves as a dynamic correlate of symptom dimensions, thus bridging neuroimmune signaling to obsessive-compulsive behavior. Additional studies using cytokine profiling in combination with barrier-related and neuroimaging measures are necessary to determine whether these inflammatory activities indeed contribute causally to the pathophysiology of OCD or represent downstream stress-immune interactions.

OCD is a multifactorial neuropsychiatric disorder, and growing evidence suggests the involvement of the GBA and barrier dysfunction. TJ proteins, such as tricellulin and MarvelD3, particularly those expressed in the intestinal epithelium, are integral to barrier integrity and immune regulation. Previous studies have reported elevated claudin-5, zonulin, and occludin levels in pediatric patients with OCD and mood disorders. Akın-Kınay et al. [39] have reported increased serum levels of claudin-5, claudin-12, occludin, and tricellulin in pediatric patients with OCD, further highlighting the role of TJ proteins. Similarly, Zengil and Laloğlu [40] examined the serum levels of zonulin and occludin and suggested that bipolar disorder might affect these protein levels. In a study conducted by Işık et al. [2] on serum zonulin and claudin-5 levels in children with OCD, significant changes in claudin-5 levels showed a possible relationship with the severity of the disease, while no difference was observed in zonulin levels. Similarly, in a study conducted by Kılıç et al. [41], claudin-5 was found to be associated with OCD, but zonulin levels did not show a significant relationship. Zonulin plays a role in the regulation of intestinal permeability, whereas claudin-5 is a major TJ protein in the BBB. Considering that our findings showed significant correlations between tricellulin and MarvelD3 intestinal TJ proteins and OCD severity, it is possible that intestinal permeability also plays a role in the pathophysiology of OCD. These findings indicate that barrier dysfunction may differ in individuals with OCD, which needs to be investigated further.

In addition to their intestinal functions, TAMP family proteins participate in neurodevelopmental and endothelial processes, which have important implications for psychiatric conditions. Tricellulin and MarvelD3 are expressed not only in epithelial cells but also in cerebral endothelial cells, contributing to BBB integrity, cellular polarity, and morphogenesis. Experimental studies have demonstrated that MarvelD3 regulates the MAPK signaling pathway and cell migration during neural development [42,43], whereas tricellulin influences angiogenesis and BBB maturation [44]. Dysregulation of these pathways may have dual consequences on neuroimmune homeostasis and synaptic maturation during critical developmental stages. Considering these findings within the greater neuroimmune developmental model places our study in a broader context than that of the GBA and suggests that TJ alterations may represent systemic rather than purely intestinal pathophysiology in pediatric OCD.

In a study by Zengil and Laloğlu [21], zonulin and occludin levels were significantly higher in patients with OCD, and this increase was positively correlated with the time since symptom onset and disease severity, according to Y-BOCS and HDRS (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale) scores. In the current study, positive correlations were observed between occludin, tricellulin, and MarvelD3 levels and disease severity according to the MOCI score; however, serum TJ levels were not significantly different between the groups. Similar to zonulin and occludin, MarvelD3 may also be associated with impaired barrier function. However, more comprehensive and controlled studies are required to better understand the mechanisms underlying this relationship and to support these findings. These findings indicate the potential role of intestinal permeability and BBB disorders in the pathophysiology of OCD. Due to the heterogeneity in outcomes between studies, TJ-related biomarkers are likely to exhibit differential sensitivity depending on the developmental stage, comorbidity profiles, and methodological variation. This emphasizes the necessity of confirming biological heterogeneity across psychiatric disorders rather than the homologous involvement of barriers.

Furthermore, evidence from other neuropsychiatric conditions supports the role of TJ proteins in disease pathology. In their study on the effect of the roles of TAMP family members in TJs and barrier functions on ADHD, Ferahkaya et al. [11] found that serum claudin-5 and tricellulin were significantly lower in the ADHD group compared to the control group. In addition, Lee et al. [45] found that zonulin and claudin-5 levels were associated with cognitive functions associated with neurological diseases such as ADHD. These studies support the possibility that the disruption of TJs may be associated with neuropsychiatric diseases. When studies focusing on the relationship between different psychiatric diseases and TJ proteins have been conducted, different results were obtained. These differences may be due to the different etiologies of these diseases and their widespread heterogeneity. This aligns with a growing body of evidence indicating an altered barrier integrity in various neuropsychiatric conditions. For instance, the relationships demonstrated in a recent study by Yazla et al. [46] investigating peripheral biomarkers related to BBB damage in patients with schizophrenia indicated that more comprehensive research is needed to understand whether BBB defects contribute to the clinical characteristics of these patients.

The findings of our study contribute to the growing body of literature examining the relationship between OCD and TJ proteins while also revealing several limitations and directions for future research. Particularly, the association between biochemical marker levels and clinical symptoms appears to be complex, and their reliability and generalizability should be confirmed through larger multicenter longitudinal studies. Further investigation is warranted into the role of barrier function, particularly in epithelial and endothelial structures, such as the intestinal and BBB, as well as the broader contribution of inflammatory processes to OCD pathophysiology.

Despite these contributions, this study has some limitations. First, clinically validated reference ranges for the TJ proteins occludin, tricellulin, and MarvelD3 are not yet available. Therefore, the serum levels of these proteins were analyzed for research purposes only and not for diagnostic interpretation. Furthermore, the study included adolescent participants (median age: 14 years in the OCD group and 13 years in the control group), which limits the generalizability and physiological interpretation of the findings. Age- and sex-specific reference values were not applied to any serum biomarkers. Considering the potential influence of hormonal and developmental changes on biomarker expression during adolescence, caution is warranted when applying these findings to broader populations. Second, the cross-sectional nature of the study limited causal interpretations between biomarker levels and OCD symptom severity. Third, cytokine and TJ protein levels were assessed in the serum rather than in the plasma, which may introduce matrix-related variability in biomarker concentrations. Fourth, the absence of statistical correction for multiple comparisons increased the likelihood of Type I errors (false positives). In addition, the inability to control for subthreshold anxiety and depression symptoms in the correlation analyses conducted with MOCI represents a limitation of this study. Finally, although participants with comorbid psychiatric diagnoses were excluded, the presence of dimensional symptom overlap or subthreshold psychiatric features may have influenced the biomarker variability. Cross-group comparisons of symptom content could not be performed because MOCI was intentionally administered only within the OCD group to capture disorderspecific phenomenological patterns. Future studies including dimensional obsessive-compulsive measures in healthy controls may help clarify whether the observed biomarker associations are disorder-specific or reflect broader trait characteristics. Future research should adopt larger sample sizes and longitudinal or interventional designs to better elucidate the dynamic role of TJ proteins in neuropsychiatric disorders such as OCD.

CONCLUSION

This study investigated the association between serum TJ proteins, inflammatory markers, and OCD symptom severity in a well-characterized, drug-naïve pediatric sample. Although no diagnostic-level group differences were observed, the correlations between tricellulin, MarvelD3, TNF-α, and OCD severity indicators suggest that these markers may reflect dynamic pathophysiological changes in the disorder. Although these biomarkers may not serve diagnostic purposes in isolation, they may still hold value as adjunct markers in multi-dimensional clinical assessment models. Incorporating biological measures, such as TJ proteins, into future personalized approaches could facilitate better treatment monitoring and help elucidate the biological underpinnings of OCD. Further research with larger sample sizes and longitudinal designs is required to confirm these preliminary findings.
