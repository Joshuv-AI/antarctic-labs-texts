Prolonged empirical antibiotic therapy despite negative blood cultures in neonates: a retrospective stewardship-oriented analysis
Authors: Fitim Alidema, Arieta Hasani Alidema, Lirim Mustafa, Mirlinda Havolli, Fellenza Abazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmpirical antibiotic therapy is routinely initiated in neonatal units for suspected sepsis; however, continuation of treatment despite negative blood cultures represents a major antimicrobial stewardship challenge. Prolonged exposure in early life may increase the risk of adverse outcomes and antimicrobial resistance.ObjectivesTo evaluate the prevalence and independent predictors of prolonged empirical antibiotic therapy in neonates with negative blood cultures over a three-year period.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 550 neonates treated with empirical antibiotics between January 2023 and January 2026. Clinical data, laboratory parameters (C-reactive protein, white blood cell count, procalcitonin), gestational age, birth weight, indication for therapy, blood culture results, and antibiotic regimens classified according to the WHO AWaRe framework were analyzed. Prolonged therapy was defined as antibiotic treatment exceeding five days despite negative cultures. Days of therapy (DOT) and DOT per 1000 patient-days were calculated. Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors.ResultsBlood cultures were negative in 82.4% of neonates. Among these, 41.8% received antibiotics for more than five days. The mean duration of therapy was 6.7 ± 2.4 days, and DOT reached 724 per 1000 patient-days. Prematurity (< 37 weeks) was independently associated with prolonged therapy (OR 2.36; 95% CI 1.58–3.52; p < 0.001), particularly < 32 weeks (OR 3.41; 95% CI 2.01–5.79; p < 0.001). Birth weight < 1500 g (OR 2.87; 95% CI 1.74–4.72; p < 0.001), elevated CRP (> 20 mg/L) (OR 1.94; 95% CI 1.29–2.91; p = 0.002), and mechanical ventilation (OR 2.11; 95% CI 1.32–3.37; p = 0.001) were significant predictors. “Watch” antibiotics accounted for 38.6% of initial regimens.ConclusionsProlonged empirical antibiotic therapy despite negative blood cultures remains frequent in neonatal practice and is independently associated with prematurity, very low birth weight, inflammatory markers, and clinical severity. Targeted antimicrobial stewardship interventions are warranted to optimize neonatal antibiotic use.

Introduction

Neonatal sepsis continues to represent a major global health burden and remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in the first month of life, particularly among preterm and very low birth weight neonates [1, 2]. Because early clinical signs are subtle and non-specific, empirical antibiotic therapy is routinely initiated in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) when infection is suspected. Although this approach is considered life-saving, it inevitably results in substantial antibiotic exposure among neonates ultimately found not to have microbiologically confirmed infection [3].

Blood culture remains the gold standard for the diagnosis of neonatal sepsis; however, its sensitivity is limited by low-level bacteremia, prior maternal antibiotic exposure, and small sample volumes [4]. Consequently, clinicians frequently continue antibiotic therapy despite negative culture results, particularly in the presence of clinical instability or elevated inflammatory markers. This practice reflects a tension between the risk of undertreating a potentially fatal infection and the growing recognition of harms associated with unnecessary antibiotic exposure [5].

Accumulating evidence indicates that prolonged empirical antibiotic therapy in neonates without culture-proven infection is associated with adverse outcomes, including necrotizing enterocolitis, invasive fungal infections, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, disruption of gut microbiome development, and increased mortality [6, 7]. Early-life antibiotic exposure may also contribute to long-term immune dysregulation and metabolic consequences. From a public health perspective, unnecessary use of broad-spectrum agents accelerates the emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), a major global threat recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) [8].

To address rising AMR rates, the WHO introduced the AWaRe (Access, Watch, Reserve) classification to optimize antibiotic use and support antimicrobial stewardship [9]. In neonatal settings, stewardship efforts aim to ensure timely initiation of antibiotics in suspected sepsis while promoting early discontinuation when cultures remain negative and the clinical condition stabilizes. Despite these recommendations, several studies have reported that a substantial proportion of neonates with culture-negative sepsis continue to receive empirical antibiotic therapy beyond 48–72 h, frequently extending to five days or more [10]. Such variability reflects differences in clinical thresholds, biomarker interpretation, and institutional culture.

Prematurity and very low birth weight are consistently identified as major drivers of prolonged therapy. Extremely preterm neonates often present with respiratory distress, hemodynamic instability, and abnormal laboratory findings that may mimic infection, which often results in prolonged antibiotic exposure, a practice that has been associated with adverse outcomes in preterm neonates [11]. Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), abnormal white blood cell counts, and increased procalcitonin levels are frequently used to support treatment continuation, although their diagnostic accuracy in isolation remains limited. Similarly, the need for mechanical ventilation or central vascular access may influence decisions to maintain antimicrobial coverage.

Antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs) implemented in NICUs have demonstrated reductions in antibiotic days of therapy (DOT), broad-spectrum antibiotic use, and resistance patterns without increasing mortality or treatment failure [12]. Core strategies include standardized sepsis evaluation protocols, predefined stop rules at 36–48 h in the presence of negative cultures, prospective audit and feedback, and integration of biomarker-guided algorithms. Nevertheless, stewardship practices remain heterogeneous, and real-world data quantifying the burden and predictors of prolonged empirical therapy are still needed to inform targeted interventions.

In this context, evaluating the prevalence of prolonged empirical antibiotic therapy despite negative blood cultures and identifying independent predictors is essential for optimizing neonatal antimicrobial use. A better understanding of clinical and laboratory factors associated with treatment continuation may facilitate risk stratification, refinement of local guidelines, and alignment with AWaRe-based stewardship principles. The present study therefore aims to assess the frequency and determinants of prolonged empirical antibiotic therapy in neonates over a three-year period, with particular emphasis on prematurity, very low birth weight, inflammatory markers, and indicators of clinical severity. By integrating epidemiological analysis with stewardship metrics such as DOT per 1000 patient-days, this investigation seeks to provide evidence to support rational antibiotic prescribing in neonatal practice.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in the Department of Pediatrics and Neonatology at the Regional Hospital of Gjilan, a secondary-care referral center providing neonatal care for southeastern Kosovo. The department includes a neonatal unit managing term and preterm neonates requiring medical treatment, respiratory support, and empirical antibiotic therapy for suspected sepsis.

The study period extended from 1 January 2023 to 31 January 2026. Medical records of neonates who received empirical antibiotic therapy during hospitalization were reviewed.

Study population

All neonates (0–28 days of life) who were initiated on empirical intravenous antibiotic therapy for suspected early- or late-onset sepsis during the study period were eligible for inclusion.

Definitions

Empirical antibiotic therapy was defined as the initiation of systemic antibiotic treatment based on clinical suspicion of sepsis before microbiological confirmation.

Negative blood culture was defined as no bacterial growth after standard incubation according to institutional microbiology protocols.

Prolonged empirical therapy was defined a priori as continuation of antibiotic treatment for more than five days despite negative blood cultures and absence of microbiological confirmation.

Prematurity was defined as gestational age < 37 weeks, with further stratification into:

Very low birth weight (VLBW) was defined as birth weight < 1500 g.

Days of therapy (DOT) were calculated as the number of days a patient received a specific antibiotic, irrespective of dose. DOT per 1000 patient-days was calculated by dividing total DOT by total patient-days and multiplying by 1000. Antibiotics were categorized according to the WHO AWaRe classification (Access, Watch, Reserve).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version XX (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups (prolonged vs. non-prolonged therapy) were performed using:

Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables.Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables.

Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables.

Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of prolonged therapy. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical data were collected retrospectively from paper and electronic medical records of neonates hospitalized in the Department of Pediatrics and Neonatology at the Regional Hospital of Gjilan.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Regional Hospital of Gjilan. Additionally, formal authorization to access and analyze patient medical records was granted by the Director of the Department of Pediatrics and Neonatology at the Regional Hospital of Gjilan.

Due to the retrospective design of the study and the use of fully anonymized data, the requirement for written informed parental consent was waived. All data were coded prior to analysis and handled in accordance with institutional standards for confidentiality and data protection.

Results

A total of 550 neonates treated with empirical antibiotic therapy between January 2023 and January 2026 were included. Blood cultures were negative in 82.4% (n = 453). Among culture-negative neonates, 41.8% (n = 189) received antibiotics for more than five days.

Among the 453 neonates with negative blood cultures, 189 (41.8%) received prolonged empirical antibiotic therapy for more than five days, whereas 264 (58.2%) received antibiotics for five days or less. The mean duration of antibiotic therapy among culture-negative neonates was 6.7 ± 2.4 days (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that prolonged empirical antibiotic therapy despite negative blood cultures remains frequent in neonatal practice, affecting 41.8% of culture-negative neonates. This proportion is consistent with previously reported rates ranging between 30% and 60% in NICU settings, confirming that discontinuation of antibiotics after negative microbiology remains a persistent clinical challenge [13, 14]. Our findings suggest that even in a secondary-care regional hospital, antibiotic continuation beyond five days is common, particularly among vulnerable subgroups. The threshold of > 5 days for defining prolonged therapy, while clinically practical, may vary across institutions and should be interpreted with caution when comparing results across different settings.

Prematurity emerged as the strongest independent predictor of prolonged therapy, especially among neonates born before 32 weeks of gestation. Substantial variation in antibiotic use across neonatal intensive care units has been well documented [15]. The overlap between physiological immaturity and signs suggestive of sepsis complicates clinical decision-making and often results in a precautionary approach favoring continuation of treatment.

Very low birth weight neonates have been shown to experience higher antibiotic exposure and are more likely to receive prolonged antibiotic therapy [16]. Clinicians may be less inclined to discontinue antibiotics early in this population due to concerns regarding rapid clinical deterioration, even in the absence of microbiological confirmation.

Elevated CRP levels were also significantly associated with treatment duration exceeding five days. Although CRP is widely used in neonatal sepsis evaluation, its specificity remains limited, particularly in preterm neonates [17]. The reliance on biomarkers such as CRP in isolation may lead to overestimation of infection risk, particularly in preterm neonates where inflammatory responses are often nonspecific. Several studies have shown that reliance on single inflammatory markers may contribute to unnecessary antibiotic exposure when cultures remain negative [18]. Biomarker-guided discontinuation protocols, incorporating serial CRP or procalcitonin measurements, have demonstrated reductions in antibiotic duration without compromising safety [19]. The integration of such structured algorithms may represent a feasible intervention in similar hospital settings.

Mechanical ventilation independently predicted prolonged therapy, reflecting the interplay between clinical severity and antimicrobial exposure. Neonates requiring respiratory support are often perceived as high risk for sepsis, and antibiotic discontinuation may be deferred despite negative cultures. Clinical uncertainty and the fear of undertreating a potentially life-threatening infection remain important drivers of prolonged antibiotic use in neonatal care. Previous studies have identified clinical severity and the need for intensive care support, including mechanical ventilation, as factors associated with prolonged antibiotic therapy in neonatal intensive care units [20].

A notable finding of this study is the substantial use of Watch antibiotics (38.6%) and the strong association between Watch- and Reserve-based regimens and prolonged therapy. Third-generation cephalosporins and carbapenems demonstrated the highest proportion of therapy exceeding five days. This pattern is concerning in light of global efforts supporting the WHO AWaRe (Access, Watch, Reserve) classification, which aims to optimize antibiotic use and support antimicrobial stewardship by encouraging the use of Access antibiotics and restricting Watch and Reserve agents [21]. This suggests a potential imbalance in prescribing patterns, highlighting the need for targeted stewardship interventions to promote more rational antibiotic selection. In neonatal intensive care units, the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics has been associated with increased colonization by multidrug-resistant organisms and the emergence of antimicrobial resistance [22].

Carbapenem-based regimens, in particular, were associated with the highest likelihood of prolonged treatment. Similar trends have been reported in European NICUs, where early escalation to meropenem frequently resulted in extended courses regardless of culture results [23]. From a stewardship perspective, carbapenem initiation should ideally be reserved for confirmed or strongly suspected resistant infections. Our findings underscore the need for structured escalation and de-escalation protocols tailored to local resistance epidemiology.

The overall antibiotic exposure measured as 724 DOT per 1000 patient-days indicates a considerable stewardship opportunity. Comparatively, centers implementing active antimicrobial stewardship programs have reported substantial reductions in DOT without increased adverse outcomes [24]. Interventions such as automatic stop orders at 48–72 h, prospective audit and feedback, and multidisciplinary review have been shown to safely reduce unnecessary antibiotic use in neonates [25].

Importantly, this study provides data from a regional hospital in Southeast Europe, a context that remains underrepresented in neonatal stewardship literature. Local epidemiological evidence is essential for designing pragmatic interventions adapted to available resources and institutional culture. The identification of prematurity, very low birth weight, elevated CRP, and mechanical ventilation as independent predictors may assist clinicians in developing targeted decision-support tools aimed at balancing patient safety and antimicrobial prudence.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective design precludes assessment of clinician rationale for antibiotic continuation and may introduce residual confounding. This limitation may have influenced the observed associations, as clinical decisions regarding antibiotic continuation were not standardized and may reflect individual physician judgment rather than objective criteria. In addition, incomplete or missing clinical data inherent to retrospective datasets may have introduced information bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of regression estimates. Blood culture sensitivity is inherently limited, and some cases categorized as culture-negative may have represented true infections. Additionally, long-term outcomes such as microbiome alterations or resistance patterns were not evaluated. Furthermore, diagnoses such as necrotizing enterocolitis, focal bowel perforation, pneumonia, omphalitis, cellulitis, peritonitis, and septic arthritis were not consistently documented in the retrospective dataset and therefore could not be analyzed separately. Consequently, some prolonged antibiotic courses may have reflected clinically justified treatment decisions rather than unnecessary antibiotic exposure. Nevertheless, the relatively large cohort and the integration of AWaRe classification and DOT metrics strengthen the validity and clinical relevance of the findings. Another limitation is that the retrospective dataset did not allow reliable stratification of neonates according to early-onset sepsis (EOS) and late-onset sepsis (LOS) across all cases. Consequently, separate analyses for EOS and LOS could not be performed. Given the known differences in clinical presentation, inflammatory marker interpretation, and antibiotic prescribing practices between EOS and LOS, future prospective studies should evaluate these subgroups separately.

In conclusion, prolonged empirical antibiotic therapy despite negative blood cultures remains common and is primarily driven by clinical vulnerability markers and the use of broad-spectrum agents. Stewardship interventions focusing on early reassessment, biomarker-guided discontinuation, and prioritization of Access antibiotics are warranted. Reducing unnecessary antibiotic exposure in neonates represents not only an individual patient safety objective but also a critical component of global antimicrobial resistance containment efforts.

Conclusions

Prolonged empirical antibiotic therapy despite negative blood cultures remains common in neonatal practice, affecting nearly half of culture-negative neonates in this cohort. Prematurity, very low birth weight, elevated inflammatory markers, and the need for mechanical ventilation independently increase the likelihood of extended treatment duration.

The substantial use of Watch and Reserve antibiotics and their strong association with prolonged therapy underscore a critical antimicrobial stewardship gap. Broad-spectrum regimens, particularly third-generation cephalosporins and carbapenems, were disproportionately linked to therapy exceeding five days, highlighting the need for structured reassessment and de-escalation strategies.

Implementation of evidence-based discontinuation protocols, biomarker-guided decision-making, and prioritization of Access antibiotics in accordance with the WHO AWaRe framework may significantly reduce unnecessary neonatal antibiotic exposure. Strengthening stewardship practices in neonatal units is essential not only for optimizing individual patient outcomes but also for mitigating the broader threat of antimicrobial resistance.
