Assessment of Oral Health Status among Children with Type I Diabetes Mellitus: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Nishna Thankappan, Vennila Chandran, Malini Venugopal, Greeshmaja Sharma Nirmala, Fathima M Najeeb, Fini Raj Ajith Radha, Niveditha Kartha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDiabetes mellitus (DM) is among the four noncommunicable diseases. There are two types—type I and type II. The prevalence of type I DM in India is 0.26/1,000 population (1992). This study was carried out to assess dental caries status and gingival status. The study group was compared with the nondiabetic control group to find any association with metabolic control of the disease.MethodologyA total sample size of 200 subjects was selected out of 100 patients referred to treat their poorly controlled type I diabetes mellitus at the tertiary hospital. Another 100 nondiabetic subjects were selected as controls. An oral clinical examination used a mouth mirror, dental explorer, visible light source, and cotton gauze. Decayed, missing, and filled teeth (DMFT), gingival index, plaque index, and calculus index were evaluated.ResultsComparison of the gingival index between cases and control was found to be significant, where the p-value is <0.001. This points out that type I diabetic patients have more chance of gingival problems than others. The increased plaque index indicates the need to give more importance to oral health as it may lead to an increased calculus index in the future, leading to periodontal problems. It was found that glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) is directly proportional to the gingival index.ConclusionMaintaining good oral health through regular check-ups with dental experts and motivating and educating type I diabetic patients as they are more prone to dental issues.How to cite this articleThankappan N, Chandran V, Venugopal M, et al. Introduction

Diabetes is among the major noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) in India. Diabetes is characterized by the impairment of the body to produce or respond to insulin hormones resulting in elevated blood glucose levels. Diabetes mellitus (DM) is essentially categorized into two: type I (insulin-dependent DM) and type II (noninsulin-dependent DM). Type I DM is a genetic disorder where the pancreas cannot produce insulin. It is estimated that about 8.4 million people are affected with type I DM,1 with 20% of them residing in low- and middle-income countries. The average remaining life expectancy of 10-year-old children diagnosed with type I DM in low-income countries was 13 years while it was 65 years in high-income countries.1 A 60–107% hike is predicted in cases of type I DM in low-income countries by 2040 with adults affected more.1 India records the highest prevalence of cases at 22.9 million followed by 15.7 million in the United States of America and 9.2 million in Brazil. The highest incidence of cases under 19 years is in India.2 Despite the high prevalence and increasing incidence of 3–5% cases every year,3 type I DM has not been prioritized as important compared to type II DM.4

About 30 among 1 million children up to the age of 14 years in India are at risk for being diagnosed with type I DM. Literature reports the prevalence in Karnataka at 17.93%, Karnal at 10.2%, and Chennai at 3.2%.4 The national prevalence is estimated to be 0.26/1,000 population, according to a survey conducted in 1992.3

Type I DM has bimodal onset. The first occurs at 4–6 years of age followed by another window during early puberty period (10–14 years) with a higher predilection for females. The etiology of type I DM has been found to be associated with autoimmune disorders.5 The coupled effect of factors like hereditary and environment has contributed to the etiopathology of the disease. It has been estimated that the risk of type I DM is 15-fold for first-degree relatives, and there is a 30% risk for a child to be affected by type I DM if their parents are also type I diabetic.4 Children with a genetic history have chances of developing type I DM when they are exposed to environmental factors that can trigger the onset of the disease. Viral infections,6 endocrine-related syndromes resulting in obesity,7 diet, high socioeconomic status, vitamin D deficiency, pregnancy-related issues of the mother, especially her age, diet, and gestational diabetes seem to be factors affecting the onset of type I diabetes mellitus in children. The common manifestation of type I DM is hyperglycemia without acidosis. Polyuria, polyphagia, and polydipsia are typically seen in such patients. Almost all patients report weight loss due to increased catabolism. The second most common feature of the disease is diabetic ketoacidosis without any other evident symptoms, which is considered an acute medical emergency.

Several microvascular and macrovascular complications are seen in type I DM. They include cardiovascular diseases, retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy. The damaging effect of pathogenesis in type I DM primarily affects endothelial cells and neurons. The risk of damage to eyes, kidneys, and nerves is higher due to more vascularization.8 As the oral cavity is also highly innervated and vascularized, complications related to dental health are expected.9 Diabetic patients are considered a high-risk group with greater susceptibility to periodontal destruction. Diabetic patients have increased prevalence, severity, and progression of periodontal diseases leading to tooth loss. This relationship between diabetes and periodontal diseases in children and adolescents has been reported in a few articles.10–12 The relationship between the prevalence and incidence of dental caries with diabetes is still debated.13,14 The role of microbes, including oral yeasts, Mutans streptococci, and lactobacilli, along with the decreased salivary flow and increased amount of carbohydrates in the saliva could predispose the patient to dental caries.

Hyperglycemia also affects other systems. The endothelial and peripheral nerve cells are more damaged than any other body cell.15 Due to uncontrolled blood glucose levels, the target cells cannot maintain stability in intracellular glucose levels.16 This triggers production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) leading to the inhibition of the enzyme glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). In diabetic tissue damage, four downstream agents are involved, namely (1) increased polyol pathway flux; (2) expression of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) [receptor for advanced glycation end-products (RAGEs)] receptors; and (3) activation of protein kinase C (PKC) and (4) hexosamine pathway. The AGE's mechanisms in periodontal diseases are very similar to complications of diabetes mellitus, which include increased inflammation, delayed wound healing, and enhanced oxidative damage. The AGEs accumulation causes connective tissue damage making diabetic patients more susceptible to joint-related disorders. Periodontitis also shares the same pathological pathway as AGE accumulation characterized by gingival connective tissue destruction, periodontal ligament damage, and alveolar bone resorption.11,17–19 Both type I and type II diabetic patients have shown increased AGEs and RAGE in saliva and gingival tissue.17,18 Thus, oral health changes are common in diabetic patients and need to be managed concomitantly. This study is aimed at assessing the oral health status of children with type I DM.

Methodology

This cross-sectional observational comparative study was done among children in the age-group of 5–14 years. The study was undertaken for a duration of 2 months from September to October 2022. The children were recruited to be categorized into two groups. The first group consisted of type I DM children recruited from the Department of Endocrinology and Department of Pediatrics of a tertiary health care center in Kerala, India. The second group (comparator) consisted of children without type I DM or any other systemic disease recruited from two schools in Kerala, India. Children having any medications that alter the mouth flora and immune system or stimulate the inflammatory response; any developmental anomaly or pathology of the teeth were excluded.

Assessment of demographic characteristics like age, gender, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels obtained from their previous medical records. The HbA1c is generally measured in either of these two units of measurement: mmol/mol and percent (percentage of total hemoglobin). In the present study, the unit used was HbA1c%. Oral health status was measured using standardized indices, namely decayed, missing, and filled teeth (DMFT)/deft for dental caries,20 Silness and Loe plaque index (PI)14 and calculus component of Ramfjord periodontal diseases index14,21 or oral hygiene and Loe and Silness Index14 for gingivitis. We assessed oral health status through parameters like dental caries, plaque deposits, calculus deposits, and gingival bleeding. Indices were scored ranging from 0 to 3 according to the severity of gingival inflammation and dental caries.

Sample size estimation was done based on the results of a previous study.20 Comparing the mean and standard deviation (SD) of DMFT in children with type I DM (1.26 ± 2.49) and those without type I DM (0.46 ± 1.02) and with a power of 80% and a confidence interval of 95%, the minimum sample size was estimated to be 89 in each group. To account for any missing data, sample size was increased by 10% resulting in 100 children in each group totaling 200.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board ECASM-AIMS-2022-154B and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Statistical analysis was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20. Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean and standard deviation for continuous variables and frequency and percentage for categorical variables. The t-test for significant differences between the two groups was used for outcomes following normal distribution and Mann–Whitney U test was used for nonnormal distributions. A p-value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study was conducted to analyze and compare the oral health status of children with type I DM with normal children. It was hypothesized that children with type I DM could have poor oral health indicators compared to their normal counterparts.

One of the most common dental diseases affecting children is dental caries. Dental caries is a multifactorial disease. There is a complex interaction between acidogenic bacteria and food residue. The salivary pH and survival rate of noncariogenic bacteria decide the ecology of the mouth. There is an equilibrium between the biofilms of bacteria and minerals of the tooth in the naturally healthy condition of the oral cavity. But when there is a disequilibrium inside the oral cavity, bacteria like Streptococcus mutans and related variants, lactobacilli, start producing polysaccharides which are adhesive to the tooth surfaces, causing demineralization. Thus, cariogenic lifestyle habits affect caries progression. Type I DM patients have adverse oral conditions like degenerating salivary glands leading to xerostomia and increased glucose content in saliva.22

In this study, it was observed that both groups had no significant differences in dental caries status assessed using DMFT/deft scores. One of the probable reasons for this observation could be attributed to the recruitment of children from a tertiary care hospital where a diet plan was prescribed by the medical practitioners with the help of recognized dieticians. Almost all multispecialty and tertiary hospitals offer a consultation with an endocrinologist, diabetologist, and dietician. These hospitals often conduct motivational and educational gatherings and get-together programs. Kerala is an Indian state with educationally forward communities and better health-seeking behavior, which could also have influenced our observations.

The calculus index was also comparable in the study group and control group. The reason may be the same as explained before. Diabetic patients are advised to take fiber-rich food dietary counseling, regular blood glucose monitoring, and flexibility in insulin intake. Some population-based studies have shown an inverse association between a diet with fiber-rich foods and C-reactive protein (hs-CRP). Certain studies show that C-reactive protein is a marker of inflammation.23 A pentameric protein named CRP, which is synthesized by the liver, rises in response to inflammation. The higher incorporation of green leafy vegetables and fruits in the diet is associated with lower C-reactive protein, so having a predominantly vegetarian diet tends to have decreased risk of metabolic syndrome.24 Flavonoids in fruits like apples, cranberries, and tea have antiinflammatory properties. These dietary polyphenols reduce the risk of caries.25 Fibrous foods act as saliva-stimulating agents. The intake of fiber-rich food would negatively affect the formation of calculus deposits.26 The calculus deposition also depends on different interpersonal routines and oral hygiene habits.27

Plaque index was significantly higher among type I DM children which may be related to the reluctance of diabetic patients to undergo a dental examination despite having regular medical consultations. Additionally, during the COVID-19 pandemic, regular dental visits were avoided unless there was pain (Fig. 1).28

Studies have demonstrated that parents’ and guardians’ oral health-related behavior influenced children's oral hygiene concerning the frequency of tooth brushing, maintenance of dental appointments in regular intervals, and the approach toward a frequent need for consultation.29 A positive attitude toward dental health will only develop in childhood with dental treatment experience and adequate knowledge about dentistry and its importance in general health. Poor oral hygiene habits and low motivation in daily oral cavity care in type I DM patients influence oral health.30 There was a significantly higher mean plaque index in type I DM children than in healthy subjects.31 These children are potentially at a higher risk of future oral disease due to poor oral health, which is exaggerated in uncontrolled blood glucose levels. Other extra-oral factors present may also impact the commencement and progression of oral diseases.32

The study shows that the gingival index was also significantly higher in the diseased group (Fig. 2). This could be attributed to the formation of AGEs. Type I DM patients have an increase in the expression of RAGEs. AGEs play a pivotal role in the advancement of periodontitis in persons with DM because research showed that blocking RAGE decreases periodontal tissue breakdown.9,33 Vascular changes in the diabetic-affected body increase gingival inflammation and bleeding.14 Vascular changes are observed more in uncontrollable diabetes than in well-controlled diabetes.34 A study by Vidya et al. also reported high glycemic index (GI) scores in uncontrollable diabetes.35

This study found that the gingival index was correlated with HbA1c suggesting a positive association (Fig. 3). The HbA1c levels, which provide feedback of glycemic control for the past 2–3 months are more reliable than random or fasting blood glucose levels, especially in children due to daily diet variations. It is also more acceptable for children to avoid the discomfort of multiple pricks.35,36

The gingival bleeding among type I diabetes mellitus is significantly higher in both dentitions when compared to control groups. It is also observed that a significant association of gingival bleeding with the duration of diabetes, mean HbA1c, and body mass index.37 The difference in gingival indexes in diabetic-affected people is mainly due to the variations in blood glucose levels.20 Diabetes-associated oral health complications affect the child by the age of 6 years or possibly earlier. Oral hygiene awareness among diabetic pediatric patients may be necessary to prevent future periodontal complications.38 Gislen et al. observed that children with poor oral health have uncontrollable metabolic glucose levels more than children with proper management.20,39

Carelli et al.40 mentioned the role of glycemic control in diabetes patients, their impact on early alterations of oral microbiota, and their relation with the pathogenesis of both periodontitis and caries. They have found that children with a poor glycemic index had increased presence of Streptococcus mutans and Veillonella spp., increasing the probability of periodontal and caries experiences in these children. They also mentioned decreased microbiological species like Tannerella forsythia, Treponema denticola, and Porphyromonas gingivalis, red-complex bacteria in children who routinely brush their teeth and get professional help for good oral health.40

Based on the observations from the study, we recommend educating diabetic-affected children and their parents on maintenance of good oral hygiene. This could be achieved by regular dental visits and referrals to dentists by medical practitioners. Self-oral care practices should be stressed for this groups as children might not prefer multiple visits to the hospital. We also recommend to include oral health in health policies. The Kerala state government introduced a program in 2018 called “Mittayi” by the Kerala Social Security Mission (KSSM) to offer free treatment for T1DM children from poor socio-economic classes, but unfortunately, they do not include an oral health component.

We acknowledge a few limitations of the study. The samples were recruited from a tertiary care hospital in an urban area who could be more educated with better socioeconomic status. Hence, positive health care behaviors could have resulted in better oral health outcomes like dental caries. The same observations may not be similar for children from poor socioeconomic status. The study was also cross-sectional because of which causal associations could be established. A longitudinal or cohort study might provide more precise estimates.

Conclusion

In our study, we found that the increase in poor gingival health was directly related to glycemic control among children with type I DM. Plaque index was higher among the diseased group indicating possibility of worsening oral health. No differences were noted in their caries status.
