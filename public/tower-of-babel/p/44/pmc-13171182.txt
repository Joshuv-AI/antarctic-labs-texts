Epidemiological pattern of dengue infection in South Andaman: a 5-year analysis of primary and secondary infections
Authors: Rajamani Murugesan, Kannan Thiruvengadam, Sunish Pulikkottil, Vijayachari Paluru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDengue fever remains a significant public health concern in endemic regions. This study examines the occurrence of primary and secondary dengue infections in South Andaman over a 5-year period (2018–22), mainly focusing on demographic and environmental factors influencing transmission. A total of 14 783 individuals with suspected dengue were tested using NS1 Antigen ELISA, IgM, and IgG antibody assays. Data on age, gender, location (urban vs. rural), household exposure, and clinical symptoms were analysed to identify patterns and risk factors related to dengue infections. Primary dengue infections were found in 6.6% of cases, while 2.5% had secondary infections, and 5.5% showed signs of past infections. Higher infection rates were observed among males and individuals aged 16–30 years, followed by those of <15 years. Urban areas reported higher rates of both primary and secondary infections compared to rural areas. Household exposure to dengue cases significantly influenced infection rates. Respiratory symptoms were also strongly linked to dengue infections. This study identifies key demographic and urban-rural risks for dengue in South Andaman. Our results dictate the need for targeted vaccination in the age group of <15 years, as per WHO criteria, intensified urban vector control, and preventive household interventions to effectively reduce the disease burden in this endemic region.

Introduction

Dengue, caused by virus, is a prevalent mosquito-borne disease predominantly found in tropical and subtropical regions. The virus is primarily transmitted to humans by its main mosquito vectors, Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus. Dengue is categorized into four distinct serotypes, viz; DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, and DENV-4. Each serotype can lead to a spectrum of clinical manifestations, ranging from mild dengue fever to severe forms such as dengue haemorrhagic fever (DHF) and dengue shock syndrome (DSS). The circulation of multiple serotypes in endemic areas poses challenges for disease management and immunity development [1, 2].

Primary dengue infection occurs when an individual has initial exposure to the virus and lacks pre-existing immunity. Clinically, it is characterized by symptoms like high fever, severe headache, joint and muscle pain and rash [3].

Secondary dengue infection occurs when a previously affected individual, with one or more dengue serotype is exposed to a different serotype. This type of infection is associated with a higher risk of severe dengue manifestations, such as dengue haemorrhagic fever and dengue shock syndrome, due to antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) [4]. This condition occurs when antibodies from prior infection facilitate the entry of new serotype into cells, leading to increased viral replication. This heightened replication can provoke a more intense immune response, potentially causing severe symptoms and complications including plasma leakage, bleeding, organ impairment, and shock[5–7].

The non-structural protein 1 (NS1) antigen typically appears in the bloodstream early in dengue infection, usually within 1–3 days after the onset of symptoms. It can persist for up to 9–10 days [4]. Immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibodies are usually detectable 3–5 days after the symptom onset, peak within a few weeks, and typically persist for about 2–3 months, serving as a marker of recent dengue infection [8]. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies appear near the end of the first week of infection and usually become detectable after the acute phase. They can persist for years, providing long-term immunity [9].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), an estimated 100–400 million dengue infections occur worldwide annually, resulting in ∼10 000–40 000 deaths each year [5, 10]. The regions most affected by dengue in Asia include Southeast Asia and the Western Pacific. Dengue is a significant public health concern in various countries across Asia. Countries such as Thailand, Vietnam, The Philippines, India [11], Indonesia and Sri Lanka [12] have reported a high burden of dengue cases [13, 14], and severe dengue outbreaks, characterized by increased severity due to secondary dengue infections. In India, the states of Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, two-thirds of the cases were secondary dengue while in other states, it is primary dengue [15].

In Andaman and Nicobar Islands, dengue was first identified in the year 2010, during which 80 positive cases of dengue were confirmed from 516 suspected cases using IgM ELISA and RT-PCR tests. The serotypes DENV1 and DENV2 were identified [14, 16]. By 2013, there was a spike in dengue cases among the security staff in Sri Vijaya Puram (Port Blair), and DENV3 serotype was identified [17]. In 2014, an outbreak in Havelock Island involved A. albopictus mosquitoes, during which DENV3 was identified [18]. DENV4 serotype was identified in the year 2018, in dengue-suspected patients from the hospital [19]. These outbreaks resulted in a surge of hospitalization, strained healthcare resources, and significant public health challenges. These instances underscore the substantial impact of secondary dengue infections on public health systems in the region [20].

Understanding the distribution of primary and secondary dengue is crucial for several reasons, like, improving clinical management and reducing mortality rates. Recognizing areas with predominant secondary dengue cases is important for effective implementation of vaccine. Identifying newly introduced dengue cases allows for targeted disease prevention and control strategies. This will ensure efficient resources and proactive measures to manage and control dengue effectively. Early intervention and supportive care are essential to reduce the risk of mortality [21, 22].

Methods

The study was carried out in the health facilities of South Andaman, Andaman and Nicobar Islands between January 2018 and December 2022. The facilities included were the district hospital, community health centre and primary health centres. The study participants included were those who visited these health facilities and were suspected of dengue infection by a physician, as per the World Health Organization, i.e. patients with one or more symptoms; fever with a temperature of 40°C/104°F, headache, eye pain, joint pain, nausea, vomiting, swelling, and rash. The study was approved by the institutional human ethics committee.

Sample collection

The patients were enquired on their demographics, household information, and recent travel history, because dengue virus can disseminate by travel, irrigation, and poor housing [23]. Blood samples (3–5 ml) were collected from people suspected of dengue symptoms. Blood was drawn into a vacutainer without EDTA. The vacutainer was centrifuged to separate the serum. Serum was collected in a cryovial and kept at −80°C for further processing. The dengue suspected patients having symptoms for <5 days were tested with NS1 Antigen ELISA, and those with symptoms ⩾5 days were tested with IgM Antibody ELISA. All the suspected samples were tested for IgG Antibody ELISA. The ELISA was selected, as this is the most practical tool for routine public health surveillance, which was the primary scope of this study, despite the known potential for cross-reactivity.

Dengue infection: case definition

Patients were selected, if they had high fever, severe headache, retro-orbital pain, arthralgia (joint pain), myalgia (muscle pain), rash and mild bleeding manifestations (such as petechiae or gum bleeding). In severe dengue cases (DHF and DSS), patients are enrolled by plasma leakage, haemorrhage, organ impairment severe hypotension and circulatory failure [24].

Respiratory infection case definition

Respiratory symptoms related to dengue were less common but can occur, particularly in severe dengue cases. Patients with cough, dyspnoea (shortness of breath), and pleural effusion (accumulation of fluid in the pleural cavity) were selected [25].

Diagnosis of primary and secondary dengue infection

To identify primary dengue, dengue NS1 antigen ELISA was carried out in patients with 1–5 days of symptoms, while IgM antibody ELISA in those reported of symptoms for more than 5 days. Samples that were NS1/IgM positive were processed for IgG secondary antibody ELISA. If both the primary and secondary ELISA tests were positive, the patient was considered to have secondary dengue fever [22].

Classification of primary, secondary and past infection for dengue

Patients were categorized based on the following serological profiles: a positive test for NS1 and/or IgM with a negative IgG was classified as a primary dengue infection. A positive result for NS1 and/or IgM with a positive IgG was classified as a secondary dengue infection. A patient testing negative for NS1 and IgM but positive for IgG was indicative of past dengue infection [26].

Statistical methodology

The statistical analysis was performed using STATA version 18.5. Descriptive statistics for categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, with prevalence estimates calculated alongside 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using exact binomial methods.

Associations between categorical variables and dengue infection stages were evaluated using Chi-square tests. Prevalence ratios (PRs) with 95% CIs were estimated through log-binomial regression models to identify factors associated with primary, secondary and past dengue infections. Reference groups were predefined for each variable category, and the significance of associations was assessed using Wald chi-square tests with two-sided p-values. A P values less than .05 was considered statistically significant. Graphical representations were used to display symptom proportions across infection classifications.

Results

Among 14 783 individuals suspected of dengue infection in South Andaman, overall prevalence rates were determined (Figure 1). Primary dengue infection was identified in 6.6% of patients (95% CI: 6.2–7.0), while secondary infection accounted for 2.5% (95% CI: 2.3–2.8). Notably, a significant finding emerged regarding past infections, estimated to be 5.5% (95% CI: 5.1–5.9). These results suggest that 15% of the suspects were exposed to dengue.

Demographic analyses revealed that male individuals showed a higher infection rate when compared to females, with rates of 7.3% and 5.8%, respectively (P < .001) (Table 1). Considering the type of infection, primary infection was most prevalent among individuals aged 16–30 years (8.3%), followed by those aged ≤15 years (7.2%). The prevalence of primary infection among patients aged ≥45 years was 4.0%. For secondary infection, the highest prevalence was observed in individuals aged 16–30 years (3.2%), and the lowest in those aged ≥45 years (1.5%). Past infections were most common in the age group of ≥45 years (6.2%) (Table 1). From the analysis it was observed that dengue haemorrhagic fever was highest in the those with secondary infection (84.4%) (Table 1).

Moreover, residents of urban areas demonstrated a higher prevalence of primary (7.8%) and secondary dengue infection (3.2%) compared to their rural counterparts (primary: 5.0%, secondary: 1.7%) (P < .001 for both). Similarly, urban residents showed a higher rate of past infection (7.1%) than rural residents (3.3%) (Table 1).

Respiratory symptoms were significantly associated with dengue infection. Patients with more than two respiratory symptoms were found with the highest prevalence rate of primary dengue (15.3%), secondary dengue (10.7%), and past infection (10.5%). Those with at least one respiratory symptom had a primary infection rate of 7.5% (PR = 6.90, 95% CI: 5.80-8.20; P < .001), Secondary infection rate of 6.9% (PR = 14.63, 95% CI: 10.39–20.60; P < .001), and past infection rate of 8.0%. Patients without any respiratory symptoms had a primary infection rate of 2.5%, Secondary infection of 0.5%, and past infection of 4.3%. Respiratory symptoms were associated (P > .001) with both primary and secondary dengue infections; PR = 8.95 (95% CI: 7.80–10.28) for primary, and 22.66 (95% CI: 16.93–30.33) for secondary infections, respectively (Table 2; Fig. 2). In case of dengue haemorrhagic fever, the prevalence of patients with a positive secondary infection was 84.4% (95% CI: 76.9–90.2) among those diagnosed with dengue haemorrhagic fever.

Discussion

In the present study, the dengue prevalence of 6.6% (primary infection) in South Andaman indicates a state of moderately endemic, and is consistent with other regional studies. This finding is significant within the global context, where an estimated 400 million people are infected annually [13], and it identifies South Andaman as another endemic region requiring sustained public health attention.

Primary and secondary infections

There was gender difference, with higher primary infection among males. Similar findings of gender-based differences in dengue prevalence were reported from other parts of India [27, 28]. The higher seroprevalence in males likely reflects their predominance in outdoor activities, vector-prone sectors like fishing, agriculture, tourism, construction, and port activities. The urban-rural difference in secondary infections highlights how travel, tourism, and population density in South Andaman play a significant role in dengue transmission dynamics [29, 30], as this area is a major destination for tourist across India and abroad. Among different age groups, individuals in the group 16–30 years had higher susceptibility to dengue infection compared to older people. Younger individuals are often vulnerable to primary dengue virus because they lack early exposure to infection and immunity. On the other hand, the lower prevalence of primary infection in older age groups could indicate acquired immunity from past infections [28, 29]. High rate of primary infections within the households indicates focal transmission, where infected Aedes mosquitoes feed on multiple family members. This underscores the need for community-based surveillance and presumptive interventions, like larval source reduction in containers to prevent cluster outbreaks [31, 32]. Also, a significant proportion of past infections are observed (5.5%), which indicates that this population has been already exposed to the virus, which can also affect the future outbreaks and severity of the infection. It is very important to understand and study the seroprevalence, immune system in the host to design an effective vaccine and to identify the impact of new dengue serotypes [23]. When compared with other studies our findings align with global trends observed in other research. For example, one study reported that dengue prevalence and severity are affected by demographic and environmental factors, underscoring the need for targeted public health interventions [3]. In the present study, severe dengue (DHF) was found to be more prevalent among individuals with secondary infections, which is almost 84.4% prevalence. Compared to primary infection, it was 68.8% higher in secondary infection patients, which is mainly due to antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) [3]. This highlights the need for effective vaccination strategies to prevent severe forms of the disease, particularly in children under 15 years of age, as recommended by WHO. Similarly, another study indicates the differences in urban-rural transmission which was also observed in the present study by highlighting the various important challenges in the control of infection in Southeast Asia [33].

The findings from this study are also relevant to the emerging dengue situation in the European Region, where autochthonous transmission driven by A. albopictus is rapidly expanding [34]. World Health Organization has recently reported that [35] dengue is spreading to the European and Eastern Mediterranean regions. In the year 2024, 308 cases were reported from three European countries (France, Italy, and Spain), and an additional 1291 cases and four deaths were recorded in the French overseas territories of Mayotte and Reunion. Europe’s population is currently largely dengue-naive, experiencing primary infections; however, with progressive introduction of multiple serotypes via international travel, the transition to secondary infections, and the associated ADE-driven severity documented in our cohort (84.4% DHF in secondary cases) becomes an imminent risk [36, 37]. Our travel-associated risk findings (PR = 2.26 for primary; PR = 2.11 for secondary) directly mirror how imported viraemic travellers seed European outbreaks [4, 38]. The NS1/IgM/IgG-based surveillance framework employed here offers a scalable, low-cost model adaptable to European public health systems with limited dengue testing experience [36].

Studies have shown a significant relationship between dengue and respiratory symptoms. For example, cough and rhinorrhoea were present in a significant number of dengue patients which supports the findings in South Andaman. In addition, a significant number of cases have household contact with a history of dengue and more probable explanation for the observed clustering is the pervasive presence of infected Aedes mosquitoes within and around households, simultaneously exposing all family members to infective bites [39]. Another study suggests a potential for atypical transmission of dengue virus, but the dominant route remains mosquito-borne [25]. In our data, respiratory symptoms were strongly associated with both primary and secondary dengue infection, with the highest prevalence ratios observed for secondary infection, suggesting that respiratory involvement may be a clinical correlate of more severe or complicated disease. This is biologically plausible, as severe dengue and secondary infections are characterized by heightened systemic inflammation, capillary leak, and pleural effusion, all of which can manifest as cough, dyspnoea, and other respiratory complaints.

This study provides a critical epidemiological profile of dengue in South Andaman, revealing distinct risk patterns that inform targeted public health action. The high burden of primary infections among 16–30 age group in urban centres like Sri Vijaya Puram (Port Blair) underscores the need for focused vector control and community education in these demographics. The age group <15 years are equally found with high primary infection rates (7.2%). This age group can be targeted for vaccination as per WHO recommendation [34, 40].

The significant household clustering of cases supports implementing a proactive “household cluster” response strategy around confirmed infections. Furthermore, strong association with respiratory symptoms suggests their integration into clinical surveillance facilitating in earlier detection. For clinicians in South Andaman, respiratory symptoms, particularly when multiple features are present in a suspected dengue case should be treated as a red flag for possible secondary infection and higher risk of severe disease, warranting closer monitoring and prioritized triage.

The factors linked to secondary infections highlight populations at heightened risk for severe dengue, guiding the need for enhanced clinical training and early warning systems. The geographic distribution of past infections (urban residents with more respiratory symptoms and those >45 years) offers a strategic map for future serosurveys. The significant association of past infection with the age groups of 16–30 and 31–45 years suggests that many individuals in these cohorts were exposed to dengue infection earlier in life, resulting in a high seroprevalence of past infection. For CYD-TDV (Dengvaxia), current WHO-aligned recommendations restrict use to persons with evidence of prior dengue infection because vaccination of seronegative individuals increases the risk of severe disease; therefore, a high proportion of seropositive individuals in this age range would actually favour the safer implementation where this vaccine is available. For TAK-003 (Qdenga), which can be administered irrespective of baseline serostatus but shows higher efficacy in seropositive recipients, elevated seroprevalence in 16- to 45-year age group strengthens the expected impact of vaccination campaigns in suitable high-transmission settings. Thus, our findings support the potential relevance of targeted dengue vaccination in this age groups, subject to local policy, programmatic feasibility, and careful benefit risk assessment. The association of dengue infections (primary, secondary and past) with urban areas highlights the need for more frequent and stringent vector control awareness campaigns to these residents, especially through the school/college students and also through the local administration. The presence of respiratory symptoms was also significantly associated with dengue infection, underscoring the need to raise awareness among the community and health workers to screen individuals presenting with such symptoms. Given the extended monsoon season in the study area (from May to November), respiratory symptoms are likely to be common in the community during this period.

While these findings provide a foundation for intervention, they should be considered with the limitations of the study, as ELISA was used without PRNT confirmation, flavivirus cross-reactivity cannot be ruled out. Additionally, the hospital-based study design may have missed mild or asymptomatic dengue cases in the community.
