Proliferative Necrotising Otitis Externa in a Cat Treated Successfully With Oclacitinib Maleate With Two Delayed Relapses
Authors: Tian Chee, David Robson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case report documents the successful treatment of a cat with proliferative necrotizing otitis externa (PNOE) using oclacitinib. Novel to this report, two episodes of delayed relapses occurred prior to complete spontaneous resolution, with an apparent dose‐dependent response to oclacitinib. Oclacitinib led to complete remission of the initial lesions and those of the first relapse, and the lesions of the second relapse resolved spontaneously. This adds additional evidence to support the use of oclacitinib as an alternative or adjunctive off‐label treatment for PNOE, especially in refractory cases. Further studies are needed to assess the safety of long‐term use, and to confirm ideal dose rates and treatment duration.Keywords: cat, externa, necrotizing, oclacitinib, otitis, proliferative

Introduction

Proliferative necrotizing otitis externa (PNOE) is a rare, feline immune‐mediated disease with a strong predilection for young cats. PNOE predominantly affects the external auditory meatus and concave pinnae, with lesions characterized by proliferative adherent crusting, overlying erosive to ulcerative dermatitis with epidermal necrosis, often with secondary infections (Tjalsma and Meertens 2023; Mauldin et al. 2007; Vidémont and Pin 2010). A single study has also reported extra‐auricular lesions associated with the otic lesions of PNOE (Panzuti et al. 2021).

The cause of PNOE is not well understood, but it shares some histopathological similarities with (especially hyperkeratotic) erythema multiforme. Both conditions involve apoptotic keratinocytes scattered throughout a markedly hyperplastic and spongiotic epidermis with changes extending along follicular walls. There is typically a moderate to marked, superficial, interstitial to perifollicular, lymphocyte‐rich and occasionally eosinophilic inflammatory infiltrate. One case report of a cat with PNOE describes a close association between caspase‐3‐stained keratinocytes and CD3+ T cells, suggesting T‐cell‐mediated keratinocyte apoptosis (Vidémont and Pin 2010). Underlying triggers for PNOE are unknown, but the presence of cytotoxic T cells suggests possible links with drug administration, infection (particularly viral), neoplasia and autoimmunity (Vidémont and Pin 2010).

Reported treatment options are limited and include ciclosporin, tacrolimus, oral and/or topical antimicrobials, oral and/or topical corticosteroids, and more recently, oclacitinib (Tjalsma and Meertens 2023; Mauldin et al. 2007; Vidémont and Pin 2010; Panzuti et al. 2021; Koch and Torres 2023). Spontaneous resolution has been documented between 10 months and 1.5 years of age in juvenile onset cases, but there are emerging reports of medium‐term relapses (Panzuti et al. 2021).

Oclacitinib is a Janus kinase inhibitor (JAKi) developed for the symptomatic treatment of canine atopic dermatitis (FDA Freedom of Information 2013). Oclacitinib specifically inhibits cytokines that signal via the JAK/STAT signalling pathway and is relatively selective for JAK1 (FDA Freedom of Information 2013). It inhibits receptor activation of several cytokines potentially involved in sterile, immune‐mediated diseases, including IL‐2 and IL‐15, which are mediated via JAK1/JAK3, and IFN‐γ, which is mediated via JAK1/JAK2 (Walczak and Burton 2020). There are several published case reports of the efficacy of oclacitinib in the treatment of canine auto‐immune subepidermal blistering dermatoses, generalized ischaemic vasculopathies, pemphigus vulgaris and hyperkeratotic erythema multiforme (Walczak and Burton 2020).

Apoquel (oclacitinib maleate, Zoetis) is not licenced for use in cats but has been used at doses of 1–2 mg/kg once daily for up to 5 months (Moore et al. 2022) for the treatment of feline atopic skin syndrome (Walczak and Burton 2020), pemphigus foliaceous (Carrasco et al. 2021) and PNOE (Koch and Torres 2023; Chan et al. 2024). Adverse reactions to oclacitinib monotherapy have been rarely reported, though there are reports of transient gastrointestinal upset in some cats receiving doses of 2 mg/kg once daily (Lopes et al. 2019). In addition, a single case report describes fatal disseminated toxoplasmosis in an FIV‐positive cat treated with oclacitinib 1 mg/kg twice daily per os as monotherapy for 5 months (Moore et al. 2022). In that case, systemic corticosteroids were used for a month prior to the oclacitinib course, but no concurrent medications were being used at the time of the Toxoplasma diagnosis. Further reports are required regarding the pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics and safety of oclacitinib in cats.

In this case study, we report the response to oclacitinib in the treatment of one cat with PNOE through two relapses, to its ultimate spontaneous resolution.

Case Report

A 9‐month‐old domestic short hair cat presented with a 4‐month history of marked, bilateral, ceruminous and purulent aural discharge and aural discomfort, observed as headshaking and ear‐scratching. Prior to referral, topical antibacterial and anti‐inflammatory treatments (Dermotic ear and skin suspension twice per day for 7 days, Troy Laboratories Pty Ltd, AUS, Baytril Otic, Bayer, USA, once per day for 7 days) and oral cephalexin (22.4 mg/kg twice daily for 14 days, Kefvet, Pharmachem, Eagle Farm, QLD, AUS) had not effected any improvement in the appearance of the lesions, or in the associated discomfort. An anti‐inflammatory dose of oral prednisolone (initially 0.3 mg/kg for 4 days, then increased to 1.1 mg/kg once daily for 10 days, Solone, iNova Pharmaceuticals AU Pty Ltd) had succeeded in reducing the aural discomfort but had not improved the lesion appearance. A flea and mite preventative containing selamectin and sarolaner (Revolution Plus, Zoetis Pty Ltd, AUS) had been concurrently administered.

At the time of initial referral presentation, the cat was bright, alert and in good body condition (bodyweight 3.86 kg, body condition score 5/9). Marked, adherent crusts overlying erythematous plaques were evident extending from the external auditory meatus to the vertical canal of each ear, with ceruminous exudate further extending to the deep horizontal canals (Figure 1, top left). Routine physical examination was otherwise unremarkable. Cytology of a swab from the vertical otic canals was consistent with sterile inflammation. Biopsy of the aural lesions was declined by the owner, but a presumptive diagnosis of PNOE was made on the typical species, age and lesion appearance. Considering the suboptimal response to corticosteroid therapy, treatment with oclacitinib was commenced following informed consent regarding the risks both of treatment without histopathological confirmation, and of using oclacitinib off‐label in cats.

Collage of PNOE lesions at the time of initial presentation (top left), and 1‐, 2‐ and 3‐weeks following initiation of oclacitinib treatment (top right, bottom left and bottom right, respectively).

Prednisolone was tapered from the 1.1 mg/kg every 2nd day dose at the time of presentation and was discontinued after 3 days. Treatment with oclacitinib (Apoquel, Zoetis Pty Ltd, AUS) was started immediately following this at a total dose of 4 mg (1 mg/kg) twice daily per os. Significant improvement was reported by Day 7 of treatment with partial lesion resolution and easier crust removal (Figure 1, top right). Crusting was minimal by Day 14 of oclacitinib treatment, with grossly normal epithelium starting to appear at previous lesion sites (Figure 1, bottom left). At Day 18, the oclacitinib dose was halved to 4 mg once daily. Improvement of the lesions continued and by Day 21, all remaining crusting had resolved (Figure 1, bottom right).

The first reassessment, in clinic, was at Day 32. This revealed no active lesions, but mild waxy exudate was evident in both ear canals with cytology consistent with bacterial otitis. Topical antibiotic ear medication (Surolan Otic Suspension, Vetoquinol, USA 0.25 mL applied to each ear canal twice per day for 6 days) was prescribed. Oclacitinib was continued at 4 mg once daily for an additional 4 weeks before cessation. The cat was 1 year old at this point.

Reassessment 2 weeks later confirmed no relapse of lesions, and otic cytology revealed only occasional degenerate neutrophils. Topical antibiotic treatment was discontinued, and the cat was monitored in case of relapse.

First Relapse

Thirty‐three weeks following cessation of oclacitinib, mild lesions described as small, dark crusts (approximately 5 mm) at the entrance of the ear canals were reported by the owner. At reassessment 43 days later, there was severe crusting and erosive lesions bilaterally in the external auditory meatus, but not in the canals nor more distally on the pinnae. Cytology confirmed superficial coccoid bacterial pyoderma. Marbofloxacin (Zenequin, Zoetis Pty Ltd, AUS) was instituted orally at 3.6 mg/kg once daily for 18 days. This dose corresponded to one whole 25 mg Zenequin tablet for a 6.89 kg cat; achieving the minimum recommended dose of 2.75 mg/kg/day would have required splitting tablets into less practical fractions. The duration of therapy was guided by prevailing recommendations for superficial bacterial folliculitis at the time (21–28 days), in conjunction with the scheduling of the follow‐up appointment. This was prior to the publication of the 2025 ISCAID guidelines on pyoderma. Oclacitinib was restarted concurrently at original dose of 4 mg twice daily. Due to significant weight gain (bodyweight 6.89 kg with a body condition score of 7/9), this represented a significant reduction in the oclacitinib dose rate to 0.58 mg/kg.

Rapid improvement occurred, and the dose was reduced to 4 mg once daily after 14 days. Continued but slower improvement was maintained with the once daily dosing, but 41 days after recommencement of treatment, mild lesions persisted. This prompted a dose increase to 4 mg twice daily for 14 days before returning to once daily dosing. Three weeks later, the inflammation and erosions had reduced but mild lesions persisted. With the bodyweight increasing further to 7.25 kg, the dose rate had reduced to only 0.55 mg/kg, thus an increase to 8 mg (1.1 mg/kg) once daily for 4 weeks was implemented. Treatment was then discontinued due to the owner's temporary absence.

The lesions never fully resolved despite the higher oclacitinib dose, and significant deterioration, particularly of the left ear lesions, ensued following cessation of treatment. Repeat cytology 5 weeks following discontinuation of treatment (impression smear from erythematous plaques beneath freshly lifted crust) confirmed relapse of superficial coccoid bacterial pyoderma, which was treated with topical mupirocin (Bactroban cream, GSK) twice daily for 14 days. Oclacitinib was restarted concurrently at 8 mg (1.05 mg/kg) once daily. Complete lesion resolution occurred within 6 weeks. Oclacitinib was continued at the same dose for 8 more weeks before cessation. Reassessment 5 weeks later revealed a single, pinpoint erythematous lesion at the right ear canal entrance, which was left untreated.

Second Relapse

Mild relapse of bilateral dark crusting recurred 15 weeks post‐oclacitinib cessation, but 4 weeks later, the lesions in the right ear resolved spontaneously and completely. Despite mild lesions persisting in the left ear, treatment was withheld given contralateral spontaneous resolution. Within 2 weeks, significant improvement occurred with the crusting epilating easily and revealing normal skin beneath. The cat's age was 2 years 9 months. The lesions remained in remission over a subsequent 22‐month follow‐up period.

Discussion

This case report describes a young cat with persistent, severe PNOE. Following a minimal response to corticosteroids, treatment with oclacitinib resulted in a positive response and eventual lesion resolution. This is the fourth, published case report of successful treatment of PNOE with oclacitinib in a cat, and provides further support for the use of oclacitinib as an efficacious and well‐tolerated, alternative treatment for this condition (Koch and Torres 2023; Chan et al. 2024). While a histopathologic diagnosis was not achieved, the characteristic clinical presentation and lesion appearance, and the subsequent response to immunosuppressive therapy, provide clinical confidence for a presumptive diagnosis of PNOE (Mauldin et al. 2007; Momota et al. 2017).

The mainstay of treatment of PNOE in cats is immunosuppressive therapy, typically with corticosteroids, ciclosporin and/or topical tacrolimus (Tjalsma and Meertens 2023; Mauldin et al. 2007; Panzuti et al. 2021; Chan et al. 2024). In the current case, prednisolone therapy administered prior to referral had not resulted in any significant improvement. The maximum, daily prednisolone dose was only 1.1 mg/kg and it is possible that higher doses would have proved more effective, but response to corticosteroids as sole treatment for PNOE has consistently been reported as limited or inconsistent (Mauldin et al. 2007; Panzuti et al. 2021; Chan et al. 2024). Oclacitinib was chosen in this young cat due to lesion severity, the previously reported favourable treatment outcomes, and its comparatively lower risk of adverse effects (Koch and Torres 2023; Chan et al. 2024). This rational was further supported by the inconsistent response rates described in other, more traditional therapies (Mauldin et al. 2007; Panzuti et al. 2021; Chan et al. 2024) and the relatively high incidence of adverse effects associated with prolonged, high‐dose corticosteroid administration (Lowe et al. 2008).

Response to oclacitinib was repeatedly superior at higher doses, with 1 mg/kg twice daily achieving remission within 21 days, unlike 1 mg/kg once daily. Indeed, marked weight gain during disease progression resulted in reduced treatment efficacy, suggesting a dose‐dependent response. While the first case reported Chan et al. (2024) achieved a good response with a relatively high oclacitinib dose (1.5 mg/kg twice daily), their second case achieved a similar outcome with only 0.5 mg/kg twice daily. Indeed, response variations likely reflect differences in lesion severity and inter‐individual pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic variations in cats. As in the current case, the drug was well tolerated in all three previously reported cases, with no apparent adverse effects regardless of dose rate.

As is typical, the cat described here developed lesions at a young age and, after a prolonged period of intermittent treatment, eventually underwent spontaneous remission. Previous reports describe typical age of onset between 2 months and 5 years with remission between 12–24 months. However, the condition is described in older cats (Momota et al. 2017) and the lesions can be persistent (Mauldin et al. 2007). As described in the current case, relapse of lesions is also reported (Tjalsma and Meertens 2023). The cat in this study experienced two relapses: the first at 33 weeks following cessation of an initial 8‐week oclacitinib treatment course, and the second 15 weeks after completion of a second, 15‐week oclacitinib course. In contrast, none of the three previously reported cases treated with oclacitinib experienced relapse. Complete remission was achieved within 5, 7 and 12 weeks, with total treatment durations of 7, 9 and more than 64 weeks, respectively. This again highlights the individual variability in treatment responses and raises the possibility that perhaps higher dose rates may have been more effective in this case. However, the ideal drug dose and treatment duration remains uncertain.

Conclusion

This case report describes the successful treatment of PNOE in a cat using oclacitinib. The findings add additional evidence to support the use of oclacitinib as an alternative or adjunctive, off‐label treatment for PNOE, especially in refractory cases. Further studies are needed to assess the safety of long‐term use, and to confirm ideal dose rates and treatment duration.
