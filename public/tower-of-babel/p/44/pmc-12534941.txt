The effect of remote ischemic conditioning combined with tirofiban on early neurological deterioration in small artery occlusive stroke
Authors: Zhiyong Zhang, Xinxiu Liu, Lei Wang, Xunming Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesEarly neurological deterioration (END) remains a significant challenge in the treatment of small artery occlusive (SAO) stroke. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of remote ischemic conditioning (RIC) combined with tirofiban for patients with SAO stroke experiencing END.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 143 consecutive patients with acute SAO stroke experiencing END who received intravenous tirofiban between January 2021 and December 2024. According to the use of RIC treatment, the patients were divided into RIC group (72 cases) and control (no-RIC) group (71 cases). The primary efficacy outcome was early neurological improvement (a decrease of ≥2 points in the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score at 7 days after END). The secondary efficacy outcomes included absolute reduction in NIHSS score at 7 days, modified Rankin Scale scores at 90 days, proportion of disabling stroke, recurrence of ischemic stroke within 90 days. The safety outcomes were symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, any ICH, adverse events, and 90-day all-cause mortality. ResultsThe RIC group exhibited a greater absolute reduction in NIHSS score at 7 days after treatment (mean ± SD: 1.67 ± 1.80 vs. 0.96 ± 1.91; adjusted P=0.015), and early neurological improvement was achieved in 55.6% of the RIC group and 29.6% of the control group (adjusted odds ratio, 3.34 [95% CI, 1.61-6.93]; P=0.001). No symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage or any intracranial hemorrhage occurred in either group. Additionally, the 90-day all-cause mortality did not differ significantly between the RIC and sham groups (1.4% vs. 2.8%, hazard ratio, 0.49 [95% CI, 0.04–5.41]; P=0.561). ConclusionsIn patients with small artery occlusive stroke experiencing END, the combination of RIC and tirofiban seems associated with early neurological improvement without increasing the risk of intracranial hemorrhage or serious adverse events. Future multicenter, large-sample randomized clinical trials are warranted to validate these results.Keywords: Remote ischemic conditioning, Early neurological deterioration, Small artery occlusive, Stroke, Tirofiban

Introduction

Small artery occlusive (SAO) stroke, primarily caused by lesions in penetrating arteries or terminal small arteries, often affects deep brain structures such as the basal ganglia, pons, and corona radiata, accounting for 20%−30% of all stroke cases [1, 2]. Although the symptoms are relatively mild at onset, more than 20% of SAO strokes experience progression of neurological symptoms, a condition known as early neurological deterioration (END), which leads to poor clinical outcomes [3–5]. Intracranial branch atherosclerotic thrombosis [6], microcirculatory hypoperfusion [7, 8], endothelial dysfunction [9], and inflammation [5, 10] are considered contributing factors to the END.

Intravenous thrombolysis is still the recommended treatment for acute SAO stroke within 4.5 h after onset, according to current guidelines. However, some patients may miss the thrombolytic window due to initially mild symptoms or delayed hospital arrival. In addition, some study have indicated that intravenous thrombolysis can’t adequately prevent early neurological deterioration, particularly in stroke patients with penetrating artery disease [11]. For neurological deterioration caused by large vessel steno-occlusion, mechanical revascularization is a rescue option. However, this strategy is not suitable for patients with SAO stroke. The CHANCE trial findings have led to the widespread adoption of dual antiplatelet therapy in patients with minor stroke [12]. Furthermore, two additional randomized controlled trials demonstrated that the combination of clopidogrel and aspirin reduces early neurologic deterioration at 7 days for patients with AIS. However, the incidence of END remains high due to drug resistance and the neurological impairment of dysphagia, which might make it difficult to administer medications promptly [13, 14]. Currently, there remains a lack of high-level recommended and effective treatment strategies for neurological deterioration caused by SAO stroke.

Tirofiban is a highly selective, rapid-onset and non-peptide antagonist of glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (Gp IIb/IIIa) platelet receptor, prevents thrombus formation by blocking the final stages of platelet aggregation [15]. Tirofiban is widely used in the treatment of acute coronary syndrome and has been extensively investigated in both preclinical and clinical studies for acute ischemic stroke. Previous studies have shown that low-dose tirofiban decreases the risk of early neurological deterioration and improves neurological outcomes for stroke without large or medium-sized vessel occlusion [16, 17]. Nevertheless, a subset of patients with END still exhibit ongoing neurological worsening despite the administration of tirofiban [18]. Therefore, there is a need to explore novel strategies to mitigate neurological deterioration.

In recent years, remote ischemic conditioning (RIC), as a non-invasive physical intervention, has shown multi-target neuroprotective potential in the treatment of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) [19]. RIC induces protective effects on distant organs (such as the brain) through repeated cycles of transient limb ischemia and reperfusion [20, 21]. Several studies have demonstrated that the application of RIC in patients with acute ischemic stroke is safe, feasible, and effective. For instance, it has been shown to reduce 30-day National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale [22] (NIHSS, range 0–42; higher scores reflect more severe deficits) scores and improve 90-day neurological outcomes [21, 23–25]. The exact neuroprotective mechanisms of RIC remain unclear but may involve promoting collateral circulation in ischemic regions, improving microcirculatory perfusion, and reducing post-stroke inflammation [26–28]. However, prior studies of RIC in AIS have not stratified stroke by subtype, nor have they specifically investigated its effects in progressive SAO stroke. Moreover, the combination of RIC and tirofiban is theoretically expected to have synergistic effects on the neurological deterioration of SAO stroke, but no studies have yet validated the efficacy and safety of this combined strategy.

Herein, we examined whether RIC combined with tirofiban can better improve neurological function and outcomes in patients with SAO stroke experiencing END, compared to the use of tirofiban alone.

Methods

Study population

In this retrospective study, consecutive patients with acute SAO stroke experiencing END and treated with intravenous tirofiban were evaluated at the Beijing Geriatric Hospital between January 2021 and December 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) acute ischemic stroke within 48 h of symptom onset; (3) small artery occlusion as the etiology of the stroke, according to the TOAST (Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) classification; (4) had END, as defined as an increase of ≥ 2 points in the total NIHSS score or ≥ 1 point in the motor items of the NIHSS within 7 days after symptom onset [8, 29]; (5) intravenous tirofiban was administered within 24 h of END. The exclusion criteria were if a patient: (1) had received endovascular therapy or intravenous thrombolysis; (2) END caused by ICH or malignant edema; (3) Modified Rankin Scale [30] (mRS, ranging from 0 [no symptoms] to 6 [death]) score ≥ 2 prior to onset; (4) had any contraindication for tirofiban, such as thrombocytopenia (< 100 × 10⁹/L), elevated international normalized ratio (>1.5), prolonged prothrombin time (>1.3 times the upper limit), systemic bleeding tendencies (e.g., hematological disorders), or hypersensitivity to tirofiban components; (5) glomerular filtration rate < 60mL/min/1.73 m2.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Beijing Geriatric Hospital (IRB No. BJLNYY-2023-31) and conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients or their legal representatives provided written informed consent for the gathering of data. Before tirofiban or RIC was used, further written informed consent was signed.

Procedures

All patients received optimal medical management according to Chinese stroke guidelines. Emergent head CT was performed immediately after END to rule out cerebral hemorrhage or malignant edema.

At our institution, intravenous tirofiban is administered as early as possible within 24 h of END onset in SAO stroke patients, except for those with contraindications to tirofiban. After confirmation of END and completion of the informed consent process, the existing oral antiplatelet regimen was discontinued and replaced with intravenous tirofiban (Lunan Beite Pharmaceutical Co Ltd, Shandong, China; Specification: 50 mL/12.5 mg), with a loading dose of 0.4 µg/kg/min×30 min, followed by a maintenance dose of 0.1 µg/kg/min×71.5 h. Four hours before tirofiban was discontinued, all participants were given aspirin (Bayer HealthCare Pharmaceuticals Co Ltd, Specification: 100 mg/tablet) 100 mg plus clopidogrel (Sanofi Pharmaceutical Co Ltd, Specification: 75 mg/tablet) 75 mg per day until day 21 of symptom onset and then switched to single antiplatelet therapy.

In recent years, several randomized controlled clinical trials have demonstrated that RIC confers neuroprotective effects and improves neurological outcomes in patients with AIS [23, 25, 31]. Furthermore, the Chinese Expert Consensus on Remote ischemic Conditioning in the Treatment of Ischemic Cerebrovascular Diseases [32] recommends early initiation of RIC in acute ischemic stroke patients, given its favorable safety profile. Therefore, in our institution, for patients with acute SAO stroke who developed END despite conventional antiplatelet therapy, in addition to intensifying antiplatelet treatment with intravenous tirofiban, we also recommend the application of RIC as an adjunctive neuroprotective therapy, unless the patient or family refused or specific contraindications were present. These contraindications included uncontrolled severe hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 180 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 110 mm Hg after agent treatment), a distal upper limb with perivascular lesions or serious soft tissue injury, fracture, vascular injury and severe coagulation dysfunction. The patients received RIC treatment on the bilateral upper limbs at the same time or within 24 h of tirofiban application. The RIC procedure was administered using an electric automatically controlled device (patent number ZL201410833405.2, China) by a trained nurse. The RIC protocol consisted of 5 cycles of cuff inflation (200 mm Hg for 5 min) and deflation (for 5 min), for a total procedure time of 50 min, twice daily for 7 days [25]. Blood pressure and heart rate were recorded prior to cuff inflation and after completing five cycles of cuff inflation.

Based on whether RIC treatment was administered within 24 h of intravenous tirofiban application, the patients were divided into RIC group (RIC + tirofiban) and control (tirofiban alone) group.

Data collection and assessment

Baseline demographic, vascular risk factors, laboratory information for each enrolled patients were collected from the database. including age, sex, and presence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, hyperhomocysteinemia, prior acute ischemic stroke, coronary artery disease, current or previous smoker, and current or previous drinker; admission blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, and location of cerebral infarction. NIHSS scores at admission, the occurrence of END, and 7 days after END were obtained. We also obtained data about mRS scores at 90 days, recurrence of ischemic stroke, death within 90 days, and adverse events including RIC-related adverse events or other serious adverse events.

The primary efficacy outcome was an early neurological improvement (ENI; a decrease of ≥ 2 points in NIHSS score at 7 days after END [8, 33]. The secondary efficacy outcomes included: (1) absolute reduction in NIHSS score at 7 days compared with END onset; (2) the proportion of patients with mRS score of 0–1 at 90 days, which represents an excellent functional outcome; (3) ratio of patients with mRS score of 0–2 at 90 days, which reflects a favorable functional outcome [25]; (4) distribution of mRS score at 90 days; (5) proportion of patients with disabling strokes at 7 days after END, which was defined by any of the following criteria: complete hemianopia (NIHSS item 3 score ≥ 2); severe aphasia (NIHSS item 9 score ≥ 2); neglect (NIHSS item 11 score ≥ 1); any limb weakness with persistent inability to resist gravity (NIHSS items 6 or 7 score ≥ 2); NIHSS total score >5 due to functional deficits; any deficit perceived as potentially disabling by clinicians or patients, including: impaired ability to perform basic activities of daily living (bathing, ambulating independently, toileting, personal hygiene, or feeding); inability to return to work [34]. (6) recurrence of ischemic stroke within 90 days; Ischemic stroke recurrence was defined as sudden onset of a new focal neurologic deficit, with clinical or imaging evidence of infarction lasting 24 h or more and not attributable to a nonischemic cause (i.e., not associated with brain infection, trauma, tumor, seizure, or degenerative neurologic disease) [12].

The safety outcomes were: (1) 90-day all-cause mortality; (2) symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (using European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study III classification [35]) within 72 h after RIC and/or tirofiban; (3) the incidence of any intracranial hemorrhage within 72 h after RIC and/or tirofiban; (4) adverse events related to RIC, including arm pain (measured on a visual analog scale [36]), arm redness or swelling, arm skin petechiae, limb numbness, palpitations, and dizziness absent at study initiation. The qualified neurologists further determined if the adverse event was related to the RIC treatment; (5) other serious adverse events after treatment, included pneumonia, gastrointestinal bleeding, myocardial infarction and seizure within 90 days.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), and comparisons were made using the Student’s t tests or Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as the rate (percentages) and Chi-square tests were used to compare differences between two groups. For the primary and secondary outcomes, which included the proportion of patients with an early neurological improvement, mRS score of 0–1 and mRS score of 0–2 at 90 days, binary logistic regression analyses were performed in adjusted analysis and adjusted for 4 predefined prognostic factors: age, sex, NIHSS score at END and history of acute ischemic stroke. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to report treatment effects for these outcomes. The absolute reduction in NIHSS scores at 7 days from END was compared using the Student’s t-test in the unadjusted analysis and a generalized linear model in the adjusted analysis, with treatment effects expressed as mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs and regression coefficients (B values) with 95% CIs. Cox regression models were utilized to compare the 90-day all-cause mortality, recurrence of ischemic stroke and other serious adverse events occurring within 90 days after END, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) accompanied by 95% CIs. The Mann–Whitney U test was employed to assess the distribution of mRS scores at the 90-day.

Subgroup analysis of the primary outcome was performed on 7 prespecified subgroups(were selected according to the major risk factors associated with small artery occlusive stroke) : age (< 65 years or ≥ 65 years), sex (female or male), infraction location (anterior circulation or posterior circulation), history of hypertension (yes or no), history of diabetes (yes or no), current or previous smoker (yes or no), number of risk factors (≥ 3 or < 3). The heterogeneity of treatment effects across subgroups was evaluated using the binary logistic regression model, with the P value for the interaction term reported.

A 2-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using the SPSS Version 27.0 (IBM Corp.©).

Results

From January 2021 to December 2024, we identified 1,108 consecutive patients diagnosed with acute ischemic stroke at Beijing Geriatric Hospital. Of these, 143 patients with SAO stroke experiencing END and treated with intravenous tirofiban were ultimately included in our analysis. The detailed inclusion flow chart is shown in Fig. 1. Among them, 72 (50.3%) were treated with RIC (RIC group) and 71 (49.7%) were not treated with RIC (control group). The mean age was 64.9 years (SD, 11.7), and 105 patients (73.4%) were male. The median NIHSS score within 24 h after END was 5 (IQR, 4–8). Efficacy outcomes

For the primary efficacy outcome, early neurological improvement (a decrease of ≥ 2 points in NIHSS score at 7 days after END) was achieved in 55.6% of the RIC group and 29.6% of the control group, with an unadjusted OR of 2.98 (95% CI, 1.49–5.93; P = 0.002). After adjusting for age, sex, history of prior cerebral infarction and NIHSS score at END, the difference remained significant, with an adjusted OR of 3.34 (95% CI, 1.61–6.93; P = 0.001), as shown in Table 2.

For the secondary efficacy outcomes, the RIC group demonstrated a greater absolute reduction in NIHSS scores compared to control group at 7 days from END (mean ± SD: 1.67 ± 1.80 vs. 0.96 ± 1.91), with a statistically significant difference between the two groups (adjusted P = 0.015) (Table 2). Although the proportion of patients with an mRS score of 0–1 at 90 days was higher in the RIC group than control group (59.7% vs. 53.5%), the difference did not reach statistical significance, with an adjusted OR of 1.49 (95% CI, 0.63–3.52; P = 0.368) (Table 2). Similarly, the proportion of patients with an mRS score of 0–2 at 90 days showed an improving trend (86.1% vs. 80.3%, adjusted OR, 1.91 [95% CI, 0.57–6.44]; P = 0.295) (Table 2). The proportion of disabling strokes at 7 days was lower in the RIC group (62.5%) than in the control group (71.8%), but this difference was not statistically significant (adjusted OR, 0.50 [95% CI, 0.18–1.35]; P = 0.170) (Table 2). Additionally, no significant difference was observed in the recurrence rate of ischemic stroke within 90 days between the two groups (RIC 1.4% vs. sham 2.8%, adjusted HR, 0.68 [95% CI, 0.05–8.73]; P = 0.766) (Table 2). Safety outcomes

No symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage or any intracranial hemorrhage occurred in either group. Meanwhile, there was no significant difference in the 90-day all-cause mortality between the RIC and control groups (1.4% vs. 2.8%, HR, 0.49 [95% CI, 0.04–5.41]; P = 0.561) (Table 3).

With respect to RIC-related adverse events, one patient (1.4%) experienced arm pain during RIC treatment, with a visual analog scale rating of 3. Additionally, two patients (2.8%) with skin petechiae on the arm and three patients (4.2%) with limb numbness were reported, but they could cooperate well to complete the procedures. No events of limb redness or swelling, palpitations, or dizziness were noted (Table 3).

Other serious adverse events: There was no significant difference in the incidence of pneumonia between the two groups (RIC 2.8% vs. sham 4.2%, HR,0.65[95% CI, 0.11–3.91]; P = 0.641). The control group reported 1 case (1.4%) of gastrointestinal bleeding and 1 case (1.4%) of myocardial infarction, while no such events occurred in the RIC group. No seizures were reported in either group during the treatment period (Table 3).

Subgroup analyses

Prespecified subgroup analyses (including age, sex, infraction location, history of hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and number of risk factors) demonstrated that there is no significant treatment heterogeneity in the effect of RIC on the primary efficacy outcome across different patient subgroups. Although the therapeutic effect was more pronounced in certain subgroups (e.g., males, patients with hypertension, diabetes, and those with multiple risk factors), the interaction P values did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This retrospective study revealed that the combination of RIC and tirofiban, compared with tirofiban alone, resulted in a greater likelihood of early neurological improvement in patients with SAO stroke experiencing END. Additionally, it exhibited a trend toward better neurological outcomes at 90 days, without increasing the risk of intracranial hemorrhage or serious adverse events.

Several clinical trials have studied the neuroprotective effects of RIC in patients with AIS [23, 25, 37, 38]. However, there were several differences between this and previous studies. First, existing research mostly focuses on non-specific AIS populations, whereas our study specifically targets patients with SAO-type stroke who were experiencing END. To our knowledge, this has not been reported in previous studies. Second, this study innovatively explored the effect of RIC combined with tirofiban on early neurological deterioration, rather than RIC treatment alone. Although several clinical trials [16, 18] have demonstrated that tirofiban can improve neurological outcomes in patients with END, a proportion of individuals exhibit ongoing neurological worsening even with appropriate tirofiban administration. From a theoretical perspective, tirofiban rapidly inhibits platelet aggregation to block thrombus expansion [17, 39], while RIC improves microcirculatory perfusion and mitigates inflammatory and oxidative stress damage [27, 40], providing a dual protective mechanism for patients with progressive SAO stroke. Our findings confirm the efficacy and safety of this combined approach, suggesting a potential synergistic effect, which offers new insights into overcoming the current therapeutic challenges in progressive SAO stroke. Third, early neurological improvement was used as the primary outcome in the present study, while previous studies often focused on 90-day functional outcomes as the primary endpoint. Multiple studies have demonstrated that increased cerebral blood flow [41, 42] and elevated levels of anti-inflammatory and antioxidant factors [28] can be detected shortly after RIC treatment, indicating its potential for early neuroprotection.

Nevertheless, certain prior investigations [43–45], including the landmark RESIST trial, have reported neutral or negative outcomes of RIC efficacy, which may be attributed to the following factors. First, in the RESIST trial, the unilateral RIC protocol potentially delivered insufficient conditioning stimulus, whereas our protocol utilized bilateral limb stimulation. Second, the RESIST trial included a mixed cohort (82% ischemic and 18% hemorrhagic stroke), while our study exclusively enrolled ischemic stroke patients, in whom RIC’s mechanisms may differ fundamentally. Third, only 11–14% of patients in the RESIST trial had small artery occlusion (SAO) strokes, and the majority (75%) underwent reperfusion therapy. In contrast, our cohort comprised purely SAO patients, none of whom received reperfusion treatment. This distinction suggests that RIC’s neuroprotective effects may be subtype-dependent.

In our study, although a greater proportion of patients in the RIC group achieved mRS scores of 0–1 or 0–2 at 90 days compared to the control group, this difference did not attain statistical significance. Potential reasons for this observation include: (1) the relatively mild initial neurological deficits and a high rate of spontaneous recovery in patients with SAO stroke [6], leading to an underestimation of the long-term neuroprotective effects of RIC; (2) rehabilitation interventions in the later stages of stroke may partially obscure the therapeutic differences attributable to RIC. Therefore, future studies should design more rigorously controlled rehabilitation trials to better elucidate its efficacy [12]; (3) the limited sample size of this study, which may have resulted in insufficient statistical power to detect differences in secondary endpoints.

Subgroup analyses demonstrated that there is no significant treatment heterogeneity in the effect of RIC on the primary efficacy outcome. However, the therapeutic effects were more pronounced in male patients and those with comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and multiple risk factors. In an animal study, RIC was neuroprotective in both sexes, but males had a 46% reduction of infarct volume compared with 23% in females [46], which aligns with our findings. Furthermore, prior studies suggested female gender is a factor of worse outcome in acute stroke, even after thrombolytic treatment or endovascular therapy [47, 48]. Potential sex-dependent differences in the response to RIC may be due to the effect of the androgen-receptor system, enhancing its anti-inflammatory effects indirectly [49]. However, the observed gender differences require further validation, as females accounted for only 26.6% of the study population. Additionally, patients with diabetes and multiple risk factors often exhibit severe metabolic disturbances and mitochondrial dysfunction. Studies have demonstrated that RIC can enhance glucose metabolic reprogramming in the brain and improve mitochondrial function and energy metabolism, which may provide more significant neuroprotection in these patients [50, 51]. Similarly, the relatively small proportion of low-risk patients in the study may have led to an underestimation of the treatment effect, which warrants further investigation.

In our study, no intracranial hemorrhage or serious adverse events related to RIC were observed, which is consistent with previous studies [23, 25]. Although six cases of mild RIC-related adverse events were reported in the RIC group, the case records show that these patients completed the RIC procedure as normal, which demonstrated the safety of RIC combined with tirofiban during the acute phase of cerebral infarction. Coupled with the accessibility of the device and its simple operation, RIC is particularly well-suited for patients who are ineligible for reperfusion therapy and regions with limited medical resources.

This study has several limitations. First, we included only Chinese patients in this study, and the results may lack generalizability to other ethnic groups, as SAO represents a more prevalent stroke etiology in Chinese populations compared to Caucasian populations. Second, as a retrospective analysis, the allocation of RIC was mainly based on the treating physician’s discretion rather than a standardized protocol, which may have introduced selection bias. Third, the retrospective diagnosis of some patients as having SAO remains susceptible to misclassification bias and could potentially affect the interpretation of the results, particularly since the definitive cause of stroke is often uncertain in the acute phase. In addition, this study was conducted at a single center with a relatively small sample size, resulting in limited statistical power for secondary endpoints. Finally, the lack of a standardized rehabilitation protocol after discharge may have influenced the 90-day neurological function outcomes, potentially masking the long-term therapeutic differences of RIC. More rigorously controlled rehabilitation trials are needed to minimize this confounding factor.

Conclusions

For patients with small artery occlusive stroke experiencing END, there is currently no established standard of care. Our findings demonstrated that the combination of RIC and tirofiban seems associated with early neurological improvement without increasing the risk of intracranial hemorrhage or serious adverse events for this subtype of stroke. However, due to certain limitations in the present study, such as a single ethnic background, potential selection bias in patient allocation for RIC, and inherent bias in the retrospective diagnosis of stroke etiology, further high-level clinical studies are warranted to validate these findings.
