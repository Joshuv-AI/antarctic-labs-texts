The first case of diaphragm pacing system implantation in a patient with high cervical spinal cord injury in Taiwan: a case report and literature review
Authors: Yi-Hsiang Lai, Kuan-Hsun Lin, Hsu-Kai Huang, Tsai-Wang Huang, Yen-Shou Kuo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis report presents the first case of a patient with high cervical spinal cord injury who underwent successful laparoscopic implantation of a diaphragm pacing system in Taiwan. It also compares the pros and cons of laparoscopic and thoracoscopic implantation and discusses postoperative care.BackgroundThe diaphragm pacing system (DPS) represents a substantial advancement in respiratory support technology, particularly for patients with chronic respiratory insufficiency. It electrically stimulates the phrenic nerve, which in turn activates the diaphragm—the primary muscle involved in respiration [1]. This stimulation mimics the natural neural impulses that drive diaphragmatic contractions, thereby promoting inhalation and a more efficient lung ventilation. The DPS typically consists of implanted electrodes, an external pulse generator, and connecting leads [2]. It is mainly used in patients with high spinal cord injuries, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and central hypoventilation syndrome. These conditions often result in compromised neural control of the diaphragm, leading to severe respiratory insufficiency. By restoring diaphragm function, DPS can enhance the patients’ quality of life, reduce dependence on mechanical ventilators, and lower the risk of ventilator-associated complications [3]. Despite its benefits, DPS is not without challenges. Patient selection and the surgical approach are critical to perform successful DPS implantation for the restoration of diaphragm function [4]. This report presents the first case of a patient with cervical spine injury who underwent successful laparoscopic implantation of DPS in Taiwan. Furthermore, it discusses postoperative ICU care and reviews the pros and cons of different surgical approaches to performing DPS implantation.

Case presentation

Chief complaints

Generalized limb weakness and difficulty weaning off the ventilator since May 2022.

History of the present illness

The patient was 71-year-old man with no significant medical history. In May 2022, he fell and sustained cervical spine injury, which led to C3–C6 decompression surgery. After the injury, he developed quadriplegia and chronic respiratory failure, which required total dependence on daily activities. Tracheostomy and gastrostomy were performed during his initial hospitalization. Since then, he has been staying in a subacute care unit for long-term care. Currently, he is admitted for a second opinion, further evaluation, and management.

The patient underwent laparoscopic diaphragm pacing system (DPS) implantation on December 12, 2023. Because intraoperative visualization of diaphragmatic contractions was required, neuromuscular blocking agents were avoided. Anesthesia was induced with propofol and remifentanil, and maintained with desflurane. A laparoscopic approach was employed in which the falciform ligament was transected and the motor points on both diaphragms were identified. Two optimal sites on each diaphragm were selected for electrode implantation based on the maximal diaphragm contraction (Fig. 1). The electrode leads were tunneled to a percutaneous exit site, and a fifth electrode was subcutaneously implanted as an anode (Fig. 2). The entire system was tested intraoperatively, and the procedure was successfully completed without complications.

Postoperatively, the patient began a structured respiratory training regimen according to the DPS protocol. The training initially involved 6 sessions per day, each lasting 30 min. By December 14, 2023, the patient demonstrated good tolerance to DPS with ventilator support, showing increased tidal volumes from 350 to 400 mL. Although the patient reported transient dyspnea and discomfort during training, no significant oxygen desaturation occurred. Figure 3 presents the diaphragm pacing spikes, which are visible as sharp, vertical lines before the QRS complex on the ECG monitor.

The training was progressively intensified. By December 21, 2023, the patient was able to tolerate prolonged training sessions with a tracheal mask and DPS for up to 4 h twice daily. As the training continued, the patient was able to sustain DPS use for 18 h a day, although temporary fatigue was observed during CPAP attempts. By January 12, 2024, the patient successfully tolerated continuous 24-h sessions of DPS use. On January 22, 2024, the patient was transferred to the respiratory care center (RCC) for further respiratory training as his condition had stabilized. Personal and family history

The patient underwent emergency cervical spine decompression laminectomy for his injury. He experienced cardiac arrest during hospitalization in May 2022. He denied any history of smoking, alcohol consumption, or betel nut use. Furthermore, he had no known allergies and reported no specific congenital diseases or family history of cancer. Recently, he was transferred from the USA to our hospital for further evaluation and management.

Physical examination

Physical examination indicated normal findings on the chest, heart, abdomen, and back. Neurologically, the patient was alert, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of E4M6VT. However, mentation and speech were undetectable. Cranial nerve examination revealed normal visual acuity and visual fields, with equal and reactive pupils. The extraocular movements were free and conjugated, and the sensory function of the trigeminal nerve was intact. In addition, there was no obvious facial palsy. Hearing acuity was essentially normal, and nystagmus was nonexistent. Muscle power was flaccid across all four limbs, with a score of 0/5 power in all extremities. Deep tendon reflexes were present in the biceps, triceps, brachioradialis, and knees, and ankle clonus was observed. The Babinski and Hoffman signs were absent bilaterally. Moreover, sensory function in all four limbs was impaired, and cerebellar signs were undetectable.

Laboratory examinations

The patients’ laboratory results on admission were as follows: arterial blood pH, 7.560; PaCO2, 31.5 mmHg; PaO2, 119.4 mmHg; HCO3, 27.6 mmol/L; and SO2, 98.3%. The PaO2/FiO2 ratio was 298.5 mmHg. The patient was supported with pressure control ventilation (PEEP, 5 cmH2O; pressure, 18 cmH2O; rate, 12 bpm; FiO2, 40%), with SpO2 ranging from 90% to 95%. Blood examination showed elevated C-reactive protein level of 8.20 mg/L and a D-dimer level of 1.72 µg/mL.

Imaging examinations

A brain computed tomography scan showed calcification in the bilateral periventricular white matter, globus pallida, and cerebellar dentate nuclei, which were suggestive of Fahr’s syndrome, with no evidence of acute intracerebral hemorrhage. Magnetic resonance imaging of the cervical spine showed that the cervical cord was thin in caliber and had increased signal intensity on T2-weighted images at the C3–C5 levels, consistent with the sequela of traumatic injury. Electroencephalogram revealed essentially normal findings. However, chest X-ray showed blurring of the bilateral costophrenic angles, which may have been due to pleural thickening or effusion.

Multidisciplinary expert consultation

The patient received intensive care in the neurological intensive care unit with ventilator support. He was also administered empiric antibiotic treatment for urinary tract infection and ventilator-associated pneumonia under the guidance of an infection specialist. Before surgery, the postoperative pacing protocol and ventilator weaning plan were extensively discussed with the multidisciplinary team, including thoracic surgeons, neurologists, intensive care unit nurses, and respiratory therapists.

Final diagnosis

The patient’s final diagnosis was traumatic cervical spinal cord injury with sequela of quadriplegia, dysautonomia, and chronic respiratory failure.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient’s post-DPS hospital course is thoroughly summarized in Table 2. After extensive discussions with the patient and family, the decision to proceed with DPS surgery was made, and the procedure was successfully performed. Postoperatively, the patient began a structured respiratory training regimen in accordance with the DPS protocol. Notably, he demonstrated increased tidal volumes during ventilator support. By January 12, 2024, the patient successfully tolerated 24-h DPS sessions. On January 22, 2024, he was transferred to the RCC for further training under relatively stable conditions. Discussion

Indication of DPS

Diaphragmatic pacing is a viable intervention for patients with ventilatory failure secondary to severe diaphragmatic weakness or paralysis, serving to mitigate or obviate dependence on mechanical ventilatory support [5, 6]. An effective diaphragmatic pacing requires intact phrenic nerve functionality. Conventionally, phrenic nerve stimulation is achieved at the cervical or thoracic level. Nevertheless, direct phrenic nerve stimulation at the diaphragmatic level represents an alternative approach for certain patient cohorts [5].

Surgery (VATS vs. laparoscopic surgery)

Historically, phrenic pacing has been performed using cervically implanted electrodes [7]. This noninvasive approach is appealing. However, electrode damage caused by neck mobility has been a significant concern. Furthermore, the phrenic nerve at the base of the neck is often incomplete, with the accessory phrenic nerve from C5 merging with the C4 contingent behind the clavicle.

Le Pimpec-Barthes F et al. reported their 10-year experience of intrathoracic phrenic pacing. They concluded that VATS implantation of four-pole electrodes around the intrathoracic phrenic nerve is a safe procedure [8].

The laparoscopic approach for phrenic pacing is advantageous owing to its relatively low technological requirements and simplified operative and postoperative procedures, potentially promoting cost-effectiveness [9].

The benefits of VATS implantation include the rapid identification of the phrenic nerve by direct vision under a thoracoscope. However, this procedure required direct contact with the nerves and the need to mobilize a section of the nerve, which may increase the risk of nerve injury. Furthermore, VATS implantation requires more time due to the two-sided procedure, which may also increase the risk of perioperative complications, such as pneumothorax or malposition of the trocar into the liver. During thoracoscopic implantation, the risk of atelectasis and pneumonia also increases due to lung collapse, but it can be mitigated by optimizing airway clearance [10].

In comparison, implantation using the laparoscopic approach is easy and time-saving. However, mapping of the phrenic nerve motor point is crucial to determine the efficacy of the DPS. The success of this procedure requires the accumulation of experience and familiarity with the instruments.

In the absence of comparative studies, the establishment of definitive criteria for selecting between these techniques remains challenging.

This report presents the first case of a patient who underwent laparoscopic DPS implantation in Taiwan after a thorough study and discussion with an experienced surgical physician. We believe that the best implantation method can be determined based on the patient’s previous operation history as well as the expertise of the operator and the hospital facility.

Complications

The postoperative complications after the DPS implantation include a variety of issues, largely depending on the patient population and underlying conditions. Some of the reported complications were infection [11], lead displacement [12], diaphragmatic muscle fatigue [13], pneumothorax [14], device malfunction [11], as well as pain and discomfort [15, 16].

In the present case, postoperative diaphragmatic muscle fatigue and painful sensation were reported by the patient’s caregiver. Therefore, the patient’s airway hygiene was maintained, empiric antibiotic treatment was administered, and pain management was carried out during the postoperative course to facilitate the resolution of diaphragm paralysis.

Challenges faced for the first time in performing DPS implantation

Before DPS implantation, we closely collaborated with neurologists, pulmonologists, respiratory therapists, and intensive care unit nurses to thoroughly review the patient’s medical history, diagnosis, and surgical indications. The team familiarized themselves with the basic principles, functionality, and components of the DPS as well as postoperative wound care following a severe course of lectures in order to understand the mechanism of DPS action, implantation procedure, and device configuration. The synchronization between the DPS and ventilator settings was also extensively discussed.

Among the common issues with pacing that occur postoperatively are patient discomfort and pain during stimulation in the DPS, which can be alleviated by appropriate medication adjustments. Frequent adjustments of the DPS and ventilator settings is an extra workload for nurses and respiratory therapists, but it remains affordable for the them to accomplish the patient’s weaning process by the assistance of DPS system.

Our result is consistent with those of previous studies [17, 18] indicating that DPS implantation is a safe and effective treatment option for patients with high cervical spinal cord injuries, even when performed by a team implementing this treatment for the first time.

Conclusion

DPS implantation represents a safe and effective therapeutic option for patients with high cervical spinal cord injuries, demonstrating favorable outcomes even when performed by a team with no prior experience in this specific procedure Furthermore, laparoscopic implantation of the DPS after precise mapping of the motor point of the phrenic nerve is easy to perform and time-saving.
