Ten tips for the diagnosis and management of fibromuscular dysplasia
Authors: Julie Piotte, Maria Simona Stoenoiu, Cathlin Jamison, Alexander Woywodt, Alexandre Persu, Constantina Chrysochou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) is an under-recognized, non-atherosclerotic, and non-inflammatory arteriopathy that predominantly affects women and can involve multiple vascular territories. It commonly presents with hypertension, cerebrovascular events, or arterial dissections in otherwise low-risk individuals, yet diagnostic delays and management variability remain frequent. This ten tips review provides practical guidance for clinicians to improve recognition, diagnosis, and management of FMD in everyday practice. Key recommendations emphasize maintaining a high index of suspicion in patients with early-onset or resistant hypertension, pre-eclampsia, pulsatile tinnitus, or unexplained headaches. High-quality imaging with computed tomographic angiography or magnetic resonance angiography is central to diagnosis, alongside a one-time head-to-pelvis vascular assessment to detect multivessel involvement. The review highlights the importance of recognizing atypical presentations—usually unexplained vascular events—and the limited role of routine genetic screening, while advocating selective evaluation of at-risk relatives. Management strategies, including percutaneous transluminal angioplasty, focus on individualized care. Medical management prioritizes strict blood pressure control, smoking cessation, and selective use of antiplatelet therapy, while statins are not routinely indicated. Lifestyle guidance supports regular physical activity with avoidance of high-risk activities that may predispose to dissection. Special considerations include pregnancy, which is generally safe with appropriate monitoring, and migraine management, which requires balancing efficacy with vascular safety. Finally, this review underscores the importance of multidisciplinary care, patient education, and participation in registries and support networks to advance knowledge and improve outcomes in this rare disease.Keywords: aneurysm, arteriopathy, dissection, fibromuscular dysplasia, FMD, hypertension, renovascular disease

INTRODUCTION

Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) is an under-recognized, idiopathic, non-atherosclerotic, and non-inflammatory arteriopathy that disproportionately affects women and leads to stenosis, aneurysm formation, or arterial dissections of primarily small- and medium-sized arteries. It frequently presents with hypertension, cerebrovascular events, or spontaneous arterial dissections in otherwise low-risk individuals [1]. Although renal and cerebrovascular arteries are most commonly involved, FMD can affect virtually any vascular bed. Multivessel involvement is common and reported in up to 57% of patients [2].

Despite increasing awareness, diagnostic delays and management variability remain common. Subtle clinical clues are easily overlooked and atypical presentations further complicate recognition. Consequently, FMD remains under-diagnosed, with many patients receiving delayed or suboptimal care. The following ten tips (Fig. 1) aim to help bridge the gap between consensus statements and day-to-day patient care, providing clinicians with focused guidance for managing FMD and addressing patient concerns effectively.

Ten tips to recognize, diagnose, and manage fibromuscular dysplasia.

This review is based on a targeted narrative synthesis of key sources, including the International Consensus statement, major registry data [e.g. United States Registry, the European/International FMD Registry and Initiative (FEIRI), Assessment of Renal and Cervical Artery Dysplasia (ARCADIA)], and recent relevant literature identified through focused PubMed searches. Given the limited availability of randomized data in FMD, most of the following recommendations are based on a combination of observational studies, registry data, and expert consensus. Where evidence is limited, this is explicitly indicated to guide interpretation.

TIP 1: KNOW WHEN TO SUSPECT FIBROMUSCULAR DYSPLASIA

FMD should be suspected in middle-aged women presenting with a combination of suggestive features such as early-onset or resistant hypertension, pulsatile tinnitus (a “swooshing” sound in the ears synchronized with the heartbeat), cervical bruit, or unexplained vascular or neurological events. Suggestive events include subarachnoid hemorrhage [3], carotid/vertebral dissection [1, 4], renal infarction [5–7], mesenteric ischemia [8, 9], pre-eclampsia, or pregnancy-induced hypertension—especially in early onset or when severe—[10, 11] in otherwise healthy individuals (Fig. 2). Acute cerebrovascular presentations in young patients without significant atherosclerotic risk factors deserve particular attention. This might include anterior circulation (e.g. limb weakness, sensory loss, vision changes, language or cognitive difficulties) or posterior circulation (e.g. vertigo, ataxia, diplopia, sudden dizziness) signs, new-onset neck pain, or change in the character of headache. In this setting, urgent computed tomographic angiography (CTA) extending from the aortic arch to the intracranial circulation may help identify cervical artery dissections and aneurysms [12, 13].

Fibromuscular dysplasia suspicion, diagnosis, and management.

Common symptoms such as headache or neck pain—while frequently reported in FMD—are non-specific and should not, in isolation, prompt systematic screening. Although the mean age at diagnosis is ∼50 years [2, 14, 15], symptoms are often present for several years beforehand, with diagnostic delays averaging nearly 5 years [15].

While only 10%–20% patients are men [2, 14, 15], they tend to present with a more severe phenotype. Compared with women, men consistently have a higher prevalence of arterial dissections [2, 16, 17]. Additionally, men are more likely to exhibit renal complications, including renal artery dissection, renal infarction, or renal insufficiency [16, 18]. This might explain why men are often diagnosed with FMD and hypertension at a slightly younger age [2]. Nevertheless, hypertension and headache remain common presenting features in both sexes.

TIP 2: USE THE RIGHT IMAGING STRATEGY FOR DIAGNOSIS AND LIFELONG SURVEILLANCE

Establishing the diagnosis

Definitive diagnosis of FMD requires high-quality vascular imaging. Consistent with the International Consensus [1], the preferred first-line evaluation for suspected renal FMD is CTA or, if contraindicated, contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance angiography (MRA). CTA is generally favored for its superior spatial resolution and visualization of small calcifications. If non-invasive imaging is negative but clinical suspicion remains high, intra-arterial catheter-based angiography (IA)—the gold standard for FMD diagnosis—may be considered. However, IA is invasive and should be reserved for selected cases.

For cerebrovascular FMD, both CTA and MRA are considered appropriate first-line modalities. IA is rarely performed purely for diagnostic purposes and is typically reserved for therapeutic planning (e.g. aneurysm repair or revascularization).

In emergency settings such as acute ischemic stroke or suspected cervical artery dissection, CTA is preferred because it can rapidly evaluate not only the cervical and intracranial arteries, but also the aortic arch and thoracic vasculature. This broader imaging approach may help distinguish FMD from other arteriopathies and vascular disorders that can present with similar manifestations, including Takayasu arteritis, segmental arterial mediolysis, and vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome [13].

Performing “head-to-pelvis” imaging once

Given the high prevalence of multivessel involvement and the potential for FMD to affect virtually any vascular bed—including visceral and extremity arteries—patients with confirmed FMD in one territory should undergo a one-time comprehensive head-to-pelvis imaging (CTA or MRA) to assess for additional stenoses, aneurysms, or dissections [1].

Repeat imaging, however, should primarily target previously affected vascular territories to monitor known lesions (e.g. stenosis, aneurysm, dissection). Systematic re-imaging of all vascular beds is not recommended in the absence of new symptoms, given that the risk of developing new lesions in previously unaffected vascular beds appears low [19]. Evaluation of new vascular territories should be symptom-driven or guided by new clinical suspicion rather than performed routinely.

Follow-up and surveillance

Patients should have at least an annual specialist follow-up (vascular medicine, nephrology, or cardiology) to monitor blood pressure, review symptoms, and reassess vascular risk. Periodic imaging of known affected vessels is recommended to monitor for aneurysm formation or disease progression. The optimal surveillance interval remains uncertain. Imaging frequency and modality should be individualized, balancing disease severity against cumulative radiation exposure—particularly in younger patients. Although there are insufficient data to recommend a precise imaging follow-up algorithm, the authors suggest that a periodic imaging at intervals varying between 2 and 3 years may be reasonable, alternating between Doppler ultrasound/MRA and CTA (when higher spatial resolution is required and radiation exposure is acceptable). This interval could be extended to 5 years if lesions are mild or stable and non-progressive in repeated imaging. In the case of aneurysm, follow-up should follow specific aneurysm guidelines. Factors such as lesion severity, vascular bed involved, and particularly aneurysm characteristics (e.g. size, location, and morphology) should guide surveillance frequency.

Controversial role of duplex ultrasound

The role of renal duplex ultrasound in FMD remains debated. The first consideration is that it may provide functional information, particularly hemodynamic assessment through velocity measurements and waveform analysis. However, diagnostic velocity thresholds have been extrapolated from studies in atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis; therefore, their validity in FMD is uncertain [13, 20]. Also, although it is non-invasive and avoids both contrast and radiation, renal duplex ultrasound is highly operator-dependent and technically demanding [21]. Image quality may be limited in patients with elevated body mass index or significant bowel gas, and distal arterial segments or branch vessels—where FMD frequently occurs—may not be adequately visualized. Mild or subtle lesions can also be missed. For these reasons, duplex ultrasound should be performed only in experienced centers with specific expertise in FMD imaging [1]. It may be useful for follow-up in selected patients but should not replace high-quality cross-sectional imaging in most clinical settings.

TIP 3: RECOGNIZE ATYPICAL PRESENTATIONS AND ASSOCIATED VASCULAR MANIFESTATIONS

Atypical lesions

FMD does not always present with the classic multifocal “string-of-beads” appearance. However, the presence of at least one arterial stenotic lesion is required to establish the diagnosis [1]. Vascular tortuosity, dissections, or aneurysms alone—even in commonly affected vascular beds—are nonspecific and should prompt careful evaluation to exclude alternative arteriopathies. Nonetheless, patients presenting with these anomalies deserve close attention. These findings may represent an early or incomplete expression of FMD, or they may reflect a distinct vascular disorder altogether. In young patients presenting with renal artery dissection, a systematic brain-to-pelvis CT angiography could be considered as it may convey relevant prognostic information. In a series of patients with nontraumatic renal artery dissection systematically explored with whole-body imaging, an underlying vascular disease was identified in ∼60% of cases, including FMD in 26%, and was associated with a substantially higher risk of new-onset renal or extrarenal dissections during follow-up [22]. It seems reasonable to extrapolate this strategy to cervical or other dissections. Lesion morphology and distribution should therefore guide initial vascular workup and follow-up strategy. Referral to a vascular specialist is advisable when diagnostic uncertainty exists.

Association with spontaneous coronary artery dissection

The relationship between spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) and FMD remains debated. It is unclear whether SCAD represents a coronary manifestation of FMD or a distinct but overlapping arteriopathy. Nonetheless, a strong association exists: approximately half of patients with SCAD have extracoronary FMD lesions [23, 24]. Conversely, SCAD develops in only 1%–3% of patients with known FMD (unpublished results, Constantina Chrysochou and Alexandre Persu), although earlier registry data suggested rates as high as 10% [25]. Thus, while FMD is common among patients with SCAD, SCAD is relatively uncommon in patients with FMD.

Coronary imaging is invasive, SCAD is infrequent, and coronary FMD-like changes often manifest as nonspecific tortuosity without clear therapeutic implications. Importantly, even if coronary abnormalities were detected, no proven preventive strategy exists to reduce SCAD risk. Therefore, routine coronary screening in asymptomatic FMD patients is not recommended.

However, in patients with known FMD who present with Takotsubo syndrome or myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries, coronary imaging should be carefully reviewed to exclude missed SCAD, as this may influence acute management and secondary prevention strategies.

TIP 4: CONSIDER FAMILIAL SCREENING SELECTIVELY

Although familial clustering has been reported in a minority of cases (∼2.4%–7.3%) [14, 15], the overall prevalence of familial cases remains low, and the yield of screening for FMD in asymptomatic relative would probably be limited. Thus, routine familial screening is not recommended. Selective clinical screening should only be considered in first-degree relatives who present with suggestive features—such as early-onset or resistant hypertension, pulsatile tinnitus, unexplained stroke, or arterial dissection in any vascular territory. These patients should undergo careful clinical evaluation and targeted vascular imaging of the relevant arterial bed. In addition, consideration may be given to screening first-degree relatives of patients with particularly severe and disabling disease.

Genetic investigations have consistently identified the PHACTR1 locus as a common susceptibility variant for FMD, with additional associations reported at the LRP1, ATP2B1, and LIMA1 loci [26–28]. However, these associations confer only modest increases in risk and currently have limited clinical utility at the individual patient level. Rare pathogenic mutations have been reported, but they are not associated with a consistent phenotype that would allow reliable clinical identification [29, 30], with the exception of a mutation in COL5A1 (c.1540G>A, p.(Gly514Ser)), which seems more associated with a dissection phenotype [30]. Therefore, at present, systematic genetic screening of family members is not recommended [1].

Importantly, while routine genetic testing for FMD is not indicated, clinicians should remain vigilant for alternative heritable arteriopathies. A family history of arterial dissections—a very uncommon feature in FMD and highly suggestive of inherited vascular disorders—should prompt further evaluation. Likewise, if clinical features raise suspicion for conditions such as vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (e.g. easy bruising, thin translucent skin, spontaneous pneumothorax, bowel rupture), Loeys–Dietz syndrome (e.g. arachnodactyly, pectus deformity, abnormal uvula, joint laxity), or other connective tissue disorders [31, 32], appropriate genetic evaluation should be pursued.

TIP 5: OPTIMIZE MEDICAL MANAGEMENT

Prevent complications

The cornerstone of medical management in FMD is aggressive risk factor modification, particularly smoking cessation. Smoking has been consistently associated with a more severe disease phenotype, including earlier symptom onset, increased need for renal interventions, higher prevalence of aneurysms, and greater major vascular event rates [33, 34]. Although there are no prospective data demonstrating that smoking cessation improves the natural course of FMD, it remains a sensible recommendation considering the general cardiovascular benefits and the association of smoking with more aggressive disease.

Although no randomized head-to-head trials exist, low-dose aspirin is often recommended in patients with carotid or vertebral FMD, especially in those with prior transient ischemic attack or dissection [1]. It could also be considered in severe, widespread, multivessel disease. This approach is consistent with expert opinion reflected in the International Consensus statement [1]. The rationale is the potential prothrombotic milieu created by turbulent flow within areas of arterial stenosis, dilation, or tortuosity. However, given the limited evidence, decisions regarding initiation and duration of antiplatelet therapy should be individualized after careful assessment of bleeding risk.

In contrast, statins are not routinely indicated in FMD, as the disease is non-atherosclerotic and lipid lowering does not target its underlying pathophysiology. Recent observational data support current practice [35]. Nevertheless, statins should be used when there is an independent indication according to lipid profile, overall cardiovascular risk and comorbidities such as chronic kidney disease, or established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

Treat hypertension effectively

Hypertension is the most common manifestation of FMD and may be resistant to therapy. Optimal blood pressure control is essential to reduce the risk of stroke, aneurysm rupture, and other vascular complications. However, disease-specific blood pressure targets have not been defined. In practice, applying contemporary general population guidelines—such as the 2024 European Society of Hypertension recommendations—is considered appropriate [36].

No randomized trial has demonstrated superiority of a specific antihypertensive class in FMD. The primary objective is effective blood pressure control. That said, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) are often preferred in patients with renal artery involvement because they target activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone (RAA) system, which plays a central role in renovascular hypertension [37], although consistent RAA activation remains controversial in multifocal disease [38, 39]. These agents are therefore widely considered first-line therapy in renovascular hypertension. Careful monitoring of renal function and serum potassium is essential, particularly in patients with bilateral renal artery stenosis.

TIP 6: UNDERSTAND THE ROLE OF ANGIOPLASTY

Treat the right lesions

In renal FMD, not all stenoses require intervention. Most patients with FMD-related hypertension respond well to medical therapy, which remains the first line of treatment. Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) should be reserved for hemodynamically significant lesions, particularly in young patients with early-onset FMD, as well as patients with resistant or difficult-to-control hypertension. Notably, younger individuals and those with a shorter hypertension duration consistently demonstrate greater likelihood of blood pressure improvement or cure following revascularization compared with older patients or those with longstanding disease [40].

When non-invasive imaging is inconclusive, catheter-based angiography remains the gold standard for diagnosis and allows direct measurement of translesional pressure gradients. A pressure ratio (Pd/Pa) <0.90—corresponding to a gradient of ∼10% of mean aortic pressure—is generally considered hemodynamically significant in atherosclerotic disease [41] and has been pragmatically adopted in FMD pending disease-specific validation.

For symptomatic renal artery FMD, balloon angioplasty alone is preferred, as FMD lesions are non-atherosclerotic and respond well to simple dilation. Data from the ARCADIA-POL registry showed no clinical benefit of systematic stenting in FMD and highlighted a substantial rate of stent-related complications (58%), including symptomatic in-stent occlusion manifesting as recurrent hypertension or deterioration in kidney function [42]. These findings are consistent with previous case reports and expert opinion [1]. Accordingly, routine stenting is discouraged and should be reserved for procedural complications such as flow-limiting dissection, pseudoaneurysm, or arterial rupture [1].

Weigh the risks

PTA is generally safe but not without risk. Most complications are related to vascular access. Overall adverse events in FMD patients are reported in ∼12% of procedures [40]. Reported events include puncture site bleeding or infection (3%); renal complications such as renal failure, embolization or infarction, and renal artery dissection, occlusion, thrombosis, perforation, or rupture (8%); and mortality (<1%). Notably, these data are based on relatively older studies and may not entirely reflect current clinical practice. Careful patient selection and performance in experienced centers are therefore essential.

Emerging tools: improving patient selection

There is growing interest in non-invasive techniques to better assess the functional severity of renal artery stenosis and refine selection for angioplasty. One promising approach involves computational modeling of renal hemodynamics [43]. Using patient-specific CTA or MRA data, 3D reconstructions of the renal arteries are generated and analyzed with computational fluid dynamics to create a “digital twin” of the patient’s vasculature. This allows simulation of translesional pressure gradients without invasive measurement.

An alternative image-based strategy uses quantitative analysis of angiographic geometry without full computational modeling [44]. In proof-of-concept studies, software-based analysis of multifocal “string-of-beads” lesions has been used to estimate translesional pressure gradients (ΔP) using empiric hemodynamic laws.

If validated in larger studies, these technologies could help identify patients most likely to benefit from PTA while avoiding unnecessary invasive procedures.

TIP 7: ENCOURAGE PHYSICAL ACTIVITY BUT AVOID HIGH-RISK ACTIVITIES

A sedentary lifestyle contributes to weight gain, physical deconditioning, and poor blood pressure control. Therefore, patients with FMD should be encouraged to engage in regular physical activity. The World Health Organization recommends 150–300 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, along with muscle-strengthening activities on at least two days per week [45].

However, data specifically addressing the safety of exercise in FMD are limited. Because FMD is associated with predisposition to dissection, certain movements or mechanical stressors may theoretically increase vascular risk. Thus, physical activity should be encouraged but adapted to minimize mechanical vascular stress. The goal is not restriction, but thoughtful moderation—promoting cardiovascular health while reducing avoidable vascular risk.

Current guidance—largely based on expert consensus and pragmatic considerations rather than robust evidence [46]—suggests the following practical principles:

After arterial dissection: Strenuous activity should be deferred until imaging confirms healing. Resistance training is generally avoided for ∼8–12 weeks.

TIP 8: ADDRESS PREGNANCY CAREFULLY BUT REASSURINGLY

While FMD is often diagnosed years after pregnancy, early recognition in women of childbearing age is particularly valuable. When FMD is identified before pregnancy, preconception counseling should focus on optimizing blood pressure control and carefully assessing vascular lesions, including renal artery stenosis and cerebral aneurysms. Preemptive treatment of renal artery stenosis by renal angioplasty may be considered in selected cases; furthermore, all aneurysm, regardless of size, should be systematically evaluated to determine the need for prophylactic repair [47]. Counselling regarding pregnancy should also include a discussion about maternal and fetal risks. Most women can achieve successful pregnancies with appropriate multidisciplinary monitoring and management, as recommended by the International Consensus [1]. Therefore, pregnancy should not be systematically discouraged, but careful follow-up at a high-risk obstetrics clinic is indicated.

Pregnancy-related complications: understanding the risk

Older data suggested an alarmingly elevated risk of preeclampsia in women with FMD [48]. However, more recent and larger studies provide reassuring estimates. The largest dataset to date—FEIRI [10]—included 534 pregnancies in 237 women. Overall, 40% of women experienced at least one pregnancy-related complication, most commonly gestational hypertension (25%) and preterm birth (20%), rates higher than those observed in the general population [49, 50]. Importantly, the prevalence of preeclampsia was 7.5%, modestly increased compared with the general population (1.4%–4.0%) [49], but substantially lower than rates reports in women with chronic essential hypertension (22%–26%) [51, 52]. Resistant hypertension occurred in 5% of pregnancies and led to preterm delivery in more than half of those cases. Severe maternal–placental syndromes were uncommon: HELLP (Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes and Low Platelets) syndrome (1%), abruptio placentae (1%), and intrauterine fetal death (4%) were rare events [10]. A recent report from the U K [11] including 185 pregnancies in 83 women showed similar findings. Gestational hypertension occurred in 22% of pregnancies, preeclampsia in 5%, and stillbirth in 2%. Notably, the rate of preterm birth (12%) was closer to that observed in the general population.

Vascular complications: not a major hurdle

Arterial complications during pregnancy or the postpartum period appear to be rare. In FEIRI, the overall rate was <1%, with one case of carotid/vertebral artery dissection occurring 19 days postpartum and one case of subarachnoid hemorrhage due to intracranial aneurysm rupture at 24 weeks’ gestation [10]. These findings should still be interpreted with caution, as the cohort predominantly included patients with renal FMD, with relative underrepresentation of those with cerebrovascular involvement or prior arterial dissection. Overall, although pregnancy constitutes a hemodynamic and hormonal stressor and theoretical concerns exist regarding aneurysm growth or arterial dissection, contemporary registry data suggest that catastrophic vascular complications are uncommon. Nevertheless, postpartum imaging to assess for arterial progression is advisable.

TIP 9: MANAGE MIGRAINES THOUGHTFULLY

Severe chronic headache is a cardinal and often debilitating manifestation of FMD. In the US Registry, headaches were reported in 67.5% of patients, with 27.4% experiencing daily symptoms [53]. Migraine is the most common headache subtype, affecting 28%–38% of patients [14, 53]. Migraine is often the primary clinical complaint and a major determinant of quality of life. Effective management—both acute and preventive—is therefore essential. While migraine management largely follows general population guidelines, specific considerations arise in FMD (Fig. 3).

Practical answers to common patient questions.

Acute treatment: balance efficacy and vascular safety

Referral to a headache specialist is strongly advisable. Acute treatment strategies should be individualized and, when significant vascular disease is present, discussed within a multidisciplinary framework. The International Consensus acknowledges the absence of migraine-specific treatment studies in FMD and advises caution with vasoconstrictive agents such as triptans and dihydroergotamine, particularly in patients with a history of cerebral or coronary dissection, given theoretical concerns about ischemic risk [1]. Although available data are partially conflicting [54], treatment decisions should carefully balance symptom control with vascular safety.

CGRP-targeted therapies: a promising but uncertain option

While most patients can be managed with standard therapies; advanced treatments such as calcitonine gene-related peptide (CGRP)-targeted agents may be considered in a subset of patients with refractory disease. Novel prophylactic and abortive therapies like CGRP receptor antagonists (gepants) and monoclonal antibodies targeting CGRP or its receptor have emerged as preventive options for episodic and chronic migraine in the general population [55–57]. However, no dedicated safety studies have been conducted in FMD and most trials excluded patients with significant cardiovascular risk.

Concerns have been raised that blocking CGRP’s vasodilatory and potentially protective effects could exacerbate ischemic conditions such as strokes or coronary ischemia. Real world data show cardiovascular (CV) events in 3.1% of patients over ∼1.5 years, including rare serious outcome like stroke and coronary artery dissection, though causality could not be established [58]. In contrast, pooled long-term clinical trial data for erenumab showed no increased incidence of ischemic CV or cerebrovascular events across risk groups [59]. A 2024 pharmacovigilance analysis reported cardiovascular signals, including hypertension, arrhythmias, myocardial infarction, and thrombosis, varying by agent [60].

Given these uncertainties and the absence of FMD-specific data, some experts recommend avoiding CGRP-targeted therapies in patients with small-vessel disease, distal stenosis, or prior dissections [61] pending more robust long-term safety data.

Preventive strategies: consider dual-benefit therapies

Preventive options may appear limited in FMD, but certain antihypertensive medications offer dual benefits. Strong evidence supports the use of beta-blockers (propranolol, nadolol, metoprolol) for episodic migraine prevention, with propranolol also effective in chronic migraine. The ARB candesartan is also strongly recommended for episodic migraine prevention [62]. These agents may be particularly attractive in FMD patients with concomitant hypertension or SCAD, where beta-blockers are often considered.

TIP 10: GET INVOLVED IN RESEARCH AND REACH OUT TO PATIENT SUPPORT GROUPS

FMD remains an underrecognized and incompletely understood vascular disease. Important questions persist regarding its pathophysiology, genetic underpinnings, natural history, and optimal surveillance strategies. Much of what we currently know stems from collaborative registry efforts such as the North American Registry for fibromuscular dysplasia, FEIRI, and national initiatives including ARCADIA, a French initiative. These registries have substantially advanced our understanding of disease phenotype, vascular complications, and outcomes.

Continued clinician participation in such registries is essential to improve risk stratification and develop evidence-based management strategies. In rare diseases like FMD, multicenter collaboration is essential.

Patients should also be encouraged to engage with reputable patient support organizations. Connecting patients with these organizations can reduce isolation, improve disease literacy, and empower individuals to participate actively in their care. These groups provide education, peer support, advocacy, and updates on research opportunities. In Europe, patients may contact the European Fibromuscular Dysplasia Patient Association (www.fmd-eu.com). In North America, the Fibromuscular Dysplasia Society of America (www.fmdsa.org) offers educational resources, patient meetings, and research updates. Other regional organizations, such as the FMD Society of UK & Ireland (https://www.facebook.com/FibromuscularDysplasiaSocietyUKandIreland/), the Dutch FMD Groep Nederland (www.fmdgroep.nl), and the Fibromuscular Dysplasia Australasia Advocacy, may also provide local support and information (exhaustive list here: www.fmd-eu.com/helpful-links/).

CONCLUSION

FMD remains an underrecognized and heterogeneous disease, often associated with delayed diagnosis and variable management. This review highlights the importance of early clinical suspicion, appropriate use of high-quality vascular imaging—including one-time head-to-pelvis assessment,and careful recognition of atypical presentations. Management should be individualized, combining targeted revascularization for selected patients with optimized medical therapy, strict blood pressure control, and risk factor modification, particularly smoking cessation. Attention to specific clinical contexts such as pregnancy, physical activity, and migraine is essential to provide comprehensive, patient-centered care. Ultimately, continued engagement in registries, research, and collaboration will be key to addressing remaining knowledge gaps and further improving care for patients with FMD.
