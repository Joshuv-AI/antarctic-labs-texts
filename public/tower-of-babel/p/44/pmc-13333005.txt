Cilostazol for Probable Swallow Syncope Associated With Sick Sinus Syndrome in a Nonagenarian Receiving Home‐Based Care: A Case Report
Authors: Hiromitsu Yamashita, Sandeep Shakya, Masayoshi Shiota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSwallow syncope is a rare reflex syncope, typically treated with pacemaker implantation. Background

Swallow (deglutition) syncope is a rare neurally mediated reflex syncope triggered by swallowing, reported in approximately 0.3% of patients with recurrent syncope [1]. Exaggerated vagal activation during deglutition causes bradyarrhythmia and cerebral hypoperfusion. Permanent pacemaker (PPM) implantation is the standard treatment, with near‐universal symptom resolution [1].

When PPM implantation is not feasible or not concordant with goals of care, pharmacological alternatives may be considered [2]. Cilostazol, a phosphodiesterase type 3 inhibitor, has positive chronotropic effects in sick sinus syndrome (SSS) and has been used when PPM implantation is declined or contraindicated [3, 4, 5]. To our knowledge, cilostazol for probable swallow syncope in a patient with both severe aortic stenosis (AS) and SSS has not previously been reported. We describe such a case managed in a home‐based care setting.

Case Presentation

The patient was a woman in her nineties with dementia, very severe AS (peak aortic jet velocity 5.58 m/s, mean pressure gradient 88.1 mmHg), heart failure, and SSS. She was admitted for decompensated heart failure. During admission, telemetry showed transient bradycardia to 30 beats per minute, prompting 24‐h Holter electrocardiography (Figure 1), which identified eight sinus pauses exceeding 3 s (maximum 4.18 s), consistent with SSS. First‐degree atrioventricular block was also noted on review. As she remained asymptomatic during monitoring, the attending cardiologist advised that PPM implantation represented a Class IIa indication under Japanese guidelines rather than a Class I recommendation [6], and that the procedure carried elevated risk given her advanced age, frailty, very severe valvular disease, and heart failure. The family was counseled regarding the risk of sudden death, and advance care planning discussions were initiated before discharge. Home‐based medical care was then started.

A Holter electrocardiographic tracing obtained during index hospitalization, demonstrating a sinus pause of 4.18 s.

Several weeks after discharge, she developed recurrent syncopal episodes during meals, occurring several times per month. According to repeated family reports and home observations, she would suddenly lose grip on her chopsticks and become unresponsive immediately after swallowing, with spontaneous complete recovery within 1–2 min. She was at baseline between episodes, and resting heart rate at home visits was consistently 40–50 beats per minute.

Ambulatory ECG during symptoms was not obtained because the family declined rehospitalization for monitoring. Probable swallow syncope was diagnosed clinically on the basis of the reproducible temporal relationship between swallowing and loss of consciousness, previously documented SSS with sinus pauses on Holter monitoring, and the absence of other identifiable triggers. A contribution of severe AS to syncope susceptibility could not be excluded [7].

After discussion of management options, including hospital admission for PPM evaluation, the family reaffirmed their preference to avoid rehospitalization. Cilostazol was selected primarily for chronotropic support in the context of SSS; its antiplatelet profile was considered an additional practical advantage because silent cerebral infarction had previously been identified on head CT.

Cilostazol 100 mg twice daily was started. Within approximately 1 week, resting heart rate increased to 60–80 beats per minute and syncopal episodes ceased completely. At regular home visits, pulse rate, blood pressure, and symptoms were monitored, with no hypotension, oedema, or worsening heart failure observed. She has remained free of syncope for 10 months.

Discussion

Definitive diagnosis of swallow syncope usually requires documentation of bradyarrhythmia time‐locked to swallowing by telemetry or ambulatory monitoring [1]. This was not achievable in our patient because the family opposed rehospitalization. In home‐based care, however, the living context itself can support clinical reasoning: the syncope occurred consistently during meals, a pattern difficult to capture in a single outpatient encounter. Together with previously documented sinus pauses on Holter monitoring, this supported a diagnosis of probable swallow syncope, as similar pauses during swallowing could plausibly have caused cerebral hypoperfusion and loss of consciousness. Severe AS may also contribute independently to syncope risk and may have lowered the threshold in this patient [7].

Prolonged ambulatory monitoring could have provided symptom–rhythm correlation, and contemporary self‐applied patch and wearable recorders—increasingly available in Japan and addressed in a 2025 national consensus statement [8]—make this more feasible in home‐based care than before. Such monitoring was not pursued at the time, reflecting practice patterns that are still evolving. Nonetheless, baseline Holter monitoring had already documented sinus pauses up to 4.18 s, and additional symptom–rhythm correlation, while it would have strengthened diagnostic certainty, was unlikely to alter management given the family's decision to decline pacemaker implantation.

The decision not to pursue PPM implantation reflected an overall assessment of advanced age, frailty, very severe AS with heart failure, dementia, and the family's preference to avoid hospitalization. Cilostazol increases heart rate through phosphodiesterase type 3 inhibition. In a retrospective study of 192 patients with SSS, it reduced the 6‐month PPM implantation rate from 55.8% to 20.4% [3], with heart rate improvement reported within days to one week [4, 5]—consistent with the response seen here. Because cilostazol also has vasodilatory effects, caution is warranted in severe AS, where peripheral vasodilation could theoretically worsen haemodynamic instability. In our patient, however, close home monitoring over 10 months revealed no haemodynamic deterioration. Other pharmacological options were considered less suitable. Although theophylline has documented positive chronotropic effects in sinus bradycardia [9], its use is limited in older adults by a narrow therapeutic window and a risk of gastrointestinal intolerance—leading to discontinuation in some elderly patients with sick sinus syndrome [10]—as well as tremor and central nervous system stimulation. These concerns were particularly relevant in this frail patient with dementia, in whom reduced oral intake would be undesirable. Cilostazol was therefore preferred, supported additionally by a coexisting indication for antiplatelet therapy.

This case highlights the role of the home‐based care physician in shared decision‐making when standard care pathways conflict with patient and family values. The family's preference for home‐based care was revisited and confirmed over time as recurrent episodes clarified both the clinical risk and the burden of invasive care, supported by the continuity of care and trust that home visits foster [11, 12]. Beyond prescribing, management required structured home monitoring of pulse, blood pressure, and heart failure status, with the family reporting meal‐related episodes.

Conclusion

Cilostazol may be considered as a noninvasive option in carefully selected patients with probable swallow syncope complicating SSS when PPM implantation is not concordant with goals of care, even in the presence of severe AS and multiple comorbidities. Further evidence is needed, but home‐based care physicians should be aware of this potential option and of the importance of explicit shared decision‐making and close follow‐up.
