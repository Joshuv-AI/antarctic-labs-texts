Attitudes of women towards uterus transplantation in Turkey: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Elif Balkan Kuru
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUterus transplantation represents a viable option for women suffering from uterine factor infertility, offering the prospect of conception and motherhood. As techniques and procedures continue to develop, uterus transplantation is expected to become increasingly important and commonplace. The purpose of this study was to evaluate women’s attitudes towards uterus transplantation in Turkey.MethodsThis population-based survey study conducted via online questionnaires to 189 women between March and August 2023. The “Attitudes Towards Uterus Transplantation Questionnaire” was used to collect the data. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsOf the women, 54.0% had heard of uterus transplantation, but 74.1% of them did not know that it was legally performed in Turkey. The options supported by women were assisted reproductive treatments (95.8%) or adoption (86.8%), rather than uterus transplantation (60.8%). Surrogacy was the least supported method of having a child (20.1%). Around half of the women did not want their relatives to know if they were uterus recipients (51.3%) or donors (48.1%). The best donor was thought to be an anonymous donor (52%). The mean score given by women for their attitudes towards uterus transplantation was 7 out of 10, on a scale of 0 to 10.ConclusionAlthough women’s attitudes towards uterus transplantation were good, their awareness and knowledge of the procedure were quite low. This lack of awareness or negative attitudes meant that some of the women were hesitant about uterus transplantation. It is suggested that awareness be raised among women regarding uterus transplantation as a potential solution for those desirous of conception, particularly those encountering difficulties in conceiving.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-03943-5.

Background

Uterus transplantation (UTx) is a surgical procedure involving the transplantation of a healthy uterus into an organism in which the uterus is either absent or not functioning properly [1]. The latest data shows that over 140 UTx procedures have been performed worldwide, resulting in the birth of over 70 babies [2]. UTx, which has been the focus of considerable research into organ transplantation in the previous decade, is described as a treatment option that enables women with uterine factor infertility to conceive and give birth in a biological way [3, 4]. Therefore, UTx can be considered a novel organ transplant when compared to other types of organ transplants. In 2000, Fageeh et al. performed the first human-to-human UTx in Saudi Arabia, but the recipient required a hysterectomy due to acute vascular thrombosis 99 days after surgery [5]. Brännström and his team was initiated nine UTx via live donors in 2012 [2, 6]. In 2013, Erman Akar et al. performed a human-to-human UTx in Turkey [7]. The recipient became pregnant 18 months after the donation, but it resulted in miscarriage [8]. For the first time in UTx history, a healthy baby was born following an embryo transfer to one of the recipients from Brännström et al.’s study [3]. Following the first successful delivery, interest in UTx increased, leading to more clinical trials on the subject [3, 6, 9–13]. Current data shows that more than 140 UTx procedures have been performed in 19 countries, including the United States, Sweden, India, the Czech Republic, and Germany. Of these, more than 70 have resulted in successful live births [2]. In addition, The International Society of Uterus Transplantation (ISUTx) was formed in January 2016 in Gothenburg, Sweden, at a meeting attended by around 70 clinical doctors and scientists with a special interest in UTx [14].

Because the uterus is not a vital organ, it can be transplanted with organs from both living and deceased donors [15]. However, determining the donor often presents an ethical dilemma. It is anticipated that the greatest choice for a living donor will be the woman’s mother or sister, as this will increase graft compatibility [16]. Furthermore, the living donor must realise and accept that she/he will be unable to conceive a child naturally following the transplant, and that there are possible risks associated with the surgery. The anonymity of the identities of both the recipient and the donor raises a variety of ethical concerns. These include the formation of complicated relationships between donors and recipients, and feelings of shame, anxiety or guilt [17].

Various countries offer organ transplants for purchase legally. However, organ transplantation in expectancy of payment may lead to socioeconomic inequalities in society [18]. In order to use an organ from a deceased donor, either the donor must have given prior approval for organ donation, or their family members must have given permission after their death. In most countries, death must be declared unambiguously, as the donor is considered alive as long as their cardiac functions continue, even if their brain functions have ceased [19].

It is expected that UTx will become increasingly important in the coming years. It is predicted that the use of minimally invasive and robotic surgical techniques in living donor transplantation will reduce hospital stays and accelerate recovery. Reducing the risks associated with surgery could encourage more people to donate their uterus [20]. Additionally, using bioengineering and a uterine graft grown from an individual’s own cells could eliminate the need for donors and the associated problems of immunosuppression. Significant advances in organ reconstruction and tissue engineering, as demonstrated in animal studies, suggest that bioengineered UTx may be possible in the future [21–24]. These developments suggest that UTx will become more widespread around the world. For this reason, it is important to understand women’s thoughts and attitudes towards UTx, as there is limited relevant literature.

The aim of this study is to assess the attitudes of women in Turkey towards uterus transplantation.

Methods

Study aim

This study aimed to evaluate the attitudes of women in Turkey towards uterus transplantation.

Study design

This study was conducted as a cross-sectional survey study.

Study setting and sampling

This study was conducted online between March and August 2023. As the population consists of women in Turkey, the snowball sampling method was employed to achieve a larger sample size. As the study was conducted online, it was not possible to calculate the response rate. The sample consisted of 189 women who met the study’s inclusion criteria: (i) being over 18 years old, (ii) being biologically female, and (iii) being able to read and understand Turkish.

Data collection tools

Participant Information Form and “The Attitudes Towards Uterus Transplantation Questionnaire” were used for data collection. The forms were completed by participants in approximately 10 min.

Participant information form

This form, which was prepared by the researcher, contains 10 questions about the participants’ characteristics and obstetric features.

The attitudes towards uterus transplantation questionnaire

Since there are no valid scales to assess the attitudes towards UTx, researcher has searched for similar studies in the literature. Finally, with the help of two Japanese studies [25, 26] a questionnaire form was prepared by the researcher for this study. The questionnaire consists of 21 questions that evaluate women’s thoughts and attitudes towards uterus transplantation. The survey form does not include a score. (see Supplementary File).

Data collection

The data were collected via an online survey. The researcher integrated the data collection forms into Google Forms and forwarded the survey links to five female participants in her close social circle. The researcher then asked the five participants to suggest improvements and ask questions about any parts of the survey that they did not understand. In line with these participants’ responses, the form was finalised, and the survey link was sent to individuals at work, at school, in academic groups, in friend groups, on social media and on public transport. They were asked to forward the link to women around them. Additionally, a brochure inviting women to participate in the research was created with a QR code to access the link. This brochure was placed on the researcher’s office door to reach more women. Responses to the online survey were accepted between March and August 2023. A voluntary consent form was included at the beginning of the survey, and participants could not access the questions without giving their consent to participate in the study.

Data analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.0 was used for the analysis. Descriptive analyses were performed on the data and normality distributions were then determined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data comparisons were made using an independent t-test, one-way ANOVA and Pearson’s correlation tests. Additionally, multiple linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate the effect of characteristic features on attitudes towards uterus transplantation. The significance level in the statistical analysis was accepted as p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. It was approved by the Social and Human Sciences Research Ethics Committee of Istinye University (Date: 07.03.2023 Number: 23–46). Information was given and consent to participate was taken from all the participants before participating the study.

It was found that the majority of women (95.8%) support assisted reproductive techniques for couples who cannot have children (Fig. 1). Participants also believed that women who have lost their uterus due to conditions such as cancer were the most suitable candidates for UTx (91.0%) (Fig. 2). The ‘anonymous donor’ was perceived as the best donor option (52.4%) (Fig. 3). Women generally expressed negative attitudes towards UTx under headings such as the uterus being a special organ, the potential ethical problems of transplantation, impaired body integrity, fear of surgery and complications, the existence of alternatives such as adoption, and the difficulty of the process (Figs. 4 and 5).

Participants were asked to rate their general attitude towards UTx on a scale from 0 to 10. The average attitude score was found to be 7.30 ± 2.21 (minimum: 0, maximum: 10) (Table 2). Of the participants, 66.1% stated that they wanted UTx to be preferred more under suitable conditions, and 94.7% thought that society should be more informed about UTx, with greater awareness of the subject being required. Attitudes towards UTx were more positive among those who thought it was an alternative, those who would want to donate their uterus, those who would look favourably on the procedure if they could not have children, and those who wanted the procedure to become more widespread and raise awareness (Table 2).

The effect of characteristics assumed to be associated with attitudes towards UTx was determined using multiple logistic regression modelling. All characteristics were considered when defining the variables to be included in the model. Table 3 shows the results of the regression model. The independent factors predicted 4.1% of the dependent variable (R² = 0.041). However, the characteristics did not predict attitudes towards UTx statistically significantly (Table 3).

Discussion

This survey study reached 189 women from seven different geographical regions of Turkey and evaluated their attitudes towards UTx. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first survey study on the public attitudes of Turkish women on UTx. The results showed that, while women generally had positive attitudes towards UTx, their awareness and knowledge of the subject was quite low.

The findings of our study highlight the fact that, although most women are familiar with the concept of uterus transplantation, they are unaware that this procedure is legally performed in Turkey. They also did not know that the uterus is an eligible organ for donation under the Ministry of Health’s regulations. In their study in the United Kingdom, Vali et al. found that the majority of women had not heard of UTx [27]. Kisu et al. stated that UTx is not well known by Japanese women [25]. In line with the findings of our study and the literature, the fact that uterus transplantation differs from vital organ transplantation and is a relatively novel procedure can be considered an effective factor in this situation.

Although most women believe that UTx is an alternative method, our study found that the proportion of women who can donate their uterus or want to become a recipient is much lower. Nakazawa et al. found that 34.4% of participants would be considering UTx if they suffer from absolute uterine factor infertility (AUFI) [26]. Saso et al. stated that almost all women with AUFI would choose UTx over surrogacy or adoption [28]. Chmel et al. found that 62% of women with Mayer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser (MRKH) syndrome were motivated to undergo UTx [29]. Gauthier et al. reported that more than half of women with AUFI could participate in a clinical trial for UTx [30]. Jones et al. stated that transgender women thought that UTx would be beneficial due to their desire of having menstruation or gestation experience [31]. It could be assumed that the difference in the ratio of women who could be uterus recipients/donors in our study compared to other studies is because our study was conducted with women who have a uterus, whereas other studies were conducted with women who do not have a uterus, including those who are AUFI or transgender.

According to the results of our study, women had some negative attitudes towards UTx due to reasons such as the uterus being a special organ, the procedure involving ethical dilemmas, and the fact that it disrupts body integrity. According to Vali et al. approximately 30% of the women reported that religious barriers would prevent them from donating their uterus, and culturally, donation after death is unacceptable for them [27]. In the same study, 23% of the women do not intend to donate their uterus as it is not a lifesaving transplant [27]. The results of our study, which are consistent with previous research, suggest that people have been hesitant to donate their uteruses. This hesitancy may stem from negative attitudes and a lack of awareness about UTx.

In our study, it was found that women mostly support in vitro fertilisation for couples who cannot conceive naturally. Participants believed that women who have lost their uterus due to cancer are the most suitable recipients, and that the best donors are anonymous. According to Kisu et al., when it comes to the best donor, Japanese women tend to think of deceased donors, while for the best recipient, they most often consider individuals affected by uterine agenesis, like those with MRKH. Their research indicates a prevalent support for adoption as the preferred option [25]. In the study of Nakazawa et al. women indicated that the best donor was the mother and sister, the best recipient was women who have no uterus congenitally, and they reported that UTx was a more favourable option than surrogacy and adoption [26]. As with these studies, our study showed that UTx is more widely accepted than surrogacy. In this context, the prohibition of surrogacy in Turkey and Japan may be a contributing factor. However, as UTx is a legal procedure, it is important to consider women’s perceptions of UTx as part of their reproductive health options.

The study found that participants had positive overall attitudes towards UTx, and most thought that society should be more aware of it. According to Pittman et al. women in Australia have requested that UTx become a potential fertility option [32]. In the study of Vali et al. most of the women believed that transplants for improving quality of life should be made available on National Health System [27]. In Saso’s study, all women with AUFI believed that funds could be directed into UTx research [28]. In Kisu et al.'s study, the majority of participants stated that UTx should be allowed with more discussion [25]. In another study, 36.5% of the participants stated that UTx should be approved [26]. According to Riggan et al. health care providers agree that fertility treatments must be covered by insurance, but UTx should be covered by individuals since it is considered as an elective procedure, and the high cost of UTx raises ethical concerns regarding equal access to healthcare resources [33]. Järvholm et al. claimed that as the UTx procedure becomes more widely accessible, greater numbers of candidates may be expected [34]. In this regard, it can be said that UTx is socially acceptable, despite the ethical dilemmas it raises.

Study limitations

As no valid and reliable measurement tool could be found in the literature to evaluate attitudes towards uterus transplantation, the study was conducted using a questionnaire. Additionally, the low number of participants who responded to the online survey constitutes a limitation of this study.

Conclusion

This study found that women had positive overall attitudes towards UTx and that most of them thought society should be more aware of it. However, awareness and knowledge of UTx were quite low. Although most women had heard of UTx, they were unaware that it is legally performed in Turkey, which was the first countries to perform a human-to-human uterus transplant procedure. Despite considering UTx an alternative method, most women were hesitant due to a lack of awareness or negative attitudes.

In this context, it is suggested that comprehensive, large-scale studies should be conducted to examine women’s negative attitudes towards uterus transplantation in depth. Additionally, the opinions of AUFI and transgender women, as well as the general population, regarding UTx should be evaluated, as it could play an important role in their reproductive options. Possible access barriers, and health care providers’ perspectives of UTx should also be examined.
