An unusual presentation of a large lower extremity abscess caused by Aggregatibacter aphrophilus: A case report
Authors: Takashi Higuchi, Yoshihiro Araki, Mikino Saito, Atsushi Taninaka, Satoru Demura
Published: 2025

Introduction

Aggregatibacter aphrophilus (A. aphrophilus), a member of the HACEK (Haemophilus, Aggregatibacter, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, Kingella) group, is a fastidious Gram-negative coccobacillus typically colonizing the oral cavity and upper respiratory tract [1,2]. While often considered a commensal, it is a rare but recognized cause of severe systemic infections as well as periodontitis [1,2]. Due to its low pathogenicity, infection is usually associated with predisposing factors such as poor dental hygiene, recent dental procedures, or immunosuppression [3]. Despite its oral origins, A. aphrophilus can disseminate hematogenously to infect distant sites, causing conditions such as infectious endocarditis, lung abscesses, and brain abscesses [1,2]. Reports have also described osteomyelitis, including case involving the vertebral and sternoclavicular joints, and deep-seated intramuscular abscesses like psoas abscesses—all sites notably rich in blood supply and susceptible to hematogenous bacterial infections [4,5]. However, the presentation of A. aphrophilus as a large, spontaneously draining soft tissue abscess in an extremity has not been well reported.

Such atypical presentations can pose a significant diagnostic challenge. The imaging findings, which can include extensive soft tissue involvement, heterogeneous signal changes, and surrounding infiltration, often mimic those of a malignant soft tissue tumor, potentially delaying an accurate diagnosis and appropriate management [6].

This report describes an unusual case of A. aphrophilus infection in a 63-year-old male with untreated diabetes mellitus who presented with a large, spontaneously draining lower extremity abscess. The lesion initially resembled a hematoma and subsequently raised suspicion of a soft tissue tumor. Given that third-generation cephalosporins are the standard of care for HACEK organisms, this case also highlights the importance of accurate identification for guiding appropriate therapy. This case underscores the importance of considering A. aphrophilus as a rare but significant pathogen in atypical, non-traumatic soft tissue infections, and emphasizes the critical role of microbiological culture for achieving a definitive diagnosis and ensuring timely treatment. The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [7].

However, 10 weeks later, he developed a fistula with purulent drainage. A repeat MRI showed a progressive, complex mass, raising suspicion for a soft tissue tumor, which prompted referral to our hospital (Fig. 1c-f).

On admission, physical examination revealed a large, tender fluctuant popliteal mass with an open draining fistula. Laboratory tests showed a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 1.52 mg/dL, a white blood cell (WBC) count of 6400/μL with neutrophilia, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) of 7.9 %. Contrast-enhanced MRI revealed a large, peripherally enhancing extraarticular mass with extensive soft tissue inflammation. While no signal changes extended into the joint cavity, a slight increase in T2 signal intensity was noted within the bone marrow of the proximal tibia, consistent with early osteomyelitis (Fig. 2b-f). A needle biopsy of the lesion was performed due to concern for a fungating soft tissue sarcomas; both the biopsy specimen and purulent discharge from the surface were submitted for culture.

Initial Gram staining of the culture samples identified Gram-negative rods with phagocytosis, leading to a preliminary diagnosis of a soft tissue tumor complicated by bacterial infection. The patient was treated with intravenous piperacillin (PIPC), which was switched to ceftriaxone (CTRX) due to eosinophilia, and received intensive insulin therapy for his diabetes. The clinical course required aggressive wound management, including surgical debridement, the application of topical metronidazole and povidone‑iodine paste, and negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT).

Four days after admission, preliminary culture results indicated Pasteurella-like bacteria. Further analysis was therefore ordered for definitive identification, which subsequently identified A. aphrophilus. Histopathological examination of the biopsy revealed acute inflammatory cells and necrotic debris without atypical tumor cells, consistent with an abscess. No other systemic abscesses were found upon further evaluation; transthoracic echocardiography and a chest X-ray showed no evidence of endocarditis or lung abscess.

After the second week of hospitalization, blood tests showed normalization of both WBC count and CRP levels. MRI findings at this time confirmed the disappearance of the soft tissue abscess, though the increased signal intensity within the tibial bone remained. By the fourth week, the wound was almost completely closed (Fig. 3). Discussion

The present report demonstrates an extremely rare presentation of A. aphrophilus as a large lower extremity abscess, which posed a significant diagnostic challenge by initially mimicking a hematoma and subsequently a soft tissue tumor. While musculoskeletal infection by HACEK organisms has been reported, primary soft tissue abscesses of this magnitude are a clinical rarity, underscoring the importance of considering uncommon pathogens in atypical lesions, especially in immunocompromised hosts [8,9].

Chronic, indolent infections can create imaging characteristics on MRI—such as heterogeneous signal intensity, peripheral enhancement—that closely mimic those of soft tissue sarcoma [10]. This diagnostic pitfall can lead to delays in treatment or inappropriate interventions, such as extensive oncologic resection [6,10]. Therefore, infection must remain a key differential diagnosis for mass-like lesions, even with seemingly aggressive radiological features. There are currently no known pathognomic radiological or histopathological findings for soft tissue infections caused by HACEK organisms. The presentation is typically that of a non-specific abscess or inflammatory mass, making definitive diagnosis reliant solely on successful microbiological identification.

The patient's uncontrolled diabetes mellitus was a critical predisposing factor, creating an immunocompromised state that facilitated hematogenous seeding of A. aphrophilus from a presumed oral source and its subsequent proliferation in the soft tissues [9]. Definitive diagnosis required microbiological analysis, which can be challenging for fastidious HACEK organisms [11]. While traditional culture was successful in our case, modern techniques like MALDI-TOF MS and 16S rRNA gene sequencing can offer more rapid and accurate identification [11]. A limitation of this report is that the primary source of bacteremia, presumably of oral or dental origin, was not definitively identified through a formal dental examination during admission. However, the absence of trauma and the known nature of the pathogen strongly support a hematogenous route.

The HACEK organisms, including A. aphrophilus, are typically susceptible to third-generation cephalosporins (e.g., CTRX) and beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations (e.g., PIPC) [11,12]. The successful outcome of the present case was dependent on a multidisciplinary approach. Orthopaedic surgery led the surgical debridement and wound care, while internal medicine was vital for managing the patient's diabetes. Furthermore, radiology was crucial for interpreting the tumor-mimicking images, and microbiology and pathology provided the definitive diagnosis that guided targeted therapy and ruled out malignancy. This collaboration was essential for an accurate diagnosis and effective treatment.

Conclusion

This case presents a rare, tumor-mimicking extremity soft tissue abscess caused by A. aphrophilus. Atypical infectious etiologies should be included in the differential diagnosis for patients presenting with mass-like lesions, particularly in the context of immunosuppression such as poorly controlled diabetes. This case emphasizes that timely and accurate diagnosis through microbiological culture is crucial to guide appropriate antibiotic therapy, prevent unnecessary surgical procedures, and avoid delays in treatment.
