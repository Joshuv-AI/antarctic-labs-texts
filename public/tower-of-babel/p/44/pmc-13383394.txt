STAT1 and STAT3 signaling in ovarian cancer: prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets
Authors: Esther PB Rodman, Aditya Bhagwate, Hannah M Smith, Michael J Emch, Nicole A Pearson, Zhifu Sun, John R Hawse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpithelial ovarian cancer continues to be a highly fatal female reproductive malignancy despite treatment with aggressive chemotherapy, anti-angiogenic agents, PARP inhibitors, and surgical debulking strategies. JAK/STAT signaling is reported to be oncogenic in ovarian cancer and constitutively active in a proportion of chemotherapy-resistant ovarian cancer cell lines and human tumors. However, the individual contributions of specific JAK/STAT pathway components, in particular STAT1 and STAT3, in mediating oncogenic activity are not well understood.MethodsTo delineate the precise molecular functions of STAT1 and STAT3 in the two most common ovarian cancer subtypes, high-grade serous and endometrioid, OVSAHO and MDAH-2774 cell lines were treated with IFNγ or IL6, to activate canonical STAT1 and STAT3 signaling respectively, with and without STAT1 and STAT3 knockdown. RNA-sequencing and ChIP-sequencing were utilized to globally define STAT1 and STAT3 specific transcriptomes and cistromes. STAT1 and STAT3 specific gene signatures were derived and applied to publicly available datasets to evaluate their predictive/prognostic value.ResultsThrough these global and unbiased assessments, unique and overlapping STAT1 and STAT3 transcriptional profiles, and their associated pathways, were identified. From these data, STAT1 and STAT3 gene signatures were developed that are representative of transcriptionally active STAT1 and STAT3. STAT1 specific gene signatures were found to be more lowly expressed in ovarian tumors compared to normal ovarian tissue while STAT3 specific signatures were higher in malignant tissue. Elevated levels of STAT1 and STAT3 signaling were associated with non-responsiveness to initial chemotherapy treatment. Finally, STAT1 and STAT3 signatures were associated with worse progression free survival for ovarian cancer patients, while only STAT1 signatures were associated with worse overall survival. Importantly, these results were specific to the gene signatures indicative of STAT activity and differed from assessment of STAT1 and STAT3 gene expression alone.ConclusionsThese studies further inform the precise mechanisms of STAT1 and STAT3 action in ovarian cancer and have defined gene signatures that have both prognostic and predictive value. Additionally, these results can be leveraged for the identification and development of pharmacologic approaches that elicit specific effects on the JAK/STAT signaling pathway and/or selectively alter the functions of individual STAT transcription factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16226-5.Keywords: STAT1, STAT3, Ovarian cancer, JAK, Gene signature

Background

Ovarian cancer is the second most common gynecological cancer worldwide with an estimated 20,890 new diagnoses and 12,730 deaths in the United States alone in 2025 [1]. Despite radical debulking surgery and aggressive neoadjuvant and/or adjuvant chemotherapy, the median disease-free survival (DFS) rate is only 1.7 years [2], with 5- and 10-year overall survival (OS) rates of 47% and 29%, respectively [3]. These realities are partially driven by a lack of targeted therapies that can be administered in the adjuvant setting to further reduce the risk of recurrence. Problematically, recurrent disease is increasingly drug-resistant despite initial response to first-line regimens [4, 5]. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop novel treatment approaches that improve DFS and are effective against advanced forms of ovarian cancer as well as to identify biomarkers with the capability of stratifying patients to the most appropriate lines of therapy.

The JAK/STAT pathway is an important signaling cascade that mediates inflammation, cell-to-cell cross talk, and cancer progression [6, 7]. STAT1 and STAT3 are members of the signal transducers and activators of transcription gene family that confer gene expression changes in response to JAK activation [8]. Both STAT1 and STAT3 have been implicated in ovarian cancer progression, as well as platinum and PARP inhibitor (PARPi)-resistance [9–11]. STAT1 is reported to be more abundantly expressed in late/advanced stage ovarian tumors compared to normal, borderline, or benign lesions [12–15]. Additionally, STAT1 can be highly phosphorylated and activated in platinum treated ovarian cancer cells [16], and its overexpression in treatment naïve cells has been shown to confer cisplatin resistance [17]. However, the role of STAT1 in ovarian cancer initiation and progression is contested given the evidence that high tumoral STAT1 expression is associated with longer progression-free survival (PFS), improved DFS, and extended OS [14, 18, 19]. STAT3 is well accepted to be upregulated and constitutively activated in ovarian tumors compared to normal tissues [11, 15, 20–23] and its expression is elevated in response to paclitaxel [24–26], platinum [27–30], and PARPi treatment [31]. Higher STAT3 expression is also associated with decreased OS, increased risk of recurrence, and an overall poor prognosis [29].

STAT1 and STAT3 activation rely on kinase-mediated phosphorylation of specific amino acid residues which drive dimerization, nuclear translocation, and regulation of gene expression [32, 33]. According to the PhosphoSitePlus database, there are 23 and 30 residues on STAT1 and STAT3 respectively that are known to be phosphorylated [34]. The most well-characterized phosphorylation sites are Y701 and Y705 on STAT1 and STAT3 respectively, followed by S727 which is conserved in both transcription factors [34]. These post-translational modifications, among others including acetylation, methylation, sumoylation, and ubiquitination, differentially affect STAT protein activity. However, systematic interrogation of every possible post-translational modification, and their multifactorial combinations is unrealistic. As such, the ability to infer STAT1 and STAT3 activity in human tissues and existing datasets based solely upon their mRNA and/or protein expression is not possible. We therefore sought to develop gene signatures that were indicative of overall STAT1 and STAT3 activity/signaling as a surrogate for the many post-translational modifications that are known to occur on, and regulate the activity of, these two critically important transcription factors.

In previous studies [35], we identified lestaurtinib, a broad kinase inhibitor that strongly suppresses JAK/STAT signaling, as a potent therapeutic against multiple ovarian cancer cell lines and PDX (patient derived xenograft) models [35]. The anti-neoplastic properties of lestaurtinib were shown to be partially dependent on (1) the ability of the compound to inhibit two STAT1 and STAT3 phosphorylation residues (Tyr701/705 and Ser727), and (2) the basal activity of the JAK/STAT pathway in a given ovarian cancer cell line or PDX model [35]. Given these findings, and the conflicting literature reports surrounding the roles of STAT1 and STAT3 in ovarian cancer progression and response to standard-of-care therapies, we aimed to delineate the individual contributions of STAT1 and STAT3 to specific transcriptional networks to better understand the precise mechanisms by which these transcription factors function in the context of ovarian cancer. Here, we report the STAT1 and STAT3 cistromes and transcriptomes in response to cytokine-mediated pathway activation, with and without STAT1 and STAT3 knockdown, in high-grade serous (OVSAHO) and endometrioid (MDAH-2774, shortened to MDAH) ovarian cancer cell lines. We derived gene signatures from these results that are composed of genes directly regulated by a given STAT and are reflective of active STAT1 and STAT3 signaling. We applied these signatures to publicly available datasets to evaluate their associations with tumor characteristics, drug responsiveness, and long-term patient outcomes. Our findings revealed that STAT1 and STAT3 exert distinct and non-redundant transcriptional programs, and that signatures reflective of these programs offer prognostic and predictive insight into patient outcomes and drug responses that differ from that of STAT1 and STAT3 expression levels alone. These results suggest that selective STAT inhibition may provide a more favorable therapeutic index than broad JAK inhibition in specific sub-sets of patients.

Methods

Reagents

Recombinant human IFNγ and IL6 proteins, as well as galiellalactone, were purchased from R&D systems (Minneapolis, MN). Non-targeting control, STAT1, and STAT3 ON-TARGETplus siRNA SMARTpools were purchased from Dharmacon (Layfayette, CO). Lestaurtinib was purchased from Cayman Chemicals (Ann Arbor, MI). Ruxolitinib, pacritinib, and fludarabine were purchased from SellekChem (Houston, TX). JSI-124 was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). LLL12 was purchased from Abcam (Waltham, MA).

Cell lines

MDAH and OVSAHO cells were a generous gift from Dr. Gustavo Baldassarre (National Cancer Institute, Italy) and were maintained in phenol red-free RPMI medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA) containing 10% (v/v) FBS (Gemini Bioproducts, West Sacramento, CA) and 1% (v/v) antibiotic/antimycotic (Gibco). FT194 and FT282 normal fallopian tube cell lines were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA) and were maintained in phenol red-free DMEM/F12 (JangoBio, Fitchburg, WI) containing 10% (v/v) FBS (Gemini Bioproducts) and 1% (v/v) antibiotic/antimycotic (Gibco). Cells were cultured in a humidified 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. Prior to and following the experiments detailed here, cells were authenticated by IDEXX BioAnalytics (Colombia, MO). Additionally, cells were tested every 6 months to ensure that they were mycoplasma-free using the Myocplasma Detection Kit (SouthernBiotech, Birmingham, AL).

Proliferation assays

Cells were plated at a density of 1000–2000 cells per well in 96 well plates in 100 µL of medium and allowed to adhere overnight. Dose-response curves were generated for indicated drugs using 9 concentrations of 2-fold serial dilutions starting at 2.5 µM for lestaurtinib, 50 µM for ruxolitinib, 10 µM for pacritinib, 1 µM for JSI-124, 50 µM for itacitinib, 2.5 µM for fludarabine, 20 µM for galiellalactone, and 2.5 µM for LLL12. Cells were maintained under these treatment conditions in a total volume of 200 µL of medium per well until vehicle treated cells neared confluence (between 5 and 10 days). At that time, cells were fixed and stained with crystal violet, as previously described [35]. Crystal violet levels were quantified following solubilization using a plate reader set at 550 nm excitation. IC50 values were calculated using a sigmoidal dose-response curve (log(inhibitor) vs. normalized response -- Variable slope) in GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.1, Boston, MA).

ChIP sequencing

Given the scant literature for STAT1 and STAT3 ChIPseq experiments, and discrepancies in the antibodies utilized, we first sought to identify and validate ChIP compatible antibodies. In collaboration with the Mayo Clinic Epigenomics Development Laboratory, we compared the performance of multiple STAT antibodies (Supplemental Table 1) in ovarian cancer cells following standardized procedures [36]. Primers for previously reported STAT1 and STAT3 binding sites are listed in Supplemental Table 2. Real-time PCR analysis was performed using PerfeCTa™ SYBR Green Fast Mix™ (Quanta Biosciences) and a Bio-Rad CFX Real-Time PCR detection system. Enrichment of STAT1 or STAT3 chromatin association was compared to the negative control locus hC19intergenic. Based on these screening and validation results, the following antibodies were prioritized for use: STAT1 (#SC417, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX) and STAT3 (#CST9139, Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA).

For the ChIPseq experiment, MDAH and OVSAHO cells were plated at a density of 20 million cells per 150 mm dish. After 24 h, cells were treated in triplicate with vehicle (PBS [Gibco]), 10 ng/mL IFNγ, or 30 ng/mL IL6 for 30 min in a total volume of 20 mL per plate. Cells were crosslinked using 1.25 mL per plate of 16% formaldehyde (1% final) (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA) and placed on a rocker to mix for exactly 10 min at room temperature. Glycine (Fisher Scientific, Hampton, NH) was added to each plate at a final concentration of 125 mM and mixed well by slowly swirling 10 times followed by incubation at room temperature for 5 min. Fixed cells were washed twice with cold 1X TBS (Bio Rad, Hercules, CA), scraped into 15 mL collection tubes, and centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the cell pellet was washed with 3 mL of cold 1X TBS by inverting the tubes multiple times. Cells were again pelleted at 2500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and supernatant was removed. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Ribicon Genomics ThruPLEX DNA-seq kit (Takara Bio, Mountain View, CA) and sequencing was conducted by the Mayo Clinic Genome Analysis Core using an Illumina HiSeq 4000 instrument.

ChIP sequencing analysis

Raw ChIPseq datafiles for STAT1 and STAT3 were processed using the HiChIP pipeline [37]. Paired-end reads were aligned to the human genome (hg38) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA, v0.5.9) [38]. Post-alignment, custom filtering scripts retained only read pairs where one or both ends mapped uniquely to the genome. PCR duplicates were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates (v1.67, https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Peak calling was performed using MACS2 (v2.0.10) to identify STAT1 and STAT3 binding sites [39]. Statistically significant peaks were defined using a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold ≤ 1%. Peaks were annotated to their nearest genes and genomic features using the HOMER package (v4.11) [40]. Peak distribution relative to the transcriptional start site (TSS) was performed using GREAT [41]. Normalized tag density profiles were generated using Bedtools (v2.16.2) [42] and in-house Perl scripts with a 200 bp window and 20 bp step size. Peak profiles were visualized using deepTools computeMatrix and plotHeatmap with 1 kb surrounding the center of each peak [43]. Differential binding analysis between experimental conditions was performed using the DiffBind package (v2.14.0) [44], with peaks defined as being significantly different between indicated treatments using an FDR < 0.05 and an log2 fold change > 2. Overlap between peaks was performed by first converting peak files to bed format followed by using the “bedtools instersect” command to find overlap between two sets of bed files. Minimal one base overlap was first performed and then more stringent mutual 50% overlap (-r -f options) was applied. Overlap between peaks was also assessed using the associated gene nearest to each peak, and was used for comparison to RNAseq datasets. Motif enrichment analysis of differentially bound regions was conducted using the HOMER findMotifsGenome.pl tool [45], with default settings and the hg38 genome as a reference. Enriched transcription factor binding motifs were ranked by p-value to validate on-target antibody-mediated capture of STAT1 and STAT3 and to identify candidate co-factors. To elucidate biological pathways enriched among peak-associated genes, REACTOME pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the web-based REACTOME portal [46]. Genes associated with differentially bound peaks were submitted via the REACTOME gene list analysis interface, and pathways with adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

RNA sequencing

MDAH and OVSAHO cells were plated at a density of 100,000 cells per well in 6-well plates and transfected 24 h later with 5 nM non-targeting (control), STAT1, or STAT3 siRNA SMARTpools using 2 µL per well of Dharmafect Reagent 1 (Dharmacon) following the manufacture’s guidelines. Seventy-two hours post-transfection, siRNA-containing medium was removed, and cells were treated with vehicle (PBS), 10 ng/mL IFNγ, or 30 ng/mL IL6 for 2 h. Total RNA was harvested using the Qiagen miRNeasy mini kit (Hiden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Library preparation was performed using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Library Prep Kit v2 (San Diego, CA) and sequencing was conducted by the Mayo Clinic Genome Analysis Core using an Illumina HiSeq 4000 instrument. Data processing was carried out as previously described [35] using significance cut-offs of |fold change| ≥ 1.5, p-value < 0.05 and FDR < 0.1. Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) software (Qiagen) was used to identify significantly altered biological pathways and likely upstream regulators from the differentially expressed gene lists, with significance defined as p-value < 0.05 and |z-score| > 2.

Real-time qPCR

Cells were seeded at 100,000 cells per well in 6-well plates and treated as indicated, followed by total RNA isolation using TRIzol™ Reagent (Qiagen). cDNA was generated from 1 µg of total RNA via an iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio Rad) and subsequently employed for RT-qPCR using PerfeCTa™ SYBR Green Fast Mix™ (Quanta Biosciences) and a Bio-Rad CFX Real-Time PCR detection system. Primers were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies, and primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

Western blot

Cells were seeded at 100,000 cells per well in 6-well plates and treated as indicated, followed by cell lysis and protein isolation with NETN buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 20 mM pH 8.0 Tris, 0.5% NP-40) containing 1X cOmplete™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail without EDTA (PI) (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Twenty µg of total protein lysates were loaded into each well of SDS-containing Criterion XT gels (BioRad, Hercules, CA), separated by electrophoresis, and transferred to PVDF membranes followed by blocking in 5% milk in 1X TBST for one hour at room temperature. Membranes were incubated with primary antibody overnight, with rocking, at 4 °C, washed 5 times with 1X TBST, and incubated with secondary antibody for one hour, with rocking, at room temperature. Residual secondary antibody was removed with 5 washes of 1X TBST and blots were imaged via chemiluminescence on an Odyssey Fc (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE) system. Antibody information is listed in Supplemental Table 3. All sample lysates for STAT1 or STAT3 western blot analysis were run on the same SDS-page gel and transferred to the same membrane. After transfer, blots were cut at 50kD in order to blot for STAT1 or STAT3 (75-90kD) and actin (37kD) on the same lysates. The cut membranes were imaged separately and the white space in between the membranes indicates the cut that was made. Large rectangles indicate the boarders of the membrane and smaller squares indicate the bands included in Supplemental Fig. 7. Western blots were quantified using ImageJ software, and total protein levels were normalized to beta-actin as a loading control. All quantification data were derived from the images included in the figures and are representative of at least 3 biological replicates.

TNM plotter

The Tumor, Normal and Metastasis (TNM) database [47] was accessed via: tnmplot.com. Indicated genes/gene signatures (Fig. 6D) were input into the gene expression comparison analysis tool and applied to the compiled RNAseq data for ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma. The compiled RNAseq dataset was used given that multiple genes from the STAT1- and STAT3-derived signatures were missing from the gene chip dataset. For ovarian cancer, the TNM dataset only contains normal and tumor tissue samples. The gene signature analysis feature was used to calculate the mean expression of each input gene across each specimen, which was subsequently summarized in a box plot. Mann Whitney p-values, fold changes (tumor compared to normal), and the number of input genes are reported.

ROC plotter

The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) database (rocplot.com) [48] was used to evaluate the overall prognostic performance of indicated genes/gene signatures. Specifically, an individual gene or gene list (Fig. 6D) was input into the “validate predictive biomarkers in ovarian cancer” page. A few genes in each list were not found in the ROC database and therefore excluded from the analysis: For the STAT1 signature, ACER2, ANXA2R, and LIN01132 were excluded, while NTMT1 was excluded from the STAT3 signature. Outputs were filtered by response, relapse-free survival at 6 months (n = 1347 patients, with 130 non-responders and 1217 responders), any chemotherapy, and any histology/grade/stage/debulking status. The JetSet only setting was used to identify the best available probe for any given gene with the “no outliers”, and “multi-gene analysis” features selected, where appropriate. The output included the mean, median, error, minimum, and maximum expression of the indicated gene or gene signature input for responders and non-responders, as well as the ROC p-value of the difference between non-responders and responders.

KM plotter

The Kaplan-Meier plotter (kmplot.com/analysis) [49] was employed to evaluate the association of STAT1 and STAT3, as well as their gene signatures, with PFS and OS. Indicted genes or gene lists were input into the “multiple gene feature” where appropriate and “mean expression of selected genes” was employed. Genes that were found to be downregulated by cytokine treatment in the RNAseq datasets were inverted. Patients were stratified into “high” vs. “low” expressing groups via the “auto select best cutoff” feature. The JetSet best probes for each gene were used and all histologies, stages, grades, mutation statuses, debulking statuses, and treatment types were included. The probability of PFS and OS for indicated genes or gene signatures was determined and hazard ratios (HR), log rank p-values, median PFS, median OS, and the number of patients at risk in each group over time were reported.

Statistics

All experiments were conducted with at least 3 biological replicates, including 3–8 technical replicates per assay. Representative data sets are shown. Graphical representations and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism or with publicly available programs as indicated.

Results

Anti-neoplastic activity of JAK/STAT pathway inhibitors

In previous studies, we demonstrated that inhibition of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway suppressed the growth and viability of ovarian cancer cell lines and ex vivo PDO [35]. To expand upon these observations, we analyzed the effect of a panel of small molecules that inhibit JAK activation or directly target STAT1 and STAT3 activity in two normal fallopian tube (FT) epithelial cell lines (FT194 and FT282) and two ovarian cancer cell lines (MDAH and OVSAHO). MDAH and OVSAHO cancer cells were chosen since they are highly representative of the two most common subtypes of ovarian cancer; endometrioid and high grade serous [50], which account for approximately 15 and 80% of all ovarian cancer diagnoses respectively. A range of clinically relevant concentrations were studied for all drugs that have been evaluated in humans. When known, the Cmax achieved in patient plasma following standard drug administration is indicated in the figure with the referenced trial included in the text. JAK kinase inhibitors were found to have variable impacts on ovarian cancer and normal FT cell line proliferation/viability. Lestaurtinib (Cmax 7µM [51, 52]) (Fig. 1A) and pacritinib (Cmax 10µM [53, 54]) (Fig. 1B) inhibited MDAH and OVSAHO cells at concentrations below clinically achievable concentrations. FT194 was similarly sensitive, however FT282 was less sensitive to both lestaurtinib and pacritinib compared to the cancer cell lines. Itacitinib (Cmax 1µM [55, 56]) (Fig. 1C) and ruxolitinib (Cmax 1µM [57, 58]) (Fig. 1D) did not inhibit ovarian cancer cell lines at clinically achievable concentrations and exhibited overall lesser effects on FT cell lines. JSI-124 (Fig. 1E), a potent JAK inhibitor, blocked proliferation of ovarian cancer and FT cell lines at very low concentrations, with greater inhibitory effects on malignant cells compared to normal cell lines. However, JSI-124 has not yet been studied in humans and thus clinically achievable levels are unknown. The STAT1 selective inhibitor, fludarabine (Fig. 1F), potently inhibited cancer cells with no effect on FT cells. Fludarabine has been tested in clinical trials where a Cmax of 4.2µM was identified [59], which is 100-fold greater than the IC50s identified here (Fig. 1F). Two STAT3 selective inhibitors, galiellalactone (Fig. 1G) and LLL12 (Fig. 1H), also significantly inhibited ovarian cancer cells with lesser effects on the FT282 model. These data validate our previous findings [35], and reinforce the notion that inhibition of JAK/STAT pathway constituents can elicit anti-neoplastic effects in ovarian cancer, many of which show selective inhibition of cancer cells over normal fallopian tube epithelial cell line models. However, we appreciate that not all ovarian tumors will respond to JAK/STAT inhibitor therapy and that patient benefit will likely be dependent upon specific JAK/STAT pathway activity. However, it is currently impossible to infer pathway activity, or STAT-specific activity, without systematically assessing STAT protein levels and their many post-translational modifications. We therefore sought to develop gene expression signatures indicative of STAT1 and STAT3 transcriptional activity that could be derived from tumoral transcriptomic profiling which is becoming routine practice in the clinical management of cancer patients.

Elucidation of STAT1 and STAT3 cistromes

Following identification and validation of ChIP-compatible STAT1 and STAT3 antibodies (Supplemental Fig. 1), we performed ChIPseq for these two transcription factors in MDAH and OVSAHO cell lines. STAT1 and STAT3 are canonically activated by IFNγ and IL6 respectively [6, 7, 60], thus cells were treated with either vehicle (PBS), 10 ng/ml IFNγ, or 30 ng/ml IL6 for 30 min to induce STAT1 and STAT3 nuclear translocation and DNA binding. Very few STAT1 binding sites were identified in MDAH or OVSAHO cell lines treated with PBS vehicle (101 peaks in MDAH, 12 peaks in OVSAHO) or in response to IL6 treatment (243 peaks in MDAH, 1 peak in OVSAHO) (Fig. 2A-B). However, robust STAT1 chromatin association was observed following IFNγ treatment in both cell lines (5143 peaks in MDAH, 1195 peaks in OVSAHO) (Fig. 2A-B). Similarly, STAT3 chromatin association was minimal following vehicle (101 peaks in MDAH, 369 peaks in OVSAHO) and IFNγ treatment (371 peaks in MDAH, 414 peaks in OVSAHO) (Fig. 2A-B). Interestingly, IL6 treatment had little effect on STAT3 chromatin binding in MDAH cells (211 peaks) but induced robust STAT3 chromatin association as expected in OVSAHO cells (3529 peaks) (Fig. 2A-B). For each treatment and cell line, STAT chromatin binding sites were bioinformatically assigned to be associated with the nearest transcriptional start site (TSS)/gene, and their associated genomic annotation, transcription factor binding motifs, and associated pathways were determined (Supplemental Figs. 2–5).

To identify chromatin regions with differential STAT1 and STAT3 occupancy in response to cytokine treatment, differential binding analyses were conducted on IFNγ and IL6 treated samples relative to vehicle treated controls. Following IFNγ treatment, 4516 and 1195 chromatin sites were found to be differentially occupied in MDAH and OVSAHO cells respectively (Fig. 2C), all of which were enriched in response to treatment. No differentially bound sites were found for STAT1 in either cell line following IL6 treatment (Fig. 2C). Regarding STAT3, there were few differentially bound sites identified in response to IFNγ treatment (18 peaks in MDAH cells, 3 in OVSAHO cells) (Fig. 2C). All 18 sites in MDAH cells were found to be increased in STAT3 occupancy in response to IFNγ while all 3 sites in OVSAHO cells were depleted. A single site was found to be differentially occupied by STAT3 in IL6 treated MDAH cells, and this site was actually depleted in response to treatment (Fig. 2C). In OVSAHO cells, 331 STAT3 binding sites were significantly altered, all of which were enriched in response to IL6 compared to vehicle control (Fig. 2C). We next compared the differentially bound sites between treatments to identify unique STAT1 and STAT3 target sites by employing three methods of overlap. First, we compared the list of genes associated with each binding site, excluding any duplicate genes (Supplemental Fig. 6A). Of note, this comparison results in an apparent “loss” of peaks since it is based on gene name whereby each gene is only counted once even if there are multiple individual peaks linked to that gene. Second, we identified common peaks based on genomic sequences that overlapped by at least 1 bp (Supplemental Fig. 6B). Third, we employed a more stringent approach that required at least 50% of the peak-associated genomic sequence to overlap in order to be counted as a common peak (Fig. 2D). In the setting of IFNγ treatment, the large majority of STAT3 bound sites were also associated with STAT1 binding sites in both cell lines (9 out of 18 STAT3 sites in MDAH cells and 2 out of 3 STAT3 sites in OVSAHO cells) (Fig. 2D). Of note, the two overlapping sites in OVSAHO cells exhibited increased STAT1 occupancy, but decreased STAT3 occupancy, in response to IFNγ. There were no sites found to be differentially bound by STAT1 in either cell line following IL6 treatment, thus there was no overlap with IL6-mediated STAT3 binding sites (Fig. 2D). To determine if STAT1 and STAT3 target genes were conserved between cell line models, we compared the sites for IFNγ-induced STAT1 sites identified in MDAH and OVSAHO cells. This analysis revealed 685 STAT1 bound sites that were common in both cell lines, with another 3831 and 510 unique sites in MDAH and OVSAHO cells, respectively (Fig. 2D), all of which exhibited increased STAT1 occupancy. Only 1 STAT3 binding site was identified to be differentially occupied in MDAH cells, and this site was actually depleted in response to IL6. The gene associated with this site was not identified to have differential STAT3 occupancy under vehicle vs. IL6 stimulated conditions in the OVSAHO cell line (Fig. 2D). Representative ChIPseq tracks at loci identified to be preferentially bound by either STAT1 (Fig. 2E, Supplemental Fig. 6E) or STAT3 (Fig. 2F, Supplemental Fig. 6F) in both cell lines are shown.

In both MDAH and OVSAHO cells, the majority of IFNγ-regulated STAT1 binding sites were found to be associated with binding sites for 2 potential target genes and were most frequently located between 50 and 500 kb from the nearest TSS of associated genes (Supplemental Fig. 7A-B, 8 A-B). Peak annotation revealed that over 80% of all IFNγ-regulated STAT1 sites were located in either intergenic or intronic regions consistent with enhancer elements (Supplemental Fig. 7C, 8 C). The remaining sites were primarily located within promoter regions, and a small number of sites were immediately adjacent to a TSS (Supplemental Fig. 7C, 8 C). Motif analysis identified STAT1 consensus sequences as the most abundant in both cell lines treated with IFNγ, with STAT3, STAT4, and STAT5 consensus sequences included in the top 5 identified motifs (Supplemental Fig. 7D, 8D). REACTOME analysis using the list of genes associated with all IFNγ-regulated STAT1 binding sites revealed interferon signaling, cytokine signaling, and growth hormone signaling as the most abundant pathways likely regulated by IFNγ, through STAT1-mediated transcriptional control (Fig. 2F-G).

Regarding STAT3, there were very few chromatin locations identified to be differentially bound in response to either IFNγ or IL6 treatment in MDAH cells. The genomic distribution of these sites was largely distal to a TSS with the majority being located within introns and intergenic regions, as was the case for STAT1 (Supplemental Fig. 7E-G, 7I-K). Motif analysis of the single IL6-induced STAT3 binding site in MDAH cells showed it was most similar to ZNF669, RFX, and GRE(NR)/IR3 consensus sequences (Supplemental Fig. 7H), while the 18 differentially bound sites identified for STAT3 in MDAH cells following IFNγ treatment again identified STAT consensus sequences (Supplemental Fig. 7L). The genes associated with these STAT3 sites are primarily involved with inflammatory signaling and STAT related pathways (Supplemental Fig. 7M). In OVSAHO cells, many of the STAT3 binding sites enriched in response to IL6 treatment were associated with 2 genes and were largely dispersed between 5 and 500 kb from the nearest TSS (Supplemental Fig. 8E-F). Compared to STAT1, a larger fraction of STAT3 binding sites were located at gene promoters (22%), with the remainder of sites found to be within intergenic and intronic regions (Supplemental Fig. 8G). As expected in the OVSAHO cells, motif analysis identified STAT3 as the most common consensus sequence followed by STAT4, STAT1, and STAT5 (Supplemental Fig. 8H). REACTOME analysis using the list of genes nearest to the OVSAHO STAT3 IL6-induced binding sites identified pathways involved with development, RUNX family of transcription factors, and cytokine/inflammatory signaling (Fig. 2H). For the 3 differentially bound sites identified for STAT3 in OVSAHO cells following IFNγ treatment, motif analysis identified IRF and STAT consensus sequences (Supplemental Fig. 8L) and the genes associated with these sites were linked with interferon signaling (Supplemental Fig. 8M).

To provide a comprehensive overview of the ChIPseq data for all treatment conditions and cell lines studied, we merged all STAT1 and STAT3 peaks identified in a given cell line, agnostic of treatment, and generated heatmaps for STAT1 and STAT3 binding sites. As shown in Supplemental Fig. 9A, this analysis clearly highlights the observation that far more STAT1 binding sites are detected in MDAH cells while STAT3 binding is significantly more abundant in OVSAHO cells. Additionally, we generated two more sets of heatmaps, one for STAT1 and a second for STAT3. For these, all STAT1 or STAT3 peaks, agnostic of cell line and treatment, were merged and heatmaps were generated to show the abundance of each of these sites under vehicle, IL6 and IFNγ treatment for both cell lines (Supplemental Fig. 9B-C).

Elucidation of STAT1 and STAT3 transcriptomes

To further delineate specific STAT1 and STAT3 target genes, we conducted RNAseq on MDAH and OVSAHO cells following 72 h of transfection with either a control, STAT1 or STAT3 specific pool of siRNAs, and with or without IFNγ or IL6 treatment. All counts and RPKM for genes meeting > 1 RPKM cut off are listed for all samples in Supplemental Table 4. The knockdown of STAT1 and STAT3 was confirmed via RT-PCR and western blotting prior to RNAseq (Supplemental Fig. 10–11). Knockdown of STAT1 in the setting of vehicle treatment resulted in repression of 326 genes and induction of 94 genes, while depletion of STAT3 resulted in downregulation of 116 genes and upregulation of 43 genes in MDAH cells (Fig. 3A-B, Supplemental Tables 5–6). Surprisingly, in the OVSAHO cell line, only STAT1 and VAMP7 were downregulated after STAT1 knockdown and only STAT3, RPA2, and SELENOF were downregulated after STAT3 knockdown, with no genes found to be significantly induced (Fig. 3C-D, Supplemental Tables 7–8). The knockdowns were specific to STAT1 and STAT3 as none of the other STATs were significantly altered following knockdown in either cell line (Supplemental Table 9). When comparing differentially expressed genes following STAT1 and STAT3 knockdown, 139 were altered by both transcription factors in MDAH cells, while 281 and 20 genes were found to be uniquely regulated in response to STAT1 and STAT3 knockdown, respectively (Fig. 3E). Of the few genes altered in OVSAHO cells, none were shared in response to STAT1 and STAT3 knockdown, however both downregulated genes following STAT1 knockdown were also downregulated in MDAH cells (Fig. 3E). Two of the 3 genes downregulated following STAT3 knockdown in OVSAHO cells were also downregulated in MDAH cells (Fig. 3E). Interestingly, of the 139 genes significantly regulated in response to both STAT1 and STAT3 knockdown in MDAH cells, their direction of regulation was 100% concordant (Fig. 3F).

Ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) was used to identify altered pathways in MDAH cells following STAT1 and STAT3 knockdown using the full list of differentially expressed genes identified for each siRNA (Fig. 3H-I). The top 10 pathways identified following STAT1 knockdown were all suppressed and included extracellular matrix remodeling (degradation of the extracellular matrix, extracellular matrix organization), cancer signaling (molecular mechanisms of cancer, signaling by EGFR), immune response (caveolar-mediated endocytosis, neutrophil degranulation), multiple actin and cytoskeletal processes (SRP-dependent protein targeting to membrane, regulation of actin-based motility by Rho, actin nucleation by ARP-WASP complex), and cellular stress (unfolded protein response) (Fig. 3H). Following STAT3 knockdown, immune response (regulation of granulopoiesis, caveolar-mediated endocytosis), cancer signaling (molecular mechanisms of cancer, signaling by MET, FAK signaling), cell migration and extracellular matrix remodeling (transcriptional regulation by RUNX2, extracellular matrix organization, integrin cell surface interactions), and cardiac hypertrophy signaling pathways were all repressed (Fig. 3I). The kinesins pathway was the only upregulated pathway among the top 10 following knockdown of STAT3 in MDAH cells (Fig. 3I). Upstream regulator analysis was used to identify factors predicted to drive the observed gene expression changes that occurred following STAT1 and STAT3 depletion. Results of this analysis were largely unique for a given knockdown, however, CG complex and TGFB1 signaling activity was predicted to be impaired while ELDR (a lncRNA) activity was predicted to be enhanced in response to downregulation of both STAT1 and STAT3 (Fig. 3J-K).

As an additional layer of assessing transcriptional events driven by STAT1 and STAT3, we determined the effects of IFNγ and IL6 treatment on MDAH and OVSAHO cells. Cells were transfected with a pool of control siRNAs for three days, followed by treatment with each cytokine for two hours to identify immediate gene responses. In MDAH cells, treatment with IFNγ altered the expression of 133 genes, of which 125 were upregulated and 8 were downregulated (Fig. 4A, Supplemental Table 10). Surprisingly, no significant gene expression changes were identified following IL6 treatment (Fig. 4B). This is consistent with the ChIPseq data where no differential STAT3 binding was observed in response to IL6. In OVSAHO cells, both IFNγ and IL6 induced substantial gene expression changes of which the majority were upregulated (Fig. 4C-D, Supplemental Tables 11–12). Given that IL6 had no effect on gene expression in MDAH cells, no genes were commonly regulated by both cytokines in this cell line (Fig. 4E). In OVSAHO cells, 11 genes were commonly regulated by both IL6 and IFNγ (Fig. 4E), of which the directionality of regulation was the same in response to both treatments (Fig. 4F). In comparing MDAH and OVSAHO cells, no genes were commonly regulated by IL6, however, the expression of 54 genes was regulated by IFNγ in both cell lines (Fig. 4G), all of which were induced (Fig. 4H).

Development of STAT1 and STAT3 gene signatures

Using the identified cistromes and transcriptomes for STAT1 and STAT3, we sought to develop gene signatures representative of active STAT1 and STAT3 signaling and to determine if these signatures are associated with patient outcomes and/or drug responses. There were no genes determined to be regulated by IL6 treatment in MDAH cells, and to be consistent with how the STAT1 and STAT3 signatures were derived, we chose to only define the signatures using data generated from the OVSAHO model. Further, OVSAHO cells are a high-grade serous model, and are thus representative of the majority of ovarian tumors. Towards this goal, we followed an iterative process (Fig. 6A) in which the first step involved identification of genes regulated by either IFNγ or IL6 via RNAseq analysis in OVSAHO cells and which were found to have an associated STAT1 or STAT3 binding site in the ChIPseq datasets following respective treatments. There were 50 genes that met these criteria for STAT1 (Fig. 6B), and 12 genes for STAT3 (Fig. 6C). We next narrowed these lists further by requiring that regulation of a gene by a given cytokine be lost when its associated transcription factor (STAT1 or STAT3) was knocked down. This trimmed the STAT1 gene list to 21 (Fig. 6B) but did not affect the STAT3 gene list given that all 12 genes met these criteria (Fig. 6C). To be as comprehensive as possible, we also included genes whose expression was still regulated by IFNγ or IL6 even when STAT1 or STAT3 were knocked down, as long as the magnitude of regulation was more than halved in the setting of STAT1/3 knockdown (Fig. 6A). Of the 29 genes whose expression remained regulated by IFNγ in OVSAHO cells following STAT1 knockdown, 9 met this criterion (Fig. 6B). Thus, there were a total of 30 and 12 genes that were included in the STAT1 and STAT3 signatures, respectively, and labeled as List 1 (Fig. 6A& Fig. 6D). We also developed a second signature for each STAT by comparing the genes included in List 1 with two publicly available STAT1 ChIPseq datasets (GSE165947, GSE110067) and three publicly available STAT3 ChIPseq datasets (GSE106844, GSE123398, GSE50723). Of the STAT target genes included in List 1, 16 of the 30 STAT1 genes (STAT1-List2) and 9 of the 12 STAT3 genes (STAT3-List2) were also found to be associated with the respective STAT chromatin binding site in these other datasets. These 16 and 9 genes were defined as List 2 (Fig. 6A & Fig. 6D).

Prognostic/predictive implications of the gene signatures

These gene signatures were applied to publicly available databases to determine their potential value as prognostic and predictive biomarkers in ovarian cancer. Using the TNM RNAseq database [47], both STAT1 gene signatures were found to be downregulated in ovarian tumors compared to normal ovarian tissue (Fig. 7A). Interestingly, expression of STAT1 itself was found to be higher in ovarian tumors compared to normal ovarian tissue (Supplemental Fig. 13). In contrast, the STAT3 gene signatures were upregulated in ovarian tumors, with STAT3-List1 having a larger magnitude (Fig. 7A), while STAT3 itself was actually lower in ovarian tumors compared to normal ovarian tissue (Supplemental Fig. 13). To determine if these signatures were predictive of therapeutic response, we used the ROC plotter [48] to compare gene signature expression levels in known chemotherapy responders and non-responders. A significantly higher abundance of STAT1 List 1, but not List 2, was found in tumors that failed to respond to chemotherapy vs. tumors that did respond to treatment (Fig. 7B). No such association was found for expression of STAT1 alone (Supplemental Fig. 13). For STAT3, both gene signatures were more lowly expressed in tumors that responded to chemotherapy compared to those that did not (Fig. 7B). Similarly, STAT3 expression alone was also higher in non-responsive tumors (Supplemental Fig. 13). Using the KMplotter [49], elevated expression of both STAT1 signatures was significantly associated with worse PFS (hazard ratio (HR): 1.57 and 1.54 for List 1 and 2 respectively) (Fig. 7C). High expression of STAT1-List1 showed a trend towards an association with worse OS but was not significant (HR: 1.19, P = 0.15) (Fig. 7D). However, high expression of STAT1-List2 was significantly associated with worse OS (HR: 1.29) (Fig. 7D). In contrast, high STAT1 gene expression alone was associated with worse PFS (HR: 1.19) but better OS (HR: 0.84) (Supplemental Fig. 13). For STAT3 gene signatures, high expression of both lists was significantly associated with worse PFS (HR: 1.23 and 1.42 for List 1 and 2 respectively), but neither signature was associated with OS (Fig. 7C-D). STAT3 gene expression alone was significantly associated with worse PFS (HR: 1.26) but not OS (HR: 0.91, P = 0.37) (Supplemental Fig. 13).

Discussion

Previous work by our group [35] and others has shown that JAK/STAT signaling is classically elevated in ovarian tumors compared to normal tissue [11, 13, 19, 20, 22, 23, 61] and that some JAK/STAT inhibitors potently suppress ovarian cancer cell proliferation and tumor progression in vitro and in vivo [11, 25, 26, 28, 62, 63]. For these reasons, we assessed the efficacy of inhibitors that target upstream components of STAT1 and STAT3 signaling, as well as molecules reported to directly target the STAT proteins. Further, we sought to identify distinct and overlapping roles of STAT1 and STAT3 in ovarian cancer, to define the STAT1 and STAT3 cistromes and transcriptomes, and to develop gene expression signatures as surrogates for STAT1 vs. STAT3 activity.

We found that both JAK kinase inhibitors and STAT1 or STAT3 selective inhibitors significantly suppressed ovarian cancer cell viability/proliferation. However, only lestaurtinib, pacritinib, and fludarabine were shown to elicit these inhibitory effects at clinically achievable concentrations. For most drugs, lesser to no inhibitory effects were observed in normal fallopian tube epithelial cell lines indicating that these compounds preferentially acted upon malignant models. Interestingly, the FT194 cell line was universally more sensitive to JAK/STAT inhibitors than FT282 cells, results which may be due to differential immortalization methods or cross-talk with other signaling pathways that vary between these models. Alternatively, we have also observed that FT194 cells grow much more rapidly than FT282 cells, which may make them more susceptible to JAK/STAT inhibition. Nevertheless, a number of these drugs have been studied in humans with tolerable side effect profiles.

We comprehensively defined the STAT1 and STAT3 cistromes and transcriptomes in ovarian cancer cells and have discriminated between direct vs. indirect target genes that they regulate. We utilized IFNγ and IL6 to induce STAT1 and STAT3 activity respectively as these cytokines are well known to drive the function of these two transcription factors [64–67]. With regard to STAT1, we found very little chromatin association under basal growth conditions. However, in response to IFNγ treatment, STAT1 was driven to numerous sites across the genome where it bound to consensus STAT1 elements as expected. Many of these sites were found to be common between the MDAH and OVSAHO cell lines. Consistent with these observations, IFNγ treatment induced the expression of approximately 100 genes within two hours of treatment in both cell lines. STAT1 knockdown differentially regulated the expression of approximately 400 genes in MDAH cells, of which 25% had associated STAT1 binding sites following IFNγ treatment. IFNγ treatment could not rescue the expression these genes in the setting of STAT1 knockdown indicating the direct relationship between IFNγ signaling and STAT1 transcriptional activity in MDAH cells. In OVSAHO cells, STAT1 knockdown alone only affected the expression of itself, while the combination of STAT1 knockdown and IFNγ treatment impaired the ability of IFNγ to regulate 87% of the identified IFNγ regulated genes. We found that about half of these genes were linked to an associated STAT1 binding site, indicating that they are likely direct targets of STAT1 in OVSAHO cells. It is possible that a larger proportion of IFNγ regulated genes in both cell lines are direct targets of STAT1, but our ability to conclusively identify all of these is limited by the use of siRNA instead of complete STAT1 knockout, and by the use of standard ChIPseq technologies which do not reflect the 3D architecture of chromatin as could be appreciated with the use of Hi-C or 3 C modalities.

STAT3 chromatin association under basal growth conditions was overall low in both cell models. Interestingly, MDAH cells were completely non-responsive to IL6 treatment as there was only a single STAT3 genomic binding site identified to be differentially occupied following IL6 exposure. In agreement with these results, no genes were found to be differentially expressed in response to IL6 treatment of MDAH cells. Conversely, OVSAHO cells were highly responsive to IL6 as reflected by an abundance of enriched STAT3 binding sites and over 800 differentially expressed genes. While OVSAHO exhibited high responsiveness to IL6, there was a relatively low number of IL6 regulated genes that had a corresponding IL6 induced STAT3 binding sites. The basis for lack of IL6 responsiveness in MDAH cells is unclear but could be related to a number of factors including a potential inhibitory mechanism or feedback loop that is not present in OVSAHO cells. It is also possible that STAT3 signaling is higher in MDAH cells than OVSAHO cells under basal growth conditions which limits their ability to respond further to exogenous IL6 treatment. This possibility is supported by the observed elevated expression of STAT3, IL6, IL6ST, and IL6R in MDAH cells relative to OVSAHO cells as reflected in the RNAseq data (Supplemental Table 17). It is also important to note the treatment times employed here. All ChIPseq studies were conducted following 30 min of cytokine treatment while RNAseq was performed following 2 h of treatment. These timepoints were intentionally chosen as our goal was to identify immediate responses and direct target genes. However, this could be a potential basis for the lack over overlap between IL6 regulated genes and IL6 induced STAT3 binding sites in OVSAHO cells and the assessment of different time points could reveal significantly different results. Further study is required to address all of these possibilities.

The results presented here are the first to define the STAT1 and STAT3 transcriptomes and cistromes in ovarian cancer to our knowledge. There is one previous study that employed CRISPR KO of STAT3 and identified alterations in EMT, cell cycle, E2F, and stemness signaling pathways in ovarian cancer cells [68]. Those findings are similar to the alterations found here as they pertain to cancer signaling, cell migration, and extracellular matrix remodeling pathways. However, the present report discriminates between STAT1 and STAT3 using unbiased and genome-wide RNAseq and ChIPseq technologies and various cytokine treatments, in combination with STAT1 or STAT3 specific knockdown.

Given the increased routine practice of tumoral transcriptomic profiling in the clinical management of cancer patients, we derived functional gene signatures from these analyses reflective of non-overlapping molecular activities of STAT1 and STAT3 and subsequently applied these signatures to large publicly available patient datasets. Both STAT1 signatures were found to be more lowly expressed in ovarian tumors compared to normal ovarian tissue. Elevated levels of these STAT1 gene signatures were found to correlate with a decreased likelihood of chemotherapy response and worse PFS and OS. Conversely, previous studies have reported that high STAT1 protein expression actually correlates with improved PFS and OS [18, 19], as well as improved response to chemotherapy [19, 61]. In the patient datasets examined here, STAT1 mRNA expression was elevated in tumor tissue, had no association with chemotherapy response, and was associated with worse PFS but better OS. These discrepancies are likely due to the fact that assessment of total STAT1 protein expression in tumors via IHC may not correlate with STAT1 mRNA levels. Additionally, the abundance of STAT1 mRNA or protein may have no association with STAT1 transcriptional activity. To our knowledge, no studies have specifically examined how phosphorylated forms of STAT1 in ovarian tumors impact therapy responses or correlate with long-term patient outcomes. However, high levels of IFNγ in the tumor microenvironment is predictive of poor prognosis in ovarian cancer patients [60]. Further, molecular mechanisms that suppress STAT1 phosphorylation have also been shown to exert tumor suppressive activities in ovarian cancer models [62, 63]. Finally, multiple studies have demonstrated that STAT1 transcriptional activity is significantly elevated in therapy resistant tumors [16, 17, 69, 70] which are in agreement with the results presented here. These findings highlight the importance of assessing STAT1 activity, and not simply total STAT1 mRNA expression or protein abundance, for prognostic and predictive purposes.

Unlike STAT1, the roles of STAT3 in ovarian cancer are much less controversial and many reports have defined it as an oncogene and demonstrated its correlation with poor prognosis [11, 15, 20–23, 29]. Using the STAT3 gene signatures developed here, we confirmed elevated STAT3 transcriptional activity in ovarian tumor tissue compared to normal tissue. Additionally, elevated expression of STAT3 signatures were predictive of chemotherapy resistance and were associated with worse PFS. This is consistent with other reports demonstrating that elevated levels of phospho-STAT3 are associated with worse outcomes in ovarian cancer patients [71–73]. Interestingly, our STAT3 activation signatures were not associated with differences in OS despite a number of previous reports demonstrating that increased STAT3 phosphorylation is associated with worse OS. An important distinction between these previous results and those presented here is that all of the IHC based studies for phosphorylated STAT3 have focused only on Y705, with no consideration for S727 or other residues that could additionally regulate its transcriptional effects. Thus, our STAT3 signatures are likely indicative of differential STAT3 signaling that is not recapitulated by phosphorylation of Y705, a possibility that requires further study. Overall, we discovered that STAT1- and STAT3-specific signatures have divergent associations with malignant vs. normal tissue expression, chemotherapy response, PFS and OS. Interestingly, the associations of these signatures were largely discordant with the associations found for STAT1 or STAT3 expression alone. These findings highlight the non-redundant functions of STAT1 and STAT3 in ovarian cancer biology and provide evidence that drawing conclusions about STAT activity based on their mRNA or protein expression profiles alone should be performed with caution.

From a therapeutic perspective, alterations in JAK/STAT signaling pathways have been linked to therapy resistance. In studies combining a PARPi (olaparib) with immune checkpoint blockade, increased tumor infiltration of CD8 + T cells and NK cells, along with upregulation of STING and type I IFN signaling, were observed [70]. In the MEDIOLA clinical trial [69], which combined olaparib with durvalumab in metastatic ovarian cancer patients, elevated STING, type I IFN response, and JAK/STAT signaling were observed following treatment [69, 70]. In a 2022 study of a PARPi-resistant, Brca1 deficient mouse model, PARPi treatment was shown to increase Stat3 signaling in the tumor and promoted pro-tumor polarization of macrophages, which was confirmed in BRCA1 mutant PDX models [74]. Further, STAT3 ablation in tumor cells was found to decrease pro-tumor macrophage polarization and led to increased tumor infiltrating T cells during PARPi treatment [74]. Taken together, elevated STAT3 activity in response to PARPi treatment appears to drive resistance while activation of STAT1 signaling drives immune cell infiltration and improved patient outcomes in the setting of checkpoint inhibitors. Thus, the STAT1 and STAT3 signatures reported here may have predictive value for response to such treatment strategies and could be used to monitor developing resistance phenotypes. Further, these data suggest that strategies that specifically inhibit STAT3 signaling while sparing STAT1 may offer superior clinical benefits.

It should be noted that there are limitations to this study. First, we used two representative cell line models of high grade serous (OVSAHO) and endometrioid (MDAH) ovarian cancer subtypes, as these represent the two most commonly diagnosed subtypes, and we found the cell lines exhibited variable STAT signaling throughout this study. The two subtypes vary in their genetic background, with 90% of high grade serous tumors having TP53 mutations and 50% exhibiting homologous recombination deficiencies (primarily BRCA1/2 mutations) [75, 76]. For endometrioid tumors, frequent mutations include AIRD1A, PIK3CA and KRAS mutations and these tumors are associated with the loss of PTEN [77, 78]. High grade endometroid tumors also exhibit TP53 and BRCA1/2 mutations in about 50% of cases [77, 79]. The two cell lines chosen for study here are largely representative of these alterations with OVSAHO cells harboring TP53 mutations and is BRCA2 deficient [80] and MDAH cells harboring TP53 mutations and alterations in PIK3CA signaling [81]. Regardless, the treatment of these two subtypes remains largely identical [5]. Many additional models, representative of all ovarian cancer subtypes, would need to be studied to determine if subtype-specific STAT1 and STAT3 signatures exist. Secondly, we utilized IFNγ and IL6 to activate STAT1 and STAT3 signaling respectively. In ovarian tumors, IFNγ [26, 82] and IL6 [22, 83, 84] are routinely found in the tumor microenvironment at high levels. However, there are additional cytokines and signaling cascades that can induce STAT1 and STAT3 signaling including type I and type III interferons, IL2 and IL10 family members, a variety of growth factors, as well as toll-like receptor signaling [85–87]. Therefore, it is possible that the STAT1 and STAT3 activation signatures reported here may not be completely representative of these other means of regulation. Further, we chose 30 min and 2 h treatment times for the ChIPseq and RNAseq experiments respectively, with the idea of capturing immediate responses to cytokine treatment. We acknowledge that different lengths of treatment and different concentrations of cytokines may address some incongruencies seen here and would undoubtedly alter the results. Therefore, future studies examining different treatment conditions could reveal additional information for the refinement of STAT1 and STAT3 specific gene signatures. Additionally, these gene signatures were derived from ovarian cancer cell lines but have been applied to RNAseq datasets from human tumors which contain many additional cell types comprising the tumor microenvironment, including immune cells. Thus, it is not possible to only assess these signatures within the cancer cell population with bulk RNAseq datasets and stronger prognostic and predictive information may be gleaned from single cell transcriptomic profiles of tumors where cancer cell expression profiles could be interrogated specifically. Finally, it is not clear if these signatures have prognostic or predictive value across all histologic subtypes of ovarian cancer given that they were derived from a high-grade serous model and have been applied to largely high-grade serous patient cohorts.

Conclusions

Overall, this study has implemented unbiased and genome-wide approaches to delineate the STAT1 and STAT3 cistromes and transcriptomes in two ovarian cancer cell lines. These studies have revealed largely unique functions of these two transcription factors in ovarian cancer that have implications for tumor progression and therapeutic response. Using these data, in combination with other publicly available datasets, we have developed gene signatures reflective of STAT1 and STAT3 specific activation. Application of these signatures to RNAseq data generated from ovarian tumors is shown to be of differential prognostic and predictive value. The implications of these findings suggest that broad JAK/STAT inhibition is not an ideal therapeutic approach. Rather the continued development of drugs and strategies that selectively modulate specific STATs, and/or their downstream effectors, may offer superior clinical benefit, particularly when combined with standard-of-care therapies and immune checkpoint blockade.
