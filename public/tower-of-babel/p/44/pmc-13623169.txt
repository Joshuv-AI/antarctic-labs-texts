Advancing Occupational Noise-Induced Hearing Loss Risk Assessment in the Era of Large-Scale Data
Authors: Wei Qiu, Michael Simpson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Occupational noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL) remains a leading occupational disorder worldwide and continues to impose substantial burdens on workers, employers, compensation systems, and health systems. ISO 1999 remains the principal framework for estimating population-level hearing loss associated with occupational noise exposure, while ISO 9612 provides the corresponding methodology for exposure determination. However, the context in which these models are applied has changed substantially. Hearing conservation programs, surveillance systems, and retrospective industrial cohorts now generate large audiometric and exposure datasets that allow direct comparisons between predicted and observed outcomes in real worker populations. This narrative review examines how such data can strengthen occupational NIHL risk assessment through validation, characterization of prediction gaps, population-specific calibration, harmonized reporting of early high-frequency indicators, and prevention-oriented interpretation. It also considers the continued value of energy-based approaches and the limits of LAeq-only modeling in complex-noise environments characterized by impulsiveness, intermittency, or non-Gaussian temporal structure. The argument is not that established standards should be discarded, but that their implementation should become more transparent, data-informed, and adaptive. A staged pathway is proposed in which established frameworks are retained while their use is strengthened through structured validation procedures, calibration strategies, explicit applicability statements, and data infrastructures that support future model refinement. The proposed framework also treats real-world data heterogeneity, healthy-worker selection, co-exposures, baseline-rule inconsistency, and the field operability of complex-noise metrics as explicit determinants of model transportability. For prediction applications, it emphasizes prespecified endpoints, transparent calibration, external validation, and avoidance of overinterpretation by machine learning or other high-dimensional methods.

INTRODUCTION

Noise-induced hearing loss is one of the oldest recognized occupational disorders, yet it remains a major public health and workplace problem.[12] In many countries, occupational noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL) continues to rank among the most frequently reported work-related diseases, and its burden extends beyond pure-tone threshold elevation alone. Workers with NIHL may experience difficulty understanding speech, reduced situational awareness, fatigue, tinnitus, impaired work performance, and deterioration in quality of life. At the population level, NIHL creates costs for employers, compensation systems, and health services and complicates the management of an aging workforce in which occupational and non-occupational hearing risks coexist.[34] A recent global-burden analysis also indicates that the occupational NIHL burden remains substantial and is projected to persist without stronger prevention.[5]

For decades, occupational noise risk assessment has relied on the equal-energy principle, according to which the damaging potential of exposure is determined primarily by cumulative acoustic energy. This principle underlies the widespread use of equivalent continuous sound level metrics such as equivalent continuous A-weighted sound pressure level (LAeq) and provides much of the scientific foundation for occupational exposure limits, hearing conservation practice, and statistical risk estimation.[67] ISO 1999 is the best-known international framework for estimating hearing loss associated with occupational noise exposure at the population level.[8] It has remained influential because it links exposure level, exposure duration, and age-related hearing status in an operationally usable statistical model. ISO 9612, in turn, provides the standardized methodology for determining occupational noise exposure.[9]

The contemporary context differs markedly from the one in which many foundational NIHL risk models were developed. Occupational health surveillance systems now capture large numbers of audiograms, often linked to job titles, industries, and exposure information, while exposure assessment practices have become more standardized.[91011] Together, these developments create an opportunity to ask a more practical question than whether a risk model is simply accepted by tradition: how well do its predictions align with observed hearing outcomes in real worker populations, and how should discrepancies be interpreted?

This review argues that occupational NIHL risk assessment should evolve from static estimation alone toward a broader implementation framework that includes validation against large datasets, explicit characterization of prediction gaps, population-specific calibration, harmonized reporting of early high-frequency outcomes, and prevention-oriented interpretation. The goal is not to replace ISO 1999 but to strengthen the way it is applied, evaluated, and interpreted in contemporary occupational health practice. SCOPE AND APPROACH

This article is a narrative review with an implementation-oriented focus. It does not aim to provide a systematic review or meta-analysis of all NIHL literature. Instead, it synthesizes evidence and concepts that are most relevant to the contemporary use of occupational NIHL risk models, with particular emphasis on three issues: the continuing role of established standards such as ISO 1999 and ISO 9612, the growing availability of large occupational audiometric datasets, and the need for validation and calibration strategies that improve interpretation in real-world worker populations.

The review, therefore, combines a discussion of established evidence, practical limitations in current implementation, and a staged framework for future development. Although evidence supporting some descriptors of temporal structure, especially kurtosis in relation to complex noise, has grown substantially, challenges remain in harmonization, comparative validation, and standardized implementation across populations and exposure environments.[1213] The aim is, therefore, not to claim premature consensus on all proposed refinements but to identify a structured pathway for comparative evaluation and future standard development.

HISTORICAL FOUNDATIONS AND CURRENT STANDARDS

Early NIHL research established that sustained exposure to intense sound can produce both temporary and permanent changes in hearing sensitivity.[4] Experimental and mechanistic studies demonstrated injury to cochlear sensory and neural structures, while occupational epidemiology showed that the long-term effects of workplace noise could be described probabilistically at the population level.[141516] These parallel lines of evidence laid the foundation for modern occupational hearing risk assessment.

The equal-energy principle provided the conceptual bridge between exposure assessment and risk modeling. Under this principle, exposures with the same cumulative acoustic energy are assumed to confer comparable risk, regardless of their temporal distribution. This framework made occupational exposure assessment tractable, comparable, and operationally useful, and it remains deeply embedded in hearing conservation practice. ISO 1999 translated this body of knowledge into an internationally standardized estimation approach, whereas ISO 9612 provided a structured framework for measuring workers’ exposure to noise and calculating the exposure level.

The enduring value of ISO 1999 lies not only in its equations but also in its role as a shared language across research, regulation, and practice. Researchers, regulators, occupational health practitioners, and employers can use the same framework to discuss expected hearing loss distributions in exposed populations. At the same time, all estimation standards operate within assumptions regarding the populations studied, the exposure environments represented, and the hearing outcomes selected. Contemporary use of these standards, therefore, requires not only continued reliance on their core logic but also a clearer understanding of where their predictions apply well and where they do not. EVIDENCE BASE FOR OCCUPATIONAL NIHL RISK ASSESSMENT

The evidence base for occupational NIHL risk assessment is multi-layered. At the mechanistic level, auditory physiology and experimental otology explain how excessive sound can damage hair cells, synapses, and other cochlear structures. Animal studies further clarify how intensity, duration, intermittency, and impulsiveness may influence injury under controlled conditions.[151617] These lines of evidence are indispensable for biological plausibility, but they do not by themselves define population-level risk.

Human studies add complementary information. Laboratory studies provide insights into temporary threshold shifts and short-term auditory effects. In contrast, occupational cohort studies supply the longer timescale required to relate workplace exposure histories to permanent hearing outcomes.[18192021] Historically, such cohort studies formed the principal empirical basis for occupational hearing standards, although many were constrained by modest sample size, limited exposure characterization, or restricted industrial scope.

A newer layer of evidence now comes from large occupational surveillance and administrative datasets. Hearing conservation programs in manufacturing, construction, mining, transport, and military settings routinely generate repeated audiometric records, often at a scale far larger than that of classical research cohorts.[31011] These datasets do not replace mechanistic or epidemiological evidence. Rather, they create an opportunity to examine how well long-standing population models perform when applied to contemporary workforces, heterogeneous industries, and real-world monitoring systems.[222324] For this reason, they are especially relevant to questions of model transportability, validation, and calibration.

The broader occupational hearing literature has also recognized that hearing outcomes may be shaped not only by noise but also by co-exposures, including solvents, metals, vibration, and other workplace hazards.[41725] This point matters because it affects how model misfit should be interpreted in surveillance datasets. Apparent deviations from model expectations may sometimes reflect incomplete exposure characterization rather than a failure of the underlying noise-risk framework itself.

LARGE-SCALE DATASETS, VALIDATION, AND PREDICTION GAPS

The practical importance of large occupational datasets lies in their volume, diversity, and repeated-measurement structure.[101122] Unlike classical cohorts assembled for a specific research purpose, surveillance data arise from routine hearing conservation activities and therefore reflect the heterogeneity of real occupational health practice, including differences in industry mix, audiometric schedules, exposure assignment, baseline hearing status, and program quality.[26] This heterogeneity creates both opportunity and complexity.

The opportunity is straightforward: sufficiently large datasets allow direct comparisons between model-predicted and observed hearing outcomes across strata of age, exposure, industry, and calendar time. The complexity is equally important: surveillance systems vary in completeness, exposure characterization, and quality control, so validation should not be treated as a simple pass-or-fail exercise. Instead, it should aim to identify where prediction performs well, where it performs poorly, and how uncertainty should be interpreted.

In this context, the concept of the prediction gap should be defined a priori rather than being used only descriptively. For validation purposes, a prediction gap is the difference between observed and model-predicted hearing outcomes within a specified population, stratum, frequency, or endpoint. It should be summarized using complementary metrics, including mean bias, mean absolute error, root mean square error, calibration slope and intercept, Bland–Altman limits of agreement, standardized residuals, and the proportion of observations or strata within prespecified tolerance limits.[1011] Because audiometric variability and outcome definitions differ across datasets, universal thresholds should be avoided; instead, studies should define clinically and operationally interpretable thresholds before analysis. As a proposed practical framework for group-level interpretation, deviations below approximately 3 dB may generally be regarded as small because they are below the usual 5-dB step size and typical test–retest variability of conventional pure-tone audiometry. Deviations of approximately 3–5 dB may warrant attention when consistent across frequencies or strata, whereas persistent deviations exceeding 5 dB, or approximately 0.5 standard deviations, should prompt calibration or further investigation. These thresholds should not be interpreted as regulatory definitions of threshold shift but as pragmatic criteria for evaluating model performance in large datasets.[101122] Importantly, a prediction gap does not necessarily indicate failure of the standard itself. It may reflect imperfect transportability, exposure misclassification, inconsistent audiometric practice, baseline differences, survivor effects, or differences in hearing conservation performance. A formal prediction-gap framework is, therefore, valuable precisely because it helps separate scientific limitations from operational ones.

A further source of bias is the healthy worker effect and related survivor bias. Workers with early auditory susceptibility, poor tolerance of noisy jobs, or early threshold deterioration may transfer, leave employment, or be reassigned before later audiometric follow-up. Retained workforces may therefore appear healthier than the original exposed population, leading to an underestimation of cumulative noise-related damage.[2728] Validation studies should report baseline hearing status, employment duration, job transfer, follow-up completeness, and, where possible, exit audiograms or attrition patterns and should consider duration-stratified analyses or inverse-probability approaches when attrition is substantial.

Large-scale occupational data also shift the field from assumption-driven implementation toward evidence-informed implementation. If a surveillance database consistently shows that observed high-frequency threshold shifts exceed model-based expectations in a given sector, that finding has practical value even if it does not immediately justify revision of a standard. It may indicate poor program performance, deficient exposure assignment, important co-exposures, or limitations in model applicability. In all of these cases, transparent evaluation is a better starting point than either complacency or premature rejection.[1011]

POPULATION-SPECIFIC CALIBRATION

Population-specific calibration offers a practical middle path between uncritical reliance on default models and premature calls for wholesale replacement. Calibration does not alter the conceptual basis of ISO 1999. Rather, it provides a structured way to evaluate model performance in a target population and, where justified, adjust interpretation or reporting accordingly. A useful calibration workflow would begin with a clear definition of the target population, harmonized exposure metrics, and frequency-specific hearing outcomes. Predicted-versus-observed comparisons should then be summarized using common performance indicators, including bias, mean absolute error, root mean square error, calibration slope and intercept, and Bland–Altman limits of agreement.[2930] In sufficiently large datasets, split-sample, bootstrap, or external validation approaches should be used to examine model stability. Where model deviations are consistent and interpretable, calibration coefficients or structured reporting adjustments may be considered for that sector or population, provided that uncertainty, attrition, co-exposure information, and audiometric quality-control limitations are explicitly reported.[3132]

The main advantage of calibration is transparency. It allows occupational health programs to document whether model-based estimates tend to underpredict or overpredict observed hearing outcomes and by how much. This is especially useful when the model is being used not only for academic analysis but also for program evaluation, prevention planning, or interpretation of surveillance findings. A system that continues to use ISO 1999 while explicitly documenting local model performance is often preferable to a fragmented system in which common standards are abandoned without a validated replacement. Calibration should therefore be understood as a disciplined form of interpretation, not an ad hoc adjustment. Its scientific credibility depends on the clarity of the target population, outcome definition, exposure assignment, and uncertainty reporting. Without those elements, calibration risks becoming a post hoc explanation for poorly characterized data rather than a reproducible implementation tool.

HIGH-FREQUENCY INDICATORS FOR EARLY SURVEILLANCE

Among conventional audiometric outcomes, thresholds in the 3–6 kHz region remain the most informative markers of early occupational NIHL. This pattern is consistent with the classical 4-kHz notch phenomenon and adjacent high-frequency involvement, as well as with long-standing occupational audiology practice.[1833] However, routine reporting is often less focused than routine measurement: many surveillance systems collect thresholds at these frequencies but summarize hearing status primarily with broader or lower-frequency averages that are less sensitive to early change.[3334]

Greater emphasis on high-frequency indicators would strengthen both surveillance and model evaluation. First, it would align reporting with the early expression of occupational NIHL. Second, it would increase the sensitivity of prediction-gap analyses, since high-frequency deviations may emerge before broader threshold summaries begin to diverge. Third, it would usually impose little additional measurement burden because these frequencies are already included in most occupational audiometric protocols.

A pragmatic step would therefore be to encourage a minimal harmonized reporting set that includes individual thresholds at 3, 4, and 6 kHz and a clearly defined conventional high-frequency average calculated as the arithmetic mean of thresholds at 3, 4, and 6 kHz.[183335] Reporting the component frequencies remains important because different notches, surveillance rules, and regulatory definitions may emphasize different frequencies. Thresholds at 8 kHz and at extended high frequencies above 8 kHz should be reported separately from the conventional 3–4–6 kHz high-frequency average unless a distinct extended high-frequency endpoint is prespecified. Such reporting would not replace diagnostic criteria or lower-frequency outcomes but would improve comparability across programs and provide a more sensitive basis for early surveillance and model assessment.[3536]

COMPLEX-NOISE ENVIRONMENTS AND THE LIMITS OF ENERGY-ALONE MODELS

Equivalent-energy metrics such as LAeq remain foundational in occupational acoustics because they make exposure assessment tractable, comparable, and operationally consistent. For this reason, the equal-energy principle continues to provide the core logic of most occupational exposure limits and hearing risk models. At the same time, one of the most important unresolved questions in NIHL science is whether cumulative energy alone is sufficient to characterize risk across all workplace environments, particularly those involving impact tools, impulsive bursts, interrupted noise, or highly non-Gaussian temporal structure.

Evidence accumulated over the past two decades suggests that temporal structure may contribute risk information beyond equivalent energy alone. Experimental work showed that, for equivalent energy and spectrum, high-kurtosis non-Gaussian noise can produce greater auditory injury than Gaussian noise and that kurtosis can distinguish these exposure environments in a biologically meaningful way.[3738] Subsequent occupational and translational studies explored kurtosis and kurtosis-adjusted cumulative noise exposure as possible ways to capture aspects of temporal complexity that are not represented by LAeq alone.[12133940]

Taken together, these findings do not invalidate the equal-energy framework, but they do indicate that its applicability may vary across exposure environments and that some complex-noise settings may be insufficiently characterized by energy metrics alone.[1213] The existing literature suggests that kurtosis-based correction has promise for reducing the underestimation of risk in non-Gaussian or highly impulsive industrial noise.[3738] However, the evidence remains heterogeneous with respect to study design, exposure characterization, population, and implementation feasibility.

Large occupational datasets could play a decisive role in the next step. If future surveillance systems capture not only equivalent-energy measures but also selected descriptors of temporal structure, they would allow direct comparison of whether such metrics improve prediction beyond LAeq and exposure duration. That would move the discussion from conceptual critique toward reproducible comparative model evaluation, which is the evidentiary basis ultimately needed for future standard development.

Operational implementation of kurtosis also requires explicit engineering standardization. Kurtosis is sensitive to the acoustic sampling rate, weighting network, anti-alias filtering, microphone and preamplifier dynamic range, overload handling, analysis-window length, and method used to aggregate window-level values.[1341] Conventional personal noise dosimeters that store only integrated dose, time-weighted average, or LAeq summaries are generally insufficient for retrospective kurtosis estimation unless firmware, storage, and export functions are upgraded to capture waveforms or high-resolution time histories.[3940] A practical field pathway is to compute kurtosis in prespecified short windows, such as 60-s windows, while retaining calibration flags, overload flags, and window-level moment summaries when continuous waveform storage is not feasible.[41] Pilot industrial studies indicate that such measurements are feasible, but cross-device harmonization and transparent processing specifications are needed before broad regulatory use.[13394041]

METHODOLOGICAL PRIORITIES FOR LARGE-DATASET VALIDATION

Several methodological issues require explicit attention if large occupational datasets are to support meaningful model evaluation. The first issue is exposure assignment. In many surveillance systems, exposure is inferred from job title, department, or area measurement rather than from repeated individual dosimetry.[101122] Although this may be adequate for program administration, it can obscure within-job variability when the goal is model validation. Future studies should therefore document the exposure assessment strategy clearly and, where possible, align it with ISO 9612 concepts of measurement strategy and uncertainty.[9]

The second issue is outcome definition and audiometric quality control. Routine audiometric data are shaped by audiometer calibration, transducer type, booth or room conditions, ambient noise, tester practice, worker instructions, baseline selection, retest procedures, and the handling of temporary threshold shifts.[20222635] Differences across companies or countries can therefore generate apparent prediction gaps even when the exposure-risk model is not the primary source of error. Validation analyses should report audiometric quality-control assumptions, specify whether outcomes are frequency-specific or averaged, distinguish early high-frequency change from later broader impairment, and document retest rules, exclusion criteria, and missing-data handling. When heterogeneous surveillance systems are pooled, site-level sensitivity analyses or random-effects approaches should be considered before interpreting predicted-versus-observed differences as true model prediction gaps.

A third issue is confounding and co-exposure. Occupational hearing outcomes may be influenced not only by noise but also by solvents, metals, vibration, ototoxic medications, non-occupational sound exposure, smoking, cardiovascular risk factors, and other medical conditions. Where these variables are available, they should be incorporated as covariates, stratification variables, or sensitivity-analysis variables in validation and calibration analyses. Where they are unavailable, calibration should be interpreted as a population-specific performance adjustment rather than a causal estimate of the independent effect of noise. Future database designs should therefore include structured fields for relevant chemical exposures, vibration, ototoxic medication use, non-occupational noise, hearing protector use, and major health covariates.[4172542]

A fourth issue concerns analytic overreach. As large occupational datasets become available, there will be increasing interest in machine learning, automated phenotyping, and predictive classification. To avoid analytic overreach, these approaches should be implemented within a prespecified prediction-model framework rather than used as exploratory pattern-finding alone. The target outcome should be defined before modeling; predictors should be restricted to variables available before or at the time of prediction; and potential feature leakage from post-outcome variables, repeated measurements from the same worker, or site-specific identifiers should be explicitly prevented. Model development should separate training and testing data at the worker, site, or calendar-time level where possible and should include internal resampling as well as independent or external validation. Performance should be assessed using both discrimination and calibration metrics, with attention to class imbalance, uncertainty, and comparison against simpler transparent baseline models. Interpretability tools may help explain fitted models, but they do not establish causality, transportability, or clinical utility. Therefore, machine learning models should be reported and evaluated according to contemporary prediction-model guidance, including risk-of-bias and applicability assessment, and should complement rather than replace validated exposure assessment, audiometric quality assurance, and clinically meaningful hearing endpoints.[43444546]

Finally, comparability across settings deserves greater attention. If the field is to progress toward multi-country or multi-sector validation, data dictionaries and reporting conventions will need to become more harmonized. Minimal common elements should include exposure metric definitions, audiometric frequencies, baseline rules, demographic coding, duration variables, and core program descriptors. Baseline rules and threshold-shift definitions must be reported explicitly because definitions such as the U.S. OSHA standard threshold shift (STS) are not interchangeable with other regulatory or surveillance definitions that use different frequencies, age-correction practices, retest procedures, or diagnostic thresholds.[6] Multi-jurisdictional datasets should either harmonize outcomes to a common analytic definition or analyze results separately by regulatory definition.[1011] Table 2 summarizes the minimum reporting elements that would improve the interpretability and comparability of validation studies based on large occupational datasets.

IMPLICATIONS FOR HEARING CONSERVATION AND POLICY

The reason NIHL risk models matter is that they influence prevention. Employers, occupational health practitioners, regulators, and workers need interpretable outputs that can support action early enough to matter. A model that is statistically elegant but disconnected from hearing conservation implementation has limited real-world value. Conversely, a surveillance system that collects large volumes of data without a framework for interpretation leaves prevention opportunities unrealized.

One practical implication of the framework proposed here is that prediction outputs should be linked more explicitly to hearing conservation decision-making. This does not require redefining legal exposure limits. It requires better use of model-based expectations and surveillance data to identify sectors, jobs, or subpopulations in which risk appears higher or lower than expected. A second implication concerns program evaluation. Hearing conservation programs are often judged primarily by compliance indicators, such as monitoring coverage, training completion, or hearing protector availability. Prediction gap and calibration approaches add an outcome-oriented dimension by examining whether observed hearing change is broadly consistent with what a population model would predict.

There is also a practical prevention argument for making audiometric data more actionable. Population-based analysis of routinely collected audiograms has been proposed as a way to transform hearing conservation from a largely compliance-driven activity into a more responsive secondary-prevention system.[11] Likewise, intervention studies suggest that more direct monitoring of effective daily exposure may help reduce hearing loss risk when used as part of a broader administrative feedback system.[47]

At the policy level, improved reporting of high-frequency outcomes and calibration metrics could make comparisons across sectors or jurisdictions more meaningful.[223334] Instead of relying solely on case counts or prevalence of hearing impairment, agencies could examine whether predicted risk and observed thresholds align across industries and whether some environments, especially complex-noise settings, show persistent underprediction. That information would be useful for prevention planning, inspection targeting, and future standard revision.

A STAGED ROADMAP FOR NEXT-GENERATION NIHL RISK ASSESSMENT

A staged development pathway is preferable to abrupt reform of core risk models. In the near term, the priority is not the replacement of existing models but improvement in implementation. Practical steps include standardized templates for predicted-versus-observed comparisons, shared calibration metrics, harmonized reporting of high-frequency outcomes, and clearer applicability statements for complex-noise environments.

A second stage should focus on multi-sector and multi-country validation. The central question is whether prediction gaps are small and inconsistent across populations, or whether they show stable and interpretable patterns by industry, jurisdiction, or exposure environment. Such comparisons would be most informative if data dictionaries, exposure metrics, and reporting formats were harmonized in advance. A third stage concerns the standardization of complex-noise metrics. Among descriptors of temporal structure, kurtosis has moved beyond purely exploratory use and has support from experimental, epidemiological, and translational studies as a practical complement to energy-based metrics for assessing NIHL risk associated with complex noise.[13394041] The next task is operational rather than rhetorical: field studies should harmonize the sampling rate, weighting network, analysis-window duration, overload handling, dynamic-range requirements, and aggregation method for kurtosis estimation. The key question is, therefore, how such metrics should be comparatively validated across populations and incorporated into a normative framework without sacrificing the transparency, usability, and continuity of established energy-based models.

A fourth and longer-term stage may involve more advanced predictive tools, including automated phenotyping and machine-learning-assisted risk stratification. Recent NIHL prediction studies illustrate the potential of neural network, tree-based, and other machine-learning methods for risk stratification, especially when large routine occupational health datasets are available.[43444546] However, such approaches should be externally validated, calibrated, and evaluated for risk of bias before implementation. Importantly, these tools should be developed on harmonized data structures and clinically interpretable outcomes and should complement rather than replace validated exposure assessment and meaningful hearing endpoints.

Seen in this way, next-generation NIHL risk assessment does not require a break with the past. It requires a more mature implementation layer around existing models, coupled with a stronger data infrastructure for future comparison and refinement.

CONCLUSION

Occupational NIHL risk assessment is entering a new phase. The field continues to rely on a durable energy-based framework, anchored by standards such as ISO 1999 and ISO 9612 and supported by decades of mechanistic, epidemiological, and occupational research. That framework remains valuable and should not be discarded lightly.

What has changed is the evidentiary environment in which these models are used. Large-scale occupational audiometric datasets now make it increasingly possible to test how model predictions align with observed hearing outcomes in real worker populations. This shifts attention from estimation alone toward a broader workflow that includes validation, characterization of prediction gaps, calibration, and prevention-oriented interpretation. The revised implementation framework therefore requires explicit handling of audiometric quality-control heterogeneity, healthy-worker selection, co-exposures, baseline-rule differences, and prespecified prediction-gap metrics.

At the same time, the literature on complex noise has advanced beyond a purely exploratory stage. Among descriptors of temporal structure, kurtosis has gained substantial support from experimental, epidemiological, and translational studies as a practical complement to energy-based metrics for assessing NIHL risk associated with complex noise, and the issue has entered formal standardization discussions. The immediate task is therefore not whether such metrics merit consideration in principle, but how they should be harmonized, comparatively validated across populations, and incorporated into a normative framework without sacrificing the transparency, usability, and continuity of established energy-based models. Practical use of kurtosis will also require harmonized measurement specifications, including sampling rate, window length, dynamic range, overload handling, and data storage or firmware requirements.

The most productive path forward is therefore neither complacent reliance on tradition nor premature abandonment of established models. It is a staged, evidence-based approach in which existing standards are retained, their implementation is made more transparent, and justified refinements, especially for complex-noise environments, are pursued through harmonized comparative evaluation and careful standard development.
