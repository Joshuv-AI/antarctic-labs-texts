Comparative molecular dynamics mapping of metallocarboxypeptidase-peptide interfaces reveals potential hotspots that inspire novel inhibitor design
Authors: Amirhossein Akbarpour Arsanjani, Davood Rabiei Faradonbeh, Ziba Veisi Malekshahi, Babak Negahdari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metallocarboxypeptidases (MCPs) are critical zinc-containing exopeptidases and promising therapeutic targets for conditions ranging from thromboembolic disorders to cancer. Despite their importance, the dynamic determinants governing subfamily-specific peptide recognition remain incompletely understood. In this study, we performed 200 ns molecular dynamics simulations on five distinct A/B MCP–peptide complexes and integrated contact analysis and per-residue energy decomposition (MM/PBSA) to systematically map their interaction profiles. We identified a core set of Grade A and B conserved hotspots—specifically Arg71, Arg127, Glu163, Thr164, and Tyr248—that mediate persistent interactions across the subfamilies. Based on their energetic and geometric profiles, we categorized these residues into three functional classes. Class I (functional hotspots): Such as Arg71 and Arg127, which act as near-permanent “anchoring hubs” with contact occupancies exceeding 90% and dominant negative binding free energies. Class II (topological hotspots): Such as Glu163, which maintains the structural integrity of the active site cleft. Class III (auxiliary energetic contributors): Which provide additional stabilization through transient or non-polar contacts. Furthermore, we identified complex-specific residues, such as Glu270 in hCPB1, that serve as selectivity filters. Collectively, this study highlights the key enzyme residues that mediate persistent interactions at the interface and provides molecular insights that may guide the rational design of novel metallocarboxypeptidase inhibitors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53512-5.

Introduction

MCPs represent a crucial family of zinc-containing exopeptidases that play an essential role in the processing of proteins and peptides by cleaving C-terminal peptide bonds. These enzymes are classified into two key subfamilies: the pancreatic/digestive subfamily, known as carboxypeptidase A/B (CPA/CPB), and the regulatory subfamily (CPN/E)1. Within each subfamily, the amino acid sequence identity ranges from 25 to 63%. However, when comparing the two subfamilies, the identity decreases to only 15–25%2. Given the significant structural differences between these two subfamilies, our focus will be exclusively on the A/B subfamily.

Members of the A/B subfamily are synthesized as inactive zymogens, containing a 90–95 residue N-terminal pro-region known as the activation segment. This segment is cleverly linked to the enzyme via a connecting region, ensuring that the enzyme remains inactive until needed. When the activation segment is cleaved, it triggers the conversion of the enzyme into its active form, unleashing its powerful catalytic abilities3–5. Carboxypeptidase A (CPA) is finely tuned to prefer aliphatic and aromatic C-terminal residues, with CPA1 favoring aliphatic and small aromatic residues, whereas CPA2 targets the bulky side chain of tryptophan. In contrast, Carboxypeptidase B (CPB) zeroes in on basic C-terminal residues4,6,7.

In mammals, A/B MCPs are critical to diverse physiological processes, including digestion, inflammation, fibrinolysis, and neuropeptide processing. Their involvement in pathologies such as thromboembolic disorders and oncology has made them significant therapeutic targets7–11. CPB2, produced by the liver and known as thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI), regulates fibrinolysis by cleaving plasminogen binding sites and is extensively studied for this role5,12,13.

The development of inhibitors targeting A/B MCPs has attracted considerable attention due to their potential therapeutic applications in various diseases. For example, inhibiting CPB2 can enhance fibrinolysis, providing a promising strategy for the treatment of thromboembolic disorders12,13. Additionally, in the field of oncology, these inhibitors have shown potential in suppressing tumor growth14. To date, several small-molecule, antibody, and peptide-based inhibitors have been designed and characterized against MCPs. For instance, the development of AZD9684 and DS-1040, which target human carboxypeptidaseB2 (hCPB2), has advanced to phase 2 clinical trials. Additionally, some other molecules have successfully completed phase 1 or preclinical studies. Nevertheless, no approved metallocarboxypeptidase inhibitor has reached the market to date5,15.

Peptides offer an alternative to antibodies with multiple advantages, including simpler, lower-cost production, while still providing specific targeting of interactions, unlike small molecules16. Currently, only a few peptides have been identified that specifically inhibit A/B MCPs with nanomolar Ki values. These include the 39-residue Potato Carboxypeptidase Inhibitor (PCI), which occurs naturally in potatoes17; the 67-residue Leech Carboxypeptidase Inhibitor (LCI) from Hirudo medicinalis18; and the 75-residue Tick Carboxypeptidase Inhibitor (TCI) from the tick Rhipicephalus bursa19. While the overall amino acid sequences of these peptides lack significant homology, the C-terminal sequences of the PCI and LCI display a notable similarity (–Xaa-Pro-Tyr-Val–Xaa). This conservation points toward a shared mechanism of action for their inhibitory function19. But, The C-terminal sequence of the TCI is different and lacks the ‘Pro-Tyr-Val’ core. PCI, LCI, and TCI share a core inhibitory mechanism but employ distinct initial binding modes to achieve it. PCI and LCI act in a ‘single-headed’ manner, in which primary recognition is mediated by their C-terminal tails fitting into the enzyme’s active-site groove, mimicking a substrate. In contrast, TCI utilizes a ‘double-headed’ binding mode: its N-terminal domain first anchors to surface segments at the rim of the active site cleft, while its C-terminal tail concurrently engages the catalytic center. This dual anchoring results in a larger contact area and superior stabilization. Despite these differing modes of initial docking, the final inhibitory step is conserved. In all cases, the enzyme’s own catalytic machinery hydrolyzes the ultimate C-terminal residue of the inhibitor (Gly39 in PCI, Glu66 in LCI, and His75 in TCI). The newly exposed carboxylate group of the penultimate residue then forms a stable coordination with the catalytic zinc ion in the S1’ pocket. This anchoring interaction is reinforced by a network of strong contacts between the preceding inhibitor residues and their complementary enzyme subsite. This combined action of direct metal coordination and extensive subsite filling occupies the substrate-binding cleft, thereby impeding further access of substrates20–23.

While the structures of several MCP–inhibitor complexes have been characterized via NMR and crystallography, these static models offer limited insight into the dynamic persistence of binding motifs under physiological conditions. There remains an incomplete understanding of which specific molecular determinants are consistently maintained across distinct A/B subfamilies versus those that provide complex-specific selectivity. In this study, we performed 200 ns molecular dynamics simulations on five distinct complexes to systematically map their time-resolved interaction profiling. This comparative mapping aims to identify dynamic ‘potential hotspots’ essential for inhibition, providing a predictive framework for the rational design of more selective peptide inhibitors.

Method

Multiple sequence alignment

The amino acid sequences for the five metallocarboxypeptidase (MCP) enzymes were extracted from their respective Protein Data Bank structures (PDB IDs: 4CPA, 7EQZ, 1DTD, 1ZLI, 3LMS). Pairwise sequence similarity was initially assessed using alignments scored with the BLOSUM62 substitution matrix24. To contextualize the observed differences in inhibitor binding across the subfamily, multiple sequence alignment was performed using the Constraint-based Multiple Alignment Tool (COBALT)25. The final alignment was visualized and analyzed in Jalview v2.11.5.1 to assess residue-level conservation and identify functionally relevant residues within the catalytic and inhibitory subsites.

Selection and preparation of structures

The structures of PCI, LCI, and TCI have been studied in detail using NMR26–28, and several crystallographic structures of complexes formed between these peptides and carboxypeptidases have been reported. The available structures include PCI in complex with bovine carboxypeptidase A (EC 3.4.17.1)29, PCI in complex with Aedes aegypti carboxypeptidase B1 (EC 3.4.17.2)30, LCI in complex with human carboxypeptidase A2 (EC 3.4.17.15)27, TCI in Complex with human carboxypeptidase B1 (EC 3.4.2.2)23, and TCI in Complex with human carboxypeptidase B2 (EC 3.4.17.20)22. Non-protein molecules, such as crystallographic water, buffer ions, and irrelevant chains, were removed using UCSF ChimeraX v1.8.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

All molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using GROMACS version 2024.431. The AMBER99SB-ILDN force field was chosen, and solvation was performed with the TIP3P water model in a cubic box, maintaining a minimum distance of 1.0 nm between the solute and box edges. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions were added to neutralize the system and achieve a physiological ionic strength of 0.15 M. Energy minimization was carried out using the steepest descent algorithm (with a maximum of 50,000 steps) until the maximum force dropped below 1000 kJ/mol/nm. Long-range electrostatics were handled with the Particle-Mesh Ewald method, and both electrostatic and van der Waals cutoffs were set to 1.0 nm. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all directions using the Verlet cutoff scheme. The minimized systems underwent two equilibration phases. First, NVT equilibration was performed for 1 ns using a 2 fs timestep and the V-rescale thermostat at 300 K, with separate coupling groups for the protein and solvent. Position restraints were applied to protein-heavy atoms. Next, NPT equilibration was run for one ns at 1 bar using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat with a 2.0 ps coupling constant and isotropic pressure coupling. The temperature control settings were identical to those in the NVT phase. Finally, production MD simulations were conducted for 200 ns using the same integrator and timestep. Coordinates, velocities, energies, and trajectories were saved every 10 ps for analysis.

Structural stability and validation analysis

To evaluate the structural stability of the A/B MCP–peptide complexes during molecular dynamics simulations, root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) analyses were performed using the backbone atoms of the entire complex for alignment. Custom index groups were generated using gmx make_ndx to define separate selections for the enzyme and peptide backbone. The first frame of the production run served as the reference frame for all calculations, allowing monitoring of deviations over the 200 ns trajectory. Separate RMSD curves were generated for the enzyme and peptide in each complex. In addition to RMSD, Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) was computed for Cα atoms to assess the flexibility of enzyme residues. To provide a baseline for the flexibility, we performed an additional 200 ns simulation of the hCPB2 apo enzyme as a representative model for the A/B subfamily.

Enzyme-peptide contact analysis

Given the central role of polar and electrostatic interactions in governing molecular recognition, binding specificity, and inhibitory mechanisms in protein-peptide complexes, hydrogen bonds and salt bridges were prioritized as primary descriptors for hotspot identification using GetContacts (https://getcontacts.github.io/), a computational framework for extracting atom-level interaction networks from molecular dynamics trajectories32–34. Hydrogen bonds were identified based on a donor–acceptor distance cutoff of 3.5 Å and an angular criterion requiring the donor-hydrogen-acceptor (D-H…) angle to exceed 110°, in accordance with the default geometric parameters implemented in the GetContact framework. Salt bridges were defined as electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged side-chain atoms of acidic (Asp, Glu) and basic (Arg, Lys) residues, with an interatomic distance of less than 4.0 Å. These default thresholds implemented in GetContacts were applied uniformly across all simulations. To ensure compatibility, the GROMACS trajectory file was first converted to DCD format using MDTraj (v1.9.7). Atom indices corresponding to the enzyme and peptide were then extracted from the topology file and used to define the selection groups. Contact analysis was performed using a Docker-based GetContacts installation. Salt bridges (sb) and hydrogen bonds (hb) were computed across the trajectory. The resulting contact profiles were analyzed to identify persistent interactions, characterize time-resolved contact profiles, and pinpoint residue-level hotspots based on their interaction frequency throughout the trajectory.

Per-residue energy decomposition

To quantitatively validate the functional significance of the identified geometric hotspots and ensure, per-residue binding energy decomposition was performed using the MM/PBSA (Molecular Mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area) method35, as detailed in Supporting Information (Energy). This analysis complements the contact mapping by correlating geometric persistence with energetic criticality. To ensure statistical representativeness, the analysis was performed on 100 evenly spaced snapshots extracted at 2-ns intervals from the stable 200-ns production trajectories.

Results

Comparative sequence architecture of the A/B MCP subfamily

To address the significant sequence diversity within the A/B MCP subfamily, which exhibits global identities ranging from 25 to 63%2, we performed a multiple sequence alignment to identify the structural basis for shared inhibitory mechanisms (Fig. 2). Despite this overall diversity, the alignment reveals striking conservation of the residues constituting the catalytic and inhibitory centers. As highlighted in the red boxes in Fig. 2, the residues essential for the zinc coordination sphere (His69, Glu72, and His196) and the primary S1’ anchoring hubs (Arg71, Arg127, and Arg145) are strictly maintained across all five investigated systems23. Other critical functional nodes, such as Tyr248 and the catalytic Glu270, also exhibit high primary sequence conservation despite the vast evolutionary distance between species such as Aedes aegypti and humans. Structural stability of A/B MCP-peptide complexes

The structural stability of the A/B MCP–peptide complexes during molecular dynamics simulations were assessed by calculating the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) over 200 ns of production (Fig. 3). Throughout all systems, the enzyme components exhibited relatively low and stable RMSD values, typically fluctuating within a narrow range of 0.13–0.18 nm after the initial equilibration phase. Notably, the PCI-bound bCPA showed slightly elevated enzyme RMSDs, reaching up to approximately 0.22 nm during the early phase of the simulation, but these values stabilized over time. The peptide inhibitors showed slightly higher RMSD values in several complexes, reflecting their greater flexibility. For instance, PCI in the bCPA complex reached RMSDs of up to 0.4 nm, whereas in the CPBAe1 system, PCI remained relatively restrained, fluctuating between 0.25 and 0.3 nm. Residue-level flexibility

To evaluate the local stability of the enzyme–peptide interfaces and investigate the conformational transitions occurring upon binding, we calculated the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) for all Cα atoms across the simulation trajectory. To provide a baseline for the flexibility of the variable loop region, we performed an additional 200 ns simulation of the hCPB2 apo enzyme, a representative model of the A/B subfamily (Fig. 4). Consistent with the global stability observed in the RMSD profiles, the enzyme components of the five complexes exhibit high structural rigidity, with most residues fluctuating within a narrow range. Significant fluctuations were noted at the N-termini of hCPB1, hCPB2, and bCPA, with values exceeding 0.3 nm, 0.7 nm, and 0.4 nm, respectively. This inherent mobility is typical of solvent-exposed terminal segments. Furthermore, the loop containing residues 246–249, previously identified as highly mobile in the apo state36, showed moderated fluctuations upon interaction of hCPB2 with the inhibitor. A point-to-point comparison of Tyr248 reveals a dramatic decrease in flexibility upon inhibitor binding. In the hCPB2 apo state, Tyr248 exhibited an RMSF value of 0.15 nm, reflecting the conformational plasticity required for its role as a proton donor during catalysis. Persistent enzyme–peptide interactions

To identify the key determinants of stability within each complex, hydrogen bonds and salt bridges were analyzed using the GetContacts tool. A 50% persistence threshold was applied across the simulation frames to filter transient interactions and isolate the specific residues essential for mediating persistent interactions over time. The use of a 50% occupancy cutoff is consistent with previous molecular dynamics studies37, in which interactions exceeding this threshold have been considered structurally significant. This comprehensive analysis revealed distinct and overlapping interaction profiles across the five A/B MCP-peptide complexes. For consistency in residue annotation and to facilitate the identification of homologous positions across different enzymes, all proteins were renumbered according to the residue numbering of human carboxypeptidase B2 (hCPB2), which was used as the reference structure.

Energetic profiling of the interface

To move beyond purely geometric descriptors and to verify the functional significance of the identified contacts, we performed per-residue binding free-energy decomposition using the MM-PBSA method. This analysis acts as a secondary filter to ensure that the hotspots identified in Sect. “Structural stability of A/B MCP-peptide complexes” are energetically critical and not merely artifacts of local proximity or the chosen persistence threshold. Following standard computational benchmarks, we adopted a single energy cutoff of − 1.0 kcal/mol to distinguish significant energetic contributors from minor background fluctuations. Residues meeting this energetic criterion across the five complexes are detailed in Table 1. Discussion

A/B MCPs play essential roles in physiological functions and have emerged as promising therapeutic targets12,14. In this study, we performed 200 ns molecular dynamics simulations on five distinct complexes to systematically map their time-resolved interaction profiling.

From static snapshots to persistent interaction networks

Previous crystallographic and NMR studies have revealed the structural basis of MCP inhibition, particularly the roles of the S1′ pocket and zinc coordination in stabilizing inhibitor binding20–23. However, such static models cannot resolve which interactions are transient, which are persistent, and which are energetically indispensable under physiological conditions. Our time-resolved analysis suggest that MCP–peptide interfaces are organized around a small set of persistent interaction hubs embedded within a broader network of transient contacts. Across all five complexes, Arg71 and Arg127 emerged as universal anchoring residues with contact occupancies exceeding 90% and consistently dominant negative binding free energies. The convergence of geometric persistence and energetic contribution provides robust evidence that these residues constitute the core inhibitory scaffold of the A/B MCP subfamily. In contrast, Arg145 displayed variable persistence across complexes, indicating that its contribution is context-dependent and modulated by subfamily-specific structural environments.

Functional stratification of hotspots: mechanistic implications of the three-class model

A central conceptual advance of this study is the functional classification of hotspots into three classes based on integrated geometric and energetic criteria. This framework clarifies the distinct roles played by conserved and variable residues in MCP inhibition. Class I hotspots represent functional anchors that simultaneously exhibit high persistence and favorable binding energies. The identification of Tyr248 as a universal hotspot is particularly noteworthy. While Tyr248 has long been recognized as a catalytic proton donor29, our data suggest that it also acts as a structural stabilizer, with its conformational immobilization a hallmark of effective inhibition. The dramatic reduction in Tyr248 flexibility upon inhibitor binding supports an induced-fit mechanism in which catalytic mobility is sacrificed for inhibitory stabilization23.

Class II hotspots, such as Glu163 (or Asp163 in hCPA2), exhibit high geometric persistence but often unfavorable or neutral energetic contributions. This apparent paradox suggests that these residues play a topological rather than energetic role. We propose that Glu163 functions as a structural organizer, positioning Class I residues, particularly Arg127, into optimal binding geometries. In this sense, Glu163 represents a “topological cost” that the system pays to stabilize the primary anchoring hubs. This interpretation reconciles previous observations23 of Glu163 mobility with its persistent presence in inhibitor-bound complexes and provides a mechanistic explanation for its evolutionary conservation despite limited energetic contribution.

Class III hotspots, characterized by favorable energetic contributions but lower persistence, represent auxiliary contributors that fine-tune binding through transient interactions or hydrophobic packing. These residues, which purely geometric analyses would overlook, underscore the importance of combining contact-based and energetic approaches to obtain a complete picture of protein–peptide recognition.

The flexible 246–249 loop as a dynamic switch between catalysis and inhibition

One of the most striking findings of this study is the dynamic role of the 246–249 loop region. Previous studies have described this segment as intrinsically flexible and implicated it in catalytic function36–38. Our comparative analysis between apo and inhibitor-bound states reveals that this loop undergoes a functional transition from a flexible catalytic element to a rigid inhibitory scaffold. In the apo state, Tyr248 exhibits substantial conformational plasticity, consistent with its role in proton transfer during catalysis. Upon inhibitor binding, however, the loop becomes significantly stabilized, as evidenced by the pronounced reduction in RMSF values. This transition supports a model in which high-affinity inhibition requires the “locking” of a catalytically dynamic region into a structurally rigid conformation. Notably, residues within this loop, such as Ile247 in hCPA2 and Leu249 in hCPB2, emerged as complex-specific hotspots, indicating that the loop not only mediates catalytic flexibility but also encodes subfamily-specific recognition patterns. Thus, the 246–249 loop can be conceptualized as a dynamic regulatory switch that integrates catalytic function and inhibitory selectivity. This insight has important implications for inhibitor design, as targeting loop-specific residues may enable selective modulation of MCP activity without disrupting the conserved catalytic core.

Conserved versus complex-specific hotspots: molecular basis of selectivity

Beyond identifying universal anchoring residues, our analysis reveals a second layer of interaction determinants that govern subfamily- and species-specificity. Complex-specific hotspots, such as Glu270 in hCPB1 and the 154–157 cluster in hCPB2, function as selectivity filters that distinguish closely related MCPs.

Multiple sequence alignment of the five investigated A/B MCPs (“Comparative Sequence architecture of the A/B MCP subfamily” section, Fig. 2) reveals that Glu270 is strictly conserved across bCPA, CPBAe1, hCPA2, hCPB1, and hCPB2, consistent with its established role as a catalytic nucleophile within the active site of MCPs29. Despite this universal conservation, our time-resolved simulations suggest a striking functional divergence in its contribution to inhibitor binding. Specifically, Glu270 emerges as a persistent hotspot exclusively in the hCPB1–TCI complex, where it maintains an almost continuous interaction with Lys55 of the inhibitor, with an occupancy exceeding 99%. In contrast, in the remaining complexes, Glu270 does not participate in stable interfacial contacts. This observation highlights a critical distinction between evolutionary conservation and functional deployment. While Glu270 is universally conserved to sustain catalytic competence, its recruitment into the inhibitor-binding profile is context-dependent and determined by the structural and dynamic environment at the interface. Importantly, this dual role positions Glu270 as an actionable target for precision inhibitor design. Rather than relying on broad-spectrum peptide inhibitors that indiscriminately bind conserved catalytic residues, future therapeutic strategies could exploit Glu270’s unique engagement in hCPB1 to develop molecules with enhanced subtype selectivity. Similarly, the unique recruitment of the 154–157 region in hCPB2 highlights a structural feature absent in pancreatic MCPs, providing a molecular fingerprint for the design of CPB2-selective inhibitors.

In the comparative analysis of the hCPB1–TCI and hCPB2–TCI complexes, several shared interaction hotspots were identified. Notably, Thr119 and Thr121 in hCPB1 correspond to Ser and Lys in hCPB2, respectively. The persistence of interactions at these positions despite residue substitutions suggests that spatial positioning within the binding interface plays a more decisive role than the specific chemical identity of the side chains. Beyond Thr119 and Thr121, multiple additional residues exhibit a similar pattern of functional conservation despite sequence divergence between hCPB1 and hCPB2, as summarized in Table 3. In particular, Asn123, Met125, and Leu249 in hCPB2 correspond to Ser123, Phe125, and Pro249 in hCPB1, respectively, yet are consistently classified as hotspots in both complexes. This indicates that inhibitory function at these sites is maintained primarily through the preservation of three-dimensional geometry rather than strict residue conservation.

Collectively, these results suggest that MCP–peptide recognition is governed by a geometry-driven interaction paradigm, in which structural positioning and topological constraints outweigh specific side-chain chemistry.

These findings collectively suggest that MCP inhibition is governed by a hierarchical architecture: a conserved Arg-centered anchoring framework ensures universal binding stability, while peripheral residues and flexible loop regions encode specificity. This hierarchical model reconciles the long-standing paradox of how MCPs maintain a conserved catalytic mechanism while exhibiting diverse substrate and inhibitor preferences.

Limitations and future directions

Although the 200 ns simulations provide substantial sampling of the conformational landscape, longer or replicate simulations may further refine the interaction hierarchy. Additionally, our findings need to be validated experimentally. Mutagenesis or binding assays would strengthen the causal links between identified potential hotspots and inhibitory potency. Nevertheless, the consistent emergence of Grade A and B hotspots across five diverse complexes suggests that the reported interaction architecture reflects intrinsic properties of A/B MCPs rather than simulation artifacts.

Conclusion

The mechanistic insights from this study suggest a strategic framework for developing MCP inhibitors. Our results support a modular design strategy in which inhibitors are engineered to engage conserved Class I hotspots and complex-specific selectivity nodes simultaneously. For instance, a rigid scaffold targeting the Arg71/Arg127 pair could be functionalized with side chains designed to interact with Glu270 in hCPB1 or Ser158 in hCPA2. Such a design would preserve high-affinity binding while conferring subfamily specificity. Nevertheless, experimental validation remains necessary to correlate these identified hotspots with inhibitory potency. More broadly, the integration of dynamic interaction mapping with energetic validation provides a transferable framework for studying other protein–peptide systems. By distinguishing universal anchoring determinants from context-dependent specificity factors, this approach bridges structural biology and rational drug design.
