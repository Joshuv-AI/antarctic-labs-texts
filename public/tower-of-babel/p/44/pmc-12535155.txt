Predictors of bleeding requiring transfusion following partial nephrectomy: an analysis of the ACS-NSQIP database
Authors: Marwan Zein, Nancy Zaitouny, Nisrin Ghalayini, Sara Jaarour, Elia Abou Chawareb, Albert El Hajj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Partial nephrectomy (PN) has been established as the standard treatment for T1 kidney tumors, and postoperative hemorrhage has been reported as a potentially life-threatening complication. Purpose To estimate the risk of bleeding requiring blood transfusion using patient characteristics in order to risk-stratify patients preoperatively and identify patients at high risk of bleeding following PN. We also aim to quantify the effect of this bleeding complication on postoperative morbidity and mortality. Materials and methods The demographics, associated co-morbidities, and some pre-operative variables, operation characteristics and baseline laboratory values were examined in 23,257 patients undergoing PN in the American College of Surgeons - National Surgical Quality of Improvement Program (ACS-NSQIP) dataset from 2005 to 2017. We divided our cohort into two groups based on the requirement of a blood transfusion post-operation. Multivariable logistic regression was done to evaluate pre-operative variables associated with post-operative transfusion. The secondary outcome was analyzed, using Chi-square and student t-test, to compare post-operative morbidities and mortality between groups. Results Of the 23,257 patients who underwent partial nephrectomy (PN), 1,287 (5.5%) experienced postoperative hemorrhage requiring transfusion. Univariate analysis revealed significant differences between the hemorrhage and non-hemorrhage groups with respect to age, race, body mass index, comorbidities, ASA class, surgical approach, year of operation, and baseline laboratory values. However, on multivariate analysis, only older age, open surgical approach, presence of a bleeding disorder, having received preoperative transfusions, higher preoperative blood urea nitrogen (BUN), lower serum albumin, lower hematocrit, and prolonged partial thromboplastin time (PTT) were independently associated with an increased risk of postoperative hemorrhage. Furthermore, patients who experienced hemorrhage had significantly higher rates of other postoperative complications compared to those who did not. Conclusions Accurate estimation of bleeding risk is critical for decision making and informed consent prior to PN, especially given the demonstrated impact on morbidity and mortality of this complication. A risk calculator is a helpful tool to help minimize the occurrence of this complication.

Introduction

Kidney cancer is one of the most common urologic diseases globally with more than 400,000 cases being diagnosed each year. Partial nephrectomy (PN) has become the treatment of choice for localized lesions whenever technically feasible based on tumor size and location [1]. It has the great advantage of being a nephron sparing surgery, providing effective oncologic therapy while preserving renal function. As minimally invasive surgery technology is constantly advancing, laparoscopic, including robotic-assisted, PN is being applied to larger and more complex tumors [2]. Nevertheless, PN requires tumor excision from the highly vascular renal parenchyma and is associated with a risk of bleeding postoperatively. This complication remains at a relatively low incidence (4.2–8.1%) but is highly feared as it significantly impacts morbidity and mortality [3]. Our aim is to primarily develop a model that estimates the risk of bleeding requiring blood transfusion using patient characteristics to risk stratify patients preoperatively and identify the patients at high risk. Secondarily, we aim to determine the impact of bleeding on morbidity and mortality following partial nephrectomy.

Methods

Data management

The American College of Surgeons - National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS-NSQIP) database is a nationally validated, risk-adjusted, outcomes-based program. It encompasses 719 centers both inside and outside the United States.

We retrospectively queried the ACS-NSQIP dataset for patients who underwent partial nephrectomy (PN) between the years 2005–2017. Patients who underwent PN with the CPT codes 50,240 and 50,543 were selected. CPT 50,240 stands for Nephrectomy, partial. CPT 50,543 stands for Laparoscopic Nephrectomy, partial. Robotic-assisted cases were included within the Laparoscopic group. We excluded patients below 18, above 90 years and all patients that underwent concomitant surgery.

Outcome

We divided our cohort into two groups based on the requirement of a blood transfusion post-operation. The first group consists of patients that received transfusion after PN, while the second group are the patient who did not. We opted to check for outliers following 3 different methods: sorting, descriptive, and visualization. Extreme outliers (n = 9) were removed for a better distributed and representative cohort.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 statistical package. Numerical variables were summarized by their mean and standard deviation. The student t-test was used to compare between the two groups. Categorical variables were described by counts and frequencies. Cross tabulations in the form of 2 × 2 tables were plotted to compare and detect differences between the two groups in outcome. The chi-square test was used to detect any significant differences between the two groups, and the Fisher exact test was used where more than 20% of the cells in the cross tabulations had expected frequencies < 5. A value of p < 0.05 was considered significant in all analyses. Standardized differences were calculated for comparing means and prevalence between the two groups since the large sample size resulted in significant p-values in almost all the variables. Standardized differences were calculated and reported as described by P. Austin (2009) [4].

We developed a multivariable logistic regression to evaluate pre-operative variables associated with post-operative hemorrhage in patients who underwent PN. We included the variables with a univariate significance of p-value < 0.2. Multivariable logistic regression using backward stepwise and forward stepwise was conducted, to assess the risk factors of post-operative transfusion following PN. Only variables with a statistically significant Unadjusted Odds Ratio were displayed, for better visualization of the variables that retained a statistically significant Adjusted Odds Ratio.

We used the backward stepwise selection model to determine the multivariable predictors of hemorrhage. We confirmed these findings using forward stepwise selection.

Validation was conducted by showing ROC AUC values and analysis of Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness of fit tests (Calibration curve). The results of these analyses look at the overall discriminative power of the conditional logistic regression model, as well as the model’s approximation of the data. The secondary outcome was analyzed, using Chi-square and student t-test, to compare post-operative morbidities and mortality between groups.

A risk calculator for post-operative hemorrhage was constructed using the Backward stepwise method, that included all the subjects in our study.

Our aim is to help develop an accurate risk estimation tool, to compare different pre-operative variables including the operative approach, to allow surgeons to empirically adjust the estimates of risk before and during the operation, help in decision making and informed consent.

We confirm that our calculator is implemented correctly. Validation was done by taking randomly 10 subjects in the dataset and checking the predicted probability for each of them and the probability in the risk calculator.

Area under the curve analysis

To assess the overall effectiveness of these logistic regression models, we used the AUC for ROC curves. The assessed performances of the models ranged from good discrimination (0:70 ≤ AUC < 0:80) which are not significantly different to excellent discrimination (0:80 ≤ AUC < 0:90). The results suggest that, in addition to the possibility that hemorrhage is caused by different factors, considering data associated with patients known with bleeding disorders separately doesn’t provide improvement of predictive power to the model’s overall performance.

Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness of fit

The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness of fit test provides the second method to assess the overall suitability of the logistic regression model. Test results indicate that our model appear to provide excellent fit of R2 > 0.9.

Discussion

In our cohort, we found a postoperative hemorrhage following PN rate of 5.5% of our patients. The reported rates of bleeding after PN in the literature vary widely, ranging from 0 to 12% [5]. Moreover, the definition of hemorrhage also differs from study to another. Some authors did not distinguish between peri-operative and post-operative bleeding [6], and defined it as blood loss that requires at least one blood unit transfusion [7].This same definition was used in our study. Other definitions were broader, including any acute drop in hemoglobin [8]. Despite the varying definitions, our observed rate is within the range documented in the literature.

This study showed that the main predictor of hemorrhage after PN was the surgical approach. Patients who underwent laparoscopic PN had a lower likelihood (OR = 0.279; p < 0.001) of developing post-operative hemorrhage when compared to those who had an open surgery. This finding contradicts previous large multicentric retrospective studies that have suggested that laparoscopic PN, even in expert centers, was associated with more complications, particularly hemorrhagic complications [2, 9]. However, it is worth noting that our dataset is also based on a multicenter large dataset encompassing a huge number of centers of different volumes and advances, and spanning more recent years than the earlier published studies. This may reflect changes in practice and especially in laparoscopic procedures, with the introduction of robotic assistance and with surgeons gaining more experience with minimally invasive techniques. Additionally, the absence of data on tumor-specific characteristics, such as size and anatomical complexity scores, is a significant limitation of our study, as these factors are likely key predictors of intraoperative bleeding. This absence may partly explain the strikingly lower odds of hemorrhage observed with the laparoscopic approach. Given the retrospective design, it is plausible that patients with more favorable tumor anatomy were more frequently selected for laparoscopy, while more complex tumors were managed with an open approach, introducing a potential selection bias that could have influenced the results.

As expected, patients with bleeding disorders were more prone to hemorrhage, exhibiting 2.8 times the odds of bleeding when compared to patients without bleeding disorders. Marietta et al. found that acquired or congenital coagulopathies favors or directly cause surgical bleeding [10]. Interestingly, subjects requiring a pre-operative transfusion of more than four units had 5.7 times the odds of developing hemorrhage post-operatively. The significant association between preoperative transfusion of ≥ 4 units of pRBCs within 72 h before surgery and the need for postoperative transfusion likely reflects underlying patient instability, ongoing blood loss, or pre-existing anemia. This variable may serve as a marker for patients with more severe clinical conditions or complex surgical presentations, placing them at higher risk for continued bleeding and subsequent transfusion after surgery. Moreover, age was also shown to increase the odds of hemorrhage. Rosevear et al. associated increased age with an increased risk of postoperative hemorrhage after upper retroperitoneal laparoscopic surgery [11].

Albumin and hematocrit were shown to have protective effects against hemorrhage. After adjusting for all other variables, for every one unit increase in albumin level, the odds of hemorrhage decreased by a factor of 0.7 and for every one unit increase in hematocrit, the odds of hemorrhage decreased by a factor of 0.8.

The protective role of albumin is already discussed in the literature [12], highlighting the importance of measuring albumin before surgery, as it is not routinely assessed. Furthermore, hypoalbuminemia and anemia showed increased risk of perioperative transfusion in several urological surgeries [13, 14].

Interestingly, we did not find any impact of diabetes, INR, or platelet count on post-operative hemorrhage.

Finally, our study provided evidence of the impact of bleeding complications on post-operative morbidity and mortality, emphasizing the importance of taking all possible measures to prevent this complication. In addition, our study provided clinicians with a simple assessment tool that could properly identify patients at increased bleeding risk; hence, providing the patients with better preoperative counseling and optimization before PN.

Strengths

This study was based on NSQIP dataset, with inclusion of many covariates, increasing accuracy and generalizability of the study [15, 16].

We believe the findings are of interest since it is a contemporary study with a good balance between open and laparoscopic that reflect the contemporary management of many tertiary centers.

Limitations

The NSQIP dataset doesn’t include some confounding covariates that are known to increase risk of bleeding, such as tumor characteristics (size, complexity, etc.). The lack of these tumor-specific variables in the dataset, to provide risk-adjusted and estimation of bleeding after PN has been addressed by Jung et al. [3]. In addition to the choice of open versus minimally invasive access, the method of renal vascular control that is on-clamp versus off-clamp, can have an impact on intraoperative blood loss and the subsequent need for transfusion. Unfortunately, NSQIP does not capture data on vascular clamping strategy, so we were unable to adjust for this potentially significant confounder. Furthermore, it was not possible to assess many other factors including: the severity of bleeding, the time from operation to bleeding, use of anticoagulation, type of clamping, use of hemostatic agents and ischemia time.

Conclusion

We observed that there are multiple preoperative risk factors in addition to the surgical approach that can affect the probability of having hemorrhage requiring blood transfusion following PN. Having this complication is associated with a wide range of morbidity and mortality.

By estimating bleeding risk preoperatively, we aim to help prevent the occurrence of this complication, by taking pre-operative, intra-operative and post-operative measures, and therefore minimize morbidity and mortality following PN.
