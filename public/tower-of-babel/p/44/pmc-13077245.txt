The HE scale: a consensus-based, time-aware rapid recovery scale for lung transplantation
Authors: Jiawei Chen, Jiang Shi, Chao Yang, Guilin Peng, Chunrong Ju, Hanyu Yang, Lan Lan, Hui Liu, Ying Chen, Danxia Huang, Run Li, Xin Xu, Jianxing He
Published: 2026

Introduction

Lung transplantation remains the only therapeutic option for adults with end-stage respiratory failure. Its purpose is to signiﬁcantly improve the survival rate, relieve disability, and improve health-related quality of life (HRQL). Due to its technical and biological complexity, lung transplantation is associated with high perioperative morbidity, prolonged length of stay, and an extensive recovery period (1,2). Additionally, the majority of lung transplant candidates are frail and malnourished (3,4), with diminished physiologic reserve and greater vulnerability to adverse outcomes, which complicates perioperative management and recovery.

Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols constitute an evidence-based, multidisciplinary, and multimodal perioperative care pathway whose function is to improve patient satisfaction, accelerate postoperative recovery, and facilitate healing (5). ERAS pathways encompass preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative care—all the phases of perioperative care. In recent years, ERAS protocols have been widely adopted across a variety of surgical specialties—most notably those related to the colorectum, hepatobiliary system, and the breast (6-9). However, despite its broad application, there are currently no validated ERAS guidelines specifically tailored for lung transplantation, and the application of ERAS in lung transplantation remains in its nascent stage (9). This may be partly due to the fact that the implementation of ERAS management in the postoperative period is relatively complex and unclear. Given the high incidence of postoperative morbidity and mortality after lung transplantation, an objective, time-aware recovery metric for guiding clinical decision-making and prompting early intervention among these patients remains a critical need.

To address these limitations, we aimed to develop a rapid, brief, and consensus-based scale of ERAS specifically tailored for lung transplantation. Rather than emphasizing traditional metrics (e.g., length of stay and complication rates), we developed a simple scale, HE scale, that prioritizes the quality of recovery, which we designated as the primary endpoint.

Description of the HE scale

Research group

A multidisciplinary expert panel was assembled that comprised 28 clinicians from 20 high-volume lung transplantation centers, including thoracic surgeons (n=8), anesthesiologists (n=4), intensivists (n=4), rehabilitation specialists (n=3), nurses (n=3), dietitians (n=3), and neuropsychologists (n=3). Experts were eligible if they had at least 5 years of post-training experience in lung transplantation care and were actively involved in perioperative management. After selection, collaboration between experts was facilitated via multiple face-to-face meetings each month, teleconferences, and email communication. Institutional approvals were obtained as per the local policy for expert consensus activities.

Scale scoring

The group recognized that the adoption of a given scale depends largely on its perceived value, usability, and brevity. A measurement scale was thus developed capable of evaluating early postoperative recovery and being administered by minimally trained staff. It is suitable for repeated use and is designed to be brief yet sufficiently detailed to capture recovery across multiple domains.

Validated instruments were reviewed to identify the candidate domains potentially relevant to the posttransplant recovery and HRQL of lung transplant recipients. However, as these instruments were not developed for lung transplantation, five recovery domains—tailored specifically to lung transplant recipients—were defined based on the panel experts’ clinical experience and comprehensive literature review: language, feeding, mobility, cognition, and respiration. Selection criteria were: (I) clinical salience in the first days after transplantation; (II) direct linkage to modifiable ERAS actions; (III) observability within minutes without specialized equipment; and (IV) feasibility across centers. More subjective dimensions, such as nociceptive and emotional distress, were deliberately excluded, as these are more time-consuming to evaluate and may be difficult to standardize in patients during the first 2 postoperative weeks.

Each domain is scored from 1 (worst) to 4 (best), with higher scores indicating better function. To ensure that items were clear, relevant, and nonredundant and accurately reflected their intended constructs, they were evaluated by experts from the same multidisciplinary panel. To optimize validity, experts were also asked to identify any redundancy between items. This process yielded a concise, content-valid, and clinically practical instrument for assessing recovery in lung transplant recipients and capturing the domains closely related to HRQL (Table 1).

Assessments at multiple time points allow recovery to be evaluated over time and at periods of critical clinical decision-making. All domains are intended to be assessed between 1 hour and 14 days postoperatively. Due to the unique circumstances of transplant recipients, baseline assessments were not conducted preoperatively. Rather, they are performed in the period immediately following the operation (1–6 hours) primarily for the purpose of evaluating recovery from anesthesia. Early postoperative assessments are conducted on postoperative day (POD) 1 and 3 to assess physiologic recovery with implications for patient safety and triage. Later assessments on POD 7 and 14 focus on the transition from physiologic and home-readiness recovery to cognitive recovery and return to the previous or expected level of functioning at home or the workplace.

Consensus threshold

Recovery after lung transplantation is time-dependent, and so an observed score must be interpreted relative to what is expected at that time. Using a modified Delphi process, a multidisciplinary panel of experts first defined anchors 1–4 for five ERAS domains (Figure S1). For each time point, panelists independently rated the ideal level [1–4] for each domain (Figure S2). The time-specific consensus threshold (L*) was defined as the ideal level endorsed by ~70% of panelists. Items or thresholds that did not reach the 70% cutoff were discussed in subsequent meetings and re-rated; if consensus could not be achieved after revision, they were removed. To preserve clinical plausibility, thresholds were required not to decline over time within each domain. Bedside scoring and decision-making

At each assessment time point, the patient’s status in each domain is compared against the corresponding time-specific ideal level (L*). If the observed level meets or exceeds L*, the domain is coded as “recovered” (score =1); otherwise, it is coded as “not yet recovered” (score =0) and prompts targeted ERAS actions (e.g., respiratory therapy, analgesia optimization, early mobilization, swallowing-nutrition support, and cognitive/communication support). The sum of the recovered domains yields a simple scale count ranging from 0 to 5 at each time point, which can help identify how many key aspects of recovery have reached their expected level and trigger domain-specific clinical decisions.

Discussion

ERAS is a structured, multimodal, evidence-based perioperative care pathway that accelerates recovery through optimized perioperative management (5). ERAS was first established in colorectal surgery to improve recovery after colonic resection and has since been adopted across multiple specialties (10). In solid-organ transplantation, however, enhanced recovery remains relatively underdeveloped, even with good postoperative recovery being increasingly recognized as an important outcome (9). Postoperatively, defining recovery is a necessary step in the development of a recovery scale. Research on perioperative management after lung transplantation remains limited, tools for the rapid postoperative assessment of recovery are lacking, and patient prognoses vary widely (11,12). Therefore, a rapid, clinically actionable scoring scale to assess postoperative recovery and guide postoperative management remains an unmet clinical need.

Timely intervention after lung transplantation is associated with fewer complications, enhanced recovery, and improved HRQL. Post-transplant recovery is a complex, multidimensional process encompassing both the physiology and neurocognition of patients (11-15). Even though some instruments demonstrate aspects of construct validity in lung transplant recipients, they do not adequately reflect early postoperative recovery (16-19). Accordingly, the HE scale that characterizes early post-transplant recovery and closely reflects quality of life was developed. The HE scale focuses on observable functional domains—language, feeding, mobility, cognition, and respiration—that can be rapidly assessed at the bedside with minimal patient burden. Fundamental to the design of the measurement scale is its flexibility. The scale provides an assessment of overall functional recovery status. Additionally, it can be used to timely identify which aspect of a domain is problematic or even assess severity indicators within each domain, allowing clinicians to precisely direct rehabilitation.

During scale development, items that experts judged to be redundant within a domain were removed to reduce overlap, maximize content coverage, and maintain brevity. To support its content validity, the HE scale was developed iteratively through multidisciplinary expert consensus and refined with particular attention to avoiding overlapping items and minimizing the risk of floor or ceiling effects. Six postoperative time periods were designated to capture recovery over time and optimize discrimination between phases of recovery. The HE scale and its components were designed to track recovery across multiple domains over time and to assess progressive improvement.

The majority of existing methods require a considerable time investment by clinicians and patients and thus cannot be applied in routine clinical practice without difficulty. In contrast, our novel HE scale, combining brevity with practical utility, can be administered in minutes by minimally trained staff, supports repeated measurements, and is readily deployable across a diversity of clinical environments. Additionally, due to its simplicity and reliance on observable behaviors, the scale can be applied across different ages and language backgrounds, thereby supporting broad accessibility and efficient use in clinical practice. Most importantly, the HE Scale can aid in quantifying the magnitude and durability of improvements in HRQL at serial postoperative time points after lung transplantation, identifying domains with relative deficits, and informing clinical decision-making.

Limitations

Certain limitations to the scale and its development should be noted. To begin, more subjective dimensions were excluded to enhance scale feasibility, but this could also reduce its comprehensiveness, and thus future work should integrate this scale with established HRQL instruments and more exhaustive functional assessments. Moreover, the newly developed HE scale was based on expert consensus and has not been subjected to prospective validation in real-world cohorts. Although ratings were anonymous and discussions were moderated with de-identified feedback, consensus methods remain vulnerable to the halo effect and groupthink; residual bias cannot be excluded. Prospective, multicenter cohort studies are therefore needed to assess its reliability, test-retest stability, internal consistency, and structural validity.

Conclusions

A rapid, multidomain, early recovery scale, HE scale, was developed within an ERAS framework through a structured multidisciplinary consensus process. By consisting of defined, time-specific ideal levels of recovery across five key domains, the HE Scale offers a practical means to standardize bedside assessment of early postoperative recovery and identify domains that may benefit from targeted interventions. Rigorous prospective validation is required to determine its reliability, validity, responsiveness, and prognostic value.
