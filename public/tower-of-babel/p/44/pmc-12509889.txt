Effects of auditory enrichment on Pekin duck production and welfare
Authors: J.M. Schober, G. Ayres, G. Chambers, E. Stuart, B. Peterson, J. Curry, G.S. Fraley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Few studies have looked at the effects of auditory enrichment on the production and welfare of poultry species, while none have looked at Pekin ducks specifically. We utilized 400 grow-out Pekin ducks obtained on day-of-hatch from a commercial hatchery and evenly and randomly placed into 4 pens in 3 rooms. Each room was randomly assigned one of three audio treatments: control (CON; no auditory enrichment), classical music (MOZ; Mozart’s String Quintets) or pond sounds (POND; Pond Sounds – Relaxator on Apple Music®). Ducks were housed to closely approximate industry standards for density with30 ducks/pen. The POND and MOZ audio started on week 1 (day 7) and were played starting at 0300h (lights on) until 2100h (lights off) one hour on, one hour off, in a cyclic manner with a range of 65-75dB. Body condition scores were taken on 10 birds/pen/week (final N=80 ducks/treatment/week) using a published rubric. Production data were collected weekly. On weeks 2, 4, and 6, two ducks/pen were euthanized using pentobarbital and organ and body weights were recorded (final N=16 ducks/treatment/week). Body condition scores were analyzed using the PROC LOGISTIC procedure (SAS v9.4) and odds ratios were calculated. All other data were analyzed by 2-way ANOVA with repeated measures using PROC MIXED (SAS v9.4) and Tukey’s test for post-hoc analyses. A p≤0.05 was considered significant. The study was repeated, resulting in experiment 1 and experiment 2. For experiment 1, no significant differences were observed among groups for weekly body weights, dissection measures, or FCR. For experiment 2, ducks in the MOZ group weighed less than ducks in the POND (p=0.0010) and CON groups (p=0.0109). MOZ ducks had worse feather cleanliness scores, worse foot pad scores, and worse feather quality scores than POND and CON ducks. These present differing results, so future research is needed to fully understand how different auditory enrichment affects the production and welfare of Pekin ducks under more specific flock conditions.

Introduction

Pekin ducks are a crucial part of the poultry industry in both the U.S. and globally for meat and feather production. In 2021, the global duck meat production reached 6.2 million tons, indicating a growing demand for duck meat worldwide (FAOSTAT, 2021). With the decreasing time to market weight of Pekin ducks, they are being pushed to their metabolic limits, so it is critical to ensure that they experience positive welfare. Animal welfare has become increasingly important over the years, with consumers and the general public caring about how food animals are treated (Alonso et al., 2020). Environmental enrichment, a modification of an animal’s environment to improve biological functioning, can be used to improve welfare (Newberry, 1995; van de Weerd and Day, 2009).

There are different types of enrichment used within the poultry industry, such as pecking blocks to reduce abnormal behaviors, perches that encourage roosting, platforms to encourage movement, and dust baths to provide the birds with the opportunity to engage in natural behaviors (Zimmerman et al., 2011; Rodenburg et al., 2013; Kaukonen et al., 2017; Campbell et al., 2016; Widowski and Duncan, 2000). Pekin ducks are waterfowl; their wild-type counterparts’ enrichment is typically water-based; thus several animal welfare certifications require open water access in Pekin duck barns (e.g. American Humane Society, Global Animal Partnership, and A Greener World). A major reason to provide poultry with environmental enrichment is to reduce unwanted behaviors, e.g. feather pecking and picking. Feather pecking is the act of pecking, pulling out, and sometimes eating the feathers of conspecifics, and feather picking is an act of self-mutilation (Savory, 1995; Colton and Fraley, 2014; Schober et al., 2023). These behaviors lead to significant feather loss, injury, and even mortality.

Research on open water sources in Pekin duck production has on one hand, shown it to be beneficial, as it allows ducks to perform positive natural behaviors like wet preening and reduces negative behaviors like feather pecking (O’Driscoll and Broom, 2012). On the other hand, they can also cause problems like increased litter moisture, poor body condition, and even increased mortality (Lowman et al., 2016; Schenk et al., 2016). Semi-open water sources have also been shown to increase natural behaviors such as wet preening, but they also decrease body condition scores (Schober et al., 2023). With the differing results regarding open-water sources in Pekin duck production, it is important to also look at other types of environmental enrichment to improve Pekin duck welfare. Other types of enrichment, like auditory enrichment, can be less costly and require less maintenance than physical environmental enrichment.

Auditory enrichment has been shown to be a method of therapy for humans. Classical music has been shown to reduce pain, depression, and anxiety (McCaffrey and Good, 2000; Hilliard 2001; Vickers and Cassileth, 2001). The benefits that have been shown in humans exposed to auditory stimuli have prompted research into exploring the effects of auditory enrichment within captive animals. Studies performed on various species of captive animals in zoo settings have shown that auditory enrichment can have a positive impact (e.g. elephants: Wells and Irwin, 2008; gorillas: Robbins and Margulis, 2014; and psittacines: Williams et al., 2017). In poultry, classical music has been shown to reduce tonic immobility, heterophil to lymphocyte ratio and reduce fluctuating asymmetry in laying hens (Dávila et al., 2011). Classical music has also shown to increase comfort and preening behaviors and to reduce aggressive pecking in pullets (Zhao et al., 2023). When classical music is at 65-75 dB it reduces serum corticosterone concentration in chicks (Zhao et al., 2023b). However, no studies on auditory enrichment have been performed on Pekin ducks.

There is little research on environmental enrichment as related to commercial Pekin ducks, and no studies on auditory enrichment in Pekin ducks. Thus, the purpose of this study was to investigate the effects of auditory enrichment on Pekin duck welfare and production. Specifically, we compared two types of auditory enrichment, pond sounds and Mozart music, to a control (no auditory enrichment). We hypothesized that ducks exposed to auditory enrichment would have no significant differences in production or welfare when compared to ducks not housed with auditory enrichment. We show that Mozart music can lead to an increase in feather pecking, though more research is needed to fully understand the mechanisms behind this effect.

Materials and methods

All procedures were approved by Purdue's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (PACUC #0724002517). A total of 400 grow-out Pekin ducks were obtained from Culver Duck, Inc. (Middlebury, IN) on the day-of-hatch and brought to the Purdue University Animal Sciences Research and Education Center (ASREC) farm. They were housed in 5 m2 floor pens with 0.17m2 per duck, approximately 30 ducks per pen. They were allowed ad libitum access to water and fed industry standard starter feed for the first 10 d of life and grower feed for the remainder of life per industry recommendations (Chen et al., 2021). Ducks were placed in single rooms with an 18:6 light cycle with lights turning on at 0300 and turning off at 2100 and a temperature of 20°C to 22°C. Brooder temperatures during the first 10 d of life were maintained as recommended (Chen et al., 2021). Water lines (5 ducks per nipple, ∼50 mL/min flow rate) were placed over a pit covered with raised plastic flooring and the remaining area of the pens was covered with pine shavings which were added to, or replaced as necessary, simultaneously for all pens. At the end of the experiment, ducks were either necropsied for organ morphometric analyses or brought to the Purdue Butcher Block on d 43 for processing.

Auditory enrichment

Each room with 4 pens per room was randomly assigned one of three audio treatments: control (CON; no auditory enrichment), classical music (MOZ; Guarneri Quartet. Mozart: String Quartets & Quintets (2012) on Apple Music®) or pound sounds (POND; Relaxator. Pond Sounds (2018) on Apple Music®). Auditory enrichment began on week 1 (7 days of age), allowing the ducks to habituate to their pens before the study began. The ducks had no prior experience with music. The audio was played starting at 0300h (when lights turned on) until 2100h (when lights turned off) one hour on, one hour off, in a cyclic manner. The rooms were between 65-75 dB including the auditory enrichment and the fans. The study was replicated identically except that the auditory enrichment was randomly reassigned to different rooms. The study was repeated so rooms were assigned a new treatment (Experiment 1 and Experiment 2).

Morphometrics and body condition scores

Ducks were weighed and body condition scores were assessed on a weekly basis starting on week 2 on 10 ducks per pen using a rubric previously published (Karcher et al., 2013; Fraley et al., 2013; Schober et al., 2023). Foot pad quality, eye and nostril health, feather cleanliness and quality were all assessed. At the end of weeks 2, 4 and 6, two ducks per pen were euthanized using FatalPlus (pentobarbital, 390 mg/ml/kg) and their spleens, Bursa of Fabricius, livers, and uropygial glands were weighed (final N = 16/treatment). The ducks from 2 pens in each auditory enrichment room were taken while the auditory enrichment was playing, while the other ducks from the other 2 pens were taken when the auditory enrichment was not playing.

Statistical analyses

Morphometrics. Data were analyzed by SAS JMP (v.15, SAS Institute, Raleigh, NC). The pen was the statistical unit for all data. Body morphometrics were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with repeated measures. A p≤0.05 considered significant.

Body Condition Scores. PROC LOGISTIC (SAS 9.4) with the Firth bias correction for quasi-complete separation and odds ratios were calculated with p≤0.05 considered significant.

Discussion

The purpose of our study was to evaluate the effects of auditory enrichment on Pekin duck production and welfare. To accomplish this goal, grow-out Pekin ducks were housed with either Mozart music, pond sounds, or no auditory enrichment starting on 7 days of age. This study was repeated, resulting in 2 separate experiments. For experiment 1, we saw no significant differences among the treatments for eye scores, feather quality, foot pad scores, or nostril scores. We also saw no significant differences among treatments for weekly body weight, FCR, feed intake and no significant differences for any dissection measures. We did see a minor interaction between week and treatment for feather cleanliness scores. Overall, auditory enrichment in experiment 1 did not have an apparent effect on Pekin duck production or welfare.

However, for experiment 2, we saw that ducks in the Mozart group weighed significantly less than ducks in the pond and control groups. Ducks in the Mozart group also had worse feather quality and cleanliness scores and worse foot pad scores than the ducks in the pond and control groups. Overall, Mozart music affected ducks’ production and welfare. These differing results are not uncommon in zoo-based research on auditory enrichment. There have been studies that have shown auditory enrichment does not positively or negatively affect various species. Wallace et al. (2013) saw that 6 out of 8 Moloch gibbons showed no obvious changes in behavior when presented with classical music. A Western Lowland gorilla showed no significant differences across 4 different treatments: control, rainforest, classical music, and rock music (Robbins and Margulis, 2014). Other studies have shown that auditory enrichment can negatively affect some species. Though not significant, Robbins and Marguilis (2014) observed a gorilla’s regurgitation and reingestion behaviors, behaviors that are stereotypic for gorillas, increase when played either rock or classical music. In that same study, a different gorilla engaged in hair plucking behavior significantly more during the rock and classical music treatments compared to controls (Robbins and Marguilis, 2014). Those studies differ others that showed classical music to be beneficial to captive animals. Williams et al. (2017) saw that zoo-housed psittacines showed increased preening behaviors when played rainforest audio and talk radio, compared to the no sound control. They also produced more calming vocalizations when played pop music and the talking radio compared to the no sound control (Williams et al., 2017). Wells and Irwin (2008) saw that when Asian elephants were played classical music, they performed less stereotypic behaviors. These differing results in zoo-housed captive animals could be due to small sample sizes, individual variation, different social groups, and different housing conditions. Despite housing conditions in our two experiments being consistent, we observed very different results.

We repeated our experiment to control for any potential confounding room effects, so it is unlikely that the rooms were the reason for these differing results. We also placed the same number of ducks in each pen for both experiments, so the social groups did not differ, and we had the same sample size for both experiments. Ducks in the first experiment were placed in early fall, while the ducks in the second experiment were placed in late fall. Unlike ducklings from experiment 1, ducks used for experiment 2 were offspring of breeder ducks who were heat stressed during egg lay due to normal environmental fluctuations in Indiana. A previous study from our lab has shown that offspring of heat stressed breeder ducks have a heightened response to stressors (Oluwagbenga et al., 2023). Videla et al (2020) also saw that the offspring of quail that were exposed to heat stress had a higher inflammatory response. Offspring of wild house wrens fed corticosterone-injected mealworms showed a higher begging rate for food compared to controls (Bowers et al., 2016). It is possible that Mozart music caused the ducks in the second experiment to experience a heightened response due to pre-existing conditions. Mozart music is composed of harmonies, modulation, and chromaticism, which could all increase the ducks’ stress response when there is already a stressor present, or when they already have a more heightened stress response due to potential epigenetic concerns or due to normal variations among seasons. Alternatively, given the lack of effects of auditory enrichment from our first experiment, it is possible the feather pecking outbreak in Experiment 2 was simply of unknown etiology and the music had no impact; however, the Mozart group did show considerably worse feather pecking than any other group.

Our study is the first to assess the effects of auditory enrichment on Pekin ducks. We found differing results, so future research is needed to fully understand how different auditory enrichment affects behavior, production, and welfare of Pekin ducks. Future studies should repeat our current study during different seasons, to see if there is a relationship among the outside environment, genetics, and the bird’s physiology and behavior while housed with auditory enrichment. Studies should also look at different types of auditory enrichment, including different genres of music, talk radio, and conspecific vocalizations. Determining what auditory enrichment works best for Pekin ducks in a commercial setting could improve welfare and production while requiring less maintenance and potentially be more cost-effective for the producers.
