Barriers to accessing treatment in perinatal depression: a qualitative and quantitative study
Authors: Kübra Akbulut, Esra Yazıcı, Elif Köse, Yusuf Dökmen, Hilal Uslu Yuvacı, Ahmet Bulent Yazıcı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimThis study aims to identify culture and region-specific barriers to seeking treatment in women at risk for depression during the perinatal period, which may have been previously overlooked.Materials ve methodsThe study was conducted in two stages using both qualitative ve quantitative methods. Participants included women who were screened for depression risk using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) at the gynecology ve obstetrics clinic of Sakarya Training ve Research Hospital between January and December 2022. Women identified as at risk for depression and referred to the perinatal psychiatry outpatient clinic but who did not seek treatment were included. In the qualitative stage, 20 participants completed a sociodemographic data form ve the EPDS, followed by a qualitative interview. The qualitative component was conducted and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ). In the quantitative stage, 66 participants were administered a sociodemographic data form, the EPDS and a quantitative questionnaire developed based on the qualitative findings.ResultsThe qualitative analysis revealed five themes: individual factors, environmental factors, sociocultural factors, stigma-related factors, and clinical factors. In the quantitative analysis, the top three barriers identified were: “the societal expectation that household chores such as cleaning, ironing, cooking, ve childcare are women’s responsibilities”, “the burden of these household tasks,” and “the prioritization of daily life responsibilities over mental health concerns.” A positive correlation was found between the EPDS scores and the following questionnaire items: “lack of a safe place to leave the child”, “mental health issues perceived as unimportant by close contacts”, “the burden of household tasks”, “the prioritization of daily life responsibilities over mental health concerns”, “the societal expectation of women’s responsibility for household chores” and “lack of support or indifference from a partner during pregnancy and the postpartum period.“.ConclusionIn the qualitative phase, five themes emerged: individual factors, environmental factors, sociocultural factors, stigma-related factors and health provider-related factors. In the quantitative phase, the three highest-scoring questions were primarily related to sociocultural factors.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-07898-1.

Introduction

Perinatal mental disorders are defined as psychiatric disorders occurring during pregnancy and within the first year postpartum [36]. Perinatal depression, the most common perinatal mental disorder in the world and in Turkey, is defined as major and minor depressive episodes occurring during pregnancy or within the first 12 months postpartum [19]. The prenatal period and the first year postpartum are critical for diagnosis and represent an important period for depression screening and initiating treatment [11, 49].

The prevalence of perinatal depression is estimated to be 20% (ranging from 5% to 55%), with an expected prevalence of 25.5% in low-to-middle income countries [40]. Perinatal depression, which is highly prevalent, also leads to many negative outcomes for the mother, child, and family [10, 31, 43]. In women during this period, it can lead to significant issues such as a decrease in self-care, increased alcohol and tobacco use, and a decline in functionality [35]. In addition, it has been found that women during this period have a higher risk of suicide. Estimates suggest that suicide may be responsible for 20% of maternal deaths [11]. The perinatal period can also have negative effects on the mother-infant bond due to depression [11].

It is estimated that at least 56% of individuals with depression, regardless of whether it occurs during the perinatal period or not, are untreated worldwide [30]. In perinatal depression, despite close monitoring during pregnancy, these rates are reported to be higher, and it is also reported that even in high-income countries, only 25% of women with postpartum depression seek professional help [11, 29]. In a series of studies conducted in Turkey, when screening for perinatal depression, pregnant women identified as depressed were informed to seek medical attention; however, it was found that only 28.7% of these women sought psychiatric treatment [47].

Several reasons can be proposed to explain the existing inadequacies in the identification and treatment of mental disorders during the perinatal period. These reasons may be related to both the system and treatment team, as well as the patient. Factors related to the system and treatment team include lack of sufficient awareness about the issue, time constraints during and between consultations/examinations, absence of established follow-up policies, or unclear referral networks [6, 12, 18, 21, 22, 42]. Factors related to the patient also play a significant role in hindering treatment-seeking behavior among women during the perinatal period. These include certain maternal and pregnancy characteristics, such as low education level, being a young mother, being born abroad, and being in the first trimester of pregnancy, which are associated with lower treatment-seeking rates [4, 26].

Additionally, cultural and environmental factors, such as difficulties in child care during face-to-face psychological interventions, not knowing where to seek treatment, time limitations, transportation barriers, social stigma associated with mental illness, and fear of being separated from their babies due to their care, may also play a role [13, 17, 27, 32, 34].

Preliminary Information: In 2022, independently of the present study, a routine screening activity was conducted at Sakarya Training and Research Hospital through coordination between the hospital’s quality unit, the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, and the Department of Psychiatry. Women who visited the obstetrics and gynecology outpatient clinic during pregnancy and the postpartum period and voluntarily agreed to participate were assessed using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). High-risk individuals were informed and referred to the hospital’s perinatal psychiatry clinic with an appointment. Out of 4025 women who were screened postpartum, 356 (8.8%) were identified as high-risk. Among 1541 women screened during pregnancy, 314 (20.3%) were identified as high-risk. As a result, of the women in the high-risk group who were reached, a total of 70 (10.44%) sought outpatient consultation (6 were pregnant (1.9%), and 64 were postpartum (17.9%)). This demonstrates that, despite overcoming barriers such as unawareness of the patient’s medical condition, difficulty making appointments, and inaccessibility to healthcare providers [46, 47], the treatment-seeking rate remains low.

Based on these findings, it has been concluded that more detailed research on the topic is necessary. While many hospitals and departments may conduct perinatal depression screenings, the perinatal psychiatry clinic in our hospital is the only one in the country that specializes in this field, offering screening, information, referral, and appointment opportunities. Despite this, the low number of consultations is noteworthy.

Based on these preliminary data, our study aims to identify the barriers to treatment access for women with depression during pregnancy and the postpartum period and uncover any overlooked culture-specific reasons through a mixed-methods study using both qualitative and quantitative analyses.

Methods

This study utilized a mixed-methods design consisting of two components: a qualitative phenomenological study and an exploratory quantitative survey. This study was conducted at SEAH (Sakarya Education and Research Hospital) between July 5, 2023, and July 5, 2024. Our sample was determined based on data from a screening program initiated independently of our study in the gynecology and obstetrics clinic between January 1, 2022, and January 31, 2022. Patients identified as high-risk following the EPDS application in the screening program and referred to the perinatal psychiatry outpatient clinic but who did not seek treatment were included in the study.

During the EPDS screening program conducted by our hospital’s quality unit in 2022, patients were informed about the screening program, and they were told that the data could be used in future studies. In our study, which was planned independently of the screening program, ethical approval was obtained from patients before the data collection process began. This approval covers all study procedures, including participant selection, telephone interviews, and qualitative interviews conducted between 2023 and 2024.

Part one: qualitative stage

A list of women identified as high-risk for depression through screening with the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), who were referred to the perinatal outpatient clinic but did not seek treatment, was obtained. Participants were selected using purposive sampling, a non-random sampling method, based on the study’s objectives. Given that the status of being pregnant or postpartum may create different barriers to accessing healthcare services, the sample was stratified into two groups: pregnant women and postpartum women.

From the list of women in the high-risk group, 162 were contacted by phone, and appointments were scheduled for face-to-face or phone interviews with those who agreed to participate. Of the 28 women who accepted the invitation, 8 did not attend the scheduled interview. Despite attempts to contact them again for a new appointment, 6 women did not respond to the phone calls. One participant indicated that she would join at a later time, and one woman refused to attend due to her child being ill. As a result, a total of 20 women (12 in the pregnancy period and 8 in the postpartum period) participated in the interviews. 17 of the 20 women were interviewed face-to-face, and 3 were interviewed online.

Before the interviews, a qualitative interview form consisting of 7 questions was developed, taking into account the experiences of two psychiatrists, an obstetrician, and a public health expert in the research team, as well as examples from the literature and the research questions. Sub-questions were added to further clarify the topic and make it more comprehensible.

After obtaining written consent from the women, a sociodemographic clinical data form was given to be filled out. Then, semi-structured interviews were conducted with the participants using the prepared questions. The analysis process ran concurrently with the interviews. New participants continued to be interviewed until similar barriers were repeated, indicating data saturation.

The interviews were conducted by two psychiatrists (one female and one male) with clinical experience in perinatal mental health. The interviewers had prior experience in conducting qualitative interviews with perinatal populations. No prior relationship was established between the interviewers and the participants before the study. Participants were informed that the interviewers were psychiatrists involved in research on perinatal mental health. The interviews, lasted a total of 8 h and 48 min. The shortest interview lasted 15 min, and the longest one lasted 35 min. During the interviews, participants’ consent was obtained to record the sessions digitally. While one researcher conducted face-to-face interviews, the other researcher took notes as an observer. When an interview could not be completed during the initial meeting, it was rescheduled and continued at a later time (e.g., in the evening or after working hours) according to the participant’s availability. These follow-up sessions were conducted solely to complete the initial interview and did not constitute repeat interviews with new questions. The recordings were later transcribed verbatim by the interviewers. The written transcripts were then sent to the participants via text message or email, or in print if requested, for feedback on any possible errors, misunderstandings, or incorrect transcriptions. Participants were informed during the informed consent process that the interviewers were psychiatrists involved in research on perinatal mental health. The research team acknowledged that their clinical background could influence data interpretation and participant interaction. To enhance reflexivity, coding was performed independently by two researchers, interpretations were discussed in group meetings, discrepancies were resolved through consensus, and a third researcher reviewed coding consistency to minimise individual bias.

Meaningful units (e.g., words, sentences, paragraphs) within the data were identified separately by two researchers. These units, which formed meaningful wholes, were assigned codes or labels, making the data more manageable. After coding all the data, the researchers compared the code lists they had created. Similarities and differences were identified, and a codebook was created to establish a common language. Coding was performed manually without the use of qualitative data analysis software. The data obtained through the coding process were divided into sections, examined, and linked to each other. Multiple coding rounds were then conducted in working groups, during which researchers jointly reviewed codes and discussed interpretations. Coding consistency and coherence were subsequently assessed by a third researcher. During the coding process, n was used to indicate the number of participants who referred to a given code, while f represented the frequency with which that code was repeated across the data. Based on these, categories were created to encompass similar codes, which were then grouped into sub-themes and themes. Specific data examples supporting the themes or codes, in the form of direct quotes from participants, were included to support the descriptive analysis. Although the qualitative component was not initially based on formal theoretical models, themes emerging from the interviews showed conceptual alignment with the Health Belief Model and the biopsychosocial model. This alignment informed the development of selected items in the quantitative questionnaire.

The qualitative component of the study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist.

Part two: quantitative stage

A quantitative questionnaire consisting of 65 items was prepared using data obtained from qualitative interviews. The questions in the questionnaire were designed taking into account the codes, categories, and themes obtained from the qualitative interviews. Each sub-theme identified in the qualitative analysis was translated into one or more corresponding items in the 65-item questionnaire. Each item was rated on a 0–10 scale. For descriptive analyses, any score other than zero (1–10) was coded as ‘yes’ to indicate confirmation of that barrier, while a score of 0 was coded as ‘no’. This approach allowed us to quantify how many participants perceived each factor as relevant, regardless of the intensity of its effect. 600 women in the sample were invited to the research by phone one by one. A total of 66 people out of 600 agreed to participate in the research, 31 of whom were pregnant and 35 of whom were in the postpartum period. The interviews were conducted over the phone and during the interviews, the participants were asked to fill out the sociodemographic data form and then the EPDS. Then, the quantitative survey form was applied. The questions to the participants were asked as follows: ‘These questions were created according to the answers received as a result of the interviews conducted with women like you who did not apply to the outpatient clinic. Did the factors I will ask affect your application process to the outpatient clinic? If so, can you score them out of 10?’ The scoring at the evaluation stage expresses the extent to which individuals are affected by the factors. Accordingly, as the score increases, the weight of the factor affecting the individual can be evaluated. On the other hand, the score given for each factor was taken into consideration even if it was one out of ten, and the effect of the factor was evaluated as yes or no. It was thought that this form of expression would be valuable in terms of bringing to light the rare situations that are at the core of qualitative research and seeing the invisible [10].

Quantitative analysis was exploratory in nature. Due to the small sample size relative to the number of items, multivariate modeling and factor analysis were not possible; therefore, the analyses focused on descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations to identify potentially important factors requiring further investigation. Furthermore, for women or those who did not participate in the quantitative phase, only EPDS scores were available for analysis; additional demographic or clinical data were not accessible due to ethical constraints.

Additionally, attendance status was verified through telephone and, where applicable, face-to-face follow-ups, and only women who had not attended the perinatal psychiatry clinic or initiated treatment were included in both the qualitative and quantitative samples.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 on a computer running the Windows 10 operating system. First, descriptive and frequency analyses were performed, then the groups were compared. The normal distribution of continuous data was checked using skewness and kurtosis values. In the study, it was accepted that the skewness and kurtosis values ​​between + 2 and − 2 showed normal distribution [20]. When the groups were evaluated; Student-t test was used to compare the means of variables that were normally distributed, and Mann-Whitney U test was used for variables that were not normally distributed in the comparison.

In this study, the EPDS scores obtained and the measurements obtained from the quantitative questionnaire were compared between the groups, and correlation analyses were performed to examine the associations between EPDS scores and related factors. Categorical variables were evaluated with chi-square analysis. The significance level was accepted as p < 0.05. When the degree of relationship was stated in the correlation, 0.01–0.19 was expressed as insignificant, 0.20–0.49 as low, 0.50–0.69 as medium, 0.70–0.85 as high, and 0.86–1 as very high power [2].

Sociodemographic and clinical data form

This form was prepared by the research team and includes general data such as age and marital status, as well as risk factors identified as being associated with depression during the perinatal period (such as date of birth, marital status, number of people living in the household, monthly per capita income, total number of pregnancies, total number of healthy births, gestational week, history of previous psychiatric disorders, history of medical problems in previous pregnancies, history of psychiatric disorders in previous pregnancies, comorbid medical conditions, previous birth history, history of violence, etc.), which are queried accordingly. The quantitative instrument was designed as an exploratory item pool rather than a validated scale, and reliability indices were therefore not the primary focus of this study.

Edinburgh postnatal depression scale (EPDS)

This is one of the most commonly used scales for screening for depression during pregnancy and after childbirth. Developed by [9], this scale was designed for screening purposes to identify the risk of depression in women during the postnatal period. The scale has since been adapted for use during pregnancy. The EPDS is a 10-item self-report scale using a 4-point Likert format. Responses are scored on a scale of 0 to 3, with the lowest possible score being 0 and the highest possible score being 30. In the evaluation, items 1, 2, and 4 are scored as 0, 1, 2, 3, while items 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10 are scored as 3, 2, 1, 0, in reverse order. The Turkish adaptation of the EPDS was carried out by Engindeniz [15]. The study was later replicated by Aydın and colleagues, who demonstrated its validity and reliability [3]. The cutoff point for the EPDS was calculated as 12, and women with a scale score of 12 or higher were classified as being at risk.

Quantitative sample characteristics

From the group of 600 women who had screened positive on the EPDS and had not attended outpatient or hospital-based mental health services, 66 women could be reached by telephone and agreed to participate in the study. These women completed a brief quantitative questionnaire that served to characterize the group reached and to inform the subsequent qualitative inquiry.

At the time of contact, 21% of participants screened positive on the EPDS, identifying a subgroup with ongoing depressive symptoms relevant to the qualitative exploration of barriers to care. Given the sample size (n = 66), this estimate was associated with a wide 95% confidence interval (approximately 11%–31%), reflecting substantial uncertainty. Accordingly, the quantitative findings are presented descriptively and are not intended to represent prevalence estimates for the entire population of women who screened positive at baseline.

When the sociodemographic characteristics of the women participating in the study were examined, 18 (58.1%) of the women during pregnancy lived in city centers and 13 (41.9%) lived in suburban areas. Among women in the postpartum period, 29 (82.9%) lived in urban centers and 6 (17.1%) lived in suburban areas (p = 0.026).

When the clinical characteristics of the participants were examined, 1 (3.2%) of the women in the pregnancy period and 10 (28.6%) of the women in the postpartum period had a history of psychiatric treatment outside of pregnancy and the postpartum period (p = 0.006*).

Seven (22.6%) of the women in the pregnancy period group and 1 (2.9%) of the women in the postpartum period group were found to receive support for household chores and cleaning (p = 0.006*).

When the responses to the quantitative interview form were examined, it was found that the question “household chores such as cleaning, ironing, cooking, and childcare are the responsibility of women in society” under the sub-theme of “barriers related to gender roles” in the sociocultural factors theme received the highest average score (4.47). It was determined that the top three questions with the highest average scores were in the sociocultural factors theme.

Associations with depressive symptom severity

In the quantitative sample, participants had completed their initial EPDS assessments at different time points: some during pregnancy (at varying gestational weeks) and others during the early postpartum period. During the follow-up, conducted approximately 1.5 years after delivery, the EPDS was re-administered to all women. Using the ≥ 12 cut-off, 7 women from the pregnancy group (22.6%) and 7 women from the postpartum group (20%) scored above the threshold at follow-up.

Furthermore, a significant difference was observed in the total EPDS scores between women who participated in the interview and those who did not attend the clinic (Mann-Whitney U = 6964.0, p = 0.0026).

When the correlation of each question in the EPDS quantitative interview form was examined, the question “Not having a reliable place to leave your child” showed a low positive correlation (rs=0.372, p = 0.02), and the question “Your close circle of friends and family disregarding your mental health issues” showed a low positive correlation (rs=0.323, p = 0.008), the question “The intensity of household chores such as cleaning, ironing, cooking, and childcare” showed a low positive correlation (rs=0.431, p < 0,001), and the question “The daily hustle and bustle of life taking precedence over your mental health issues” showed a moderate correlation (rs=0.562, p < 0,001), a moderate positive correlation with the question “household chores such as cleaning, ironing, cooking, and childcare being the responsibility of women in society” (rs=0.545, p < 0,001), a low positive correlation was found with the question “your spouse’s indifference or lack of support during pregnancy and the postpartum period” (rs=490, p < 0,001). These positive correlations indicate that these factors received higher ratings among women with higher EPDS scores, suggesting that these women were more strongly affected by these factors.

Discussion

This study aimed to shed light on the barriers to treatment for patients with perinatal depression using qualitative and quantitative research methods.

Qualitative interviews conducted to understand the barriers to seeking treatment for perinatal depression resulted in a total of five themes: individual factors, environmental factors, sociocultural factors, stigma-related factors, and healthcare provider-related factors. A meta-analysis on the subject focused on three themes: problem identification, healthcare experience, and stigma [25]. In addition to these three themes, our study identified environmental and sociocultural factors as other important barriers. Unlike previous studies, it was found that among the reasons women did not seek treatment were barriers related to gender roles, psychological/internal barriers, health problems related to the mother, barriers related to the partner, and barriers related to children.

The category of depression symptoms emerged as a finding that differed from the themes and categories in the literature. The negative impact of feelings and thoughts such as hopelessness, unhappiness, and lack of motivation, which are symptoms of the disease, on women’s hospital seeking processes was noteworthy. In fact, the symptoms of the disease themselves constituted a barrier to treatment. Even if women could name these feelings and thoughts, they did not realize or did not want to realize that they could be related to an illness. As mentioned in a previous study under the theme of “recognition of the problem,” women must first be aware of the problem in order to seek help [8].

The sub-theme of ‘physical barriers’ addresses the fact that women are unable to go to hospital due to illnesses or health problems experienced during pregnancy and after childbirth. In particular, the fact that caesarean section can be as painful as any other surgical operation and can have a significant impact on women’s lives is an overlooked factor. There are only a limited number of studies that directly address this issue, and more research is needed to shed light on it.

Under the category of partner-related factors, women talked about how their partners’ substance use disorders and the burden of both their relationship problems and the entire family affected this process. A study found that having a partner with substance use disorder causes mental health issues in women and makes them feel financially and socially unsupported [28]. Due to substance use, women not only face economic difficulties but also feel unsupported in raising children, managing household chores, and maintaining a social life, all of which contribute to mental health issues. Additionally, these challenges act as significant barriers to seeking treatment.

Our country lags behind developed countries in terms of postnatal health problems in infants [50]. Prematurity or anomalies in infants necessitate close monitoring and treatment in the postnatal period. In addition to the stressful effect of this issue on women, the fact that women devote all their attention and care to their infants also seems to have a negative impact on this process. There has not yet been sufficient research on the impact of this factor on the treatment processes of perinatal depression patients, and this issue requires further clarification.

Under the sub-theme of gender role barriers, some women mentioned that they were solely responsible for all household chores such as cleaning, ironing, and cooking. It was found that some women felt angry because they had to do all these chores alone and their husbands did not help, while others saw all these as their duties. It was commonly accepted that childcare was entirely the responsibility of women. The decisive impact of gender roles on women’s lives also plays an important role in their mental health and increases their sensitivity to depression [45]. On the other hand, gender roles also have significant effects on the processes of seeking treatment and accessing treatment related to mental health. Previous studies have found that men who have masculine norms are less likely to seek help and that this is a significant barrier to their seeking treatment for depression [41]. Although similar studies have not been conducted with women, it is not difficult to foresee that gender roles may have affected women’s experiences of depression and their treatment-seeking processes to the same extent, or perhaps even more so. Therefore, further research is needed to shed light on this issue. At the same time, there is a need for social and cultural change regarding all these role distributions in terms of mental health.

Another important factor was related to the way the scale was administered. Some women stated that they did not take the scale very seriously at the time and answered the questions casually. Some women mentioned that they were unable to answer the questions carefully because the scale was administered in the hospital close to their due date or on the day they gave birth, and that they gave high scores to the questions due to the excitement of that period. All these statements may indicate denial of the problem, but they may also point to problems with the way the scale was administered. Although previous studies have shown that the EPDS has high specificity and sensitivity, the timing of the scale’s administration may have led to “overdiagnosis” [38].

From the perspective of the health belief model, in order for individuals to engage in health behaviors and seek treatment, their sensitivity and seriousness toward the disease must outweigh the barriers they will encounter in accessing treatment [23]. This study reveals that women do not perceive the consequences of perinatal depression as sufficiently serious and have low confidence in the benefits of treatment, and therefore do not take into account obstacles such as not being able to find an appointment, waiting in line, or leaving their children.

From a biopsychosocial model perspective, biological factors are not the only factors that determine individuals’ health behaviors, such as protecting their health or seeking treatment. While one person may seek treatment for the same symptoms, another person may ignore these symptoms and dismiss them. The reason for this behavioral difference is that individuals are greatly influenced not only by biological factors but also by social and psychological factors. Therefore, the fact that a woman who lacks social support, lives in a culture that is prejudiced against psychiatric illnesses, and experiences mental health issues does not seek treatment cannot be explained solely by structural barriers [7, 14]. In conclusion, health behaviors and decisions to seek treatment are strongly influenced not only by biological factors but also by individuals’ social and psychological circumstances, highlighting the multidimensional nature of barriers to treatment access.

When the participants’ EPDS scores were examined, it was found that 20% of them were still at risk for perinatal depression. This finding is consistent with previous studies, showing that perinatal depression can become chronic if left untreated and that EPDS scores will remain high [44, 48].

The three factors that received the highest scores in the quantitative questionnaires were “household chores such as cleaning, ironing, cooking, and childcare being the responsibility of women in society,” “the intensity of household chores such as cleaning, ironing, cooking, and childcare,” and “the hustle and bustle of daily life taking precedence over your mental health issues.” All these factors fall under the theme of sociocultural barriers. In addition, three questions are related to women’s gender roles within the family. This situation shows that the main reason participants did not seek help from the perinatal psychiatry clinic was that they denied their own mental health while trying to fulfill all the roles expected of them by their culture. In another study, the highest-scoring factors were identified as fear and stigma, desire to seek help, appointment scheduling tools, and appointment availability [18, 24]. Our findings differ from other studies in this regard.

In our study, several categories that frequently emerged in the qualitative interviews—particularly those barriers related to gender roles, Insufficient social support, Family intervention factor—also received high ratings in the quantitative phase. Thus, the quantitative results largely paralleled and reinforced the qualitative themes rather than contradicting them. Correlation analyses suggest that among women with higher EPDS scores, burdens related to domestic responsibilities and limited support more significantly terminate treatment-seeking and may interfere with difficulties in attending outpatient psychiatric care. Further research is needed on this topic.

In our study, social support emerged as a central and interrelated factor influencing help-seeking in the perinatal period. Insufficient partner support, family interference, and barriers related to traditional gender roles were frequently identified, suggesting that these factors tend to co-occur. This pattern is reflected in our quantitative findings, where several of the highest-rated barriers were sociocultural, including household chores being the responsibility of women, the intensity of household chores, and daily life pressures taking precedence over mental health.

Previous research shows that low social support during pregnancy is more common among women with limited partner involvement and greater caregiving burdens and is associated with higher depressive symptoms [5, 16]. Qualitative and quantitative studies further indicate that insufficient support not only increases vulnerability to depression but also constrains women’s capacity to seek help, particularly by limiting emotional validation and practical feasibility, in both antenatal and postnatal periods [1, 33, 39]. Taken together, this body of evidence highlights the decisive role of social support in both the development of perinatal depression and help-seeking behaviour, and our findings are consistent with the existing literature.

To facilitate interpretation, only the ten highest-scoring items from the quantitative interview form are presented in Table 2 within this subset, barriers related to gender roles and domestic responsibilities were particularly prominent. Items reflecting the societal expectation that household and childcare duties are primarily women’s responsibility, the intensity of domestic labor, and the prioritization of daily demands over mental health needs ranked among the highest-rated barriers. In contrast, some context-specific barriers identified qualitatively—such as physical recovery after caesarean section or partner substance use—did not appear among the highest-rated items in the quantitative assessment.

When evaluating the limitations of our study, one of the most important limitations is that the sample is based on results obtained from a single hospital rather than international or national collaboration. Although many of the obstacles mentioned in the literature have been overcome due to the presence of a perinatal psychiatry clinic in our hospital, the generalizability of these findings is limited in clinics that do not offer similar services. In addition, working with a hard-to-reach sample has led to a low number of participants and limitations in the generalizability of the results. Another limitation is that we did not examine whether the participants had received education from institutions such as maternity schools and family health centers to assess their level of knowledge about perinatal mental health.

The design of our study does not include a control group and examines patients who did not have access to treatment. However, examining patients who did seek treatment and comparing them with a control group could have added methodological richness to the study in terms of participant heterogeneity and diversity and enabled more in-depth statistical analyses. Due to the difficulty of obtaining a sample and the limited sample size that could be obtained during the study process, a control group could not be formed, which resulted in a limitation that could be considered data loss.

The very low response rate may have introduced selection bias, as women with higher barriers to help-seeking may have been less likely to participate, potentially leading to an underestimation of barrier severity and influencing the observed pattern of reported barriers. In addition, the quantitative findings based on the sample of 66 women represent a self-selected subgroup and are not generalisable due to the low response rate, and the correlation analyses conducted with this sample size should be interpreted with caution given their limited statistical power. Additionally, comparisons between participants and non-participants were limited to EPDS scores only, as no additional demographic or clinical data were ethically accessible for non-attenders. Although the timing of the initial EPDS assessments varied across participants (pregnancy weeks vs. early postpartum), this may have influenced symptom levels and limited comparability.

The strength of our study is that it combines qualitative and quantitative analysis and provides an in-depth examination of a sample that is quite difficult to reach. In addition, despite all the developments in the field of women’s mental health and perinatal psychiatry, another important point of our study is that it attempts to shed light on an area that remains inadequate when there is no cooperation with the community.

Conclusion

This study revealed that women experiencing perinatal depression face many barriers to accessing psychiatric treatment. Based on the combined analysis of quantitative and qualitative data, it was determined that factors such as domestic responsibilities imposed on women as a result of gender roles, the neglect of mental health symptoms, insufficient social support, fear of stigmatization, and negative experiences with the healthcare system negatively impact the behavior of seeking help. Numerous factors, ranging from transportation to physician attitudes, from prejudices about medications to family dynamics, directly shape women’s access to mental health support. These findings emphasize the need for a comprehensive approach to mental health services adapted to the perinatal period. In conclusion, our findings indicate that barriers to perinatal mental health care are closely linked to domestic responsibilities, limited social support, and treatment-related misconceptions. Practical responses may include integrating mental health screening into routine obstetric care, reducing logistical barriers through flexible appointments or childcare support, and providing targeted psychoeducation to address concerns about medication use. These measures may help improve access to perinatal mental health services and support timely help-seeking.
