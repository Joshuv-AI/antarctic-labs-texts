The evolving experience of academic women in engineering
Authors: Srabanti Chowdhury, Kelly Woo, Nish Sinha
Published: 2024

Introduction

Professor Srabanti Chowdhury, an associate professor of Electrical Engineering at Stanford University is joined by her students Kelly Woo, who has just defended her PhD thesis, and Nishita Sinha, a PhD candidate. Their research group focuses on devices made from the wideband gap and ultra-wide bandgap materials, and in particular thermal management of those devices. Spanning a generation of electrical engineers, from Associate Professors to PhD students, Professor Chowdhury, Ms. Woo, and Ms. Sinha compare their experiences as female researchers in electrical engineering. In doing so, we learn not only about their personal experiences but how academia has changed over time. More importantly, we get a glimpse into their opinions on what creates a successful culture of diversity and inclusion.

Miranda Vinay

1. Tell us what first got you interested in electrical engineering. Where are you now in your career?

Srabanti: During my undergraduate studies in India, amidst a rigorous curriculum, I encountered a diverse range of subjects spanning from physics to RF communications and networks. Seeking financial independence after graduation, I was steered towards learning software, specifically shell-scripting, to secure a job. However, I quickly grew bored with programming and realized it wasn’t the right career path for me. After carefully considering my options, I studied electrical engineering, which offered the perfect journey from atoms to applications. I was fascinated by the foundations of semiconductor engineering, where physics played a significant role. Electrical engineering allowed me to connect the dots from these fundamental principles to practical applications through circuits and systems.

Kelly: I already knew I wanted to pursue a career in engineering in high school. Its hands-on nature and the idea of creating things that could truly have a lasting impact on society excited me as a young student. I chose electrical engineering because of its diverse applications and relevance to many hardware-based engineering roles. However, I did not know how expansive the field was until university. During graduate school, a wonderfully taught course inspired me to focus on device-level research, leading me to wide- and ultra-wideband gap materials for devices. After recently defending my thesis on the applications of diamonds for electronics, I plan to undertake post-doctoral work with the goal of commercializing our research in a startup.

Nish: I was also first interested in electrical engineering when I studied electricity very briefly at the end of high school. When I finally understood Maxwell’s Equations, I found the interplay between electric and magnetic fields extremely fascinating and further, how different circuit components are designed based on these ideas. I studied undergraduate physics and global health because I wanted to learn how to apply STEM to real-world problems. I got increasingly interested in the climate crisis and realized how electrical engineering is critical for tackling climate change. When I got to Stanford, I was particularly excited by Professor Chowdhury’s group for their work on semiconductor devices and how they hope to apply these devices to improve the sustainability of electrical systems. I am now a second-year PhD student in this lab at Stanford.

2. When you were in school—grade school, college, and graduate school—do you remember a disparity between men and women studying physics and electrical engineering? Between male and female mentors?

Srabanti: I studied in India for both my high school diploma and undergraduate degree. In high school, I remember roughly equal numbers of girls and boys in my class. However, this balance shifted dramatically during my undergraduate years, when the ratio of women to men became significantly lower, with only one woman for every four men. Subsequently, I pursued my master’s and PhD in Electrical Engineering in the USA. I was surprised to see a similar trend in gender distribution, with a ratio of approximately one woman for every five men. When I started my PhD in January of 2006, I found myself as the only woman in my PhD cohort—just one in twenty members. This proportion improved over time, with the number of women in my cohort increasing to four out of twenty by the time I graduated in December 2010.

Kelly: When I finished high school in the USA in 2014, there was probably one woman to every three men studying physics, but an even distribution in other Science, Technology, Engineering, and Math (STEM) courses. Also in the USA, I attended a small university that focused on STEM where the ratio was more balanced, with around two women to every three men in the electrical engineering class of 2018. As a result, I never felt out of place or as a stark minority. In graduate school, I gravitated towards more diverse research groups, and I am pleased to now be in a group with about a one-to-one student-gender ratio in the USA. However, female mentors and faculty were noticeably underrepresented. In my electrical engineering courses, only one class was taught by a female professor, and throughout the university, four out of about thirty-six STEM courses were taught by women. During graduate school, the small presence of women was also extremely evident to me at international conferences in electrical engineering.

Nish: I went to high school in 2012 and college in 2017, both in the USA. I was one of the only women studying physics and this ratio became worse for higher-level classes. Additionally, there were only two female physics professors and, in my memory, only one professor of color at my undergraduate. At Stanford graduate school, about half of my PhD cohort in electrical engineering is female, and the gender ratio in my research lab is similarly balanced. Nearly 90% of my course professors have been men, and my advisor, Professor Chowdhury, is the first female semiconductor device professor at Stanford. While it is inspiring to have Professor Chowdhury as my advisor, especially because she is the first female semiconductor device professor at Stanford, it also highlights the lack of female leadership we see in semiconductor research. This imbalance needs to change if we hope to encourage more women and minorities to join this field.

3. Did you feel welcome at all educational levels? Please highlight any challenges.

Srabanti: Throughout my educational journey, I never felt unwelcome or discouraged, nor did I perceive any differential treatment based on gender, particularly on technical levels. However, the glaring underrepresentation of women in higher education did leave a lasting impression, undoubtedly impacting the overall environment. I saw that when a critical mass of women is included in any technical field, it creates an atmosphere where other women feel encouraged to join, speak up, and participate in discussions, handling acceptance and rejection without prejudice. Early in their careers, this support is even more impactful, nurturing a sense of peer equality. This “supportive environment” is where the challenges of balancing work and life, which women frequently face, are better understood and accommodated. Although balancing work and life is not strictly a gender-specific issue, it is important to acknowledge that women often encounter more demanding social pressures that can jeopardize their professional development.

Kelly: As a student, I saw that merit was recognized regardless of personal identity. The culture at my university was highly collaborative, so while many of the courses in engineering were challenging, learning in an inclusive environment allowed me to let my guard down, ask the necessary questions, share my own ideas, and focus on mastering the material. Now in graduate school, I am fortunate to be part of a research group with an even gender ratio and more than ten different nationalities and cultural backgrounds. I deeply appreciate this diversity, as it reflects the supportive and collaborative nature of the group. Moreover, interdisciplinary fields like engineering research need diversity to drive unique, impactful innovations that address complex problems in all aspects of society. Looking back, I have been able to progress so far in my academic career largely due to the diverse and inclusive environments I have been a part of. Confidently learning and pursuing opportunities should be the norm but are not often afforded to many minority groups.

Nish: I have been fortunate to have had supportive friends, parents, and mentors at every step of my educational journey. However, my worst experience with gender- and racial-based exclusion in academia was in the Advanced Physics Laboratory course during my undergraduate studies between 2017–2022 in the USA. This course was led by extremely intelligent and well-meaning professors, but they spoke differently to students from minority backgrounds. Even if unconscious, this exposed both racial and gender biases. I left this course feeling unsure if research and graduate studies were right for me. Luckily, I have generally felt extremely welcomed and supported in graduate school. A major contributor to this is that instead of ignoring gender and racial biases in STEM, many of the professors I look up to at Stanford, even male professors, discuss these issues in their classes and in meetings. This helps us all think more critically about our biases and the environments we are contributing to, ultimately leading to more inclusion.

4. Have you used or been a part of any programs specifically intended to support gender diversity? Were they helpful?

Srabanti: Within various technical committees like IEEE and similar organizations, the representation of women is consistently emphasized. I find these efforts to be incredibly valuable. Additionally, I serve on the executive committee for the IEEE International Electron Devices Meeting (IEDM). Over the past 7 to 8 years, I have witnessed a steady increase in women’s participation on this committee. This progress is not incidental but the result of efforts by the leadership to carefully include more gender and geographic diversity on the team. Initially, the committee was predominantly male in 2015–2016, but their consistent effort has achieved a healthy balance, with women now comprising over 30% of the committee—a remarkably large number in this sector at the time of writing.

Kelly: During my undergraduate years, I attended a workshop hosted by Qualcomm specifically for female students, where engineers conducted mock interviews for computer science and electrical engineering internships. The encouraging environment created by the interviewers, along with their genuine feedback, helped me build confidence and understand the expectations for future interviews. This initial stepping stone eventually led to an internship at Qualcomm, where I further developed my programming skills. Additionally, I formed lasting connections with other participants through a small hackathon during the workshop, many of whom I met again at future events. This not only kickstarted my professional development but also helped build my professional community of peers whom I could relate to.

Nish: In high school, I was part of the Girls Who Code immersion program. This is a free summer camp that teaches young women and nonbinary students the basics of computer programming. Due to the collaborative, not competitive, mindset of this program and the diversity and sensitivity of the teaching team, this was an extremely supportive space to build projects without being intimidated to ask questions. Now, in graduate school, I am part of Women in Electrical Engineering (WEE). This group has really helped me connect with other women in electrical engineering. Specifically, partnering older and younger female graduate students together has provided me with mentors who can provide advice regarding gender biases in research and a means through which I can share my experiences with younger students. Having communities that specifically support gender minorities in STEM has11gwyeokckkcm helped encourage me in my studies and research.

5. Dr. Chowdhury, you are now in the position of Associate Professor. Do you think that Universities have changed over the course of your career?

Srabanti: In my perspective, universities have undergone a significant transformation throughout my career, notably in terms of gender balance among students and faculty. A visible surge in the presence of female students and staff is shaping a more diverse educational landscape. The rise in female representation has led to an increase in role models and examples for aspiring scholars. More women are assuming leadership roles, serving as mentors, and igniting inspiration for future generations. This shift is pivotal in fostering greater female participation in higher education and academic pursuits.

Furthermore, there has been a notable expansion in gender-specific career support initiatives. Universities are now offering tailored programs and resources to address the unique challenges encountered by women in academia. These endeavors have played a crucial role in nurturing the career progression of female students and staff, contributing to narrowing the gender gap in higher education. Over the years, I have observed a notable increase in the number of women enrolling in the undergraduate Semiconductor Device class that I teach. In 2020, women made up just 17% of the cohort. Today, they constitute 50%. A similar trend is evident in my own research group; when I began my professorship, only three out of my twelve PhD students were women. Now, eight out of my sixteen PhD students are women. However, this success is not universal. To this day many research groups in electrical engineering still have zero female students.

6. Dr. Chowdhury, how does gender diversity influence your role as a professor?

Srabanti: Women often face more uncertainty when pursuing engineering. My dedication to gender equality is founded on strong moral values; it’s a lifelong commitment to nurturing a setting where everyone can flourish equally. I advocate for authentic gender diversity, rooted in the individual’s beliefs and attitudes. While it can be challenging to turn philosophies into strategies, I believe that clear philosophies naturally lead to effective actions. In my view, the path to fostering diversity is quite simple. It involves changing mindsets to support gender equality and ensuring that diversity isn’t just a box to check, but a mindset to cultivate.

I can’t pinpoint any policy or action I implemented to recruit women, but I did focus on creating a lab environment where everyone is excited about the outcomes. This environment celebrates equal partnership and leadership among all group members. In engineering, it’s easier to focus on the quality of work as the primary identity, and that’s what I’ve always emphasized. Everyone is supported and recognized for their engineering skills above all else. The philosophy of diversity as the norm instead of a quota creates an inclusive environment, which then attracts and maintains a healthy diversity of team members.

7. Ms. Sinha and Ms. Woo, do you intend to pursue a career in academia? Why or why not?

Kelly: I do not plan to pursue a career in academia as I hope to work on projects with a shorter time to adoption. While many impactful breakthroughs have emerged from academic environments, I am currently more drawn to projects where I know the technology will be deployed. As a result, I am excited to launch a startup with my colleagues to get the best of both worlds. Outside of academia, gender exclusion is still evident in industry. It is often the case that female engineers will need to work harder than their male counterparts to demonstrate their qualifications for leadership and gain support. As I look towards many tech companies big and small, it is extremely rare that I find female engineers in top leadership positions. However, I believe that the support system I have established throughout my career—with both male and female colleagues and mentors who want to see me succeed—will help mitigate the obstacles that come from gender bias.

Nish: Depending on how the next few years of my PhD research go, I may opt to continue with academic research or move towards industry, pursuing electrical engineering for climate technology. A startup might allow me to use what I learned from research to combat climate change with real-world impact. I am also curious about how my scientific background can be applied in fields such as environmental and science journalism, which can help introduce important research ideas to the general public.

8. Finally, what changes would you like to see in academia to make it more inclusive to women?

Srabanti: Striking a sustainable gender balance is a profound challenge, deeply rooted in societal norms. An enduring change requires cultivating a mindset that recognizes the importance of gender diversity in academia and the workplace. A gender equity mindset should be nurtured from an early age, starting in kindergarten. In academia, the initial step is to recruit and nurture women into STEM programs, ensuring they receive high-quality training. This not only helps achieve gender balance but also stabilizes the change. Diversity must be a sustainable effort, practiced like a marathon, not a sprint. We must focus on long-term, inclusive practices. One long-term solution is mentorship programs that connect female students and early-career researchers with experienced women in their fields, providing vital guidance and inspiration. Supporting women entrepreneurs will also play a pivotal role, shifting the focus from short-term gains to long-term successes. All stakeholders must be part of the solution, demonstrating patience and commitment to the journey toward true gender equality.

Kelly: The most effective way to enhance inclusivity in academia is to increase the presence of female mentors and leaders. Many prominent female engineers like my advisor, have sparked significant progress for gender equality. They have demonstrated invaluable contributions to their fields, changing an unfortunate but common opinion that women do not belong in engineering. Still, women face unconscious biases in many male-dominated fields, and I believe increasing minority representation is the most direct way to address this issue.

Fundamentally, to increase the number of women in leadership and mentorship positions, attitudes of equality must be cultivated at a young age. For example, promoting the exploration of all subject areas to students regardless of gender is key to building female confidence, which is often hampered by feelings of exclusion in male-dominated fields and lack of support. This would gradually, but inevitably, lead to mor`e girls discovering and persisting in their passions in STEM, creating a positive feedback loop. To encourage this, outreach programs and more female role models are needed to inspire the next generation of female engineers and show them and their support systems that they too can thrive in engineering and engineering research.

Nish: Many of the gender and diversity-based groups in academia are extremely helpful, but the next step needs to be tackling the minute day-to-day cases of sexism that are unfortunately quite commonplace in academia. One common example is in meetings, where the opinions most listened to come from those with the loudest voices. While this is not completely black and white, due to the way different genders tend to be raised and socialized, I find that cis men are generally the people who speak the loudest and with the most confidence and thus tend to be the ones listened to and eventually given leadership roles. If we truly want to make academia an inclusive space for all genders, we all need to think about how our actions may exclude certain voices or prioritize others.

As a society and individuals, we all have unconscious biases regarding gender and race. Interacting with different gender and racial identities through my lab and elsewhere have helped me confront these biases, but I am still learning. In a research setting, we need better, mandatory sensitivity training for discrimination for both research students and mentors to help labs that struggle with unconscious gender-based bias.

In addition, free university research programs specifically for younger middle and high school-aged students can help bring together many different genders and racial, and economic backgrounds in scientific settings from a younger age. These experiences can help make future researchers more aware of discrimination in academia and help remove some internal biases. But, even with attending more programs and training, many of our internal biases will remain. It is up to each one of us to continue to study minority issues and think about the impact of our actions and identities in different settings even as we get further into our careers, both on a systematic and personal level.
