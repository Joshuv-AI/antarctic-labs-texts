Effect of pulmonary rehabilitation on lung function and quality of life in pulmonary tuberculosis survivors with post-tuberculosis lung disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Abraham Tekola Gebremedhn, Kidist Bobosha, Hawult Taye Adane, Yeabsira Alemu Fantaye, Dawit Girma Abebe, Tsegab Alemayehu Bukate, Nebiat Adane Mera, Minyahil Tadesse Boltena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-tuberculosis lung disease (PTLD) is a significant global public health challenge. The contribution PTLD on long-term morbidity and impaired quality of life is increasingly recognized. There is limited evidence on effective management strategies of PTLD. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effect of pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) in improving lung function and health related quality of life (HRQoL) among individuals with post-tuberculosis lung disease.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted by following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. Studies involving pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) survivors who had completed treatment and developed post-TB lung disease (PTLD), as defined by the individual studies, were included. A comprehensive literature search was conducted on PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, ClinicalTrials.gov and Google scholar between July 25 and August 1, 2025. Data were extracted by two individuals using a structured, pre-designed Excel form. The extracted data were cleaned and analyzed using R software version 4.5. A random effect model was employed to accommodate methodological variation.ResultsA total of 13 studies, including one randomized controlled trial, one prospective cohort study, and 11 pre-post interventional studies, consisting of 747 participants were included. With very low certainty evidence, the pooled mean change of 6-minute walk distance (6MWD) was 60.04 m (95% CI: 27.83−92.20). Low certainty evidence showed the pooled mean change in forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁), forced vital capacity (FVC), the ratio of forced expiratory volume to forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC) and partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood (pao2) were 0.07 L (95% CI: 0.01;0.14), 0.06 L (95% CI: -0.02;0.14), 1.06% (95% CI: -1.66;3.78) and 2.64 mmHg (95% CI: 0.15;5.12) respectively. The mean change in SGRQ scores was −18.85 (95% CI: −22.43−15.27) based on low certainty evidence.ConclusionDespite methodological heterogeneity and lack of randomized controlled study, pulmonary rehabilitation is a promising intervention in improving exercise tolerance, lung function and HRQoL of PTLD patients, though the certainty of evidence ranges from low to very low. More high-quality Randomized Clinical Trial (RCT) and longitudinal studies are needed to have strong evidence to recommend PR programs as part of regular recommendation for PTLD care to achieve better health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04217-y.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among the infectious agent globally [1–3]. The significant efforts invested so far have been focused on reducing TB incidence and mortality. But the long-term health consequences faced by TB survivors have received relatively less attention [4, 5]. One of the most common and disabling complication after bacteriological cure of pulmonary TB is PTLD, which can significantly impair lung function and quality of life [6–8]. PTLD is a chronic respiratory condition characterized by structural and functional lung impairment persisting after microbiological cure of pulmonary TB [9].

The common manifestations of PTLD include chronic cough, breathlessness, reduced exercise capacity, long-term disability and reduced quality of life [8–12]. Due to improved treatment coverage, the global burden of PTLD is rising and presents a major challenge for health systems, particularly in high TB burden region [13, 14]. TB affects approximately 10.6 million individuals annually, and an estimated 58 million people have survived TB since 2000 [8]. The current number of global TB survivors are around 155 million [8, 15]. Recent evidence estimates that over 40% of TB survivors developed some form of long-term pulmonary lung disease, including bronchiectasis, fibrosis, and airflow obstruction [16, 17]. The World Health Organization (WHO) released a policy brief in 2021 identifying PTLD as a neglected but significant contributor to long-term morbidity, reduced quality of life, and economic burden in TB-affected populations [18–20].

In order to improve lung function, exercise tolerance and quality of life, pulmonary rehabilitation has been proposed as a potential intervention to address the physical, functional limitations and HRQOL in pulmonary TB survivors. PR is a multidisciplinary program that typically includes exercise training, education, behavioral modification and nutritional interventions [12, 21]. It has been thought as a well-established and an effective strategy for improving lung function, reducing symptoms, and enhancing quality of life in individuals with chronic respiratory diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [19, 22]. Evidence on the effect of PR in pulmonary TB survivors with PTLD is limited [23]. Currently, there are no standardized pulmonary rehabilitation protocols for PTLD, and most of the available evidence is derived from small-scale observational studies or pilot trials, underscoring the need for rigorous evaluation of the PR intervention effect on lung function and HRQOL of PTLD patients [8, 24–26]. In response to the growing concern of PTLD, a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence on the effects of PR interventions on lung function, exercise capacity, and quality of life is urgently needed. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the effect of pulmonary rehabilitation in improving lung function, functional capacity, and HRQoL among tuberculosis survivors with PTLD. The findings from this review address a critical evidence gap and offer insights to guide evidence-based practice, supporting the integration of pulmonary rehabilitation into TB care and long-term follow-up programs. Ultimately, these findings contribute to the goals of the WHO End TB Strategy and global efforts to improve the quality of life of pulmonary TB survivors.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted by following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [27]. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251044478). The research question was structured using the PICO framework, focusing on pulmonary tuberculosis survivors with post-tuberculosis lung disease (PTLD) as the population, pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) programs as the intervention, standard care, no rehabilitation, or alternative interventions as comparators, and exercise tolerance, lung function (FEV₁, FVC) and health related quality of life measures as an outcome.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Studies involving pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) survivors who had completed treatment and developed post-TB lung disease (PTLD), as defined by the individual studies, were included. Studies were required to report PR interventions and outcomes like lung function parameters (such as FEV₁ or FVC), exercise capacity (6-minute walk distance), HRQoL or respiratory symptoms. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), pre-post interventional studies, longitudinal intervention and prospective cohort studies were included. Studies published in English with no restrictions on year of publication and geography were included.

Exclusion criteria

Studies that did not report both baseline and endline outcome data, those that focused exclusively on TB treatment, and studies addressing only extrapulmonary TB without pulmonary involvement were excluded. In addition, case reports, letters, conference abstracts lacking sufficient extractable data, and animal or laboratory-based studies were excluded from the review.

Search strategy and study selection

A comprehensive literature search was conducted on PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, ClinicalTrials.gov and Google scholar between July 25 and August 1, 2025. The search was conducted using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text terms, connected using Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” (Supplementary file 1). The literature was downloaded and imported into Mendeley reference software to maintain and manage citations and facilitate the review process. Two reviewers (ATG and YAF) reviewed the articles, and a third reviewer (MTB) resolved any discrepancies. Research articles were subsequently screened based on their titles and abstracts to exclude studies that were irrelevant to the topic, such as those not PR intervention and outcome among TB survivor with PTLD. The titles and abstracts of the selected studies were reviewed for full text by two independent reviewers (ATG and MTB), and discrepancies were resolved by the third author (HTA) to decide whether the study was included.

Data extractions

Data were extracted by two independent data extractors (ATG and DGA) using a standardized data extraction excel form to ensure consistency and accuracy. Extracted data included key study characteristics such as the author, year of publication, country, study design, PTLD definition sample size, and detailed descriptions of the pulmonary rehabilitation intervention, including its components, duration, and frequency. Participant characteristics, including age and sex, were also captured. In addition, data were extracted on the specific types of interventions such as education, exercise training, nutritional support, and physiotherapy along with the follow-up duration. For each intervention, baseline and endline mean values and standard deviations were recorded for the primary outcome measures of lung function (FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC), exercise tolerance (6MWD and ISWT), and health-related quality of life (CCQ, CRQ, and SGRQ) and PaO2.

Risk of bias assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed using a standardized and revised Cochrane risk of bias assessment tools appropriate for each study design. Randomized controlled trials were evaluated with the RoB2 assessment of controlled intervention studies, which examines bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome and selection of the reported result [28]. Before-after (pre-post) studies without a control group were assessed using the RoB-I tool for before-after (pre-post) studies with no control group, which examine bias due to selection of participants into the study, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes and selection of the reported results [29]. Cohort studies were assessed using the RoB E tool for observational cohort studies, which evaluates bias due to confounding, selection of participants into the study, classification of exposures, departures from intended exposures, missing data, measurement of outcomes and selection of the reported result.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted to evaluate the effect of PR on exercise tolerance, lung function and HRQoL on tuberculosis survivors with PTLD. Extracted data were cleaned and analyzed using R software version 4.5. The within group mean change between baseline (pre-intervention) and endline (post-intervention) values were used to quantify the exercise tolerance, lung function and HRQoL. A random-effects model meta-analysis was conducted to account for expected heterogeneity arising from differences in study design, intervention components, and intervention duration. Statistical heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the I² statistic, with heterogeneity thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high respectively. To explore sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analysis by study design, intervention duration, intervention setting and meta regression for publication year and follow up durations was performed. Publication bias was evaluated through both visual inspection of funnel plots and statistical methods, including Egger’s regression and Begg’s rank correlation test. Fill and trim analysis were performed to assess the number of studies missed due to publication bias. The geographic distribution of the included studies was visualized using a global map generated using R Studio.

Results

Study selection

A total of 600 records were identified through manual and database searching. After removing duplicates, 550 articles were screened based on titles and abstracts, of which 100 were eligible for full-text review. Following full-text assessment and quality appraisal only 13 studies met the criteria for inclusion in the final analysis [30–42] (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

A total of thirteen studies were included. Of these, 11 (85%) were pre-post interventional studies without control groups, which are inherently at a higher risk of bias (Fig. 2). Only one study was a randomized controlled trial (RCT) [32]. Risk of bias assessment using Cochrane tools revealed that majority of studies (n = 11) exhibited moderate to serious risk of bias, primarily due to lack of randomization, potential confounding, and participant selection issues (Fig. 2). Narrative descriptions of exercise tolerance, respiratory symptoms and health related quality of life

A pre-post intervention study in Uganda reported the mean distance in the incremental shuttle walk test (ISWT) increased by 90 m (95% CI: 56.50, 123.50) [31]. Another study in Japan reported an 88 m increase in ISWT [38]. One study reported a decrease in chest pain from 22% at baseline to 10% post-intervention [36] and another study reported drop from 45% (13/29) to 24% (7/29) [31]. Similarly the third study reported decreased chest pain from 45% to 31% [38]. One study reported a decrease in prevalence of hemoptysis from 17% (4/29) to 7% (2/29) [31] other study reported no change in hemoptysis prevalence [42]. The included studies reported that PR improves HRQoL in individuals with post-tuberculosis lung disease. A randomized controlled trial in India reported improvements across all domains of the SF-36 questionnaire (p < 0.05) following pulmonary rehabilitation [32]. One prospective cohort study reported a chronic respiratory questionnaire (CRQ) improvement from 17.21 to 18.96 (p = 0.025) [33] and other study reported a mean change of −0.95 (95% CI: −1.18, −0.72) in the clinical chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) questionnaire (CCQ) total score [31].

Meta-analysis

Meta-analysis examined the effects of pulmonary rehabilitation on various intervention outcomes on TB survivors with PTLD. This meta-analysis focus on outcome parameters like exercise capacity, lung function, and quality of life. From 11 studies the pooled 6MWD mean change was 60.04 m (95% CI: 27.87, 92.20, I² = 97.4%, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses for 6MWD were conducted based on study design, setting and intervention/exposure duration. The corresponding 6MWD mean change reported from prospective interventional, pre-post interventional, RCT and cohort study were 41.83 (95% CI: 31.83 − 52.49; I²=85.9%, p < 0.0001), 47.53 (95% CI: 19.08 −75.99; I² = 64.6%, p < 0.0001), 197.30 (95% CI: 183.74, 210.86 and 38.00 (95% CI: 2.00 −74.00) meters respectively (Additional material 1) (Table 2). The test for subgroup differences was significant (Q = 334.44, p < 0.0001) (Additional material 1) (Table 2). Majority of the studies were health facility based. The 6MWD mean change reported in health facility based, home based, community based and one record not reporting the setting were 69.32 (95% CI: 26.44 − 112.19; I²=97.3%, p < 0.0001), 31.0 (95% CI: 12.50−49.50), 40.0 (95% CI: 26.14 − 53.86) and 36.0 (95% CI: −2.15 −74.15) with (χ2 = 2, df = 3, p = 0.4442p = 0.4442) meters respectively (Additional material 1) (Table 2).

Risk of publications bias

Publication bias was assessed for the 6MWD graphically using a funnel plot (Fig. 10) and statistically assessed using Begg’s and Egger’s tests. The Begg’s test indicated a bias estimate of 23.0 (SE = 12.85, p = 0.0734), while the Egger’s test showed a bias estimate of −5.37 (SE = 4.20, p = 0.233). Although the Begg’s test yielded a borderline p-value, suggesting a slight tendency toward asymmetry, neither test reached statistical significance at the 0.05 level. Therefore, there is no strong evidence of publication bias or small-study effects in this meta-analysis, and the overall findings are considered robust and reliable (Table 4).

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias (RoB) assessment, as summarized using the Cochrane RoB assessment tool revealed varying methodological quality across the included studies (Table 5). The single randomized controlled trial [32] showed a consistently low risk of bias across all five domains of the RoB 2 tool (Additional material 1), indicating appropriate randomization, minimal deviations from intended interventions, limited missing data, unbiased outcome measurement, and adherence to pre-specified reporting protocols. This uniform low-risk rating supports the high internal validity and reliability of its findings. In contrast, a single non-randomized cohort study [33] exhibited a critical high risk of bias due to confounding, reflecting inadequate control of baseline differences between exposure groups, alongside some concerns related to participant selection (Fig. 2). Despite low risk in other domains, these issues substantially compromise the causal interpretability of the study’s results. Furthermore, the synthesis of eleven pre-post interventional studies [30, 31, 34–42] revealed predominantly moderate risks of bias. The highest concerns were observed in domains related to confounding and participant selection, highlighting the essential methodological limitations of pre-post intervention study designs. Although some studies demonstrated low risk for missing data and outcome measurement, the aggregate bar plot (Fig. 2) indicated that most studies fell within moderate or serious risk categories. Overall, while the RCT provided robust and methodologically sound evidence, the cohort and pre-post interventional studies showed bias concerns, limiting the overall confidence in the pooled or comparative effect estimates derived from these designs.

Grade assessment

The certainty of evidence for the outcomes assessed using five domains which includes risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The outcome of pulmonary rehabilitation among TB survivors with post-tuberculosis lung disease (PTLD) were varied across measures. For 6-Minute Walk Distance (6MWD), the evidence was very low certainty (⊕ΟΟΟ) due to serious risk of bias from predominately pre-post studies, high heterogeneity (I² = 97.4%), wide confidence intervals (95% CI: 27.87–92.20), and suspected publication bias, despite a large mean improvement (60.04 m) exceeding the MCID (Supplementary file 1). Lung function (FEV1) was supported by low certainty evidence (⊕⊕ΟΟ), reflecting consistent effects (I² = 0.0%) and precise estimates, though serious risk of bias from observational designs limited confidence. Health-related quality of life (SGRQ) also had low certainty evidence (⊕⊕ΟΟ); the pooled mean change (−18.85) was substantially above the MCID, indicating a large effect, but methodological limitations of included pre-post studies reduced confidence (Supplementary file 1).

Discussion

Pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) has emerged as a promising intervention to improve functional capacity and quality of life on tuberculosis survivors with PTLD. Overall, this review founded very low certainty evidence of an improvement in exercise tolerance (6MWD), and low certainty evidence for improvements in lung function (FEV1) and health-related quality of life (SGRQ) among PTLD patients.

Despite the high methodological heterogeneity and concern risk of bias due to lack of comparators in all included study, the pooled within groups mean change in 6MWD was 60.04 m with very low certainty evidence and significantly high heterogeneity (I² = 97.4%), which substantially exceeds the established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 25–35 m mean change for chronic respiratory diseases [43, 44]. Despite the presence of risk of bias and high heterogeneity, the finding of this study showed a promising improvement in functional status among PTLD patients. Our subgroup analyses showed that this heterogeneity primarily arose from differences in study design, suggesting that the pooled estimate should be interpreted with caution. In contrast, no meaningful heterogeneity was observed in relation to intervention duration or intervention setting, indicating that these factors did not significantly influence the variation across studies. The single RCT reported a remarkable 197-meter improvement [32], while pre–post studies showed a more modest, but still significant, 47.53 m improvement in 6MWD [34, 36–39]. These differences highlight the importance of methodological rigor, as non-randomized designs without concurrent control groups may underestimate or overestimate the true intervention effect. Results from meta regression done for publication year and sample size indicated neither of them showed heterogeneity, suggesting it primarily originated from methodological differences and variability in program characteristics.

Evidence from Uganda and Japan also demonstrated significant gains (90 m and 88 m) in ISWT respectively [31, 38]. PR further contributed to reductions in respiratory symptoms. For example, chest pain prevalence declined from 22% to 10% in one study [36] and from 45% to 24% in another [31]. Findings for hemoptysis were inconsistent, with one study showing a decrease [31] where as other reported no change [42]. In contrast to the large functional improvements, gains in lung function were modest but statistically significant with low certainty evidence, with mean change of 0.07 L for FEV1. This is consistent evidence from COPD and other chronic lung diseases, where PR primarily enhances exercise tolerance through peripheral muscle conditioning, ventilatory efficiency, and desensitization to dyspnea, rather than reversing fixed structural damage [45]. Importantly, FEV1 showed no heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%), suggesting highly consistent physiological effects across diverse methodology and settings. Similarly, the consistent improvement with moderate heterogeneity in PaO₂ (2.64 mmHg; I² = 48%) suggests enhanced gas exchange that likely contributes to functional benefits.

The included studies consistently demonstrated that PR substantially improves HRQoL in individuals with PTLD. Across multiple HRQoL measures including SGRQ, SF-36, CRQ, and CCQ PR showed clinical improvements with low certainty evidence reported a pooled SGRQ of −18.85 (I² =15.0%) points, more than four times the established MCID, highlighting benefits beyond physical capacity [44, 46]. A randomized controlled trial study in India revealed significant improvements across all domains of the SF-36 questionnaire (p < 0.05) following pulmonary rehabilitation [32]. Studies using the chronic respiratory questionnaire (CRQ) and the clinical COPD questionnaire (CCQ) also noted positive changes in exercise tolerance. One prospective cohort study observed a CRQ score improvement from 17.21 to 18.96 (p = 0.025) [33], while another pre-post study reported a mean change of −0.95 in the CCQ total score (95% CI: −1.18, −0.72), indicating improvement in HRQoL [31]. Despite the overall evidence showing promising improvements in exercise capacity, lung function, and quality of life following PR in PTLD, the certainty is limited by study design weaknesses, inconsistency, and imprecision, particularly for 6MWD. Therefore, more well-designed RCT and longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these findings and to establish PR as a routine recommendation for PTLD, including its long-term benefits.

Strengths and limitations

This meta-analysis has several strengths, including a comprehensive search strategy, evaluated multiple outcomes, asses the quality of included study using the widely accepted risk of bias assessment tools. The study tried to assess source of heterogeneity by using subgroup analysis and meta-regression. However, this review has a lot of limitations. First the high heterogeneity observed for the 6MWD and PaO2 outcomes needs caution when interpreting the pooled estimates. Second the majority of included studies exhibited moderate risks of bias and few have high risk of bias, mainly due to confounding, participant selection issues, and the absence of randomization, which compromise the internal validity of the findings. Third the majority of included data came from pre-post studies, which are inherently at a higher risk of bias due to lack of randomization and control group. Fourth the analysis was conducted using within-group mean changes, meaning we only consider baseline and endline data of the intervention group without control group which may overestimate or under estimate the true improvement compared to analyses based on between-group mean changes that include control groups. Fifth the included PR programs were highly variable in their duration, frequency, setting, and specific components. These gaps of PR make it impossible to conclude PR causes improvement in exercise tolerance, lung function and HRQoL. Sixth although Egger’s and Begg’s tests were not statistically significant for 6MWD, the trim-and-fill analysis indicated that at least four studies were likely missing. This suggests the potential presence of publication bias, where smaller studies with significant or unfavorable results may not have been published. Seventh Grading the certainty of the evidence showed low certainty of evidence. Finally, the geographic concentration of studies in the southeast Asia region, particularly India, may limit the generalizability of our findings to PTLD patients in other parts of the world with different healthcare systems and patient characteristics.

Conclusion

Despite the methodological heterogeneities, the findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that pulmonary rehabilitation intervention for patients with post-tuberculosis lung disease (PTLD) indicates potential improvements in exercise capacity with very low certainty evidence and health-related quality of life with low certainty evidence. Modest gains in lung function reported with low certainty evidence. However, the limited number of randomized controlled trials and the high risk of bias in non-randomized studies constrain the certainty of these findings.

Therefore, while these results support the potential integration of PR into the standard care pathway for TB survivors, more high-quality, adequately powered Randomized Clinical Trials (RCTs) are urgently needed to confirm these effects. Future research should prioritize rigorous study designs, including longitudinal assessments and comparative evaluations of program duration and intensity across diverse geographic regions, to establish a robust evidence base for routine clinical recommendations.
