Postgraduate GPs’ experience of attending medical uncertainty training during the standardised training of residents: a qualitative process evaluation
Authors: Zhihui Liu, Yingshuai Zhao, Weili Shi, Liuyi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUncertainty is a pervasive aspect of primary healthcare, significantly impacting clinical decision-making and patient outcomes. Medical Uncertainty Training (MUT) courses aim to equip healthcare providers with the skills needed to manage this uncertainty effectively.ObjectiveTo explore post-graduate General Practitioners’ (GPs) experiences and perspectives on attending MUT courses during standardized residency training in China, focusing on the potential mechanism of such courses in handling uncertainty and identified areas for improvement.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews with postgraduate GPs who attended the MUT course. Participants were selected through purposive sampling, and recruitment continued until data saturation was reached. Interviews were conducted between January and March 2024, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed thematically using the Donabedian structure-process-outcome framework. NVivo 14 software was used to support data coding and theme development.ResultsThree principal themes emerged from the process evaluation interviews, categorized according to the Donabedian structure-process-outcome framework as teaching structure, teaching process, and teaching outcome. These themes were further subdivided into specific sub-themes including necessity of the course, comprehensive curriculum design, flexibility and accessibility, continuous feedback and reflection, cognitive, emotional, and behavioral transformation, Real-World application challenges.ConclusionThe process evaluation provided deeper insight into GPs’ experiences with the MUT course. Rather than merely increasing tolerance for uncertainty, participants emphasized the development of practical competence in uncertain clinical scenarios. By integrating cognitive, emotional, and behavioral elements, the training enhanced their confidence and skills in managing complex cases. Future research should explore the generalizability and long-term impact of these outcomes across diverse clinical contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08074-x.Keywords: Medical uncertainty training, Process evaluation, Postgraduate education

Background

In primary healthcare, the dance with uncertainty is a constant theme defining clinical practice [1]. With each patient encounter, clinicians navigate numerous variables, ranging from the subtleties of symptom presentation to the complexities of diagnostic ambiguity [2]. In this study, medical uncertainty is defined as the perceived ambiguity, unpredictability, or complexity encountered by clinicians in real-world practice, particularly related to diagnosis and treatment decisions. These manifestations are driven by underlying sources such as cognitive biases, patient variability, incomplete clinical data, and systemic or interpersonal communication barriers [3], challenging healthcare professionals to tread the fine line between confident decision-making and prudent acknowledgment of the unknown [4, 5]. Medical students who are not adequately prepared to handle uncertainty in the clinical environment may face cognitive dissonance, reduced self-efficacy, decreased empathy, maladaptive perfectionism, and ultimately, burnout later in their careers [6]. Therefore, healthcare professionals must be equipped with the skills to navigate medical uncertainty.

Post-graduate General Practitioners (GPs) play a pivotal role in shaping the next generation of doctors by participating in the standardised training of residents in China [7].In response to the need to equip healthcare professionals with the skills to effectively navigate uncertainty, Medical Uncertainty Training (MUT) has emerged in residency training programs [8]. Embedded in the standardised curriculum for residents, MUT courses foster the development of clinical acumen and resilience in the face of uncertainty. By incorporating medical uncertainty courses into standardised resident training, medical training institutions foster better understanding of how to approach ambiguous situations and make well-informed decisions [9, 10].

However, the current implementation of uncertainty training remains inconsistent and often limited in scope. While existing research highlights strong associations between physicians’ uncertainty tolerance (UT) and healthcare outcomes, with low UT linked to excessive resource use and more paternalistic clinical styles, and high UT associated with reduced burnout and improved patient engagement [11], most educational interventions continue to focus narrowly on enhancing UT as a psychological trait, rather than addressing its broader relevance to clinical practice [12]. Few curriculum go beyond this to incorporate structured instruction in critical thinking, bias recognition, evidence-based reasoning, or communication skills, which are increasingly recognized as essential competencies for managing uncertainty in clinical practice [13]. Furthermore, recent evidence suggests that even when learners show positive cognitive or behavioral gains from educational interventions, they may concurrently experience negative emotional responses such as stress or anxiety. This paradox is particularly evident in high-stakes learning environments like simulation and assessment. Without integrating emotional regulation and resilience strategies, training may leave learners cognitively prepared but emotionally vulnerable [14]. This gap underscores the need for comprehensive curricula that address not only how learners think and act under uncertainty, but also how they feel.

To address these limitations, we developed a curriculum that integrates cognitive, emotional, and behavioral components for residents coping with uncertainty. Cognitive training included lectures and case-based discussions on ambiguity in diagnosis and treatment decision-making. Behavioral components involved role-playing and communication strategy exercises to promote adaptive responses to uncertainty. Emotional education emphasized the recognition, expression, and regulation of emotions during uncertain clinical situations. Reflective discussions, empathy exercises, and psychological safety promotion were used to help participants understand their own emotional responses and develop resilience. Details of these instructional strategies are outlined in Appendix 1: Teaching plan of how to cope with uncertainty. This study aimed to explore postgraduate GPs’ perspectives on MUT during standardized residency training in China, focusing on how such courses support uncertainty management and potential areas for improvement.

Methods

Study design and participants

This qualitative study utilized semi-structured interviews conducted six months after residents completed the course. The study adopted a process evaluation approach in medical education to examine participants’ motivations, training experiences, and perceived outcomes. The interview guide was developed based on the Donabedian structure–process–outcome framework and refined by two senior general practitioners. It included open-ended questions that encouraged participants to elaborate on their thoughts (Appendix 2).

Participants were selected through purposive sampling to ensure a diverse range of experiences from the three general practice training pathways within our institution: social-track residents, postgraduate medical students, and trainees commissioned by external hospitals. Among the 27 general practitioners invited, all social-track and postgraduate trainees consented to participate, while 9 of 21 commissioned trainees took part. This distribution ensured representation across key demographic and training categories. Data saturation was defined as the point at which no new codes or themes emerged; this occurred after the 13th interview and was confirmed by two subsequent interviews. No significant differences were identified between participants and non-participants, minimizing the likelihood of systematic selection bias.

Procedure

All interviews were conducted by two clinical educators (Yingshuai Zhao and Weili Shi) in Mandarin Chinese. Both had completed standardized residency training, accumulated over five years of clinical practice, and served as clinical instructors for more than two years. Their professional background facilitated rapport but also required careful reflexivity during the research process. Residents were invited in person after completing the course and again contacted by phone six months later. After informed consent, interviews were scheduled at convenient times and conducted either face-to-face in a private office or via secure video conferencing. Each interview lasted about 25 min. The interviewers guided the discussion using the interview guide, encouraged participants to express their views freely, and avoided evaluative comments. Each interview was audio-recorded with participants’ consent, and field notes were also taken to capture contextual information and non-verbal cues.

Data collection and analysis

All audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and reviewed by the interviewers. Transcripts were analyzed in Chinese to preserve the original meaning. A theory-informed coding strategy was employed using the Donabedian model, which categorizes educational quality evaluation into three domains: Structure, Process, and Outcome. An initial coding structure was developed deductively based on this framework, and then refined through inductive, line-by-line coding of the first five transcripts. Two researchers independently coded these transcripts, and discrepancies in code application were discussed and resolved through consensus. The corresponding author Liu-yi Wang reviewed the evolving codebook to ensure alignment with the study objectives and conceptual clarity. As coding progressed, sub-codes were iteratively clustered into subthemes and organized within their respective Donabedian domains. Notably, only one new code emerged during the 13th interview, and no additional codes were generated from the 14th and 15th interviews, indicating thematic saturation. The final codebook incorporated both a priori and emergent codes. For reporting, themes and selected illustrative quotations were translated into English by the first author. Google Translate was occasionally used as an auxiliary tool during this process, but all translations were manually revised and cross-checked by another bilingual team member to ensure semantic accuracy and cultural appropriateness. To ensure analytical credibility, a one page summary of the preliminary findings (major themes and illustrative quotes) was shared with all participants via We-chat. Besides, all final themes were validated through comparison across participant subgroups and reviewed by the full research team. Thematic coherence and representativeness were further confirmed by revisiting transcript excerpts and conducting peer debriefings. The final structure of themes and codingbook is detailed in Appendix 3.

Reflexivity considerations

The research team consisted of four general practitioners with backgrounds in clinical practice and medical education. Two members conducted the interviews and also served as instructors for the MUT course. This dual role may have fostered trust and rapport but could also have constrained participants’ willingness to share critical views. To mitigate this, the interviewers emphasized voluntarism, avoided evaluative or leading questions, and encouraged participants to express both supportive and negative experiences.

With respect to participants, all of them were early-career general practitioners in residency training. Their subordinate position within the training program might have limited their willingness to voice negative views. To address this, confidentiality was strictly ensured, and participants were reminded that their comments would remain anonymous and would not affect academic assessments or career progression. These steps were taken to minimize social desirability bias and strengthen the credibility of the findings.

The research team recognized that their prior clinical and teaching experiences could shape the interpretation of participants’ narratives. To minimize this influence, transcripts were independently coded by two researchers, and discrepancies were resolved through team discussions to surface and challenge assumptions. The evolving codebook was reviewed by the corresponding author to ensure alignment with study objectives, and analytic memos were used to document decision-making. In addition, some participants were invited to review transcript accuracy and theme selection, which not only provided an external check on credibility but also helped to reduce potential errors arising from transcription inaccuracies.

Ethical considerations

This study was part of the Graduate Education Reform and Curriculum Development Project (Project No. 040012023B064.) at Zhengzhou University and reviewed and approved by the Henan Provincial People’s Hospital, which is affiliated with the university. According to the guidelines, this research was classified as exempt from ethical review due to its use of anonymised secondary data that did not involve direct harmful interaction with human subjects. However, we explained voluntary participation, confidentiality, and potential risks and benefits to all participants. Informed consent was obtained from each participant before the study commenced. Participants were also reassured that choosing not to participate would not result in penalties or affect their grades, and that they could withdraw at any time without consequences.

Teaching structure

Theme 1: Necessity of the Course

Most participants acknowledged the ubiquity of uncertainty in clinical practice and emphasized the importance of formal training to address it. They believed that unmitigated uncertainty could impair clinical decision-making, delay treatment, and compromise patient outcomes. The MUT course was perceived as essential in equipping them with skills to manage such situations more effectively.

A key benefit cited was improved confidence in discussing uncertainty with patients, which enhanced trust, strengthened the doctor-patient relationship, and supported shared decision-making. Additionally, participants reported that the course helped alleviate stress and emotional distress associated with clinical ambiguity by providing structured coping strategies. Some residents also reframed uncertainty as a stimulus for self-improvement, recognizing that knowledge gaps and limited experience could contribute to their discomfort. This mindset shift further reinforced the perceived value of the training.

“…Before attending the course, I often felt hesitant and less confident when dealing with ambiguous diagnoses or treatment plans, which sometimes led to delays in patient management.(GP1)”.

“…Uncertainty in medical practice used to take a significant toll on my mood and behaviour. I often experienced anxiety and self-doubt, which sometimes translated into defensive medicine practices, such as ordering unnecessary tests to cover all bases. (GP6)”.

“…The MUT has been pivotal for my professional development. It has provided me with a framework to approach uncertainty not as a hindrance but as an integral part of medical practice. The skills and strategies I acquired through the course, such as reflective practice and evidence-based decision-making, have significantly enhanced my clinical competence.(GP2)”.

Theme 2: Comprehensive Curriculum Design

Most participants considered curriculum design essential in equipping healthcare providers with the skills to manage uncertainty effectively, rather than merely increasing their uncertainty tolerance. The basic skills participants mentioned include recognising the nature of medical uncertainty, handling diagnostic ambiguity, decision-making under uncertainty, and communication strategies for discussing uncertainty with patients.

Students also reported that MUT balances theoretical knowledge and practical application. Lectures provided the necessary theoretical framework, while case analyses, group discussions, and role-playing exercises allowed participants to apply these concepts in real-world scenarios.

“…The MUT course thoroughly covered key topics essential for managing uncertainty in clinical practice. From understanding the sources and dimensions of medical uncertainty to learning about cognitive, and behavioural responses, the course content was comprehensive and well-structured.(GP2)”.

“…The theoretical sessions provided a solid foundation, explaining the concepts and frameworks for understanding medical uncertainty. However, the practical exercises like role-playing, case analysis, and group discussions really enhanced my learning experience. I think we need a platform such as WeChat for post-course discussions.(GP11)”.

Teaching process

Theme 3: Flexibility and Accessibility

The MUT course was designed to accommodate the busy schedules of healthcare providers. For participants, the flexible scheduling options allowed them to attend sessions without disrupting their clinical responsibilities. However, many students noted that the course should be offered before entering the standardised residency training base.

“…The accessibility of this course was a significant advantage, as it allowed me to attend sessions without disrupting my clinical responsibilities.(GP4)”.

“…I think the course should be offered before we enter the standardised residency training base. Having this training earlier would provide a strong foundation for managing medical uncertainty right from the start of our residency.(GP5)”.

Theme 4: Continuous Feedback and Reflection

Most participants reported that MUT included regular and constructive feedback sessions with instructors and peers. These sessions provided insights into their performance, highlighted areas for improvement, and reinforced positive behaviours. Participants were encouraged to engage in self-reflection exercises, which involved analysing their experiences and identifying lessons learned. These exercises helped them internalise the training content and apply it to their clinical practice. The courses also fostered a collaborative environment where participants could give and receive feedback from their peers. This was valuable for gaining different perspectives and learning from others’ experiences. Many emphasised the importance of an iterative learning process, where participants could continually improve their skills through repeated practice, feedback, and reflection.

“…During the Medical Uncertainty Training (MUT) course, I regularly received constructive feedback from both instructors and peers. These sessions provided invaluable insights into my performance, highlighting areas for improvement and reinforcing positive behaviours.(GP4)”.

“…By reflecting on my actions and decisions, I could better understand my strengths and areas where I needed improvement. This process of self-assessment was instrumental in helped me integrate the principles of uncertainty management into my daily practice.(GP8)”.

Teaching outcomes

Theme 5: Enhanced Clinical Judgment

Participants reported that MUT significantly improved their clinical judgment by enhancing their critical thinking and decision-making skills. They noted that this training enabled them to evaluate clinical situations more effectively and make better-informed decisions, leading to improved patient outcomes and overall quality of care. Many mentioned that the course improved their shared decision-making skills, allowing them to involve patients more in their treatment plans, which enhanced patient trust and satisfaction. They also highlighted that learning risk assessment skills helped them avoid medical negligence by making more balanced and informed decisions. Finally, they felt more confident in handling uncertainty, understanding it as an inherent part of clinical practice.

“…I particularly appreciated how the training improved my shared decision-making skills, allowing me to involve patients more in their treatment plans, which has greatly enhanced patient trust and satisfaction…Learning risk assessment skills also helped me avoid medical negligence by making more balanced and informed decisions.(GP5).

Theme 6: Cognitive, Emotional, and Behavioral Transformation

The MUT curriculum was designed to promote integrated changes across cognitive, emotional, and behavioral dimensions. Participants reported that the course deepened their understanding of uncertainty as an inherent part of clinical practice and equipped them with critical thinking strategies, techniques for managing cognitive biases, and structured decision-making tools. These cognitive gains supported more rational and measured clinical judgments.

Emotional resilience was another core outcome. Through stress management strategies and mindfulness exercises, trainees developed the capacity to remain calm and composed during ambiguous clinical situations. This emotional regulation contributed to improved psychological well-being and reduced reliance on defensive practices.

Behaviorally, participants described becoming more confident and proactive in their decision-making, exhibiting greater willingness to collaborate and discuss uncertainty transparently with colleagues and patients. This interplay of cognitive insight, emotional awareness, and adaptive action led to a reinforcing cycle of growth and resilience in their professional roles. Many mentioned that the course also improved their shared decision-making skills, allowing them to involve patients more in their treatment plans, which enhanced patient trust and satisfaction. They also highlighted that learning risk assessment skills helped them avoid medical negligence by making more balanced and informed decisions. Finally, they felt more confident in handling uncertainty, understanding it as an inherent part of clinical practice (Fig. 1).

“…The course highlighted the importance of recognising and accepting uncertainty as an inherent part of medical practice. This awareness helped me adopt a more measured and rational approach, reducing the likelihood of engaging in defensive or overly cautious medical practices.” (GP1).

“…MUT provided strategies for maintaining professional integrity and making informed decisions without resorting to unnecessary interventions. This approach has made me more confident in my clinical judgments and less prone to doubt my decisions.” (GP12).

“…One of the most valuable aspects of the MUT course was learning how to maintain composure in the face of uncertainty. By practicing these techniques, I’ve become more adept at handling difficult situations without letting stress or anxiety affect my performance.” (GP3).

“…This perspective shift has been incredibly liberating. I no longer see uncertainty as a personal failure but as an opportunity for growth and learning.” (GP14).

Theme 7: Real-World Application Challenges

Despite the benefits, participants noted several challenges in applying the training in real-world scenarios. They noted varying levels of support from colleagues and the healthcare system, differences in patient populations, and complexity of integrating new skills into established routines as significant challenges. Adequate medical technical support including access to up-to-date medical information, diagnostic tools, and technical staff was often lacking. Furthermore, the variability in patient backgrounds, conditions, and preferences made it difficult to apply standardised approaches, necessitating tailored strategies that could be challenging to manage.

“…I faced several challenges when applying the training in real-world scenarios. One of the main issues was the varying levels of support from colleagues and the healthcare system.(GP10)”.

Subgroup variation

Thematic patterns remained largely consistent across participant subgroups. Commissioned and social-track trainees emphasized the course’s practical relevance, particularly its impact on real-time clinical decision-making and patient communication. In contrast, master’s degree students were more inclined to highlight the course’s theoretical underpinnings and its role in reshaping their cognitive approach to uncertainty. These differences represent variations in emphasis rather than substantive disagreement, suggesting that educational background may shape how participants interpret and internalize the training content. Nonetheless, all subgroups affirmed the course’s overall value and effectiveness, reinforcing the coherence and generalizability of the identified themes.

Discussion

Main findings

This study explored post-graduate GPs’ experiences and perceptions regarding MUT. Participants reported acquiring key competencies such as critical thinking, clinical decision-making, and emotional regulation, which improved their ability to manage complex clinical cases, engage effectively in shared decision-making, and cope confidently with uncertainty in routine practice. While these perceived improvements suggest a potential enhancement of clinical practice, further research is required to evaluate the direct impact on patient outcomes and healthcare quality.

An important insight arising from this study is the shift in how residents conceptualized medical uncertainty following MUT. Unlike traditional educational approaches, which have typically emphasized uncertainty tolerance as a psychological trait aimed at reducing anxiety [15], residents in this study viewed uncertainty as an active catalyst for professional development. MUT encouraged them to perceive ambiguity not merely as a threat but as an opportunity for clinical growth and skill enhancement. By acquiring structured cognitive strategies for mitigating biases, emotional techniques for regulating stress responses, and practical behavioral methods to enhance communication with patients, participants transitioned from a passive tolerance approach toward active uncertainty management. This paradigm shift aligns with emerging frameworks such as the Erasmus + PUNC model, which defines uncertainty competence as an integrated skill set—acknowledging, exploring, and productively handling uncertainty across cognitive, emotional, and behavioral dimensions [16]. Participants highlighted that this comprehensive, multidimensional approach provided tangible benefits, including improved diagnostic reasoning, reduced reliance on defensive medical practices, and increased professional satisfaction. These outcomes underline the value of structured training programs that directly address the multifaceted nature of clinical uncertainty, suggesting that future medical education curricula should incorporate explicit strategies for building uncertainty management capabilities [17].

Theoretical and practical integration​

This study identified three interrelated mechanisms through which the Medical Uncertainty Training (MUT) course supported residents’ development: cognitive reinforcement, emotional recalibration, and behavioral adaptation.

Cognitive reinforcement: enhancing reflective reasoning

Cognitive processes are fundamental to how clinicians interpret and respond to uncertainty. Clinical ambiguity often arises from incomplete information, overlapping symptoms, and probabilistic outcomes, all of which require deliberate reasoning rather than intuitive judgment [18]. In the MUT course, cognitive reinforcement was achieved through a combination of conceptual instruction and applied learning. Participants were introduced to the definition, sources, and clinical implications of medical uncertainty, followed by exercises that emphasized bias recognition, structured case analysis, and reflective reasoning. This approach promoted cognitive flexibility and metacognitive awareness, helping participants recognize and question initial diagnostic assumptions. As one participant noted, “I now consciously question my initial diagnoses.” These findings are consistent with previous research highlighting the value of structured reflection in reducing diagnostic error [19]. By integrating theory with practical application, the training facilitated a shift from intuitive to analytical thinking during ambiguous clinical encounters.

Emotional recalibration: building resilience

Managing clinical uncertainty also requires emotional regulation. The MUT course incorporated mindfulness, peer reflection, and cognitive-behavioral strategies to help participants recognize and manage emotional responses triggered by ambiguity. Residents reported increased composure and reduced anxiety during uncertain scenarios, suggesting an improvement in emotional resilience. This aligns with the broaden-and-build theory [20], which posits that positive emotional experiences can enhance coping and adaptability. Peer discussion was particularly effective in normalizing uncertainty, consistent with findings from Weurlander et al. (2019), who demonstrated that shared emotional reflection improves well-being and reduces burnout in medical trainees [21]. These elements reflect a more holistic strategy in uncertainty management that attends to both the cognitive and affective demands of clinical care [22].

Behavioral adaptation: translating insight into action

The integration of cognitive and emotional growth was reflected in participants’ behavioral changes. Through role-play and communication skills training, residents practiced strategies for disclosing uncertainty to patients and engaging in shared decision-making. These skills correspond to evidence-based frameworks such as the SPIKES protocol [23] and support a shift from defensive to collaborative care. This behavioral transition is further explained by adaptive expertise theory [24], which emphasizes the importance of applying core skills flexibly in novel clinical contexts. Participants described greater confidence in managing uncertainty, leading to clearer communication and reduced reliance on defensive medical practices.

Implications for medical education

The evaluation of the MUT course emphasized the need for comprehensive training which could equip healthcare providers to handle clinical uncertainty effectively [13]. Integrating uncertainty management into medical curricula is vital, as traditional education often neglects this aspect.By incorporating modules on critical thinking, decision-making, and stress management, one integrated curriculum contained cognitive, emotional and behavioral education is constructed which can better prepare students for real-world challenges. Continuous feedback and self-reflection are essential for fostering a growth mindset and lifelong learning, with regular feedback sessions guiding performance improvements. Flexibility in scheduling and accessible resources, such as online modules and recorded lectures, are necessary to accommodate busy medical professionals. Introducing the course early in medical training can maximize its impact. Addressing real-world application challenges requires robust support systems, including adequate technical support and up-to-date information access. Preparing students to adapt their approaches to diverse patient needs ensures high-quality care. Future research should focus on validating the MUT course’s effectiveness across diverse settings and its long-term impact, using larger sample sizes and extended follow-up periods. Integrating these findings into medical education will better prepare healthcare providers to deliver patient-centered care in a dynamic healthcare landscape.

Limitations

The study had several limitations that may impact the generalisability of the findings. First, given that the MUT course was part of a teaching reform project, participants’ age range was relatively concentrated, meaning most were likely at a similar career stage. This may have influenced the results, as the experiences and perceptions of a more diverse age group were not fully represented. Another limitation was the lack of a control group, making it difficult to attribute the observed improvements solely to MUT. Furthermore, the short duration of the follow-up period did not enable assessing the long-term impact of the training on participants’ clinical practice. Last, this study was conducted at a single centre, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Consequently, the applicability of the findings to other settings or institutions may be limited. Future research should consider multi-centre studies to validate and expand these results.

Conclusion

This study found that the Medical Uncertainty Training (MUT) course supported postgraduate GPs in developing practical skills to manage clinical uncertainty. By combining cognitive, emotional, and behavioral components, the course improved participants’ confidence and competence in uncertain situations. These findings highlight the value of integrating structured uncertainty management into residency training and suggest that course design should align with learners’ needs and clinical realities.
