Defining Cardiomyocyte Repolarization Response to Pharmacotherapy in Long‐QT Syndrome Type 3
Authors: Ning Ge, Rui Li, Min Liu, Wenxin Xia, Stephen T. O'Brien, Veronica McInerney, Joseph Galvin, Deirdre Ward, Catherine McGorrian, Timothy O'Brien, Sanbing Shen, Terence W. Prendiville
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLong‐QT syndrome is a primary cardiac ion channelopathy predisposing a patient to ventricular arrhythmia through delayed repolarization on the resting ECG. We aimed to establish a patient‐specific, human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)‐derived cardiomyocytes model of long‐QT syndrome type 3 (LQT3) using clustered regularly interspaced palindromic repeats (CRISPR/Cas9), for disease modeling and drug challenge.Methods and ResultsHiPSCs were generated from a patient with LQT3 harboring an SCN5A pathogenic variant (c.1231G>A; p.Val411Met), and an unrelated healthy control. The same SCN5A pathogenic variant was engineered into the background healthy control hiPSCs via CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing to generate a second disease model of LQT3 for comparison with an isogenic control. All 3 hiPSC lines were differentiated into cardiomyocytes. Both the patient‐derived LQT3 (SCN5A +/−) and genetically engineered LQT3 (SCN5A +/−) hiPSC‐derived cardiomyocytes showed significantly prolonged cardiomyocyte repolarization compared with the healthy control. Mexiletine, a cardiac voltage‐gated sodium channel (NaV1.5) blocker, shortened repolarization in both patient‐derived LQT3 and genetically engineered LQT3 hiPSC‐derived cardiomyocytes, but had no effect in the control. Notably, calcium channel blockers nifedipine and verapamil showed a dose‐dependent shortening of repolarization, rescuing the phenotype. Additionally, therapeutic drugs known to prolong the corrected QT in humans (ondansetron, clarithromycin, and sotalol) demonstrated this effect in vitro, but the LQT3 clones were not more disproportionately affected compared with the control.ConclusionsWe demonstrated that patient‐derived and genetically engineered LQT3 hiPSC‐derived cardiomyocytes faithfully recapitulate pathologic characteristics of LQT3. The clinical significance of such an in vitro model is in the exploration of novel therapeutic strategies, stratifying drug adverse reaction risk and potentially facilitating a more targeted, patient‐specific approach in high‐risk patients with LQT3.

Congenital long‐QT syndrome (LQTS) is the most common and familiar life‐threatening primary cardiac arrhythmia syndrome that can present with recurrent syncope or, rarely, sudden cardiac death from ventricular arrhythmia. LQTS, mechanistically, is due to a cardiac ion channelopathy that has a net effect of delayed ventricular repolarization at a myocyte level manifesting as a prolonged QT interval on a resting surface ECG. 1 It is present in 1 out of every 2000 people in the population. LQTS type 3 (LQT3), the most malignant subtype, accounts for approximately 5% to 10% of all LQTS cases. 2 It is due to a gain‐of‐function pathogenic variant in the SCN5A gene, which encodes for the α subunit of the cardiac voltage‐gated sodium (INa) channel protein (NaV1.5). 3

Human myocardial tissue is technically invasive to biopsy and has a small but not negligible risk of harm during sample acquisition to the patient. Biopsy‐derived myocardial tissue survives poorly in an in vitro culture when attempted. 4 A cellular model using patient‐specific human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) that are subsequently differentiated into functional cardiomyocytes (human‐induced, pluripotent, stem cell‐derived cardiomyocytes [hiPSC‐CMs]) offers a viable solution to this limitation in in vivo sample acquisition. 5 , 6 To date, numerous reports have established that hiPSC‐CMs can recapitulate the pathognomonic characteristics of individuals with different subtypes of LQTS. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10

Genetic background in any given patient with a specific LQTS pathogenic variant influences the expression of disease in that individual and their response to therapy or drug interactions. One strategy to potentially control for this disease expression bias is to either introduce the pathogenic variant into an unrelated healthy control or to generate an isogenic control in the disease cells that differs only in the pathogenic nucleotide of interest. The creation of isogenic hiPSCs with single nucleotide changes using clustered regularly interspaced palindromic repeats (CRISPR/Cas9) allows a direct comparison of phenotype at a cellular level.

The primary aim of our proposal was to use hiPSC and CRISPR/Cas9 technology to recapitulate the phenotype of LQT3 in vitro from a symptomatic patient with a severe phenotype of disease. We report the successful modeling of LQT3 from patient‐derived hiPSC‐CMs harboring the SCN5A‐c.1231G>A (p.Val411Met) pathogenic variant, and the subsequent generation of genetically engineered LQT3 hiPSC‐CMs (SCN5A‐Val411Met) in an unrelated healthy control hiPSC clone to generate a disease and isogenic model via CRISPR/Cas9. The advantage of such a described model is to independently validate the disease phenotype for a given pathogenic variant of interest in a patient and to study the variant‐specific response to pharmacotherapy, controlling for genetic background.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Detailed descriptions of materials and methods are available in Data S1.

Patient Recruitment

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Galway University Hospitals (C.A.750). Clinical data and skin samples were taken following written informed consent. The clinical ECGs of the patient and control are reported in Data S1.

Generation of LQT3 Patient‐Derived hiPSCs, Establishment of the Genetically Engineered LQT3 hiPSCs, and Cardiac Differentiation

Patient‐specific hiPSCs were generated from a patient with LQT3 harboring the SCN5A‐c.1231G>A (p.Val411Met) pathogenic variant by reprogramming of the skin biopsy‐derived dermal fibroblasts. HiPSC clones generated from a healthy individual 11 were used as controls. The SCN5A variant was introduced in a control hiPSC clone using a ribonucleoprotein‐based CRISPR/Cas9 technique with the protocol described in our previous publication. 12 Briefly, a single‐stranded guide RNA (sgRNA) flanking the target variant site was designed using the optimized CRISPR design software tool (Integrated DNA Technologies). The sgRNA was synthesized using the Precision gRNA Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen). Additionally, a sequence‐matched 100‐nucleotide (nt) donor single‐stranded oligonucleotide flanking the nucleotide of interest was acquired with the insertion of the mutant target nucleotide (to generate the SCN5A disease allele). The synthesized sgRNA, Cas9 protein, and single‐stranded oligonucleotide template were electroporated into control hiPSCs using the Neon Transfection System (Invitrogen).

Furthermore, these hiPSC clones between passage 30 and 40 were differentiated into cardiac lineage using a PSC cardiomyocyte differentiation kit (Gibco). The primers used for reverse transcription‐polymerase chain reaction, quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction, and sequencing of on‐target site are listed in Table S1.

Flow Cytometry, Quantitative Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reaction, and Immunocytochemistry

The positive population of cardiac troponin T was performed using flow cytometry. The expression of pluripotency markers in hiPSCs and the expression of cardiac, sarcomere, and ion channel‐related markers in hiPSC‐CMs were determined by using quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (for RNA level) and immunofluorescence staining (for protein level). The primers and antibodies used are listed in Tables S1 and S2.

Electrophysiological Analysis in hiPSC‐CMs

We used the Maestro multielectrode array (MEA) system (Axion BioSystems) in electrophysiology recording. AxIS (version 2.5.1.10) and the CiPA analysis tool (version 3.1.8) were used to analyze the data.

The QT interval in humans is the surface ECG surrogate of cardiac ventricular repolarization. This QT measurement is directly affected by heart rate (shortening further at higher heart rates), and therefore a correction for heart rate is required to compare one ECG to another or one patient to another. There are several established formulas to correct for heart rate, and the Axion BioSystems system uses the Fridericia correction formula (see Data S1).

Field potential duration (FPD) recorded from contracting hiPSC‐derived cardiomyocytes on the MEA platform is an in vitro surrogate of the cardiomyocyte repolarization time. Similar to the QT interval on the human ECG, this FPD value is directly affected by the beat period (the time between contraction waves of the hiPSC‐CMs in culture). The FPD normally shortens with higher beat periods, but in the LQTS disease model, this proportionate shortening with higher beat periods is pathologically prolonged. We recorded the beat period at the time of FPD acquisition to calculate the corrected FPD (FPDc) using the Fridericia correction formula.

Several drug compounds with varying known effects on cardiac ion channel function were selected to investigate the ability of the in vitro phenotype to replicate disease in humans (Table S3). The dosage effect on cardiac repolarization of any given drug was compared between the 2 LQT3 models (patient and engineered) and the control clone response. In this way, the modifying effect of genetic background on response to drug exposure could be studied between the patient‐derived LQT3 model and between the genetically engineered LQT3 model along with its isogenic control.

Statistical Analysis

Results are presented as mean±SEM. Statistical comparisons were performed using SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM, Armonk, NY), and the figures were generated using GraphPad Prism 8 for Windows (GraphPad Software). The FPDc and beat period data of different groups were statistically analyzed using a 2‐sided unpaired Student t test. The FPDc data upon drug treatment and SCN5A gene expression in hiPSCs were statistically analyzed using nonparametric tests (2‐sample Fisher‐Pitman permutation). P<0.05, P<0.01, P<0.001, and P<0.0001 were considered significant.

Results

Clinical History of Donor Patient With LQT3 Phenotype

Our donor fibroblast sample was obtained from a 43‐year‐old female patient with a severe phenotype of LQTS. She had a history of multiple unprovoked syncopal events starting from childhood. Family history was notable for a similarly affected and symptomatic daughter with documented ventricular arrhythmia, syncope, and seizure. Her 12‐lead surface ECG revealed a markedly prolonged corrected QT of up to 500 ms, with a T‐wave morphology consistent with LQTS and suspicious for LQT3 (Figure 1A, Figure S1). The patient was started on β‐blockade (bisoprolol), and given the high degree of clinical concern, a continuous telemetry monitor (Reveal LINQ; Medtronic) was implanted subcutaneously. The patient was confirmed to harbor a pathogenic variant in SCN5A (heterozygous for c.1231G>A p.Val411Met) (Figure 1B; Figure S2) confirming the clinical diagnosis genetically. This is a single nucleotide substitution, resulting in a gain‐of‐function effect in the sodium cardiac ion channel that prolongs ventricular repolarization. Total sodium cardiac ion channel expression is not expected to be affected by this pathogenic variant. This variant involves the exchange of 2 nonpolar amino acids and is highly conserved across species and lies within a functionally important transmembrane domain. Functional studies also have shown this variant has an effect on protein function in vivo. 13 Polymorphic ventricular tachycardia was subsequently detected on the patient's implanted rhythm monitor, and following a lignocaine drug challenge confirming therapeutic shortening of the corrected QT from 508 to 470 ms, the patient was started on the sodium channel blocker mexiletine.

The unrelated healthy control, a 47‐year‐old woman, was clinically well and had a normal corrected QT of 362 ms on ECG confirmed. She was also confirmed to not harbor the specific SCN5A variant c.1231G>A (Figure 1B, Figure S2) and was documented to have a normal karyotype.

Generation and Characterization of Patient‐Derived LQT3 hiPSCs

Three separate and independent hiPSC clones were generated from both the patient with LQT3 dermal fibroblasts and from the healthy, unrelated control using non‐integrating Sendai virus. 11 The pluripotency of all hiPSC clones was confirmed by expression of pluripotent stem cell markers (OCT4 [POU class 5 homeobox 1], TRA‐1‐60 [Tumor‐related antigen 1‐60], SOX2 [SRY‐box transcription factor 2], TRA‐1‐81 [Tumor‐related antigen 1‐81], NANOG [Nanog homeobox], and SSEA4 [stage‐specific embryonic antigen 4]) at the mRNA and protein level (Figure 2A, Figure S3A). All hiPSC clones were confirmed to have the capacity to spontaneously differentiate in vitro into cells from all 3 germ layers including endoderm (SOX17 [SRY‐box transcription factor 17]), mesoderm (α‐SMA [α‐smooth muscle actin]), and ectoderm (NESTIN [Neuroepithelial stem cell protein]) (Figure S3B). Patient and control hiPSCs displayed normal karyotype and no gross chromosomal disruption during reprogramming (Figure S4). The fingerprinting results for 16 polymorphic microsatellite loci showed the hiPSC clones and their parental fibroblasts matched up on their identical profiles (Table S4). The preservation of the heterozygous SCN5A variant, c.1231G>A, was confirmed in the LQT3 patient‐derived hiPSC clones (Figure S2).

Establishment of Genetically Engineered LQT3 iPSCs

We used the CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing system to establish the isogenic SCN5A‐pathogenic variant hiPSC model. We designed a highly specific sgRNA targeting exon 9 of the SCN5A gene and electroporated hiPSCs with sgRNA, Cas9 protein, and a single‐stranded oligonucleotide template containing the mutant nucleotide of interest. After on‐target Sanger sequencing, 3 genetically engineered clones with the LQT3 patient‐specific pathogenic variant (heterozygous for SCN5A c.1231G>A) were selected (Figure S2). All 3 gene edited clones were assessed by Sanger sequencing for the top 14 most likely potential off‐target effects, with no insertions or deletions detected at any of the genomic loci examined. 12 Furthermore, molecular karyotyping indicated that these 3 gene edited hiPSC clones showed normal karyotype and retained pluripotency markers and spontaneous differentiation potential (Figures S3 and S4; Table S4). In addition, no differences in the expression levels of the SCN5A gene were observed by real‐time polymerase chain reaction in either the control or disease clonal hiPSC lines (P>0.05) (Figure S5).

Characterization of hiPSC‐CMs

All experimental hiPSC clones, including the healthy control, the patient‐derived LQT3 clones, and the genetically engineered LQT3 clones, were differentiated into cardiomyocytes (Figure 2B; Figure S6A). Spontaneously contracting syncytia were typically observed from day 8 to day 10 of differentiation. Flow cytometry analysis for the cardiomyocyte marker cardiac troponin T indicated that all of the clones differentiated with similar efficiency on day 14 of differentiation, with an average 60% to 70% of the cells being cardiac troponin T+ (Figure 2C, Figure S6B).

The quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction results on differentiation day 28 confirmed that all hiPSC‐CMs expressed high levels of cardiomyocyte marker gene TNNT2 and sarcomere marker gene ACTN2 (Figure 2D). In addition, all hiPSC‐CMs showed similar expression level for cardiac development marker genes (MEF2C, GATA4, TBX5, NKX2.5, NPPA, NPPB), sarcomere marker genes (ACTN2, ACTA2), cardiomyocyte‐specific genes (TNNT2, TNNI1, TNNI3, MYH7, MYH6), ventricular and atrial subtypes of cardiomyocytes genes (MYL2, MYL7, TBX18), cardiac muscle sarcoplasmic reticulum gene RYR2, and LQTS subtype‐related K+/Na+/Ca2+ ion channel genes (KCNQ1, KCNH2, SCN5A, ANK2, KCNE1, KCNJ2, CACNA1C, CAV3, SCN4B, AKAP9, SNTA1, KCNJ5, CALM1, CALM2) (Figure S6C).

The monolayer‐differentiated cells on day 28 were examined using immunofluorescence staining to assess the protein expression levels of the cardiomyocyte‐associated markers troponin T (TNNT2), sarcomere‐associated marker α‐actinin (ACTN2), and ventricular cardiomyocyte subtype‐associated marker ventricular myosin light chain MLC‐2v (MYL2). All hiPSC‐CMs showed high expression levels for screened cardiac markers (Figure 2E, Figure S6D).

Electrophysiological Phenotype of SCN5A‐Val411Met hiPSC‐CMs

We investigated the electrophysiological characteristics of (patient/genetically engineered) LQT3 and control hiPSC‐CMs using the MEA platform. In the data analysis, 1 electrode from each well (16 electrodes in each well) was selected as the golden electrode for FPD recording, an electrode that has an optimal repolarization signal across all conditions.

The QT interval in clinical practice is calculated on an ECG using the tangent method, 14 in which the end of the QT interval is defined as the point at which the tangent line extrapolated from the steepest downslope of the T wave intersects the isoelectric baseline. We sought to apply this same accurate methodology to the data we acquired in vitro. This technique provided us with much more consistent and reproducible data than the commonly used analysis method based on maximal T‐wave amplitude and mechanistically better aligned with clinical practice on ECG interpretation. Therefore, the duration from the onset of the depolarization spike (analogous to the QRS complex) to the end of the T wave (red vertical line) was the method of FPD measurement in our study (Figure 3A). 15

Sample FPDs recorded from both the patient with LQT3 hiPSC‐CMs and the genetically engineered LQT3 hiPSC‐CMs displayed a prolonged field potential duration wave morphology, analogous to the T‐wave morphology on the patient with LQT3 donor's ECG (Figure 3A). The MEA data recorded between differentiation day 20 and 50 were summarized. There was also a significantly shorter beat period of contracting cardiomyocytes observed between cases (patient with LQT3 and genetically engineered LQT3) and control (P<0.0001) (Figure 3C).

Pharmacological Response of hiPSC‐CMs

To study drug exposure and drug response, at least 1 representative clone was selected from the control cell line (clone 1), the patient with LQT3 cell line (clone 1), and the genetically engineered LQT3 cell line (clones 1 and 2). These selected clones were chosen based on reviewing the FPDc data for each clone (see Figure S7).

Response to Cardiac Sodium Channel (INa)‐Specific Blocker Mexiletine

We studied the effects of the NaV1.5 (INa)‐specific blocker mexiletine and its effects on beat‐period corrected repolarization (FPDc). Mexiletine is a class Ib antiarrhythmic drug that exerts its effects through inhibiting the NaV1.5 and hERG (human Ether‐à‐go‐go‐Related Gene) channel, and thus is recommended as therapy in patients with a gain‐of‐function pathogenic variant in SCN5A (eg, LQT3). 16 Our result revealed that mexiletine showed an expected dose‐dependent shortening of the FPDc (beat‐period corrected repolarization) on all hiPSC‐CMs tested with SCN5A +/− variant including the patient‐derived LQT3 clones and the genetically engineered LQT3 clones (P<0.05) (Figure 4A, Figure S8). Interestingly, mexiletine exposure at higher doses went so far as to entirely correct the FPDc to the same extent as the control sample at concentrations of 10 and 30 μmol for the LQT3 patient‐derived hiPSC‐CMs.

When FPDc values were normalized to the baseline FPDc measurement for each tested clone, patient and genetically engineered LQT3 hiPSC‐CMs showed a clear shortening of FPDc on exposure to mexiletine compared with the healthy control clone (Figure 4H). Interestingly, the healthy control clone showed a slight but statistically significant prolongation of the FPDc from 3 to 10 μmol (106.8±1.5% and 109.1±2.2%, P<0.01). This result suggests that mexiletine has no repolarization shortening effect in healthy controls, and that its effect is specific to those LQT3 disease samples. The genetic background influenced the magnitude of the effect, with the genetically engineered LQT3 clones showing a less dramatic response compared with the patient‐derived LQT3 clones. The effect in the genetically engineered sample was, however, statistically significant compared with the isogenic control, confirming the influence of the particular single nucleotide substitution (c.1231G>A) in the disease phenotype.

Response to Cardiac Rapid Delayed Rectifier Potassium Channel Current (IKr)‐Specific Blocker E‐4031

E‐4031 is a selective hERG channel blocker commonly used in type 2 LQTS‐specific IKr cardiac ion current research. 17 We demonstrated a dose‐dependent prolongation of the FPDc on all the hiPSC‐CMs tested when exposed to E‐4031 (P<0.05) (Figure 4B, Figure S9). Both the patient‐derived LQT3 and the genetically engineered LQT3 (SCN5A +/−) hiPSC‐CMs developed beating arrest after 30 minutes of incubation at 30 nmol of E‐4031.

When all tested clones were normalized to their baseline FPDc value, the effect of E‐4031 drug exposure was nearly entirely uniform across all concentrations, inferring a drug effect that was independent of the LQT3 variant (Figure 4I). Thus, although the absolute repolarization values (FPDc) were clearly higher for the disease samples (patient and genetically engineered) for any given drug concentration, the proportionate change in repolarization duration (FPDc) between disease and control samples with escalating drug dosage was nearly identical. This intuitively makes sense, because the selective effect of E‐4031 on hERG channels is entirely independent of the sodium (SCN5A) channels. It is interesting to note, however, that having LQT3 disease did not disproportionately affect the degree of repolarization delay from E‐4031 exposure.

Response to Calcium Channel Blockade With Nifedipine and Verapamil

We examined the effects of 2 different classes of calcium channel blocker drugs with a reported low risk of clinical harm for patients with LQTS. Nifedipine is an L‐type, dihydropyridine, Ca2+ channel blocker that has been used as a vasodilating drug for high blood pressure and to treat ventricular tachyarrhythmias. 18 Verapamil is a class IV antiarrhythmic, nondihydropyridine Ca2+ channel blocker, with overlapping hERG and L‐type Ca2+ channels inhibition. 19

Nifedipine showed a dose‐dependent shortening (from 10 nmol to 10 μmol) of the FPDc on all hiPSC‐CMs tested compared with baseline (pre‐exposure) values (P<0.05) (Figure 4C, Figure S10). At nifedipine concentrations of 100 nmol and 1 μmol, respectively, the patient with LQT3 and genetically engineered clones normalized their repolarizations values (FPDc) the same as the control, thus inferring a complete rescue of disease phenotype at these higher drug concentrations.

When FPDc was normalized for the initial baseline value, the healthy control hiPSC‐CMs showed less sensitivity on decreasing of FPDc from 10 nmol to 1 μmol nifedipine concentrations than the patient and genetically engineered LQT3 hiPSC‐CMs, thus inferring a greater effect of the drug on the LQT3 disease model over the control (Figure 4J). Also, there was a relatively equal effect on both the patient‐derived clones and the genetically engineered clones, suggesting no significant modifying effect from the genetic background between these 2 samples.

Verapamil showed a similar dose‐dependent shortening of the FPDc from 1 nmol to 1 μmol on all clones tested compared with the initial baseline value (P<0.05) (Figure 4D, Figure S10). At 1 to 3 nmol, verapamil showed minimal effect on FPDc. Again, the effect on repolarization values (FPDc) were most notable in the disease LQT3 clones over control, and this is best seen on the graph of the FPDc values normalized for the initial baseline value (Figure 4K).

This experimental data support the concept that calcium channel blockers may have a therapeutic role in LQT3.

Response to 2 Commonly Prescribed Drugs, Ondansetron and Clarithromycin, Both Contraindicated for Patients With LQT3

We studied the effects of the serotonin‐receptor blocker and an antiemetic drug ondansetron, 20 and a commonly prescribed antibiotic, clarithromycin, 21 due to their widely reported increased arrhythmia risk in patients with LQTS. Both drugs displayed a dose‐dependent prolongation on the FPDc.

Ondansetron showed a dose‐dependent prolongation of the FPDc from 0.1 to 100 μmol in the healthy control patient with LQT3 sample (SCN5A +/−) and genetically engineered LQT3 sample (SCN5A +/−) hiPSC‐CMs compared with their baseline resting values (P<0.05) (Figure 4E, Figure S11). The proportionate effect on prolonging repolarization (FPDc) with escalating drug doses of ondansetron appeared to have little difference between disease and control samples or between patient or genetically engineered LQT3 samples (Figure 4L).

Clarithromycin showed a similar dose‐dependent prolongation of the FPDc from 0.1 to 30 μmol on all tested clones compared with baseline values (P<0.05) (Figure 4F, Figure S11). Genetic background appeared to be a little more contributary to the effects of higher doses of clarithromycin (1–3 μmol) between the patient‐derived LQT3 clone and the genetically engineered LQT3 clone, as illustrated by the statistical significance of the FPDc compared with the control (Figure 4F). Again, when FPDc values were normalized to their baseline values, the proportional effect of escalating doses of clarithromycin appeared to be most notable for the control over disease clones, inferring no disproportionate effect from LQT3 disease (Figure 4M).

Response to Sotalol, Strongly Contraindicated for Patients With LQTS

Finally, we investigated the effects of sotalol, a mixed class II and class III antiarrhythmic drug with a known high risk of harm from induced ventricular arrhythmia in patients with LQTS.

Sotalol showed a dose‐dependent prolongation of the FPDc from 0.1 to 30 μmol on all clones compared with the baseline (P<0.05), with even a low concentration (0.1 and 0.3 μmol) of sotalol showing significantly increased FPDc (P<0.01) on control hiPSC‐CMs (Figure 4G, Figure S12). When the FPDc values were normalized to their baseline values, there was relatively little difference in proportional change with escalating doses of sotalol up to 3 μmol in any tested clone, inferring little genetic background modifying effect (Figure 4N).

Discussion

In this study, we generated hiPSC‐CMs from a control subject, a patient with LQT3, and a genetically engineered LQT3 clone (SCN5A +/−), recapitulating the electrophysiological phenotype using an MEA platform. These results demonstrate the usefulness of the hiPSC‐CM model and CRISPR/Cas9 in LQTS disease modeling, genetic variant screening, and the response to targeted pharmacotherapies.

Since the first published report of a hiPSC clone generated from the dermal fibroblasts of a patient with type 1 LQTS harboring a heterozygous KCNQ1‐Arg190Gln pathogenic variant, 7 others have reported the disease‐modeling potential of hiPSC‐CMs in patients with LQTS harboring KCNQ1/KCNH2/SCN5A pathogenic variants. 9 , 22 , 23 , 24 The challenges of disease expression and penetrance in genetic‐mediated disease are influenced by genetic background, epigenetic variants, and environmental factors between subjects. 25 To overcome this confounder, isogenic hiPSCs are considered the most robust platform for disease modeling between cases and controls. 26 , 27 We created genetically engineered hiPSC clones by introducing the LQT3 SCN5A variant into the healthy control hiPSCs via CRISPR/Cas9 for phenotypic reproducibility.

Manual whole‐cell patch clamp has been widely used in LQTS research to date, accurately replicating the cardiac action potential at down to the subtype ion channel level in a highly controlled state for any given atrial, nodal, or ventricular myocardial tissue. 7 , 28 , 29 MEAs are technically simpler to perform, can be repeated on live cellular cultures over time, and reflect cardiac depolarization at a tissue (rather than cellular) level, taking into account the heterogeneous mixture of cardiac cell types. The electrical field potential signals on clusters and monolayers of hiPSC‐CMs arise from the multicellular propagation of the cardiac action potential, similar in morphology with the surface ECG in human subjects.

Our MEA data recapitulated the healthy and disease phenotype model of LQTS in both control, patient, and genetically engineered samples.

In this study, we investigated the response to several compounds of interest in our established hiPSC‐CMs model. We demonstrated that the hiPSC‐CMs replicated pharmacologically relevant responses to compounds of known ion channel blocking properties. The NaV1.5 blocker mexiletine exerted amelioration of the pathogenic mechanism (FPDc prolongation) in our patient and genetically engineered LQT3 hiPSC‐CMs, which corresponded to the clinical response in shortening the patients' corrected QT on surface ECG. The pure hERG blocker E‐4031 showed a severe FPDc prolongation and caused an exacerbation of the disease phenotype already present in the patient and genetically engineered LQT3 hiPSC‐CMs, indicating that both hERG and NaV1.5 impairment have an additive deleterious effect.

We studied the pharmacological response to some commonly prescribed drugs with different purported Torsades de Pointes risk. Clear FPDc prolongation was observed following administration of ondansetron and clarithromycin, with a more significant ion effect on disease phenotype on administration of sotalol.

Conversely, we demonstrated that nifedipine and verapamil, both classes of calcium channel blockers, exerted an FPDc shortening effect, rescuing the disease phenotype in a rarely reported observation seen previously in a murine model. 30 In addition, patient and genetically engineered LQT3 hiPSC‐CMs between 10 and 100 nmol of nifedipine and 10 nmol to 1 μmol of verapamil showed similar FPDc values to the normative baseline healthy control FPDc, inferring a rescue and potential therapeutic benefit of calcium channel blockers in patients with LQT3. This may be of clinical relevance in cases of severe LQT3 as supplemental or alternative therapy to mexiletine.

We suggest that as a potential extension to the work presented above, fibroblasts taken after death in patients with sudden arrhythmic death syndrome can be successfully cultured, redirected to stem cell lineage and differentiated into cardiomyocytes. And this platform can be used for a phenotype of cardiac ion channelopathy, a prolonged repolarization time (LQTS) or potentially other malignant arrhythmia syndromes such as catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. 31 , 32 , 33 Such an approach would augment the investigative workup of families who have suffered a sudden arrhythmic death syndrome death and who may not have an identified pathogenic variant in a channelopathy gene on molecular autopsy.

Finally, genetic background can play a small but not insignificant role in response to either a therapeutic intervention (eg, response to mexiletine) in a patient with LQT3 or to a drug (eg, clarithromycin) inadvertently prescribed for another clinical reason in a patient with LQT3.

We wish to acknowledge the limitations of hiPSC‐CMs for disease modeling and drug screening due to their physiologically immature phenotypes. Several strategies have been proposed to improve the maturity of hiPSC‐CMs in vitro including prolonging the culture time, 34 3‐dimensional culture to mimic the in vivo environment, 35 , 36 and coculture with thyroid hormone and glucocorticoids to manipulate the adult‐like growth. 37 , 38

Conclusions

We demonstrated that LQT3 patient‐specific hiPSCs and genetically engineered LQT3 hiPSCs can recapitulate the electrophysiological phenotype of LQT3. In addition, this model platform can be used to study the response to clinical drugs without exposing any patient to potential harm. Furthermore, we envision this tool as a useful platform to screen novel drugs and test the response to future gene therapy interventions.

Study Limitations

There are acknowledged limitations with the data presented. Electrophysiological properties of cardiomyocytes were analyzed using MEA data only without complementary patch clamp results. Our sample size is relatively small, with 3 stem cell colony lines from the 1 LQT3 donor patient. We used the Integrated DNA Technologies tool to design the sgRNA using the SCN5A DNA sequence containing the pathogenic variant, and the candidate sgRNA showed related lower on‐target score and lower off‐target score, which may have the potential off‐target effect and affect the homology‐directed repair after the transfection. Instead, we generated an isogenic case and control stem cell line into a healthy control human sample to assist with validating the pathogenicity of our variant of interest and to further validate pharmacotherapy response. Nevertheless, we can improve the homology‐directed repair efficiency by introducing synonymous variants and a silent restriction digest site in the DNA template design and increasing the single‐cell screening scale.

We also did not investigate the other reported clinical phenotypes of SCN5A disease, including Brugada syndrome, atrial fibrillation, or sinus node disease, because it was outside the scope of our model. Lastly, at the highest drug dosages, cellular toxicity and cardiomyocyte stand‐still limited the extrapolation of interpretation.
