Current Technical Skills Assessment Tools in Otolaryngology Residency: A Scoping Review
Authors: Vanessa Helou, Alexandria Harris, Jenny Kim, Khalil Baddour, Nanki Hura, Kevin J. Contrera, Noel Jabbour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo review and synthesize the literature on technical skills assessment tools in otolaryngology residency programs.Data SourcesA comprehensive search of Medline, Embase, PubMed, and Google Scholar was performed from inception to January 2025.Review MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, studies reporting on technical skills assessment tools in otolaryngology residency training were screened and abstracted independently in duplicate. Data were extracted on study characteristics, subspecialty, procedure type, training modality (simulation, cadaveric, operating room), assessment tool design, scoring scale, number of residents, and tool validity. Study quality was assessed using the Modified Educational Oxford Centre for Evidence‐Based Medicine framework.ResultsOf 1270 screened studies, 146 met inclusion criteria, encompassing 3231 trainees across six otolaryngology subspecialties. Most studies were from the United States (59.6%), Canada (18.5%), and Denmark (10.3%). The largest subspecialty represented was otology and neurotology (42.5%), followed by rhinology and skull base surgery (21.2%), head and neck surgery (12.3%), general otolaryngology (11.6%), pediatric otolaryngology (7.5%), and laryngology (4.8%). Common assessment frameworks included Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills, Task‐Specific Checklists, and Global Rating Scales. Tools demonstrated construct, face, and content validity, with strong interrater reliability and sensitivity to training level. Innovations included automated performance analysis and 3D‐printed models.ConclusionThe synthesized list of procedures and their assessment tools can serve as a road map for otolaryngology residency programs. They support competency‐based medical education, differentiate skill levels, and guide feedback. Future work should focus on multicenter validation, standardization across programs, and integration of technology‐driven assessment methods to enhance surgical training outcomes.

Introduction

Otolaryngology‐head and neck surgery residency programs require trainees to develop both technical and nontechnical skills to achieve surgical competency. While technical skills involve procedural expertise, nontechnical skills include medical knowledge, situational awareness, adaptability, and communication skill development. Tools like “Non‐Technical Skills for Surgeons” have been adapted to otolaryngology training, particularly for assessing nontechnical skills in simulated emergency scenarios [1].

Traditionally, surgical training followed the “see one, do one, teach one” model, with technical skills assessment often based on subjective feedback from trained surgeons in the operating room (OR) and bedside [2]. However, concerns regarding patient safety, quality of care, and the adequacy of this approach in ensuring competence have sparked calls for more objective assessment tools.

In response, the shift toward Competency‐Based Medical Education (CBME) and the recent concept of Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs) as the building blocks of CBME assessment has driven the development of more structured assessment tools [3]. These now include benchmark training and assessment tools such as simulation‐based and cadaveric dissections, alongside traditional assessments.

While previous systematic reviews from 2018 offered valuable insights into technical skills assessment tools [4, 5], much has changed since then. Advances in robotic surgery, virtual reality (VR), and simulation‐based assessments have revolutionized how technical skills are taught and evaluated. In addition, the COVID‐19 pandemic highlighted the limitations of traditional training, revealing that a time‐based training approach might not be sufficient to assess competence for independent practice [6].

This scoping review aims to examine the current literature of technical skills assessment tools in otolaryngology residency programs. By synthesizing existing studies, the review will provide an overview of the available assessment tools and explore the integration of new technologies. It will also identify the challenges and limitations of these tools, gaps in the literature, and propose directions for future research to improve residency training outcomes.

Methods

We conducted a scoping review on the technical skills assessment tools in Otolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery residency according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) guidelines [7].

Search Strategy

A comprehensive search of Ovid Medline, Embase and Google Scholar was conducted from inception to January 2025. The search strategy was developed and reviewed in collaboration with a research librarian (A.Y.) from the University of Pittsburgh Health Sciences Library System. We used a combination of search terms in abstracts and titles: Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery, Residency, Training, Assessment Tool, Surgical Skills, Skill Assessment. References of included studies were also screened. Search terms included combinations of “technical skills assessment,” “otolaryngology‐head and neck residency,” “residency,” “training”, “simulation,” “evaluation tools,” and “competency‐based education.” References of included studies were also screened.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were included if they (1) developed a novel technical skills assessment tool for otolaryngology–head and neck surgery procedures, (2) validated an existing assessment tool on otolaryngology residents, or (3) both developed and validated an assessment tool within the same publication. Tool development studies, including those using Delphi or modified Delphi consensus methodology, were included to provide a comprehensive overview of available assessment instruments, even if the study did not include a formal validation component with trainee assessment. This approach ensures that newly developed tools are captured and available for future validation efforts. We also included both technical skills assessment tools and workplace‐based procedural assessment tools (including entrustable professional activity frameworks) that evaluate otolaryngology residents' operative performance. Only studies published in English were considered.

Title and Abstract Screen

Two groups of two reviewers each (V.H., A.H., J.K., and N.H) completed initial abstract screening and performed full‐text review of potentially relevant articles independently and in duplicate. Studies were excluded at this stage if they clearly did not meet the eligibility criteria—for example, studies not focused on skill assessment tools, not including otolaryngology residents, or limited to medical students. Additional exclusions included nonrelevant study designs, abstracts, letters to the editor, and reviews. Disagreements were resolved with input from the senior author (N.J.).

Data Extraction and Synthesis

Data from selected articles were extracted and organized into categories based on the otolaryngology subspecialty of the assessment tool. These categories included general otolaryngology, pediatrics, rhinology, otology, head and neck, and facial plastics. Procedures were categorized according to prevailing conventions in the literature and the focus of each study. In addition, data were collected on study characteristics, otolaryngology subspecialty, type of procedure being assessed, type of training and assessment tool (OR vs. cadaveric vs. simulation), number of residents included, details of the tool used and its scale.

Quality Assessment

The Modified Educational Oxford Centre for Evidence‐Based Medicine (OCEBM) was used to assess the quality of evidence of each included study [8]. It is a widely utilized tool that provides clear hierarchy evidence; therefore, clearly demonstrating to clinicians a higher quality of evidence to recommend one tool over another. A recommendation of one is the highest, whereas level four is the lowest.

Results

Study Characteristics

Out of 1270 studies screened for eligibility, 146 studies were included with a total of 3231 participants (Figure 1). The median year of study publication is 2017, ranging from 2002 to 2024. The majority of the studies were published from the United States (n = 87, 59.6%), followed by Canada (n = 27, 18.5%), and Denmark (n = 15, 10.3%).

General Otolaryngology

A total of 17 studies (11.6%) focused on general otolaryngology procedures, covering airway evaluation and management (n = 8), robotic surgical skills training (n = 2), and a range of otolaryngologic surgeries (n = 7). These studies employed various training and assessment methodologies using simulation models (n = 6), operating room‐based assessments (n = 9), and animal cadaveric models (n = 2).

The majority (47.0%) utilized Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS)‐based technical skills assessment checklists such as Global Rating Scale, demonstrating the widespread application of structured evaluation tools in surgical education. Alternative assessment methods included simulation‐based mastery learning for cricothyrotomy and automated performance analysis. Three studies developed technical assessment scales through a modified Delphi method with input from expert panels, refining checklists for cricothyrotomy, flexible pharyngo‐laryngoscopy, and microlaryngoscopy [9, 10, 11]. Additionally, four studies evaluated smartphone‐based, real‐time intraoperative assessment using the Society for Improving Medical Professional Learning (SIMPL) application, which assesses trainee autonomy, performance, and case complexity [12, 13, 14, 15].

Key findings from these studies highlighted the validity and reliability of structured assessment tools. These tools were shown to effectively differentiate skill levels, with high inter‐rater reliability and strong correlations between training experience and performance scores. Several studies demonstrated improvements in skill acquisition and retention following structured training interventions. Additionally, novel approaches such as crowd‐sourced skill assessment and automated evaluation systems showed promise in providing efficient, scalable alternatives to traditional expert‐led evaluations [16, 17, 18].

Pediatric Otolaryngology

Among 11 studies in pediatric otolaryngology, most focused on airway evaluation and foreign body removal (n = 6, 4.1%), followed by tonsillectomy (n = 3, 2.1%), pediatric tracheotomy (n = 1, 0.7%), and laryngotracheal reconstruction (n = 1, 0.7%). All studies utilized OSATS‐based assessments, incorporating task‐specific checklists and global rating scales. Four studies were simulation‐based, four were conducted in the operating room, and three involved animal models.

The Modified Delphi consensus method was employed to refine assessment tools for tonsillectomy (n = 3), airway evaluation and foreign body removal (n = 2), and tracheotomy (n = 1) [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24].

Key findings indicated that performance improved with training level, with assessment scores correlating with prior experience and case volume. Additionally, strong inter‐rater reliability across various tools confirmed their validity in distinguishing skill levels and providing formative feedback.

Rhinology and Skull Base Surgery

Among the 31 studies in rhinology and skull base surgery, the majority focused on endoscopic sinus surgery (n = 24, 16.4%), followed by septoplasty (n = 6, 4.1%), and cerebrospinal fluid leak repair (n = 1, 0.7%). Almost half of the studies (n = 16) utilized OSATS‐based assessments, and nine studies utilized automated data analytic skill assessment via a simulator.

The Consensus Delphi method was used to develop the European Endoscopic Sinus Surgery–Technical Skills Assessment (EE‐TSA), a 21‐item tool designed to facilitate competency‐based ESS training [25]. An ESS‐specific OSATS checklist was also created using a modified Delphi technique [26]. It included a 22‐item task‐specific checklist and a 10‐item global rating scale checklist, with a passing score of 3 out of 5.

Otology and Neurotology

Among 62 studies in otology and neurotology, the majority focused on temporal bone dissection and mastoidectomy (n = 46, 31.5%). The remaining studies focused on myringotomy and tube placement (n = 7, 4.8%), cochlear implant surgery (n = 7, 4.8%), and ossiculoplasty (n = 2, 1.4%).

Multiple structured assessment tools have been developed to evaluate technical skills in mastoidectomy. A modified Delphi technique was used to create a 22‐item task‐based checklist encompassing seven major operative tasks, scored on a 5‐point Likert scale [27]. Similarly, a global rating scale was designed to assess visual‐motor and cognitive skills required for successful mastoidectomy, with a passing score set at 3. Another 16‐item instrument further refined skill assessment using a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from “no skill” to “expert skill.” Competency cut‐offs were proposed for supervised (median 2–3) and unsupervised (median 3–4) performance levels [28]. In addition to these technique‐oriented tools, a checklist based on safety was introduced, focusing on critical errors rather than procedural correctness [29] It comprised 14 safety metrics grouped into three domains: bone removal, tool control, and structural violations. Finally, a CanadaWest Scale, based on 12 items, was developed to assess key procedural steps in mastoidectomy on 3D‐printed models, including facial recess delineation, posterior canal wall thinning, and avoidance of injury to critical anatomical structures [30].

Laryngology

Among 7 studies in laryngology, six (4.1%) assessed laryngeal microsurgery and one (0.7%) assessed Zenker's diverticulectomy.

The “Laser Surgical Skills Evaluation for Residents” (LASER) scale, described in Sedillot‐Daniel et al. represents the first validated, consensus‐based assessment tool specifically for CO2 laser microlaryngeal surgery [31]. It was developed through a rigorous modified Delphi process involving 15 expert otolaryngologists across six countries. The LASER scale uses a 5‐point Likert system and covers four domains: anesthesia considerations, preoperative and perioperative laser safety measures, and surgical techniques.

Head and Neck Surgery

Among the 18 studies in head and neck surgery, six (4.1%) studies focused on neck dissection, five (3.4%) studies focused on thyroidectomy, five (3.4%) studies focused on transoral robotic surgery. The remaining studies focused on glossectomy (n = 1, 0.7%) and head and neck ultrasound (n = 1, 0.7%).

Several studies have developed and validated structured assessment tools for evaluating competency in neck dissection. Dowling et al. used a modified Delphi process followed by prospective validation on 17 residents performing fresh‐frozen cadaveric selective neck dissections [32]. The final instrument comprised a 25‐item task‐specific checklist and a 5‐item global rating scale, each scored on a 5‐point Likert scale, with a minimum score of 3 set as the threshold for independent performance. Strong correlations were observed between scores, resident training level, and number of head and neck rotations completed. Similarly, Mercier et al. employed a modified Delphi process with six head and neck surgeons to define the core elements of neck dissection, generating a 24‐item task‐specific checklist and 7‐item global rating scale [33]. In this pilot study of 11 residents, senior trainees achieved significantly higher scores than junior trainees, with clear progression over time.

Yang et al. developed and translated the Neck Dissection Assessment Tool through a multicenter Delphi process and prospective validation in the operating room with 12 residents [34]. The tool included a 24‐step task‐specific checklist and a 7‐item global rating scale, both scored on a 5‐point Likert scale. Significant differences in scores between junior and senior residents supported construct validity.

Discussion

This scoping review identified 146 studies encompassing 3231 participants and spanning over two decades of surgical education research in otolaryngology. The majority were conducted in North America and published within the last decade, reflecting a growing emphasis on competency‐based medical education and structured technical skills assessment in otolaryngology–head and neck surgery residency. By categorizing studies across subspecialties, distinct patterns emerge in the development, validation, and application of assessment tools. To facilitate translation of these findings into educational practice, Table S1 provides a comprehensive reference of procedural assessment tools, study characteristics, and validation approaches intended for use by otolaryngology residency program directors, educators, and educational researchers.

The assessment tools employed varied widely but were predominantly structured competency‐based frameworks, including OSATS, Global Rating Scales, Task‐Specific Checklists, and other modified evaluation instruments. The modified Delphi process was frequently employed, ensuring expert consensus and content validity. Many studies incorporated simulation‐based assessments using cadaveric models, animal models (e.g., ovine laryngotracheal reconstruction), high‐fidelity mannequins, and 3D‐printed airway models. Some studies integrated real‐time operating room‐based assessments, with expert faculty evaluating trainee performance during actual surgical procedures [14, 15]. Video‐based blinded assessments were also frequently utilized, ensuring objectivity and interrater reliability. Interrater reliability was generally strong, with ICC values often exceeding 0.7.

Otology and neurotology dominated the literature, with mastoidectomy and temporal bone dissection serving as central procedures for assessment tool development. The complexity, risk profile, and accessibility of temporal bone models likely explain the emphasis in this domain. Rhinology and skull base surgery, particularly endoscopic sinus surgery, also accounted for a substantial proportion of studies.

In contrast, pediatric otolaryngology and laryngology were less represented, despite the unique technical challenges and patient safety concerns inherent to these subspecialties. Pediatric procedures such as tonsillectomy and airway foreign body removal carry risks of airway compromise, hemorrhage, and respiratory adverse events in smaller and more delicate anatomical fields [35]. Laryngology procedures like microlaryngoscopy demand fine motor precision in structures critical to voice and airway function, where errors can cause permanent impairement [36]. This gap likely reflects the historical concentration of simulation and assessment research in temporal bone and endoscopic sinus surgery, where cadaveric and virtual reality models are more available, and the difficulty of standardizing assessment across heterogeneous pediatric populations. Developing and validating subspecialty‐specific assessment tools in these areas represents a key opportunity to strengthen competency‐based evaluation in high‐stakes procedural domains.

Importantly, while many of the studies included are situated within CBME‐informed training environments, this review focuses specifically on procedural skills assessment instruments. These instruments should be interpreted in the context of the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery milestones, which mandate semi‐annual evaluations of surgical competence across patient care milestones [37]. Many studies did not explicitly align their assessment tools with milestone reporting. Nevertheless, validated tools such as OSATS, Global Rating Scales, and Task‐Specific Checklists could complement milestone‐based programmatic assessment by providing structured, reliable measures of technical skills.

Evolving Innovations in Assessment

Notably, several studies explored novel approaches to expand scalability and objectivity. Automated performance analysis and crowd‐sourced video assessments provided efficient alternatives to expert‐led evaluations [16, 17, 18, 38, 39, 40]. This suggests a future direction for minimizing faculty time burden while maintaining rigorous feedback. Subspecialty‐specific instruments, such as the EE‐TSA, the LASER scale for microlaryngeal surgery, and the CanadaWest Scale for mastoidectomy, demonstrate a shift toward tailored competency frameworks. These tools not only capture technical precision but also emphasize safety, critical errors, and cognitive decision‐making.

Educational Impact

Consistent with CBME principles, the studies collectively demonstrated that structured assessments facilitate skill differentiation, monitor progression, and provide actionable feedback. Trainees at more advanced levels reliably outperformed juniors, supporting the construct validity of these tools. Pre‐post intervention studies demonstrated significant skill acquisition, with notable improvements in OSATS scores and self‐reported confidence levels. For instance, simulation‐based courses in pediatric airway endoscopy led to a 61.4% improvement in OSATS scores and a 43% increase in confidence levels (p < 0.001) [41]. Similarly, tonsillectomy assessment tools showed significant score increases correlating with prior case volume and experience [22], reinforcing their utility in evaluating trainee progress. However, a few studies noted instances where post‐intervention scores did not significantly improve in certain participants [40, 42, 43], suggesting that competency acquisition is multifaceted and may be influenced by individual learning curves and prior exposure. Finally, the ability of these tools to identify weaknesses and track longitudinal improvement underscores their value for both formative and summative evaluation. Limitations and Future Directions

While procedural skills assessment tools provide valid and reliable measures of technical competence, they represent only one component of CBME. Van Melle's framework identifies five core elements—outcome competencies, sequenced progression, tailored learning experiences, competency‐focused instruction, and programmatic assessment [44]. These instruments primarily address programmatic assessment, yet full CBME implementation requires integration across all components, including longitudinal data collection, aggregation, and high‐stakes decision‐making [45, 46]. Thus, findings from this review should be interpreted as evidence for one key CBME building block rather than as evidence of comprehensive CBME implementation.

Despite these conceptual limitations, several practical challenges remain. First, there is heterogeneity in study design, sample sizes, and validation rigor. Many studies were limited to single institutions, restricting generalizability. Categorization of procedures by subspecialty required some judgment; for example, myringotomy with tube placement could also reasonably be classified as general otolaryngology. This may have influenced subspecialty‐specific trends. Simulation models, though effective for skill acquisition, may lack fidelity compared with real operative environments. Furthermore, while construct validity was frequently demonstrated, fewer studies assessed predictive validity—whether high assessment scores translate into improved operative performance or patient outcomes. Finally, the absence of standardized instruments across institutions hinders benchmarking and broad implementation.

Future research should prioritize multicenter validation of assessment tools to establish generalizability and standardization across training programs. Underrepresented subspecialties, particularly pediatric otolaryngology and laryngology, warrant greater attention given their technical complexity and clinical relevance. Integration of artificial intelligence and motion‐tracking technology holds promise for real‐time, objective performance feedback, potentially reducing reliance on faculty evaluators. Longitudinal studies following trainees across residency would provide critical insights into skill retention, plateau effects, and the predictive value of assessment scores on independent practice. Finally, aligning assessment tools with EPAs and accreditation frameworks may facilitate their integration into programmatic assessment systems within competency‐based curricula.
