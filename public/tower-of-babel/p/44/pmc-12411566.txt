Proportion of participants meeting falls prevention guidelines in an evidence-based community-based exercise program
Authors: Maria Fernanda Fuentes Diaz, Kathryn M. Sibley, Kate Giberson, Martin Sénéchal, Danielle R. Bouchard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough exercise is strongly recommended to prevent falls in older adults (exercise that challenges balance, performed three hours per week on an ongoing basis), few community-based programs meet these recommendations.AimsAssess the proportion of participants meeting fall prevention exercise recommendations in a community-based program and explore how adherence varies by individual characteristics and participation mode (in-person, tele-exercise, or hybrid).MethodsA cross-sectional analysis of Zoomers in Balance participants who completed an online questionnaire about their demographic data, mode of participation, and their perceived balance intensity in a 12-week series using the Balance Intensity Scale (1-no effort at all to 5-maximal effort). Weekly attendance was self-reported after each 12-week series, and ongoing participation was assessed using registration data over one year.ResultsThe average perceived balance intensity was 3.2 ± 0.7 (range 1–5), the average attendance was 1.3 ± 0.6 h/week (range 0.2–4.2), and participants attended an average of 3.4 ± 0.6 series/year (range 2–4). None of the participants met all three guidelines. The most fulfilled recommendation was ongoing participation (43%), which was greater in the hybrid (B = 2.68; p < 0.001) and tele-exercise (B = 1.28; p < 0.001) groups compared with the in-person mode. In addition, the mode of participation was associated with meeting one or more guidelines (χ2 = 23.05; p < 0.001), without any significant difference between modes.DiscussionOffering hybrid participation options could lead to greater adherence to evidence-based guidelines, thereby reducing the risk of falls.ConclusionThe proportion of participants meeting fall prevention guidelines in a community-based program is low, with a trend indicating that hybrid options are more effective.

Introduction

A fall is defined as an event that results in a person coming to rest inadvertently on the ground, floor, or other lower-level [1]. The prevalence of falls increases with age, with a global prevalence of 26.5% of seniors reporting a fall or more yearly [2]. Falls are also the second leading cause of unintentional injury deaths worldwide, contributing to 80% of all injury-related hospitalizations [1]. Falls adversely affect physical function, quality of life, loss of independence, hospitalization, morbidity, and mortality [3, 4]. However, the consequences of falls extend beyond the individual, affecting families and the broader community [4]. At the individual and family level, fall-related injuries are accompanied by large increases in problems with self-care and usual activities [5, 6]. At the community and societal level, falls also impose a substantial financial burden on families and healthcare services [5, 7]. The direct costs of falls are the payments related to treatment, and the indirect costs are related to the financial loss from the absence from work (of the individual and the family caregiver), injury-related disability, and dependence [8]. Furthermore, data from the United States indicates that the estimated medical cost attributable to fatal and nonfatal falls is approximately $50 billion, with fatal falls alone representing a cost of USD 754 million [9].

Abundant evidence suggests that the best strategy to reduce falls in community-dwelling older adults is exercise three hours per week on an ongoing basis, while incorporating balance challenges [10, 11]. A systematic review conducted by Sherrington et al., [10] concluded that exercise also reduces the number of people experiencing one or more falls by 15% and may reduce the number of people experiencing fractures by 27%. Additionally, Sibley et al. [12], , determined that activities that include anticipatory and reactive control, dynamic and functional stability, and flexibility were associated with the least risk of falls OR (95% CI) by 0.14 (0.03–0.56), compared to other sixteen combination of exercise. In spite of this, only approximately 16% of older adults engage in activities that challenge balance at least twice weekly [13].

Community exercise programs are a public health strategy to reduce the risk of falls in a massive group of individuals [14–16]. Community exercise programs are publicly or privately funded group programs that are easily accessible to people living within the community with the intention of promoting the health and well-being of its members [17] These programs offer opportunities for older adults to exercise and reduce fall risk in a supervised setting and are usually hosted at senior centers, retirement communities, gyms, and faith-based organizations [18]. These programs have been successful in reducing the risk of falls and have also promoted improved physical performance, including balance, functional mobility, extremity strength, and fear of falling [18]. A recent national investigation of fall prevention community exercise program leaders in Canada, conducted by Touchette et al., [19], found that programs were mostly delivered twice a week (one hour per class) by either a certified fitness professional or a health professional, and only 45% of these programs were offered continuously throughout the year [19]. Furthermore, only 6% of the exercise instructors reported program characteristics that meet fall prevention guidelines [19].

According to Touchette et al. [19], Canadian community exercise programs were unlikely to offer the recommended dose of exercise required to prevent falls. As a result, participants are less likely to meet these recommendations. The rapid development of digital health solutions, especially since the COVID-19 period has provided new options for delivering digital health promotion initiatives [20], such as telehealth-based exercise (tele-exercise). Moreover, as the benefits of tele-exercise programs have been reported in recent years [21], this potential path to meet the three fall prevention recommendations (1- exercise that challenges balance, 2- performed three hours per week, 3- on an ongoing basis) incorporating a range of exercise options warrants more research. Tele-exercise refers to the provision of exercise remotely utilizing various telecommunication tools with or without a video connection [22, 23]. These can be delivered synchronously (e.g., live-streamed classes), or they can be delivered asynchronously (e.g., prerecorded, website- or app-based classes) [24]. Tele-exercise programs have demonstrated comparable physical function, quality of life, and psychosocial improvements to traditional in-person exercise programs [25, 26].

This study aims to describe the proportion of participants in an evidence-informed community exercise program who are meeting fall prevention exercise recommendations and to determine if the proportion differs based on individual characteristics or the mode of participation (in-person, tele-exercise, or hybrid).

Methods

Design and setting

This study is a cross-sectional analysis that took place within an evidenced-based community fall prevention exercise program called Zoomers in Balance (previously called Zoomers on the Go). This is a peer-led program that aims to reduce the risk of falls in adults 50 years and older. In 2023–2024, in-person and tele-exercise classes of this program were offered across the province of New Brunswick, with an average of 28 sites, 34 volunteer leaders, and 1,200 participants (30% tele-exercise) per series (four series per year). Volunteer leaders aged 50 and older receive a 2-day training that includes basic exercise principles and physiology, safety principles for group-based classes, elements of the class, class design and development, leadership and communication skills, and tips for tele-exercise instruction. After this training, leaders are evaluated on their practical skills to deliver a class and get a certification from a provincial fitness organization to teach Zoomers in Balance. It was launched in 2009 with in-person series and starting in 2020, due to COVID-19, tele-exercise was offered as well (live-streamed and pre-recorded classes). Each series of the program lasts 12 weeks, with the exception of the Summer series, which lasts 10 weeks. Both program participation methods have been proven effective in reducing the risk of falls [27, 28]. This program meets fall prevention exercise recommendations: it is offered continuously throughout the year, three hours per week, using balance-challenging exercises. Classes at individual sites are delivered 1–3 times per week in person, and between 5 and 10 tele-exercise classes are offered weekly. A typical class consists of a 10-minute warm-up and dynamic flexibility, 10–15 min of strength training using body weight and/or chairs, 25–35 min of balance exercises, and ends with 10–15 min of stretching and flexibility exercises. Balance exercises include activities of anticipatory and reactive balance, and dynamic and functional stability.

Data collection

We utilized two sources of data: online questionnaires and registration data. Because one of the main outcomes of the study was participation in the last year, participants had to have been registered for the program for at least 12 months to be eligible to participate. This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and it was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of New Brunswick and is under file REB 2023 − 176. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

One online questionnaire was sent in February to 919 people registered in the 2024 winter series (January-March). This questionnaire included a question based on the Balance Intensity Scale [29], a validated self-report measure to rate exercises performed by adults. Respondents rated the effort needed for balance maintenance during a typical class of the program using a scale with 1 (no effort at all), 2 (a little effort), 3 (some effort), 4 (a lot of effort), and 5 (maximal effort). This questionnaire also included questions about demographic characteristics including sex, age, main language spoken at home, ethnicity, relationship status, education, employment status, and total household income.

Participation was measured by the number of series participants registered for the program (1–4), and it was collected from registration data for each series offered in the previous year.

Additionally, during the past year, questionnaires were sent to participants at the end of each series to report their weekly attendance. Participants reported their estimated average times per week and the number of weeks they participated in the program. These two values were multiplied to calculate the total attendance per series. Since each class lasts 60 min, these data were used to estimate the weekly attendance.

The utilized mode of participation (e.g., in-person, tele-exercise, or hybrid) was taken from the registration data based on the option chosen by participants when they filled out the registration form.

Descriptive statistics are reported as mean ± SD, n (%). One-way ANOVA was used to test differences in exercise volume, balance intensity, and ongoing participation between groups. Chi-square tests were used to assess differences between guidelines in different participation modes. Logistic regression was used with the dependent variable (Yes/No meeting each guideline) and the mode of participation as the independent variable. These models were further adjusted for other descriptive variables (e.g., age, sex, ethnicity). IBM SPSS Statistics Version 28 was used to conduct all statistical analyses.

About the participation mode, 75% attended in-person, 8.9% tele-exercise, and 16.1% attended hybrid (in-person and tele-exercise) classes. Based on Chi-square analysis, the mode of participation was associated with meeting one or more guidelines, with no significant difference between the modes. In addition, the participation mode was not related to the proportion of participants meeting the exercise volume or balance intensity recommendations, but it was for the ongoing participation criterion (x2 = 43.11; p < 0.001). Furthermore, logistic regression revealed a greater proportion of participants reaching this criterion in the hybrid (B = 2.68; p < 0.001) and tele-exercise (B = 1.28 p < 0.001) groups compared with the in-person mode. None of the descriptive variables were significantly associated with the dependent variables.

Regarding the number of guidelines criteria met by participation mode, almost half of the attendees (47.5%) to in-person classes did not meet any guidelines, 41.8% met one guideline, and only 10.7% of them met two guidelines. On the other hand, 61.9% and 76.3% of participants met one guideline in the tele-exercise and hybrid mode, respectively (See Table 4). Finally, the mode of participation was associated with meeting one or more guidelines (x2 = 23.05; p < 0.001) without any significant difference between modes.

Discussion

This study aimed to identify the proportion of participants in an evidence-informed fall exercise prevention program who meet current recommendations. None of the participants met all three guidelines with the ongoing participation being the most reached guideline. Interestingly, a low percentage of people used technology to engage in the program, but participating in tele-exercise or hybrid classes was associated with greater continuous participation in the program. Hence, adding technology options to in-person offerings could lead to greater adherence to fall prevention guidelines in community settings.

Previously, only 6% of programs were reported as meeting the three guidelines [19]. Our study, at the individual level, suggests similar findings, with none of the participants meeting all guidelines. The most fulfilled recommendation was ongoing participation in the program, followed by balance intensity ≥ 3/5, but only 1% of participants participated an average of three hours per week. This overall low compliance with fall prevention guidelines should be taken as a call to action to adapt current programs and evaluate several factors that can influence this compliance. Some strategies to address this critical evidence-to-implementation gap may include health professionals encouraging their clients to be more active, systems to support health professionals in referring patients to suitable community exercise opportunities, and support for community organizations in delivering suitable exercise opportunities [30]. Although tele-exercise classes have the potential to increase accessibility and participation, this study did not observe a high proportion of people meeting the recommended three hours of exercise per week. Therefore, future research focused on how to enhance participation in tele-exercise among older adults is warranted, as they tend to experience challenges using technology, such as sensory impairments, unfamiliarity with technology, privacy concerns, and perceptions of difficult use or increased risk of injury [24, 31, 32].

Some strategies could help people to meet the recommendations. First, incorporating balance-challenging activities into people’s daily lives, based on preferences and context, could be more acceptable than relying solely on formal exercise programs. This way, aging adults could participate in organized activities and complete the required training times while performing activities of daily living such as picking up objects from the floor, standing on one leg while talking over the phone, or watching television as proposed previously by others [33].

Second, using technology could help adhere to the guidelines. Despite the increasing use of the internet [34] and the popularity of tele-exercise programs among aging adults [21], only a low percentage of people engaged in the tele-exercise portion of this program. However, participating in tele-exercise or hybrid classes seemed to help people engage continuously. Tele-exercise programs are expected to increase continuous participation as they remove some common barriers to exercise (e.g., time, transportation, the absence of community offerings) [35, 36]. Despite the recent increase in internet use among older adults many factors impact participation in tele-exercise programs. These factors include, but are not limited to personal preferences, poor digital literacy, technology-related challenges, age or income [37–39]. All these barriers call for implementing tailored strategies among aging adults to address the barriers they commonly face when accessing tele-exercise programs.

Another interesting finding is that the ongoing basis recommendation was the most met, regardless of the season, especially when using technology (hybrid and tele-exercise modes). As a result, it seems worthwhile to provide a program continuously throughout the year, despite holidays and weather variations. Previous behaviour change research supports that recurring triggers influence strong habits in the performance context [40], this might suggest that the availability of the program all year long acts as a context cue that influences the habit of attending the program regularly [40]. However, only 42% of programs in Canada are offered on an ongoing basis [19], which needs collaborative work to develop ongoing programs to promote sustained effects. Based on the current study, the solution might lie in using technology to access tele-exercise options.

As with any study, it is important to acknowledge both strengths and limitations. While this is the first study to objectively measure balance intensity in a community group setting, incorporating participants from both in-person and tele-exercise modes, we adapted the Balance Intensity Scale (BIS) in a manner that has not yet been validated for this purpose. Its application in group settings remains untested, emphasizing the need for further validation studies. Additionally, balance intensity itself is inherently challenging to measure accurately, as highlighted in earlier research [41, 42]. Another limitation is the absence of data on participants’ digital literacy, which may have influenced their engagement with tele-exercise classes but was not assessed. We did not account for other physical activities or exercise programs participants might have engaged in alongside Zoomers in Balance, which could impact their total weekly exercise volume. There was also a significant difference in participation modes among the groups. Finally, data were retrospectively reported and therefore influenced by recall bias.

Future research should include a more comprehensive examination of individual, ecological, and program-related factors that can influence adherence to fall prevention guidelines. Moreover, future studies should validate the use of the Balance Intensity Scale in community group settings and with non-health professionals, such as volunteer instructors. Additionally, future research should develop strategies to improve the use of technology among older adults to help them participate in evidence-based programs and meet guidelines.

Conclusion

The proportion of participants meeting fall prevention guidelines in the Zoomers in Balance program was low when evaluated in 2023–2024. Although the mode of participation was not associated with perceived balance intensity or weekly volume, tele-exercise and hybrid modes were associated with ongoing participation in the program.

Organizations can offer different ways to reach the recommendations besides the typical in-person exercise programs. Our study suggests using technology, but promoting balance-focused activities in participants’ daily living activities could also be explored. Organizational strategies must be developed to support participants in optimizing the efficacy of fall prevention programs.
