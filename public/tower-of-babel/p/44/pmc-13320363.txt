Introduction

Prostate cancer is one of the most common malignancies worldwide. Although prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) screening has enabled earlier detection, approximately 5% of patients still present with distant metastases at diagnosis [1, 2]. The most frequent metastatic sites are bone, lymph nodes, liver, and thorax [3]. In contrast, ureteral metastasis from prostate cancer is exceedingly rare, with only approximately 50 cases reported [4, 5].

We report two cases of clinically diagnosed bilateral ureteral metastases from prostate cancer presenting with postrenal acute kidney injury. In both cases, ureteroscopic biopsy failed to provide pathological confirmation, and the diagnosis was established based on the marked response to androgen deprivation therapy (ADT).

Case Presentation

Case 1

A 77‐year‐old man presented with renal dysfunction (serum creatinine 2.62 mg/dL, eGFR 19.4 mL/min/1.73 m2) and anorexia. CT demonstrated bilateral hydronephrosis, left renal atrophy, and bilateral distal ureteral obstruction (Figure 1A,B). PSA was elevated to 121 ng/mL.

Computed tomography and retrograde pyeloureterography findings in Case 1. (A) Axial computed tomography (CT) images at the level of the kidneys demonstrate bilateral hydronephrosis with marked atrophy of the left kidney. (B) Axial CT images at the pelvic level show bilateral ureteral obstruction associated with ureteral wall thickening. (C) Retrograde pyeloureterography (RP) of the right ureter reveals filiform stenosis slightly proximal to the right ureteric orifice. (D) RP of the left ureter demonstrates complete obstruction just proximal to the left ureteric orifice.

Retrograde pyeloureterography revealed filiform stenosis of the right ureter and complete obstruction of the left ureter (Figure 1C,D). A right ureteral stent was inserted. Urine cytology from the right ureter was classified as Class IIIb. Ureteroscopic biopsies from the right ureter (Figure 2A) and blind biopsy of the left ureter showed no evidence of malignancy.

Intraoperative findings and computed tomography after combined androgen blockade therapy in Case 1. (A) Ureteroscopic view of the right ureter shows edematous mucosa and luminal narrowing without evidence of an intraluminal tumor. (B) Axial computed tomography (CT) images at the level of the kidneys demonstrate resolution of left hydronephrosis following combined androgen blockade (CAB) therapy.

Prostate biopsy revealed acinar adenocarcinoma with a Gleason score of 4 + 4 (Grade Group 4). No distant metastases were identified, and the clinical stage was cT2cN0M0. Combined androgen blockade was initiated. Although placement of a left nephrostomy tube had been planned, follow‐up CT demonstrated spontaneous resolution of the left hydronephrosis within 1 month without any urinary diversion procedure (Figure 2B). The marked improvement in ureteral stenosis strongly supported the diagnosis of bilateral ureteral metastases from prostate cancer.

The PSA level remained undetectable thereafter. Eight years later, the patient died of carcinomatous pleural effusion secondary to undifferentiated lung cancer.

Case 2

A 74‐year‐old man was referred for right hydronephrosis detected during a health checkup. CT and retrograde pyeloureterography revealed severe stenosis of the right pelvic ureter (Figure 3A,C). Two weeks later, he developed postrenal acute kidney injury with newly developed left hydronephrosis (Figure 3B), as serum creatinine increased from 1.14 to 4.06 mg/dL and eGFR decreased from 38.7 to 12.2 mL/min/1.73 m2. Emergency left ureteral stenting was performed (Figure 3D).

Computed tomography and retrograde pyeloureterography findings in Case 2. (A) Axial contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) images at the level of the kidneys demonstrate right hydronephrosis with marked renal atrophy, without evidence of left hydronephrosis. (B) Axial CT images obtained 2 weeks later demonstrate newly developed left hydronephrosis. (C) Retrograde pyeloureterography (RP) of the right ureter reveals filiform stenosis in the pelvic ureter. (D) RP of the left ureter demonstrates extrinsic obstruction of the pelvic ureter.

Bilateral ureteroscopy demonstrated ureteral narrowing without intraluminal tumor (Figure 4A,B). Histopathological examination and urine cytology showed no evidence of malignancy.

Intraoperative findings and computed tomography after androgen deprivation therapy with upfront ARSI in Case 2. (A) Ureteroscopic view of the right ureter demonstrates luminal narrowing due to extrinsic compression, without evidence of an intraluminal tumor. (B) Ureteroscopic view of the left ureter shows edematous mucosa and luminal narrowing without evidence of an intraluminal tumor. (C) Axial computed tomography (CT) images at the level of the kidneys demonstrate resolution of right hydronephrosis with persistent renal atrophy following androgen deprivation therapy combined with upfront androgen receptor signaling inhibitor (ARSI) therapy.

Prostate biopsy revealed acinar adenocarcinoma with a Gleason score of 5 + 5 (Grade Group 5) in a patient with a PSA level of 8.199 ng/mL. Although no distant metastases were detected, rectal invasion was present (cT4N0M0). Based on the clinical course and high‐grade pathology, ureteral metastasis from prostate cancer was strongly suspected. ADT combined with an androgen receptor signaling inhibitor was initiated. Follow‐up CT performed 6 months after initiation of treatment and 1 month after removal of the right ureteral stent demonstrated resolution of the right hydronephrosis (Figure 4C), supporting the clinical diagnosis.

Eighteen months later, despite a low PSA level (0.047 ng/mL), CT revealed irregular enlargement of the prostate. Repeat biopsy demonstrated neuroendocrine adenocarcinoma, leading to a diagnosis of treatment‐related neuroendocrine prostate cancer. The patient was subsequently treated with etoposide, cisplatin, and radiotherapy, resulting in disease remission.

Discussion

Ureteral metastasis from prostate cancer is extremely rare. Autopsy studies report an incidence of ureteral metastasis from all malignancies ranging from 0.1% to 4.3% [6, 7], with breast, gastric, and colorectal cancers being the most common primary sites [8]. Bilateral ureteral involvement causing renal failure as the initial manifestation of prostate cancer is particularly uncommon.

The differential diagnosis includes urothelial carcinoma, lymphoma, direct invasion from adjacent malignancies, inflammatory strictures, and retroperitoneal fibrosis [9, 10]. Although CT, MRI, and PET/CT are useful for evaluating ureteral lesions, imaging findings are not sufficiently specific to distinguish metastatic ureteral tumors from other causes [10].

The exact mechanism of ureteral metastasis remains unclear. Retrograde dissemination through periureteral lymphatic channels is considered the most widely accepted mechanism, resulting in tumor infiltration of the ureteral wall while sparing the urothelial mucosa [10, 11, 12]. Consequently, urine cytology and ureteroscopic biopsy frequently fail to establish a definitive diagnosis because metastatic lesions are often confined to the submucosa [11, 13, 14].

Ureteral metastases were classified into three pathological patterns: periureteral adventitial infiltration (Type I), transmural involvement (Type II), and mucosal involvement (Type III) [15]. Both of our cases were most consistent with Type II disease, characterized by ureteral wall infiltration without mucosal involvement. Although nephroureterectomy may occasionally provide pathological confirmation [4, 5, 8, 13, 14, 16], it is invasive and offers no therapeutic benefit. Therefore, diagnosis may depend on clinical findings and treatment response. In both cases, marked improvement in ureteral obstruction following ADT strongly supported the diagnosis.

Management of bilateral ureteral obstruction requires ureteral stenting or nephrostomy to preserve renal function. Neither approach has been shown to be clearly superior, and treatment should be individualized [17].

Most reported cases have involved advanced or castration‐resistant prostate cancer, or tumors with a Gleason score of 7 or higher [5, 8, 12, 14, 16, 18], suggesting that ureteral metastasis may preferentially occur in biologically aggressive disease. Although metastatic ureteral tumors are generally associated with poor prognosis [19, 20], ureteral metastases from castration‐sensitive prostate cancer may respond favorably to hormonal therapy. Long‐term disease control was achieved in Case 1.

This report has several limitations. First, pathological confirmation was not obtained in either case, and the diagnosis relied partly on therapeutic response. Second, ureteral stenting may have confounded assessment of treatment response. Finally, the findings are based on only two cases.

Conclusion

We report two rare cases of bilateral ureteral metastases from prostate cancer presenting with postrenal acute kidney injury. When pathological confirmation is difficult, improvement in ureteral obstruction after ADT may provide an important diagnostic clue.
