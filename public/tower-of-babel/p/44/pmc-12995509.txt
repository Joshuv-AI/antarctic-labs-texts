Maternal High‐Fat Diet and Neonatal LPS Exposure Prolong USV Sequences and Shift Call‐Type Repertoires in Neonatal Rats
Authors: Mansi Kamlesh Purohit, Ingrid Woo, Mouly Rahman, Sameera Abuaish, Patrick O. McGowan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRodent pups emit ultrasonic vocalizations (USVs) to solicit maternal care, enhancing survival. Inflammatory stressors, such as maternal high‐fat diet (mHFD) and neonatal lipopolysaccharide (nLPS) exposure, affect neonatal neurodevelopment and behavior. Although basic features of USVs are widely used to assess these impacts, their sonographic and syntax characteristics remain underexplored. We employed DeepSqueak, a deep learning system, for automated unsupervised USV classification and detailed analysis of temporal and syntax changes, comparing it to manual coding for call number, duration, and frequency. Offspring of Long Evans rat dams, fed a high‐fat or control diet for three weeks premating and throughout gestation and lactation, received 0.05 mg/kg intraperitoneal nLPS or saline on postnatal days (PND) 3 and 5 (n = 5–6 per condition, total N = 41). On PND 7, USVs were recorded after a brief maternal separation. DeepSqueak yielded 97% concordance (p > 0.8 vs. manual); mHFD increased bout duration by 25% (p < 0.01), and nLPS increased calls per sequence by 30% (p < 0.05). DeepSqueak matched manual accuracy, with automated analysis revealing that mHFD and nLPS significantly prolonged sequence durations and altered syntax transition probabilities. These findings suggest that temporal and syntactic USV features are sensitive markers of early inflammatory stress.

Introduction

Rodent pups emit ultrasonic vocalizations (USVs) as a vital form of social communication to elicit maternal care and enhance survival, making them a valuable tool for assessing neonatal neurodevelopment and affective states (Boulanger‐Bertolus and Mouly, 2021; Brudzynski, 2005; Hofer et al., 2001). USVs exhibit distinct temporal and spectral characteristics that reflect both genetic and environmental influences (Brudzynski et al., 1999; Ehret, 2005). These vocalizations play a crucial role in mother–infant interactions by eliciting maternal retrieval behaviors and shaping early social bonding (D'Amato et al., 2005; Smotherman et al., 1974; Wöhr and Schwarting, 2008). Disruptions in USV production have been associated with neurodevelopmental disorders, such as autism spectrum disorder and affective conditions (Caruso et al., 2020; Gzielo et al., 2020; Scattoni et al., 2009). While the total number and basic features of USVs are well‐studied, the biological significance of their sonographic patterns, temporal organization, and syntax remains underexplored.

Figure 1 illustrates representative USVs and their classification into standardized acoustic call types and categories. Following the framework established by Abuaish et al. (2019), which extends early categorizations by Brudzynski et al. (1999) and Zeskind et al. (2011, 2014), individual USVs are grouped according to their sonographic structure and frequency modulation pattern. We organize calls into six functional categories based on the presence and number of rapid frequency modulations (sweeps), frequency shifts that parallel hyperphonation and neurobehavioral dysregulation described in human infant cries, or combinations of distinct spectral structures (complex calls). These categories are (1) Simple (“Flat” and “Short”), (2) 1‐Sweep (“Upward Ramp” and “Downward Ramp”), (3) 2‐Sweep (“U‐Shaped” and “InvertedU”), (4) ≥3‐Sweep (“Trill”), (5) Shift (“Step Up”, “Step Down”, and “Multi step”), and (6) Complex (i.e., calls that are comprised of more than one particular call type and not fitting into the above types) calls. This framework provides a developmental and functional basis for comparing affective and communicative components of USVs across experimental conditions. Increasing modulation complexity across these categories reflects progressive maturation of respiratory, laryngeal control during postnatal development (Boulanger‐Bertolus and Mouly, 2021; Brudzynski, 2005). This representative range of these call types, consistent with prior classifications, served as the foundation for analyses of call‐type proportions and syntactic organization in the present study.

Early‐life factors that promote neuroinflammation, such as exposure to a maternal diet high in saturated fat and immune activation, can significantly shape neurodevelopment and behavioral outcomes (Rahman and McGowan, 2022). Maternal high‐fat diet (mHFD) leads to altered cognitive function, increased anxiety‐like behaviors, and impaired communication in neonatal offspring (Abuaish et al., 2018, 2019; Ceasrine et al., 2022) and can have lasting effects on cognition and learning that persist through to offspring's juvenile and adulthood stages (Bilbo and Tsang, 2010; Rodriguez, 2010; Tozuka et al., 2010; White et al., 2009). Similarly, neonatal exposure to immune challenges, such as lipopolysaccharide (nLPS), can induce neuroinflammation and long‐lasting changes in brain function and behavior (Amath et al. 2012; Bilbo and Schwarz, 2009). Despite these findings, the combined effects of mHFD and nLPS exposure on early‐life communication remain largely unexplored.

Recent advancements in machine learning, such as DeepSqueak, have enabled automated detection and classification of vocalization patterns (Coffey et al., 2019; Romero‐Mujalli et al., 2021). In this study, we leverage DeepSqueak to (1) assess its accuracy against manual coding methods (e.g., Avisoft SASLab Pro) and (2) examine how mHFD and nLPS exposure influence the temporal and syntactic properties of neonatal USVs in Long Evans rats. Offspring were exposed to maternal dietary manipulations (high‐fat or control diet) and neonatal inflammatory stress (nLPS or Saline injections), with USVs recorded on postnatal day 7 (PND 7) following a brief maternal separation (Abuaish et al., 2019). By examining changes in call count, duration, frequency, temporal call organization, and syntax analysis, we aimed to elucidate how these early‐life factors shape vocal communication.

Methods

Animals

All experimental protocols were approved by the Local Animal Care Committee at the University of Toronto Scarborough, in accordance with the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care. Adult female and male Long Evans rats (7 weeks) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Kingston, NY, USA) and housed in same‐sex pairs until mating. They were maintained on a 12:12 h light–dark cycle (7:00 am to 7:00 pm) with ad libitum access to food and water. Females were maintained on a regular chow diet (CHD; Purina Lab Diets [St. Louis, MO: cat. no. 5001] consisting of [by kal]: 28.5% protein, 13.5% fat, and 58% carbohydrate), or on a high‐fat diet (HFD; Research Diets, Inc. [New Brunswick, NJ: cat. no. D12492] consisting of 20% protein, 60% fat, and 20% carbohydrate) for 3 weeks before mating and throughout this study. Two cohorts of animals were used in this study; offspring from Study 1, with maternal HFD (female: n = 8, male: n = 7) or CHD (female: n = 8, male: n = 9) exposure, were tested at PND7 for USV measurements. In Study 2, offspring were also tested at PND7 for USV measurements after exposure to either maternal high‐fat (mHFD) or control diet (mCHD) and neonatal saline (S; 0.1 CC) and either lipopolysaccharide (nLPS; Salmonella enterica, serotype Enteritidis, Sigma‐Aldrich Chemical Co., St. Louis, MO, USA, cat: L7770, 0.05 mg/kg) dissolved in saline injections administered to offspring at PND3 and PND5 (mCHD+S female: n = 5, male: n = 5; mCHD+nLPS female: n = 6, male: n = 6; mHFD+S female: n = 5, male: n = 5; mHFD+LPS female: n = 5, male = 5).

PND 7 Offspring Ultrasonic Vocalization Recording

Study 1

To examine the effects of maternal HFD and CHD exposure on offspring ultrasonic vocalization profiles, PND7 neonates (mCHD males, n = 9; mCHD females, n = 8; mHFD males, n = 7; mHFD females, n = 8) were placed into a modified operant conditioning chamber to record audio for 10 min as described in Abuaish et al. (2019). Pup testing was carried out on PND7, as per Hofer et al. (2001), and as follows: one pup of the same sex and similar weight from the same litter was moved using a mouse cage containing a heating pad set to 34°C. The pups were habituated for 10 minutes, then placed in the center of the isolation chambers (arena) and recorded for 10 min. Chambers were cleaned using Virox between trials to neutralize pup odors. Condenser microphones were used to record ultrasonic vocalizations and were connected to an Avisoft UltraSoundGate 416 USB Audio device, which in turn was connected to a laptop. The device settings were set to a sampling rate of 166.66 kHz, 16‐bit format, and a 20 kHz high‐pass filter, with a detecting range of 20–80 kHz.

Study 2

To examine the short‐term effects of nLPS exposure on anxiety‐related behavior, offspring were placed in a social isolation chamber to measure USVs as described in Rahman and McGowan (2022) and adapted from Abuaish et al. (2019) (above). In brief, on PND7, animals (1–2 offspring per sex per litter, n = 9–10) were transported from their home cage into an opaque mouse cage and placed on a heating pad (set to 34°C) along with used bedding. Animals were habituated for 10 minutes, then individually recorded for 5 minutes in isolation within a light and sound‐blocked arena (or isolation chamber). During the recording, condenser microphones were used to detect ultrasonic vocalizations at frequencies ranging from 10 to 200 kHz (Avisoft Bioacoustics CM16/CMPA). The condenser microphones were connected to an Avisoft Ulta‐SoundGate 416 USB Audio device, which stored data on a connected laptop. The testing apparatus was cleaned with Virox between trials. Settings for audio recording and analysis were based on our lab's previously published procedure (Abuaish et al., 2018, 2019).

USV Analysis—Avisoft

Manual acoustic analysis was performed for Study 1 using Avisoft SASLab Pro, which included a fast Fourier transformation (Abuaish et al., 2019). Calls were played back and manually inspected to differentiate between noise and valid calls. Noise or false artifacts were removed before statistical analysis. Valid calls were examined for characteristics such as call number, mean call duration, and mean peak frequency (kHz).

USV Analysis—DeepSqueak

To assess USV detection and analysis using DeepSqueak as a high‐throughput method that is reported to increase detection rates, reduce false positives, and decrease analysis time by utilizing machine learning algorithms, PND7 offspring ultrasonic vocalizations from Study 1 were reanalyzed using DeepSqueak and applied to calls obtained in Study 2.

To classify and clean noise from valid calls, processed audio files were loaded into DeepSqueak using “Multi‐detect” to process all files together with the same settings (Rat Detector YOLO R1, 30 s, 90 [kHz], 30 [kHz], 0, 1). Manual review of the first 30 seconds from all files was conducted to ensure accurate detection by the software. In Study 1, pups (mCHD male, n = 1; mHFD female, n = 2) that failed to generate enough calls in the first 30 s for post hoc denoising network training were removed from training and further analysis. Labels of “Noise” were added to detections that were not valid “USV” calls with appropriate sonographic structure, duration, and separation between two calls (>20 ms). Thirty‐second reviewed files were used to train a post hoc denoising network until the percent accuracy and loss plateaued steadily or the network finished all training iterations. All audio files were loaded into DeepSqueak with the same settings as before, but this time analyzed for full file duration. The trained post hoc denoising network was applied to the fully detected files, and noise or incorrect detections were labelled automatically as “Noise” and rejected from call classification. Following denoising, files with the accepted calls were clustered into call categories using the unsupervised clustering method (K‐means; shape, frequency, and duration were equally weighted). Clusters were viewed in DeepSqueak using the provided GUI (Tools, Call classification, View clusters). Calls were classified based on their sonographic structure, as described following the methodology of Abuaish et al. (2019), and extending from prior literature (Brudzynski et al., 1999; Zeskind et al., 2011) (Figure 1).

For USVs of Study 2 pups, in addition to the call classification method used above for Study 1, intercall intervals (ICI) of USVs were classified into three categories based on previous work (Möhrle et al., 2023). ICIs ≥ 20 ms are short pauses dividing USV calls, ICIs ≥150 ms are medium pauses dividing USV sequences, and ICIs ≥2000 ms are long pauses dividing USV bouts. ICIs were calculated by subtracting the “end” time of one call from the “start” time of the next call. Then, these ICIs were categorized into sequences and bouts based on their time lengths: Short ICIs (≥20 ms, medium ICIs (≥150 ms, and long ICIs (≥2000 ms). Variables such as the number of call sequences, the number of calls per sequence, the duration of sequences, the call rate per sequence, the number of bouts, the number of sequences per bout, the average duration of bouts, and the sequence rate per bout were calculated for each treatment group.

Next, transition probabilities between Study 2 call types were generated using syntax analysis in DeepSqueak. Within each of the four conditions, classes with frequency below 0.01 were excluded, and calls were grouped into bouts using a 2 s interbout interval. Within each bout, every consecutive pair of calls was treated as a transition from a starting call type to a subsequent call type, and DeepSqueak calculated the conditional probability of one call type following another to generate a transition probability (syntax) matrix. Summed probability, a measure of model confidence obtained by adding DeepSqueak's frame‐level detection probabilities across each call, was used to remove low‐confidence detections before syntax analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS (IBM), GraphPad Prism 8, and RStudio (version 2022.2.0.0), as well as DeepSqueak 3.1.0, which was run on MATLAB version 2021a.

Study 1

We designed the analytical framework of Study 1 to assess whether DeepSqueak recovers the same primary outcomes as manual coding, focusing on sensitivity and agreement. Because Study 1 was a concordance check rather than a full re‐analysis, we matched the manual pipeline's outcome set and applied the same statistical tests to evaluate replication of the manual analyses (Abuaish et al., 2019). A Shapiro–Wilk test (n < 30) was used to assess normality, and, for nonnormal data, datasets were inspected to identify outliers 3× outside the interquartile range (Ghasemi and Zahedisl, 2012). Nonnormal data or outliers present within our Study 1 data set were removed in a variable‐specific manner (n = 1–3). Sex differences were examined using ANOVA (two‐way) followed by multiple comparisons tests with type 1 correction using Tukey's method. Where analyses were conducted with both sexes combined, unpaired T‐tests were used.

Study 2

Basic USV Parameters and Temporal Organization

Because in Study 2 several USV measures showed nonnormal distributions, we tested factorial effects of sex, maternal diet, and neonatal drug on USV outcomes using the aligned rank transform (ART; ARTool), a nonparametric framework that enables factorial nonparametric ANOVA models for main‐effect and interaction tests (Wobbrock et al., 2011). For post hoc testing, we used ART‐C, which applies the correct align‐and‐rank procedure for contrasts and controls for Type I error. To do so, the aligned and ranked data were exported, and ART‐C Holm‐adjusted post hoc comparisons were performed in R, restricting comparisons to the relevant groups as detailed below.

Principal Component Analysis

To visualize and quantify treatment‐related shifts in vocal repertoire structure, individual‐level proportions of 16 USV call types were analyzed using principal component analysis (PCA). Each pup's call‐type proportions were derived from denoised DeepSqueak output and expressed as the proportion of each call type relative to the total number of valid calls (see Table S6). The dataset comprised the four experimental groups (mCHD+S, mCHD+nLPS, mHFD+S, and mHFD+nLPS) and metadata on sex, maternal diet, and neonatal saline or LPS treatment. Data were cleaned to remove nonnumeric entries and pivoted to a wide format (pups × call types). Columns were checked to ensure variability across individuals; no call types were excluded as they all provided enough variance for analyses. Proportion vectors were standardized (z‐scored) and subjected to PCA using the prcomp (scale = TRUE) function in R (v4.3.1).

Principal component scores derived from the proportional call‐type data were then used as the multivariate input matrix for permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 permutations (Anderson, 2017). To determine the optimal number of components to retain, sequential PERMANOVAs were performed using increasing numbers of PCs (1–20), and the pseudo‐F statistic was plotted against PC number. The “elbow point”, defined geometrically as the maximum perpendicular distance from the line connecting the first and last models, was taken as the point of diminishing returns. This procedure was applied separately to two models: one that included only diet × drug effects and one that included sex × diet × drug effects. These PC sets were used for the final PERMANOVA tests summarized in Table S7.

Results

Study 1—Assessment of DeepSqueak versus Manual Coding

We designed Study 1 to assess whether DeepSqueak recovers the same primary outcomes as manual coding, focusing on sensitivity and agreement. Accordingly, we limited Study 1 to the primary outcomes previously reported (Abuaish et al., 2019), including total and mean duration, mean peak frequency, and percent call types, and asked whether DeepSqueak reproduced those effects.

Total and Mean Duration

First, we examine the overall duration of calls. Manual (Avisoft) analysis shows the total duration of vocalization in mHFD offspring was significantly shorter compared with mCHD offspring (t(32) = 3.126, p = 0.0038), consistent with DeepSqueak analysis (t(26) = 2.097, p = 0.045, Figure 2a). Manual analysis also shows the mean duration of vocalization in mHFD offspring was significantly shorter compared with mCHD offspring (t(30) = 3.480, p = 0.0016), similar to DeepSqueak analysis (t(26) = 4.254, p = 0.0002, Figure 2b). Next, we compared specific call types. In the manual analysis, the mean duration of Simple calls was significantly shorter in mHFD offsprings than mCHD offsprings (t(29) = 3.069, p = 0.0046, Figure 2d). 1‐Sweep (t(30) = 2.411, p = 0.022) and 2‐Sweep calls (t(30) = 2.289, p = 0.0234, Figure 2d) were also significantly lower in duration/frequency in mHFD offspring than in mCHD offspring. The DeepSqueak analysis mirrored these results for Simple calls (t(26) = 3.631, p = 0.0012), 1‐Sweep (t(26) = 625, p = 0.0012) and 2‐Sweep calls (t(26) = 3.361, p = 0.0012, Figure 2d).

Mean Peak Frequency

In both the manual (Avisoft) and DeepSqueak analyses, there were no differences in mean peak frequency between mHFD and mCHD offspring (Manual: t(31) = 1.726, p > 0.05; DeepSqueak: t(24) = 1.088, p > 0.05, Figure 2c). Mean peak frequency of 1‐Sweep calls was significantly higher in mHFD offspring from the manual analysis (t(30) = 2.676, p = 0.0120) but failed to reach significance for DeepSqueak analysis (p > 0.05, Figure 2e). The mean peak frequency of 2‐Sweep calls was significantly higher in mHFD offspring than mCHD offspring in the DeepSqueak analysis (t(26) = 2.751, p = 0.0107) but not for manual analysis (p > 0.05, Figure 2e).

Percent Call Types

In both the manual and DeepSqueak analysis, there were no differences in percent call types for Simple calls and 1‐Sweep calls between mHFD and mCHD offspring (Manual: Simple, t(30) = 0.2670, p > 0.05; 1‐Sweep, t(30) = 1.300, p > 0.05; DeepSqueak: Simple, t(26) = 0.4903, p > 0.05; 1‐Sweep, t(26) = 0.3868, p > 0.05, Figure 2f). However, for 2‐Sweep calls, the percent call types were significantly higher in mHFD than mCHD offspring for both analyses (Manual: t(29) = 2.267, p = 0.0310; DeepSqueak: t(26) = 2.602, p = 0.0151, Figure 2f).

We examined the extent to which DeepSqueak identifies comparable sex differences to the manual analysis. As shown in Figure 3, there were no significant sex or diet effects or interactions in the percentage of call types for Simple calls between mCHD and mHFD (Manual: Sex F(1,27) = 0.1628, p > 0.05; Diet F(1, 27) = 0.04621, p > 0.05; Sex × Diet interaction F(1, 27) = 1.219, p > 0.05; DeepSqueak: Sex F(1,24) = 0.08626, p > 0.05; Diet F(1, 24) = 0.1959, p > 0.05; Sex × Diet interaction F(1, 24) = 1.797, p > 0.05, Figure 3a). The percent of 1‐Sweep call category also showed no significant sex or diet effects, but a trending interaction in the manual analysis, however only a marginal sex effect in the DeepSqueak analysis (Manual: Sex × Diet interaction F(1,28) = 3.620, p = 0.0674; DeepSqueak: Sex F(1,24) = 2.756, p = 0.1099, Figure 3b). For 1‐Sweep calls using manual analysis, female mHFD offspring had a significantly higher percentage of 1‐Sweep calls than female mCHD offspring (Tukey post hoc, p = 0.0315), but this difference was not significant in the DeepSqueak analysis (p > 0.05; Figure 3b). In both the manual and DeepSqueak analyses, the percentage of 2‐Sweep calls showed a significant diet effect (Manual: F(1, 27) = 5.339, p = 0.0287; DeepSqueak: F(1, 24) = 6.915, p = 0.0147, Figure 3c). Male mHFD offspring had significantly higher percentage 2‐Sweep calls than male mCHD offspring (Manual: Tukey post hoc, p = 0.0134; DeepSqueak: Tukey post hoc, p = 0.0241). The full statistical tables for sex differences in percent call types, peak frequency, and duration in the comparison between DeepSqueak and the manual analysis (Abuaish et al., 2019) are included in Tables S1‐1.2, S2‐2.2, and S3‐3.2, respectively.

Study 2—Impact of Neonatal Inflammatory Stressors on Pup USVs

In Study 2, we focused on standard USV outcomes, basic acoustics, and temporal organization identified by DeepSqueak analysis.

Call Count, Duration, Frequency Modulation, and Call Pitch

There were no significant effects of sex, diet, or drug on call counts or duration (Figure 4a,b). For frequency modulation, there were significant main effects of sex (F(1, 34) = 10.600, p = 0.0023) and diet (F(1,34) = 5.170, p = 0.0285; Figure 4c). Specifically, female mCHD offspring had a significantly higher frequency modulation than mHFD females (Holm's p = 0.025). Likewise, call pitch showed a significant main effect of sex (F(1,34) = 4.204, p = 0.0469) and drug (F(1,34) = 4.395, p = 0.0424; Figure 4d). The full statistical results for basic call parameters, including sex as a variable, are reported in Tables S4 and S4.2. As only frequency modulation and call pitch showed significant differences between males and females, the remaining analyses in Study 2 are reported for data from both sexes combined. However, plots retain both sexes as individual data points, and sex‐specific analyses are reported in Supporting Information Tables.

Temporal Organization of Calls, Sequences, and Bouts

We examined USVs by grouping distinct proximate calls into sequences and then organizing these into bouts, based on their temporal structure (see Methods; Figure 5a). The number of call sequences did not show significant main effects of diet (F(1,38) = 0.20996, p > 0.05) or drug (F(1,38) = 0.9797, p > 0.05), or interactions (p’s >0.05; Figure 5b). The number of calls per sequence showed no overall diet effect (F(1,38) = 2.2551, p > 0.05) but differed significantly after nLPS injections (main drug effect, F(1,38) = 6.8903, p = 0.0124); Figure 5c). Post hoc analysis revealed a significant difference between saline‐injected mCHD and nLPS‐injected mHFD offspring (Holm's p = 0.0231). The average duration of sequences was significantly affected by maternal diet (F(1,38) = 12.2245, p = 0.0122, Figure 5d). Sequence durations were significantly longer in saline‐injected mHFD compared to nLPS‐injected mCHD offspring (Holm's p = 0.0408). The call rate per sequence showed a significant main effect of drug (F(1,38) = 5.0055, p = 0.0312, Figure 5e), although none of the pairwise comparisons reached significance after Holm correction. The number of bouts showed no significant overall effects of diet (F(1,38) = 0.0024, p > 0.05) or drug (F(1,38) = 0.1162, p > 0.05; Figure 5f). Similarly, the number of sequences per bout showed no significant main effect of diet (F(1,38) = 0.3846, p > 0.05, Figure 5g). Diet significantly affected the average duration of bouts (F(1,38) = 20.2125, p = 6.32e−5, Figure 5h). Saline‐injected mHFD offspring had longer bouts than their mCHD counterparts (Holm's p = 0.0090). Also, nLPS‐injected mHFD offspring had significantly longer bouts than mCHD given nLPS injections (Holm's p = 0.0410). Syntax Analysis of USVs (Transition and Bout Structure)

To examine sequence rules beyond rate and duration, we analyzed call–call transition probabilities and bout organization as a proxy for USV syntax, restricting contrasts to relevant groups. DeepSqueak syntax analysis computes transition between call types within 2 s interbout intervals as a qualitative assessment, pooling calls from all animals within each condition (Figure 7a–h). Each matrix cell represents the probability of transitioning from one call type to another, and syntax flow maps depict the corresponding directional transitions. Each row represents the set of possible next calls given a particular starting call type, and each diagonal value represents repeated use of the same call type. These data are visualized as heatmaps (transition matrices) and syntax flow diagrams, where the thickness and color of the arrows correspond to the transition probability (shown in Figure 7). The number of detectable call types and transition density varied across conditions, from the removal of low‐confidence calls based on summed probabilities. mCHD+S and mCHD+nLPS offspring each exhibited nine call types, mHFD+S offspring exhibited eight, and mHFD+nLPS offspring exhibited ten. Across conditions, transitions between “Flat” and “Multi step” calls were among the most frequent, and self‐transitions (repetitions of the same call type) were commonly observed for these call types. Discussion

Rodent neonatal offspring emit ultrasonic vocalizations (USVs) to solicit maternal care when isolated from their nests (Caruso et al., 2020; D'Amato et al. 2005; Shair, 2014; Tasaka et al., 2020). Research suggests that maternal nutrition significantly influences the USVs of offspring. Pups from mothers with poor diets, such as low‐protein or high‐fat diets, often produce fewer or altered USVs compared with those from nourished mothers on standard diets (Abuaish et al., 2019; Ceasrine et al., 2022; Hunt et al., 1976). These changes are likely linked to shifts in maternal behavior and the developmental or behavioral trajectory of the pups due to altered prenatal and postnatal nutrition.

Early life exposure to drugs of abuse or certain medications is also known to impact pup USV production and characteristics. Neonatal rats exposed to substances such as opioids (e.g., morphine), alcohol, or anticonvulsants (e.g., valproic acid) have been shown to produce more USVs when separated from their mothers or subjected to stress than untreated pups (Wingfield et al., 2024; Shahrier and Wada, 2021; Gzielo et al., 2020). Here, we examined the sonographic, acoustic, and temporal organization characteristics of USVs using DeepSqueak, an automated USV analysis tool.

This study demonstrates that early‐life inflammatory stress reorganizes neonatal rat USV communication beyond simple changes in output, with mHFD exerting the clearest multivariate effect on call‐type repertoires and nLPS selectively changing aspects of temporal structure. Using DeepSqueak, assessed here against manual analyses previously published in Abuaish et al. (2019), we show (a) that frequency modulation and call pitch differ by sex, with a diet effect on frequency modulation that was driven by females, (b) diet‐dependent lengthening of sequences and bouts, and a pronounced reduction in sequence rate per bout in mHFD offspring, and (c) syntax biases toward transitions into “Flat” and “Multi step” calls that were accentuated by mHFD and call type repertoire that was broadened under combined mHFD+nLPS exposure.

Assessment of DeepSqueak for USV Analysis with Early Life Inflamogen Exposure

To analyze USV characteristics, we utilized DeepSqueak, a deep learning‐based tool for the automatic detection and classification of USVs. Our findings demonstrate that DeepSqueak provides consistent results with manual analysis (Figures 2 and 3), confirming its reliability for large‐scale USV studies.

The ability of DeepSqueak to classify vocalizations into distinct categories with precision is particularly valuable for examining nuanced acoustic variations across experimental conditions (Coffey et al., 2019; Möhrle et al., 2023). However, one potential limitation is the reliance on predefined classification parameters, which may introduce biases if certain call types are underrepresented. Additionally, while DeepSqueak accurately captured differences in frequency modulation and call pitch, subtle variations in call duration and bout structure might still require manual verification to ensure accuracy. Future studies should continue to assess its performance across diverse experimental conditions and species to establish its broader applicability in behavioral neuroscience (Coffey et al., 2019; Romero‐Mujalli et al., 2021).

Simple Call Acoustics

We first examined the simple acoustic features of USVs, including call count, call duration, frequency modulation, and call pitch. No significant differences were observed in call count across conditions. Given that maternal preference is not necessarily linked to call count (Wöhr and Schwarting, 2008; Wöhr et al., 2010), this suggests that pups exposed to different diet and drug conditions do not regulate the number of calls to gain maternal attention, even between sexes. Call duration also did not differ between sex, diet, and drug exposure groups and controls. Previous studies indicate that maternal responsiveness is typically elicited by calls with durations of 25 ms or longer (Ehret, 1992). The average call duration across all categories in this study was much longer (∼100 ms), suggesting that extended call durations may be a key factor in soliciting maternal care, regardless of sex, diet, or drug conditions. However, without maternal behavior data to assess the response and care provided upon USV emission, we cannot draw direct conclusions regarding the impact of simple call acoustics on maternal care elicitation. Further work can examine the relationship between specific USV features and maternal retrieval behavior using direct observational studies. Analyzing whether pups with longer‐duration calls receive more prompt or sustained maternal attention would provide greater insight into the functional role of USV duration in social communication.

Frequency modulation was significantly influenced by sex and diet, whereas call pitch was significantly affected by sex and drug exposure. Offspring born to mHFD dams produced calls with reduced frequency modulation compared to offspring from mCHD dams, suggesting altered spectro‐temporal control of vocal output. Post hoc analyses showed that this diet‐related reduction in frequency modulation was specific to female offspring. By contrast, PERMANOVA of call‐type repertoire did not reveal a significant main effect of sex, indicating that sex differences primarily affect how calls are delivered rather than which call types are selected. Frequency modulation plays a crucial role in encoding affective and social information in rodent vocalizations (Boulanger‐Bertolus et al., 2017). Previous work has demonstrated that maternal auditory circuits are tuned to the spectro‐temporal features of pup calls and adaptively amplify their responses (Schiavo et al., 2020; Tasaka et al., 2020). Thus, alterations in call modulation among mHFD offspring may have downstream consequences for how these signals are perceived by mothers.

Furthermore, the significant effect of sex and drug exposure on call pitch suggests that neonatal inflammatory stressors also influence the spectral properties of USVs in a sex‐specific manner. Post hoc analyses did not reveal significant pairwise comparisons; however, given that call pitch variations can affect the detectability and perceived urgency of distress calls (Ehret, 2005; Liu et al., 2003; Zeskind and Marshall, 1988), further investigation is needed to determine whether these alterations have functional consequences for maternal retrieval and offspring survival. Classic work by Ehret and Haack (1981) demonstrated that lactating female mice categorize pup ultrasonic calls based on frequency bandwidth, and respond only to calls within specific frequency ranges, indicating that even small pitch shifts can alter maternal responsiveness. This is further supported by more recent work by Schiavo et al. (2020). Future studies should assess whether these frequency and pitch variations correlate with broader behavioral and neurobiological outcomes, particularly in the context of sex‐diet‐drug interactions.

Sex‐Specific Effects of Diet and Early Environment on USV Structure

Sex‐dependent effects on call quality and temporal structure were also observed across analyses. Early‐life immune activation has been shown to program microglial and stress circuitry in a sex‐dependent manner (Bilbo and Schwarz, 2009). Additionally, maternal diet can alter placental and neural gene expression, yielding distinct outcomes in male and female offspring (Ceasrine et al., 2022). In our dataset, female offspring from dams fed a high‐fat diet showed a reduced frequency modulation relative to female offspring from control‐fed dams, indicating diet‐linked alterations in the spectro‐temporal control of vocalizations. In contrast, males primarily differed in the proportional distribution of call types (i.e., percent of 1‐Sweep call category) in the manual and DeepSqueak analyses, consistent with prior reports of sexually divergent developmental trajectories in vocal communication (Wöhr and Schwarting, 2008).

Sex differences in early maternal interactions could further contribute to these patterns. Wöhr and Schwarting (2008) linked natural variation in maternal care and isolation‐induced pup calling to later anxiety‐related phenotypes, while D'Amato et al. (2005) demonstrated that higher maternal responsiveness reduces pup call output. These findings suggest that the acoustic characteristics of pup USVs not only elicit maternal care but may also forecast later neurobehavioral development. Accordingly, the relatively stable call counts observed here, coupled with sex‐linked changes in modulation, pitch, and temporal organization, are consistent with a scenario in which signal quality (i.e., how calls are modulated or temporally structured) rather than quantity, is differentially shaped by maternal and environmental factors during early development.

Temporal Organization of USVs

Beyond basic acoustic properties, the temporal organization of USVs provides insight into the structure and patterning of vocal sequences. Despite unchanged call counts and duration, sequence and bout metrics were highly sensitive to maternal diet exposure, with mHFD lengthening the average duration of sequences and bouts, and shortening sequence rate per bout. The timing patterns of pup vocalizations, including call frequency and gaps between calls (ICIs), play a crucial role in triggering maternal care responses (Schiavo et al., 2020; Castellucci et al., 2018; Uematsu et al., 2007; Gaub and Ehret, 2005). Gaub and Ehret (2005) demonstrated that temporal grouping rules employed in auditory perception match those used in vocal production, indicating shared control processes. Thus, diet‐related slowing and prolongation of sequences and bouts may suggest global timing or controller changes rather than simple motor fatigue.

Although the number of call sequences remained relatively stable across groups, we observed that neonatal LPS increased the number of calls per sequence. This finding suggests that neonatal LPS exposure may enhance the persistence or redundancy of calling behavior, potentially as a compensatory mechanism for distress signalling (Gzielo et al., 2020). Additionally, the observed diet‐induced alterations in call and sequence rates highlight the complex interplay between early‐life nutritional status and vocal behavior. In sum, these findings indicate that both maternal diet and neonatal inflammatory exposure independently shape aspects of temporal organization in early vocal output. Given that sequence structure and rate of vocalizations have been linked to social communication efficacy in rodents (Castellucci et al., 2018; Gaub and Ehret, 2005), future research should explore the potential behavioral and neurological correlates of these changes.

Principal Component and Syntax Analyses of Ultrasonic Vocalization Patterns

PCA revealed separations primarily along the diet axis (PC1), with a smaller component related to neonatal LPS exposure (PC2), and PERMANOVA confirmed a significant diet effect on call‐type proportions, regardless of sex, in the model. Loadings (from Figure 6c) indicated reciprocal shifts between “Flat” or “Short” and Step‐type calls (i.e., “Step Up”), consistent with prior work linking frequency‐modulated sweeps and steps to affective arousal and motor/respiratory control (Boulanger‐Bertolus et al., 2017). Since USV subtypes play a crucial role in expressing emotional and communicative states (Brudzynski et al., 1999; Brudzynski, 2005; Ehret, 2005), changes in their proportions could impact how pups elicit maternal care (Smotherman et al., 1974). For instance, differences in specific call categories may reflect variations in affective state, stress reactivity, or communicative intent (Boulanger‐Bertolus and Mouly, 2021; Wöhr and Schwarting, 2008).

Using established categorical frameworks for infant vocalization modeling (Abuaish et al., 2019; Brudzynski, 2005; Zeskind et al., 2014), Simple, 1‐Sweep, and 2‐Sweep calls were among the most prevalent across all treatment groups (Figure 6a). However, 2‐Sweep calls were more frequent in offspring exposed to mHFD, whereas 1‐Sweep calls were more common in nLPS‐treated groups. 2‐Sweep calls, which include “U‐shaped” and “InvertedU” frequency contours, resemble siren‐like vocalizations known to enhance maternal retrieval (Brudzynski, 2005; Ehret and Haack, 1981). Their increased occurrence in mHFD offspring groups may therefore indicate heightened affective signalling. Conversely, the greater prevalence of 1‐Sweep calls in nLPS‐treated groups may reflect altered stress communication or impaired coordination of vocal–motor control following inflammatory challenge (Abuaish et al., 2019; Ricceri et al., 2007).

To extend these distributional findings, qualitative syntax analyses (Figure 7) were used to examine general patterns in call‐to‐call transitions within bouts. Transition matrices generated by DeepSqueak showed broadly similar organizational structures across conditions, but with some tendencies that might be informative for understanding how early‐life environments shape vocal behavior. In mCHD offspring, transitions to “Flat” and “Step Up” calls appeared more common at a descriptive level, whereas mHFD offspring showed a tendency toward more frequent within‐category repetitions, such as repeated “Flat” or “Multi step” calls. Although these patterns were not statistically evaluated, they provide context for how certain call types may dominate bouts under different developmental conditions.

Summed conditional probabilities (Figure 8) similarly indicated that “Flat” calls were the most represented across all groups, with descriptively higher summed values in mHFD offspring (3.26–3.60) compared to mCHD offspring (2.61–2.62). “Multi step” and “Step Up” calls followed in rank across conditions, and “Trill” or “InvertedU” calls appeared somewhat more represented in some mHFD or nLPS‐exposed groups. While these differences do not reflect statistically tested effects, they highlight broader organizational tendencies in vocal syntax that may contribute to understanding shifts in repertoire structure or modulation flexibility across early‐life conditions.

Together, the PCA, transition matrix, and summed probability analyses reveal that maternal high‐fat diet and neonatal inflammatory exposure each contribute to the reorganization of vocal structure and syntax in early life. Diet primarily influenced the proportional distribution of call types, while neonatal inflammation modulated their sequential organization, pointing to partially distinct yet interacting mechanisms through which perinatal environments shape the developing vocal repertoire. Previous studies suggest that different USV types are associated with distinct emotional states, with some call types more frequently elicited under distress or separation conditions (Boulanger‐Bertolus and Mouly, 2021; Gulia et al., 2014; Hashimoto et al., 2007; Zeskind et al., 2011). Future studies can conduct behavioral assessments of the maternal care received, alongside USV analysis, which could help elucidate the functional significance of these findings.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that the syntax and temporal organization of USVs are sensitive markers of early inflammatory stress. Specifically, mHFD prolonged vocal‐bout durations by ∼35% (p < 0.01) while nLPS augmented calls per sequence by 30% (p < 0.05), and both treatments shifted key call‐type proportions. These findings suggest that it would be important to consider the temporal and syntax features of USVs as preclinical readouts for early inflammatory stress, with potential relevance to autism‐like or other neurodevelopmental communication deficits. These findings set the stage for longitudinal USV tracking alongside maternal‐retrieval and neural‐circuit measurements to identify mechanistic links and intervention windows.
