Oral health-related quality of life and diabetes outcomes: Evidence from a nationally representative U.S. sample
Authors: Hsueh-Fen Chen, Jou-Hsun Lee, Huey-Er Lee, Ping-Shen Lin, L Joseph Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposeThe association between self-perceived oral health–related quality of life (OHRQoL) and objective diabetes outcomes is largely unknown. This study examined six domains in Locker’s framework, focusing on poor OHRQoL associated with adverse diabetes outcomes—poor glycemic control, diabetes-related complications, and polypharmacy—among U.S. adults with diabetes.Materials and methodsData were drawn from the 2021–2023 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. The sample included 1179 adults aged ≥20 with diagnosed or undiagnosed diabetes (HbA1c ≥ 6.5 %), representing approximately 30.2 million U.S. adults. Six survey questions (OHQ620–OHQ680) from the Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP) were analyzed individually to reflect Locker’s conceptual domains. Outcomes included HbA1c ≥ 8 %, diabetes-related complications, and polypharmacy (defined as ≥ 5 prescribed medications). Weighted logistic regressions estimated odds ratios (OR) controlling for sociodemographic and behavioral factors.ResultsPoor ratings in the domains of handicap (OHQ630), social disability (OHQ640), and psychological discomfort (OHQ680) were associated with higher odds of adverse outcomes. Specifically, individuals with poor OHQ630 and OHQ640 had greater odds of poor glycemic control (range of OR = 1.637–2.644) and diabetes-related complications (range of OR = 2.220–2.262), and poor OHQ630 and OHQ680 had greater odds of having polypharmacy (range of OR = 1.697–2.225) compared with those without poor ratings.ConclusionSelf-perceived poor OHRQoL is associated with objective diabetes outcomes, highlighting the need for integrated medical-dental care for diabetes as a public health priority. Additionally, integrating OHIP screening into community outreach programs could help identify individuals at risk for diabetes and oral health problems, facilitating early interventions.Keywords: Diabetes-related complications, Glycated hemoglobin, Polypharmacy, Oral health-related quality of life, Oral health impact profile

Introduction

In 1988, Locker proposed a multidimensional conceptual framework that describes how oral diseases impair quality of life across seven domains: functional, physical pain, psychological discomfort, physical disability, psychological disability, social disability, and handicap.1 Based on the conceptual framework, the Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP) was developed as a validated tool to measure oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL), reflecting each domain and capturing how individuals perceive oral health problems in terms of their impact on quality of life.2,3 For example, individuals may avoid particular foods due to oral health problems related to the domain of physical disability.2,3 OHIP has since been widely applied to assess changes in OHRQoL before and after dental treatment, between individuals with and without chronic diseases, and to identify disparities due to race/ethnicity and socioeconomic status.4–7

OHRQoL among patients with diabetes has drawn significant attention because of a bidirectional relationship between diabetes and periodontal diseases,8–12 a chronic inflammatory condition that destroys the supporting structures of the teeth and contributes to pain, chewing difficulties, and tooth loss if untreated.13 Patients with diabetes are likely to have periodontal diseases, and for those with both conditions, they are likely to have insulin resistance.8,14,15 The systematic review and meta-analysis showed that individuals with type II diabetes experience significantly poorer OHRQoL compared to those without diabetes.16

According to Lockers’ conceptual framework, poor OHRQoL limits patients’ abilities to manage their diet control. As poor diet control accounts for about 25 % of diabetes-related mortality and 35 % of disability adjusted life years,17 poor OHRQoL would eventually worsen diabetes outcomes. Despite the theoretical assumption, empirical evidence regarding whether poor OHRQoL is associated with objective diabetes outcomes is lacking. The present study addresses this critical gap by examining objective diabetes-related outcomes—poor glycemic control, diabetes-related complications, and polypharmacy—which are available in the 2021–2023 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from the United States. The findings from this study would provide insights into addressing global challenges in diabetes.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The 2021–2023 NHANES, the first survey conducted after the COVID-19 pandemic, includes six OHIP survey questions, the most comprehensive OHIP survey since 2008. The National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) selects participants who represent the noninstitutionalized civilian population residing in the 50 states and the District of Columbia, and collects demographics, health exams, laboratory results, and other health data through in-person or telephone interviews with these participants.18 The NCHS Institutional Review Board approved the study protocols, and all participants provided written informed consent. Additional information about the scope of the survey and the methods of sampling and data collection is available at the NCHS website (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/).

Study sample

The study sample included participants with self-reported diabetes (receiving a diagnosis of diabetes from a physician) and those with undiagnosed diabetes (never receiving a diagnosis but having glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5 %). We excluded pregnant participants because pregnancy affects blood sugar. We also excluded participants aged less than 20 because the OHIP survey questions required participants to be at least 20 years old. Finally, observations with missing data in OHIP survey questions were excluded. The total number of qualified participants was 1,179, representing 30,200,261 community-dwelling individuals with diabetes and undiagnosed diabetes in the U.S. Fig. 1 provides the flow chart of the study sample selection.

Variable measures

Dependent variables

The outcome variables included HbA1c, diabetes-related complications, and polypharmacy. HbA1c is a key indicator of diabetes control, reducing the risk of micro- and macrovascular complications.19–21 Following previous studies and the standard of diabetes care,19–22 we defined poor diabetes control using two criteria: HbA1c ≥ 7 % and HbA1c ≥ 8 %, as physicians may avoid strict HbA1c control to reduce the risk of hypoglycemia. It is a dichotomous variable, with a value of 1 indicating poor diabetes control and zero otherwise. The variable of diabetes-related complications, defined by the American Diabetes Association, 23 is a dichotomous variable, with one representing individuals who were told by physicians or health professionals to have any of the following conditions (i.e., congestive heart failure, coronary artery disease, angina, heart attack, and weak/failing kidneys), and zero otherwise. Polypharmacy is coded as one if individuals took five or more prescribed medications in the past 30 days, and zero otherwise. These conditions can be identified in NHANES.

Key independent variables

The OHIP in the 2021–2023 NHANES contains six Oral Health Questionnaire (OHQ), with a series of OHQ numbers (OHQ 620, 630, 640, 660, 670, and 680) to measure different domains in Lockers’ theoretical conceptual framework.1–3 The six OHQ questions ask about “how often during the last year you have had a poor experience” because of problems with teeth, mouth, or dentures. These questions include 1) OHQ 620 for “a painful aching anywhere in your mouth” for the domain of physical pain, 2) OHQ630 for “feeling that life in general was less satisfying” for the domain of handicap, 3) OHQ640 for “having difficulty with doing usual job or attending school” for the domain of social disability, 4) OHQ660: for “avoiding particular food “for the domain of physical disability, 5) OHQ670 for “feeling uncomfortable to eat food” for the domain of physical pain, and 6) OHQ680 for “being self-conscious or embarrassed” for the domain of psychological discomfort and disability.1–3 Participants rated these questions on a five-point ordinal scale, where 1 indicated ‘often’, 2 indicated ‘fairly often’, 3 indicated ‘occasionally’, 4 indicated ‘hardly ever’, and 5 indicated ‘never’.2,3 Following previous studies,24,25 survey questions rated as ‘very often’ or ‘fairly often’ were categorized as poor OHIP (coded 1), and zero otherwise, resulting in a dichotomous variable.

Participants’ sociodemographic and health behaviors are also associated with diabetes outcomes. We controlled the following confounding variables: age (<65, with ≥65 as the reference group), sex (male as the reference group), race/ethnicity (non-White, with White in the reference group), the status of married or cohabitates (single and noncohabitates as the reference group), education (below high school and high school, with college as the reference group), uninsured (those insured as the reference group), and income (federal poverty level [FPL] ≥ 130 %, with FPL <130 % as low income in the reference group), or smokers (current or former smokers, with never smokers in the reference group).

Analytical approaches

Because each OHQ measure represents different domains of physical and psychosocial dysfunction,1 we examined individual OHQ measures to test our theoretical hypotheses. Following NHANES’s analytical guidelines,18 we conducted weighted analyses to compare the differences in study variables between participants who rated poorly and those who did not rate poorly for each OHQ measure. The weighted percentage and 95 % confidence interval (CI) were presented in the study. The proc surveylogistic models were applied for the analyses. The odds ratios were calculated to estimate the magnitude of the differences. The NHANES sample weights and Taylor series were applied to compute variance estimates.18 SAS 9.4 was used for data management and analysis. All reported findings were statistically significant at P values of less than 0.05.

In general, participants with a rating of poor in OHIP measures had a higher percentage of having an HbA1c ≥ 7 % or ≥8 %, and a higher percentage of experiencing diabetes-related complications than those without a rating of poor. For example, 9.16 % of participants rated OHQ630 as poor, while 90.84 % had no rating of poor. Among those with a rating of poor, 65.41 % and 47.62 % had HbA1c ≥ 7 % (95 % CI: 56.89–73.94) and HbA1c ≥ 8 % (95 % CI: 35.69–59.56), respectively, compared to 50.19 % for HbA1c ≥ 7 % (95 % CI: 44.98–55.40) and 26.86 % for HbA1c ≥ 8 % (95 % CI: 23.14–30.58) among those without a rating of poor (P = 0.004 for HbA1c ≥ 7 % and P < 0.001 for HbA1c ≥ 8 %). A similar pattern was observed in diabetes-related complications for OHQ630. For those with a rating of ‘poor’, 46.84 % had at least one diabetes-related complication (i.e., congestive heart failure, coronary artery disease, angina, heart attack, and weak/failing kidneys), compared to 30.73 % among those without a rating of ‘poor’ (P = 0.003). For control variables, only income and insurance status were statistically different between those with and without a rating of poor across all six OHQ measures (P values ranged from 0.027 to < 0.001). Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the theoretical relationship between poor OHRQoL and objective diabetes outcomes. Individuals reporting poor OHRQoL in the domains of physical pain, handicap, psychological discomfort and disability, and social disability had significantly higher odds of experiencing at least one adverse diabetes outcome—HbA1c ≥ 8 %, diabetes-related complications, or polypharmacy—than those without such impairments. These findings support our hypotheses and extend Locker’s framework, which reinforces the growing evidence that diabetes management strategies are incomplete without addressing oral health conditions.8–12

Consistent with prior studies,24,25 our study found a variation in self-reporting ‘often’ and ‘fairly often’ among individual OHQ measures; for instance, the social disability (OHQ640) was least rated as ‘often’ or ‘fairly often’ (2.5 %) while physical pain (OHQ670) was most frequently rated (11.5 %) among all OHQs. This variability underscores the value of examining individual domains of OHRQoL, as each domain may capture distinct behavioral and psychosocial pathways linking oral health conditions and diabetes management.

Our study also found contrary findings between poor diabetes outcomes and age and insurance status. Compared to adults aged ≥65, those aged <65 were less likely to report complications or polypharmacy but more likely to exhibit poor glycemic control, potentially reflecting unrecognized or untreated diabetes among non-elderly.26,27 Also, our findings regarding uninsured individuals reporting a lower likelihood of having polypharmacy than insured counterparts, likely reflecting underdiagnosis and delayed treatment for the uninsured rather than better health.28

This study has limitations related to the NHANES data. First, diabetes and complications were self-reported, which may introduce recall bias. Second, uncontrolled type I and type II diabetes patients are at risk of periodontal disease, but in slightly different forms;12,29 however, the type of diabetes is unavailable in NHANES. Third, the functional domain in Locker’s framework and other diabetes-related complications, such as neuropathy and retinopathy, could not be examined because relevant questions were unavailable. Finally, differences in the healthcare system, dental coverage, and culture may impact how individuals perceive and report their oral health experiences,24 suggesting that future cross-cultural studies are warranted to assess the universal association between objective diabetes outcomes and OHRQoL, as measured by the OHIP.

Despite the limitations, our results carry substantial implications for policy and community outreach. Although international organizations have advocated for medical–dental integration for decades,14,15,30 inadequate dental insurance remains a major barrier.31,32 With diabetes-related expenditures rising 338 % globally over the past 17 years,33 expanding dental coverage could facilitate integrated medical-dental care, ultimately easing the long-term financial burden of diabetes.

Even in countries with dental insurance coverage, including Taiwan, Japan, and South Korea, policy gaps persist in integrating oral health into diabetes care. In Taiwan, nearly half of patients with type II diabetes develop periodontal disease within 10 years of diagnosis,34 yet dental referrals are excluded from the diabetes pay-for-performance (P4P) program.35 Incorporating dental checkups into standard diabetes care or P4P requirements could strengthen medical–dental integrated care and the effectiveness of the P4P program.

Globally, the prevalence of periodontal disease remains approximately 40 %–42 % in Taiwan, Japan, and the U.S.,36–38 regardless of dental insurance coverage. The effectiveness of community interventions39,40 indicates the need to address periodontal disease through community outreach. OHIP, a low-cost, self-administered tool, is suitable for community outreach programs and settings, such as faith communities and workplaces, for OHRQoL assessment. Typically, with an estimated 250 million adults living with undiagnosed diabetes globally in 2024,33 community-based OHIP screening offers a practical public health strategy for early detection of periodontal disease and potential undiagnosed diabetes.

In conclusion, using nationally representative NHANES data (2021–2023), this study provides the first evidence that poor OHRQoL—particularly handicap, social disability, and psychological discomfort—is associated with HbA1c ≥ 8 %, diabetes-related complications, and polypharmacy. These findings advance Locker’s model and highlight the need for integrated medical-dental care. Also, community-based OHRQoL assessment through OHIP could strengthen both early clinical identification and interventions. Given the growing burden of diabetes, improving oral healthcare through policy and community outreach is a priority for public health.
