Dendritic Cell α-Ketoglutarate Regulates Tfh Polarization in Allergy
Authors: Anuj Tharakan, Xufeng Qu, Carmen Camarena, Araba Abaidoo-Myles, Parth Naik, Jeremy Allegood, Ankit Kumar, Mihir Pokhriyal, Joseph C. Lownik, Matthew P. Zellner, Grayson W. Way, Jinze Liu, Lauren Ashley Cowart, Stephanie C. Eisenbarth, Joseph W. Landry, Daniel H. Conrad, Rebecca K. Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Initiation of allergic responses requires activation of specialized subsets of T follicular helper (Tfh) cells called Tfh13 cells. Differentiation of these cells from naïve CD4+ T cells is induced by antigen presenting cells, such as type 2 conventional dendritic cells (cDC2s), following allergen exposure. Here we show that allergen stimulation induces a distinct metabolic program in cDC2s characterized by increased glutaminolysis which results in intracellular accumulation of α-ketoglutarate. Inhibition of glutaminolysis suppresses Tfh13 cell differentiation and promotes inhibitory T follicular regulatory (Tfr) cell activation in vivo. Further, glutamine-derived α-ketoglutarate in cDC2s regulates Tfh13 polarization by modulation of JmjD3 mediated histone demethylation and modulation of HIF-1α activity. JmjD3 or HIF-prolyl hydroxylase (HIF-PH) blockade reduced Tfh13 and germinal center IgE production. Genetic deletion of HIF-1α in cDCs, in contrast, enhanced Tfh13 polarization and IgE synthesis. Finally, we show that glutaminase or HIF-PH inhibitor treatment in allergen-sensitized mice induces allergen tolerance characterized by reduced asthmatic airway resistance, antigen-specific IgE production, and airway mast cell degranulation. Thus, we demonstrate that α-ketoglutarate production regulates epigenetic and gene expression changes critical for allergic sensitization by cDC2s following allergen exposure, leading to induction of Tfh13 cells and antigen-specific IgE formation. Therefore, this glutaminase 1-JmjD3/HIF-PH axis represents a promising new target for controlling IgE-mediated allergic disease pharmacologically.

Introduction

Allergic asthma is a chronic pulmonary disease characterized by intermittent respiratory symptoms caused by bronchoconstriction and airway hyperreactivity [1]. This respiratory pathology is caused by airway inflammation mediated by type 2 cytokines and allergen-specific IgE production [2]. Allergen-specific IgE promotes mast cell activation and degranulation which is a major driver of bronchoconstriction, and therefore respiratory symptoms, in allergic asthma [3, 4].

The initiation of IgE responses, in both humans and animal models, depends on a subset of T follicular helper (Tfh) cells called T follicular helper 13 (Tfh13) cells, which induce high-affinity IgE production by B cells [5, 6]. The mechanisms involved in the initial differentiation of Tfh13 cells from naïve CD4+ T cells, however, are poorly understood. Tfh cell polarization is dependent on type 2 conventional dendritic cells (cDC2s) which express the co-stimulatory molecules CD80/86, CD40L, OX40L, and ICOSL [7, 8]. Further, previous work on Tfh polarization demonstrated that high T cell receptor (TCR) affinity and high levels of antigen presentation by DCs preferentially induce the Tfh cell fate [9, 10]. It is unclear, however, if these same processes regulate Tfh13 polarization by DCs.

Recent studies have identified cellular metabolic pathways as a key regulators of DC phenotype and function [11-14]. Activated DCs undergo a rapid shift to glycolytic metabolism with a reduction in oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) activity [11, 14]. These metabolic changes, however, vary depending on the specific stimulus by which DCs are activated [12, 13]. Additionally, prior work has demonstrated that α-KG, a downstream product of glutamine catabolism, modulates macrophage polarization by regulating the activity of histone demethylases and HIF-prolyl hydroxylases, which use α-KG as a substrate [15, 16]. Here, we investigated the metabolic perturbations that occur in DCs following activation with allergic and non-allergic stimuli and the functional consequences of those perturbations in the initiation of type 2 immune responses.

Allergen activated DCs underwent a shift in cellular metabolism characterized by increased glutamine catabolism resulting in the accumulation of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle metabolite α-ketoglutarate (α-KG). Further, inhibition of glutaminolysis in DCs prior to sensitization with the prominent fungal allergen Alternaria alternata results in impaired Tfh13 polarization and promotes induction of T follicular regulatory (Tfr) cells, which suppress high-affinity antibody production [5]. Additionally, we show that glutamine-derived α-KG induces C-type lectin gene expression by allergen exposed DCs via activation of the α-KG dependent histone demethylase JmjD3. We also show that α-KG promotes degradation of HIF-1α via the HIF-PH enzymes which play a role in allergic sensitization. Significantly, we find that administration of a glutaminase or a HIF-PH inhibitor can reverse allergic sensitization, suppressing antigen-specific IgE production and reducing airway hyperreactivity in mice with established allergic asthma. These findings reveal that glutamine-derived α-KG is critical for allergen-induced DC antigen presentation to ultimately induce Tfh13 cell polarization and initiate IgE synthesis. Glutaminase and downstream α-KG dependent enzymes, therefore, may represent potential new targets to control allergic disease.

Results

The Aeroallergen Alternaria Induces Tfh13 Cell Polarization via Activation of cDCs

DCs are professional antigen presenting cells which are typically involved in priming of naïve T cells and initiating Tfh responses [17]. Broadly, DCs can be divided into monocyte-derived DCs (moDCs) and conventional (cDCs), the latter of which can be further divided into cDC1s and cDC2s [18]. To determine which subset of DCs are required for Tfh13 polarization, we utilized an adoptive transfer model using cultured moDCs and cDCs as previously described [19]. These cultured DCs were pulsed with the fungal aeroallergen Alternaria, washed, and adoptively transferred intranasally into recipient mice. Mice that received Alternaria pulsed cDCs exhibited significant Tfh13 cell polarization in the mediastinal lymph nodes (medLN) whereas those receiving moDCs did not (Figure S1A,B), indicating that cDCs are sufficient for Tfh13 cell priming. In general, Tfh polarization is thought to require antigen presentation by cDC2s [7] so we focused on this subset for further analysis.

To identify molecular processes involved in Tfh13 polarization by DCs, we first developed in vivo models of asthma comparing the Tfh13 inducing allergen Alternaria to the non-Tfh13 inducing stimulus, LPS, alongside the bystander antigen ovalbumin (OVA). In these models, Alternaria induced a typical allergic response characterized by airway eosinophilia, IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 production, and synthesis of OVA-specific IgE (Figure 1A-D). LPS, in contrast, induced airway neutrophilia, IFN-γ and IL-17A production, and OVA-specific IgG1 production (Figure 1B,E,F). Importantly, a single exposure to Alternaria induced a significant Tfh13 response compared to LPS (Figure 1G-I). Therefore, we used Alternaria and LPS as model stimuli to investigate the mechanisms by which DCs promote the Tfh13 vs. Tfh1 state.

Alternaria Induces Downregulation of OXPHOS Gene Expression in Lung Migratory cDCs In Vivo

To evaluate the process of Tfh13 polarization by DCs, we treated mice intranasally with Alternaria or LPS alongside AF647-conjugated OVA to identify migratory cDC2s in the medLN. Following these exposures, we found no difference in the numbers of migratory cDC2s, expression of co-stimulatory molecules, or amount of antigen uptake by cDC2s between LPS and Alternaria sensitized mice (Figure 2A and S2A-E). The expression of CXCR5, a chemokine receptor involved in positioning of DCs to the T-B interface of draining lymph nodes where Tfh polarization is thought to predominantly occur [7], was also not different between Alternaria and LPS stimulated cDC2s in vivo (Figure S2F). Additionally, we observed no differences in numbers of migratory cDC1s between these treatment groups (Figure S2G). These data indicate that Tfh13 cell priming is not a result of distinct differences in DC co-stimulatory molecule or chemokine receptor expression.

To determine if Alternaria resulted in activation and migration of specific subsets of lung cDC2s, we performed droplet-based single cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) on medLN cDC2s following Alternaria or LPS sensitization. scRNA-seq data was filtered to remove contaminating cell populations to further analyze migratory cDC2s. cDC2s were identified using the canonical markers Zbtb46+, Dpp4+, Flt3+, and Sirpa+ or S100a4+ and cells expressing markers of other cell types (Cd3e, Cd79a, Fcgr1, and Mertk) were excluded from downstream analysis (Figure S3A) [20]. We identified five transcriptionally distinct clusters of cDC2s (Figure 2B). These subsets can be identified as the following: Cluster 1 (Icam1+, Klf6+, Irf1+), Cluster 2 (Ctsh+, Il4i1+, Cxcl16+), Cluster 3 (Il12b+, S100a11+, Calm1+), Cluster 8 (Cd209a+, Klf4+), Cluster 12 (Il15ra+). Analysis of these subsets revealed only modest differences in the proportions of each cluster between Alternaria and LPS sensitized mice (Figure 2C). Alternaria sensitization, however, induced greater expression of the C-type lectin receptors Mgl2, CD209a, and CD209d than LPS in cluster 8, which have been previously implicated in DC-mediated allergic sensitization (Figure S3B) [21, 22]. As each of these clusters were similarly represented in the LPS and Alternaria treated mice, this suggests that Tfh13 priming is not induced by a unique subset of cDC2s, but rather by distinct activation states of cDC2s.

To further define transcriptional processes in migratory cDC2s involved in Tfh13 priming, migratory cDCs in the medLN were sorted following intranasal exposure to Alternaria or LPS and analyzed by RNA-seq. Sorted cDCs analyzed by RNA-seq expressed canonical cDC markers Zbtb46, H2-ab1, and Dpp4 and low levels of macrophage, T cell, and B cell markers (Figure S4A,B). RNA-seq analysis of these cDCs revealed significant differences in global transcriptional signatures in Alternaria compared to LPS treated cDCs (Figure 2D). To identify specific transcriptional programs that may regulate Tfh13 cell priming, gene set enrichment analysis was performed on the RNA-seq data. This analysis using the KEGG pathway database revealed a significant downregulation of proteasome and oxidative phosphorylation genes following Alternaria exposure compared to LPS (Figure 2E,F). The reduction in proteasome genes is likely attributable to the induction of proteasome dependent cross-priming by LPS in cDC1s [23]. Other changes noted, such as RIG-I like receptor signaling, NOD-like receptor signaling, and cytosolic DNA-sensing pathways were associated with significant gene expression changes in Irf7, Zbp1, Adar, Nlrc5, and Naip5 (Figure S4C,D) which are likely attributable to local type 1 interferon signals, which has previously been implicated in suppressing Th2 cell differentiation [24]. We therefore predicted that the oxidative phosphorylation changes may be most pertinent to Tfh13 priming. To further assess mitochondrial activity in cDC2s, mitochondrial membrane potential was analyzed in migratory cDC2s. This revealed that Alternaria activated cDC2s exhibit reduced mitochondrial activity compared to LPS activated cDC2s (Figure 2G). Mitochondrial activity in migratory cDC1s, however, was unchanged between Alternaria and LPS stimulation (Figure 2H).

Allergic and Non-Allergic Stimuli Trigger Distinct Metabolic Programs in cDCs

The downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation suggests that Alternaria may induce alterations in cellular metabolic pathways in cDC2s compared to those activated by LPS. Recent studies have identified that DC activation results in perturbations in cellular metabolism, which can regulate subsequent DC function [12-14]. DCs have been shown to increase their glycolytic rate in response to a variety of activating stimuli [13]. In concordance with these studies, we found that glucose uptake was significantly elevated in both LPS and Alternaria-stimulated bone marrow derived dendritic cell cDC2s (BMDC-cDC2s) from Flt3L cultures compared to mock stimulated controls (Figure 3A). Activated DCs predominantly utilize glucose anaerobically to generate lactate and therefore rely on alternative carbon sources to replenish TCA cycle intermediates [11, 13, 14]. The most abundant alternative carbon sources are fatty acids and glucogenic amino acids such as glutamine [25, 26]. Evaluation of fatty acid uptake revealed that LPS stimulation induced significant long and medium chain fatty acid uptake by BMDC-cDC2s as compared to mock treatment, while Alternaria activation failed to induce long or medium chain fatty acid uptake (Figure 3B,C). Alternaria, however, induced upregulation of the glutamine transporter, Slc38a2 [27], compared to mock whereas LPS stimulation had no effect on Slc38a2 surface expression (Figure 3D-F).

To further define the metabolic profile of these DCs, we performed untargeted metabolomics analysis on Alternaria and LPS activated BMDCs. Alternaria- stimulated BMDCs exhibited divergent metabolic profiles compared to LPS and mock stimulated BMDCs. Notably, Alternaria-stimulated BMDCs significantly accumulated the TCA cycle metabolites α-KG and succinate as compared to mock (Figure 3G,H), while LPS significantly induced fumarate and malate accumulation (Figure 3I,J and S5). Additionally, treatment of BMDCs with the glutaminase 1 inhibitor, CB-839, prior to Alternaria exposure prevented the accumulation of α-KG (Figure 3K). Taken together, these data suggest that Alternaria promotes glutaminolysis in cDC2s that leads to accumulation of α-KG and succinate, whereas LPS, in contrast, strongly induces lipolysis and accumulation of fumarate and malate. To further analyze these findings in DCs in vivo, we analyzed gene expression of the solute carrier (SLC) gene family from bulk RNA-seq data of migratory DCs following LPS or Alternaria sensitization. These analyses demonstrate that Alternaria induced upregulation of the glutamine transporters Slc1a5 and Slc38a1 whereas LPS sensitization resulted in upregulation of the fatty acid transporter Slc27a3 (Figure 3L). The accumulation of α-KG and succinate in Alternaria activated DCs indicates a break in the TCA cycle, restricting conversion of succinate to fumarate by the enzyme succinate dehydrogenase (SDH). Malonate, an endogenous inhibitor of SDH [28, 29], was significantly elevated in Alternaria activated DCs (Figure 3M). Thus, the generation of malonate may be a central regulator of this metabolic switch resulting in α-KG and succinate accumulation in Alternaria activated DCs.

Glutamine-Derived α-KG in cDCs Critically Regulates Tfh13 Polarization

Based upon the results of our untargeted metabolomics data, we hypothesized that glutamine catabolism may be necessary for Tfh13 cell polarization by cDC2s. Glutaminolysis involves the conversion of glutamine to glutamate by glutaminase. Glutamate can then be further metabolized by glutamate dehydrogenases to α-KG which can then be used to fuel the TCA cycle [30, 31]. To directly evaluate the role of glutamine-derived α-KG in Tfh13 cell polarization, mice were treated intranasally with the glutaminase 1 inhibitor, CB-839 [32] prior to Alternaria exposure. This significantly reduced Tfh13 cell polarization (Figure 4A,B). CB-839, however, had a minimal effect on overall medLN Tfh and Th2 cells, indicating that the effect was specific to Tfh13 cell priming (Figure 4C,D). Tfh13 cells promote high-affinity IgE production by inducing IgE class switch recombination in germinal center B cells (GCBCs) [6]. CB-839 treated mice exhibited a reduction in IgE+ GCBCs and IgE producing plasma cells (PCs) compared to vehicle treatment, demonstrating a reduction in OVA-specific IgE production (Figures 4E,F and S6A).

To elucidate how CB-839 yielded this reduction in IgE+ GCBCs, we further analyzed Tfh cells that were induced by Alternaria following CB-839 administration. We hypothesized that CB-839 would result in induction of T follicular regulatory cells (Tfr) by cDC2s, which inhibit germinal center responses [33, 34]. Indeed, CB-839 treated mice exhibited a significant increase in Tfr cells compared to vehicle controls (Figure 4G,H). This effect was also restricted to the Tfr population, as T regulatory (Treg) cells were unaffected by CB-839 treatment (Figure 4I). Additionally, CB-839 treatment had no effect on Tfh1, Tfh17, or Th17 cell differentiation following exposure to LPS + OVA (Figures 4J,K and S6D,E), but Th1 cell differentiation was significantly increased in CB-839 treated mice (Figure S6F). Further, CB-839 treatment led to a reduction in Tfr polarization following LPS exposure (Figure S6G). To determine if the effects of CB-839 on Tfh polarization was driven by a reduction in α-KG accumulation, we administered cell-permeable dimethyl α-KG to mice after CB-839 treatment and found that this restored Tfh13 polarization and reversed induction of Tfr cells (Figure 4B,F,H).

To confirm that this effect of CB-839 treatment was mediated by DCs, BMDCs were pretreated with CB-839 or vehicle control prior to stimulation with Alternaria + OVA in vitro and intranasally adoptively transferred the BMDCs into mice as previously described (Figure 4L) [19]. Mice that received CB-839 treated BMDCs exhibited a significant reduction in Tfh13 polarization compared to mice that received vehicle treated BMDCs. (Figure 4M). To examine whether the effect was only seen with Alternaria, we found that CB-839 induced a similar reduction in Tfh13 polarization and IgE+ GCBC induction following intranasal sensitization with house dust mite extracts (HDM), indicating that this suppressive effect is generalizable to other allergens (Figure S6B,C). Additionally, to determine that this effect was not specific to CB-839, we also tested the ability of BPTES, a less potent inhibitor of glutaminase 1, to suppress Alternaria induced Tfh13 priming. Intranasal BPTES treatment yielded a modest reduction in medLN Tfh13 cells (Figure S6H). CB-839 was used in subsequent experiments due to its more pronounced suppression of Tfh13 polarization and Tfr induction.

Additionally, the role of fatty acid metabolism was assessed in allergen activated DCs by fasting mice prior to Alternaria exposure. Fasted mice exhibited reduced Tfh13 polarization, airway eosinophilia, and antigen-specific IgE production following Alternaria challenge (Figure S7A-C). Additionally, induction of fatty acid oxidation via the PPAR-γ agonist rosiglitazone suppressed Tfh13 cell priming and germinal center formation following Alternaria exposure (Figure S7D,E). These results demonstrate that inducing fatty acid utilization, rather than glutaminolysis, to replenish TCA cycle intermediates in DCs suppresses Tfh13 polarization. Overall, these findings demonstrate that induction of glutaminolysis and the generation of α-KG in DCs is essential for allergic sensitization following allergen exposure.

α-KG Is Necessary for Alternaria-Mediated DC Antigen Uptake and Presentation

To determine the mechanisms by which glutaminolysis regulates Tfh13 polarization by DCs, the effects of CB-839 on migratory DC populations following intranasal Alternaria sensitization was examined. Glutaminase inhibition resulted in similar numbers of migratory cDC2s in medLNs after Alternaria exposure (Figure S8A). Further, while a modest reduction in CD40 expression was seen, no differences in the expression of the co-stimulatory molecules OX40L, ICOSL, PDL1, and PDL2 or the chemokine receptor CXCR5 were found (Figure S8B-H). Additionally, transcription of cytokines that have been previously implicated in Tfh polarization were unchanged in BMDCs following CB-839 treatment (Figure S9).

Previous reports have determined that TCR binding avidity is a key driver of the Tfh cell fate [9, 35, 36]. We therefore hypothesized that CB-839 treatment may induce Tfr cells over Tfh13 cell induction by reducing antigen presentation by cDC2s, thereby reducing TCR binding avidity during the interaction between DCs and naïve T cells. To determine the effect of glutaminolysis on antigen uptake and presentation by cDC2s, mice were treated with CB-839 or vehicle and sensitized intranasally with Alternaria + Eα-GFP. The Eα-GFP system permits the quantification of Eα-peptide loaded MHC-II on the surface of DCs via the Y-ae antibody, as well as direct measurement of antigen uptake by quantifying GFP levels [37, 38]. These studies revealed that CB-839 treatment resulted in a significant reduction in antigen uptake and presentation in migratory cDC2s in vivo and this was reversed by administration of dimethyl α-KG (Figure 5A-C). zWe predicted that increased antigen dosage would therefore overcome this reduction in antigen uptake and presentation. Indeed, when CB-839 treated mice were sensitized with Alternaria alongside increasing doses of OVA protein, the increased OVA dose (400 μg) resulted in normal Tfh13 induction following CB-839 treatment (Figure 5D).

To further characterize the underlying mechanisms by which glutaminolysis may regulate allergen uptake by cDC2s, the effect of glutaminase inhibition on cell surface expression of C-type lectin receptors (CLRs) was examined. These receptors have been previously reported to regulate allergen recognition and uptake by DCs and macrophages, and may therefore regulate receptor-mediated endocytosis of allergens [21, 39-41]. CB-839 treatment resulted in reduced expression of the CLRs DC-SIGN and CD301b in migratory cDC2s in vivo following Alternaria sensitization (Figure 5E,F). DC-SIGN and CD301b cell surface expression were also reduced in CB-839 treated BMDCs after Alternaria stimulation (Figure 5G,H).

To assess the role of glutaminolysis in human cDC2 responses to allergen, a humanized mouse model was utilized [42]. NSG-FLT3 mice were busulfan treated and engrafted with human CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells 12 weeks prior to experimental manipulation. Engraftment was verified by analysis of human CD45+ cells in the spleen (Figure S10A). These humanized mice were then intranasally exposed to Alternaria + OVA-AF647(Figure 5I). Human migratory cDC2s in medLNs exhibited reduced AF647 uptake following CB-839 treatment at 20 h post Alternaria exposure (Figure 5J and S10B). Further, CB-839 induced significant suppression of CD301b, DC-SIGN and CD369, another CLR with previously reported involvement in allergic disease, in human cDC2s (Figure 5K-M) [43].

DC-SIGN is a mannose binding receptor that has been previously reported to induce antigen uptake by DCs [44]. To further examine the role of DC-SIGN in the induction of Tfh13 priming, a DC-SIGN blocking antibody was administered intranasally to mice prior to Alternaria exposure. Blockade of DC-SIGN resulted in suppression of Tfh13 polarization and IgE+ GCBC induction (Figure 5N,O). Collectively, these data demonstrate that inhibition of glutaminolysis results in a reduction in allergen uptake and presentation by DCs in murine and human cDC2s, which may be mediated by reduced expression of CLRs needed for allergen recognition.

α-KG Regulates Allergen-Induced DC Function via Modulation of JmjD3 and HIF-1α Activity

α-KG, in addition to its role in the TCA cycle, regulates HIF-1α activity by functioning as a substrate for the HIF-prolyl hydroxylase (HIF-PH) enzymes such as PHD1/2/3, which promote degradation of HIF-1α [45]. Thus, we next examined the role of HIF-1α and HIF-PH enzymes on allergic sensitization. Analysis of migratory cDC2s following LPS or Alternaria exposure demonstrated that Alternaria stimulation significantly reduced levels of HIF-1α after compared to LPS exposure, suggesting that Alternaria promotes increased degradation of HIF-1α in cDC2s (Figure 6A). Intranasal treatment with the HIF-PH inhibitor roxadustat prior to Alternaria exposure resulted in significant reduction in Tfh13 cell polarization and IgE GCBC differentiation in vivo (Figure 6B,C). Further, roxadustat administration enhanced Tfr differentiation in response to Alternaria (Figure 6D). Analysis of migratory cDC2s following roxadustat and Alternaria treatment revealed that HIF-PH inhibition reduces DC-SIGN surface expression on cDC2s (Figure 6E).

To further assess the role of cDC Hif1α in allergic sensitization, Hif-1α was deleted from cDCs using the zDC-cre driver bred to Hif1afl/fl (DC-Hif1α−/−) mice [46]. Following exposure to Alternaria + OVA, mice with Hif-1α deficient cDCs exhibited significantly greater induction of Tfh13 and Tfh2 cell polarization, and decreased Tfr proliferation (Figure 6F-H). Additionally, DC-Hif1α−/− mice exhibited increased IgE+ GCBC differentiation, and IgE+ plasma cell proliferation (Figure 6I,J). Additionally, DC-Hif-1α−/− mice demonstrated unchanged Th2 and Treg cell polarization, and IgG1+ GCBC differentiation (Figure S11A-C).

In addition to regulating Hif1α activity, α-KG, also acts as a cofactor for epigenetic enzymes such as the JmjC domain containing enzymes which function as histone demethylases [45]. Demethylation of H3K27me3, a repressive histone modification, has previously been implicated in DC activation in response to various stimuli [47, 48], and is necessary for the expression of the CLR CD206 in macrophages [16]. Therefore, we examined whether α-KG may exert its effects in DCs by modulating the activity of JmjD3, a histone demethylase which preferentially targets H3K27me3 [49]. Following Alternaria stimulation, BMDCs exhibit significant global H3K27 demethylation which is suppressed by inhibition of glutaminolysis (Figure 6K). Further, we found that intranasal administration of the specific JmjD3 inhibitor, GSK-J4, prior to Alternaria exposure significantly reduced Tfh13 polarization and IgE+ GCBC differentiation (Figure 6L,M). GSK-J4, however, had no effect of Tfr induction (Figure 6N).

To evaluate the effects of JmjD3 inhibition on cDC2 antigen uptake and presentation, mice were treated intranasally with GSK-J4 prior to exposure to Alternaria + Eα-GFP. Mice treated with GSK-J4 exhibited significantly reduced antigen uptake and presentation compared to vehicle treated controls (Figure 6O,P). Further, GSK-J4 treatment on cDC2s in vivo resulted in modestly reduced expression of DC-SIGN, and CD301b, but significant decreases in CD40 (Figure 6Q-S), similar to the changes observed following CB-839 treatment. Additionally, CB-839 and GSK-J4 reduced transcription of Cd40 and Cd209a genes following Alternaria stimulation (Figure S12A,B), further demonstrating that glutaminolysis regulates CD40 and DC-SIGN expression at the transcriptional level. To directly assess the effect of glutaminolysis and JmjD3 activity on H3K27me3 marks on these genes, ChIP-PCR was performed. These analyses demonstrate that Alternaria exposure induces a significant decrease in H3K27me3 marks in the promoter region of the CD209a gene, however, CB-839 and GSK-J4 treatment had no effect on H3K27 methylation at this locus (Figure S12C). Similarly, H3K27me3 marks on the Cd40 promoter were also decreased by Alternaria exposure but unaffected by glutaminase or JmjD3 inhibition (Figure S12D).

Collectively, these data suggest that α-KG induces degradation of HIF-1α and demethylation of H3K27me3 histone marks which results in increased allergen-induced DC antigen uptake and presentation, thereby promoting Tfh13 polarization. Blockade of this effect either by using a glutaminase inhibitor (Figure 4A,B), a JmjD3 inhibitor, or a HIF-PH inhibitor reduces Tfh13 and IgE levels.

Inhibition of Glutaminase or HIF-PH Promotes Allergen Desensitization in Established Allergic Asthma

The observation that inhibition of glutaminolysis in DCs induced Tfr cells rather than Tfh13 cells led us to evaluate the potential of CB-839 to induce allergen desensitization in established allergic asthma. We utilized a murine model of asthma in which mice were sensitized, boosted, and challenged with Alternaria + OVA and were treated with CB-839 or vehicle control intranasally prior to boost and the first challenge (Figure 7A). Pulmonary function testing revealed a substantial reduction in airway resistance in mice receiving CB-839 treatment compared to vehicle (Figure 7B). CB-839 treatment also resulted in a reduction in serum OVA-specific IgG1 and IgE, while total IgE was unaffected (Figure 7C,D and S13). BALF levels of mast cell protease-1 (MCPT-1), a marker of mast cell degranulation, was significantly reduced in CB-839 treated mice compared to controls (Figure 7E). There was no difference in airway eosinophilia in CB-839 treated mice, further indicating that CB-839 had no effect on Th2 cell polarization (Figure 7F). CB-839 treated mice exhibited a modest reduction in airway neutrophilia, which is consistent with previous reports indicating that Th17 cells depend on glutaminolysis (Figure 7G) [50]. Further, as the HIF-PH inhibitor roxadustat exhibited similar Tfh13 and Tfr changes as CB-839, we next examined the effect of roxadustat in a similar treatment paradigm in a murine model of asthma. Mice treated with roxadustat also exhibited a significant reduction in airway hyperreactivity in response to methacholine challenge (Figure 7H). Further, roxadustat treatment yielded significant reduction in OVA-specific IgE levels as well as a modest, non-statistically significant reduction in BALF MCPT-1 levels (Figure 7I,J). Together, these data indicate that inhibition of α-KG production or its downstream target, HIF-PH, can be used as an allergen desensitization agent to ameliorate disease severity in allergic asthma.

Discussion

Polarization of Tfh13 cells represents a critical step in allergic sensitization by regulating the generation of high-affinity, allergen specific IgE. The polarization of naïve CD4+ T cells to the Tfh13 fate requires appropriate interaction with DCs, however the mechanisms involved in this process are not well understood. Adoptive transfer experiments revealed that cDCs are the antigen presenting cells which are necessary Tfh13 cell priming (Figure S1A). As cDC2s, rather than cDC1s, are primarily involved in priming CD4+ T cells and Tfh cells in particular [7, 8], we focused our analyses on mechanisms by which allergens activate cDC2s. In vivo analyses show that Alternaria exposure, but not LPS, reliably promotes Tfh13 polarization at day 8, the peak of Tfh13 induction.

Next, the cell-intrinsic mechanisms by which cDCs induce Tfh13 priming were analyzed. No significant differences in costimulatory molecule expression or in numbers of migrating cDC2s was found between Alternaria and LPS activated cDC2s in vivo. Droplet based single-cell RNA sequencing identified 5 distinct clusters of cDC2 migrating to the medLN, however, only modest differences in CLR marker expression was seen, suggesting that Tfh13 polarization is induced by differential activation states of cDC2s, rather than a unique, stable cDC2 population. RNA-sequencing analysis of migratory cDCs following Alternaria or LPS exposure, however, demonstrated significant differences in expression of oxidative phosphorylation genes. Further, measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential in migratory cDC2s in the medLN confirmed that Alternaria activated cDC2s exhibit reduced mitochondrial activity compared to LPS-activated cDC2s.

Several studies have highlighted the role of cellular metabolism on DC activation, function, and subsequent T cell priming. These studies largely focus on glucose and fatty acid metabolism in activated DCs and their metabolic requirements for migration and synthesis of inflammatory mediators [11-14]. Analysis of glucose uptake between Alternaria and LPS stimulated DCs indicated similar glucose utilization in each condition (Figure 3A).

Activated DCs, however, largely use glucose anaerobically to generate lactate rather than fuel the TCA cycle [13]. TCA cycle intermediates therefore must be replenished by alternative carbon sources such as fatty acids or glucogenic amino acids. Untargeted metabolomics analysis revealed that Alternaria stimulated cDCs demonstrated a significant accumulation of α-KG and succinate. Accumulation of α-KG likely reflects anaplerosis via glutamine catabolism. Further supporting this role was the increased expression of the glutamine transporters Slc1a5, Slc38a1, and Slc38a2 in Alternaria stimulated cDCs. Additionally, inhibition of glutaminolysis prevented this accumulation of α-KG, demonstrating that the α-KG accumulated following Alternaria exposure is derived from glutamine. In contrast, LPS stimulation resulted in increased fatty acid uptake and expression of fatty acid transporters. Additionally, inducing DCs to utilize fatty acid oxidation, rather than glutaminolysis, to fuel the TCA cycle resulted in impaired Tfh13 polarization. This further demonstrates that induction of glutaminolysis in DCs is necessary for Tfh13 priming and the initiation of IgE synthesis.

The observed accumulation of α-KG and succinate in Alternaria activated DCs indicates a break in the TCA cycle, restricting conversion of succinate to fumarate by the enzyme succinate dehydrogenase (SDH). Malonate is an endogenous inhibitor of SDH [28, 29], which was significantly elevated in Alternaria activated DCs. Thus, the generation of malonate may be a critical regulator of this metabolic switch resulting in α-KG accumulation in Alternaria activated DCs.

Upon allergen exposure, DCs exhibited increased expression of glutamine transporters followed by an accumulation of the TCA cycle metabolites α-KG and succinate. To examine whether α-KG accumulation is important for Tfh13 and antigen specific IgE production we used CB-839, which is an inhibitor of glutaminase [32]. Excitingly, this inhibitor significantly blunted the induction of Tfh13 cells and GCBC IgE synthesis following Alternaria exposure. Inhibition of glutaminolysis in DCs yielded a reduction in allergen uptake and presentation by migratory cDC2s following allergen sensitization, which resulted in impaired Tfh13 cell polarization and induction of Tfr cells. Kinetics of the Tfr response could be further explored. Further analyses demonstrated that cDC2 antigen uptake and presentation are impaired by inhibition of glutaminase following allergen exposure. Significantly, we show that CB-839 mediated suppression of Tfh13 priming can be overcome by exposure to supraphysiological antigen doses, further indicating that the effect of CB-839 on Tfh13 polarization is mediated by reduction in cDC2 antigen uptake and presentation. Allergic disease and IgE synthesis is known to be profoundly dependent on antigen dose, with only a narrow range of doses promoting IgE production and higher doses inducing IgG2 [51-53]. Further, glutaminase inhibition reduced surface expression of the CLRs DC-SIGN and CD301b, which likely mediate recognition of allergens by DCs and subsequent DC antigen presentation and Tfh skewing. These changes in cDC2s were recapitulated in a humanized mouse model, demonstrating that glutaminolysis modulates CLR expression in human cDC2s. Tfh13 polarization in this humanized murine model could not be evaluated due to MHC incompatibility, thus the link between DC α-KG accumulation on human Tfh polarization remains to be explored. Future studies may involve analysis of glutamine transporter expression and glutamine catabolism in human DCs from allergic and non-allergic subjects.

Additionally, we explored the mechanisms by which α-KG regulates cDC2 function via two distinct α-KG-dependent cellular processes. Cellular metabolism, in addition to energy generation and biosynthetic processes, also generates metabolic intermediates which act as substrates and cofactors for various cellular enzymes such as HIF-PHs and epigenetic enzymes [54]. α-KG is a substrate for the HIF-PH enzymes which promote degradation of the transcription factor HIF-1α, which has previously been implicated in initiation and propagation of allergic disease [55]. We show that intranasal treatment with the HIF-PH inhibitor roxadustat significantly impairs Tfh13 polarization and IgE+ GCBC differentiation following Alternaria exposure. IgE staining for GCBCs excludes IgE bound to surface receptors but does not exclude IgE that is internalized in antigen immune complexes bound to CD23. This is a limitation of the work and a complication of current IgE staining techniques by flow cytometry. Further, roxadustat treatment resulted in a significant induction of Tfr cells, similar to the response observed with inhibition of glutaminolysis but was not recapitulated with JmjD3 inhibition. To clarify the role of HIF-1α in cDC2s during allergen exposure, we utilized a model of genetic deletion of Hif1α in cDCs. Ablation of HIF-1α in cDCs significantly exacerbated allergic sensitization to Alternaria, as measured by Tfh13 and Tfh2 polarization, IgE synthesis by GCBCs and PCs, and modest reduction in Tfr priming. These results indicate that allergen exposure promotes activation of JmjD3 and HIF-PHs in parallel in an α-KG-dependent manner, and both of these pathways are necessary for allergic sensitization and efficient Tfh13 priming. These findings, however, do not rule out the possibility that these changes are partially mediated by the influence of α-KG on cDC bioenergetics, resulting in impaired Tfh13 polarization.

Additionally, α-KG is a substrate for two types of epigenetic modulators: the TET family of DNA demethylases [56] and the JmjC domain containing demethylases, which are histone demethylases [57]. Demethylation of H3K27me3 has previously been implicated in DC activation in response to various stimuli [47, 48], and is necessary for the expression of the CLR CD206 in macrophages [16]. We demonstrate that Alternaria induces significant demethylation of H3K27me3 marks at the DC-SIGN gene promoter, which is partially reversed by inhibition of glutaminase 1 or JmjD3.

Treatment of cDC2s with the JmjD3 inhibitor GSK-J4 suppressed Tfh13 induction and GCBC IgE production. Additionally, GSK-J4 recapitulated the reduction in DC-SIGN and CD301b expression seen in CB-839 treatment both in vivo and in vitro (Figure 6Q-S). Intriguingly, Tfr induction was not seen, perhaps indicating that additional mechanisms, such as other α-KG dependent epigenetic enzymes, are affected by inhibition of glutaminolysis. Additionally, other metabolites, such as fumarate and 2-hydroxyglutarate, act as competitive inhibitors of many histone demethylase enzymes [58, 59]. Fumarate derivatives such as dimethyl fumarate are used clinically for the treatment of multiple sclerosis, though their exact mechanism of action is unclear [60]. This may represent an alternative mechanism by which α-KG-dependent epigenetic enzymes may be targeted for allergic disease therapy. To further support this, multiple genome-wide association studies have found that single nucleotide polymorphisms in the gene D2HGDH, which is a key regulator of cellular α-KG levels, are significantly associated with asthma, atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis, and food allergy, further suggesting a role for α-KG in allergic sensitization [61-63].

Our findings raise additional questions regarding the link between epigenetic regulation and DC function and subsequent antigen uptake and presentation. To date, there are no known associations between epigenetic modification and antigen uptake by allergen-activated DCs. Our data demonstrates that demethylation of H3K27me3 regulates transcription of genes involved in allergen recognition and uptake by DCs. Previous work has identified that α-KG induced JmjD3 activity in macrophages is necessary for the expression of CD206, a mannose receptor with ligand specificity similar to that of DC-SIGN [16]. Thus, α-KG mediated expression of C-type lectin receptors such as DC-SIGN and CD301b, which are involved in binding and recognition of glycoproteins on allergens, may be involved in allergen-mediated DC activation and receptor-mediated endocytosis of allergen particles. Signaling through these receptors then alter DC activity, characterized by increased antigen uptake and presentation.

One element of this process which our study does not address is the mechanism by which allergen stimulation induces glutaminolysis. Prior work has shown that LPS stimulation of DCs induces glycolysis directly via TLR signaling [14]. It is possible, therefore, that a similar mechanism regulates glutaminolysis induction by way of a CLR involved in allergen recognition such as the dectin family. Dectin-1 is a C-type lectin receptor involved in DC responses to Alternaria [64]. Stimulation of DCs with the dectin-1 agonist, depleted zymosan, induces increased glycolysis. This same pathway may therefore be involved in the induction of glutamine catabolism in DCs.

Overall, the data is consistent with process in which allergen exposure induces increased cDC2 glutamine utilization. This glutamine is catabolized via glutaminase 1, leading to the accumulation of α-KG intracellularly. This α-KG promotes epigenetic modifications via JmjD3 which, in combination with the degradation of HIF-1α by HIF-PHs, induces transcriptional changes enhance allergen uptake and presentation to permit Tfh13 polarization and allergic sensitization. Blockade of this α-KG accumulation, JmjD3 activity, or HIF-PH activity reduces Tfh13 and antigen specific IgE production. Genetic deletion of HIF-1α in cDCs, in contrast, increases Tfh13 and Tfh2 polarization, thereby enhancing IgE responses. These findings, together with previously published work, suggest that DCs respond to specific stimuli by altering their metabolism to accumulate particular metabolites which translate external signals into changes in DC phenotypes. These changes subsequently critically regulate DC function following allergen exposure and subsequent T cell activation and polarization.

The approach demonstrated here overcomes key obstacles in the management of allergic diseases such as asthma. Glutaminase inhibitor treatment in a murine model of allergic asthma ameliorated disease severity as measured by airway resistance, antigen-specific IgE production, and airway mast cell degranulation (Figure 7). Mast cell mediator release is thought to be particularly important in human asthma [65], which likely explains the effectiveness of anti-IgE therapies such as omalizumab [66]. Eosinophil accumulation was, however, not effected, indicating that Th2 cell produced IL-5 release was not reduced. Thus, glutaminase inhibitors would potentially provide rapid and durable allergen desensitization via Tfr induction and Tfh13 suppression, which would result in reduced allergen-specific IgE production and mast cell activation thereby improving pulmonary function, though longer term studies to evaluate safety and efficacy of these compounds in allergic disease will be critical to assess their translational potential. Additionally, the spectrum of activity against a diverse range of allergens, disease severity, and inter-individual variability remains to be evaluated. Current therapeutic modalities involve either such as allergy immunotherapy, or omalizumab therapy, neither of which directly inhibit IgE synthesis. Other therapeutic options target downstream inflammatory mediators such as mast cell degranulation products. Further, this approach will need to be evaluated in comparison to an active control arm, such as inhaled corticosteroids or omalizumab. Additionally, the role of Tfr cells in the modulation of IgE responses in this therapeutic model requires further characterization as some studies have demonstrated that Tfr cells promote IgE production in models of food allergy [67]. Glutaminase inhibitor therapy and perhaps JmjD3 inhibitor or HIF-PH inhibitor treatment would be cost-effective and safe, and should allow for simultaneous desensitization to multiple allergens.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

The sample size in each experiment was determined based on the level of expected heterogeneity of the samples, the expected or observed difference, and previous publications. No power analysis was performed. Data collection all had previously defined endpoints and these endpoints were adhered to in each experiment. Each experiment had at least three replicates. This study was designed to test the hypothesis that cDCs responsible for Tfh13 polarization would be a unique population with distinct processes that would result in downstream Tfh13 polarization. This initial hypothesis was followed by supporting hypotheses generated with each new piece of data acquired prior to conducting a new set of experimentation. The experiments were controlled laboratory experiments conducted on animals. Mice were either all purchased C57bl/6J mice from Jackson Laboratory or bred in house and littermates were used as controls. Mice were randomized with cages and ages were matched for each experiment. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.

Mice and Airway Exposure

C57Bl/6J mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. cDC-Hif1α−/− mice were generated by crossing zDC-cre (B6.Cg-Zbtb46tm3.1(Cre)Mnz/J; #028538) with Hif1αfl/fl mice (B6.129-Hif1atm3Rsjo/J; #007561). Mice were housed under specific-pathogen-free conditions in the instiutional barrier vivarium facility in accordance with the humane treatment of laboratory animals sets forth by the National Institutes of Health and the American Association for the Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care. All mouse protocols were conducted with the permission and oversight of the VCU Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were randomized for experiments and equal numbers of male and female mice were used unless otherwise specified. Experimental mice were 6–12 weeks old. Where indicated, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and treated intranasally with the indicated stimuli in a 25 μL volume. Murine asthma models were induced via intranasal administration of 10 μg Alternaria (Greer Laboratories) or 5 μg LPS from Salmonella enterica (Sigma) + 100 μg OVA (Sigma) on days 0 and 10. Mice were then treated intranasally with 5 μg Alternaria or 1 μg LPS + 50 μg OVA on day 20–23 and analyzed on day 24. In some experiments, mice were treated intranasally with 25 μg CB-839 or vehicle control (0.5% DMSO) 24 and 6 h prior to day 10 and 20 intranasal Alternaria administration. For fasting experiments, mice were randomized and place in clean cages. Food was removed 12 h prior to intranasal Alternaria administration on days 0 and 10 and replaced 2 h after intranasal exposure. In DC-SIGN blocking experiments, mice were intranasally administered 20 μg of anti-DC-SIGN (Clone # BE0220) 1 h prior to administration of Alternaria + OVA.

Human antibodies used for flow cytometry analysis were hCD45(clone: H130), HLA-DR(clone: L243), CD14 (clone: M5E2), CD1c (clone: L161), CD301 (clone: 744812), CD86 (clone: IT2.2), DCSIGN (clone: 9E9A8), and CD369 (15E2). Viability staining was performed with Zombie Aqua, Zombie NIR Fixable Viability Dye (Biolegend) or DAPI (ThermoFisher). Cells were stained with viability dye and blocked with anti-CD16/32 (clone 2.4 g2) prior to staining on ice with antibodies for surface molecules. Cells were then analyzed on a LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences) or a Cytek Aurora (Cytek) analyzer. Intracellular cytokine staining was performed after incubation for 4 h with 1 μM ionomycin (Sigma-Aldrich), 50 ng mL−1 phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (Sigma-Aldrich) and Brefeldin A (Biolegend). Cells were then fixed and stained using Fixation buffer (Biolegend) and Intracellular Staining Permeabilization Wash Buffer (Biolegend) per manufacturer's instructions.

Transcription factor staining was performed by fixation and permeabilization using the True-Nuclear Transcription Factor Buffer Set (Biolegend). MedLN Cytokine Production

Mice were euthanized by isoflurane inhalation and medLNs were harvested. For T and B cell analysis, medLNs were forced through a 100 μm filter and collected in complete media. For measurement of total cytokine production in Figure 1B,C, antigen restimulation was performed by incubating cell suspensions with 100 μg/mL OVA for 4 days. Supernatants cytokine levels were analyzed by ELISA (ThermoFisher).

In Vivo Tracking of Dendritic Cells

Following intranasal exposure to OVAAF647 + either LPS or Alternaria, mice were euthanized by isoflurane inhalation and medLNs were incubated for 30 min in 1 mg/mL collagenase IV (Sigma) and 0.5 mg/mL DNase I (Sigma), then forced through a 100 μm filter and collected in complete media [7]. Single cell suspensions were then stained to identify OVA+ cells.

To analyze antigen uptake and presentation, mice were intranasally instilled with 50 μg of Eα-GFP (kindly provided by Dr. Marc K. Jenkins) +10 μg Alternaria and medLNs were analyzed for Eα-GFP+ cDC2s 24 h later.

Tfh13 Analysis

Mice were euthanized by isoflurane inhalation and medLN cell suspensions were stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (50 ng/mL) and ionomycin (1 μg/mL) in 96-well U bottom plates in complete IMDM media for 4 h, and Brefeldin A was added for the last 3 h. Cell suspensions were then processed for flow cytometric analysis as described above.

Staining for IgE B Cells

IgE+ B Cell staining was performed as previously described [68]. medLN cell suspensions were incubated on ice for 10 min with viability dye and 2.4G2. Surface staining was performed with the addition of unlabeled anti-IgE (RME-1) to saturate Fc receptor bound IgE. Cells were then fixed with and permeabilized with BD intracellular staining kit and stained with fluorochrome conjugated anti-IgE (RME-1), anti-IgG1 overnight at 4°C.

ELISA

OVA-specific IgG1 ELISA was performed as previously described [69]. Briefly, plates were coated with 30 μg/mL OVA in carbonate buffer, blocked (PBS with 0.02% Tween-20 and 2% FBS), and incubated with diluted serum samples. Samples were detected with goat anti mouse IgG1-AP (Southern Biotech) and developed with phosphate tablets (Sigma). OVA-specific IgE ELISA was performed as previously described [70]. Briefly, plates were coated with 5 μg/mL anti-IgE(R1E4), blocked, and incubated with diluted samples. Detection was performed with biotinylated OVA followed by streptavidin-HRP (ThermoFisher). Plates were developed with tetramethylbenzidine (BD Biosciences) and stopped with sulfuric acid before reading at 450 nm on a microplate reader (Molecular Devices). A sandwich ELISA for total IgE or IgG1 at a concentration of 10 ng/mL was used as a reference standard to calculate arbitrary units. MCPT-1, IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, IL-17A, and IFN-γ ELISA assays were performed according to manufacturer's instructions (ThermoFisher).

Lung Analysis

Mice were euthanized by isoflurane inhalation and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BAL) was collected by injecting 2 mL cold MACS buffer (into the lungs via a secured tracheal cannula). BALF was centrifuged, and the supernatant was analyzed for cytokine levels and MCPT-1 by ELISA. The cell pellet was resuspended and analyzed by flow cytometry. Following BAL, lungs were perfused with cold PBS and the left lung was collected and fixed in buffered formalin for histological analysis. Pulmonary function testing was performed via invasive measurement of dynamic resistance with increasing doses of methacholine using the Flexivent system (Scireq).

scRNA-Seq

For droplet-based single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), 5000 to 10,000 sorted cDC2s were encapsulated into droplets, and libraries were prepared using Chromium Single Cell 5′ gene expression kit reagent kits v2 according to manufacturer's protocol (10× Genomics). Sequencing libraries were loaded on an HiSeq4000 (Illumina, San Diego, Ca) and sequencing was performed at Azenta (GeneWiz).

Single Cell RNA-Seq Analysis

The sequencer produced Chromium single-cell data and then the cell ranger toolkit was used to de-multiplex samples from raw sequencing reads to gene-count matrices with alignment to the mm10 genome. For the downstream analysis such as cell-type identification and differential gene expression analysis, Seurat (V3) R package [71] was used to integrate the gene-counts matrices from samples and provide the analytical insight. A five-step process was performed using the Seurat package: (1) For quality control and data pre-processing, we discarded genes expressed in fewer than three cells and discarded the low-quality cells that had less than 100 genes. We also assessed the percentage of mitochondria transcripts for each sample and filtered out cells that have greater than 20% and 10% of mitochondrial counts for “Alternaria” and “LPS” respectively. (2) We used “sctransformation” in Seurat to integrate “Alternaria” and “LPS”, which performs between-samples normalization and reserves biological distinction of samples. (3) For dimension reduction and clustering, we applied PCA to reduce the dimensionality of the integrated data to 30 principal components and then performed a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) modularity-based algorithm [72] for clustering. We utilized the UMAP to visualize the clustering results as shown in Figure S3C. (4) For cell-type identification, we created a dot-plot (Figure S3A) using 33 cell-type-specific marker genes to reveal the identities of each cluster. (5) In differential gene expression analysis, we used a Wilcoxon Rank Sum test to identify the up/down-regulated sets of genes for each cluster between samples. Other analytical results from Seurat were visualized in the Figure S3C,D. The former illustrates the changes in the proportion of clusters between “Alternaria” and “LPS”, and the latter violin plot shows the changes in gene expression for Mgl2, Cd209a, and Cd209d.

RNA-Seq

5000–10,000 fluorescence activated cell sorted OVA+ cDCs (AF647+, B220−, CD11chi, MHCIIhi) were collected using the FACSAria Fusion (BD Biosciences). Cells were pelleted and resuspended in TRIzol reagent (ThermoFisher) and processed for ultra-low input RNA-sequences at GeneWiz. The samples were sequenced using a 2 × 150 Paired End configuration. Trimmed reads were analyzed by FastQC for quality then aligned to the mm10 reference genome by HiSAT2. Count tables were obtained using featurecounts which were then used to generate PCA plots and DEG lists by DESeq2. Count tables were then used for pathway analysis via EGSEA. KEGG pathways were used for gene set enrichment analysis and the Wilkinson method was utilized to combine p-values [73].

BMDC Culture

Hips, femurs, and tibiae were harvested from WT mice and crushed in a mortar and pestle. Cell suspensions were then filtered and plated at a density of 2–3 × 106 cells/mL in complete IMDM (IMDM medium with 10% FBS, 1.1 mg/mL β-Mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich), 2 mM L-glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and Antibiotic/Antimycotic (ThermoFisher)) in the presence of 200 ng/mL murine Flt3L (Peprotech). Cells were used for experiments after 10–12 days of culture. moDCs were cultured in 20 ng/mL GM-CSF (Peprotech) as previously described [19].

BMDC Adoptive Transfer

Cultured BMDCs were collected and pulsed with 10 μg/mL Alternaria for 4 h. BMDCs were then washed twice and resuspended in cold PBS. 400,000 cells were then adoptively transferred into recipient mice intranasally. In some experiments, BMDCs were pretreated with CB-839 for 1 h prior to Alternaria stimulation.

Metabolite Uptake Assays

For 2-deoxy-2-[(7-nitro-2,1,3-benzoxadiazol-4-yl)amino]-D-glucose (2-NBDG) uptake, BMDCs were stimulated with 10 μg/mL Alternaria or 100 ng/mL LPS for 4 h. Cells were then incubated with 100 μg/mL 2-NBDG for 10 min, washed, and processed for flow cytometric analysis. For BODIPY uptake, BMDCs were stimulated as above and incubated for 30 min with 500 μg/mL C12 or C16 BODIPY prior to staining for flow cytometry.

Untargeted Metabolomics Analysis

BMDCs were stimulated with 10 μg/mL Alternaria or 100 ng/mL LPS for 16 h, collected, washed, and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen. Snap-frozen cell pellets (2 × 106 cells/sample) were resuspended in 20% methanol/water and cells were lysed via sonication. Samples were centrifuged for 5 min at 5000× g to pellet insoluble material. Supernatant was analyzed by liquid chromatography/tandem mass spectrometry using the ThermoFisher Q-Exactive HF system. Cell lysates and a pooled sample were resolved by liquid chromatography on a Vanquish UHPLC system on a silica column using a gradient from 50:50 acetonitrile: water with 0.1% formic acid to 1:99 acetonitrile: water with 0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of 300 μL/min. Samples were analyzed in both positive and negative ion mode. In each ion mode, aliquots of each sample were pooled to generate representative MS2 spectra. Compound Discoverer v.3.1. was used to deconvolute raw LC/MS data with respect to alignment and peak area determination. Compounds were identified relative to the MS2 spectra from the pooled sample. Statistical analysis was performed by Compound Discoverer v.3.1.

qRT-PCR

BMDCs were stimulated as indicated, pelleted, and Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (ThermoFisher) per the manufacturer's instructions. RNA was quantified using a NanoDrop ND-100 spectrophotometer. One microgram of total RNA was reverse transcribed using BioRad Kit. The mRNA expression levels obtained for each gene were normalized to the expression level of the housekeeping gene, Tata box binding protein (Tbp): Normalized relative expression = 2−ΔΔCt [74].

ChIP-PCR

BMDCs were generated as described above and treated with 2 μM CB-839, GSK-J4, or vehicle control prior to stimulation with 5 μg/mL Alternaria for 4 h, washed, and incubated overnight before pelleting. ChIP was performed as previously described. Briefly, 1.0 × 106 cells were resuspended in lysis buffer with protease inhibitors and sonicated to a 500 bp average size using a Covarius sonicator. Pulldowns were performed using antibodies to histone H3, histone H3 K27me3 and histone H3 K4me3 (Cell Signaling #14269, #9733 and #9751). Qpcr was performed and Ct values were used to determine enrichment relative to input. Primer sequences are available upon request.

Humanized Mice

Humanization experiments were performed in the Massey Cancer Center UltraBarrier Animal Facility under the supervision of the Cancer Mouse Models Core personnel at VCU in accordance with the humane treatment of laboratory animals sets forth by the National Institutes of Health and the American Association for the Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care. NOD.Cg-Flt3em2Mvw Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl Tg(FLT3LG)7Sz/SzJ (FLT3L-NSG) mice from Jackson Laboratories were injected with a 5 mg/mL busulfan solution at 25 mg/kg (i.p.), 24 and 48 h prior to transplantation. Following day, 10e4 MACS (Cat#130-100-453) purified human CD34+ stem cells from discarded cord blood samples (VCU IRB# HM20021583) were administered IV in 100 μL PBS. After 12 weeks, mice were considered engrafted.

Statistical Analysis

The details of the replicates for each experiment are listed in the respective figure legends. In all experiments, data are shown as mean ± SEM, and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.2.1). Unpaired Student's t-test, two-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparisons post-test, or Mann–Whitney test were used for statistical analysis as indicated in figure legends. One-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons test was used to compare the variance in more than two groups with one independent factor. In bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq experiments, p-values were adjusted via Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control false discovery rate. In scRNA-seq experiments, Statistical significance is defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, or NS (not significant).
