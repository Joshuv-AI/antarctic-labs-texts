Presentations, management strategies, and outcomes of intracardiac cement embolism following spinal vertebral augmentation: a systematic review of case reports
Authors: Omar Lubbad, Giuseppe L Morassi, Laila Lubbad, Moadh Hwessa, Wajeeh Ullah Mahmood, Nektarios K Mazarakis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntracardiac cement embolism (ICE) is a rare, but potentially life-threatening complication of percutaneous vertebroplasty (PV), percutaneous kyphoplasty (PK), and cement augmented pedicle screw (CAPS) fixation. This systematic review aims to summarise and compare patient presentations, management approaches, and clinical outcomes of ICE.MethodsPRISMA guidelines were followed. Standard medical databases such as MEDLINE, Embase, Pubmed, and Cochrane Library were searched to find case reports or series on patients with ICE following PV, PK, or CAPS. Data on patient demographics, embolus location, diagnostic modalities, management strategies, and outcomes were recorded.Results67 case reports and 2 case series were included with 71 patients in total. 67 patients presented symptomatically with the most common complaints being chest pain and dyspnoea, the other 4 cases were incidentally discovered. Emboli were predominantly found in the right heart and were primarily discovered via echocardiography or computed tomography. Open-heart surgery was the most adopted management strategy (46 cases, 100% retrieval success), especially in complicated symptomatic cases. Endovascular (11 cases, 72% retrieval success) or conservative approaches (14 cases, 2 mortalities) were reserved for asymptomatic cases with smaller emboli or patients not deemed fit for surgery. Reported mortality was low across all patients, but delayed diagnosis was associated with more severe cardiopulmonary complications. Notably, 28 patients presented within the first day postoperatively, indicating an early peak in presentation.ConclusionsOpen-heart surgery seems to be the preferred, most effective intervention for ICE, while endovascular and conservative management were viable in select cases. However, due to the potential for delayed complications, conservative management should be avoided. Earlier diagnosis is imperative for improving patient outcomes and preventing severe complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06380-0.Keywords: Intracardiac cement embolism, Percutaneous vertebroplasty, Percutaneous kyphoplasty, Augmented pedicle screw fixation, Open-heart surgery

Introduction

With the rising prevalence of osteoporosis in the aging population, osteoporotic fractures of the spine, hip, and wrists have become a significant health concern. Among these injuries, vertebral compression fractures (VCFs) are the most common, affecting up to 50% of women in the United States over the age of 80 [1]. In the United Kingdom alone, approximately 3.5 million individuals are diagnosed with osteoporosis, and around 66,000 VCFs are reported clinically each year [2]. Despite the large number of documented cases, studies indicate that the actual incidence is significantly higher, as only about one in three vertebral compression fractures are clinically diagnosed [3]. Minimally invasive vertebral augmentation has increasingly become the preferred treatment option over conservative management, offering enhanced pain relief, functional recovery, and improved health-related quality of life [4, 5]. Vertebral augmentation is performed using various methods, including percutaneous vertebroplasty (PV), percutaneous kyphoplasty (PK), and cement-augmented pedicle screw fixation (CAPS). Generally, these procedures involve injecting bone cement, polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA), into the vertebral body to provide stability and improve alignment [5, 6].

Although these procedures are highly effective and generally safe, when complications do occur, they can have serious consequences for patients. Patients can develop osteomyelitis, neurological injury from cement leakage into the spinal canal, anaphylaxis, or adjacent vertebral fractures resulting from increased spinal rigidity [7–9]. More critically, cement can extravasate into the epidural veins, leading to emboli that migrate through the venous system to the heart and lungs [10]. It is essential to note that approximately 24% of patients undergoing percutaneous vertebroplasty experience cement extravasation, with the rate of complications from this event ranging from 1% to 10% [10]. A recent retrospective study found that intracardiac cement embolisms (ICE) occurred in 3.9% of patients who had undergone percutaneous vertebroplasty [12]. Nonetheless, although rare, ICE is a potentially fatal complication that presents in various forms and often goes undetected due to asymptomatic presentations. Prompt management of ICE is critical, as the escalating sequelae can include cardiac perforation, valvular damage, and secondary pulmonary emboli [6]. This systematic review aims to highlight ICE as a serious complication of vertebral augmentation procedures, with a focus on patient presentations and management outcomes.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines14.

Data sources and search strategy

For this systematic review, we performed an electronic database search of MEDLINE, EMBASE, PubMed, and The Cochrane Library for relevant studies. Articles published until January 2025 were searched for, using the Medical Subject Headings “Cardiac cement embolism following kyphoplasty”, “Cardiac cement embolism following vertebroplasty”, and “Cardiac cement embolism following augmented pedicle screw fixation”. Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) were applied to refine and optimise search results. The titles from the search results were independently reviewed by two authors and were selected to either be included or excluded from the study. The referenced list of studies included was searched to identify any additional articles.

Study selection

No randomised controlled trials or comparative studies were identified; therefore, case reports and series detailing presentations, management, and outcomes of ICE were included. Reports on ICE following non-spinal procedures, patients with cement emboli without cardiac involvement, and those with insufficient clinical detail were excluded. There was no restriction on the country of origin for the studies, language, or hospital of origin. The main outcomes we were searching for were patient presentations, management approaches, and outcomes. The process and results of the literature retrieved are shown in Fig. 1.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers extracted the data from the included studies into a predefined data form, including patient demographics, cement type, embolus location, presentations, diagnostic modalities, time from operation to presentation, management, and outcomes. There were no discrepancies in the selection of the trials or data extraction between the reviewers.

Risk of bias assessment

Due to the inclusion of only case reports and series, formal risk assessment was performed using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist for case reports/series [13].

Results

The primary database search led to two hundred and forty-eight studies, of which one hundred and seventy-nine were excluded after initial screening. The final review included sixty-nine studies (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included reports

Sixty-seven case reports and two case series were included with 71 patients in total [6, 10, 13–79]. The included reports were published between 2008 and 2025. The mean patient age was 65.4 (24–86), with a predominance of female patients (52 female, 18 male, 1 unreported). Of the procedures, 20 were PK, 41 were PV, 6 involved augmented fixations, 3 patients underwent both PK and PV, and 1 case was not described. Thirty-eight procedures were performed in the lumbar spine, 12 in the thoracic spine, 18 involved both thoracic and lumbar vertebrae, 1 involved the lumbosacral spine, and 2 were unreported. PMMA was used in 39 cases, methyl methacrylate (MMA) was used in 8, and other cement types were used in 5 cases, while the cement type was not reported in the remaining cases (Supplementary Table 1).

Quality of included studies assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists for case reports and case series [13]. Overall, the reports demonstrated good reporting quality, with sixty-three studies scoring 8 out of 8, seven scoring 7 out of 8, and one scoring 6 out of 8. Most reports adequately reported patient demographics, diagnosis methods, presentations, management, and outcomes (Supplementary Table 2).

Clinical presentation

Among the 71 cases included in this review, the most common clinical presentations of ICE were chest pain or tightness (n = 44) and dyspnoea (n = 37). Dyspnoea presentations varied significantly from mild exertional shortness of breath to acute respiratory distress and hypoxaemia, with some cases requiring intubation. Eleven patients also presented with signs of haemodynamic instability such as hypotension, syncope, and shock, indicating more severe embolic burden or structural cardiac compromise. Palpitations or abnormal electrocardiogram findings such as atrial fibrillation, tachycardia, premature ventricular contractions, ventricular tachycardia, supraventricular tachycardia, ST-segment elevation/depression, right bundle branch block, or right heart strain were reported in 22 patients. Other less common presentations included heart murmurs, epigastric pain, diaphoresis, impaired left/right ventricular systolic function, tricuspid/pulmonary regurgitation, heart failure, cardiac tamponade, and cardiac arrest. Elevated d-dimer or cardiac markers troponin and pro-BNP levels were also noted in 7 cases. Four cases were incidentally discovered as patients were asymptomatic. The time from surgery to symptom manifestation widely varied between cases, with presentations occurring from the intraoperative or immediate postoperative period to several months or years after the initial procedure. Interestingly, a large proportion of patients presented within the first day postoperatively, suggesting this represents a period of high risk. Beyond this, distribution was widely spread, with notable clusters of patients presenting weeks, months, and years postoperatively (Fig. 2).

Embolus location and diagnostic methods

Cement emboli were most frequently located within the right heart, with 17 cases involving the right atrium, 34 in the right ventricle, and 17 spanning both chambers across the tricuspid valve. Additionally, 2 cases involved the left ventricle, and 1 case reported an unspecified location within the right heart. Cardiac perforation occurred in 17 cases, most commonly affecting the right ventricular wall (13 cases) and, less frequently, the right atrium (4 cases). In 36 cases, ICE was also associated with pulmonary cement emboli, with multiple fragments identified throughout the pulmonary vasculature. Cement emboli were also observed in the IVC and SVC in select cases. Initial detection of cement emboli was primarily through transesophageal/transthoracic echocardiography or chest radiography. Computed tomography (CT) was commonly used to confirm the presence and location of emboli. Several studies also used angiography and fluoroscopy alongside other modalities to aid in diagnosis.

Management strategies and outcomes

Open-heart surgery was the most commonly adopted management strategy, being employed in 46 cases. Open surgical management featured a 100% success rate of emboli retrieval, with most cases featuring uneventful patient recoveries and no postoperative complications. Tricuspid regurgitation was still noted in 3 cases following open-heart surgery, likely due to extensive damage caused by emboli. One patient also required pacemaker implantation due to developing total atrioventricular block. Mortality occurred in 2 patients who had received open surgical intervention; one occurred 10 days postoperatively due to multiple organ failure, and the other passed 7 months postoperatively, likely due to liver failure. Endovascular management was employed in 11 cases, with a 72% embolus retrieval success rate. It was predominantly reserved for cases where patients were deemed unfit for open surgery due to age or comorbidities. In cases where embolus retrieval was successful, patients generally remained asymptomatic and recovered well. In one case where endovascular retrieval failed, the patient ended up receiving open-heart surgery and achieved full recovery. However, in the 2 other cases, endovascular intervention caused the emboli to break into pieces, with some fragments migrating to pulmonary arteries and others persisting in the heart; open-heart surgery was excluded due to risk concerns. One of them developed severe tricuspid regurgitation, and the other’s postoperative condition was not reported. Conservative management was reserved primarily for asymptomatic patients, as well as those unwilling or unfit to undergo surgery. Fourteen patients were managed conservatively, primarily consisting of anticoagulation in the form of low molecular weight heparin or direct oral anticoagulants. Most patients saw improvement in their symptoms or remained asymptomatic, even those with long-term follow-ups. However, one patient who had presented with angina and dyspnea 1 day following PK, who was conservatively managed, was found to have right ventricular wall perforation 4 days later, requiring open-heart surgery. After a prolonged hospital stay, he was discharged to a rehabilitation centre. Mortality occurred in 2 patients who received conservative management, one on day 7 following admission for symptoms due to cardiac and respiratory failure, and the other 3 months following symptoms due to septicaemia from a surgical site infection. One patient passed away 3 h post PV due to a sudden cardiac arrest, which was discovered to be caused by a cement embolus perforating the right atrium and causing cardiac tamponade.

Discussion

This systematic review aims to highlight the differences in patient presentations, management approaches, and outcomes of patients with ICE following vertebral augmentation. Despite being a relatively rare complication, with a symptomatic complication rate of 0.3%, the resulting sequelae can have severe consequences due to the risk of cardiac perforation and haemodynamic compromise [12]. The wide spectrum of ICE presentations underscores the need for heightened vigilance in the postoperative period, especially in patients presenting with cardiopulmonary symptoms following cement-based spinal interventions. Our findings suggest that the right cardiac chambers seem to be the most common sites for embolus lodging, consistent with the venous drainage pathway of the vertebral venous plexus [14]. Interestingly, a significant number of patients also presented with concurrent pulmonary emboli, suggesting emboli migration from the right heart into the pulmonary circulation, further exacerbating symptoms and complicating management. Regarding treatment, open-heart surgery remains the gold standard for large, mobile, or structurally damaging emboli and is associated with high retrieval success and generally favourable outcomes. However, when open-heart surgery would not be tolerated, endovascular and conservative approaches have shown promise in select cases. While retrieval was successful in multiple instances, endovascular management carries the risk of embolus fragmentation, leading to embolus migration and incomplete retrieval. Conservative management was also effective in several cases, especially in incidentally discovered asymptomatic ones; however, as highlighted by one of the reported cases, delayed complications of ICE, such as cardiac perforation, underscore the need for vigilant postoperative monitoring. As PMMA is biologically inert and non-biodegradable, it cannot be resorbed by the pulmonary circulation and may persist as a foreign body, serving as a nidus for infection and further embolic complications [81]. In light of these risks, a conservative approach should be avoided, and active intervention ought to be pursued whenever feasible.

Despite the incidence of symptomatic cases being rare (0.3%), it has been shown that 3.9% of patients develop ICE following vertebral augmentation, suggesting patients presenting several months or years following surgical intervention may have had longstanding asymptomatic ICE prior to the occurrence of symptoms. The heterogeneity of symptom onset, ranging from the intraoperative period to several years postoperatively, raises the question of whether routine postoperative imaging should be implemented to facilitate early detection of ICE. However, given the rarity of ICE and its complications, the practicality and cost-effectiveness of such surveillance remain uncertain. Routinely performed postoperative plain radiographs can be used to help assess for adequate cement confinement within the vertebral body and identify obvious cement extravasation. Obtaining these radiographs immediately postoperatively is crucial, as our findings indicate that a substantial proportion of patients present with ICE during this period, making it a critical window for early detection and monitoring. When radiographs are inconclusive or raise suspicion, cross-sectional imaging with CT should be considered to better delineate cement migration. Although no standardised protocol currently exists for the diagnosis of ICE, symptomatic patients should be initially investigated via echocardiography in the form of TTE or TEE, which allow for immediate bedside assessment of the cardiac chambers and valvular involvement. A chest CT may be warranted in cases where echocardiography is inconclusive or further visualisation is required, especially in cases where there is suspicion of pulmonary or mediastinal extension of cement material. In confirmed cases of ICE, CT can also provide valuable information on cement morphology, distribution, and most importantly, the extent of cement leakage and embolic burden, thereby helping to guide management decisions. Patients with ICE should be closely monitored in an intensive care setting to enable continuous haemodynamic and rhythm monitoring, ensuring early detection and management of acute complications.

Multiple risk factors have been implicated in increasing the likelihood of embolic events. Patient-related factors include advanced age, severe osteoporosis, and high thoracic fracture location, all of which are associated with smaller pedicle size and reduced cancellous bone volume, thereby increasing venous leakage risk [82, 83]. Fracture-related factors such as burst-type fractures, endplate disruption, or cortical breach create direct venous pathways for cement extravasation. Procedure-related factors are particularly important and include the number of augmented levels, large injection volumes, use of low-viscosity cement, rapid injection under high pressure, and inadequate real-time fluoroscopic or CT guidance [82, 83]. Since many of these variables are modifiable, several technical refinements have been proposed to mitigate risk. High-viscosity cement has been shown to reduce leakage rates, limit injection volumes, and improve postoperative pain outcomes compared to low-viscosity formulations [84]. Injection technique should prioritise slow, controlled, incremental delivery of cement, ideally during its viscous phase, to minimise uncontrolled venous migration [84]. Intraoperative imaging with continuous biplanar fluoroscopy or CT-fluoroscopy improves real-time detection of early venous extravasation, allowing for immediate cessation of injection. Additional strategies include avoiding excessive pressurisation, limiting augmentation to necessary vertebral levels, and careful patient selection, particularly in high-risk thoracic fractures [82–84]. These adjustments collectively help reduce the incidence of cement leakage and subsequent embolisation, thereby lowering the likelihood of potentially life-threatening complications such as intracardiac or pulmonary cement embolism.

In the broader context of osteoporotic VCF management, conservative strategies such as analgesia, bracing, and physiotherapy remain foundational, although high-quality data has yet to establish their superiority over augmentation techniques [85]. Contemporary evidence indicates that PV and PK are comparably effective in alleviating pain and improving short-term function; however, the long-term advantages of these procedures over conservative approaches have been questioned, highlighting the need for better patient selection [86, 87]. Looking forward, ongoing and planned studies such as the VOPE2 randomised sham-controlled trial are poised to clarify the efficacy of PV by focusing on rigorously defined patient subgroups [88]. Such developments underline that while vertebral augmentation remains a widely practiced option, its benefits and risks continue to be refined, as highlighted by our review. An additional concern following vertebral augmentation is the elevated incidence of secondary vertebral fractures. While augmentation provides short-term relief, these procedures may be associated with new fractures in treated or adjacent vertebrae, with an overall incidence of approximately 16.6% [89]. Notably, adjacent vertebrae account for over 26% of these events. Risk factors for secondary fractures include reduced bone mineral density, excessive cement volume, and cement leakage, most of which are already implicated in the pathogenesis of ICE [90, 91]. These parallels suggest that the mechanisms contributing to secondary fractures may also predispose to cement migration and embolisation, underscoring the need for careful patient selection and refined procedural technique.

Our study was the first systematic review to comprehensively evaluate and compare patient presentations, management strategies, and outcomes of ICE following vertebral augmentation. The majority of included trials had high appraisal scores on the JBI checklist, suggesting good reporting quality in the case reports and series reviewed, increasing the confidence of our findings. However, our study does have several limitations. Given the rarity of ICE and the lack of higher-quality evidence, our review was limited to case reports and series, which inherently carry a significant risk of bias. Additionally, there was limited follow-up data, as several studies failed to report the postoperative condition of patients, and among those that did, very few included long-term follow-ups. Moreover, given the nature of these studies, the quality of follow-ups may also be compromised, as they are not standardised and may differ significantly across included cases. Further multi-centre prospective studies are warranted to evaluate the effectiveness of techniques aimed at minimising cement extravasation, ideally incorporating standardised postoperative imaging for surveillance. In addition, these studies should aim to identify patient and procedure risk factors for both cement leakage and ICE to better refine preventative strategies and improve patient risk stratification.

Conclusion

ICE is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication of vertebral augmentation procedures, with presentations ranging from incidental findings to severe cardiopulmonary compromise. Management strategies and outcomes vary between cases based on embolism size, location, symptoms, and patient health, with open-heart surgery being the most effective treatment. Endovascular and conservative management approaches were also shown to be viable strategies; however, due to the potential for delayed complications, conservative management should be avoided. Given the severe consequences of ICE, clinicians must be aware of such a complication, and that the risk be adequately conveyed to patients undergoing cement-based spinal interventions.
