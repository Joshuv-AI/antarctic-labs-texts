Analytical comparison of three canine C-reactive protein assays in clinical use in Japan
Authors: Shu HOTOMI, Masaaki OCHI, Noboru FUJITANI, Akihisa HATA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractC-reactive protein (CRP) is a key biomarker for diagnosis and prognosis in dogs. Accurate and consistent CRP measurements are essential for clinical decision-making. In Japan, different CRP measurement systems are commonly used, but their compatibility has not been validated. In this study, we evaluated the correlation and agreement among three CRP assay systems (immunonephelometry, immunoturbidimetry, and dry chemistry) using 138 dog plasma samples. Strong correlations (r >0.9) were observed, but regression analysis and Bland-Altman plots revealed systematic errors, indicating that these systems are not interchangeable. These findings highlight the need for system-specific reference ranges and clinical decision thresholds and suggest that a common calibration agent could enhance comparability and support the standardization of CRP measurements in veterinary medicine.Keywords: acute-phase protein, canine, C-reactive protein, standardization

The level of C-reactive protein (CRP), an acute-phase protein, increases rapidly in the blood following inflammation and decreases just as swiftly during recovery, making it a useful biomarker in both human and veterinary medicine [1, 4, 12]. In dogs, CRP is routinely used in clinical practice to assess inflammation associated with surgery, internal disease, and infections [2, 8,9,10]. It plays a critical role in diagnosis, treatment monitoring, and prognostic evaluation. Multiple CRP measurement systems are used in veterinary clinical laboratories, based on immunoturbidimetry, immunonephelometry, and dry chemistry [3, 5, 7, 9, 11]. These methods differ in reagents, detection principles, calibration protocols, and measurement ranges. However, the comparability of these methods has not been thoroughly validated, potentially leading to variability in CRP assay results across facilities.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the correlation and agreement among three CRP assays used in Japan: Arrows Laser CRP (immunonephelometry; Arrows Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan), Shima Lab Canine CRP Neo for automatic biochemical analyzer use (immunoturbidimetry; Canine CRP Neo, Shima Laboratory Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) with an automatic biochemical analyzer (Model 3100, Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), and Fuji Dri Chem CRP (dry chemistry; Fuji Dri Chem NX500V, FUJIFILM Medical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

This study was reviewed by the Ethics Review Committee of the Veterinary Medical Education Hospital, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Okayama University of Science (approval number: 2020-0007). The committee determined that performance testing of clinical laboratory reagents using residual samples does not fall within the scope of ethical review and was therefore exempt from ethical consideration. In total, 138 leftover heparinized plasma samples were used in this study. All samples were from client-owned dogs that presented to a veterinary secondary care hospital for various clinical reasons.

Within-run precision for each analytical system was assessed using 10 repeated measurements on pooled plasma (approximately 10 mg/L). Comparative statistical analyses included Spearman correlation, Passing–Bablok regression, Bland–Altman plot, and observed total error (TEobs; defined as the sum of bias between two measurement methods and twice the coefficient of variation [CV], where CV was calculated as the standard deviation divided by the mean of repeated measurements and expressed as a percentage; TEobs=|Bias%| + 2 × CV%) analyses.

Analytical performance was interpreted according to the performance goals (TEopt, TEdes, and TEmin) recommended by the American Society of Veterinary Clinical Pathologists (ASVCP) guidelines [6]. Although the ASVCP guidelines indicate that the total error values based on biologic variation are not intended as current performance requirements, they are presented as potential future quality goals. Therefore, in the present study, these values were used as reference indicators for evaluating the analytical performance of canine CRP assays. Additionally, proportional bias in Bland–Altman plot analysis was statistically assessed using linear regression of the differences against the means, performed using Microsoft Excel (Data Analysis ToolPak; Redmond, WA, USA), where a significant non-zero slope (P<0.05) was considered evidence of proportional bias. Outlier candidates were identified based on regression residuals. For each comparison, residuals were calculated as the difference between the observed and predicted values from Passing–Bablok regression and standardized with the residual standard deviation. Samples with standardized residuals greater than ± 3 were considered discrepant. These samples were retained in the analysis but are discussed separately to illustrate the extent of disagreement between systems. Statistical analysis was performed using samples that fell within the measurable ranges of two compared methods.

The CV of within-run precision was 6.18% for Arrows Laser CRP, 1.29% for Shima Lab CRP Neo, and 7.58% for Fuji Dri Chem CRP. These results achieved the recommended CV (12.16%) for CRP analysis, as outlined by the ASVCP [6].

The regression equation for Arrows Laser CRP (x) and Fuji Dri Chem CRP (y) was y=1.250x + 1.438. The distribution pattern of the Bland–Altman plot, together with the results of regression analysis of bias against the mean, indicated the presence of both fixed bias and weak proportional bias between the two measurement methods. The bias and 95% limits of agreement (mean ± 2SD) for the difference between the two measurement methods were 36% and −42% to 114%, respectively (Fig. 2A). The TEobs was 50.9%, exceeding the biologic variation-based performance goals (TEopt, TEdes, and TEmin) for canine CRP outlined in the ASVCP guidelines as potential future quality targets [6]. This result indicates that none of the recommended performance targets were met, suggesting that clinical interchangeability of CRP assay results between these measurement systems is poor, both within and across facilities. In practice, it may be necessary to establish separate reference intervals and clinical decision thresholds for each system; otherwise, applying the same cut-off values could lead to misinterpretation in clinical decision-making.

The regression equation for Arrows Laser CRP (x) and Shima Lab Canine CRP Neo (y) was y=0.688x + 5.953. The distribution pattern of the Bland–Altman plot, together with the results of the regression analysis of bias against the mean, indicated the presence of both fixed bias and proportional bias between the measurement methods. The bias and 95% limits of agreement for the difference between the measurement methods were 6% and −84% to 96%, respectively (Fig. 2B). The TEobs was 18.3%. Additionally, the measurement values showed a relationship where Shima Lab Canine CRP Neo was higher in the low-concentration range, while Arrows Laser CRP yielded higher values in the high-concentration range.

The regression equation for Fuji Dri Chem CRP (x) and Shima Lab Canine CRP Neo (y) was y=0.552x + 5.183. The distribution pattern of the Bland–Altman plot, combined with the results of the regression analysis, demonstrated both fixed bias and proportional bias between these systems. The bias and 95% limits of agreement for the difference between the measurement methods were −19% and −91% to 53%, respectively (Fig. 2C). The TEobs was 34.2%. Furthermore, the measurement values indicated a relationship in which Shima Lab Canine CRP Neo results were higher in the low-concentration range, whereas Fuji Dri Chem CRP produced higher values in the high-concentration range.

In the comparison between Arrows Laser CRP and Fuji Dri Chem CRP systems, the TEobs values (50.9%) exceeded even the minimal performance goal (TEmin=44.37%) presented in the ASVCP guidelines [6], indicating poor interchangeability. In contrast, in the other two comparisons, the TEobs values were below the minimal goal, with 18.3% for Arrows Laser CRP versus Shima Lab Canine CRP Neo (within the desirable goal, TEdes=29.58%) and 34.2% for Fuji Dri Chem CRP versus Shima Lab Canine CRP Neo (between TEdes and TEmin). However, Bland–Altman plots for both of these pairs demonstrated wide 95% limits of agreement and revealed proportional systematic bias, in which opposite-direction errors at low and high concentrations likely canceled each other out. Consequently, the apparently favorable TEobs values may underestimate the true degree of disagreement.

In this study, deviations following proportional or fixed systematic errors were observed in different specimens. However, the regression residual analysis revealed a few samples with discrepant measurements beyond the pattern of systematic bias. For example, the CRP concentration in one sample was measured as 11 mg/L using Arrows Laser CRP, and this was comparable to the concentration determined using Shima Lab Canine CRP Neo (12 mg/L) but markedly higher than the concentration obtained using Fuji Dri Chem CRP (56 mg/L). The CRP concentration in another sample was measured as 85 mg/L on Arrows, which was considerably lower than the result obtained using Shima Neo (43 mg/L) and intermediate to the value on Fuji (69 mg/L). Similarly, the CRP concentration in a sample was measured as 84 mg/L on Arrows, 44 mg/L on Shima Neo, and 70 mg/L on Fuji. The CRP concentration in a sample was determined to be as high as 165 mg/L on Arrows but only 46 mg/L on Shima Neo, while Fuji Dri Chem yielded values above its upper measurement limit (>70 mg/L). These discrepancies were reproducible on repeat measurements, suggesting that they were not due to random analytical error but rather due to matrix effects or assay principle-dependent reactivity.

It is widely recognized that the readings of immunoturbidimetric and immunonephelometric assays, which are performed with test tubes, can be affected by turbidity caused by chylomicrons. For dry-chemistry methods, which involve multiple steps including antigen–antibody, enzymatic, and colorimetric oxidation reactions, the package inserts indicate that considerable hemolysis and the presence of reducing agents or enzyme inhibitors can act as interfering factors. Among the samples showing discrepant CRP measurements, mild turbidity due to chylomicrons was observed in some samples, but this was considered unlikely to account for the magnitude of the observed differences. Furthermore, no visible interference due to hemoglobin or bilirubin was detected. While it is possible that some components in the samples contributed to the discrepancies in CRP concentrations, no definitive evidence was obtained.

This study demonstrated that while the three commercially available canine CRP measurement methods showed a high correlation in measurement values, they lacked compatibility and were therefore unsuitable for clinical interchangeability. Hence, it is necessary to establish separate reference ranges and clinical cut-off values for each measurement method.

The high correlation and presence of systematic errors observed in this study indicate that measurement errors can be reduced by standardizing the calibrators [13]. For example, fixed errors (e.g., between Arrows Laser CRP and Fuji Dri Chem CRP) can be corrected by adjusting the zero point or reference point and proportional systematic errors (e.g., between Arrows Laser CRP and Shima Lab Canine CRP Neo and between Fuji Dri Chem CRP and Shima Lab Canine CRP Neo) can be addressed by sensitivity correction using standard materials of multiple concentrations. In human laboratory medicine, international reference standards for CRP (e.g., World Health Organization reference material) have already enabled assay harmonization. In contrast, no equivalent standard calibrators currently exist in veterinary CRP testing, and each manufacturer relies on proprietary material. The introduction of a common calibrator may enable future standardization of measurement values and improvement of compatibility.
