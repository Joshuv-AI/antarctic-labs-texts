Effects of Alcohol Septal Ablation on Exercise Hemodynamics, Symptoms, and Quality of Life in Obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Anne M Dybro, Torsten B Rasmussen, Roni R Nielsen, Bertil T Ladefoged, Anders L D Pedersen, Christian J Terkelsen, Mads J Andersen, Morten K Jensen, Steen H Poulsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe impact of alcohol septal ablation (ASA) on exercise hemodynamics, symptoms, and quality of life in patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is poorly described according to contemporary standards. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of ASA on filling pressures at rest and during exercise, left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, symptoms, and quality of life in patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.MethodsThis prospective study enrolled 24 patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy referred for ASA. Right‐heart catheterization and echocardiography were performed at rest and during exercise, before and ≈7 months after ASA. The primary outcome was the difference in pulmonary artery wedge pressure at 75 W. Secondary outcomes assessed left ventricular outflow tract gradients, New York Heart Association functional class, and the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire Clinical Summary Score.ResultsASA significantly reduced pulmonary artery wedge pressure at 75 W (33±9 mm Hg versus 24±10 mm Hg; P<0.001), decreased left ventricular outflow tract gradients at rest (49 [interquartile range (IQR), 24–90] mm Hg versus 17 [IQR, 14–27] mm Hg; P<0.01), at peak exercise (45 [IQR, 29–90] mm Hg versus 32 [IQR, 21–61] mm Hg; P = 0.02), and after exercise (96 [IQR, 34–161] mm Hg versus 28 [IQR, 21–62] mm Hg; P<0.01). The Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire Clinical Summary Score was significantly increased (65.4±16 versus 76.7±16; P<0.01). ASA improved New York Heart Association functional class, with 54% in class III before treatment, compared with 13% after treatment (P<0.001).ConclusionsASA significantly reduced exercise‐induced filling pressures and LVOT obstruction, leading to improved symptoms and quality of life in patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.,Keywords: alcohol ablation, exercise hemodynamics, obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, septal reduction therapySubject Categories: Hemodynamics, Cardiomyopathy

Obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (oHCM) is a complex disease characterized by left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy and mitral valve abnormalities, causing LV outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) and, hence, myocardial dysfunction. 1 The majority of patients with oHCM are disabled by symptoms of heart failure, with dyspnea and fatigue limiting their functional capacity. 2 The first line of treatment to relieve symptoms is medical with nonvasodilating β‐adrenergic receptor blocking agents. 1 , 3 Nevertheless, a significant number of patients continue to experience severe symptoms, requiring more advanced treatment options such as septal reduction therapy (SRT) or novel medical treatment with myosin inhibitors. 1 , 4 Alcohol septal ablation (ASA) was introduced in the mid‐1990s as a minimally invasive SRT for patients with oHCM in whom symptoms were not relieved by medical treatment. 5 The catheter‐based injection of alcohol into a septal perforator artery aims to induce a localized infarction of the proximal hypertrophied septum, reducing septal wall thickness and consequently abolishing outflow obstruction. The technique has proven effective in reducing LVOTO and symptoms in most patients with oHCM. 6 , 7 Early studies demonstrated that, with extensive ablation, a complete elimination of LVOTO was associated with favorable hemodynamic changes and an improvement in exercise capacity. 8 However, the extensive ablation also came with a greater risk of pacemaker implantation and increased mortality rate. Over the past 2 decades, the technique has been further refined, and today a more restricted ASA strategy is practiced, and the injection of large volumes of alcohol into multiple septal branches is generally not recommended. 1 , 9 In light of new emerging treatment options with myosin inhibitors for patients with oHCM, we performed a comprehensive study to assess the clinical and hemodynamic effects of contemporary ASA at an experienced tertiary referral center. 10 , 11 , 12 We analyzed the impact of ASA treatment on outflow obstruction, exercise hemodynamics, symptoms, and quality of life (QoL).

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The data sets include deidentified participant data and are subject to ethical and legal restrictions as approved by the Ethics Committee of the Central Denmark Region (1‐10‐72‐62‐19). Data sharing will be considered for academic purposes only and contingent on the execution of a data‐sharing agreement.

Study Design

We performed a prospective study on the effect of ASA in symptomatic patients with oHCM. Patients referred for ASA at the Department of Cardiology at Aarhus University Hospital in Denmark from May 2019 to March 2023 were screened for eligibility. Patients were examined before and after ASA. Effect parameters were assessed on these 2 examination days. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Central Denmark Region (1‐10‐72‐62‐19) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent, and the study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04066777).

Patients

Adult patients (aged >18 years) with a diagnosis of oHCM without previous ASA or myectomy, who fulfilled the European Society of Cardiology guideline criteria for SRT and had been referred for ASA, were eligible. 1 oHCM was defined as wall thickness ≥15 mm not explained solely by loading conditions, and a peak LV outflow tract (LVOT) gradient ≥30 mm Hg at rest or ≥50 mm Hg at Valsalva maneuver or post exercise. Eligible patients for SRT were those with an LVOT gradient ≥50 mm Hg (rest, Valsalva, or exercise), severe symptoms (New York Heart Association [NYHA] functional class III–IV), or recurrent syncope despite medical treatment. Also, patients with mild symptoms (NYHA class II) refractory to medical therapy with a resting or maximum provoked gradient of ≥50 mm Hg (exercise or Valsalva) were eligible. Patients had to be able to perform a cardiopulmonary exercise test. Patients with atrial fibrillation or flutter and those who had a pacemaker and were pace dependent were excluded.

Procedures

Guideline‐recommend medical therapy for oHCM remained unchanged between the 2 assessments. The assessment included evaluation of NYHA functional class, the Canadian Cardiovascular Society angina score, the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire, 23‐item version, and measurement of NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide). 13 Clinical assessment included a 12‐lead ECG and automatic blood pressure monitoring (average of 5 separate measurements in a 20‐minute period). All patients underwent simultaneous transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and right‐heart catheterization at rest and during cardiopulmonary exercise testing.

Transthoracic Echocardiography

Examinations were performed using Vivid E95 (General Electric) and analyzed offline using EchoPac Version 203. Both before and after ASA, a comprehensive echocardiographic examination was performed at rest according to guideline recommendations. 14 Details on assessment of LVOTO and mitral regurgitation are listed in Data S1. During exercise, LVOT gradients were assessed at each level of exercise and during recovery.

Right‐Heart Catheterization

Under local anesthesia and guided by ultrasound, a 7.5‐French sheath was inserted into the right internal jugular vein. Transducers were leveled and zeroed at the phlebostatic axis. A 7‐French Swan–Ganz catheter (Edwards Lifesciences) was advanced into the pulmonary artery, guided by pressure waveforms and fluoroscopy. Mean right atrial pressure, systolic and diastolic pulmonary artery pressure (PAP), pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP), and cardiac output (CO) were obtained at rest. Mean PAP was estimated by 0.61×systolic PAP+2 mm Hg. 15 All pressures were assessed at end‐expiration as the average of 3 cardiac cycles. Right atrial pressure and PAWP were measured at mid–A wave. CO was measured using thermodilution and as the mean of 3 measurements with <10% variation. Stroke volume (SV) was calculated as = CO/heart rate. Pulmonary vascular resistance = (mPAP−PAWP)/CO, and systemic vascular resistance = (mean arterial pressure−RAP)/CO were calculated using standard formulas. PAWP/CO was calculated as = (PAWPpeak exercise–PAWPrest)/(COpeak exercise–COrest). Arterial‐venous oxygen content difference (AVO2diff) was measured directly as the difference between systemic and pulmonary arterial O2 contents = (saturation x hemoglobin x 1.36).

Simultaneous TTE and Invasive Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing

A symptom‐limiting cardiopulmonary exercise test was performed on a semisupine bicycle (eBike L Ergometer, General Electric) with patients in a 45‐degree upright position with a left lateral tilt to ensure an acoustic window for simultaneous TTE. The workload started at 0 W, with resistance increasing every 3 minutes in 10 W or 25 W increments (depending on the patient's individual physical capacity as judged by the primary investigator). Patients were encouraged to maintain a fixed pedaling speed of 60 to 70 rounds per minute and to exercise until exhaustion (Borg scale >18), which defined peak exercise. 16 To determine the peak oxygen uptake (pVO2), gas exchange was measured using a breath‐by‐breath technique (Jaeger MasterScreen CPX, CareFusion). The pVO2 was calculated as the highest 15‐second average values within the final 30 seconds of peak exercise. The respiratory exchange ratio, defined as the amount of carbon dioxide produced and the oxygen used, was obtained at peak exercise. At each level of exercise, right atrial pressure, systolic PAP, diastolic PAP, mean PAP, PAWP, CO, heart rate, and noninvasive blood pressure were recorded. At rest and peak exercise, a mixed venous blood sample was drawn from the pulmonary artery and analyzed for levels of lactate concentration and saturations. All echocardiographic and invasive hemodynamic measures were obtained 2 minutes into each workload, ensuring a steady state.

Outcome

The primary outcome was the effect of ASA on PAWP at 75 W (or maximum exercise, if this was <75 W). The secondary outcome was the effect on peak hemodynamics, LVOT gradients and Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire Clinical Summary Score (KCCQ‐CSS).

Statistical Analysis

The power calculation was based on measures of filling pressures from previous trials of patients with oHCM. 17 , 18 We assumed that before ASA, PAWP would be 18±7 mm Hg at rest, increasing to 32±10 mm Hg at 75 W. To detect a 7‐mmHg change in PAWP 19 at 75 W after ASA (SD of ±10mmHg), using a two‐sided significance level of 5% and a power of 90%, a sample size of 24 patients were required. Normality of data was assessed by Q‐Q plots and histograms. Based on this assessment, normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean±SD and were analyzed using paired Student's t tests. Nonnormally distributed continuous variables are reported as median with interquartile range (IQR; 25th–75th percentile) and were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Categorical variables are reported as counts with percentages (%) and were analyzed using the χ2 test. For correlation analysis, Pearson's coefficient was calculated, and linear regression was used to estimate the PAWP/CO slope. All analyses were done in the modified intention‐to‐treat population, which in this study included all enrolled patients, as all completed both examinations. All statistical tests were conducted at a 2‐sided 0.05 level of significance. P values and 95% CI presented in this report have not been adjusted for multiplicity. No imputation was made for missing data. Analyses were performed using STATA/IC version 15.0 (StataCorp LP).

RESULTS

Baseline Patient Characteristics

Twenty‐four patients with oHCM were enrolled in the study. The mean age of patients was 60±11 years, with a slight overweight of men, and all patients were of White race (Table 1). The majority of patients were on guideline‐directed medical treatment with either β blockers or nondihydropyridine calcium antagonists. There was an almost equal distribution of patients in NYHA classes II and III (Table 2). Echocardiographic measures showed asymmetrical septal hypertrophy with an average septal wall thickness of 20±3 mm, and an LV ejection fraction of 70%±8%. Diastolic parameters showed an increased left atrial volume index of 55±17 mL/m2 and E/e´ of 17±9. LVOT gradients at rest were 49 (IQR, 24–90) mm Hg and 102 (IQR, 78–139) mm Hg during the Valsalva maneuver. On average, patients were examined 3 months before ASA and 7 months after ASA.

ASA Procedure

In all patients, ASA was performed in only 1 septal perforator artery, guided by simultaneous ultrasound contrast TTE. 20 On average, 2.5 mL of absolute alcohol was injected into the selected artery. In the catheterization laboratory, the procedure was associated with an 82% reduction in invasively assessed resting LVOT gradients (Table 1). Patients were hospitalized for ≈6 days after the procedure to rule out significant conduction disturbances. Advanced atrioventricular block was seen in 13% of patients, all of whom had a pacemaker implanted before discharge. No other serious complications were reported in relation to the procedure or during the observation period.

Invasive Hemodynamic Changes from Rest to Exercise

The primary end point of change in PAWP at 75 W was reduced with ASA treatment (−9 mm Hg [95% CI, −5.0 to −13.1 mm Hg]; P<0.001; Figure 1). Furthermore, PAWP was significantly reduced by 35% at rest, 31% at 25 W, 30% at 50 W, and 25% at peak exercise. Similarly, all other intracardiac pressures, both at rest and during peak exercise, were reduced with ASA treatment (Table 3). There was a significant reduction in both PAWP A and V wave, the latter indicating attenuated mitral valve regurgitation, which was also observed with TTE. Resting CO was unchanged with treatment (P=0.84), and we observed a blunted CO reserve with only 2‐fold increase at peak exercise, and no significant effect of ASA. Accordingly, the estimate of PAWP/CO slope, with higher values reflecting more severe heart failure, was reduced from 3.8 to 2.7, yet it did not attain statistical significance (Figure 2). The overall pVO2 was unchanged (P=0.26). In 2 patients, the ventilatory threshold was reached at <75 W, in 6 patients it was at 75 W, and in the remaining 16 patients, the threshold was reached at ≥100 W (for further details see Data S1).

Left Ventricular Function and Outflow Obstruction

ASA was associated with a significant reduction of the proximal interventricular septum diameter, a decrease in LVOT gradients, and a reduced volume of the left atrium (Table 2). The LVOT gradients after ASA were reduced by 65% at rest (49 [IQR, 24–90] mm Hg versus 17 [IQR, 14–27] mm Hg; P<0.01) and by 51% during the Valsalva maneuver (102 [IQR, 78–139] mm Hg versus 52 [IQR, 34–90] mm Hg; P<0.001). In total, 79% of patients presented with a resting LVOT gradient <30 mm Hg after ASA treatment. During peak exercise, the reduction in gradients was 29% (45 [IQR, 29–90] mm Hg versus 32 [IQR, 21–61] mm Hg; P=0.02), and after a recovery of 5 minutes, it was reduced by 71% (96 [IQR, 34–161] mm Hg versus 28 [IQR, 21–62] mm Hg; P<0.01; Figure 3). The degree of mitral valve regurgitation was also significantly reduced, with 50% having moderate to severe regurgitation before ASA, compared with 17% after ASA (P=0.046). The systolic function assessed by LV ejection fraction remained unaffected by the procedure (P=0.52). ASA was associated with a significant reduction in left atrial volume (−9 mL/m2 [95% CI, −15 to −3 mL/m2]; P<0.01).

Effect of ASA on QoL and Symptoms

ASA was associated with a clinically significant improvement in symptoms and QoL, with an increase in the KCCQ‐CSS (11.3 [95% CI, 4.5–18.2]; P<0.01; Table 2). The improvement was confirmed by changes in NYHA functional class. Before ASA treatment, 54% of patients were in NYHA functional class III compared with 13% after ASA (P<0.001; Figure 3). Similar findings for the Canadian Cardiovascular Society angina class were observed, with 67% reporting a degree of angina before ASA, which was reduced to 33% after ASA (P=0.02). There was no significant correlation between the reduction in LV filling pressures and improvement in KCCQ‐CSS (r 2=0.2, P=0.3).

DISCUSSION

This study assessing the comprehensive effects of contemporary ASA in patients with oHCM demonstrated that the procedure was associated with a significant reduction in LV filling pressures both at rest and during exercise, along with an improvement in symptoms and QoL. The procedure led to favorable functional and structural changes, with significant reductions in LVOTO gradients, severity of mitral valve regurgitation, and a reduced left atrial volume.

LV Dysfunction and Outflow Obstruction

The relationship between the pathophysiology and development of symptoms in patients with oHCM is multifactorial and complex. 21 Together with inherent abnormalities of the myocardium, the presence of an LVOTO leads to elevated LV systolic pressure, which triggers a complex chain of abnormalities, including prolonged ventricular relaxation, increased LV diastolic pressure and myocardial ischemia. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 The increase in ventricular diastolic pressure is subsequently transmitted backward to the left atrium, causing dilation of the chamber. Regurgitant mitral flow may further exacerbate the hemodynamicburden on the left atrium. The resulting pressure overload in the left atrium and pulmonary circulation often manifests clinically as exertional dyspnea. 26 Data from the present study, demonstrating a reduction in LV filling pressures, are consistent with previous research evaluating the acute hemodynamic effects of ASA. A prior study assessing the acute effects of ASA on resting filling pressures found that the relief of systolic contraction load influenced myocardial relaxation, thereby impacting diastolic properties and subsequently reducing left atrial filling pressures. 18 These findings were further supported by another study investigating acute hemodynamic changes with ASA, which showed a significant decrease in end‐diastolic pressure and a notable improvement in diastolic stiffness following the procedure. 27 Data from the present study extend this knowledge by examining the median‐term effects, which indicate sustained benefits in reducing LV filling pressures over time.

In oHCM, symptoms are often exacerbated during exertion, and previous studies have demonstrated that exercise in symptomatic oHCM is associated with an abnormal increase in LV filling pressures. 28 , 29 , 30 In this study, we chose the primary end point of PAWP to be assessed at submaximal exercise estimated to 75 W, reflecting insights into the cardiovascular system's response to real‐world stressors rather than peak exercise. However, we observed that in one third of the included patients, the ventilatory threshold was ≤75 W. Further analysis of the stepwise increase in LV filling pressures from rest to 25 and 50 W revealed a consistent pattern after ASA, with a significant decrease in PAWP observed at all levels of exercise. After 7 months, ASA was associated with a sustained reduction in LVOTO both at rest and with exercise. Consequently, the degree of mitral valve regurgitation and LV filling pressures were significantly decreased, which was also reflected in a reduced volume of the left atrium. These findings may indicate that long‐term reduction in LVOTO is necessary for remodeling to occur and align with previous studies demonstrating a reduction in pulmonary pressure after relief of LVOTO by surgical myectomy. 19 The finding that CO did not increase significantly by treatment, may be related to the inherent hypercontractile properties of the myocardium, which was not significantly affected by the mechanistic relief of LVOTO by ASA. The estimation of the PAWP/CO slope indicated a decrease with treatment, yet it did not reach significance. However, the slope value is an important parameter when assessing patients with heart failure and preserved ejection fraction, as it reflects the dynamic interplay between LV filling pressure and CO, underscoring the pathophysiologic changes related to symptoms. 31 , 32 Several larger trials of myosin inhibitors conducted in recent years have demonstrated significant effects on symptoms and remodeling. 10 , 11 , 12 These trials have shown a clear signal of a reduction in NT‐proBNP, which did not reach statistical significance in this study. This finding is surprising because natriuretic peptides serve as a surrogate marker of the hemodynamic burden on the cardiac chambers, and it is possible that the small sample size affects these outcomes. 33 Additionally, the presence of postcapillary pulmonary hypertension has been linked to increased all‐cause death in oHCM. 34 In this study, ASA reduced pulmonary arterial pressures, suggesting that ASA could be beneficial with respect to reducing the mortality rate in these patients. This is consistent with the survival rates of patients treated with ASA, which are comparable to those in the general population. 35 Finally, we observed considerable variability in LVOT gradients, a well‐known phenomenon in oHCM, with great day‐to‐day fluctuations. 36 This reflects the dynamic nature of LVOTO, which is dependent upon ventricular preload, afterload, and contractile state. 37 This variability is illustrated in Figure 3, where some patients exhibited relatively low resting gradients, despite meeting the criteria for referral for SRT during multiple assessments of symptoms and echocardiographic evaluations before ASA treatment.

Symptom Improvement

The primary goal of treatment in oHCM is to relieve symptoms, improve cardiac function, increase physical capacity, and enhance QoL. 1 , 4 Data from this study indicate that ASA is associated with a significant and clinically relevant improvement in NYHA and Canadian Cardiovascular Society functional class, along with a marked increase in the KCCQ‐CSS score. 13 The Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire score is well validated in oHCM and reflects patients´ self‐reported symptom burden, physical function, social limitations, and QoL. 38 In the present era of larger randomized trials assessing the effect of myosin inhibitors in oHCM, primary end points have focused on symptom improvement and functional parameters rather than traditional major cardiovascular end points of death and heart failure hospitalizations, as related deaths are rare. 39 The effect of ASA on the KCCQ‐CSS score in this study is comparable to results from trials of myosin inhibitors; however, a direct comparison is challenging since our study lacked a sham‐controlled arm. 10 , 11 , 12 In the SEQUOIA‐HCM (Safety, Efficacy, and Quantitative Understanding of Obstruction, Impact of Aficamten in Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy) trial, 24 weeks of treatment with aficamten resulted in a 7‐point increase in the KCCQ‐CSS score compared with placebo, while the EXPLORER‐HCM (Clinical Study to Evaluate Mavacamten [MYK‐461] in Adults With Symptomatic Obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy) trial reported a 9.1‐point increase with mavacamten over 30 weeks compared with placebo. As reported in the myosin inhibitor studies, we also found ≈15%–20% of ASA‐treated patients to be nonresponders, with 5 patients showing increased filling pressures and 3 patients demonstrating a decline in their KCCQ‐CSS score after ASA treatment (Figure 3). Potential explanations for these findings include differences in anatomic structures, daily fluctuations in LVOTO, and varying stages of HCM. 40

Study Limitations

Several limitations to this study should be noted. First, it was a relatively small, single‐center observational study, and the generalizability of the findings should be approached with caution. Although ASA significantly affected the primary end point, the smallsample size lacked sufficient power to detect changes in NT‐proBNP and peak VO₂. The nonsignificant results may reflect a type II error, suggesting that a larger study could potentially demonstrate a significant effect of ASA treatment. Second, the lack of a sham‐controlled arm limits our ability to accurately assess the true impact of treatment on symptoms and QoL, as placebo effects are commonly observed in placebo‐treated groups. Third, ASA theoretically solves a mechanistic problem with outflow obstruction, and hence remodeling is seen after 7 months. Whether the treatment influences the natural progression of the myocardial disease over time remain unknown, leaving conclusions about its effects on death and prognosis speculative, yet previous studies have documented favorable long‐term outcomes associated with this treatment. 7 Finally, the robust but complex examination setup, which allows for simultaneous assessment of right‐heart catheterization, cardiopulmonary exercise testing, and echocardiographic measures, may affect patients' ability to reach a level of peak exercise comparable with out‐of‐hospital physical activity.

CONCLUSIONS

In patients with oHCM, ASA effectively reduced LV filling pressures both at rest and during exercise. This effect was attributed to the alleviation of LVOTO and mitral valve regurgitation, which subsequently led to a decrease in left atrial size. These favorable structural and hemodynamic changes were associated with improvements in symptoms and QoL.
