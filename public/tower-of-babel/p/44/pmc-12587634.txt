Crosstalk between airway epithelial cells and mast cells in airway inflammation
Authors: Junfan Wang, Yuting Liang, Liting Wu, Ting Xu, Wenfang Zhuang, Jia Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Airway epithelial cells (AECs) and mast cells (MCs) are pivotal initiators and amplifiers of airway inflammation, orchestrating a dynamic crosstalk that drives pathological hyperreactivity in respiratory diseases. AECs, as the frontline barrier, detect pathogens and allergens, releasing cytokines (e.g., IL-33, TSLP) and chemokines to activate neighboring MCs. Conversely, MC-derived proteases (tryptase, chymase) and mediators (histamine, leukotrienes) disrupt epithelial junctions (e.g., E-cadherin, occludin), exacerbating barrier dysfunction and perpetuating cycles of inflammation. This reciprocal interaction establishes a molecular hub for ‘hyperinflammation’ in asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and viral infections, while also contributing to pathological processes such as airway remodeling, fibrosis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Therefore, elucidating the synergistic mechanisms underlying AEC-MC crosstalk is critically important. This review synthesizes emerging insights into AEC-MC crosstalk, emphasizing context-specific mechanisms in viral, allergic, and chronic inflammatory settings, and provide suggestions for future research directions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03388-2.

Introduction

Airway inflammation is the central pathological process of various respiratory diseases, including pathogen infection, asthma, allergic rhinitis and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [1]. It is characterized by the infiltration of immune cells, increased mucus production, and airway hyperreactivity, ultimately leading to significant morbidity and impaired quality of life [2]. Understanding the cellular and molecular mechanisms driving airway inflammation is essential for developing effective therapeutic strategies to alleviate symptoms and prevent disease progression.

The inflammatory response within the airways involves the participations of multiple cell types. Among these, the AECs and MCs serve as the initiating and effector cells respectively [3]. AECs, a dynamic and multifaceted barrier, is composed of various specialized cell types including ciliated, club, goblet, basal, and suprabasal cells [4]. These cells collectively maintain airway homeostasis through a delicate balance of physical barriers and immune functions [5]. Disruption of the barrier, often precipitated by environmental insults or infectious agents, can lead to a cascade of inflammatory responses and tissue remodeling [6, 7]. MCs are found throughout the airway, characterized by their granules filled with histamine and other inflammatory mediators [8]. Their strategic positioning allows them to detect and respond to pathogens, allergens, and other irritants with rapid degranulation and mediator release [9]. This response significantly influences the integrity and function of epithelial barrier.

In reality, the intricate relationship between AECs and MCs represents a crucial axis in the regulation of respiratory immune responses and inflammation [10]. AECs serve as the first defense in the airway and are the initial triggers of inflammation, while MCs are important effector cells in airway inflammation [11, 12]. Their crosstalk shapes a vital aspect of the local immune response [13]. Thus, in this review, we will briefly delineate the role of AECs and MCs and then focus on the current understanding of the crosstalk between AECs and MCs in airway inflammation.

AECs

AECs structure and functions

The bronchial epithelium is a complex structure composed of five main cell types: ciliated, club, goblet, basal, and suprabasal cells [14, 15]. In addition, it contains rare cell types such as ionocytes, neuroendocrine cells, and tuft/brush cells [16, 17]. These cells are organized in a pseudo-stratified manner overlying a basement membrane, forming a critical barrier for the respiratory tract [18]. Ciliated cells line the airway walls and play a pivotal role in clearing material trapped in mucus from the cell surface through coordinated beating of their cilia [19]. Club cells are responsible for secreting substances required for airway protection and repair. These substances include protease inhibitors, proteins that stabilize surfactants, antimicrobial peptides, and components of the innate immune response [20]. Goblet cells secrete mucins and other glycoproteins that contribute to the viscosity and protective properties of mucus. This mucus traps pathogens and particulates, facilitating their removal from the respiratory tract [21, 22]. Basal cells serve as both quiescent stem cells and dividing progenitors, replenishing secretory and ciliated cells following damage [23]. They play a crucial role in maintaining the integrity and repair of the airway epithelium.

The physical barrier function of the epithelium depends on the integrity of its constituent cells and their adhesions, particularly tight and adherens junctions [24]. Tight junctions are composed of proteins such as claudins, occludin, and zonula occludens (ZO), which are critical for regulating their function [25, 26]. These junctions maintain the selective permeability of the epithelial barrier, preventing the paracellular passage of pathogens and allergens while allowing the passage of essential ions and molecules [27, 28]. Notably, the integrity of these junctions can be compromised by exposure to air pollutants such as dust, vehicular pollution, wildfire smoke, and cigarette smoke [29].

The epithelium also serves as an immune barrier, secreting pathogen defense proteins and transporting immunoglobulins (IgA and IgM) from the basolateral membrane to the apical surface [30]. These immunoglobulins bind and trap pathogens in mucus, facilitating interaction with immune cells [31]. Mucus contains antimicrobial peptides that neutralize pathogens trapped within it [32]. The mucus, along with its trapped pathogens, is then removed from the body through mucociliary clearance, protecting the underlying submucosal layer from air pollutants, allergens, and respiratory pathogens [21]. These pollutants induce mucociliary dysfunction, characterized by the loss of cilia and goblet cell metaplasia, resulting in increased mucus production [33]. Recognition of particulate matter by epithelial dendritic cells leads to the proliferation of adaptive immune cells and the release of inflammatory cytokines, further inducing epithelial damage and dysfunction [34].

Airway epithelial barrier disruption

Disruption of airway epithelial barrier can result from a variety of factors including viral or bacterial infections, exposure to allergens, and chronic inflammatory conditions such as COPD [35]. Pathogens can directly damage the airway epithelium or trigger host immune responses by the activation of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) [36]. Pathogens trigger the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), from infected epithelial cells and immune cells [37]. These cytokines promote inflammation and recruit immune cells to the site of infection, contributing to tissue damage and barrier disruption [38]. Activation of PRRs expressed on the surface of AECs, such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and NOD-like receptors (NLRs), triggers a cascade of intracellular signaling events, resulting in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and antimicrobial peptides [39]. These inflammatory mediators not only combat the pathogens but also contribute to the disruption of the epithelial barrier [40, 41] (Fig. 1).

The exposure to allergens such as pollen, dust mites, and pet dander activates innate immune cells, including dendritic cells and MCs, which release mediators that can damage epithelial cells [42]. In asthma, the expression of E-cadherin, an adherens junction protein, is often reduced in the airway epithelium, possibly due to EMT [43]. This process involves epithelial cells losing their polarity, adhesiveness, and anchorage to the basal membrane while acquiring mesenchymal features such as enhanced migratory abilities [44]. Loss of E-cadherin-mediated inhibition can activate dendritic cells, leading to allergic sensitization [45]. Epithelial damage also triggers the release of fibrogenic cytokines such as IL-1β, IFN-γ, TNF, IL-4, and IL-13, which decrease the expression of junctional proteins, creating a cycle of epithelial damage, airway remodeling, and inflammation [46]. Furthermore, epithelial repair mechanisms in asthma appear to be dysregulated, exacerbating impaired epithelial integrity [47, 48]. An increase in poorly differentiated basal cells, reduced proliferation of epithelial cells, and defects in wound healing contribute to airway remodeling and asthma pathogenesis [49, 50].

In COPD, long-term exposure to cigarette smoke, air pollutants, and other pathogenic factors leads to airway inflammation, causing damage and apoptosis of epithelial cells [51]. These damaged or apoptotic epithelial cells release various inflammatory mediators, such as cytokines, chemical mediators, and reactive oxygen species [52]. These inflammatory mediators activate and attract various immune cells, including neutrophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes [53]. These immune cells secrete more inflammatory mediators, establishing a positive feedback loop that sustains and exacerbates airway inflammation [54].

MCs

Origin and heterogeneity

MCs arise from multipotent hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) in the bone marrow [55]. Under the influence of specific growth factors and cytokines, such as stem cell factor (SCF), interleukin-3 (IL-3), and IL-9, HSCs commit to the MC lineage [56]. Committed HSCs give rise to MC progenitors [57]. MC progenitors leave the bone marrow and migrate through the bloodstream to peripheral tissues [58]. Once in the tissues, they localize at sites prone to contact with pathogens or allergens such as the airway [59], intestines, skin, and mucous membranes [58, 60]. In the tissues, MC progenitors undergo maturation and acquire characteristic granules filled with preformed mediators such as histamine and proteases [61]. They also express receptors for various immunological and inflammatory signals [3].

MCs can be categorized into connective tissue-type mast cells (CTMCs) and mucosal mast cells (MMCs) based on their distribution and function [60]. CTMCs are primarily found in the skin and connective tissues, containing large amounts of histamine and heparin, and are mainly involved in allergic reactions and local inflammatory responses [61, 62]. In contrast, MMCs are located in the mucosal layers of the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts, containing different types of proteases, and play a key role in allergic reactions in these areas as well as in defense against parasites [3].

Mast cells (MCs) are immune cells strategically distributed at environmental interfaces, often beneath epithelia and near blood vessels. This positioning enables them to act as key sentinels in immune surveillance. Upon activation, they release a diverse array of mediators, including cytokines (e.g., TNF, IL-1), lipid mediators, and chemokines, which collectively promote inflammation and recruit other immune cells to sites of challenge [9, 57, 63].

MCs functions

MCs express numerous surface receptors, including FcεRI and FcγRIIA (for immunoglobulin E), the KIT receptor (for stem cell factor), and various G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) such as adenosine receptors (A2A, A2B, A3), cannabinoid receptors (CB1, CB2), histamine receptors (H1R, H4R), the Mas-related G protein-coupled receptor X2 (MRGPRX2), and complement component C3a receptor (C3aR) [64]. These receptors enable MCs to be stimulated to specific actions, such as the release of intracellular mediators or total mast cell degranulation [3]. Additionally, cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) and coreceptors facilitate their binding to different cells, tissues, and surfaces [60] (Fig. 2).

MCs store a wide range of biologically active mediators with potential positive or negative effects on various target cells [61]. Upon activation, MCs can release mediators within minutes or produce and release new mediators several hours after activation [12]. These mediators include cytokines (e.g., IL-1, IL-3, IL-6, IL-18, IL-33, TNF-α, SCF, TGF-β), chemokines (e.g., MCP-1, RANTES, TARC), growth factors (e.g., VEGF, NGF, GM-CSF, M-CSF), proteases (e.g., tryptase, MMPs), proteoglycans (e.g., heparin), amines (e.g., histamine, serotonin), neuropeptides (e.g., CRH, VIP), and lipid derivatives (e.g., LTC4, LTD4, LTE4, PGD2, PAF) [65].

MCs in respiratory disease

Given the relationship between asthma exacerbation and viral infection, the response of MCs to respiratory viral infection is of interest. Early studies using bovine respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) models demonstrated that MCs undergo degranulation during infection [55]. By analyzing the urine of infants hospitalized for wheezing, Dr. Lage et al. found MC activation in wheezing infants infected with RSV, characterized by degranulation and release of lipid mediators [66], and suggested that virus-specific IgE may play an important role in some cases [67]. However, more direct studies of interactions between RSV and MCs reveal a somewhat different picture. In the absence of virus-specific IgE, complement activation, and other factors that may play a role in vivo, RSV has been found to have limited capacity to induce degranulation or leukotriene generation [68]. Additionally, RSV shows limited transcription of viral products in human primary MCs [69]. Nonetheless, contact between RSV and human MCs induces substantial production of chemokines and type I interferons (IFNs), indicating the important role of MCs in antiviral defense [68].

MCs also respond to influenza and parainfluenza viruses [70]. Studies have demonstrated that these viruses can induce mediator release from MCs in animal models, with MC hyperplasia and activation linked to more severe airway inflammation [70, 71]. For instance, in the Brown Norway rat model, which has a Th2 predisposition, influenza virus infection led to significant MC activity and airway inflammation [72]. In humans, MCs exposed to influenza A virus (IAV) showed limited viral replication but produced cytokines, chemokines, and type I IFN [73].

Rhinovirus (RV) infections are closely associated with asthma exacerbations, and MCs are significantly involved in this process [74]. MCs release mediators like histamine and leukotriene, along with various cytokines and chemokines, including CXCL8 and TNF, in response to RV infection [75]. Additionally, RV infection can induce apoptosis in MCs [76]. Importantly, pre-treatment with IFNβ has been shown to reduce RV infection in MCs, suggesting a protective mechanism that could be impaired in individuals with asthma, who often have deficient IFNβ responses [77].

In the context of SARS-CoV-2 infection, recent findings have highlighted the critical role of MC degranulation in driving hyper-inflammation and subsequent tissue damage. SARS-CoV-2 infection can trigger rapid MC degranulation, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory factors that exacerbate alveolar epithelial inflammation and contribute to acute lung injury, a key factor in COVID-19 severity [78]. This was demonstrated in ACE2 humanized mice and rhesus macaques, where MC degranulation was induced by Spike-RBD binding to ACE2 receptors. The resulting MC degranulation altered various signaling pathways in alveolar epithelial cells, disrupting tight junctions and promoting inflammation. However, the use of clinical MC stabilizers such as ebastine or loratadine were shown to effectively block degranulation, dampen the production of pro-inflammatory factors, and prevent lung injury. These findings suggest that MC stabilizers could be repurposed as immunomodulators in COVID-19 treatment to mitigate lung inflammation and injury by inhibiting MC degranulation and the resultant cytokine storm [78].

MCs exhibit a dual role in respiratory viral infections. They contribute to early antiviral defenses by producing type I IFNs and recruiting effector immune cells [79]. However, their activation can also lead to exacerbated inflammatory responses, especially in conditions like asthma, where they may contribute to severe airway inflammation [80]. This duality underscores the importance of MCs in both protective immunity and potential pathology during viral infections (Table 1).

Crosstalk between AECs and MCs

The effects of AECs on MCs

In asthmatic patients, an increased number of MCs is observed on the airway mucosal surface [81]. Given that human airway epithelial cells (HAECs) actively participate in airway inflammatory responses and are in direct contact with MCs in the mucosa, research has found that HAECs express both mRNA and protein for soluble and membrane-bound stem cell factors and are capable of secreting soluble stem cell factors [82].

AECs produce key cytokines like IL-33, IL-25, and TSLP, which activate and recruit MCs. For instance, IL-33 is released by AECs in response to stress or injury caused by allergens or viral exposure [83]. Upon release, IL-33 binds to the ST2 receptor on MCs, initiating their activation cascade and leading to degranulation and the release of mediators such as histamine, tryptase, and various cytokines, which contribute to the inflammatory response typical of allergic reactions [84]. IL-33 not only directly promotes mast cell activation and airway smooth muscle (ASM) wound repair but also indirectly enhances the contractile effect of ASM by up-regulating IL-13 secretion from MCs [85], suggesting IL-33 as a potential target for modulating MC and ASM interactions in asthma. In respiratory infections, pathogens capable of producing dsRNA may amplify local Th2 inflammation in asthmatic patients through the production of TSLP and IL-1 by epithelial cells, which subsequently activates Th2 cytokine production by MCs in the airways [86]. Recent studies indicate that type 2 inflammation and indirect airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) in asthma are associated with mast cell migration to the epithelium and IL-33 signaling [87]. Furthermore, IL-25 enhances the production of Th2 cytokines by MCs and other immune cells, promoting eosinophil recruitment and mucus production [87]. TSLP primes MCs for increased responsiveness, supports their survival and proliferation, and acts on dendritic cells to promote a Th2 immune response [88].

In addition to cytokines, AECs produce chemokines such as CCL2, CCL5, and CXCL8 that attract MCs to sites of inflammation. CCL2 recruits MCs by interacting with CCR2 receptors, while CCL5 attracts MCs, T cells, and eosinophils, sustaining chronic inflammation. CXCL8 is a potent chemoattractant for neutrophils and MCs, aiding in the early phase of inflammatory responses [89]. AECs also release lipid mediators like prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) and leukotrienes, which amplify inflammation. PGD2 acts on MCs through DP1 and CRTH2 receptors, promoting their recruitment and activation, while leukotrienes contribute to bronchoconstriction and increased mucus production [90].

The interaction between AECs and MCs during respiratory viral infections is intricate and multifaceted. In a study by Ryan C et al., co-culturing rhinovirus (RV)-infected AECs with MCs revealed that AECs mount a strong antiviral response following infection. This response leads to significant alterations in MC gene expression, particularly in genes related to antiviral activity, leukocyte activation, and type 2 immune pathways. Additionally, rhinovirus-infected AECs enhance MC activity by releasing epithelial-derived interferons, which amplify the immune response of MCs and contribute to the progression of inflammation [91].

The replication efficiency of RSV in HMC-1 MCs is lower compared to human epithelial A549 cells. HMC-1 MCs only exhibit degranulation, along with increased TNFαsecretion, when co-cultured with RSV-infected A549 cells. Neither direct exposure of HMC-1 cells to RSV nor incubation with the supernatant from RSV-infected A549 cells induces degranulation, suggesting that the presence of infected epithelial cells is critical for mast cell activation during RSV infection, while direct infection of MCs is insufficient. Moreover, primary AECs can suppress histamine release from human lung mast cells (HLMCs) by releasing a soluble, unstable lipid mediator that acts through G0/Gi-coupled receptors [92].

Therefore, AECs can both promote and inhibit mast cell degranulation, reflecting the complex regulatory mechanisms by which AECs modulate mast cell activity. This dual role underscores the intricacy of their interaction and suggests potential therapeutic avenues for asthma management by targeting these regulatory pathways.

The effects of MCs on AECs

MCs have a profound impact on AECs through various mechanisms, influencing both acute and chronic inflammatory responses [12]. One of the primary effects of MCs is the disruption of the airway epithelial barrier. MCs release a range of mediators, including histamine, proteases, and cytokines, upon activation [93]. Research has demonstrated that mast cell chymase degrades junctional proteins in epithelial cells, leading to the disruption of intercellular connections. This degradation not only diminishes cell adhesion and viability by reducing the expression of intracellular structural proteins like cytokeratins and focal adhesion kinase (FAK), but also significantly impacts the overall integrity and function of epithelial tissues. As a result, this disruption facilitates the entry of allergens, pollutants, and pathogens into the underlying tissue, which exacerbates inflammation and contributes to symptoms such as bronchoconstriction, mucus hypersecretion, and persistent cough [94]. Additionally, mast cell-derived vesicles have been shown to mediate the induction of EMT. EMT is a process where epithelial cells acquire mesenchymal traits, which contributes to airway remodeling. This transition is triggered by a cascade of protein phosphorylation events initiated by mast cell activation, leading to increased fibrosis and structural alterations in the airways that are characteristic of chronic inflammatory diseases like asthma [95].

Beyond barrier disruption and EMT, MCs also play a critical role in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and epithelial injury. The serine protease chymase, released by mast cells, plays a significant role in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and is particularly notable for inducing bronchial epithelial injury [96]. By degrading ECM components and altering the balance between ECM deposition and degradation, chymase-driven remodeling becomes a crucial pathological feature in inflammatory lung diseases, contributing to airway fibrosis and structural changes [97]. By co-incubating the bronchial ring of human lung tissue with the 50 mU/ml of mast cell chymase, Dr. Zhou et al. found that chymase not only disrupted epithelial cell connections, but also inhibited wound healing and impaired epithelial barrier function, thereby affecting airway wall function and ECM remodeling [94]. Beyond their shared role in remodeling, mast cell tryptase contributes uniquely to asthma and COPD pathophysiology by promoting bronchoconstriction and airway hyperreactivity. Tryptase acts as a potent agonist for protease-activated receptor-2 (PAR-2) on airway smooth muscle cells and sensory nerves, leading to smooth muscle contraction and neuronal activation that heighten airway responsiveness [98]. Furthermore, by stimulating the release of endothelin-1 [99] and potentiating the effects of histamine [100], tryptase amplifies bronchoconstrictive responses. These mechanisms collectively contribute to the persistent airflow obstruction and symptomatic exacerbations characteristic of asthma and COPD [101].

Conclusion and perspectives

The interaction between AECs and MCs serves as a central hub in the pathogenesis of airway inflammation. Together, they maintain airway homeostasis, and their dysregulated interplay plays a pivotal role in initiating and sustaining inflammatory responses in various respiratory diseases. AECs activate neighboring MCs and promote their degranulation by releasing epithelial-derived cytokines (e.g., IL-33, TSLP). Conversely, proteases (e.g., tryptase, chymase) and inflammatory mediators (e.g., histamine, leukotrienes) released by MCs disrupt epithelial junctions (e.g., E-cadherin, occludin), exacerbating barrier dysfunction and driving a vicious cycle of inflammation (Fig. 3). This constitutes the molecular core of “hyperinflammation” in conditions such as asthma, COPD, and viral infections, while also contributing to pathological processes like airway remodeling, fibrosis, and EMT.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of the experimental models used in the studies discussed herein. Many mechanistic insights into AEC-MC interactions derive from in vitro models, such as the A549 alveolar epithelial cell line and the HMC-1 mast cell line. While invaluable tools, these cell lines have inherent limitations. The A549 line is carcinoma-derived and may not fully replicate the complex biology and differential responses of primary human airway epithelial cells. Similarly, the HMC-1 line represents an immature, leukemic mast cell phenotype with low FcεRI expression, which limits its relevance for IgE-mediated responses and may not accurately mirror the behavior of mature, tissue-resident mast cell subtypes. Therefore, findings from these models should be interpreted with caution, and future research prioritizing primary cells or more advanced, co-culture systems will be crucial to validate and translate these mechanistic insights.

Future research must urgently address several critical areas. One key focus is to elucidate the receptor selectivity (e.g., ST2, CD48) and dynamic regulatory mechanisms when MCs are activated by alarmins (e.g., IL-33, TSLP) released from AECs. Another area involves deciphering the stimulus-specific disparities in downstream MC signaling pathways (e.g., MyD88/NF-κB, MAPK) and regulatory networks between viral triggers and classical allergen-driven activation. Additionally, it is crucial to evaluate the phenotypic remodeling of MCs (degranulation patterns, mediator profiles, and cellular lifespan) induced by the synergistic actions of multiple AEC-derived alarmins.

To effectively bridge these knowledge gaps, the field must embrace more sophisticated experimental models that accurately recapitulate the human airway microenvironment. Beyond traditional cultures, future work should leverage advanced systems such as airway organoids derived from primary human cells, which self-organize into structures containing multiple epithelial cell types and can be co-cultured with mast cells to study complex interactions. Furthermore, microfluidic “airway-on-chip” devices that introduce dynamic fluid flow, mechanical stretch, and an air-liquid interface offer an unprecedented opportunity to model the MC-AEC axis under physiologically relevant conditions. These advanced models will be indispensable for precisely elucidating the molecular dialogue in both health and disease and for screening potential therapeutic interventions.

Clarifying these core questions will lay the theoretical foundation for developing dual-functional inhibitors with both antiviral and antiallergic effects. Deepening the understanding of AEC-MC interactions, particularly by filling these critical research gaps, will not only uncover novel mechanisms of disease progression but also identify disease-specific biomarkers and inspire precision intervention strategies targeting key nodes in the vicious cycle of airway inflammation. Ultimately, this will improve the clinical management of chronic respiratory diseases.
