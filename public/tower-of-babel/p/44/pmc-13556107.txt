Integrated physiological, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analysis of exogenous trehalose regulating cold tolerance in grape callus
Authors: Ling Wang, Jianbo Wang, Ziyi Zhu, Ruilong Li, Jingyuan Wang, Jie Chang, Ruijin Zhou, Guirong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVitis vinifera is a fruit tree species of great economic value worldwide. Nevertheless, chilling injury induced by cold stress restricts its yield and hinders the development of the grape industry. Based on previous findings of our research group that exogenous trehalose enhances abiotic stress tolerance in grape callus, this study adopted physiological, transcriptomic and metabolomic approaches to explore the potential regulatory relationships associated with exogenous trehalose.ResultsIn this study, trehalose treatment alleviated chilling injury under 12 °C cold stress and increased the fresh weight of Vitis vinifera ‘Thompson Seedless’ callus. Meanwhile, trehalose application significantly elevated endogenous trehalose and soluble sugar content in grape callus, accompanied by increased activities of POD, SOD, and CAT, as well as reduced levels of MDA and O₂•⁻. KEGG enrichment analysis of transcriptomic data revealed that DEGs were mainly enriched in pathways related to biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, plant hormone signal transduction, starch and sucrose metabolism, and the MAPK signaling pathway in plants. Key DEGs included BAMS, STS, IAA, RLKs, and BGLU, while differentially expressed transcription factors were predominantly distributed in the AP2/ERF-ERF and MYB families. WGCNA identified the brown and turquoise modules as modules putatively correlated with physiological traits in grape callus, from which candidate genes potentially involved in trehalose-mediated cold response, including VQ22, RGLG2, PPR21, CML16, and SPL6, were screened. Combined transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis showed that DEGs and DAMs were jointly notably altered in the metabolic pathways of flavonoids, benzenes and their substituted derivatives, alkanolamines, terpenoids, and alkaloids. Moreover, all DAM-interacting genes identified were DEGs from the transcriptome. RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that the expression patterns of the selected cold-responsive genes were reliable and consistent with the transcriptomic results.ConclusionsUsing integrated physiological, transcriptomic and metabolomic approaches, we identified candidate modules and genes related to cold tolerance in grape callus, as well as physiological responses and potential pathways underlying trehalose-mediated cold tolerance. We also characterized candidate genes involved in this regulatory process, providing a theoretical reference for exploring cold tolerance mechanisms in grape callus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09523-5.

Introduction

Grape (Vitis vinifera L.) is one of the most economically important fruit crops worldwide [1]. However, its growth is constrained by cold stress [2]. Cold limits grape growth and development, affects fruit yield and quality, and exerts substantial negative impacts on the grape industry [3]. Cold stress damages grape plants at multiple levels. It disrupts cell membrane structure and increases membrane permeability, reduces photosynthetic efficiency [4], and induces excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), further disrupting intracellular oxidative homeostasis [5]. To mitigate the adverse effects of cold stress, the use of exogenous substances to regulate plant stress resistance has become a major research focus in recent years [6]. Numerous studies have reported that applying exogenous substances can enhance plant cold tolerance [7, 8]. Therefore, identifying efficient and safe exogenous regulators and elucidating the mechanisms underlying cold resistance are of great theoretical and practical importance for improving plant cold tolerance.

Trehalose (α-D-glucopyranosyl-1,1-α-D-glucopyranoside) is a safe and reliable natural sugar. Under normal conditions, it occurs at trace levels in plants, typically 100–1,000 times lower than sucrose [9]. However, under stress conditions, plants rapidly synthesize and accumulate trehalose, which plays an important role in stress responses [10]. In higher plants, trehalose biosynthesis involves two enzymatic reactions. First, trehalose-6-phosphate synthase (TPS) catalyzes the formation of trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P). Subsequently, trehalose-6-phosphate phosphatase (TPP) dephosphorylates T6P to produce trehalose [11]. Trehalose and genes involved in its biosynthesis modulate plant responses to cold stress. For instance, in Arabidopsis thaliana, AtTPS1 and AtTPP1 enhance plant cold tolerance by regulating the accumulation and synthesis of soluble sugars [12, 13]. In rice, OsTPS1, class II OsTPS, and OsTPP1 positively regulate cold tolerance. Moreover, class II OsTPSs form a functional complex with OsTPS1, and OsTPP1 is directly targeted and activated by OsICE1 [14–17]. In wheat, overexpression of TaTPS11 improves cold tolerance by modulating carbohydrate metabolism [18]. In peanut, four AhTPS and four AhTPP are induced by cold stress. In Arabidopsis, overexpression of AhTPS9 enhances cold tolerance by protecting the photosynthetic apparatus and regulating sugar metabolites and related genes [19]. In grape, VvTPPA is induced in leaves under cold conditions, and both trehalose and T6P show marked accumulation after cold storage [20]. Additionally, VvTPS10 interacts with VvSnRK1, modulates sucrose metabolism, and positively regulates trehalose biosynthesis and cold tolerance [21]. In summary, efforts to improve plant cold tolerance using endogenous trehalose have mainly relied on transgenic approaches. However, transgenic systems for most plant species remain immature and time-consuming. Therefore, exogenous trehalose application is commonly used as an alternative strategy to enhance plant cold resistance.

Exogenous application of trehalose can up-regulate plant stress-responsive genes, promote the accumulation of various osmotic regulators, and enhance plant tolerance to cold stress [22]. For example, exogenous trehalose treatment enhances cold tolerance in pepper fruits by improving the antioxidant enzyme system and protecting cell membrane integrity [23]. In tomato, melon, and peach, trehalose alleviates the detrimental effects of cold stress by inducing the production of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and nitric oxide (NO), reducing membrane lipid peroxidation and cellular damage, increasing endogenous Abscisic acid (ABA) content, and improving photosynthesis [24–27]. Under cold conditions, exogenous trehalose improves cold tolerance in guava by regulating soluble sugar content and related enzyme activities [28]. It alleviates cold damage in Madagascar periwinkle by increasing chlorophyll content and antioxidant enzyme activities, as well as positively regulating alkaloid metabolism [29]. In rapeseed, trehalose enhances cold tolerance by upregulating genes associated with antioxidant defense, cold response, and trehalose biosynthesis [30]. Similar effects have been observed in grape, where exogenous trehalose enhances cold tolerance by improving antioxidant enzyme activities, endogenous hormone levels, and soluble sugar content [31]. Nevertheless, studies on the regulatory effects of exogenous trehalose on grape cold tolerance remain limited.

Cold stress can be classified as chilling (0–15 °C) and freezing (< 0 °C) stresses [32]. Our previous study found that exogenous trehalose treatment enhanced the tolerance of grape plants to abiotic stresses, including freezing stress (< 0 °C) [33]. However, the role of trehalose in grape defense against prolonged chilling stress (0–15 °C) and the underlying molecular mechanism remain unclear. In this study, we investigated trehalose function by analyzing the phenotypic and physiological indices of grape callus under trehalose and cold (12 °C) treatments. For clarity and ease of description, “cold stress” in this study refers specifically to chilling stress at 12 °C, unless otherwise noted. Concurrently, we integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses to screen candidate differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and related metabolic pathways associated with trehalose-mediated cold response under cold stress. Furthermore, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted to construct co-expression modules of cold-responsive genes in grape and explore potential regulators involved in trehalose-induced cold stress tolerance. This study may provide preliminary insights into potential molecular strategies for improving stress tolerance in grape and the regulation of environmental stress tolerance by exogenous substances, using grape callus as experimental material.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

The experiment was conducted at the Comprehensive Experimental Platform of the College of Horticulture and Landscape Architecture, Henan Institute of Science and Technology. Callus derived from the European grape cultivar ‘Thompson Seedless’ were cultured on MS medium (4.4 g/L MS, 30 g/L sucrose, 7 g/L agar; pH 5.8, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) under a 16-h photoperiod at 25°C. To investigate the role of exogenous trehalose in the long-term cold stress response of grape, callus tissues with consistent subculture passage, uniform age, similar growth status, and comparable weight and size were selected. Each biological replicate consisted of four callus pieces (approximately 0.3 g each, 5 mm in diameter), with all three replicates derived from the same maternal callus line (subcultured every 30 d) and then randomly divided into four groups: control (CK), cold stress (Cold), trehalose treatment (Tre), and cold stress plus trehalose (Cold_Tre). Trehalose (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) was added to the medium at a final concentration of 20 mM. The Cold and Cold_Tre groups were pre-cultured at 25°C for 10 d and then subjected to cold treatment at 12°C. During the treatment period, phenotypic changes were observed every 2 d, and the treatment cycle lasted for 14 d; the CK and Tre groups were maintained at 25°C for 24 d. After treatment, the phenotypes of the callus in the four groups were observed simultaneously, and samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80°C (Ningbo Pulangte Instrument Co., Ltd.) for subsequent measurement of physiological and biochemical indices, RNA extraction, and transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses.

Determination of physiological and biochemical indices

To assess the physiological effects of trehalose under cold stress, physiological and biochemical indices were measured. For fresh weight measurement, sterile filter paper was used to remove residual medium and surface moisture from the callus, followed by rapid weighing and recording. NBT and DAB staining were performed according to the method described by Yue et al. [34]. The activities of peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) were assayed using the guaiacol colorimetric method, nitroblue tetrazolium method, and potassium nitrite method, respectively, as reported by Ren et al. [35]. Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined using the thiobarbituric acid method, as described by Du et al. [36]. The contents of superoxide anion radical (O₂•⁻), trehalose, and soluble sugars were measured using commercial assay kits (Suzhou Grace Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Each treatment group included three biological replicates.

RNA sequencing and transcriptomic data processing

The above samples were submitted to Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for total RNA extraction, cDNA library construction, and high-throughput sequencing. First, total RNA was extracted, and cDNA was synthesized via reverse transcription. After purification and fragment size selection, PCR amplification was performed to generate the final sequencing library. The library was quality-controlled and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq platform with 150-bp paired-end reads. Each sample yielded ≥ 7 Gb of clean data, with a Q30 ratio ≥ 93%. Raw reads were filtered using fastp to remove adapter sequences, reads with > 10% ambiguous bases (N), and reads with > 50% low-quality bases (Q ≤ 20). Clean reads were aligned to the Vitis vinifera reference genome (ASM3070453v1, Ensembl Plants release-61) using HISAT2. Gene-level raw counts were quantified with featureCounts, and the resulting count matrix was used for differential expression analysis via DESeq2.

To evaluate data reproducibility, principal component analysis (PCA) and correlation analysis were performed. Raw read counts were used for differential expression analysis with DESeq2, and gene expression was normalized via the median-of-ratios method. FPKM values were used solely for visualization. DEGs were identified using DESeq2, with thresholds of |log₂(fold change)| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05. GO enrichment analysis was conducted by hypergeometric test. KEGG pathway enrichment was performed with FDR < 0.05 to explore trehalose-mediated cold-responsive potential regulatory relationship.

Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) and module identification

A weighted gene co-expression network was constructed using R, and gene co-expression modules were identified. Prior to WGCNA, the input FPKM expression files were filtered using the varFilter function from the genefilter package in R to remove genes with low expression levels and stable/unaltered expression across samples, thereby improving network construction accuracy. The pickSoftThreshold function was used to determine the soft threshold (β), with a scale-free network fitting index (R2 > 0.85) set as the criterion. A clustering tree was constructed from gene expression correlations to complete module partitioning, and the dynamic tree-cutting method was used to merge similar modules within the hierarchical clustering tree. Module–trait relationships were evaluated by analyzing the correlations between modules and samples, and the results were visualized via a correlation heatmap. It should be noted that the WGCNA analysis in this study was based on a small sample size (n = 12), which may affect network stability. However, the modules showed good biological consistency, and the results should be interpreted as candidate associations.

Identification of cold-responsive candidate modules and genes based on WGCNA

The gene co-expression network modules constructed above were integrated with physiological indices, including MDA, SOD, POD, CAT, trehalose, and soluble sugars, to analyze correlations between the modules and these indices. To identify candidate genes highly correlated with cold stress-responsive modules, modules were considered significantly correlated if |r| > 0.8 and P < 0.005. Modules positively correlated with cold stress were selected as target modules for subsequent analysis. Based on the weight values (weight > 0.3) in the co-expression network, the top 30 genes with highest connectivity were identified using Cytoscape software. Gene co-expression network relationships were visualized using the Metware Cloud Platform (https://www.metware.cn).

Metabolomic analysis

Untargeted metabolomic profiling was performed using a Shimadzu LC-30 A UPLC system coupled with a SCIEX TripleTOF 6600 + Q-TOF-MS/MS with ESI source operating in positive and negative modes. Chromatographic separation was conducted on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) at 40 °C, with a constant flow rate of 0.40 mL/min and an injection volume of 4 µL. Mobile phases were water-0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile-0.1% formic acid (B), with gradient elution: 0 min 5% B, 5 min 65% B, 6–7.5 min 99% B, 7.6–10 min 5% B. MS conditions: ion source temperature 550 °C, spray voltage 5000 V (ESI+)/−4000 V (ESI−), mass range 50-1250 Da.

For sample preparation, 30 mg lyophilized grape callus powder was extracted with 1500 µL pre-cooled 70% methanol, vortexed 30 s every 30 min for six cycles, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 3 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane. QC samples were pooled from all extracts and injected every 10 samples for stability monitoring.

Raw MS data were converted via ProteoWizard, and peak detection and alignment were performed using XCMS. Missing values were imputed by KNN combined with 1/5 minimum substitution, and peak areas were corrected by SVR. Metabolites were annotated following MSI Level 2 criteria using in-house, public and predictive databases together with metDNA, with mass accuracy thresholds of < 5 ppm for precursors and < 50 mDa for fragments. Only metabolites with identification score > 0.5 and QC CV < 0.5 were retained. Data were normalized by log₂-transformation, zero-centering and UV scaling. OPLS-DA was conducted using MetaboAnalystR. For DAMs, two-group comparisons were performed using fold change analysis combined with OPLS-DA, where differentially accumulated metabolites were defined as those with VIP > 1 and |log₂FC| ≥ 1 (equivalent to fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5); no statistical P-value or FDR correction was applied to the two-group DAM lists. For multi-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA was used, and metabolites with VIP > 1 and unadjusted P < 0.05 were considered as differentially accumulated; no additional multiple testing correction was applied to the multi-group DAM lists. The number of metabolites passing each filter for each comparison is provided in Supplementary Table S1. KEGG pathway P-values were adjusted by the Benjamini-Hochberg method (FDR < 0.05).

Integrated analysis of metabolomic and transcriptomic data

Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were conducted on the same biological replicates. Based on KEGG annotation and enrichment analyses of differential metabolites and DEGs, KEGG pathways jointly enriched in both omics datasets were identified, and KEGG enrichment bubble plots were generated. Correlation analysis was performed using the quantitative profiles of genes and metabolites across all samples. Correlations with an absolute correlation coefficient > 0.8 and FDR < 0.05 were selected to construct a nine-quadrant correlation diagram and a correlation network diagram, respectively.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (RT-qPCR)

To verify the reliability of the transcriptomic data, nine candidate genes were selected for validation using quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from grape callus RNA using the M5 Super plus qPCR RT Kit with gDNA Remover (Mei5 Biotechnology, China). RT-qPCR amplification was performed using SYBR qPCR Mixture (GenStar, China) on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). Each reaction included three biological replicates. Based on previous studies, stably expressed VvActin (AY680701) was used as the internal reference gene [33], and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The specific primers used for RT-qPCR are listed in Table S2.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t test for selected pairwise comparisons in IBM SPSS Statistics 32.0 software. Asterisks above the bars indicate significant differences between the indicated groups (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001). Data visualization and plotting were performed using GraphPad Prism 11.0.1 and Adobe Illustrator 2025.

Results

Effects of exogenous trehalose on physiological characteristics of grape under cold stress

To investigate the role of trehalose in the long-term cold tolerance of grape, grape callus was subjected to cold treatment at 12 °C for 14 d. Phenotypic analysis showed no significant differences in callus growth status or size between the CK and Tre groups. Under cold stress, the Cold_Tre group exhibited better growth, larger callus volume, and higher fresh weight than the Cold group, which displayed obvious browning (Fig. 1a, b). MDA content was significantly lower in the Cold_Tre group than in the Cold group (Fig. 1c). Endogenous trehalose and soluble sugar content in the Tre and Cold_Tre groups were significantly higher than those in the CK and Cold groups, respectively (Fig. 1d, e). To assess the effect of trehalose on ROS accumulation under cold conditions, qualitative analysis of O₂•⁻ and H₂O₂ in callus was performed using NBT and DAB staining (Fig. 1f). No obvious differences in staining intensity were observed between the CK and Tre groups. However, callus was significantly darker in the Cold group than in the Cold_Tre group. Quantitative analysis confirmed that O₂•⁻ content was significantly lower in the Cold_Tre group than in the Cold group (Fig. 1g). Additionally, POD, SOD, and CAT activities were significantly higher in the Cold_Tre group than in the Cold group (Fig. 1h–j). These findings indicate that trehalose supplementation enhanced ROS-scavenging capacity in callus by significantly upregulating antioxidant enzyme activities and reducing O₂•⁻ content, thereby improving grape tolerance to cold stress.

Quality evaluation of RNA-Seq data

We constructed 12 high-quality gene libraries following transcriptome sequencing of callus from the CK, Tre, Cold, and Cold_Tre groups. Next, the raw reads were assembled and aligned to the reference genome. The genome mapping ratio ranged from 94.79% to 95.14%, with an average of 94.98%. The average GC content of the 12 samples was 47.66%, and the Q30 value was 94.35% (Table S3). Correlation analysis and PCA revealed that the correlation coefficient within each group exceeded 0.9, and that samples from the same treatment clustered together (Fig. 2a, b). These results demonstrate that this study generated high-quality transcriptomic data, providing a solid foundation for further transcript-level analysis.

Screening and analysis of DEGs

We identified a total of 38,920 effectively expressed genes through transcriptome sequencing, among which 11,296 genes were classified as DEGs (Table S4, Fig. S1, and Fig. 2c). Comparative analysis of DEGs among different groups showed that the total number of DEGs in Cold_Tre_vs_Cold was 2,070, including 759 up-regulated and 1,311 down-regulated genes. The total number of DEGs in Cold_Tre_vs_Tre was 4,422, with 1,893 up-regulated and 2,529 down-regulated genes. The total number of DEGs in Cold_vs_CK was 4,250, including 1,777 up-regulated and 2,473 down-regulated genes. The total number of DEGs in Tre_vs_CK was 554, including 142 up-regulated and 412 down-regulated genes. We performed Venn diagram analysis to examine the relationships among DEGs under different treatments. The results showed 985 common DEGs between Cold_Tre_vs_Cold and Cold_Tre_vs_Tre, 1,183 between Cold_Tre_vs_Cold and Cold_vs_CK, and 132 between Cold_Tre_vs_Cold and Tre_vs_CK. Additionally, 2,470 common DEGs were identified between Cold_Tre_vs_Tre and Cold_vs_CK, 303 between Cold_Tre_vs_Tre and Tre_vs_CK, and 422 between Cold_vs_CK and Tre_vs_CK. A total of 55 genes were differentially expressed across all four comparison groups (Fig. 2d).

GO analysis

GO enrichment analysis of DEGs regulated by trehalose in response to cold stress in grape showed that DEGs from the four comparison groups were classified into three categories: cellular component, molecular function, and biological process. DEGs under different treatments were mainly associated with terms such as cellular anatomical entity within the cellular component category. DEGs were primarily enriched for binding and catalytic activity within the molecular function category and were significantly enriched for cellular process and metabolic process within the biological process category (Fig. S2). GO enrichment bubble plots further illustrated the top 15 pathways with the smallest p-values across these three categories (Fig. 3a). DEGs in Cold_Tre_vs_Cold were mainly enriched for hydrolase activity, secondary metabolite biosynthetic process, and phenylpropanoid metabolic process. DEGs in Cold_Tre_vs_Tre were mainly enriched for RNA modification, hydrolase activity, oxidoreductase activity, and extracellular space. DEGs in Cold_vs_CK were mainly enriched for hydrolase activity, chemical homeostasis, phenylpropanoid metabolic process, and external encapsulating structure. DEGs in Tre_vs_CK were mainly enriched for external encapsulating structure, response to oxidative stress, secondary metabolite biosynthetic process, and antioxidant activity.

KEGG metabolic pathway analysis

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the DEGs to identify and compare pathways that showed notable differences between the comparison groups. The top 20 significantly enriched metabolic pathways were identified based on p-values (Fig. 3b and Fig. S3). DEGs in Cold_Tre_vs_Cold were mainly enriched in pathways related to plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK signaling pathway-plant, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and starch and sucrose metabolism. DEGs in Cold_Tre_vs_Tre were primarily enriched in pathways related to plant hormone signal transduction, metabolic pathways, and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. DEGs in Cold_vs_CK were predominantly enriched in pathways related to plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK signaling pathway-plant, metabolic pathways, and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. DEGs in Tre_vs_CK were predominantly enriched in pathways related to plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK signaling pathway-plant, metabolic pathways, and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. Because this study focused on elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which trehalose regulates the response of grape to cold stress, only DEGs from Cold_vs_CK and Cold_Tre_vs_Cold were selected for subsequent metabolic pathway analysis.

Analysis of DEGs involved in biosynthesis of secondary metabolites

Secondary metabolites are induced in plants under abiotic stress. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs mediating the regulation of grape responses to cold stress by trehalose were mainly concentrated in the following secondary metabolic pathways: phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites, and stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid, and gingerol biosynthesis (Fig. 4). Venn diagram analysis showed that 74 DEGs were shared among these three pathways, including 2 commonly up-regulated genes (Vitis18g00195, CADH6; Vitis09g01602, BAMS1) and 4 commonly down-regulated genes (Vitis14g01875, PER55; Vitis18g00194, PER64; Vitis06g00184, BGL12; Vitis07g00359, BGL11) (Fig. 4a).

A total of 36 DEGs were common across comparisons for phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, mainly including 11 genes encoding phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), 9 peroxidase (POX) genes, and 3 4-coumarate–CoA ligase (4CL) genes (Fig. 4b and Table S5). Among these, 18 genes were significantly up-regulated in Cold_vs_CK but significantly down-regulated in Cold_Tre_vs_Cold, whereas 3 genes exhibited the opposite expression pattern. In both comparisons, Vitis18g00195 (CADH6) was significantly up-regulated, while Vitis14g01875 (PER55) and Vitis18g00194 (PER64) were significantly down-regulated. A total of 23 DEGs were common across comparisons for stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid, and gingerol biosynthesis, mainly including 19 stilbene synthase (STS) genes. All DEGs in this pathway were significantly up-regulated in Cold_vs_CK but significantly down-regulated in Cold_Tre_vs_Cold (Fig. 4c and Table S6). A total of 15 DEGs were common across comparisons for biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites, including 5 beta-glucosidase (BGLU) genes, 2 UDP-glycosyltransferase (UGT) genes, 2 beta-amyrin synthase (BAMS) genes, and 2 cytochrome P450 82 (CYP82) genes (Fig. 4d and Table S7). Among these, six genes were significantly up-regulated in Cold_vs_CK but significantly down-regulated in Cold_Tre_vs_Cold, whereas two genes showed the opposite expression trend. In both comparisons, Vitis09g01602 (BAMS1) was significantly up-regulated, while Vitis06g00184 (BGL12) and Vitis07g00359 (BGL11) were significantly down-regulated.

Analysis of DEGs involved in plant hormone signal transduction

KEGG analysis indicated that trehalose may modulate grape responses to cold stress by affecting DEGs associated with the plant hormone signal transduction pathway. Venn diagram analysis revealed 64 DEGs common to both Cold_vs_CK and Cold_Tre_vs_Cold, mainly including 8 auxin-related genes encoding Auxin-responsive protein (IAA) and Small Auxin-Up RNA (SAUR), 7 probable LRR-RLKs (RLKs), 6 probable carboxylesterases (CXEs), and 3 Retrotransposon-associated Pols (POLs) (Fig. 5a, b and Table S8). Among these, Vitis02g00899 (BSK2) and Vitis11g00590 (IAA) were significantly up-regulated in both Cold_vs_CK and Cold_Tre_vs_Cold, whereas Vitis17g00050 (bHLH), Vitis18g01895 (TAR3), and Vitis07g01123 (RLKs) were significantly down-regulated. Moreover, the other three auxin IAA family genes (Vitis05g00739, Vitis09g00401, and Vitis07g00604) were significantly down-regulated in Cold_vs_CK but significantly up-regulated in Cold_Tre_vs_Cold. Notably, all CXE and POX genes were significantly up-regulated in Cold_vs_CK but significantly down-regulated in Cold_Tre_vs_Cold.

Analysis of DEGs involved in starch and sucrose metabolism

To clarify the effect of trehalose on sugar metabolism in grape under cold stress conditions, we analyzed DEGs involved in starch and sucrose metabolism. The results showed that 26 DEGs were commonly regulated between Cold_vs_CK and Cold_Tre_vs_Cold, mainly including 8 glucan endo-1,3-beta-glucosidases (E13G), 5 BGLU, 4 endo-1,4-beta-glucanases (E14G), and 1 trehalose-phosphate phosphatase A (TPPA) (Fig. 6a, b and Table S9). Among these, Vitis16g00880 (TPPA) was significantly up-regulated in Cold_vs_CK but significantly down-regulated in Cold_Tre_vs_Cold. Vitis07g00359 (BGLU), Vitis06g00184 (BGLU), Vitis08g01734 (E13G), Vitis02g00046 (E14G), and Vitis17g01475 (SUS6) were significantly down-regulated in both Cold_vs_CK and Cold_Tre_vs_Cold, whereas Vitis07g00403 (E14G) was significantly up-regulated in both comparisons. Within the E13G gene family, except for Vitis08g01734, most genes were significantly down-regulated in Cold_vs_CK but significantly up-regulated in Cold_Tre_vs_Cold.

Analysis of DEGs involved in the plant MAPK signaling pathway

To elucidate the regulatory effect of trehalose on grape under cold stress, we analyzed DEGs involved in the plant MAPK signaling pathway. A total of 35 DEGs were common to both Cold_vs_CK and Cold_Tre_vs_Cold, mainly comprising 11 WRKY genes, 6 RLK genes, 3 MAPK genes, and 2 basic pathogenesis-related protein (PRb) genes (Fig. 7a, b and Table S10). Construction of the weighted gene co-expression network

To investigate the regulatory gene network underlying the response of grape to cold stress under exogenous trehalose treatment, a weighted gene co-expression network was constructed from 18,844 highly expressed DEGs. During soft-thresholding power screening, the scale-free fit index (R2) reached 0.85 at a power of 9, satisfying the criterion for scale-free network distribution while maintaining high average connectivity (Fig. 9a). Hierarchical clustering analysis was then performed using the topological overlap matrix, and initial modules were identified using the dynamic tree cut algorithm. Subsequently, modules were merged according to the similarity of their eigengenes, yielding a total of eight co-expression modules, each distinguished by a different color. Among these, the turquoise module contained the most genes (6,984), followed by the blue module (6,249), whereas the pink module contained the fewest (316) (Fig. 9b).

Correlation heatmap analysis between module eigengenes and sample treatment conditions

Correlation analysis between module eigengenes and physiological index data was performed. Modules were screened using the criteria |r| > 0.8 and P < 0.005, resulting in the identification of two co-expression modules significantly associated with various physiological indices. The brown module was positively correlated with MDA and O₂•⁻ contents, while the turquoise module was positively correlated with SOD, POD, and CAT activities. No significant correlation was observed between trehalose or soluble sugar content and any specific module (|r| < 0.8) (Fig. 9c and Table S12). These results indicate that the two modules are closely related to cold tolerance in grape, suggesting the need for further analysis of their related genes and functional enrichment.

To identify candidate genes associated with trehalose-mediated cold response within these modules, gene co-expression sub-networks were constructed in Cytoscape using weight values (> 0.3) from the co-expression network. In the brown module, the top 30 network pairs were selected, from which two highly connected candidate genes putatively linked to cold tolerance were identified: Vitis01g00134 (VQ22) and Vitis01g00718 (RGLG2) (Fig. 9d). In the turquoise module, three highly connected candidate genes potentially involved in trehalose-mediated cold responsewere identified: Vitis01g00054 (PPR21), Vitis01g00010 (SPL6), and Vitis01g00240 (CML16) (Fig. 9e and Table S13).

Analysis of differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs)

Metabolomic analysis of grape callus in response to cold stress identified 3,852 DAMs across the four comparison groups (Table S14). Among these, 1,146 DAMs (679 up-regulated and 467 down-regulated) were detected in Cold_vs_CK, 425 DAMs (267 up-regulated and 158 down-regulated) in Tre_vs_CK, 850 DAMs (694 up-regulated and 165 down-regulated) in Cold_Tre_vs_Cold, and 1,431 DAMs (1,081 up-regulated and 350 down-regulated) in Cold_Tre_vs_Tre (Fig. S4a–d). To examine dynamic trends in metabolite abundance across groups, we applied unit variance scaling to the filtered DAMs, followed by K-means clustering analysis (Fig. S4e–h). The DAMs were classified into four distinct expression patterns. Subclass 1 showed notable upregulation under Tre treatment but a continuous decrease under Cold and Cold_Tre treatments. Subclass 2 exhibited an overall sustained down-regulation trend. Subclass 3 was significantly up-regulated during the Cold phase but down-regulated under Tre and Cold_Tre treatments. Subclass 4 displayed a gradual upward trend with minor fluctuations.

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that low concentrations of trehalose enhance plant tolerance to abiotic stresses without side effects, whereas high concentrations may cause metabolic disorders [37]. In the preliminary experiments of this study, grape callus was treated with 20 mM and 50 mM trehalose. The results showed that 50 mM trehalose induced browning of grape callus under normal growth conditions (data not shown), consistent with previous reports. Therefore, we used 20 mM trehalose to investigate its function and potential regulatory relationship in grape callus in response to cold stress. Phenotypic observations indicated that exogenous trehalose alleviated cell damage and oxidative browning caused by cold stress in grape callus. Further physiological and biochemical analyses indicated that exogenous trehalose promoted the accumulation of endogenous trehalose and soluble sugars and significantly increased antioxidant enzyme activities, thereby enhancing ROS-scavenging capacity. These results suggest that exogenous trehalose mitigates cold injury in grape callus through multiple physiological pathways.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed that the total number of DEGs in Cold_Tre_vs_Cold was 3.74-fold that in Tre_vs_CK, while the total number of DEGs in Cold_Tre_vs_Tre was 1.04-fold that in Cold_vs_CK. These results suggest that cold induces more extensive changes in gene expression than normal temperature and that trehalose treatment may induce more genes in response to cold, thereby enhancing the cold resistance potential of grape callus. GO enrichment analysis showed that DEGs across all four treatment groups were enriched for molecular function terms such as hydrolase activity. Combined with KEGG analysis of Cold_Tre_vs_Cold, our findings identified pathways associated with trehalose-mediated cold stress response in grape callus, including plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK signaling pathway–plant, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and starch and sucrose metabolism.

KEGG analysis revealed that DEGs involved in secondary metabolic pathways included PAL, encoding an enzyme that catalyzes the first step of the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway, STS, encoding a key enzyme gene in stilbene biosynthesis, and additional genes such as BGLU, PER, 4CL, CYP82, and BAMS, each encoding critical enzymes in these pathways. Consistent with the results of the present study, PAL and STS have been previously identified by our group as key genes in the cold stress response in grapevine here [33]. However, most PAL and STS genes were significantly up-regulated in the Cold_vs_CK comparison and significantly down-regulated in the Cold_Tre_vs_Cold comparison, although their expression levels remained higher in Cold_Tre than in CK. By contrast, in our previous study, the corresponding genes were significantly up-regulated in both the Cold_vs_CK and Cold_Tre_vs_Cold comparisons. Therefore, we speculate that these discrepancies may result from differences in the duration of cold stress, causing a shift from a stress-induced defense response to adaptive homeostatic regulation in grape callus.

Analysis of DEGs associated with plant hormone signal transduction pathways affected by trehalose identified pathways associated with trehalose-mediated cold stress response in grape callus, including auxin signal transduction, phosphorylation cascades, and plant hormone homeostasis. Among these, Vitis11g00590 (IAA) and Vitis07g01123 (RLKs) were significantly up-regulated and down-regulated, respectively, in both the Cold and Cold_Tre groups. Previous studies have demonstrated that IAA enhances plant cold tolerance by modulating the metabolism and signal transduction of other phytohormones [38]. Overexpression of BrRLCK1/2 in Arabidopsis reduces plant freezing tolerance and negatively regulates freezing resistance via the CBF pathway [39]. These results suggest that exogenous trehalose may be associated with improved cold tolerance in grape callus, correlating with increased IAA transcript levels and decreased RLK transcript levels. However, their functional conservation in grape callus remains to be experimentally verified.

In this study, DEGs in grape callus in response to trehalose under cold stress were significantly enriched in the starch and sucrose metabolism pathway. Among these, Vitis16g00880 (TPPA), a key gene involved in trehalose metabolism, was up-regulated under cold conditions. However, its expression was suppressed by exogenous trehalose, although it remained higher than in the CK group. We infer that pathways associated with exogenous trehalose-mediated cold stress response in grape callus may involve the regulation of related genes in endogenous trehalose metabolism. This finding is consistent with our previous results, although the related genes affected in this pathway differ [32]. The expression of the tomato SlBGLU2 gene is inhibited under cold conditions, and its homolog, Atsfr2, is a known cold-responsive gene [40]. Here, the BGLU family members Vitis07g00359 and Vitis06g00184 were significantly down-regulated in both the Cold_vs_CK and Cold_Tre_vs_Cold comparisons. Therefore, we propose that pathways associated with exogenous trehalose-mediated cold adaptation in grape callus may involve the repression of BGLU gene expression and reduction of β-glucosidase activity. Consistent with previous reports in other plant species, trehalose-associated pathways play evolutionarily conserved roles in abiotic stress tolerance. For instance, exogenous trehalose confers heat tolerance in the woody plant Ginkgo biloba by activating GbTPS1-dependent sugar metabolism and secondary metabolic pathways [41]. In rice, OsTPS1 and OsTPP1 enhance cold tolerance by regulating soluble sugars accumulation and trehalose metabolism [15, 42].

Under cold conditions, DEGs related to the plant MAPK signaling pathway regulated by trehalose were mainly concentrated in gene families such as WRKY and RLKs. Most RLK genes were significantly up-regulated in the Cold_vs_CK comparison but significantly down-regulated in the Cold_Tre_vs_Cold comparison, indicating that RLK genes are activated by cold to initiate stress responses, whereas exogenous trehalose reduces their expression to alleviate excessive stress responses and maintain cellular homeostasis. Notably, the WRKY family members Vitis12g01352, Vitis07g00488, and Vitis15g01389 all exhibited notable down-regulation in both the Cold_vs_CK and Cold_Tre_vs_Cold comparisons. Previous studies have shown that WRKY41 acts as a negative regulator in response to cold stress in Arabidopsis. Cold induces the degradation of WRKY41, thereby relieving its inhibition of the CBF pathway and enhancing plant freezing tolerance [43]. Thus, we speculate that pathways associated with trehalose-mediated cold resistance in grape callus may involve these three WRKY genes as potential negative regulators.

Under cold stress, numerous TFs are activated or repressed to improve plant cold tolerance [44]. In the present study, analysis of differentially expressed TFs identified in the Cold_vs_CK and Cold_Tre_vs_Cold comparisons identified one commonly up-regulated TF (Vitis04g00625) and one commonly down-regulated TF (Vitis06g01552) within the C2H2 family, suggesting that C2H2-type TFs exert bidirectional regulatory roles in plant responses to cold stress. In addition, most differentially expressed TFs were significantly up-regulated in the Cold_vs_CK comparison but significantly down-regulated in the Cold_Tre_vs_Cold comparison, although their expression levels remained higher than those in the CK group. These results indicate that cold induces the upregulation of most TFs, while exogenous trehalose can moderately suppress their transcriptional levels. By contrast, six bHLH family genes were significantly down-regulated in the Cold_vs_CK comparison but significantly up-regulated in the Cold_Tre_vs_Cold comparison, although their expression levels remained lower than those in the CK group. This finding suggests that bHLH family TFs may play a distinct role in trehalose-mediated alleviation of cold stress. TFs have been established as key regulators under cold stress responses in various plant species. For example, BpERF98 [45], ZmC2H2-69 [46], RsMYB90 [47], VhWRKY44 [48], CaNAC064 [49], and PavbHLH1 [50] all play essential roles in plant cold resistance. These findings suggest that exogenous trehalose mediates the expression of related TFs and may participate in downstream cold responsive cascades in grape callus.

Through WGCNA, we identified a gene module (brown) correlated with MDA and O₂•⁻ content, and another module (turquoise) significantly correlated with SOD, POD, and CAT activities. Further analysis revealed several candidate genes with high connectivity within the co-expression network, including VQ22 and RGLG2 in the brown module, as well as PPR21, SPL6, and CML16 in the turquoise module, which are putatively associated with trehalose-regulated cold tolerance.Previous studies have demonstrated that homologous genes play vital roles under cold stress responses across diverse plant species. In winter rapeseed, VQ22 is demethylated under cold conditions and regulates plant cold tolerance [51]. In Arabidopsis, RGLG1/5 enhance tolerance to cold stress [52]. Arabidopsis plants overexpressing the PPR protein SOAR1 show improved resistance to drought, salt, and cold stress [53]. CML proteins enhance plant adaptation to cold by upregulating cold-responsive genes such as CsCML2/3/9/80 [54]. SPL3 genes in sugar beet and rice regulate plant cold tolerance [55, 56]. In grape callus, these homologous genes share sequence similarity with functionally characterized genes in other species. However, whether they contribute to cold tolerance through the trehalose pathway in grape callus remains to be experimentally verified.

Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses suggested that both DEGs and DAMs were enriched in metabolic pathways and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites in the Cold_vs_CK and Cold_Tre_vs_Cold comparisons. This finding indicates that both pathways represent important routes underlying grape callus responses to cold stress and the regulatory effects of exogenous trehalose, with strong coordination between transcriptional and metabolic levels. In the Cold_vs_CK comparison, DEGs were additionally enriched in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, whereas DAMs were further enriched in biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites. By contrast, in the Cold_Tre_vs_Cold comparison, DEGs were additionally enriched in starch and sucrose metabolism, and DAMs were further enriched in linoleic acid metabolism. These results suggest that exogenous trehalose may mediate the coordinated regulation of multiple pathways associated with cold induced damage in grape callus. Further analysis of positively correlated metabolites using a nine-quadrant plot showed that Cold_vs_CK and Cold_Tre_vs_Cold shared enrichment in metabolic pathways related to flavonoids, benzene and substituted derivatives, alcohols and amines, terpenoids, and alkaloids. All these categories fall under metabolic pathways and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, consistent with the DEG enrichment results from the transcriptomic analysis. Ren et al. systematically summarized the multifaceted roles of flavonoids in plant stress responses, highlighting that they enhance cold tolerance primarily through scavenging excessive reactive oxygen species, maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis, stabilizing plasma membrane structures, and participating in osmotic adjustment [57]. In conjunction with our omics data and the above functional understanding, the coordinated activation of the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway and the accumulation of its metabolites under cold stress may represent one of the important strategies by which grape callus withstands cold stress. Moreover, the concurrent alterations in other secondary metabolites, such as benzene derivatives, terpenoids, and alkaloids, also suggest that plant adaptation to cold stress may rely on the synergistic regulation of multiple secondary metabolic pathways. Taken together, under cold stress and trehalose treatment, candidate DEGs putatively associated with upstream signaling pathways were associated with grape callus responses, suggesting a putative link to these metabolic pathways. Nevertheless, the present study was limited to cellular-level analysis of trehalose-mediated cold tolerance in grape callus. Further investigations are therefore needed to characterize cold-tolerance mechanisms in intact grape plants under field conditions.

Based on our physiological and multi-omics data, we propose a working model for trehalose-mediated cold response in grape callus (Fig. 13). Exogenous trehalose induces endogenous trehalose and soluble sugars accumulation, enhances antioxidant enzyme (SOD, POD, CAT) activities, and reduces ROS levels. These major physiological changes are experimentally validated (solid arrows). Transcriptomics, metabolomics, and WGCNA further identified candidate genes and key DAMs involved in flavonoid and lipid metabolism, with their expression patterns consistent with RT-qPCR results. The potential regulatory relationships between these gene-metabolite modules and cold tolerance remain hypothetical (dashed arrows). This model clearly separates experimental evidence from speculative links, providing a concise framework for future functional studies.

Conclusions

Phenotypic evaluation of callus chilling injury, along with measurements of endogenous trehalose, soluble sugars, MDA content, and antioxidant physiological indices, revealed that exogenous trehalose application improved the tolerance of grape callus to prolonged cold stress. Transcriptomic analysis showed that DEGs were involved in pathways such as biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, plant hormone signal transduction, starch and sucrose metabolism, and MAPK signaling, suggesting their potential association with trehalose-mediated cold tolerance. Several differentially expressed TFs related to cold response were also identified in this study. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses further revealed that DEGs and DAMs were co-enriched in flavonoid-related metabolic pathways, benzene and substituted derivatives, alcohols and amines, terpenoids, and alkaloids. Moreover, the related genes responsible for metabolite interactions were all identified as differential genes in the transcriptomic dataset. Using WGCNA, candidate genes putatively associated with cold tolerance, including VQ22, RGLG2, PPR21, CML16, and SPL6, were identified, and their expression patterns were examined via RT-qPCR.
