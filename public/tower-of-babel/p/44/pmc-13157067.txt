Health Care Resource Utilization and Associated Costs Across Proteinuria and Declining Kidney Function in Patients With IgA Nephropathy: A Retrospective Study in Three European Countries
Authors: Antonio Ramirez de Arellano, Garth Baxter, Sonali Dasgupta, Joseph Imperato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rationale & ObjectiveImmunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) is the leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage kidney disease. Immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) is the most common primary glomerular disease and the leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage kidney disease.1 Characterized by dominant IgA glomerular deposits, patients experience visible or microscopic hematuria, with febrile illness common in children/young adults and varying proteinuria in adults.1 The 2023 estimated European annual incidence was 0.76 per 100,000 adults,2 and approximately one-third of patients develop end-stage kidney disease within 20 years of diagnosis.3 IgAN has poor long-term outcomes, with a median survival of ∼11 years and a mean age at kidney failure or death of 48 years.4

The 2021 global Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) IgAN Guidelines state that a proteinuria level >0.75 to 1 g/d is associated with a high risk of progression to CKD.5 A reduction of proteinuria to <1 g/d is recommended as a reasonable target for improved kidney outcomes.5 However, UK registry data,4 together with findings from other retrospective studies, suggest more stringent targets. Thus, the updated 2024 KDIGO draft guidelines for public review recommend proteinuria <0.5 g/d (ideally <0.3 g/d), and further updates are expected.6

A United States retrospective cohort study demonstrated higher health care resource utilization (HCRU) and costs for IgAN patients with high-risk proteinuria and worsening kidney function.7 Similarly, UK real-world evidence showed that CKD-associated HCRU and costs have a substantial economic burden, particularly in advanced stages.8 Hospitalization rates for severe CKD (stratified using urinary albumin-creatinine ratio and estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]) were >3 times higher than less severe disease, with associated increases in annual patient costs.8

However, few studies have assessed European HCRU and costs in patients with IgAN.9 Thus, this study describes the characteristics of patients with IgAN in the United Kingdom, Spain, and Germany and examines the association of proteinuria and CKD stage with HCRU and associated costs.

Methods

Study Design

This was a retrospective, descriptive study of UK, Spanish, and German anonymized patient data collected between October 1, 2015 and October 1, 2023. The index date was the date of IgAN diagnosis between October 1, 2015 and October 1, 2022, allowing for ≥1 year of follow-up. Health care visits and costs were analyzed within a year post-index (Fig 1).Figure 1Study design. Study design. Abbreviations: HCRU, health care resource utilization; ICD, International Classification of Diseases; IgA, immunoglobulin A.

Initially, HCRU costs were analyzed by visit type, CKD stage, and 2 proteinuria levels—high or low—based on the 2021 KDIGO target of <1 g/d (defined below).5 HCRU and associated costs due to major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) or nephrotic syndrome were also reported.

Subsequently, proteinuria was categorized into 4 categories (<0.5 g/d, 0.5 to <1 g/d, 1 to <2 g/d, and ≥2 g/d) per the 2024 KDIGO recommended target of <0.5 g/d proteinuria.10 This more granular analysis was used to calculate costs stratified by a composite variable considering both CKD stage and proteinuria levels. Because of low patient numbers and a lack of emergency visit data in Germany, composite CKD/proteinuria costs were only developed for Spain and the United Kingdom.

Data Sources

HCRU data were obtained from TriNetX’s European, Middle East, and Africa electronic medical record (EMR) dataset. TriNetX includes EMRs (diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory values, genomic information) from member health care organizations, which are usually large academic centers comprising main and satellite hospitals as well as outpatient centers.

Ethics approval and informed consent were not required, as the analysis utilized anonymized EMR data. Data collection, processing, and transmission were performed in compliance with all applicable Data Protection laws, including the EU Data Protection Law Regulation 2016/679, the General Data Protection Regulation for the processing of personal data, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, and United States federal law for protecting the privacy and security of health care data (see Supplementary Methods 1for additional information).

Study Cohort

Eligible patients had ≥1 record the following International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes during the study period: ICD-9: 583.9 (nephritis and nephropathy, not specified as acute or chronic, with unspecified pathological lesion in kidney); ICD-10: N02.8 (recurrent and persistent hematuria with other morphologic changes); and N02.9 (recurrent and persistent hematuria with unspecified morphologic changes). No exclusion criteria were applied.

Definition of Study Subcohorts

Patients were categorized into 5 groups using ICD-10 codes and eGFR values and based on KDIGO CKD stages G1-G5.10 Stage G1 was defined as eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2, stage G2 as eGFR 60-89 mL/min/1.73 m2, stage G3a as eGFR 45-59 mL/min/1.73 m2, stage G3b as eGFR 30-44 mL/min/1.73 m2; stage G4 as eGFR 15-29 mL/min/1.73 m2; and stage G5 as eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2.

Patients were also categorized according to proteinuria levels defined in 2 ways: first, as low (protein <1 g/24 h, albumin-creatinine ratio <100 mg/mmol or <100 mg/mmol/24 h, albumin-creatinine mass ratio <100 units, protein-creatinine ratio <0.1 g/mmol, or protein-creatinine mass ratio <885 mg/g) or high (protein ≥1 g/24 h, albumin-creatinine ratio ≥100 mg/mmol or ≥100 mg/mmol/24 h, albumin-creatinine mass ratio ≥100 units, protein-creatinine ratio ≥0.1 g/mmol, or protein-creatinine mass ratio ≥885 mg/g); and second, into 4 ranges: <0.5 g/d, 0.5 to <1 g/d, 1 to <2 g/d, and ≥2 g/d (detailed definitions in Table S1).

CKD stage patient counts were not mutually exclusive, so a patient could be in 2 different CKD stages in the 1-year study period. Although this only affected a small number of patients, it was included to capture any increase in costs associated with worsening disease. However, proteinuria patient counts were mutually exclusive, so a patient could not be in 2 different proteinuria bands in the 1-year study period.

Finally, the codes used to identify subgroups of patients with 3-point MACEs and nephrotic syndrome are reported in Supplementary Methods 2.

Study Outcomes

Demographics and clinical characteristics were reported descriptively for each country. Outcomes included cost per visit, mean cost per patient, and total cost. Results were reported by visit type (inpatient, outpatient, emergency visits), CKD stage, and proteinuria level (low/high or one of the 4 ranges). Results were also reported for those participants who experienced either 3P MACEs or nephrotic syndrome in the analysis using 2 proteinuria categories. In the 4 proteinuria category analysis, the HCRU costs for each CKD stage were further stratified by proteinuria levels to produce composite costs based on both variables.

HCRU Analysis

The TriNetX platform was used to identify unique patients and the average number of inpatient, outpatient, and emergency visits per patient, which were then multiplied to calculate the total number of visits for each visit type.

HCRU-Associated Costs by CKD Stage

UK inpatient costs (elective and nonelective inpatient long-/short-stay costs) were based on 2021-2022 National Health Service reference costs. Interventions and disease type/complexity of care (Casemix Classification scores) were mapped to KDIGO CKD stages G1-G510 according to clinical advice (Table S2).11 Outpatient and emergency visit costs by CKD stage were based on Pollok et al,8 by mapping CKD stages to eGFR values as detailed in Supplementary Methods 3. Costs were inflated to 2022-2023 values based on the Unit Costs of Health and Social Care 2023 Manual (Table S3).11

Spanish inpatient visit costs for CKD stage G5 were assumed equal to “GRD 470.04 Chronic kidney disease of extreme severity” in the Official Journal of the Catalan Government 2020.12 For outpatient visits, the cost category used for CKD stage G5 cost was based on “External consultation based on II.4.B Other specialized and out-hospital care services.”12 CKD stage G5 emergency visit cost was based on Escobar et al.13 Costs were inflated to 2023 values based on Eurostat Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices for Health.14 Costs for other stages were calculated by applying the cost ratios between CKD stage G5 and other CKD stages to the UK cost estimates (Table S4).

For German inpatient visits, major diagnostic category codes relevant to nephritic disease were collated, and an average valuation ratio was calculated.15 For outpatient visits, CKD stage G5 costs were based on Pöhlmann et al.16 Emergency visit costs for CKD stage G5 were the average of Diagnosis-Related-Groups weights for partially inpatient renal patients.15,17 CKD stage G5 costs were multiplied by German Federal base rate of 202217 and inflated to 2023 prices based on Eurostat Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices for Health.14 Costs for other stages were calculated by applying the cost ratios between CKD stage G5 and other CKD stages to the UK cost estimates (Table S5).

HCRU-Associated Costs by Proteinuria Levels

The HCRU-associated costs for low (urinary albumin-creatinine ratio 0-30 mg/g and 30-300 mg/g) and high (urinary albumin-creatinine ratio ≥300 mg/g) proteinuria were based on Pollock et al.8 These values were mapped to urinary protein-creatinine levels (Table S6) based on clinical advice and inflated to 2022-2023 costs. Cost estimates for the United Kingdom, Spain, and Germany by proteinuria category are shown in Tables S6 and S7. The 2 proteinuria categories (high and low) were further stratified into 4 categories by urinary protein-creatinine levels for Spain and the United Kingdom. As neither National Health Service reference costs nor Pollock et al8 provided explicit cost data for these 4 strata, cost allocation across categories was also guided by clinical judgment.

HCRU-Associated Costs Using the Composite Variable for CKD Stages and Proteinuria Levels

The composite analysis examined how the combination of both CKD stage and proteinuria level was associated with costs. The total cost across the 4 proteinuria categories was calculated, and the proportion of cost attributable to each proteinuria category was determined by dividing the cost for each category by the total cost across all categories. These proportions were multiplied by the total cost for each CKD stage.

Statistics

The study was analyzed descriptively. Continuous variables are reported as means and standard deviations (SDs). Categorical variables are reported as numbers and percentages.

Results

Baseline Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

United Kingdom

A total of 6,940 UK IgAN cases were recorded during the study (Table S8). Patients had a mean (SD) age of 62 (18) years, and 43% were women (n = 2,960). Over one-fifth (21.9%, n = 1,520) had low proteinuria and 8.1% (n = 560) high proteinuria. When proteinuria was stratified into 4 groups, patients primarily had <0.5 g/d proteinuria (18%; n = 1,450).

Spain

There were 1,080 Spanish IgAN cases during the study (Table S8). Patients had a mean (SD) age of 53 (25) years, and 34% (n = 370) were women. Over half of patients had low proteinuria (58.3%; n = 630). When classified using 4 proteinuria groups, most patients were in the <0.5 g/day category (32.4%; n = 1,080).

Germany

Overall, there were 1,600 German IgAN cases during the study period (Table S8). The mean (SD) age was 58 (23) years, and 33% (n = 530) were women. Patients with proteinuria data (1,110 of 1,600) were evenly distributed between the low and high proteinuria categories (35%; n = 560 and 34.4%; n = 550, respectively).

Discussion

This study shows that declining kidney function was associated with higher HCRU and associated costs in patients with IgAN across 3 European countries. The increases were lower in Germany than in the United Kingdom or Spain, which may reflect limitations within the German dataset. The study also shows that increasing proteinuria levels are associated with greater total and average costs per patient. The analysis based on 2 proteinuria categories used the 2021 KDIGO recommended target of <1 g/d proteinuria to define participants with low or high proteinuria levels.5 However, following evidence from the literature regarding more stringent targets, the study subsequently employed 4 categories to allow assessment of HCRU and costs based on the 2024 KDIGO draft guideline recommendation of <0.5 g/d proteinuria.10 This allowed clearer elucidation of proteinuria, HCRU, and costs. The lower HCRU and associated costs with lower levels of proteinuria further support the reduction in target proteinuria levels recommended in the draft 2024 KDIGO guidelines.10 This guideline acknowledges that patients with IgAN are at risk of progressive loss of kidney function if they have proteinuria ≥0.5 g/d (or equivalent), while on or off treatment, and emphasizes the need for treatment or additional treatment in such cases.10 Thus, improved treatment to ensure lower levels of proteinuria could contribute to reduced HCRU, through avoidance of procedures such as dialysis,8 and subsequently reduced costs.

There are few studies of HCRU and costs among patients with IgAN,9 with this analysis providing significant insights. A 2023 systematic literature review highlighted the limited pool of published data, while noting the significant humanistic and economic burdens posed by IgAN.18 Our observations align with a real-world study in patients in the United Kingdom with CKD, which served as a point of reference for the estimations made in our study and noted increased HCRU and associated costs.8 Moreover, as identified in the UK study, our analysis noted that inpatient costs were the primary driver.8 A study in the United States similarly noted a significant association between advancing CKD stage, proteinuria, and mean total per-patient costs.7 CKD stages G1 and G5 were associated with average annual costs per patient of US$25,332 and US$128,432, respectively.7 High-risk proteinuria (≥1 g/d) was associated with an average annual cost per patient of US$44,784 versus US$17,484 for low-risk proteinuria (<1 g/d).7 Compared with the United States study, CKD stages G1 and G5 exhibited in the United Kingdom an average annual cost per patient of £3,517 and £64,910, respectively (€3,195 versus €58,969 in Spain). Likewise, high proteinuria in the United Kingdom was associated with £14,505, compared with £3,242 for patients with low proteinuria (€5,585 versus €2,617 in Spain). In addition, there was a marked increase in outpatient visits in the United States with increasing disease severity.

Study strengths include the TriNetX EMR real-world dataset, which offered a unique opportunity to gain insights into the epidemiology of patients with IgAN in the United Kingdom, Spain, and Germany. This dataset includes extensive EMR data from a diverse patient population across various health care settings, providing a rich source of real-world data. This study has limitations common to all EMR analyses as the data may be subject to misclassification, incorrect coding, and documentation errors. Although the TriNetX database applies coding harmonization to reduce the likelihood of disease misclassification across different institutions and countries, it was not possible to perform a manual review of cases to confirm the accuracy of coding. TriNetX captures patient data from select health care organizations that participate in the EMR network. If patients receive care at an out-of-network facility, this information would not be visible. Participating health care organizations are also mostly large academic centers, which may bias patient characteristics. Moreover, historical patient records may be incomplete depending on several factors including digitization of historical medical records and data sharing agreements between TriNetX and participating health care organizations. The German data were constrained by a lack of emergency visit figures and a low number of outpatient visits. This meant that it was not possible to calculate composite costs, which may affect the generalizability of the data. It is also possible that the assumptions made in mapping the 3 urinary albumin-creatinine ratio levels reported by Pollock et al8 into the 4 categories used in this study could have introduced errors into the costs reported. However, a consistent mapping approach based on the respective National Health Service cost categories was applied to minimize the introduction of any bias. It should also be noted that this was a descriptive analysis; therefore, it is not possible to make causal inferences from the data. Finally, as there is no specific ICD code for IgAN, we used ICD-9 583.9 and ICD-10 N02.8 and N02.9 codes to identify patients. Although these codes were selected to proxy the disease phenotype as closely as possible, some patients without IgAN might have been inadvertently selected.

It would be of interest in the future to extend this analysis by pooling the data with other IgAN datasets such as European registries, performing additional manual chart reviews, and including statistical analysis to provide more granularity on the association of CKD stage and proteinuria with HCRU costs. Future studies could also examine the relationship between cardiac disease or overt nephrotic syndrome and IgAN costs.

This study adds to the limited pool of published data reporting HCRU and costs among patients with IgAN in Europe, demonstrating that declining kidney function and increased proteinuria are associated with substantial economic burden on health care systems.
