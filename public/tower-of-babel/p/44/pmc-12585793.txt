Low Sensitivity of Jolt Accentuation of Headache in Screening for Herpes Zoster–Associated Meningitis: A Retrospective Study of 70 Cases From a Single Institute
Authors: Hajime Miyazawa, Ken Natsuga, Takashi Seo, Hideki Houzen, Hideyuki Ujiie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundJolt accentuation of headache (JAH) is well‐described as a highly sensitive indicator for detecting meningitis. However, its effectiveness in identifying herpes zoster (HZ)‐associated meningitis remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study aims to evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of JAH and other clinical manifestations in diagnosing HZ‐associated meningitis.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study of medical records from a single institution, comparing clinical features between HZ patients who developed meningitis and those who did not.ResultsA total of 70 HZ patients were identified, 17 of whom were diagnosed with meningitis and the remaining 53 who were not. JAH was significantly more prevalent in HZ patients with meningitis compared to those without (29.4% vs. 3.8%, p = 0.0077). The sensitivity of JAH for detecting HZ‐associated meningitis was 0.29, while its specificity was 0.96. Other symptoms and signs, including fever, nausea/vomiting, headache in areas without a rash, altered mental status, paralysis, and neck stiffness, exhibited similar results.ConclusionWhile JAH demonstrates high specificity, it lacks high sensitivity in diagnosing HZ‐associated meningitis and does not outperform other meningitis‐related indicators.Keywords: herpes zoster, jolt accentuation of headache, meningitis, neck stiffness, varicella‐zoster virus

Introduction

Herpes zoster (HZ), commonly known as shingles, is typically a self‐limited disease caused by the reactivation of varicella‐zoster virus (VZV) and manifests as painful vesicles with surrounding erythema [1]. HZ poses a significant public health concern, with its incidence increasing alongside the aging population in developed countries [1]. HZ‐associated meningitis is a rare complication of VZV reactivation [2] and often requires hospitalization. The management of HZ‐associated meningitis remains unstandardized, lacking both established guidelines and large‐scale randomized controlled trials. In contrast, existing case reports consistently advocate for an extended duration of treatment beyond that typically administered for uncomplicated HZ. Therefore, effective screening strategies for HZ‐associated meningitis are essential.

Jolt accentuation of headache (JAH) is a gold standard in the physical examination for detecting meningitis due to its high sensitivity [3]. However, its utility in screening for HZ‐associated meningitis has not been well described. Here, we present a retrospective study conducted at a single institution to determine the sensitivity and specificity of JAH and other clinical manifestations in detecting HZ‐associated meningitis.

Methods

Medical records of patients diagnosed with HZ, with or without meningitis, who visited the Department of Dermatology or Neurology at Obihiro Kosei General Hospital between April 2015 and March 2021, were retrospectively reviewed. All patients who underwent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) testing exhibited at least one of the following manifestations: JAH, neck stiffness, fever, nausea/vomiting, headache in areas without rash, altered mental status, paralysis, or bladder/rectal disturbance. HZ‐associated meningitis was diagnosed when all of the following criteria were met: the presence of HZ skin lesions; symptoms of meningitis (including fever, headache in areas without rash, stiff neck/JAH, altered mental status or focal neurologic symptoms, and nausea/vomiting); an elevated CSF cell count; and detection of VZV DNA in the CSF by polymerase chain reaction. Patients with incomplete data or those diagnosed with encephalitis were excluded from the study. The presence of JAH and other clinical data, including age, sex, medical history, clinical presentation, symptoms, lesions, neck stiffness, treatment received, and prognosis were collected from these records. As this was a retrospective study, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. The sample size was determined by the total number of patients who met the criteria above. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California, USA), with p‐values calculated using the Mann–Whitney U‐test for continuous variables (e.g., age) and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables (e.g., clinical signs). This study was approved by the institutional review board of Obihiro Kosei General Hospital (ID: 2021‐057) and was conducted according to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

A total of 70 patients with HZ were included in the study, comprising 17 cases with concurrent meningitis and 53 without. In addition to the 17 cases of HZ‐associated meningitis, 7 patients were suspected of having meningitis, but the diagnosis was ruled out due to normal cell counts in CSF. Therefore, these 7 patients were included in the non‐meningitis group. The clinical features of 70 cases (HZ patients with meningitis vs. those without) are summarized in Table 1. There were no significant differences in the demographic profiles (age and sex) between the two groups. Notably, immunocompromised conditions, including concurrent connective tissue disease, cancer, and human immunodeficiency virus infection, did not appear to affect the incidence of meningitis.

Among the clinical signs and manifestations associated with meningitis, JAH was significantly more prevalent in HZ patients with meningitis compared to those without (29.4% vs. 3.8%, p = 0.0077). Similarly, most other indicators were more commonly observed in patients with meningitis: fever (52.9% vs. 13.2%, p = 0.0017), nausea/vomiting (52.9% vs. 7.5%, p = 0.0002), headache in areas without rash (47.1% vs. 5.7%, p = 0.0003), altered mental status (11.8% vs. 0%, p = 0.0563), paralysis (11.8% vs. 0%, p = 0.00563), and neck stiffness (23.5% vs. 0%, p = 0.0031). In contrast, the incidence of bladder/rectal disturbance was similar between the two groups (5.9% vs. 5.7%, p > 0.9999). No specific affected skin areas were observed more frequently in the meningitis group. All patients with HZ‐associated meningitis were hospitalized and received intravenous acyclovir. No patients died, although two patients continued to exhibit paralysis throughout follow‐up.

Discussion

JAH and neck stiffness are typically important physical signs employed in diagnosing meningitis [4]. According to initial reports, JAH demonstrated a high sensitivity of 97.1% for detecting meningitis, while neck stiffness exhibited a much lower sensitivity of only 14.4% [3]. Subsequent studies have reported varying sensitivity ranges for JAH in meningitis, from 21% to 84.4%, and for neck stiffness, from 13% to 64.4% [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. This varying sensitivity reported across studies might be attributed to differences in study populations, disease prevalence and clinical settings, and causative pathogens (e.g., bacterial vs. viral) [7].

However, to our knowledge, the effectiveness of JAH in detecting HZ‐associated meningitis has not been previously documented. Neck stiffness in VZV meningitis, including HZ‐associated meningitis, has been reported in 36.4%–38% of cases, which is notably lower than in herpes simplex virus‐related (75.9%–100%) and in bacterial meningitis (74%) [2, 9]. Consistent with previous findings, the prevalence of JAH and neck stiffness in our study was 29.4% and 23.5%, respectively, suggesting that indicators of meningeal irritation are not highly sensitive for this condition as compared with other types of meningitis. Two factors may contribute to the relatively low frequency of meningeal signs in HZ‐associated meningitis: (1) VZV may act as a less irritative pathogen compared to others, and (2) the presence of cutaneous eruptions may facilitate earlier clinical recognition and intervention, potentially preventing the full development of meningeal symptoms. Therefore, the absence of JAH and neck stiffness should not be used to exclude the possibility of HZ‐associated meningitis.

Given the high specificity of JAH (96%), a positivity of result warrants further investigation via CSF examination. Previous studies that included patients with headache and fever likely resulted in a greater number of false positives, thereby lowering specificity [2, 9]. In contrast, our study targeted HZ patients, in whom the likelihood of JAH positivity unrelated to meningitis is inherently low. This reduced the potential for false positives and may have contributed to the higher specificity observed in our findings.

Previous reports have described the clinical manifestations of HZ‐associated meningitis, including headache (75%–100%), vomiting/nausea (41.7%–50%), fever (50%), and acute focal neurologic deficit (15.4%) [2]. Disturbance of consciousness is typically absent in these cases [2]. Our study corroborates these findings. In contrast, HZ alone frequently presents with fever (31.1%), lower motor neuron deficit (0.8%), and bladder/rectal disturbance (4%) [10, 11, 12]. Vomiting/nausea and neurologic symptoms such as paralysis and altered mental status are not generally associated with HZ and may therefore serve as specific indicators for HZ‐associated meningitis. Since HZ does not cause pain in areas opposite to the site of eruption, the presence of headache in areas without rash might be relatively sensitive for HZ‐meningitis. As JAH alone may fail to detect a significant number of HZ‐associated meningitis cases, clinicians should not rely solely on this sign. Instead, a comprehensive assessment of accompanying symptoms should guide clinical suspicion. CSF testing is warranted when other symptoms or signs of meningeal irritation are apparent, but not in all HZ cases, given the invasive nature of the procedure.

This study has three primary limitations. First, patients without clinical symptoms suggestive of meningitis did not undergo CSF testing and were included in the non‐meningitis group, which may have affected the results. Second, the study was conducted in a general hospital setting, which generally treats patients with more severe symptoms than those seen in small clinics. This could have influenced the sensitivity and specificity findings in our analysis. Third, information on VZV vaccination status, which could potentially have influenced the results, was not available in this study. However, since VZV vaccination was not routinely recommended in Japan during the study period, we believe its impact on the findings is likely negligible.

In conclusion, while JAH is specific, it is not sensitive for HZ‐associated meningitis, indicating that a negative JAH result does not exclude this condition. The sensitivity and specificity of JAH are comparable to those of other clinical indicators associated with meningitis.
