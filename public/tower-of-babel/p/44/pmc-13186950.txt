Green combustion synthesis of monoclinic Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ nanoparticles using simarouba glauca leaves for efficient visible-light-driven photocatalytic degradation of Rose Bengal dye
Authors: Shashank Puttaswamy, Murali Krishna Panchangam, Nagaraju Kottam, K R Pooja, G Nagaraju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of visible-light-active photocatalysts through sustainable synthesis routes is crucial for advancing environmental remediation technologies. Therefore, Well-designed photocatalysts with high catalytic activity attracted a lot of interest due to their remarkable charge separation capabilities and efficient absorption of visible light. In this work, bismuth molybdate (Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉) nanoparticles were synthesized via a green combustion process employing powdered Simarouba glauca leaves as an eco-friendly fuel. XRD pattern confirmed the formation of a monoclinic Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ phase with space group P2/c (13), evidencing high crystallinity and phase purity. Complementary characterization techniques, including FT-IR, UV-DRS, SEM-EDAX, TEM, and PL, were utilized to elucidate the structural, morphological, and optical features of the material. The nanoparticles exhibited a direct band gap of 2.22 eV, indicative of efficient visible-light absorption. Photocatalytic evaluations indicated remarkable degradation efficiency toward Rose Bengal dye (99.1%) under visible-light irradiation at pH 5, can be related to efficient charge separation and the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Scavenger and fluorescent probe tests indicated that hydroxyl radicals (•OH) played a significant role in the breakdown mechanism. Furthermore, recyclability tests demonstrated minimal loss in activity over successive cycles, affirming the catalyst’s stability and reusability. These findings establish green-synthesized Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ nanoparticles as an efficient, stable, and environmentally benign photocatalyst, offering a promising route for the sustainable degradation of dye-laden wastewater.Keywords: Bismuth Molybdate nanoparticles, Reactive oxygen species, photoluminescence, photocatalytic studies, Latent fingerprintSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The availability of clean water is critical to survival, but rising water contamination levels pose a serious environmental problem. Industrial effluent-induced water pollution by textiles, leather tanning, pesticide production, pharmaceuticals and printing industries releases substantial quantities of dyes and organic chemicals into water bodies, which are toxic and non-biodegradable1,2. These pollutants are not only harmful but also recalcitrant, raising serious health concerns for humans, aquatic life, and microbial populations as a result of their potential carcinogenicity and bioaccumulation hazards. Conventional methods, including physical adsorption, chemical oxidation, and biological treatment, have shown limited efficiency in completely removing these dyes3,4. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) driven by semiconductor photocatalysts have emerged as efficient, eco-friendly strategies for dye degradation under solar or visible-light irradiation. Nanostructured photocatalysts have attracted significant attention owing to their high surface reactivity, tunable band structures, and efficient charge separation dynamics5,6. Among these, bismuth molybdate-based nanomaterials have exhibited excellent photocatalytic activity under visible light. Their unique crystal structure and electronic configuration enable efficient generation of reactive species such as hydroxyl and superoxide radicals, which are vital for degrading complex dye molecules. Previous studies, demonstrated that bismuth molybdates offer superior performance in decomposing organic pollutants due to their narrow band gaps and high photostability, positioning them as promising alternatives to traditional photocatalysts like TiO₂, ZnO and CuMoO4. The present study aims to explore the potential of bismuth molybdate nanomaterials in efficient dye degradation, contributing to the development of sustainable and eco-friendly water treatment solutions2,7–9. From a quantitative perspective, the present system demonstrates near complete degradation of 5 mg L⁻¹ Rose Bengal using only 10 mg of catalyst per 100 mL solution, corresponding to approximately 50 mg of dye degraded per gram of catalyst per cycle. This mass-based efficiency highlights the effective utilization of the catalyst and indicates scalability. Combined with the demonstrated recyclability over multiple cycles, the results suggest that the present BMO photocatalyst holds promise for scaled-up wastewater treatment applications.

Recent studies on bismuth-based oxide nanomaterials have explored multiple approaches to tune their structure and functionality for enhanced photocatalytic efficiency. For instance, BiVO₄-Bi₂MoO₆ heterostructures synthesized via hydrothermal methods have shown superior charge separation and improved degradation of Rhodamine B under visible light through interfacial heterojunction formation. Likewise, rare-earth or transition-metal-doped Bi₂MoO₆ systems have demonstrated tunable band gaps (2.3–2.7 eV) and improved quantum efficiencies attributed to defect-mediated charge transfer10–13. Furthermore, composite architectures such as Bi₂MoO₆/g-C₃N₄ and Bi₂MoO₆/Fe₂O₃ have exhibited synergistic charge transport and enhanced photostability, broadening their applicability to both dye degradation and hydrogen evolution reactions14,15. Despite these advances, issues such as charge carrier recombination and limited stability still restrict their large-scale implementation. In contrast, the oxygen-deficient Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ phase offers a more intricate monoclinic structure and mixed-valence environment that can promote extended carrier lifetimes and stronger redox activity, potentially overcoming the limitations of conventional Bi₂MoO₆ phases16–18.

Bismuth molybdate (BixMoyOz) nanomaterials exist in several structural polymorphs, each exhibiting distinct physicochemical properties that govern their catalytic and electronic behaviour. Commonly studied phases include α-Bi₂Mo₃O₁₂, β-Bi₂Mo₂O₉, and γ-Bi₂MoO₆, with band gaps typically ranging from 2.5 to 2.8 eV, rendering them suitable for visible-light photocatalysis19–21. Their crystal structures, orthorhombic (Aurivillius-type) in Bi₂MoO₆ and monoclinic in Bi₂Mo₂O₉, significantly affect charge transport and surface reactivity. Some compositions, such as Bi₃Mo₀.₅W₀.₅O₁₀, incorporate tungsten to induce synergistic photocatalytic effects. In contrast, Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ represents a more complex oxygen-deficient phase with unique redox and defect characteristics that enhance charge separation efficiency, making it a promising candidate for environmental remediation16–18. The photocatalytic applications of Bi2​MoO6​ have been extensively studied, but those on Bi26​Mo10​O69​ remain limited. highlighting a gap in understanding its potential for dye degradation under environmental conditions. Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ offers distinct structural and electronic advantages compared to conventional bismuth molybdate phases such as Bi₂MoO₆ and Bi₂Mo₂O₉. Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ possesses a more complex oxygen deficient crystal framework derived from δ-Bi₂O₃, which inherently promotes defect assisted charge transport. The presence of oxygen vacancies and an extended Bi-O-Mo network facilitates enhanced electronic delocalization, suppresses charge carrier recombination, and improves visible-light absorption. These structural and electronic features enable more efficient generation of reactive species under visible-light irradiation, rendering Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ a superior photocatalyst compared to commonly reported bismuth molybdate phases.

The present work addresses this gap by demonstrating a sustainable green combustion synthesis of phase-pure Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ using Simarouba glauca leaf powder as a bio-fuel and systematically correlating precursor-to-fuel ratio with structural evolution, defect chemistry, and optoelectronic properties. Unlike conventional bismuth molybdates, the complex monoclinic structure and oxygen vacancy rich nature of Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ promote enhanced charge separation, band gap narrowing, and superior visible-light photocatalytic performance.

Experimental section

Materials

Bismuth (III) nitrate pentahydrate [Bi(NO3)3·5H2O], Ammonium heptamolybdate tetrahydrate ((NH4)6Mo7O24.4H2O) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. All chemicals purchased were used without processing anything further.

Formulation of green fuel

Simarouba glauca, a member of the Simaroubaceae family, is a tropical tree native to the rainforests of Central and South America, including countries such as Brazil, Suriname, and Colombia. This plant is recognised not only for its ecological value but also for its rich phytochemical profile. The leaves, bark, and seeds of Simarouba glauca are abundant in bioactive compounds such as quassinoids, alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, and phenolic acids, which endow the plant with potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and anticancer properties. These characteristics have attracted attention in the field of green nanotechnology, where plant-derived extracts are utilised as reducing and stabilising agents in nanoparticle synthesis8,22,23.

The Simarouba glauca (locally known as Lakshmi taru) leaves used in this study were sourced locally from RBI Colony Park, TPV Layout, Hebbal, Bengaluru, Karnataka. A publicly accessible cultivated tree, and the species is neither endangered nor protected under any national or international biodiversity regulations (including IUCN and CITES listings). Therefore, no specific collection permissions were required. The sampling complies with the general principles of the Nagoya Protocol and Access and Benefit Sharing (ABS) guidelines. Since the plant was sourced from a public non-protected area and is not a restricted species, no permit or license was applicable. The specimen was identified based on its morphological characteristics. which do not impose restrictions on non-commercial research involving non-protected species. Although no specific studies have yet reported the synthesis of bismuth molybdate (Bi26​Mo10​O69​) nanoparticles using Simarouba glauca leaf extract, a number of studies have recently shown that metal and metal oxide nanoparticles, including Ag, CuO, ZnO, and TiO₂, etc., may be successfully biosynthesised24,25. For instance, the use of Phyllanthus glauca in Bi26​Mo10​O69 nanoparticle synthesis confirms the feasibility of using botanical sources in producing complex oxides. For instance, the combustion behaviour and phase evolution are strongly influenced by variety of phytochemical compounds such as polyphenols, terpenoids, flavonoids etc., containing different functional groups such as hydroxyl (-OH), carboxyl (-COOH), carbonyl (C = O), and amine (-NH₂) groups in the Simarouba glauca leaf extract. These biomolecules act as effective fuels and mild reducing agents during the combustion process. These functional groups undergo rapid oxidation in the presence of metal nitrates, generating intense localized heat and gaseous byproducts (CO₂, H₂O, and NOₓ), which facilitate precursor decomposition and crystallization. In addition, oxygen containing groups such as -OH and -COOH can weakly coordinate with Bi³⁺ and Mo⁶⁺ ions prior to ignition, promoting homogeneous mixing at the molecular level. This synergistic fuel chelation effect aids uniform nucleation and favors the formation of phase-pure crystalline Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ during combustion26,27.

To prepare the Simarouba glauca leaf extract, fresh leaves were thoroughly washed with normal water, followed by deionised water to remove surface impurities. After drying in the shade for 2–3 days, the leaves were finely powdered and stored in airtight containers. A known quantity of this powder was then boiled in deionised water to extract the active phytochemicals, which were subsequently filtered and used as the bio-reducing medium for synthesising Bi26​Mo10​O69 nanoparticles. Utilising Simarouba glauca in the green synthesis of bismuth molybdate nanomaterials offers a sustainable, low-cost, and environmentally friendly alternative to conventional chemical methods. The synergy between the plant’s bioactive metabolites and metal precursors could potentially yield nanoparticles with enhanced morphological stability, crystallinity, and functional properties, making them ideal candidates for applications in catalysis, sensors, biomedicine, and environmental remediation.

Synthesis of Bismuth Molybdate Nanoparticles

Bismuth(III) nitrate pentahydrate [Bi(NO₃)₃·5 H₂O], was used as an oxidizers along with ammonium heptamolybdate tetrahydrate [(NH₄)₆Mo₇O₂₄·4 H₂O]. These precursors were dissolved in approximately 10 mL of deionized (DI) water and stirred thoroughly to obtain a homogeneous solution. The green fuel, Simarouba glauca, was then introduced into the precursor solution in varying precursor-to-fuel ratios of (1:0.5), (1:1), and (1:1.5), followed by continuous stirring to ensure uniform mixing. The resulting mixture was heated on a hot plate for around 30 min, till a gel-like consistency was obtained, cooled down to ambient temperature, followed by subjecting it to combustion in order to activate the redox reaction. The combustion process yielded a white coloured bismuth molybdate nano powder, designated as BMO, synthesized via the solution combustion method. The obtained product was cooled, finely ground, and calcined at approximately 600 °C for 3 h. The use of Simarouba glauca leaf powder in the present combustion synthesis produced well crystallized Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ nanoparticles with narrow size distribution and competitive visible light photocatalytic performance. Literature on plant mediated metal-oxide synthesis shows that extract composition strongly influences nucleation, growth rate and defect formation, which in turn control crystallinity and catalytic efficiency some commonly reported extracts such as, Azadirachta indica and Aloe vera extracts have been used to produce highly dispersed but sometimes broader sized ZnO and other oxides, whereas Ocimum extracts frequently yield stable, well-capped nanoparticles with good optical activity. The present Simarouba-assisted combustion route combines efficient fuel release with favourable phytochemical chelation, yielding better crystallinity and photocatalytic activity than many reported plant-derived syntheses and demonstrating its suitability as a robust green fuel for combustion synthesis28–30. Characterisation of prepared bismuth molybdate

Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer (Cu-Kα, λ = 1.5406 Å) was used to analyse powder X-ray diffractograms (XRD). SEM (scanning electron microscopy) and EDS (energy-dispersive spectroscopy) were used to evaluate the BMO morphological features, elemental analysis, and size on a Zeiss Ultra-55 instrument at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV, X-Max detector, and FT-IR was analysed using a Bruker FT-IR-84000s instrument to analyse different functional moity present, based on stretching and bending vibrations from 4000 –400 cm-1. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, TECnai G2) was used to determine the morphological and structural characteristics of the produced nanoparticles. To gain deeper insight into the crystallinity and interplanar spacing, High-Resolution TEM (HRTEM) images were obtained using the F30 model. To study the optical behaviour and charge carrier recombination rate, photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded using an F-2700 FL Spectrophotometer. These measurements provided insight into the efficiency of the synthesised material in photocatalytic applications and to analyse the optical properties of the material.

Photocatalytic studies

A photoreactor was used to systematically examine the catalytic reaction’s efficiency under controlled circumstances while exposed to visible light. The experiment was carried out at room temperature using a 300 W/230 V AC visible light source to deliver uniform and effective light exposure. A 125 mL quartz tube, chosen for its excellent light transmittance properties, was filled with 100 mL of the Rose Bengal (RB) dye solution along with varying amounts of the photocatalyst, to create adsorption-desorption equilibrium among the catalyst surface as well as the dye molecules, the mixtures were first kept in the dark for around ten minutes, as this step is critical and it allows the dye molecules to interact with the catalyst surface before initiating the photocatalytic process. To evenly provide consistent light exposure, the system was precisely placed inside the photoreactor. In order to activate the photocatalyst and start the degradation process, the solution was placed under visible light. After that, 2 ml from the solution was drawn out at regular intervals to track the reaction proceedings31.

To ensure accurate analysis, the BMO nanomaterial particles were effectively separated from the solution by centrifuging the collected samples. Using a UV-visible spectrophotometer to measure absorbance, the supernatant was examined for variations in dye concentration. The percentage degradation expression was then used to calculate the degree of dye degradation, giving information about the photocatalyst’s effectiveness. In addition to ensuring accuracy, this methodical technique made it possible to assess the catalyst’s effectiveness when exposed to visible light.

Results and discussion

The solution combustion process produces the milky white coloured bismuth molybdate nanoparticle (BMO). In order to remove any organic contaminants that may have originated from the green fuel source used for synthesis, the finished product is cooled, pulverized, and calcined for three hours at around 600 °C.

XRD analysis

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of bismuth molybdate nanoparticles synthesized via the green combustion route at different precursor-to-fuel ratios are presented in Fig. 1a. The results clearly demonstrate that The phase formation, crystallinity and purity of the synthesized samples are strongly influenced by the Bi: Mo fuel ratio in the synthesized Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ (BMO) nanoparticles. At the optimized stoichiometric ratio of (1:1), all diffraction peaks can be indexed to the monoclinic Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ phase with space group P2/c (13), and the calculated lattice parameters (a = 11.7458 Å, b = 5.7987 Å, c = 24.7921 Å) are in excellent agreement with the standard JCPDS reference (No. 01-087-0036). The absence of any additional diffraction peaks confirms the formation of a single-phase, highly crystalline BMO structure.

a: XRD of Bismuth molybdate NP’s. b: Crystal structure of copper molybdate NP’s.

Whereas samples synthesized with fuel deficient (1:0.5) and fuel rich (1:1.5) ratios exhibit weak additional reflections and reduced peak intensities, indicating the presence of minor secondary phases and/or incomplete crystallization. In the fuel deficient (1:0.5) case, insufficient combustion heat likely limits complete phase evolution, while excess fuel results in rapid gas evolution and localized temperature fluctuations, leading to lattice disorder and reduced phase purity. These observations highlight that precise control of the precursor to fuel ratio is essential to achieve phase pure Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ through combustion synthesis.

The average crystallite size of the optimized BMO (1:1) sample, calculated using the Scherrer equation, was found to be approximately 25 nm32 confirming its nanocrystalline nature. Thus, only the 1:1 ratio can therefore be attributed to the optimal thermodynamic and kinetic conditions achieved during combustion, which are not satisfied under fuel deficient or fuel rich regimes for the formation of phase pure Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉. Figure 1b schematically illustrates the arrangement of Bi2+ (Bismuth ions), Mo6+ (Molybdenum ions), and O2− (Oxygen ions) inside the lattice in the crystal structure of bismuth molybdate (Bi26​Mo10​O69). The red, purple, and cyan spheres stand for the atoms of oxygen, bismuth, and molybdenum, respectively. The structure demonstrates how the atoms of molybdenum and bismuth coordinate polyhedrally, forming MoO₄ tetrahedral units and Bi-O polyhedral units, respectively, forming a stable three-dimensional network. The material’s electrical characteristics and photocatalytic activity depend on this ideal configuration, which makes it suitable for use in photocatalysis, energy storage, and sensor applications.

UV-DRS analysis

The UV-Vis reflectance spectra were recorded in diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) mode within the wavelength range of 200–800 nm, as illustrated in Fig. 3a. Using the Kubelka-Munk function (Fig. 3(b)), the estimated band gaps (Eg) for BMO (1:0.5), BMO (1:1), and BMO (1:1.5) nanoparticles (NPs) were determined to be 2.65 eV, 2.22 eV, and 2.38 eV, respectively34. This variation in band gap with precursor to fuel ratio can be rationalized in terms of defect chemistry associated with the combustion process. Under fuel deficient conditions (BMO 1:0.5), lower combustion temperatures and incomplete fuel oxidation limit oxygen vacancy formation, resulting in reduced band-tail states and a wider band gap. In contrast, the stoichiometric fuel ratio (BMO 1:1) provides an optimal redox environment that promotes uniform crystallization and controlled oxygen vacancy generation, introducing shallow defect states near the conduction band and effectively narrowing the band gap. Excess fuel (BMO 1:1.5), however, leads to over reduction and excessive defect formation, causing defect state localization and partial electronic disorder, which results in a comparatively increased band gap35,36. Among the synthesized samples, BMO (1:1) exhibits the lowest band gap (2.22 eV), enabling efficient photoexcitation of electrons from the valence band to the conduction band under visible-light irradiation. This band gap is sufficiently narrow to maximize solar light utilization while remaining wide enough to preserve adequate redox potentials for reactive oxygen species generation. Accordingly, conduction band electrons can reduce surface-adsorbed oxygen to superoxide radicals (O₂•⁻), while valence band holes retain strong oxidative ability. Furthermore, the reduced photoluminescence intensity observed for the optimized BMO sample indicates suppressed electron-hole recombination and prolonged charge-carrier lifetimes, resulting in enhanced photocatalytic activity. Consequently, BMO (1:1) was selected for further studies.

TEM Analysis

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) serves as a prominent characterisation tool for probing the morphology, crystallinity, and nanostructural features of advanced materials at high spatial resolution. The TEM micrographs shown in Fig. 5(a-c) offer clear insights into the size distribution, particle shape, and dispersion of the synthesised BMO nanoparticles. The high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) analysis in Fig. 5d provides atomic-level detail, with visible lattice fringes corresponding to a measured d-spacing of 0.46 nm. This interplanar distance is attributed to the (013) crystal plane of the monoclinic BMO phase, corroborating well with the XRD findings and confirming the crystallinity of the nanoparticles. The Selected Area Electron Diffraction (SAED) pattern of the BMO nanoparticles, as shown in Fig. 5e, reveals distinct diffraction rings that are indicative of the material’s polycrystalline nature37. The indexed 1/2r (cm−¹) values of 6.17, 7.99 and 19.13 correspond to the (-311) (118) and (132) crystallographic planes, respectively, which align closely with the data obtained from X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. This strong agreement confirms the degree of crystallinity in the synthesised BMO nanoparticles and further validates the monoclinic phase assignment.

(a-c) TEM images of BMO nanoparticles at different magnifications, (d) HR-TEM images of BMO NP’s, (e) SAED pattern of synthesised BMO NP’s and (f) Histogram plot of BMO NP’s.

In addition, Fig. 5f presents the particle size distribution histogram derived from TEM observations. The analysis reveals that the BMO nanoparticles exhibit an average particle size of 23.054 nm, demonstrating narrow size dispersion.

The crystallite size estimated from XRD using Scherrer’s equation (~ 25 nm) is in close agreement with the average particle size obtained from TEM analysis (~ 23 nm). The slight difference between these values falls within experimental uncertainty and arises from the inherent differences between the two techniques. While XRD provides an average size of coherently diffracting crystalline domains, TEM measures the physical particle dimensions based on statistical sampling. Minor discrepancies can also originate from instrumental broadening effects in XRD and particle size distribution analysis in TEM. The close correspondence between the two values confirms the nanocrystalline and largely single-crystalline nature of the synthesized BMO nanoparticles. These results are crucial in understanding the surface reactivity, charge transport properties, and overall performance of the nanomaterial.

Photoluminescence studies

The rate at which photogenerated electron-hole pairs recombine is a crucial factor in determining the photocatalytic effectiveness of semiconductor materials. This rate has a direct effect on the production of reactive oxidative species that are involved in dye degradation processes. To evaluate this recombination behaviour, photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy was employed as a diagnostic tool to monitor the intensity of electron-hole pair recombination across the BMO samples. As depicted in Figs. 6a and b, the PL spectra revealed prominent emission peaks at 475 nm and excitation peaks at 268 nm. Typically, a higher PL emission intensity corresponds to an elevated recombination rate, which is detrimental to photocatalytic performance, as it limits the availability of charge carriers for surface redox reactions34.

(a) Emission spectrum, (b) Excitation spectrum c) CIE diagram of BMO NPs (1:1).

Lower PL emission intensity typically reflects suppressed radiative recombination of photogenerated charge carriers, implying more efficient charge separation and prolonged carrier lifetimes. This facilitates enhanced radical formation through sustained interactions of electrons and holes with surface-adsorbed oxygen and water molecules. Among the investigated samples, BMO (1:1) nanoparticles exhibited the weakest PL intensity, confirming their superior charge separation efficiency and minimal recombination losses. These observations are consistent with the UV-visible absorption results, which demonstrate a strong response to visible light. Accordingly, the BMO (1:1) sample was identified as the most active photocatalyst and was subsequently selected for detailed dye degradation studies, owing to its favourable optoelectronic properties and efficient utilization of photogenerated carriers.

The chromaticity coordinates of the synthesised BMO nanoparticles were determined based on the CIE 1931 colour space using an excitation wavelength of 268 nm. The resulting coordinates were plotted on the CIE diagram to evaluate the emission colour characteristics, as depicted in Fig. 6c. A distinct marker was employed to indicate the precise location of the photoluminescent emission on the chromaticity map, effectively visualising the perceived colour of light emitted by the nanoparticles38. The corresponding (x, y) coordinates, summarised in the inset table of Fig. 6c, provide quantitative insight into the hue and chromatic quality of the emitted light. The placement of the marker within the blue spectral region confirms that the nanoparticles exhibit a dominant bluish-cyan luminescence, which is consistent with their emission peak observed in PL spectra. Such information is valuable for potential optoelectronic or forensic imaging applications where emission colour precision is critical.

Photocatalytic activity of BMO NPs

Photocatalysis has emerged as a transformative technology in addressing environmental challenges, particularly in wastewater treatment and air purification. The process leverages the unique properties of semiconducting materials to harness light energy and drive chemical reactions that degrade harmful pollutants into harmless by-products39. Photocatalytic applications extend to the breakdown of synthetic dyes, pesticides, and other organic pollutants, which are often resistant to conventional treatment methods. In dye degradation, photocatalysis plays a pivotal role in converting toxic dye molecules into environmentally benign substances, such as carbon dioxide and water, under visible or UV light. Beyond wastewater treatment, photocatalysis is also employed in air purification by decomposing volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and reducing greenhouse gases. Furthermore, it holds potential in hydrogen production through water splitting, showcasing its versatility in both environmental remediation and sustainable energy applications. This innovative approach not only ensures cleaner ecosystems but also promotes sustainable industrial practices, contributing to a greener future40,41.

The degradation of dyes via advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) plays a vital role in meeting global environmental sustainability goals and safeguarding ecosystem health for future generations. The catalyst’s surface produces electron-hole pairs when photons with energy equal to or higher than the semiconductor’s band gap (BMO) are absorbed under visible light irradiation42. If these charge carriers avoid recombination, they migrate to the surface, where electrons are captured by the semiconductor (BMO), and holes are trapped by surface hydroxyl groups. The trapped holes produce OHads radicals, while the electrons react with O2 and H2O to form HO• and •O2−. These reactive radicals cause the oxidation of organic pigments. The photocatalytic performance in organic dye degradation is influenced by various factors such as phase composition, degree of crystallinity, morphology, particle size and distribution, specific surface area, porosity, surface hydroxyl concentration, and the band gap energy of the photocatalyst43. Using BMO nanoparticles (NPs) as a photocatalyst, the degradation of Rose Bengal (RB) dye was monitored under visible light. A 2 mL aliquot of the sample was collected, and its absorption was measured, with the rate of decolourization assessed based on changes in the intensity of the dye’s characteristic peak at λmax (548 nm). Rose Bengal (RB) was selected as the model organic dye in this study owing to its chemical stability, complex molecular structure, and strong absorption in the visible region, which make it suitable for evaluating visible light driven photocatalysts. RB is a halogenated xanthene dye containing multiple aromatic rings and heavy iodine substituents, rendering it more resistant to oxidative degradation compared to simpler azo or cationic dyes. As shown in Fig. 7a, preliminary screening using Alizarin Red (AR), Evans Blue (EB), Neutral Red (NR), Rhodamine B(RhB), and Rose Bengal (RB) revealed distinct degradation behaviours under identical visible-light conditions, with RB exhibiting a well-defined degradation profile in the presence of the BMO photocatalyst. Moreover, RB possesses a high molar extinction coefficient and photosensitization capability, enabling sensitive monitoring of photocatalytic kinetics and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. The linear pseudo-first-order kinetic behaviour observed for RB degradation (Fig. 8a) further confirms its suitability as a reliable probe molecule for assessing charge-carrier utilization and photocatalytic efficiency. Therefore, RB was chosen as a representative and chemically demanding model pollutant to systematically evaluate the visible-light photocatalytic performance of Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ nanoparticles.

(a) Kinetic studies of bismuth molybdate catalyst on different dyes, (b) Effect of scavengers on dye degradation, (c) Coumarin studies on dye degradation, (d) Catalytic recycling studies of BMO.

Scavenger experiments were carried out to determine the prominent reactive species involved in the photocatalytic breakdown of RB dye. To capture reactive species such as holes (h⁺), superoxide radicals (O₂⁻), and hydroxyl radicals (•OH), certain chemical scavengers were introduced to the reaction system44. The dominating reactive species was determined by tracking variations in the dye degradation rate with each scavenger. Additionally, coumarin, a fluorescent probe, was added to the process to measure hydroxyl radicals. 7-hydroxycoumarin, which produces fluorescence, is created when coumarin combines with hydroxyl radicals. The quantity of hydroxyl radicals produced during the reaction was precisely ascertained by measuring the intensity of this fluorescence. This technique aids in comprehending how reactive species function in the photocatalytic process.

Variation of photocatalyst load

Figure 7b shows the effect of BMO nanoparticle dosage on the photocatalytic degradation of Rose Bengal under visible light irradiation. The catalyst loading was varied from 10 to 40 mg per 100 mL of a 5 ppm RB solution at pH 7. An increase in degradation efficiency was observed up to a catalyst dosage of 10 mg, which exhibited the highest degradation efficiency of 56.5%, indicating the availability of sufficient active sites for photocatalytic reactions. However, a further increase in catalyst dosage beyond 10 mg resulted in a gradual decline in degradation efficiency. This reduction can be attributed to excessive catalyst particles causing agglomeration, which reduces the effective surface area, as well as light scattering and shielding effects, limiting photon penetration into the solution. Additionally, higher catalyst loadings can increase solution turbidity, thereby restricting mass transfer and reducing the utilization efficiency of photogenerated charge carriers. Based on these observations, 10 mg was selected as the optimal catalyst dosage for subsequent photocatalytic studies, as it provides a balance between active surface availability and effective light absorption45.

Effect of RB dye concentration

Figure 7c depicts the influence of Rose Bengal (RB) dye concentration on the photocatalytic performance of BMO nanoparticles. The experiments were conducted using an optimal catalyst dosage of 10 mg with RB dye concentrations of 5, 10, 15, and 20 ppm in 100 mL of solution at neutral pH. The degradation rate is considerably increased when exposed to a light source(visible). Beyond the saturation point, the degradation efficiency decreases due to the screening effect, which occurs when the surface of the BMO catalyst becomes deficient in available active sites for dye adsorption. As a result, the degradation rate is comparatively higher at lower dye concentrations.

Influence of pH

pH modulation was carried out by adding aqueous NaOH for the basic range and HCl solution for the acidic range. The degradation efficiency was evaluated over a wide pH range while maintaining optimal concentrations of RB (5 ppm) and BMO NPs (10 mg). The selected pH values in this study were chosen to represent acidic, near neutral, and basic environments commonly encountered in wastewater systems. As shown in Fig. 7d, the photocatalytic degradation efficiency strongly depends on the solution pH. The maximum degradation efficiency of 99.1% was achieved at acidic pH 5. This behavior can be linked to pH induced changes in the surface charge of the catalyst arising from the protonation and deprotonation of surface hydroxyl groups. Metal oxide surfaces are known to undergo spontaneous hydroxylation due to dissociative adsorption of water molecules, resulting in amphoteric surface -OH groups, under acidic conditions, these surface -OH groups become protonated, resulting in a positively charged catalyst surface. This promotes strong electrostatic attraction between the positively charged BMO surface and the negatively charged Rose Bengal molecules (an anionic dye), thereby enhancing dye adsorption and interfacial charge transfer between RB dye molecules and catalyst NPs. In contrast, at higher pH values (basic conditions), surface deprotonation leads to electrostatic repulsion with RB molecules, resulting in reduced degradation efficiency46.

Scavenger studies

Figure 8b displays the key species that cause the RB dye solution to degrade. Reducing surface-active sites and suppressing reactive species are the functions of scavenger. The experiment included ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid disodium salt (EDTA-2Na, 1 mM), ascorbic acid (1 mM), and tert-butyl alcohol (TBA, 1 mM) as scavengers for holes (H+), superoxide anion radicals (O2 •−), and hydroxyl radicals (•OH). As seen by the sharp decline in degradation percentage (27.5%), the scavenger investigation suggests that hydroxyl radicals (•OH) are the most important factor in photocatalytic dye degradation. Therefore, the reactive molecules responsible for the breakdown of RB dye are •OH radicals.

Quantisation of •OH radicals

The •OH produced during the BMO NPs photocatalytic degradation reaction is determined using a spectrofluorometer, as depicted in Fig. 8c. Typically coumarin is used as probe molecule in research since it reacts with •OH to create the extremely fluorescent 7-hydroxycoumarin. Procedure for fluorescence probe experiment follows, 10 mg of BMO catalyst dispersed in 100 mL of a 1 mM coumarin solution and stirred for 30 min prior to illumination. The photoluminescence (PL) intensity was monitored using an excitation wavelength of 320 nm, with aliquots collected at 10-minute intervals. A progressive rise in PL intensity with time indicates the continuous formation of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) throughout the photocatalytic process, confirming the efficient generation of reactive oxygen species. The linear increase in PL intensity further demonstrates that •OH radicals are produced steadily on the surface of the BMO catalyst during dye degradation.

Recycling studies on catalyst

When choosing a practical and affordable catalyst for pilot-scale clean-up systems, recovery and recycling are important factors to take into account. Using 100 mL of the RB dye, we conducted four cycles of RB photo-degradation to examine the reusability efficiency of our best NPs. The stability and recyclability of the BMO catalyst after four cycles throughout the 180-minute procedure are displayed in Fig. 8d. After treatment, centrifugation made it straightforward to recycle and repurpose the BMO catalyst. The findings demonstrate that following four extremely successful cycles, BMO’s photoactivity dropped from 99% to 89% (only 10%). Photocatalysis activity may somewhat decline as a result of material losses during the recovery process (washing and drying). This would result in a lower dose in the subsequent cycle, which would lower surface catalytic activity and lower the performance. After the recycling cycles, the consistent photocatalytic performance observed over multiple runs indicates good functional stability of the BMO nanoparticles. These repeated degradation cycles conducted under identical experimental conditions also confirm the reproducibility and reliability of the photocatalytic results (n = 4). The observed variations in degradation efficiency fall within acceptable experimental limits for heterogeneous photocatalytic systems. The retention of degradation efficiency suggests that no significant structural degradation or surface deactivation occurred during the photocatalytic process.

Probable photodegradation mechanism

Scheme 2 illustrates the photocatalytic mechanism of BMO under visible-light illumination, involving a sequence of redox processes responsible for pollutant degradation. Upon exposure to visible light, BMO absorbs photons with energy equal to or greater than its bandgap, promoting electrons (e⁻) from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB) and leaving corresponding holes (h⁺) in the VB58. The photogenerated charge carriers subsequently migrate to the catalyst surface, where they drive oxidation-reduction reactions. In this process, the oxygen deficient crystal framework present in the BMO lattice can introduce localized defect states that act as effective electron trapping centers, suppressing rapid electron-hole recombination and prolonging charge carrier lifetimes. These defect sites also serve as favorable adsorption and activation centers for molecular oxygen. The excited electrons react with dissolved oxygen (O₂) to produce superoxide radicals (•O₂⁻), while the photogenerated holes oxidize water molecules or hydroxide ions to yield hydroxyl radicals (•OH). These highly reactive species, including •OH and •O₂⁻, attack and decompose dye molecules or other organic contaminants into benign end products such as carbon dioxide (CO₂) and water (H₂O). Consequently, BMO efficiently promotes dye degradation and decolourization, exemplified by the breakdown of Rose Bengal. The involvement of h⁺ and •O₂⁻ as dominant reactive species, as identified from scavenger experiments, further supports an oxygen vacancy assisted photocatalytic mechanism. The involvement of superoxide radicals and holes is strongly supported by scavenger experiments, which revealed a pronounced suppression of photocatalytic activity upon the addition of superoxide and hole scavengers. In addition, the quantified hydroxyl radical generation using coumarin fluorescence probing, together with the observed band gap position, reduced photoluminescence intensity, and kinetic behaviour, collectively corroborate the proposed photocatalytic mechanism. These complementary results provide reliable indirect evidence for ROS-mediated degradation pathways, consistent with widely reported photocatalytic systems.

Latent fingerprint applications

The potential of Bi26Mo10O69 nanoparticles for latent fingerprint (LFP) detection arises from their distinctive optical characteristics and strong surface affinity, which together facilitate enhanced contrast, clarity, and ridge resolution59. As illustrated in Fig. 9, the powder dusting method was employed to develop LFPs using BMO NPs. Their application on diverse non-porous surfaces such as compact discs (CDs), mobile phone screens, and mortar substrates is demonstrated in Fig. 10, where the developed fingerprints show high fidelity and definition.

Latent fingerprints primarily comprise moisture, amino acids, lipids, and trace residues that act as anchoring sites for nanoparticles. Upon application, the BMO NPs interact with these residues via electrostatic interactions, van der Waals forces, and hydrogen bonding, enabling strong adhesion to the fingerprint ridge patterns. Additionally, the nanoscale size of the particles allows them to penetrate fine grooves within the fingerprint, enhancing the visibility of minute details. The inherent photoluminescent properties and surface reactivity of BMO NPs contribute to high-contrast fingerprint imaging, ridge bifurcations, deltas, and sweat pore structures, significantly aiding in accurate personal identification60. These observable features correspond to the three established levels of fingerprint analysis. Level 1 addresses the overall ridge flow and pattern classification (e.g., loops, whorls, arches), Level 2 emphasizes ridge minutiae like bifurcations, ridge endings, and short ridges, while Level 3 dives deeper into microscopic details such as sweat pore location, shape, and ridge edge morphology, especially under UV light illumination. Moreover, their stability and compatibility with different substrates make them promising candidates for forensic applications, offering rapid, non-destructive, and cost-effective fingerprint visualization.

The luminescent ridge structures captured in Fig. 11 highlight the robust adhesion and interaction of BMO nanoparticles with the fingerprint residues. This strong interaction enables the development of well-defined ridges, enhancing contrast even on complex or contaminated surfaces. Furthermore, the nanoscale dimensions and surface functionalities of BMO NPs allow them to adapt to the intricate topography of latent fingerprints, thus enabling precise forensic visualisation under optical or fluorescence-based imaging systems. This makes Bi26Mo10O69 NPs a compelling candidate for advanced fingerprint development in forensic science.

Conclusion

A visible light-responsive photocatalyst was successfully synthesised via an eco-friendly combustion method utilising powdered Simarouba leaves as a novel, low-cost green fuel. This sustainable approach aligns with circular economy principles yielding phase-pure, nanocrystalline BMO with favourable optoelectronic properties. XRD confirms the crystalline phases, FTIR spectroscopy revealed characteristic vibrations of Bi-O and Mo-O bonds, confirming the metal-oxide framework. Optical analysis through UV-DRS revealed a narrow band gap energy of 2.23 eV, among the various samples tested, the BMO (1:1) composition demonstrated the highest photocatalytic activity toward the degradation of Rose Blue (RB) dye the observed photocatalytic efficiency of Bi₂₆Mo₁₀O₆₉ is closely related this optimized band gap, which enables effective absorption of visible light up to approximately 560 nm positioning the material as an efficient photocatalyst under visible light. PL spectroscopy suggesting moderate recombination of photo-generated electron-hole pairs. SEM imaging showed that the particles were agglomerated with irregular shapes, which may influence surface area and charge dynamics. EDAX confirmed the elemental composition validating the formation of Bi26​Mo10​O69. The proposed photocatalytic mechanism, based on UV-Vis absorption and PL data, involves the generation of electron-hole pairs upon light exposure, where holes (h⁺) and superoxide radicals (O₂•⁻) were identified as the dominant reactive species through radical scavenger experiments.

BMO NPs also exhibited excellent potential for forensic applications, particularly in latent fingerprint (LFP) detection, delivering high-contrast, sharp ridge patterns with minimal background noise. The demonstrated latent fingerprint visualization highlights the multifunctional nature of the synthesized BMO nanoparticles. Both photocatalytic and forensic performances originate from the same intrinsic material properties, including surface defect states, oxygen vacancies, and favourable optoelectronic characteristics that promote strong interfacial interactions. While photocatalysis exploits these features for efficient reactive oxygen species generation and pollutant degradation, latent fingerprint visualization benefits from enhanced surface adhesion, contrast, and luminescent behaviour. From a practical perspective, integrating these functionalities within one nanomaterial reduces the need for separate specialized materials, broadening the scope of real-world deployment in environmental remediation, sensing, and forensic analysis. This dual functionality in both environmental remediation and optical sensing underscores the multifunctional nature of the material.
