OCT analysis of white clover leaves affected by regional ozone stress
Authors: Hayate Goto, Jumar Cadondon, Maria Cecilia Galvez, Edgar Vallar, Tatsuo Shiina
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlants are exposed to atmospheric conditions for extended periods, allowing the observation of their color, texture, and internal structure to infer the surrounding atmospheric conditions. High ozone concentrations, particularly in urban areas, are harmful to plants. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is a non-destructive method to evaluate samples’ internal structure and optical properties, allowing potential measurements without any influence on the sample. This study aimed to estimate the ozone damage on plant leaves by measuring the OCT of white clover (Trifolium repens), an indicator plant of ozone influence, collected from different areas. Initially, to compare the influence of ozone and the impact of transportation from sampling regions, the temporal changes in water transpiration from cutting leaves were evaluated. Next, the leaves of white clover collected from various regions were measured using OCT. Changes on OCT images in light intensity, layer thickness, and texture (contrast, correlation, energy) within the leaves were analyzed to compare the presence or absence of the stresses (ozone and water stresses). The first experiment confirmed that the trends in Energy changes in the OCT images were opposite for ozone and water stress, with ozone increasing Energy due to widespread tissue disruption that blurred texture patterns, and water stress decreasing it due to localized shrinkage. In the second experiment, similar to the first experiment, a decrease in the intensity of the palisade tissue, an increase in thickness, and an increase in Energy were observed in regions with particularly high ozone concentrations. These results confirmed that OCT image analysis could detect specific plant changes due to ozone and water stress. This study demonstrates the potential of in-situ measurements of plants using OCT to infer the environmental conditions to which the plants are exposed.Keywords: Optical coherence tomography, Indicator plant, Environmental assessment, Ozone, GLCMSubject terms: Optics and photonics, Plant stress responses

Introduction

Environmental conditions such as atmospheric gases, soil, and water contamination in urban and commercial areas harm plants1. Plants are exposed to the surrounding atmospheric environment for extended periods, and the plant’s internal structure reflects the pollution conditions in the initial stage. In urban and industrial areas, ozone gas concentrations become high, significantly impacting the interior of plants2,3. High ozone concentration can enter through the stomata of leaves, destroy the palisade tissue, inhibit growth, and reduce crop yield4. Environmental evaluations are needed to improve crop production’s safety and quality. As part of these environmental evaluations, the utilization of indicator plants is highly effective for assessing environmental contaminants. Indicator plants are the ones that exhibit high sensitivity to specific pollutants. They allow for a comprehensive assessment of multiple and long-term environmental factors compared to gas-measuring instruments5,6. They are often observed by visual inspection or microscopic observation. This approach can help identify nutrient deficiencies and environmental pollution from the plants’ conditions and provide an overall assessment of the surrounding conditions for plant growth.

The color and number of leaves are typically evaluated as visual inspections of indicator plants, but these methods lack quantitative observation. Microscopic examination requires cutting out samples7. Remote sensing methods, such as thermal and spectroscopic observations8,9, have been studied, but they have not been able to identify the specific stresses that cause internal changes in plant conditions. A method that can non-invasively and quantitatively measure the internal conditions of plants allows for a more detailed analysis of indicator plants, leading to the evaluation of small or initial changes due to stresses.

In this study, we developed a portable optical coherence tomography (OCT) to measure indicator plants10. OCT is a non-destructive, non-contact, and non-invasive technique that can quantitatively evaluate the internal structure of a sample11,12. By using OCT, the internal structure can be non-destructively quantified layer by layer, and the locations affected by the external environment can be identified. Since stress responses in plants appear first in the interior of the plant, OCT has the potential to elucidate the stresses that cause plant internal changes, leading to the evaluation of environmental conditions. Structural comparisons before and after optical clearing have previously been conducted using both OCT and conventional optical microscopy, as detailed in10.

OCT is a technique that uses low coherent light to obtain the internal structure and optical properties of a sample. It is mainly used in fields such as ophthalmology13–15, dentistry16,17, bacteriology18, and dermatology19. In plant measurement applications, OCT has been used to diagnose fruit diseases such as apple brown spot20,21 and leaf litter disease in persimmons22, identify rice seeds23, and observe the effects of Zn on lentil seeds24. There has also been a study where OCT was used on-site to diagnose plant diseases25,26. However, in agricultural applications, research utilizing OCT to measure plants and infer environmental conditions has not been deeply examined.

This study focused on ozone as an environmental stress on white clover (Trifolium repens) leaves, an indicator plant for ozone influence. Ozone is harmful to plants and animals at concentrations above 0.1 ppm and poses a risk in industrial and urban areas27. In Chiba Prefecture, Japan, where the study was conducted, various areas report different ozone concentrations of 0.04 ppm to 0.16 ppm28. The purpose of this study is to perform OCT measurements on leaves collected from those areas with different ozone concentrations to determine whether the damage to the leaves can be classified according to ozone concentration and to assess when these measurements of indicator plants can effectively infer the environmental conditions. This research explores the feasibility of estimating environmental conditions through OCT measurements of indicator plants. We hypothesize that environmental stress can alter leaf tissue structure, which is detectable by OCT as changes in signal intensity and layer thickness. Since different types of stress affect different parts of the leaf and cause different levels of change, we applied texture analysis (GLCM) to identify these stress-specific patterns, focusing on ozone-induced damage in palisade tissue29. Ozone generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) within cells, impairing physiological functions and causing structural alterations in leaf tissues which we aim to capture in this study using OCT30.

Initially, laboratory experiments were conducted to observe how ozone appears on the OCT images of leaves, aiming to establish a baseline for understanding the specific impacts of ozone on the internal leaf structure. Subsequently, to monitor changes during the collection stages from each region, temporal measurements of leaves with cut stems were carried out as water stress. This step was essential to differentiate between changes caused by ozone stress and water stress. Finally, leaves collected from each region with different ozone concentrations were measured using OCT to determine if the observed damage could be systematically classified based on ozone levels. Because the effects of ozone exposure accumulate over time, structural alterations observed in leaf tissue are presumed to reflect the average stress conditions of each growth location. When plants are exposed to high concentrations of ozone, ozone enters through the stomata and destroys the palisade tissue of the leaves31. Ozone causes structural changes in the palisade tissue, resulting in changes in the optical properties of the palisade layer. In this study, we analyzed the changes in intensity, thickness, and texture analysis of the palisade layer within the leaves. This approach allows for an accurate assessment of environmental conditions and validates OCT measurement of indicator plants as a tool for environmental assessment.

Method

Portable plant OCT

Figure 1a shows the configuration of TD (Time Domain)-OCT developed specifically for plant measurement32. The wavelength of the SLD light source is 1320 nm, and the light emitted from the light source is split into a sample light path and a reference light path by a fiber coupler. The wavelength was selected to avoid absorption by chlorophyll. The reference light path uses a rotating light path mechanism, and the light path length changes over time as the reflector rotates. The probe that illuminates the light into the sample is designed to be compact, flexible, and long depth of field. A tomographic image of the sample is formed by horizontally moving the probe. As shown in Fig. 1b, the portable plant OCT is small and can be powered by a DC battery, making it possible to carry it outside for measurements. During measurement, one A-line was obtained from the average of 16 times, and a B-scan image was created by scanning the probe horizontally 400 times at 10 μm intervals. The probe operation and data acquisition with a PC-oscilloscope(Picoscope 2205) were automated using the LabVIEW program.

Plant portable OCT.

Indicator plant samples

Mature white clover leaves of similar size were collected from Nishi-Chiba sites (0.08 ppm of ozone concentration) and transplanted into pots. These plants were cultivated under controlled conditions for approximately two weeks in an incubator (25 °C, 15 h light(blue and red)/9 h dark cycle) before the ozone exposure experiments commenced. Subsequently, an ozone generator was placed inside the incubator, and temporal changes were monitored under an ozone concentration of 0.2 ppm.

Figure 2a shows a schematic of the measurement of ozone effects on the white clover leaf. To evaluate the temporal changes in white clover due to ozone gas, leaves were selected and measured without cutting them, while they remained potted. To minimize interruption of ozone exposure during imaging, five mature leaves were selected from a single plant. This approach allowed rapid measurement without prolonged removal from the incubator, ensuring consistent stress conditions. To observe changes in the same leaves exposed to ozone over time, measurements were taken in pots at 0, 3, 6, 10, and 14 days after exposure to ozone gas.

Experimental setup.

To evaluate the changes in OCT images of leaves caused by transportation from the plant sampling area to the laboratory, we conducted observations on leaf subjected to stem cutting, which induces transpiration. Figure 2b illustrates the measurements of stem-cutting leaves. To minimize the effect of water loss through transpiration from the stem, the stem part of the leaf was immersed in water during the measurements. In the stem-cutting experiment, temporal trends in structural changes were assessed using a single mature leaf.

To measure the differences such as ozone gas influence in white clover by growth region, five white clover was collected from four different areas (Fig. 3)((A): Ichiba-cho(0.04ppm), (B): Masago Park(0.06ppm), (C): Miyako-cho(0.075ppm), and (D): Nishi-Chiba(0.08ppm)). Ozone concentrations in each area were measured using a portable ozone meter (Ozone Technica, OC-300) at the time of leaf sampling. The values represent the average concentration over one hour. These measurement data were confirmed to be consistent with official data from the AEROS monitoring system operated by the Ministry of the Environment Government of Japan28. For OCT measuring leaves collected from different areas, the stems were cut during sampling. To mitigate the potential effects of this procedure, measurements were carried out promptly, and five mature leaves were selected per site for comparative analysis.

Sampling area of white clover of different ozone concentrations.

The experiment consisted of OCT imaging, signal processing, peak detection, extraction of thickness and intensity, texture analysis, and comparison.

Signal and image analysis

The intensity and thickness of the palisade tissue and contrast from texture analysis were obtained from each OCT B-scan image in order to compare the measurement results. At first, background light removal, focal length correction, moving average, normalization, and logarithmization were performed as preprocessing before calculating the intensity, thickness, and texture parameters. Focal length correction was performed to compensate for changes in intensity due to deviation from the lens focal length.

To determine the signal intensity at the center of the palisade tissue, all A-lines within the B-scan image were aligned at the surface position and averaged. Subsequently, the local minimum value under the first peak (surface) was considered the intensity at the center of the palisade tissue (red dot in Fig. 4). The thickness of the palisade tissue was calculated by using a peak detection algorithm developed in Matlab program, identifying the distance between the first and second peaks(red dot lines in Figs. 4 and 5). The first peak corresponds to the boundary between the surface and the palisade tissue, while the second peak corresponds to the boundary between the palisade tissue and the spongy tissue. Although the measured thickness represents an estimated optical path length, we refer to it simply as “thickness” throughout this paper for clarity and consistency.

Texture analysis was performed on the palisade layer of the OCT images utilizing GLCM (Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix)33,34. In GLCM, a matrix is created by counting the number of intensity values pairs of adjacent pixels. These counts are then arranged into a co-occurrence matrix, where each element(i,j) represents the frequency of occurrence of a pixel with intensity value i adjacent to a pixel with intensity value j. p denotes each element of the GLCM, representing the probability of occurrence for a given gray-level pair (i,j), while σ indicates the standard deviation. Contrast indicates abrupt intensity transitions, enabling assessment of changes in signal strength between cell walls and intercellular regions. Correlation reflects the degree of uniformity in structural alterations, indicating whether tissue layers are disrupted evenly or partially. Energy evaluates differences among neighboring pixel pairs and thus enables the assessment of fine structural changes in tissue texture. The combination of these variation trends is discussed specifically for each stress. Ozone disrupts the palisade tissue, altering brightness values in the image and increasing Contrast. Because the damage occurs uniformly throughout the leaf, pixel intensities tend to decrease while maintaining repetition, leading to an increase in Correlation. Additionally, the destruction of fine internal structure is expected to raise Energy values by changing the texture regularity. Values change in response to ozone exposure, whereas tissue thickness and GLCM parameters remain stable under normal conditions. Mean values and standard deviations were used to evaluate temporal change and regional differences in growth conditions.

Result and discussion

Stress appearance on OCT images of the leaves

Figure 4 shows representative OCT signal profiles of white clover leaves under normal conditions and after exposure to ozone. In healthy leaves, the signal exhibits distinct peaks at tissue boundaries, where abrupt changes in refractive index produce strong backscattering. Within the palisade layer, signal intensity is lower due to a relatively uniform refractive index and scattering within intact cells. After ozone exposure, the A-line signal in the palisade region is further attenuated, indicating structural disruption and a reduction in scattering interfaces such as cell walls and intercellular boundaries.

Figure 5a is an OCT image of white clover leaves grown under normal atmospheric conditions, (b) is an OCT image taken 10 days after exposure to high ozone concentration, and (c) is an OCT image after cutting the stem (4 h). The red dotted lines in Fig. 5b,c indicate the peak detection results corresponding to the boundaries of the first and second tissue layers. The region between the two detected signal peaks corresponds to the palisade layer, while the layer below the second peak represents the spongy layer. The color bar in Fig. 5 indicates the corrected signal intensity, represented as the logarithm of the intensity I(LN(I)). The LN(I) scale is commonly used in medical OCT35.

The result after ozone exposure (Fig. 5b) shows small gaps in the palisade layer near the adaxial surface compared to leaves under normal atmospheric conditions (Fig. 5a), indicating that the palisade tissue was disrupted by ozone. These gaps are observed in the region label “P” in Fig. 5b, located between the two red lines. Additionally, there are areas in the veins where the signal is not visible, corresponding to the region outlined in white and marked as “V”. In the results after cutting the stem (Fig. 5c), signal reduction in the spongy tissue due to transpiration effects can be observed. The gaps in the palisade tissue were not as clear as the ozone exposure results.

Figure 6 shows the thickness of the palisade tissue calculated from peak detection. Figure 6a shows the thickness change over the days of ozone exposure, and Fig. 6b shows the thickness change over time due to transpiration effects. It can be seen that ozone exposure gradually increased the thickness of the palisade tissue layer over 10 days. This apparent increase in thickness observed in Fig. 6a can be attributed to intensity gaps similar to those seen in Fig. 4, where signal reductions reflect underlying structural disruptions near the adaxial surface. This increase in palisade tissue thickness is presumed to result from a reduction in OCT signal intensity within the tissue, caused by structural degradation due to ozone. The decreased signal intensity, particularly in the region indicated by the red dot in Fig. 4, likely reflects a loss of cellular integrity, which reduces the tissue’s reflectivity. As a result, peaks in palisade tissue become attenuated or undetectable, leading the peak detection algorithm to interpret deeper regions of the tissue as the second peaks. This detected peak shift expands the calculated distance between the two peaks, thereby yielding an increased thickness value. Additionally, a different trend with reduced thickness was observed 14 days after the plant started withering. Figure 6b shows an apparent increase in palisade tissue thickness following the stem-cutting effect. This effect is attributed to the reduced internal water content in the palisade layer, which diminishes optical reflectivity and weakens the OCT signal. Consequently, deeper signals originating from the underlying spongy tissue (corresponding to the second peak) become more prominent, resulting in overestimation of thickness during peak detection.

The x-axis of Fig. 7 represents the number of days after ozone exposure, and the x-axis of Fig. 8 represents the time after cutting the stem.

GLCM results (Ozone) (n = 5).

GLCM results (Stem-cutting) (n = 1).

The Contrast increased within the 10th day but decreased after 14 days in Fig. 7a. This change is similar to the thickness changes. The internal signal changes due to cell destruction caused an increase in Contrast, and after the plant started to wither, a different trend emerged with reduced thickness. The Correlation was increased by exposure to ozone gas in Fig. 7b. In the GLCM analysis, the cell wall appears as irregular points within the leaves, and its reduction results in changes in the relationships between pixels, leading to an increase in correlation values. This increase is attributed to the homogenization of the internal texture of the leaves, where the differences in intensity between neighboring pixels become smaller. After 14 days, the Correlation value was significantly increased. The Energy values were not changed in Fig. 7c.

In Fig. 8, the contrast(a) value was increased similarly to the ozone effect in Fig. 7. The values of Correlation (Fig. 7b) show some variability, but overall, there is little change in the values. The values of Energy(c) gradually decrease, which is a different result compared to the ozone experiment. Stem cutting reduced the internal water content, leading to unmatching refractive indices within the leaves. This caused random light reflection and signals, and decreased Energy.

Although the intensity differences observed in Fig. 4 were also appeared at points A-D, there was no significant difference in the thickness of the palisade tissue among the sampling regions. In region (D), with the highest ozone concentration, the Contrast was lower, while Correlation and Energy were higher compared to the other regions. The variations in Correlation and Energy values show similar trends between the incubator experiment (Fig. 7b,c) and the experiment using leaves collected from each region (Fig. 9c,d), which differ from the results observed in the stem-cutting experiment (Fig. 8b,c). This alignment in the two parameters indicates that the internal structural characteristics of leaves collected from each region reflect the influence of ozone concentration level in each local region. The values of Contrast showed an opposite trend to those in Figs. 7 and 8, indicating the influence of other stress factors. The individual differences in leaves (such as leaf size and growing process) covered the primary influence of ozone. However, the trends in Fig. 9c,d were similar to those in Fig. 7b,c, indicating a probable impact of ozone on the leaves despite cutting the stems when transporting them to the laboratory. By performing the on-site OCT measurement on indicator plant leaves, the influence of stem cutting will not be present, allowing the ozone effects to be evaluated more clearly. Further consideration of the effects of other stress factors will provide deeper insights. This result indicates that OCT measurement can quantitatively assess complex stresses on plants in a single measurement.

One of the key advantages of this plant OCT is its ability to measure living leaves directly, allowing for longitudinal observation of the same live specimen over time. Unlike conventional methods that require chemical fixation or staining, this plant OCT provides access to internal tissue structures without pretreatment under on-site observation. This enables faster, simpler, and more detailed analysis of physiological changes. This capability allows OCT to detect internal tissue changes caused by disease or environmental stress, such as ozone exposure, drought, or temperature shifts, before visible symptoms appear. By capturing early-stage disruptions in cellular architecture, OCT enables quantitative assessment of plant stress responses under dynamic environmental conditions. Early detection makes it possible to take action before serious damage occurs. For example, growers can adjust watering, change environmental conditions, or apply treatments to protect the plant. Because OCT is non-invasive and can be used repeatedly on the same live leaf, it is well-suited for tracking changes over time and supporting timely, informed decisions in plant management.

Conclusion

This study highlights the feasibility of using optical coherence tomography (OCT) to assess environmental stress in plants, focusing on ozone exposure’s impact in white clover leaves. Through OCT measurements, changes in intensity, thickness, and texture within the palisade tissue were analyzed, including the effect of cutting stems for laboratory evaluations. Additionally, white clover leaves collected from regions with varying ozone levels were examined, revealing internal structural changes linked to ozone exposure. The findings demonstrate OCT’s capability to evaluate different types of environmental stress, as shown in the case of ozone and water stress. This suggests that OCT has the potential to monitor multiple stress responses in plants, provided that further studies validate its effectiveness under other conditions such as temperature, humidity, and light intensity. Continued research in this direction could expand OCT’s utility in optimizing crop environments and improving agricultural productivity. Future research on complex stress conditions could further improve crop quality and supply stability.

Portable plant OCT offers the potential to diagnose indicator plants and estimate ozone concentration directly at plant growth sites. By monitoring internal plant conditions through automatic OCT measurements, issues such as diseases, moisture, and light deficiencies can be detected early, boosting productivity. The ability to estimate atmospheric and soil conditions on-site from a single OCT measurement provides a promising approach to advancing crop management and environmental monitoring.
