Nemabiome metabarcoding of wild conservation bison herds and co-grazing cattle reveals different species compositions and low-level benzimidazole resistance
Authors: Kaylee R Kipp, Elizabeth M Redman, Joe L Luksovsky, Dani Claussen, Lee C Jones, Danielle E Buttke, Christine M Budke, Walter E Cook, John S Gilleard, Guilherme G Verocai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastrointestinal nematodes (GIN) live in complex communities within American plains bison (Bison bison), a keystone ungulate species of North American prairie ecosystems. These trichostrongylid nematode species vary in their level of pathogenicity and can cause disease ranging from subclinical to clinical. However, the GIN species diversity and distribution of North American bison are understudied, especially in conservation herds in the United States. Fecal samples from conservation bison were collected from six herds in 2022, with five of those herds resampled in 2023 across six states (Colorado, Iowa, Nebraska, North Dakota, South Dakota, Oklahoma). Fecal samples from a herd of longhorn cattle that co-graze with a bison herd from Oklahoma were also collected in both years. Following fecal egg counts and coproculture, third-stage larvae were processed using ITS2 rDNA metabarcoding to determine the trichostrongylid species composition and screened for the 200Y (TTC > TAC) polymorphisms of the isotype-1 β-tubulin gene to assess possible benzimidazole resistance. Alpha diversity was determined for each herd using the Shannon and the inverse Simpson diversity Index. The Beta diversity was assessed between herds using the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity index. The three most common species found were Haemonchus placei, Ostertagia ostertagi, and Cooperia oncophora. The herds with the highest parasite species diversity were in Oklahoma, Iowa, and North Dakota. The herd in Colorado showed the least diverse parasite communities. Over the course of the two-year study, moderate to high GIN species diversity was observed across several of the conservation bison herds. Additionally, different parasite species proportions were determined between co-grazing bison and cattle herds. Lastly, low frequencies of the 200Y (TTC > TAC) polymorphisms, associated with benzimidazole resistance, were detected. Our study reveals differences in species diversity and richness among conservation bison herds across the United States and provides the first evidence of benzimidazole resistance markers in wild bison in North America.Keywords: Anthelmintic drug resistance, Gastrointestinal nematodes, North American bison, Species richness, Trichostrongyloidea, Wildlife health and conservation

Introduction

The American plains bison (Bison bison) is a keystone species of the North American prairie ecosystem and serves as a foundation of cultural identity for many Indigenous peoples (Knapp et al., 1999; Boyce et al., 2022; U.S. Department of the Interior, 2024; Oyler-McCance et al., 2024). Prior to the 19th century, there were an estimated 30–60 million bison in North America. However, due to extreme overhunting, North American bison were pushed to the brink of extinction, with less than 1000 plains bison estimated by the late 1880s (Hartway et al., 2020; U.S. Department of the Interior, 2024; Oyler-McCance et al., 2024). Small remnant herds remained in widely scattered geographic locations from which animals were gathered into foundation herds as recovery began, and from which modern conservation bison descend. Multiple translocation and augmentation events among these foundation and derived herds have occurred over the years (Hartway et al., 2020; Stroupe et al., 2022).

The significant decline of the continental bison population resulted in a bottleneck that affected not only the bison themselves but likely also the parasites that resided within them. In addition, historic efforts to crossbreed cattle and bison early in the recovery period, coupled with contemporary grazing of shared ranges, confound our understanding of species-specific parasite communities (Avramenko et al., 2018; Berezowski et al., 2018; Wiese et al., 2021).

Today, an estimated 500,000 bison live in North America (Avramenko et al., 2018; Hartway et al., 2020; National Bison Association, 2021; Stroupe et al., 2022). Bison and farmed cervids are both unique in that these species are commonly managed for two different purposes: conservation or production. Of the total estimated bison in North America,20,000 of these bison are managed in conservation herds (Oyler-McCance et al., 2024), and another 20,000 are managed through Tribes in the United States (U.S. Department of the Interior, 2024) under a variety of production and conservation models. However, the majority of the bison today are privately owned and managed under production models.

The Department of the Interior oversees around 11,000 wild bison in 18 herds across 12 states (National Park Service, 2021; U.S. Department of the Interior, 2024; Oyler-McCance et al., 2024). These wild conservation herds are minimally managed to facilitate the natural selection process and are captured and handled only as needed to maintain population objectives. Although routine anthelmintic administration during annual captures has been discontinued in most conservation herds for many years, some herds have previously been given medicated blocks distributed across the range ad libitum for the control of internal parasites (National Park Service, 2021; U.S. Department of the Interior, 2024).

North American bison have been recorded to share the gastrointestinal nematode (GIN) species that affect cattle and other domestic and wild ungulates. As early as 1923 Dr. Cameron in Alberta, Canada, found both Haemonchus sp. And Ostertagia ostertagi in the abomasum of slaughtered bison. He even went as far as to say that these parasites appear to be responsible for the same diseases as in cattle (Cameron, 1923). Since then, there have been numerous reports of trichostrongyle GIN of cattle in both conservation and commercial bison, often associated with clinical signs and mortality (Roudabush, 1936; Frick, 1951; Wade et al., 1979; Tessaro, 1989; Dies and Coupland, 2001; Avramenko et al., 2018; Barone et al., 2020; Wiese et al., 2021; Kipp et al., 2025). Although acute disease and mortality draw the most attention, the more subtle, chronic effects of GIN infections can be equally important in wild bison populations. Because persistent, low-level parasitism often goes unnoticed, it is easy to overlook the importance of parasite control. These GIN species can affect the health of bison by diverting nutrients away from the growth and maintenance of the animal, leading to reduced weight gain and decreased body condition. Along with that, infected animals can experience a decrease in foraging efficiency and GIN can compromised immune response, increasing the animals' susceptibility to other pathogens and environmental stressors (Stien et al., 2002; Barone et al., 2020; Koltz et al., 2022).

However, not all the original bison parasitic helminth fauna has been replaced with cattle parasites. While some parasites are indeed shared between bison and domestic livestock, emerging molecular research has revealed that bison have parasites that were previously overlooked or assumed to be the same as those found in cattle. For example, Danks et al. (2022) found that the lungworm species infecting the North American plains bison is genetically distinct from Dictyocaulus viviparus of cattle and from the Dictyocaulus species infecting European bison, which challenges earlier assumptions of bison and cattle sharing the same lungworm species (Chapin, 1925; Dikmans, 1936). Despite the rarity of finding ancient helminths, paleoparasitologists have also helped shed light on the ancient parasitic fauna of bison. A male and female worm were found in a mummified Bison priscus (Steppe bison) in Russia, dating from around 37,000 years old, and were originally described as the nematode genus Skrjabinagia paleobisonica (Shakhmatova, 1988). However, the parasite was later revised to Ostertagia bisonis (Chapin, 1925), and finally Orloffia bisonis (Dróżdż, 1995; Lichtenfels and Hoberg, 1993; Kuznetsov, 2011). This parasite likely traveled with the ancient Steppe bison roughly 10,000 years ago over the Bering Strait into North America, evolving alongside the bison (Hoberg et al., 2012). Today, wild conservation bison and a few commercial bison in Canada have been reported to carry O. bisonis within their herds (Boddicker and Hugghins, 1969; Tessaro, 1989; Avramenko et al., 2018), along with other wild North American ungulates, including mule deer (Odocoileus hemionus) and pronghorn (Antilocapra americana) (Boddicker and Hugghins, 1969). Though rarely reported, O. bisonis has also been reported in beef cattle in Canada in extremely low prevalence (0.3 %) (De Seram et al., 2022). Despite its ability to infect a wide range of ruminant hosts, O. bisonis appears to have more of a specificity for bison species, even in regions where multiple host species coexist.

The complex history of bison, including its drastic population decline in modern times, current cattle integration and range sharing, and human-mediated movement of animals among herds, makes understanding the factors underlying modern bison parasite species composition challenging. While many modern herds have occupied the same ranges since the 1960s, the parasite species composition of bison and other sympatric wild ungulates (e.g., mule deer, white-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus), and elk (Cervus canadensis) has only begun to be thoroughly explored in recent years with the use of molecular techniques like multiplex PCR and next-generation sequencing (Avramenko et al., 2018; Barone et al., 2020; Wiese et al., 2021; Johnson et al., 2022). These advanced molecular tools not only enhance our ability to identify and characterize the diversity of gastrointestinal nematodes present but also allow the detection of genetic markers associated with anthelmintic resistance, specifically to the benzimidazole drug class. Resistance to benzimidazoles is linked to specific single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the isotype-1 β-tubulin gene at codons 200Y (TTC > TAC), 167Y (TTC > TAC), 198 L (GAA > TTA), and 198 A (GAA > GCA) (Kwa et al., 1994, 1995; Gilleard, 2006; Avramenko et al., 2019). High-throughput molecular assays, such as deep-amplicon sequencing, can also detect these resistance-associated polymorphisms even when present at low frequencies. As a result, research in privately owned and captive bison has detected the presence of anthelmintic resistance in GIN, although it is still largely understudied (Avramenko et al., 2020; Springer et al., 2022; Kipp et al., 2025). Multiple studies in European captive bison (Bison bonasus) have also detected resistance to benzimidazoles and other anthelmintic classes (Springer et al., 2022; Galazka et al., 2023). However, to date, anthelmintic resistance has not been reported in wild conservation bison in North America.

The aim of this study is to investigate the parasite species diversity between six wild bison herds in six different states of the United States across two consecutive years using a next-generation sequencing metabarcoding technique, as well as to compare the parasite species compositions in a co-grazing longhorn cattle herd. Trichostrongyle GIN were also screened for benzimidazole resistance polymorphisms in the isotype-1 β-tubulin gene to investigate the frequency of benzimidazole drug resistance in wild conservation bison herds. We present one of the most comprehensive studies on the gastrointestinal nematode fauna of depth assessments of GIN species composition and diversity in wild bison herds in the United States, including assessing for the presence of anthelmintic resistance.

Building on these objectives, we hypothesize that historic population bottlenecks in North American bison may have led to reduced parasite species richness, while and that the ongoing ecological exchange of parasites with domestic cattle likely influenced the species composition within these herds. Additionally, although our current analysis focused on bison and cattle, the broader transmission ecology likely encompasses other sympatric wild ruminants – including deer and pronghorn – which may serve as reservoirs for parasite diversity and anthelmintic resistance alleles. Future research expanding molecular surveillance to these additional wild ungulates would help provide a more comprehensive understanding of the parasite transmission dynamics.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Individual fecal samples were collected in July and August of 2022 (N = 256) from six wild North American bison herds (1–6) across six different states (Colorado [CO-1], n = 30; Iowa [IA-2], n = 29; Nebraska [NE-3], n = 30; North Dakota [ND-4], n = 19; South Dakota [SD-5], n = 78; and Oklahoma [OK-6], n = 35; Fig. 1, Table 1). In 2023 (N = 180), additional sampling of these herds was conducted (CO-1, n = 30; IA-2, n = 30; NE-3, n = 30; ND-4, n = 20; and OK-6, n = 35) with the exception of the herd in South Dakota (SD-5; Fig. 1, Table 1). Personnel collected fecal samples non-invasively from the ground after slowly approaching the mixed-age bison groups, which include mature, juvenile, and calves. Once the herd moved away, freshly deposited fecal samples were opportunistically collected and individually placed into conical 50 mL centrifuge tubes. To preserve sample integrity, tubes were shipped with coolant packs, taking care to avoid freezing. Samples from a Texas Longhorn cattle herd (2022: 35; 2023: 35) that has shared range with the bison herd in Oklahoma (OK-6) for nearly a century were also collected in August and analyzed. The bison and longhorn cattle herd (OK-6) in Oklahoma were provided access to fenbendazole blocks distributed across the range ad libitum from 2008 to 2021 (L.C. Jones, personal communication), while blocks were periodically provided only during the growing season to the Iowa bison herd from 2008 to 2012.

The states in which the bison herds were sampled span a large portion of the North American Great Plains, which is characterized by large temperate grasslands with varied ecological subregions throughout. The study area encompasses several different ecoregions, which include South Central Semiarid Prairies (CO-1 and OK-6), Temperate Prairies (IA-2 and ND-4), and West-central Semiarid Prairies (NE-3 and SD-5). A map of sampling locations and associated Level II ecoregions is provided in Fig. 1. The study herds varied considerably in size, ranging from 22 to 800 bison across the six sampling locations. Similarly, the rangeland provided for each herd also varied substantially, from 600–59,000 acres. To account for such variation, stocking densities were used as bison per acre, ranging from 0.006 to 0.06 bison per acre (alternatively, roughly 1–6 bison per 100 acres). Additional details on specific herd sizes, locations, and rangeland area for each study site are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Fecal egg counts (FEC) for both bison and cattle were determined using a modified Wisconsin double-centrifugal flotation technique with Sheather's sucrose solution (specific gravity: 1.26) (Verocai et al., 2020; Zajac et al., 2021). For each FEC, 3 g of feces were used to give an estimated detection threshold of 0.33 eggs per gram (EPG) of feces. The remaining feces were mixed with vermiculite and cultured individually for 14 days at room temperature (∼20 °C) to allow hatching and development of the trichostrongyle nematodes. The recovered third-stage larvae (L3) from the fecal cultures were resuspended in a known volume of water. To estimate L3 concentration, the suspension was vortexed, and three separate aliquots of a fixed volume were taken. The number of L3 in each aliquot was counted and then averaged. Based on these larval estimates, fecal samples that contain approximately 2000 L3 per individual were prepared whenever possible. In instances where fewer larvae were available, all recovered larvae were collected and used (range = 50–2000). Pooled L3 were fixed in 70 % ethanol and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Molecular analysis

First round PCR for ITS-2 rDNA nemabiome metabarcoding

The species composition of GIN in each bison herd for both years was determined using ITS-2 nemabiome metabarcoding of L3 larvae using the Illumina Miseq platform as described in Avramenko et al. (2015) and Kipp et al. (2025). Successful larval cultures yielding sufficient L3 larvae (>100 L3s) for analysis were obtained from 291 of the 365 total bison samples collected (79.7 %) and 42 of 70 longhorn cattle samples (60.0 %) across the two years. The culture success rate varied by location and year, ranging from 23.0 to 100 %, with a lower success rate from samples collected from the Colorado bison herd (Supplementary Table 2). For the preparation of genomic DNA, ethanol-fixed larvae were washed in a lysis buffer by centrifugation before using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) for DNA extraction. After DNA extraction, the ITS-2 rDNA amplicon was PCR-amplified using previously published NC1 and NC2 primers in equal proportions (Avramenko et al., 2015). Following amplification, PCR products were purified using AMPure XP Magnetic Beads (ratio 1:1; Beckman Coulter, Inc.), following the manufacturer's recommended protocol.

First round PCR for isotype-1 β-tubulin deep-amplicon sequencing

The same larvae samples were also screened for the presence and frequency of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) in the isotype-1 β-tubulin gene that is associated with benzimidazole resistance, namely 200Y (TTC > TAC), 167Y (TTC > TAC), 198 L (GAA > TTA), and 198 A (GAA > GCA) (Avramenko et al., 2020; Kipp et al., 2025). The isotype-1 β-tubulin gene was PCR-amplified using a mix of 7 forward primers and 11 reverse primers that were previously published by Avramenko et al. (2019), and Q5 HotStart polymerase (New England Biolabs, Ipswish, MA, USA). The PCR product was then purified with the AMPure XP Magnetic Beads (ratio 1:1).

Illumina library preparation

For both ITS-2 rDNA and isotype-1 β-tubulin bead-purified amplicons, a second limited-cycle PCR amplification was completed for the addition of Illumina indices and P5/P7 sequencing tags. All samples were barcoded with Illuminas' Unique Dual Indexes (UDI) as previously described in Kipp et al. (2025). This consists of the addition of 384 unique 12bp indexes to both flanks of the amplicon. Purified products were then pooled (∼50 ng) to create a normalized sequencing library and quantified with the KAPA qPCR Library Quantification Kit (KAPA Biosytems, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) following the manufacturer's recommended protocol. An Illumina MiSeq Desktop Sequencer (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) using a 500-cycle pair-end reagent kit (MiSeq Reagent Kits v2, MS-103-2003) with the addition of a 25 % PhiX Control v3 (Illumina, FC-110-3001) was used to sequence the prepared pooled library at a concentration of 12.5 nM. An average (±SD) read depth of 21,265 ± 6595 (range = 6594–51,156) was obtained for each sample.

Bioinformatic and statistical analysis

Nemabiome ITS-2 bioinformatics

A bioinformatics pipeline based on the analysis package DADA2 R version 1.14.0 (Callahan et al., 2016) was used to process the raw data (described in detail, https://www.nemabiome.ca). In brief, raw data is demultiplexed, barcode indices are removed and fastq files for each sample are generated. The pipeline uses the program Cutadapt version 2.8 (Marcel., 2011) to remove primers and filter the reads based on size (>200bp) and quality (utilizing the filterAndTrim function), discarding reads with a maximum of two expected errors in the forward read or five in the reverse read. Sequencing errors are removed using a dynamic error learning approach called “denoising” and the forward and reverse reads are then merged. Possible chimeric sequences are identified and removed from the dataset. The resulting Amplified Sequence Variants (ASVs) are classified using IDTaxa (Murali et al., 2018) at a confidence threshold of 60 % against the nematode ITS-2 rDNA database 1.1.0 (Workentine et al., 2020). The ASVs were processed further to remove singletons, potential contaminants, and samples with less than 1000 reads and then converted into species proportions based on the total number of reads per sample. Two samples from 2022 and four from 2023 failed to yield sufficient reads for analysis and were therefore excluded.

Isotype-1 beta tubulin bioinformatics

A bioinformatic pipeline based on the analysis package DADA2 R version 1.14.0 (Callahan et al., 2016) was used to process the raw data in a similar approach to the ITS-2 pipeline; the reads were filtered, trimmed, denoised, and merged using default settings. The resulting ASVs were aligned against reference sequences (Avramenko et al., 2019) using a global (Needleman-Wunsch) pairwise alignment algorithm without end-gap penalties. Following alignment, the ASVs were discarded if they were <180 bp or >350 bp long, or if they had a percentage identity <70 % to any of the reference sequences in the database. The ASV was also removed if it had fewer than 200 reads in a sample and if the ASV was only present in a single sample. Additionally, samples with <1000 reads per sample were excluded from further analysis. After applying the read threshold filter, 242 samples remained for isotype-1 β-tubulin analysis across both years (2022: 171; 2023: 71). The codons 167, 198, and 200 were then analyzed for the presence of any variants resulting in non-synonymous changes. The frequency of each of the resistance polymorphisms for each species was estimated as a proportion of reads with the resistance polymorphisms out of the total number of mapped reads for a given species.

Statistical analysis of variables

The resulting data from the nemabiome was analyzed using the Statistical Analysis System (SAS; Version 9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA) to determine the prevalence for each GIN species and the three known benzimidazole resistance polymorphisms across herds and states. Other descriptive statistics were also calculated, including mean, standard deviation, and standard error for the relative abundance of each GIN species and fecal egg counts. Using Microsoft Excel (version 2402, Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) the alpha diversity of each herd was calculated using the Shannon index and inverse Simpson index (Shannon, 1948; Simpson, 1949). Beta diversity was also determined using the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity index (Bray and Curtis, 1957). To compare the Shannon index between herds, a Hutcheson t-test was calculated. To compare parasite diversity among the different bison herds, we utilized the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test. A Dunn's post hoc test was applied to identify specific pairwise differences between the herds. To address the issue of multiple comparisons, we applied the Bonferroni correction. The same method was used for determining significant differences in individual parasite species detected between the bison herds and longhorn cattle.

Results

Parasite herd-level prevalence and infection intensities

Fecal egg/oocyst counts were performed on individual bison from six conservation herds across six states (CO-1, IA-2, NE-3, ND-4, SD-5, OK-6) in 2022 and five herds in 2023 (CO-1, IA-2, NE-3, ND-4, OK-6). All parasites detected in the fecal samples were recorded and identified to the group or genus level, which included trichostrongyles, Trichuris, Moniezia, Nematodirus, Capillaria, Strongyloides, and Eimeria. Fecal egg count (FEC) data are summarized for both 2022 and 2023 in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4, respectively. Trichostrongyle nematodes had the highest overall prevalence in the sampled bison herds for both 2022 (93.7 %) and 2023 (94.5 %). The overall average (±SE) FEC of the trichostrongyles in the bison herds in 2022 was 51.1 ± 5.1 eggs per gram (EPG; range: 0–449.0) and 89.5 ± 13.7 (range: 0–1024) in 2023. Moniezia was the second most prevalent parasite observed in the bison herds for both 2022 (38.5 %) and 2023 (31.0 %), followed by Eimeria, which had prevalences of 24.0 % and 8.3 %, respectively (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Nematodirus had an overall prevalence of less than 10 % in both sampled years (2022: 5.0 %; 2023: 6.9 %), and Trichuris was lower (2022: 4.1 %; 2023: not observed). Despite a relatively low overall prevalence of Nematodirus and Trichuris among all study herds, six of the nine overall Trichuris samples were detected in the herd SD-5 (7.7 %). Nematodirus was observed in 11 individual samples overall, with five from herd NE-3 (16.7 %) and four from herd SD-5 (5.1 %) in 2022. Strongyloides were only detected in 2023 in one individual (3.4 %) in herd IA-2, which was also the only herd in which Capillaria was detected (2022: 6.9 %; 2023: 13.8 %). Toxocara vitulorum was not detected in any of the herds in either of the years' samples.

The trichostrongyle nematodes eggs were also the most prevalent in the co-grazing Texas Longhorn cattle herd for both years (2022: 100 %; 2023: 85.7 %), with an average (±SE) EPG in 2022 and 2023 of 249.9 ± 30.8 EPG and 2.8 ± 0.8 EPG, respectively. The co-grazing longhorn cattle were similar to the bison in that Moniezia was the second most prevalent (2022: 37.1 %; 2023: 11.4 %), with Trichuris and Eimeria only detected in 2022 and with less than 3 % of the sampled animals (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4).

The inverse Simpson diversity (1/D) index was calculated to determine the GIN species richness and evenness for each herd over the two years (Table 4), with higher values indicating greater diversity. Every herd, excluding herd CO-1 (1/D: 1.6), had moderate to high levels of alpha diversity (1/D: 3.9–7.2) in 2022. To estimate the species' abundance and richness, the Shannon alpha diversity index (H) was calculated, with higher values indicating greater diversity. Shannon index was highest in herd OK-6 for both the bison (2022: 2.1; 2023: 1.8) and co-grazing longhorn cattle (2022: 1.9; 2023: 1.7), as well as herd IA-2 (2022: 2.0; 2023: 1.6) (Table 4). Herd CO-1 was the only herd that showed an increase in the Shannon index (H: 0.9) in 2023 (Table 4). The parasite species diversity in herd CO-1 in 2022 was lower compared to all herds (p < 0.001), except for herd SD-5 (P = 0.06). This contrasts with what was observed in 2023, with herd CO-1 only being significantly lower in its species abundance and richness compared to herds OK-6 and IA-2 (p < 0.001). However, in 2023, the species diversities of bison and co-grazing cattle were similar to each other.

Comparison of beta diversity of trichostrongyle nematode communities by state

To understand the variation of GIN between herds, beta-diversity was calculated for each pair of herds using the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (BC) index. This index gives a measure of dissimilarity based on the GIN species counts, ranging from 0 to 1, with 0 indicating identical parasite species compositions and 1 indicating completely different species compositions. Fig. 3 shows the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity values between the six herds. In 2022, herds OK-6 and IA-2 were the most similar in their parasite species compositions (BC: 0.27), followed by herds NE-3 and ND-4 (BC: 0.35). All other bison herd comparisons have a Bray-Curtis dissimilarity value above 0.5 (Fig. 3). In 2023, herds OK-5 and IA-2 were again the most similar in their species compositions (BC: 0.25). However, herd ND-4 shared similar species compositions compared to bison in herds IA-2 (BC: 0.29) and OK-6 (BC: 0.37). When looking at the bison in herd OK-6 and the co-grazing longhorn cattle in 2022, the species compositions were very similar (BC: 0.19); however, in 2023, the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity index increased to 0.65, indicating that the species compositions were not as similar. Kruskal-Wallis test showed significant differences (P = 0.003) in Bray-Curtis Dissimilarity values between the herds in 2022, indicating that the diversity levels vary significantly across the herds. A post-hoc Dunn's test was performed to determine the level of significance for each pairwise analysis. Based on the Bonferroni correction, in 2022, herd CO-1 was significantly lower in GIN diversity compared to herds OK-6 (P = 0.0003) and IA-2 (P = 0.002). No other pairwise analyses were considered significant in 2022 or 2023.

Benzimidazole resistance polymorphisms in GIN

To determine the level of benzimidazole resistance, trichostrongyle L3s were screened for isotype-1 β-tubulin polymorphisms SNPs known to be associated with benzimidazole resistance at codons 200, 198, and 167 using deep amplicon sequencing. Two of the six bison herds (SD-5 and OK-6) were determined to have the 200Y (TTC > TAC) polymorphism. In 2022, herd SD-5 had one individual (1/75) that was determined to have the 200Y (TTC > TAC) polymorphism in O. ostertagi (relative frequency: 40.8 %; Table 5). The 200Y resistance polymorphisms were also detected in H. contortus (2/33; range: 0.1–0.4 %) in herd SD-5 in 2022. In the herd OK-6 in 2022 H. placei was determined to have the 200Y (TTC > TAC) polymorphism in both the bison (23/35; range: 2.4–8.4 %) and the co-grazing longhorn cattle (2/33; range: 2.7–5.4 %) with an average (±SE) relative frequency of 4.6 ± 0.4 % and 4.1 ± 1.3 %, respectively. However, in 2023 the 200Y (TTC > TAC) polymorphism was only detected in bison (4/19; range: 2.8–23.6 %) with an average (±SE) relative frequency of 9.7 ± 4.8 %.

Discussion

The North American bison is a keystone species with a rich and complex historical background. Many factors confound our understanding of the gastrointestinal nematode (GIN) fauna in wild conservation bison herds, including the historic population bottleneck, shared historic ranges with cattle, animal movements, and a wide range of management models over time. In this study, we describe trichostrongyle GIN species composition and diversity in six conservation herds and one co-grazing longhorn cattle herd using a next-generation sequencing nemabiome technique that targets the ITS-2 region of trichostrongyle nematodes. Additionally, GIN were screened for the presence of benzimidazole resistance polymorphisms in the isotype-1 β-tubulin gene.

Relative abundance of trichostrongyle nematode species in conservation bison herds

The nemabiome results demonstrated that the GIN fauna of conservation bison overlapped with parasites generally associated with cattle, as previously reported (Tessaro, 1989; Van Vureni and Scott, 1995; Marley et al., 1995; Dies and Coupland, 2001; Woodbury et al., 2014; Avramenko et al., 2018; Jesudoss Chelladurai et al., 2024; Kipp et al., 2025). These various nematode species within the superfamily Trichostrongyloidea seem to have adapted to parasitizing bison and have been reported in North American bison populations in Canada (Tessaro, 1989; Dies and Coupland, 2001; Woodbury et al., 2014; Avramenko et al., 2018) and the United States (Frick, 1951; Locker, 1953; Boddicker and Hugghins, 1969; Wade et al., 1979; Eljaki et al., 2016; Jesudoss Chelladurai et al., 2024). Although the European bison is a distinct species of bison, studies have similarly reported infections with many cattle and small ruminant-associated GIN species (Karbowiak et al., 2014; Galazka et al., 2023). Additionally, both the North American and European bison have historically experienced similar population bottleneck events, habitat fragmentation, and co-grazing or rotating with domestic livestock. The aforementioned types of GIN have relatively simple, direct life cycles, and many of which have a wide range of hosts including both domestic and wild ungulates (Boddicker and Hugghins, 1969; Hoberg et al., 2001). Wild bovids and cervids can act as reservoirs for GIN, facilitating the transfer of nematodes such as Haemonchus spp., O. ostertagi, Cooperia spp., and Trichostrongylus spp. Between other wildlife and domestic livestock (Boddicker and Hugghins, 1969; Hoberg et al., 2001; Barone et al., 2020; Beaumelle et al., 2021).

The most prevalent overall GIN species identified in the bison sampled were O. ostertagi (89 %), H. placei (86 %), and C. oncophora (80 %). Both O. ostertagi and Haemonchus spp. Were found in higher frequency and are of importance due to their high pathogenicity. Avramenko et al. (2018) conducted a similar study examining GIN species composition and diversity in conservation bison herds from Canada. Their findings were consistent with ours, with C. oncophora and O. ostertagi identified as the most abundant species. In the present study, H. placei was highly prevalent, with an overall abundance of 86 % across sampled bison. In contrast, Avramenko et al. (2018) observed that H. placei was generally less prevalent than O. ostertagi and C. oncophora in most bison herds; however, it was the most abundant species in five production groups – a pattern that challenges past assumptions that Haemonchus species predominantly occur in warmer, subtropical regions (Miller, 1993; Craig, 2003; Yazwinski and Tucker, 2006). Similar to Avramenko et al. (2018), multiple studies have reported different trichostrongyle species from geographical locations previously considered unsuitable, from temperate areas of the United States and Canada into boreal and sub-Arctic environments (Halvorsen et al., 1999; Mederos et al., 2010; Carlsson et al., 2012; Kutz et al., 2012; Hildreth and McKenzie, 2020). This includes bison herds in the present study who are in more northern states, such as South Dakota (SD-5), where H. placei reached a relative proportion of 64.7 %. This highlights the variability of Haemonchus prevalence among bison populations and underscores the potential impacts of environmental and climatic factors, geographic location, and herd management.

While our study centers on conservation bison herds, previous work by Jesudoss Chelladurai et al. (2024) conducted a study examining GIN species compositions and diversity in bison herds from 16 different commercial ranches in eight states of the United States (i.e., Colorado, Iowa, Kansas, Minnesota, Montana, Nebraska, North Dakota, and South Dakota), and found that C. oncophora was the most abundant species, followed by O. ostertagi, and H. placei. However, whereas this study reported low overall GIN diversity (Shannon index <1 for most herds), the present study found moderate to high levels of GIN diversity in the conservation bison herds, with the exception of herd CO-1. This variation is likely due to the differences in management practices; commercial herds often employ intensive anthelmintic use, which tends to lower GIN diversity, whereas conservation herds experience minimal intervention. Notably, both studies found C. oncophora to be a predominant species, with O. ostertagi and H. placei also present at high frequencies, though their relative proportions differed between the commercial and conservation bison herds. Recently, Kipp et al. (2025) also looked at GIN species compositions in commercial bison from Texas and Oklahoma, finding that H. placei was the predominant species in the two herds sampled in Oklahoma (relative frequency: 82 %). In the present study, H. placei was also the most abundant GIN species in the conservation bison herd OK-6, with a relative frequency of 45.2 % in 2022. Although H. placei was abundant in both settings, its relative frequency was lower in the conservation herds compared to the commercial herds.

Orloffia bisonis, thought to be a member of the original parasitic helminth fauna of bison, was detected in four of the sampled herds (IA-2, NE-3, ND-4, and SD-5). Although mostly only found in lower frequencies, NE-3 was significantly higher overall (47.9 %; p < 0.0001) compared to all other herds, including the ND-4 herd (9.5 %; P = 0.04), despite it being the herd with the second highest prevalence. Possible reasons for the detection of a higher prevalence of O. bisonis in herd NE-3 may be a lack of anthelmintic pressure, with three of the four herds that had O. bisonis detected having little to no recent history of routine use of a benzimidazole drug class. Additionally, O. bisonis, O. ostertagi, and Haemonchus spp. Are all abomasal nematodes and compete for nutrition and resources in the GI tract. This interspecific competition can influence both the abundance and the relative proportion of the species composition (Santos et al., 2020). Additionally, the dominance of species such as O. ostertagi and Haemonchus spp., which exhibit high reproductive output and strong establishment success within the abomasum, could limit the establishment or proliferation of O. bisonis. Avramenko et al. (2018) found that O. ostertagi and Haemonchus spp. Were the most abundant trichostrongyle nematodes in commercial bison and only found O. bisonis at low levels. The same study also observed conservation bison herds and found that O. bisonis was more predominant than what was detected in the commercial bison (Avramenko et al., 2018). For the case of the wild bison herd NE-3, reduction of dominant competitors due to ecological, management strategies, or host-related factors could have allowed O. bisonis to become more abundant (Avramenko et al., 2018). Although the life cycle of O. bisonis follows the typical direct development of other trichostrongyle nematodes, further research into its life cycle parameters, specific ecological traits, and interactions within the bison host is warranted.

Comparison of alpha and beta diversity of trichostrongyle nematode communities by state

Variations in the GIN richness and abundance between herds in different states over the two years were observed using the Shannon (H) and inverse Simpson Indexes (1/D). Overall, the species richness was highest in the herds sampled in 2022, with the exception of herd CO-1 (H: 0.56; 1/D: 1.6). CO-1 only detected two and three GIN species in 2022 and 2023, respectively, indicating a lack of diversity. This was further shown when comparing the Shannon diversity index using the Hutchinson t-test. The parasite diversity in herd CO-1 was considered significantly lower compared to all other herds (P = 0.001), except for herd SD-5 (P = 0.06). This reduced diversity may be explained, in part, by climatic, seasonality, and geographical differences between CO-1 and the other sampled herds in various states (Beck et al., 2015; Avramenko et al., 2018; Martins et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021). For instance, Beck et al. (2015) observed how humidity, precipitation, and temperature affected GIN species composition in cattle in Canada. Their study observed that the geographical differences of central Alberta, with higher humidity and moderate temperatures, had a greater parasite risk than samples collected from drier or cooler regions. Similarly, the CO-1 herd is located in an environment more arid and less hospitable to trichostrongyle GIN compared to areas that are more humid and subtropical. In addition to environmental factors, host-related variables could have also contributed to the lower parasite diversity in CO-1. This herd was also recently established on newly restored habitat, which may not yet support the environmental or ecological conditions needed for a more diverse parasite community to become established. Additionally, the process of reintroduction itself can affect the parasite communities, whereby only the subset of parasite species present in the founding bison is introduced to the new area, limiting initial parasite diversity (Herskind et al., 2023). Furthermore, CO-1 was managed at a lower bison density, which could also reduce opportunities for fecal-oral transmission of parasites. Together, these factors likely contributed to the low parasite prevalence and limited species richness observed in this herd. The herds with the highest species richness were IA-2 and OK-6, with nine GIN species detected within the bison herds. This also could be due to climatic and geographical differences that have environments more suitable for GIN development (Beck et al., 2015; Avramenko et al., 2018; Martins et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021).

Both the Shannon and inverse Simpson index values declined from 2022 to 2023, suggesting a possible reduction in the GIN species diversity between the two years, with the exception of CO-1. While these numerical shifts may reflect biological or ecological changes within herds, they must be interpreted with caution. Not only is it unlikely that the same individuals were resampled across the two years - due to opportunistic sampling and larger herd sizes – but sampling at only two time points is insufficient to capture long-term variability or patterns in parasitic diversity among the different conservation bison herds. Nevertheless, sampling across two consecutive years did impact the observed species richness, with certain GIN species undetected in one year but identified in the subsequent year. These differences in species richness between years within a herd are likely due to some animals not harboring specific parasites at the time of sampling, or to infections that were patent, resulting in missed detections despite the parasite's presence in the population. This highlights that repeated, multi-year sampling increases the likelihood of detecting parasites that are lower in prevalence or not shedding at the time of collection, thereby providing a more accurate picture of the true species richness within bison populations and allowing for more meaningful ecological conclusions. This is consistent with findings from Evans et al. (2021), who conducted a four-year surveillance on a Scottish sheep farm and found differences in alpha and beta diversity in GIN species composition between the years, showing the importance of multi-year sampling to track and detect certain parasites.

Another important consideration is the variation in environmental conditions between 2022 and 2023 within and across the different geographical locations. Annual fluctuations in weather, such as drought, excessive precipitation, and unusually cold winters can dramatically impact the development, survival, and transmission of GINs (Wang et al., 2021; Babayani et al., 2022; Janeczko et al., 2023). These localized weather events may also partially explain some of the observed shifts in parasite diversity between the two years, independent of biological processes within the herds. Despite observed reductions in numerical diversity values, a Mann-Whitney U test revealed no significant difference in the overall diversity between the two years.

The variation in the parasite diversity between the different herds among several US states was assessed with the Bray-Curtis Dissimilarity Index. The index ranges from 0 to 1, with 0 indicating that the herds have similar species compositions, whereas 1 indicates complete dissimilarity of GIN species compositions. The index results indicate that there is variation (BC range: 0.49–0.92) across the different conservation bison herds. We found that CO-1 was significantly different in the overall dissimilarity index compared to OK-6 (P = 0.0003) and IA-2 (P = 0.002) in 2022. Similarly, Jesudoss Chelladurai et al. (2024) used the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity index in commercial bison from eight states and reported considerable inter-herd variation, indicating herd-specific parasite community structures. Avramenko et al. (2018) also conducted beta diversity analyses and found differences in the trichostrongyle species compositions both among the commercial and conservation bison herds. The present study, along with results from Jesudoss Chelladurai et al. (2024) and Avramenko et al. (2018), demonstrates that although GIN species are shared among bison herds, the overall species composition remains distinct among the different herds – likely due to management, geographic, and environmental factors.

Relative abundance of trichostrongyle nematode species in co-grazing longhorn cattle

The wild bison herd OK-6 has been co-grazing with a longhorn cattle herd since 1927 (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, 2024). The additional sampling of the longhorn herd allowed a unique opportunity to investigate potential similarities and differences in species composition between two phylogenetically related large ruminants sharing the same pasture. It was found that the predominant species in the bison herd was H. placei, while the predominant species in the longhorn herd was O. ostertagi. The relative frequency of O. ostertagi in the co-grazing longhorn cattle herd (75.1 %) was significantly higher than the conservation bison (27.3 %) in 2022 (p < 0.00001). In contrast, the relative frequency of H. placei in the conservation bison (45.2 %) was significantly higher than the longhorn cattle herd (5.9 %; p < 0.00001). This striking difference suggests that although the bison and longhorn herds share the same trichostrongyle nematodes, the relative proportion of those species varies. One possible reason for this difference could be grazing behavior, with bison often grazing more selectively and more broadly across the landscape compared to cattle, which graze more uniformly (Allred et al., 2011; Kohl et al., 2013). Behavioral differences in distribution and density across the range could potentially drive such differences, as bison may range more widely than longhorn cattle (Allred et al., 2011; Kohl et al., 2013). Another factor could be different immune responses to parasites, and more species-specific parasites (Gasbarre et al., 2001). Additionally, bison and cattle may not consume anthelmintic blocks similarly, with some research showing that though bison will consume a salt/mineral anthelmintic block at similar rates as what is recommended for cattle, the timing and frequency differ due to the grazing patterns in bison (Hildreth et al., 2025). It should be noted that it was not necessarily surprising to find O. ostertagi as the predominant parasitic species in the longhorn cattle, with many reports showing that O. ostertagi is commonly predominant in mature cattle (Williams et al., 1987; Snyder, 1993; Stromberg et al., 2015; Avramenko et al., 2018). Kipp et al. (2025) reported a privately owned ranched bison herd that co-rotates in pastures grazed by cattle and observed that O. ostertagi was predominant in mature bison. This difference between wild and ranched bison could be attributed to the over-reliance on anthelmintics, causing resistance and parasite competition. Avramenko et al. (2017) looked at Canadian beef cattle herds and then bison (Avramenko et al., 2018). Despite the two studies not being conducted at the same time, they were sampled in the same regions using similar approaches and, therefore, compared the GIN diversity. They found that the bison herds had higher EPGs compared to the commercial cattle herds. Though this was not observed in the 2022 samples with bison (10.9 EPG) having the lower overall EPGs compared to the co-grazing cattle herd (249.9 EPG), it was seen when resampling the herds in 2023 (bison: 269.3 EPG; longhorn cattle: 2.8 EPG).

Benzimidazole resistance polymorphisms in GIN from wild bison and co-grazing cattle

Three of the six conservation herds screened have a previous history of use of anthelmintic drugs, including benzimidazoles, with one herd being offered anthelmintic blocks seasonally for a few years. Specifically, both the OK-6 bison and co-grazing longhorn cattle herds were given fenbendazole blocks ad libitum for 13 years, although this practice was discontinued a few years prior to the initiation of this study. Of the three herds with established benzimidazole treatment histories (IA-2, SD-5, OK-6), the 200Y (TTC > TAC) resistance-associated polymorphism was detected in both SD-5 and OK-6, but not IA-2. With the IA-2 herd only having limited, seasonal use of fenbendazole blocks for a few years. Although a benzimidazole drug class was used continuously for over ten years in OK-6, sampled bison and longhorn cattle exhibited resistance at low enough frequencies that there is minimal concern. The discontinuation of routine drug exposure and the ecological presence of untreated sympatric wild ruminants (e.g., white-tailed deer and pronghorn) likely allow for recovery of drug-susceptible trichostrongyle genotypes within these communities. Together, these factors help maintain susceptibility within parasite populations even after routine anthelmintic use (Brown et al., 2022).

Despite the lower frequencies detected, anthelmintic resistance has not been previously reported in wild conservation bison in North America. Avramenko et al. (2020) used a high-throughput molecular assay to screen both wild and commercial bison herds in Canada for benzimidazole resistance polymorphisms (200Y [TTC > TAC], 167Y [TTC > TAC], 198 L [GAA > TTA], and 198 A [GAA > GCA]) of the isotype-1 β-tubulin gene but detected no resistance-associated polymorphisms in the wild bison sampled. In contrast, Kipp et al. (2025) detected resistance association polymorphisms in every ranched bison herd sampled with moderate to high frequencies in O. ostertagi, H. contortus, and H. placei. These resistance levels of O. ostertagi in ranched bison herds surpassed even what was previously reported in cattle and should be of concern due to the risk of cross-species parasite transmission between cattle and bison, as well as small ruminants. Management of trichostrongyle infections in conservation bison herds should incorporate periodic resistance and efficacy monitoring as a core component. Although routine anthelmintic use is no longer recommended in wild bison herds, targeted intervention could be used when surveillance data indicate parasite burdens likely to affect herd health. Molecular screening for resistance-associated polymorphisms – especially in herds with previous anthelmintic exposure – will help determine early and low levels of resistance within the herds. It remains uncertain whether the observed benzimidazole resistance in bison gastrointestinal nematodes stems primarily from direct selection pressure due to the treatment of bison or from the influx of resistant nematode populations originating in cattle, which are subjected to more systematic anthelmintic use. Our results also cannot rule out cross-species transmission as a contributor to resistance levels, especially in mixed-species grazing.

Parasitism of trichostrongyle GIN is both common and widespread in North American plains bison, yet its full significance for wild ungulate populations is largely overlooked. Unlike in production animals, where impacts can be quantified through growth rates and economic metrics, the subtle, subclinical effects of parasitism in wild ungulates are much more challenging to detect and quantify. However, there is evidence that demonstrates that even at low levels, chronic infections can have consequences, influencing individual health, reproductive success, calf recruitment, and ultimately, population dynamics (Stien et al., 2002; Barone et al., 2020; Koltz et al., 2022). Although clinical disease from GIN infection has not been commonly reported in wild bison, our results suggest that variation in parasite species compositions, abundance, and diversity among herds may lead to varying levels of subclinical impact. Given the keystone status of bison in the prairie ecosystems and the pressures facing conservation herds, understanding parasite ecology is crucial. By characterizing trichostrongyle communities, revealing emerging drug resistance, and highlighting both herd- and region-specific patterns of infection, future management efforts can be better informed to implement timely surveillance and adaptive control strategies that protect the health of conservation bison populations.

Conclusion

The findings from this study revealed that O. ostertagi, H. placei, and C. oncophora were found to be the predominant parasite species in wild conservation bison herds sampled. However, these nematode species varied in their relative abundance among the sampled herds, with some herds exhibiting moderate to high species richness and diversity. Notably, differences in parasite communities were also observed between co-grazing bison and longhorn cattle, suggesting that host-specific behavior, immune responses, and management practices could influence parasite species compositions between ruminants sharing the same pasture. Additionally, low frequencies of benzimidazole resistance-associated polymorphisms were detected, with such markers only detected in herds with a history of anthelmintic use.

By employing advanced sequencing techniques, this study expands the understanding of parasite diversity and resistance patterns in wild bison. The patterns of parasite species diversity in bison are complex and can be attributed to various factors, including the historical origins of these herds, climatic conditions, geographical location, and herd management practices. Our study emphasizes the importance of screening wild conservation bison herds to address knowledge gaps regarding the prevalence, abundance, and diversity of different GIN species across states, thereby facilitating the effective and sustainable management of bison health and conservation.
