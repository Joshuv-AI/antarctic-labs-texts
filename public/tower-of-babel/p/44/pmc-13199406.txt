Anticancer potential of radioactively activated zinc: mechanisms and therapeutic applications
Authors: Ferdane Danışman Kalındemirtaş, Güneş Özen Eroğlu, Esra Nazlıgül, İshak Afşin Kariper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Zinc (Zn), an essential trace element, has been shown to exhibit antiproliferative effects at high concentrations and to serve various functions in human health. Numerous studies have been undertaken to enhance the efficacy of zinc. In this study, the therapeutic efficacy of zinc in terms of activation by ionizing radiation was investigated, and whether this would increase its efficacy was determined. In this study, Zn-65 was detected by exposing zinc to X-rays using a novel approach. The present study investigates the biological effects of low-dose gamma irradiation with Zn-65 compared with zinc alone on MCF-7 breast cancer cells. Human umbilical cord endothelial cells (HUVECs) were used as a standard cell control. The study investigated the biological effects of Zn-65 (IR-Zn) on MCF-7 breast cancer cells, comparing them with non-irradiated zinc, using human umbilical cord endothelial cells (HUVECs) as a control. Cytotoxic activity, cell proliferation, apoptosis, cell cycle progression, and drug resistance profiles were evaluated. Our findings show that IR-Zn significantly enhances anticancer activity by inducing premature cell cycle arrest at the G0/G1 phase, promoting apoptosis, and suppressing ABCG2-mediated drug resistance mechanisms. This is the first study to demonstrate that low doses of gamma-emitting Zn-65 can elicit potent, selective cytotoxicity against cancer cells without severely damaging normal cells. The findings indicate that weak gamma-emitting Zn-65 may be a viable targeted and low-toxicity radiopharmaceutical candidate for prospective oncological applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46220-7.

Introduction

Zinc (Zn) is a vital trace element that plays a fundamental role in numerous biological processes in the human body and ranks among the most prevalent trace metals, with a total body concentration of approximately 2–4 g1–3. Zn possesses many mechanisms of action in the organism, including growth, immune function, and cellular metabolism. In addition to enhancing cell-mediated immune function, zinc also functions as an antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agent4–6. Due to their low toxicity and potential as anticancer agents, zinc complexes are gaining heightened interest in medicinal chemistry. When evaluating zinc-based approaches, it is known that Zn²⁺ ions, Zn(II) molecular complexes, and zinc-based nanoparticles are distinct systems. Free Zn²⁺ ions play a greater role in physiological processes, but increased intracellular levels can lead to oxidative stress and cytotoxicity7,8. The biological impact of Zn(II) molecular complexes is primarily determined by the ligand employed9. At the same time, zinc-based nanoparticles exhibit distinct mechanisms due to their cellular uptake and surface characteristics10,11. Indeed, a significant body of research is underway to develop zinc-based cancer treatments that have been explored as alternatives or adjuvants to platinum-based therapies6.

Recently, an increasing number of studies have examined the anticancer properties of Zn. Studies have demonstrated that Zn can inhibit cancer cell proliferation and induce cell death by triggering apoptotic mechanisms. In particular, it has been reported that Zn(II) complexes may exert anticancer effects through various biological mechanisms, including inducing DNA damage, increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and regulating the expression of tumor suppressor genes. The anticancer functions of Zn biology have been described in the literature based on (i) oxidative stress response and redox balance, mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis, tumor suppressor responses, and transporter systems regulating drug response. Although zinc is a redox-inert metal, it can enhance antioxidant defense through mechanisms such as Cu/Zn-SOD activity, membrane stabilization, and upregulation of metallothionein (MT) expression; furthermore, the literature highlights the link between zinc signaling and stress/life-cycle regulators such as NF-κB and p5312. It is also known that zinc accumulation in the cell by the ZIP1 (SLC39A1) transporter is associated with prostate cancer; high zinc concentrations in cells often lead to mitochondrial apoptosis via cytochrome-c release13.

Futhermore, Zn deficiency has been demonstrated to increase oxidative DNA damage and lead to increased p53 expression14. In experimental models at the nanoparticle level, green-synthesized Zn-NP applications show antitumor effects, with improvements in increased caspase-3 and oxidative stress markers15. Based on the clinical and experimental literature on MDR, ATP-dependent ABC efflux transporters emerged in the context of this study, which may be particularly important for resistance development by effluxing anticancer drugs from cancer cells; therefore, this study addressed the ABCG2 axis on the resistance mechanism side. In a study, zinc Zn-NPs were prepared using a ZnCl₂ source by Garicus bisporus (edible mushroom) extract via green (biological) synthesis. The resulting nanoparticles have ZnO surfaces. TEM images of the Zn-NPs show a spherical morphology and average particle sizes of 12–17 nm. DLS measurements, however, revealed a hydrodynamic diameter of 50–100 nm attributed to surface coating15.

In another study by Gai et al., the cytotoxic effects of different newly synthesized Zn(II) complexes were investigated and reported to be effective against cancer cells. As a result of the study, Zn(II) complexes derived from 5-chloro-2-N-(2-quinolinmethylene) aminophenol derivatives exhibited practical anticancer activity in lung cancer cell lines. In the aforementioned study, the cytotoxicity of A549, MCF-7, HeLa, and cisplatin-resistant A549cisR cell lines was investigated using the MTT test. The IC₅₀ values ​​measured for the zinc complex were found to be 18.26 µM (A549), 23.61 µM (MCF-7), 22.75 µM (HeLa), 29.33 µM (A549cisR), and 33.96 µM (normal WI-38), respectively. The selectivity index of the Zn complex in normal cells compared to A549 cells was reported as 1.865,16. These findings indicate that Zn and its derivatives can be evaluated as potential therapeutic agents in cancer treatment. However, research in this field is still in its infancy, and further studies are needed to understand the anticancer mechanisms of Zn fully.

In the context of radiation therapy integrated with chemotherapy, it is significant that, unlike conventional radiotherapy, this modality can be administered either systemically or locoregionally, akin to pharmacological treatments or biologically targeted therapies rather than via external means. Consequently, cytotoxic radiation can directly affect neoplastic cells or their surrounding microenvironment or selectively target endogenous molecular entities. This form of radiation may be delivered using vehicles that accumulate via diverse physiological pathways, thereby facilitating a targeted therapeutic approach17,18.

This technique employs sophisticated delivery vehicles that allow radiation to accumulate at specific target sites. These tools enable the controlled release of radiation into the target tissue through physiological mechanisms. Thus, the treatment’s efficacy is increased while minimizing undesirable effects on surrounding tissues. In practice, the delivery of radiation using carrier systems or biological vectors enables it to be both integrated into the systemic circulation and concentrated in localized areas18–22.

Radioisotopes are utilized in clinical nuclear medicine applications for various purposes in diagnostic and therapeutic processes. In this context, the molybdenum-99/technetium-99 m (Mo-99/Tc-99 m) generator system is widely preferred in diagnostic imaging today and has become the de facto standard in clinical fields. The short physical half-life (~ 6 h) and low-energy gamma emission characteristics of Tc-99 m render this isotope highly suitable for imaging, however, it is not suitable for therapeutic applications to produce long-term or persistent cytotoxic effects at the cellular level. Consequently, the Mo-99/Tc-99 m system is classified as a diagnostic standard rather than a therapeutic radioisotope in international nuclear medicine guidelines18,23–27.

Similarly, in clinical practice, radioactive iodine isotopes (particularly I-123 and I-131) can be used for different indications. I-131 is used for targeted therapy in differentiated thyroid cancers based on beta emission, but this approach is preferred only in limited clinical scenarios because the isotope’s biodistribution is specific to thyroid tissue, the high dose required to treat tumors, and radiation exposure to surrounding tissues. In contrast, I-123 is primarily used for diagnostic imaging and cannot provide therapeutic continuity due to its short half-life18,23,28. International clinical reviews and guidelines published by the IAEA and SNMMI have emphasized that, along with the isotope’s half-life, the radiation type, tissue penetration depth, biodistribution of isotopes, and target specificities of radionuclides should be evaluated for appropriate therapeutic radionuclide selection. Currently, molybdenum and iodine-based approaches are essentially only narrowly selected treatments in diagnostic or clinical settings, and in this context, alternative radionuclides need to be evaluated for long-term, controlled therapeutic measures with a broad spectrum of cellular effects18,23–25,27,28.

In clinical practice, diverse therapeutic radionuclides, including I-131, ¹⁷⁷Lu, and ⁹⁰Y, are used for specific indications. Nonetheless, the Zn-65 isotope possesses a longer half-life than isotopes such as I-131, ¹⁷⁷Lu, and ⁹⁰Y. The Zn-65 isotope has a physical half-life of 244 days and a characteristic γ emission line. This suggests that the Zn-65 isotope may be effective in anticancer therapy as both a low-dose and controlled radiation source. This work illustrates the specificity of Zn-65, which, to date, has not been used as a therapeutic isotope in clinical practice (despite producing low levels of Zn-65 after X-ray irradiation). It demonstrates considerable biological effects on cytotoxicity, apoptosis, and the cell cycle in cancer cells in the cellular environment. The observed impact exhibits relative selectivity compared to normal cells. This demonstrates that the Zn-65 isotope holds significant potential for future research. Other aspects, such as biodistribution, targetability, and safety profile, are outside the scope and purpose of this study. In vivo pharmacokinetic and dosimetry studies related to the integration of the Zn-65 isotope into nanoparticle or complex systems will be the subject of future research.

Radiopharmaceutical therapy (RPT) represents a novel method for cancer treatment, providing a secure and efficient targeted therapeutic option. This sophisticated therapy can specifically attach to malignant cells, facilitating the systemic or localized delivery of radiation alongside intracellular anticancer agents29.

Because gamma rays—high-energy electromagnetic waves—can ionize radiation, they are frequently used in cancer treatment. This therapeutic approach eradicates malignant cells by damaging their DNA, preventing them from dividing and proliferating. Targeted radiotherapy techniques can employ gamma rays to kill tumorous tissues. Specifically, radioisotopes such as cobalt-60 use external beam radiation to target tumors by concentrating radiation on specific areas30,31. To reduce damage to nearby healthy tissues, gamma-emitting radioisotopes, such as iridium-192, are positioned within or adjacent to the tumor32.

A review of the literature has revealed no studies investigating whether Zn, activated by ionizing radiation and emitting weak γ-rays via the Zn-65 component, exhibits selective cytotoxicity in cancer cells and, if so, how this effect occurs, including through apoptosis/cell cycle arrest and multidrug resistance.

Limited research exists in the literature regarding the use of a radioisotope with a relatively long half-life as a direct cancer treatment. As discussed in this article, the use of an isotope that emits weak gamma rays, such as Zn-65, stands out as an innovative approach due to its potential to produce a practical cytotoxic effect on cancer cells with minimal damage to healthy cells. Viewed from this standpoint, the Zn-65 isotope may represent one of the first examples of its kind.

In this study, the cytotoxicity/selectivity profile of X-ray-activated zinc (Zn-65-based low-dose γ-effect) and unirradiated zinc in MCF-7 breast cancer cells (standard control: HUVEC), as well as apoptosis, cell cycle progression, and mechanisms of ABCG2-mediated drug resistance, were investigated.

Many therapeutic radioisotopes utilized in clinics include certain limitations, such as short physical half-lives, systemic toxicity, and radiation exposure to off-target tissues. Furthermore, most radioisotope therapies struggle to achieve sufficient selectivity between tumor cells and normal cells, limiting the dose. Therefore, developing novel approaches with longer half-lives, lower energy, and controlled radiation delivery is desirable. We evaluated the hypothesis that Zn-65, which is not currently used as an established therapeutic radioisotope in clinics, could exhibit selective biological activity in cancer cells, even at exceptionally low levels within the total sample, after X-ray irradiation. Specifically, the correlation between the gamma rays emitted by Zn-65 and the cytotoxicity, apoptosis, and cell cycle regulation of cancer cells in vitro, along with the impact on normal cells, was investigated.

Materials and methods

Radioactive irradiation

Zinc acetate 2 g (analytical grade, Sigma-Aldrich) was irradiated in a Petri dish. Irradiation was performed using a Varian Medical Systems (USA) linac device in clinical treatment mode, employing a 15 MeV photon beam. The Petri dish containing the sample was positioned isocentrically, perpendicular to the beam from the device. The source-to-sample distance (SSD) was set to 100 cm. The X-ray dose rate from the device was set to 600 MU/min. The specimen was irradiated with X-rays for 20 min to achieve a cumulative dosage of 120 Gy. The conversion of radiation dose to monitor units (MU) is determined according to the standard calibration procedure for clinical linear accelerators. The device is calibrated with reference conditions (100 cm source-isomer distance, 10 × 10 cm² field size, and reference depth), so that 1 MU = 1 cGy. The dosimetric protocol commonly used in clinical radiotherapy applications forms the basis of this calibration. In this context, irradiation at a dose rate of 600 MU/min is equivalent to applying 12,000 MU for a total of 20 min, which is equal to 120 Gy under reference conditions. The formulated MU–Gy conversion is practical for constant dose rates and typical clinical calibration conditions, and the same factors were considered in all irradiation experiments conducted in the study.

This modified the zinc isotopic ratios. The alterations in isotopic ratios were confirmed using ICP-MS, and gamma rays from Zn-65 were detected by activation analysis. In the supplementary material section, the amount of the Zn-65 isotope in the sample was calculated using the measured gamma rays and the requisite calculations. The Zn-65 isotope was determined by high-resolution HPGe gamma spectrometry. A solid zinc powder sample was placed into a sealed counting container. Analyses were performed in an HPGe system under lead shielding. The device was calibrated utilizing energy sources such as 241Am/137Cs and 60Co. The full-energy peak yield (ε(E)) was determined from the yield curve for each measurement geometry. Background spectroscopy was conducted under the same conditions, and net peak areas were calculated based on background peaks. The Zn-65 isotope determination was defined by a characteristic gamma line of 1115.5 keV. Consequently, activity calculation was performed using peak area yield and emission probability. The measured activity concentration is 205 ± 65 Bq kg⁻¹. This equates to 0.299 Bq Zn-65 activity, determined from the total sample mass (Supplementary Material). Zn-65 is not efficiently produced and since only trace amounts of gamma activity (~ 0.299 Bq) have been detected, this low dose cannot be interpreted as equivalent to a therapeutic radiation dose.

High atomic-number elements like tungsten are utilized to generate high-energy X-rays. The literature states that photonuclear (γ,n) events transpire during the interaction of high-energy photons with components of the X-ray generation source, such as the target, collimators, and flattening filter, at photon energies of 10 MeV and above. As a result of photonuclear events, photoneutrons are produced as secondary particles (The American Association of Physicists in Medicine33–35. In the present study, rather than a directly targeted nuclear transformation in the zinc sample after irradiation, these events were considered as indirect and low-probability physical processes accompanying high-energy photon exposure36. The specifics are discussed in the results section.

Cell culture studies

MCF-7 (human estrogen receptor–positive breast adenocarcinoma cell line; ATCC HTB-22), MDA-MB-231 (triple-negative breast adenocarcinoma cell line; ATCC HTB-26), and HUVEC (human umbilical vein endothelial cells; ATCC PCS-100-010), which are normal endothelial cells, were used in the study (MCF-7 cell line, originating from a female breast adenocarcinoma, was used as the model for estrogen receptor-positive breast adenocarcinoma). The cell lines utilized were procured from ATCC. The cells preserved in liquid nitrogen were kept in a 37 °C water bath briefly and transferred to a flask by adding 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma) containing medium and centrifuged, then moved to a flask by adding medium again and incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in an oven atmosphere. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) containing 100 units/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco 15140-122), Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich D2650), and 10% FBS was used as the culture medium. The medium was renewed two to three times weekly. When the cells were 70% or more confluent, passage was performed using trypsin (Gibco, USA).

Evaluation of cell viability by MTT assay

The MTT (3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay evaluates cytotoxicity by analyzing cellular viability and metabolism in vitro. This is a quantitative, colorimetric method37. Briefly, cryopreserved cells were rapidly thawed in a 37 °C water bath, diluted with medium, and centrifuged to eliminate cryoprotectant (≈ 200–300 × g, 5 min). Subsequently, they were resuspended in medium and transferred to flasks for incubation. Then stock solutions of the compounds (Zn and IR-Zn) were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (5 mg/mL) and diluted in medium to the desired concentrations. In the meantime, when the cells were 70–90% confluence, they were removed from the bottom of the flask using trypsin, stained with trypan blue, and the cell viability was determined by cell counting. 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well in 90 µL of medium. Concentration ranges (1–100 µg/mL) for both Zn and irradiated Zn (IR-Zn) were prepared by serial dilution from a stock solution. Intermediate concentrations (1, 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 µg/mL) were tested in all cytotoxicity assays for 48 h. At the end of the period, 10 µl of MTT (5 mg/ml) was added. MTT was dissolved in PBS, sterile filtered before use, and DMSO (typically 100 µL per well) was used to solubilize formazan crystals before absorbance measurement.

The absorbance of the wells in the plate was measured at 570 nm in an ELISA reader. Each concentration used in all MTT assays performed on cells was replicated in triplicate wells and validated in at least three independent experiments using separate cell cultures. Dose/response curves were prepared, and IC50 (concentration inhibiting the growth of 50% of the cells) of the compounds was calculated. AAT BioQuest, IC50 calculator was used to calculate IC50 values. In MTT assays, DMSO was used at a concentration of less than 1%. To ensure the reliability of the measurements, two types of control wells were included. Blank wells containing only culture medium and MTT reagent without cells were used to determine background absorbance, and these values were subtracted from all measurements. In addition, vehicle control wells containing cells treated only with DMSO at the same concentration used to dissolve the compounds (without the tested drug) were included. Cell viability was calculated relative to the vehicle control group, which was considered as 100% viability.

Apoptotic activity

Apoptotic activity was evaluated by flow cytometry employing Annexin V/PI (Beckman Coulter). Zn and irradiated Zn have been studied in MCF-7 cancer cells. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at approximately 2 × 10⁵ cells per well and treated with Zn or IR-Zn at their respective IC₅₀ concentrations for 48 h. After treatment, cells were collected, and about 1 × 10⁵ cells per sample were used for Annexin V-FITC/PI staining. 5 µl of Annexin V-FITC and 2.5 µl of PI (propidium iodide) were added to the cells, and the cells were incubated in the dark for 10 min. After that, a 400-microliter annexin binding buffer was added for reading. Annexin V-FITC/PI staining was performed according to established protocols38 and the manufacturer’s instructions (BD Biosciences). The Beckman Coulter Navios flow cytometry device was utilized for this analysis, and the parameters were changed to create an apoptosis-suitable template. The Kaluza software analysis program was used to record the data. Annexin V/PI was used to stain the MCF-7 cell line that had received Zn/IR-Zn therapy. Cells stained with annexin V/PI were identified using flow cytometry. Necrotic cells, living cells, early apoptotic cells, and late apoptotic cells were thus identified using a four-quadrant histogram. An Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (BD Biosciences, USA) and the provided annexin binding buffer were used to assess apoptosis. Experiments were independently repeated at least three times (biological replicates), with a minimum of 10,000 events acquired per sample. Fluorescence compensation was performed using single-stained controls. Gating was utilized to eliminate debris and cell doublets, followed by four-quadrant analysis to distinguish viable (Annexin V⁻/PI⁻), early apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻), late apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺), and necrotic (Annexin V⁻/PI⁺) cell populations. In flow cytometry analysis, (i) debris was excluded based on FSC/SSC, (ii) singlet populations were selected by excluding double cells with FSC-A/FSC-H (and/or SSC-A/SSC-H), (iii) fluorescence compensation was performed with single-stained controls, and (iv) viable (Annexin V⁻/PI⁻), early apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻), late apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺), and necrotic (Annexin V⁻/PI⁺) populations were delineated through four-quadrant analysis for Annexin V–FITC and PI in singlet cells. ≥10,000 events were collected in each sample (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Cell cycle analysis cycle analysis

MCF-7 cells were administered Zn and irradiated Zn at their corresponding IC₅₀ concentrations for 48 h. Post-treatment, approximately 1 × 10⁶ cells per sample were collected, washed twice with cold PBS, and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. The cell pellets were resuspended in 0.4 mL PBS and fixed by the gradual addition of 1.2 mL ice-cold absolute ethanol. Fixed cells were preserved overnight at − 20 °C according to the ethanol-based fixation protocol recommended by the Elabscience Cell Cycle Assay Kit (E-CK-A351). Absolute ethanol was employed as a fixation method to provide quick permeabilization and efficient DNA preservation for flow cytometric cell cycle analysis, in line with the commercial kit’s ethanol-based fixation principle. This fixation process enables rapid membrane permeabilization while still preserving DNA content for accurate PI staining outcomes.

Following fixation, cells were centrifuged, rinsed with PBS, and incubated with RNase A solution (Elabscience, USA) at 37 °C for 30 min, adhering to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Subsequently, cells were stained with propidium iodide (PI) solution (BD Biosciences, USA) and incubated at 2–8 °C for 30 min in the absence of light.

DNA content analysis with PI removed debris and aggregates with FSC/SSC; doublet discrimination was performed with FSC-A/FSC-H, and DNA histograms were generated from singlets. The G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases were modeled and reported using Kaluza (≥ 10,000 events/sample) (Supplementary Fig. 1). DNA content was analyzed using a Beckman Coulter Navios flow cytometer. At least 10,000 events were acquired for each sample. Debris and cell aggregates were excluded using FSC/SSC gating and doublet discrimination (FSC-A vs. FSC-H). The distribution of cells in G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases was determined using Kaluza Analysis software39. All experiments were independently repeated a minimum of three times.

Effects on drug resistance with ABCB1 and ABCG2

To detect P-gp (ABCB1) and ABCG2 expression, single-cell suspensions were treated with fluorescently conjugated antibodies. Pgp (CD243) was identified using a PE-conjugated antibody (Beckman Coulter, IM2370U) and 2 mL (total stock volume) of 1 × 10⁶ cells with a 1:20 dilution ratio. ABCG2 (CD338) was detected using a PE-conjugated antibody (BioLegend, 332008, 5D3). ABCG2 (CD338) PE 500 µL (100 tests) 1 × 10⁶ cells with a 1:20 dilution ratio.

The collected prepared cells were washed twice with PBS containing 0.1% BSA, and the supernatant was separated. 0.4 mL PBS was added to the pellet. 5 µl of ABCB1 (CD243) antibody and 5 µl of ABCG2 (CD338) antibody were added to each tube, and the tubes were incubated for 20 min in the dark at room temperature. For each sample (Untreated cell, Zn-treated cell, and irradiated Zn-treated cells), at least 10,000 cells were studied on the flow cytometry device. The Kaluza analysis program was uploaded to a pre-prepared template, and the data were analyzed and recorded. Appropriate isotype controls were used to establish threshold values ​​and account for autofluorescence. Cells were analyzed in a flow cytometer. To evaluate nonspecific binding and validate staining specificity, suitable isotype-matched control antibodies were added to single-stained samples for fluorescence compensation and gating in flow cytometric analysis. Based on this control strategy, separate unstained controls were not applied. This method guaranteed an accurate assessment of ABCB1 and ABCG2 expressions. In ABCB1 (CD243-PE) and ABCG2 (CD338-PE) staining, positivity thresholds in the PE channel were determined via isotype checks using singlet cells after exclusion of debris and doublet cells; ≥10,000 events were collected per sample (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Statistical justification

All experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates. IC50 values are presented as mean ± standard deviation (GraphPad Software, USA; https://www.graphpad.com). Statistical comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc analysis, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant (Beckman Coulter, USA; https://www.beckman.com/flow-cytometry/software/kaluza, https://www.aatbio.com/tools/ic50-calculator).

The radioactive isotope Zn-65 was identified through neutron activation analysis. According to the results, gamma-irradiated Zn-65 was detected at only 9.8 × 10− 16 g in 1.45688 g of zinc acetate (Supplementary Material). Notwithstanding these minimal amounts, measurable but limited biological effects, potentially causing cancer, have been observed in irradiated Zn samples. This finding is consistent with existing literature, which indicates that even low doses of gamma radiation can effectively induce cytotoxic effects in malignant cells by causing DNA damage and reducing their proliferation.

The gamma activity of Zn-65 in irradiated zinc acetate samples was quantitatively determined via HPGe spectrometry. Measurements were conducted in a system that had undergone the necessary energy and efficiency calibration; background counts were independently measured for spectrum analysis, and net peak areas were calculated after background subtraction. HPGe gamma spectrometry was optimized using the Currie technique, ISO 11,929, incorporating minimum detectable activity (MDA/MDL), detector efficiency, background count statistics, measurement times, and gamma emission probability. Based on the measurements and count times, the determined MDA value of Zn-65 was found to be approximately 0.1 Bq, and the limits of the detection measurement values ​​of the best-known isotopes were also obtained from a standard HPGe laboratory in Europe. According to previous data, a report analysis showed that the Zn-65 activity level was 205 ± 65 Bq/kg. For this sample, a mass of 1.45688 g yields approximately 0.299 Bq of total Zn-65 activity. Since the indicated value is slightly larger than the MDA threshold, this trace value is considered a significant value in determining the tracing. This was not considered as efficient and direct Zn-65 production in a clinical linear accelerator because the detected Zn-65 activity was exceptionally low, and gamma activity compatible with > 10 MeV photon energies was possible with low-probability, indirect photonuclear mechanisms such as clinical LINACs. The activity calculation is detailed in the Supplementary Materials.

The absence of Zn-65 detection in the natural Zn source does not mean that this isotope was utterly absent from the starting material. However, the study also showed that, even at significantly trace levels, it did not affect cancerous cells. The Zn-65 isotope signal emerges in the zinc sample after irradiation with high-energy X-rays, indicating that the irradiation conditions indirectly affect the material’s nuclear content. However, the direct contribution of this change in nuclear content to biological outcomes was not quantitatively demonstrated in this study. The literature confirms that, in this type of high-energy X-ray production, photon energies such as 15 MeV, photonuclear (γ,n) reactions can occur due to interactions of photons with the X-ray source components. The literature also describes this photonuclear reaction as creating a low-intensity secondary neutron field accompanying the photon beam33,40,41. These photonuclear reactions are considered to be unwanted but physically present byproducts of the interaction of high-energy photons with matter42,43.

ICP-MS analyses indicate stable isotope distributions, and radioactive isotopes like Zn-65 are not expected to be included in these results. Consequently, the presence of Zn-65 should be evaluated not from stable isotope percentages, but from radionuclide-specific measurements44. For this reason, this study did not aim to determine the Zn-65 formation mechanism quantitatively, but rather to detect gamma rays from the Zn-65 isotope in a zinc sample irradiated with high-energy X-rays and to correlate these gamma rays with biological effects.

It should be emphasized that targeted radiopharmaceutical approaches, particularly those used clinically, are not directly compared to the Zn system examined in this study. Unlike clinical radiopharmaceutical applications, the present work aims to evaluate the biological responses observed in the Zn system after exploratory irradiation18,21,23,45.

In our study, we also tested pure and irradiated Zn compounds on MCF-7, MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells, and normal HUVEC cells using the MTT assay. The MCF-7 cell line is now correctly described as an estrogen receptor–positive (ER⁺) human breast adenocarcinoma model, commonly used in hormone-responsive breast cancer research, rather than implying “gender-specific behavior. MDA-MB-231 cell line is described as a triple-negative (ER⁻/PR⁻/HER2⁻) human breast adenocarcinoma model, representing a more aggressive and hormone-independent breast cancer phenotype. HUVECs were selected as a non-tumorigenic endothelial model to evaluate off-target cytotoxicity and potential vascular toxicity, given that endothelial cells are a vital normal tissue affected during systemic and radiation-related anticancer therapies.

In MCF-7 cells, the IC50 value was 32.743 µg/ml for Zn and 26.858 µg/ml for irradiated Zn, while in HUVEC cells, the IC50 value was 39.700 µg/ml for Zn and 35.315 µg/ml for irradiated Zn. According to our results, the Zn compound killed half of the cancer cells at 32.743 µg/ml, while the irradiated Zn compound increased this effect by about 1.22-fold, reducing the IC50 value to 26.858 µg/ml. As for MDA-MB-231 cells, the IC50 value for pure Zn was 31.652 µg/ml, while it was 29.741 µg/ml for irradiated Zn (Fig. 1; Table 2). In conclusion, irradiated Zn increases cell death across all cell lines, particularly in MCF-7 cancer cells. All results are presented meticulously and repeatedly. The results of the present study demonstrate that, while the concentration of pure Zn compound acting on cancer cells is comparable to that acting on HUVEC cells, it is noteworthy that HUVEC cells maintain their viability to a significant extent at a concentration of 26.858 µg/ml, at which irradiated Zn compound is effective on MCF-7 cancer cells. Consequently, there is an observed decrease in side effects in healthy cells.

Zn-65-associated low-dose γ-emission must be taken into account when interpreting the approximately 1.2-fold decrease in IC50 observed after irradiation. This minor alteration points to radiation-mediated modulation of zinc-related cellular responses rather than severe cytotoxicity. These minor but repeatable alterations may still have biological significance, particularly for strategies meant to reduce off-target toxicity. Notably, the IC50 values for the MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines were comparable. This finding suggests that the cytotoxic reaction to irradiated Zn may represent a more universal cellular response mechanism rather than being significantly reliant on the subtype of breast cancer. The radiological contribution to the observed biological effects has not been directly quantitatively proven, and over-interpreting the potential therapeutic significance of Zn-65 under the described experimental conditions is incorrect.

Representative morphological alterations observed in MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, and HUVEC cells after treatment with Zn and irradiated Zn (Zn-IR) are shown in Fig. 2. Phase-contrast microscopy was used to qualitatively assess general cellular morphology after exposure to 10 and 50 µg/mL of the compounds. Compared with control cells, treated cancer cells exhibited observable morphological alterations such as cell rounding, shrinkage, and reduced adherence, which appeared more pronounced at the higher concentration. These morphological changes were more pronounced in the Zn-IR–treated groups compared to the cells treated solely with Zn.

In contrast, HUVEC cells showed comparatively milder morphological alterations under similar conditions. The microscopy findings are qualitative and provide representative visual observations that complement the quantitative cell viability results obtained from the MTT assay.

Fig. 3Apoptotic effects of Zn and IR-Zn on MCF-7 cells.

Apoptotic effects of Zn and IR-Zn on MCF-7 cells.

One-way ANOVA was used to assess the statistical significance between the treatment and control groups. Asterisks indicate significant differences compared with the control group (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). Each bar represents the mean ± SEM of three separate experiments (n = 3).

The apoptotic activity of Zn and IR-Zn was assessed at IC50 values after 48 h. In this study, MCF-7 cells were chosen because of their heightened sensitivity to IR-Zn. The apoptotic effects of pure Zn and IR-Zn on the MCF-7 cell line are displayed in Fig. 3 via flow cytometry, by analyzing AnnexinV/PI expression. The figure shows four quadrants: viable cells (lower left), cells in early apoptosis (lower right), cells in late apoptosis (upper right), and necrotic cells (upper left)46. According to Fig. 3, a significant finding is that both Zn and IR-Zn induce cell death through the apoptotic pathway, with necrosis being nearly absent. When MCF-7 cells were treated with Zn at the IC50 concentration, 13.4% of cells underwent early apoptosis and 33.09% late apoptosis, whereas 12.80% experienced early apoptosis and 39.24% late apoptosis when treated with IR-Zn at the same concentration (Fig. 3). These results indicate that both Zn and IR-Zn promote apoptosis in MCF-7 cells, with IR-Zn showing a more pronounced increase in apoptotic activity.

Fig. 4Cell cycle analysis of Zn and IR-Zn-treated MCF-7 cells by flow cytometry.

Cell cycle analysis of Zn and IR-Zn-treated MCF-7 cells by flow cytometry.

All experiments were performed using three independent biological replicates. Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons. P* < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The cell cycle distribution of MCF-7 cells was evaluated based on Zn and IR-Zn treatments. According to the study results, untreated control cells were observed at 56.77% in the G0/G1 phase, 15.22% in the S phase, and 28.02% in the G2/M phase. Zn treatment modified these rates, resulting in increased cell accumulation in the G0/G1 phase (59.35%) and decreased cell accumulation in the G2/M phase (23.67%). In contrast, a slight, insignificant increase in the S phase was observed (16.99%). Similarly, IR-Zn treatment resulted in a more pronounced decrease in the G2/M phase (22.26%) and an additional increase in G0/G1 accumulation (60.94%) compared with Zn treatment; the S-phase ratio (16.81%) remained essentially unchanged (Fig. 4). It increased the number of MCF-7 cells in the G0/G1 phase while decreasing the G2/M population, indicating G1 cell cycle arrest rather than S-phase blockade. The increase in the G0/G1 cell population, accompanied by a decrease in the G2/M phase, indicates G1 phase cell cycle arrest in MCF-7 cells following Zn and IR-Zn treatments. G0/G1 arrest is a well-documented response in MCF-7 breast cancer cells and reflects inhibition of cell cycle progression before entry into S phase47,48. The detected accumulation of cells in the G0/G1 phase is consistent with a cell cycle regulatory response that may involve p53-p21 signaling; however, this remains a hypothesis, as no direct molecular validation was performed in the present study14,47,48.

Fig. 5The effect of Zn and IR-Zn in the presence of drug resistance antibodies MDR1 and ABCG2 on the MCF7 cell line.

The effect of Zn and IR-Zn in the presence of drug resistance antibodies MDR1 and ABCG2 on the MCF7 cell line.

P-gp (MDR1/ABCB1) and Breast Cancer Resistance Protein (ABCG2, BCRP) are ATP-dependent transporters and are among the significant determinants of multidrug resistance (MDR) in cancer cells. These transporters expel chemotherapeutic agents from the cell, preventing their accumulation and thus reducing treatment efficacy49. Furthermore, these pumps are reported to be indirectly related not only to drug excretion but also to cell cycle regulation and apoptosis response. ABCG2 expression levels change during the G1/S transition of the cell cycle and are integrated with cell-survival mechanisms50. These properties make them crucial molecules for both drug resistance and the determination of cellular fate (proliferation or death).

Our findings show that Zn treatment dramatically increased P-gp expression, suggesting a cellular response to metal-induced stress. On the other hand, IR-Zn-treated cells significantly reduced ABCG2 expression (from 5.26% to 0.75%), even though they still had high P-gp levels (Fig. 5). Because ABCG2 was downregulated, the cell was probably less able to export harmful substances, which led to an intracellular buildup of stress signals and increased apoptotic activity. In the G0/G1 phase, when ABCG2 expression is known to be lowest, the IR-Zn-treated group also displayed a significant cell accumulation50. It is possible that the phase-specific inhibition of drug efflux and decreased proliferative signaling made the cells even more vulnerable to apoptotic stimuli. Significant suppression of ABCG2 by IR-Zn prevents cells from activating drug resistance mechanisms, thereby sensitizing the cell interior to therapy and increasing apoptotic activity. The combined effects of cell cycle arrest and drug resistance suppression facilitate apoptosis.

In this experiment, we demonstrated that apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and ABCG2 modulation were experimentally investigated, while DNA damage (i.e., γ-H2AX formation), ROS, and mitochondrial dysfunction were not directly measured. Accordingly, the mechanistic elucidation of DNA damage, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial pathways should be considered as inferences drawn from well-characterized biological processes in the literature, rather than direct experimental findings. Consequently, we regard the outcomes of this study as empirical findings, not as robust mechanisms providing mechanistic evidence, and solely as hypothesis formation based on in vitro observations. The validation of these mechanisms will be investigated in more comprehensive studies in the future using γ-H2AX, ROS levels, mitochondrial membrane potential, and gene/protein expression investigations.

The findings derived from this study can be interpreted as follows. The study does not suggest that the observed cellular responses are directly caused by trace-level gamma activity consistent with Zn-65. Conversely, gamma radiation-related DNA damage and oxidative stress mechanisms, as described in the literature, are discussed as possible processes that could explain the observed biological changes. Zn-65 may have triggered cell cycle arrest and apoptosis when DNA damage was severe51,52. In contrast to MCF-7 cells, normal endothelial cells have more effective DNA repair mechanisms. HUVEC cells rely on NHEJ (non-homologous end joining) and HR (homologous recombination) to repair Zn-65-induced DNA damage53,54. HUVEC cells may sustain less irreversible damage than MCF-7 cells, allowing them to survive radiation doses that kill cancer cells. Zn-65 gamma rays may have increased reactive oxygen species (ROS). Lipid peroxidation (membrane damage), protein oxidation (loss of function in key enzymes), and mitochondrial damage may trigger intrinsic apoptosis55,56. HUVEC expresses higher levels of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, catalase, glutathione peroxidase) that neutralize ROS57. This may have protected HUVECs from the same level of oxidative stress characteristic of MCF-7. Of course, more studies are needed to understand all these mechanisms.

The results indicate that trace levels of gamma activity consistent with Zn-65 are a secondary factor that should be considered in conjunction with the biological responses observed in the post-irradiation Zn system. These findings should be viewed as preliminary experimental results rather than claims of therapeutic efficacy. This strategy targets several cancer hallmarks simultaneously by efficiently inducing DNA damage, blocking cell cycle progression, downregulating key drug resistance transporters, and initiating apoptotic pathways. One substantial benefit of its localized nature is that it can lessen systemic toxicity while delivering strong cytotoxic effects to tumor tissue. Gamma radiation’s dual nature necessitates careful optimization of dosage and delivery, yet its diverse anticancer mechanisms make it a strong candidate for focused, next-generation radiotherapeutic strategies52,58. Therefore, optimizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse effects requires careful control and targeting radiation exposure.

Despite these promising results, further investigation is necessary to assess the safety and feasibility of using Zn-65 as a therapeutic gamma source. Factors such as its half-life, emission energy, and biodistribution must be thoroughly evaluated to determine potential risks and benefits in clinical applications. Additionally, comparative studies with conventional radiotherapy sources, such as Cobalt-60 and Cesium-137, are essential to assess the relative advantages and limitations of Zn-65. Currently, the only restriction we know of for Zn-65 is the stability of the isotope (in Table 3).

According to these results, the low amount of Zn-65 is a candidate to draw cancer research’s attention to the world of isotopes. These findings suggest that Zn-65, because of its unique emission characteristics, warrants further investigation as a potential radiotherapeutic isotope. The radiological contribution to the observed biological effects has not been directly quantitatively proven, and over-interpreting the potential therapeutic significance of Zn-65 under the described experimental conditions is incorrect.

The present study shows that the anticancer efficacy of IR-Zn-treated MCF-7 cells is superior to that of Zn, as evidenced by greater cytotoxicity in MTT assays. This discovery is significant in cell biology, as it demonstrates that early-phase cell cycle arrest (G0/G1) prepares the cell for activation via apoptosis. This preparation is accomplished through two mechanisms: preventing DNA repair and promoting mitochondrial sensitization. Apoptotic gene expression analysis was not performed in this study; yet, apoptosis was confirmed using morphological observations and Annexin V/PI staining. The lack of gene-level analysis is recognized as a limitation and should be addressed in studies.

Due to the extremely low gamma activity levels detected, consistent with Zn-65, it is practically impossible to perform experimentally and verify quantitatively equivalent external gamma irradiation at the Bq level under cell culture conditions. Research into the biological activity of these microdose levels must rely on specific gamma microsources, accurate dosimetry systems, and advanced radiobiological facilities. Consequently, the control of equivalent external gamma irradiation was excluded from the scope of this study. In this study, since gamma activity consistent with Zn-65 was detected at trace levels, e.g., on the order of Bq, it is possible to estimate the absorbed dose at the cellular level only through modeling, as is done for well-established internal radiation dosimetry approaches (MIRD framework and cellular microdosimetry), rather than directly measuring it.

Nonetheless, a prudent interpretation of the dose order is feasible here, as the cellular uptake fraction, cell-dependent activity, and cellular geometry were not measured in the present experiment. Given the total activity level detected, the dose absorbed at the cellular level due to gamma emission from Zn-65 is not considered comparable to the targeted doses in therapeutic radionuclide applications in the order of Gy. The conclusions of the study should be discussed as a pioneering in vitro framework for understanding the biological response that may exist after irradiation in the Zn system, as Zn-65 should not be considered as a radiation source “equivalent” to therapeutic radionuclides. Future studies may perform intracellular and extracellular radioactive dose assays and Monte Carlo-based cellular microdosimetry simulations.

Limited photoneutron generation at photon energies above 10 MeV has been observed in secondary photonuclear processes and clinical linear accelerators (LINACs). Such systems cannot consider the Zn-64 → Zn-65 conversion as a direct and efficient nuclear production process. The gamma signal detected in this study shows a trace-level component consistent with the characteristic emission energies of the Zn-65 isotope, which is consistent with low-probability and indirect nuclear interactions that may be encountered under clinical LINAC conditions. However, the results obtained do not demonstrate the clinical production of Zn-65; instead, trace-level gamma activity consistent with Zn-65 was detected. It is not currently accurate to say that the trace levels of gamma activity consistent with Zn-65 detected in this study played a direct causal role in the observed biological effects. The findings only point to the possibility of an additional or concomitant effect on biological responses occurring after irradiation, and further quantitative and mechanistic research is needed to demonstrate a direct contribution of Zn-65.

The roughly 1.2-fold decrease in IC₅₀ values ​​is statistically significant; nevertheless, it should be acknowledged that the effect is biologically limited and context-dependent. Future studies extending cell cycle analysis to additional cancer models will be necessary to determine the generalizability of these findings. Consequently, the study results represent an exploratory and hypothesis-generating in vitro framework rather than a strong or clinically proven anticancer effect.

Conclusion

This study aimed to investigate the biological impact of X-ray irradiated zinc on cancer cells in vitro and to monitor the presence of gamma activity at trace levels consistent with Zn-65. The results show that zinc increases cytotoxicity in cancer cells after irradiation and has no beneficial effect on normal cells. The detected signal consistent with Zn-65 is exceedingly low and should not be considered as direct or clinically significant isotope production. The observed increases in ROS, DNA damage markers, and changes in the cell cycle and apoptosis also suggest that biological responses may be shaped through mechanisms related to oxidative stress. Nonetheless, these results are preliminary, and the definitive contribution of Zn-65 to the experimental effects has not yet been demonstrated with the current data. Zn-65 is not efficiently produced and since only trace amounts of gamma activity (~ 0.299 Bq) have been detected, this low dose cannot be interpreted as equivalent to a therapeutic radiation dose. The radiological contribution to the observed biological effects has not been directly quantitatively proven, and over-interpreting the potential therapeutic significance of Zn-65 under the described experimental conditions is incorrect.

Ultimately, our study presents a non-clinical, in vitro experimental paradigm. The findings provide a basis for subsequent in vivo research, extended mechanistic analyses, and controlled dosimetry investigations.
