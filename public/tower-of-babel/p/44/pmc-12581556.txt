Pilot evaluation of optical genome mapping in chronic lymphocytic leukemia: complementing FISH analysis
Authors: Simge Erdem, Ayşe Gül Bayrak Tokaç, Aynur Aday, Dilan Karaca, Mehmet Burak Mutlu, Kıvanç Çefle, Mustafa Nuri Yenerel, Şükrü Öztürk, Sevgi Kalayoğlu Beşışık
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe clinical heterogeneity observed in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is largely attributed to diverse underlying genomic alterations. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) remains the standard cytogenetic technique but is limited to predefined loci. As a genome-wide approach, optical genome mapping (OGM) facilitates the identification of structural variants (SVs), such as copy number variations (CNVs), offering a broader genomic perspective. This study was designed to compare the findings of FISH and OGM in a cohort of CLL patients. By integrating these two cytogenetic approaches, we sought to evaluate the potential of OGM in detecting additional or cryptic genomic alterations that may impact prognosis and therapeutic decision-making.MethodsTwenty newly diagnosed or treatment-naive CLL patients were analyzed using both FISH and OGM. SVs, CNVs, and chromosomal abnormalities were compared across methods. Concordance and discordance were evaluated, and OGM-specific alterations were examined for clinical relevance. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0. Given the limited sample size (n=20), only descriptive statistics were applied. Frequencies and percentages were used to summarize categorical variables, while continuous variables were expressed as median and range.ResultsThe cohort had a median age of 61.5 years (range: 44–83), with 60% male. No abnormalities were detected by either method in 2 patients. Among the remaining 18 patients OGM revealed 22 SVs, 32 CNVs, and 8 aneuploidies. In 3 patients, FISH results were negative, whereas OGM identified various abnormalities.ConclusionsAccording to our results OGM identified additional chromosomal abnormalities not covered by the FISH panel in three of our patient cohort out of the five patients in whom FISH analysis had not detected any abnormalities, highlighting the potential to reshape prognostic algorithms in CLL. Our data emphasize the utility of OGM as a valuable adjunct to standard cytogenetic assessment.

Background

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) represents the most common form of leukemia in adults and involves the clonal proliferation of mature CD5-positive B lymphocytes in the peripheral blood, bone marrow, and lymphoid tissues. The clinical course of CLL is highly heterogeneous, ranging from indolent cases that require no treatment to aggressive cases with rapid progression. This variability in disease behavior is largely attributed to underlying genetic and cytogenetic abnormalities that influence patient prognosis and treatment decisions [1].

Genomic aberrations are key contributors to both the initiation and evolution of CLL. The most common cytogenetic abnormalities detected in CLL include deletions of 13q14.2–3 (del(13q)), 11q22.3 (del(11q)), and 17p13.1 (del(17p)), as well as trisomy 12. Among these, del(13q) is associated with a favorable prognosis, whereas del(11q) and del(17p) are linked to more aggressive disease and poor outcomes [2]. Assessment of TP53 and immunoglobulin heavy chain variable region (IGHV) mutational status, along with targeted cytogenetic profiling, is fundamental for determining disease prognosis and informing the clinical management of CLL [3]. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), which targets selected genetic alterations of prognostic significance, remains the primary method for cytogenetic analysis in CLL patients. However, FISH is limited to detecting only predefined loci and lacks the ability to uncover genome-wide aberrations, particularly cryptic or complex rearrangements that may carry prognostic significance [4].

In recent years, high-throughput techniques have revolutionized cancer diagnostics and have opened the path to personalized cancer medicine. Among these techniques, next-generation sequencing (NGS) allows the sequencing of target-specific genes, the whole genome, or the whole exome (WES) [5]. NGS has also contributed to characterizing the genomic structure of CLL. In addition to the chromosomal aberrations that have been identified in CLL, Landau et al. identified 44 recurrently mutated genes and 11 somatic copy number variations (CNVs) through WES analysis [6]. Similarly, structural variants (SVs), including CNVs, telomere length, mutational profiles, and overall genomic complexity, have significant prognostic relevance in CLL [7, 8].

Optical genome mapping (OGM) is an emerging genome-wide technique increasingly adopted in clinical genetics for the comprehensive detection of SVs—including translocations, insertions, inversions, deletions, duplications, and CNVs—as well as aneuploidies, in a single analytical step [9, 10]. Unlike sequencing-based approaches, OGM relies on direct labeling of ultrahigh-molecular-weight DNA molecules, offering a unique perspective that bridges the gap between sequencing-based methods and whole-chromosome analysis. This distinctive capability has led to its characterization as “next-generation cytogenetics” [9–11].

The clinical applicability of OGM has been investigated across a broad range of hematologic malignancies [11–17]. OGM has consistently demonstrated strong concordance with conventional cytogenetic methods in these studies. Recent evidence highlights its potential in CLL in capturing genomic complexity with greater resolution than standard approaches do. In the context of CLL, where structural complexity and genomic instability are increasingly recognized as prognostic indicators, OGM offers a promising platform for deeper characterization [18, 19].

This study was designed to compare the findings of FISH and OGM in a cohort of 20 newly diagnosed or previously untreated CLL patients. By integrating these two cytogenetic approaches, we sought to evaluate the potential of OGM in detecting additional or cryptic genomic alterations that may impact patient prognosis and therapeutic decision-making. Our findings will contribute to the accumulation of evidence supporting the application of OGM in hematology and its role in complementing or even replacing traditional cytogenetic techniques for more comprehensive genomic characterization. Given the limited number of patients, this study should be regarded as a pilot investigation, and the findings interpreted with appropriate caution.

Methods

Patients and samples

This study included 20 patients who were diagnosed with CLL according to the iwCLL guidelines and enrolled at the Division of Hematology, Department of Internal Medicine, Istanbul University Faculty of Medicine, between February and November 2024. The median age was 61.5 years (range: 44–83), 60% of whom were male. At diagnosis, 55% were classified as Binet stage A, and 25% were classified as Rai stage 0. The median follow-up duration of the patients was 56 months (range: 10–277). Three patients (15%) required treatment during follow-up (P-2, P-12, and P-19). All patients were either newly diagnosed or previously untreated at the time of sample collection. Peripheral blood samples were collected for both FISH and OGM analyses. The median time from diagnosis to sampling was 48 months (range:0–276 months). None of the patients had received cytotoxic therapy or had a history of other malignant diseases prior to cytogenetic testing. All patients provided written informed consent, and the study protocol, which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Istanbul University, Istanbul Faculty of Medicine (approval date: 11/11/2022, approval number: 20). This study was supported by the Scientific Research Projects Coordination Unit of Istanbul University (project number: 39464).

FISH

FISH analysis was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol via the CLL Panel Probe Set and interphase FISH (I-FISH) method. For each patient, 200 interphase nuclei were analyzed. Images were analyzed via the Isis Fluorescent Imaging System (Metasystems, Germany). The CLL Panel (Cytocell, UK) used for I-FISH analysis included: Alpha Satellite 12 Plus for trisomy 12, D13S319 Plus Deletion for 13q deletion and P53 (TP53)/ATM Combination for 17p/11q deletion, the immunoglobulin heavy chain gene (IGH) Plus Breakapart for IGH rearrangements (deletion/translocation) and MYB Deletion for 6q deletion (the MYB deletion probe targets both the MYB locus (6q23.3) and the centromeric region of chromosome 6 (D6Z1)).

OGM

DNA extraction was performed via the SP Blood and Cell Culture DNA Isolation Kit (Bionano, cat. no. 80030) on fresh 500–7500 µL EDTA-containing blood samples, following the manufacturer’s protocol (CG-00005_Rev.C_Bionano Prep SP-G2 Fresh Human Blood DNA Isolation Protocol). For OGM analysis, 2 mL of peripheral blood was collected in EDTA tubes. Cell counts were performed on TOMA slides within 48 h, and DNA extraction was initiated using 1.5 × 10⁶ cells. DNA labeling and staining were carried out via the Direct Label and Stain (DLS) Kit (Bionano, cat. no. 80005) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Bionano Prep Direct Label and Stain Protocol, 30206, Rev G). A volume of 8.5 µL of labeled gDNA solution with a concentration between 4 and 12 ng/µL was loaded onto the Saphyr chip and scanned via the Saphyr instrument (Bionano, San Diego, USA). Saphyr chips were run to reach a minimum yield of 1500 Gb. The rare variant and variant annotation pipeline was executed on Bionano Solve software v3.7, reporting, and direct visualization of the SVs was performed via Bionano Access v1.7.2. A minimum of five molecules was required for variant calling in the analyses. In our study, the resolution thresholds recommended by the platform for variant detection were applied, and only variants identified within these parameters were reported. SVs identified by OGM outside the loci covered by the FISH panel could not be confirmed by an alternative method.

Sample quality metrics were evaluated on the basis of molecule length, N50 of molecules ≥ 150 kb (recommended > 230 kb), map rate (recommended > 70%), and > 400X effective coverage (calculated with the map rate and reference genome size). Subsequent to variant calling, SV calls were compared with a database of known genes and a SVs database derived from an OGM control cohort. Variants were filtered using a 1% control database threshold (range: 0–2% across analyzing laboratories). Variants not detected in the control group were prioritized. All 20 samples passed quality control metrics, with an average N50 (> 150 kb) of 243 kb (range: 193–325), an average N50 (> 20 kb) of 193.5 kb (range: 110–284), a mapping rate of 86.35% (range: 72.7–89.4), and a label density of 15.17/100 kb (range: 12.64–16.66). All the cytogenomic findings were interpreted and reported in accordance with the 2024 International System for Human Cytogenomic Nomenclature (ISCN 2024).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed via IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0. Given the limited sample size (n = 20), only descriptive statistics were applied. Frequencies and percentages were used to summarize categorical variables, whereas continuous variables were expressed as medians and ranges.

Results

FISH results

No abnormalities in any of the analyzed regions were detected by FISH in 5 patients (25%). The 13q deletion was detected in 12 patients (60%); this deletion was biallelic in one patient and both biallelic and monoallelic in another patient, and monoallelic in the remaining patients. One patient had an IGH rearrangement (5%), two patients had monosomy (10%), and one patient had trisomy of the IGH locus (5%). In our FISH study performed with 12 alpha satellite probes, trisomy 12 was detected in 3 patients (15%). Notably, in patient P-16, trisomy was detected at varying rates for all regions analyzed. This patient’s findings were interpreted as consistent with a triploid karyotype. In the same patient, both trisomy and tetrasomy signals were observed for chromosome 12 alpha satellite probes, findings that are suggestive of an additional chromosome 12 within the triploid karyotype. The cutoff value for FISH analysis in our laboratory is 5%.

OGM results

SVs

No genetic abnormalities were identified by OGM in 2 of the 20 patients (10%). In the remaining 18 patients, a total of 22 SVs were identified (8 translocations, 12 intrachromosomal fusions, and 2 insertions), along with 32 CNVs (26 deletions and 6 duplications) and 8 aneuploidies.

Comparison of the FISH and OGM results

In five patients, FISH did not detect abnormalities in the analyzed loci. Notably, in three of these cases, OGM revealed alterations outside the FISH-targeted regions: P-11 (dup(5), t(2;10), t(10;14)), P-19 (t(6;6), del(6q), dup(2)), and P-20 (del(3q)) (Table 2).

Chromosome 12 abnormalities

For trisomy 12, there were inconsistencies between the FISH and OGM results. In patient P-12, FISH detected trisomy 12 at 8%, but OGM did not. In patient P-16, FISH analysis revealed two distinct cell clones, one with trisomy 12 (22%) and another with tetrasomy 12 (38%). In contrast, OGM findings were consistent with trisomy 12 only in this patient (Fig. 1). In contrast to FISH, OGM provided additional structural information regarding chromosome 12. A fusion involving chromosome 12 was detected in patient P-2, and a 24.9 Mb duplication on chromosome 12 was detected in patient P-17. In contrast, findings from patient P-6 were concordant across both techniques.

Chromosome 13 abnormalities

Consistent with the FISH results, OGM detected 13q14.2q14.3 deletions in 12 patients. In contrast to FISH analysis, OGM detected deletions with different breakpoints, intrachromosomal fusions, and translocations involving chromosome 13 (Table 3) [20–34]. OGM allowed for a more detailed characterization of abnormalities related to chromosome 13. The three deletions detected in P-3 were 13q14.13q14.2 (2.5 Mb), 13q14.2q14.3 (1.7 Mb), and 13q14.3 (1 Mb), and the two deletions detected in P-5 were 13q14.2 (552 kb) and 13q14.2q14.3 (998 kb). In 2 patients (P-5 and P-8), intrachromosomal fusions of 13q involved different breakpoints. P-5: fus(13;13)(q14.11;q14.2), P-8: fus(13;13)(q12.11;q21.33), fus(13;13)(q12.11;q13.3), fus(13;13)(q13.3;q14.2), fus(13;13)(q14.2;q21.31), fus(13;13)(q14.2;q21.33). Two translocations accompanied by chromosome 13 were also detected in P-5 (t(9;13)(q21.13;q14.11), t(9;13)(q21.13;q14.2)). These different abnormalities involving chromosome 13 for P-5 and P-8 indicated the presence of chromoanagenesis in these patients (Fig. 2). As illustrated in Fig. 3, chromosome 13 abnormalities in patients P-5 and P-8 are shown genome-wide, and in Fig. 4 the deletion of chromosome 13 in patient P-15 is displayed through both circos plot and genome map views.

Chromosome 14 abnormalities

OGM revealed the IGH rearrangement detected by FISH in P-3 as an IGH-BCL2 translocation (t(14;18)(q32.33;q21.33)). OGM provided additional prognostic information involving chromosome 14 in P-1, P-11, and P-17 patients in whom no rearrangement of the IGH locus was detected by FISH. OGM detected an intrachromosomal fusion of chromosome 14 in P-1 (fus(14;14)(q12;q13.2)). Translocations involving chromosome 14 in P-11 (t(10;14)(p11.22;q24.1)) and t(12;14)(p12.1;q23.2) in P-17 were also detected. In P-17, where monosomy of the IGH locus was detected via FISH (14q23.2q31.1), a deletion (14q23.2q32.33) of 43 Mb was detected via OGM. No IGH abnormality was detected by OGM, in P-12, in which monosomy of the IGH locus was detected at a rate of 8%, and in P-16, in which trisomy of the IGH locus was detected at a rate of 50% by FISH.

Chromosome 6, 11, and 17 abnormalities

For chromosome 6, FISH detected trisomy (32%) in patient P-16, which was not confirmed by OGM. In this patient, three signals were observed for both the MYB locus and the D6Z1 using the MYB deletion probe, consistent with trisomy 6. However, OGM identified a 20 Mb intrachromosomal fusion and deletion on chromosome 6 in patient P-19. With respect to chromosome 11, FISH detected trisomy of the ATM locus (52%) in P-16, whereas OGM failed to detect this alteration. Instead, OGM revealed a 595 kb duplication on chromosome 11 in patient P-12. For chromosome 17, FISH identified trisomy (52%) involving the TP53 locus in P-16, which was not detected by OGM.

Discussion

Genetic aberrations are among the most critical markers for determining CLL prognosis. Although FISH remains the standard cytogenetic method in clinical practice, its limited genomic coverage may lead to underdetection of relevant abnormalities. In our study, OGM provided an expanded view of structural variations, identifying additional findings beyond those detected by FISH. Our discussion focuses on previously identified CLL-associated genes that were affected by the abnormalities detected in this study.

The human leukocyte antigen (HLA) gene is affected by ins(6;?)(p22.1;?) detected in patient P-6, encoding major histocompatibility complex molecules that affect the immune response, has been reported to be associated with CLL [35]. The cell cycle checkpoint kinase 2 (CHEK2) gene affected by 22q11.22 deletion in patient P-9 is a tumor suppressor involved in the ATM-BRCA1-TP53 DNA damage response pathway [45].

Another gene was RAD51B, which is affected by t(10;14)(p11.22;q24.1) in patient P-11 and is involved in the cell cycle, DNA damage, apoptosis regulation, and chemoresistance. It has been suggested that it may be a suitable biomarker and targeted therapy for CLL [38, 39]. Although several genes, such as CHEK2 and RAD51B, were affected by structural variations in our cohort, their direct prognostic or therapeutic relevance in CLL remains to be clarified. These findings should be interpreted with caution until they are validated in larger, independent studies. Loss of chromosome X was found in patient P-7. Loss of sex chromosomes has been reported to be a clonal aberration in CLL that may be a part of the oncogenic process [36].

The frequency of abnormalities involving the 13q14.3 locus in various cancers draws attention to the existence of a tumor suppressor function localized to this site. Minimal deleted region (MDR), long 13q loss, and mono/biallelic deletion, which are also frequently observed in CLL, can be detected at initial onset or at later phases of disease development. No inactivating point mutation in one of the tumor suppressor genes was found in the remaining allele in monoallelic deletions. However, these genes are significantly downregulated in CLL cells compared with normal controls [22]. The 13q deletions range in size from 300 kb to 70 Mb. They are mainly divided into two groups: short deletions (not including RB1) and large deletions (including RB1), which are associated with genomic complexity and a more aggressive course [23]. Specifically, Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) microarray studies in CLL patients have reported deleted region of 13q ranging from 0.198 to 73.775 Mb [46]. A more recent study in the Turkish population reported a 13q deleted range of 0.34 to 28.81 Mb [47]. In our OGM analysis, the13q deletion range among our patients ranged from 0.87 to 3.538 Mb.

SPRYD7 (C13orf1), one of the genes affected by the 13q14.2q14.3 deletion found in patient P-1, encodes a protein containing a significant number of SPRY domains and has been found to play an important role in the regulation of innate immunity. It is located in the region of chromosome 13q14, which is commonly deleted in CLL but is not associated with the disease [20]. The MDR at locus 13q14 contains the DLEU1/2 long noncoding RNA and the miR-15a/miR-16-1 gene cluster [21]. Deletions of the DLEU1 and DLEU2 genes, which are tumor suppressor gene candidates, are observed in the early stage of CLL [28]. These miRNAs may lead to downregulation of the antiapoptotic effector BCL2 via posttranslational mRNA repression and consequently increase antiapoptotic resistance. Mutations that disrupt the normal expression of miR-15a/miR-16-1 have been reported in CLL patients. This deletion leads to the loss of miRNAs involved in the regulation of the apoptosis mechanism of B lymphocytes; therefore, this imbalance contributes to the immortality of B cells, leading to the development of CLL [21]. Deletion of the region containing the TRIM13, miR-3613, and KCNRG genes has been reported in CLL [23, 24]. DLEU7, a tumor suppressor gene localized on chromosome 13q14, inhibits the NF-kB signaling pathway via TRAFs (tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factors). Loss of DLEU7 in CLL activates the NF-kB signaling pathway via TRAFs. The NF-kB signaling pathway is important in the pathogenesis of CLL because of its roles in cell proliferation, survival, and inhibition of apoptosis [26].

In patient P-3, the RB1 gene was also deleted in the 13q region. This deletion is involved in the progression from the G1 to S phase, causes replicative stress, leading to genome instability due to DNA double-strand breaks. Haploinsufficiency of the RB1 gene leads to cell cycle-disrupting defects, such as altered centrosome number, defects in mitotic spindle assembly, and failure of cytokinesis [28]. In addition to MDRs, a 1 Mb genomic loss affecting SETDB2, PHF11, and RCBTB1 appears to contribute to CLL disease progression. SETDB2, a methyltransferase involved in chromosome condensation and segregation, is likely to be involved in the posttranslational control of P53. Deletion of other genes expressed in B and T cells, such as PHF1129, which controls the gene expression of NF-κB, and RCBTB1, which is involved in ubiquitination, may be involved in CLL development [48]. ARL11, a tumor suppressor gene, encodes a protein member of the Ras superfamily involved in apoptotic signaling and various regulatory pathways. Genetic alterations in this gene have been reported in many solid tumors and hematological cancers including CLL [30]. Like the RB1, RCBTB1, and SPRYD7 genes, KPNA3 is markedly downregulated in B-CLL lymphocytes with loss of an allele at 13q14.3 [31].

In our cohort, OGM detected complex rearrangements, including intra- and/or inter-chromosomal translocations, as well as CNVs affecting several chromosomes, which are consistent with chromoanagenesis in two patients (P5 and P8). Chromoanagenesis is typically characterised by a series of catastrophic events involving intricate chromosomal rearrangements and alterations in copy number states across one or more chromosomes [49]. However, the biological and clinical significance of these events in CLL remain unclear and need further investigation.

A comparison of the FISH and OGM results showed discordance for two patients (P-12 and P-16). Although OGM provided additional data to those obtained by FISH in these patients, it failed to detect some abnormalities. Trisomy 12 (8%) and monosomy of the IGH locus (8%) detected by FISH in patient P-12 were not detected via OGM. OGM cannot reliably identify abnormal cell clones present in less than 5% of the analyzed cell population [12]. This limitation is likely the main reason why certain low-frequency abnormalities detected by FISH could not be detected by OGM. Similarly, abnormalities such as tetrasomy 12 (38%), trisomies involving 13q, p53, ATM, and IGH loci (50–52%), and trisomy 6 (32%) detected by FISH in P-16 were not identified by OGM. In this patient, FISH revealed two cell clones with trisomy 12 (22%) and tetrasomy 12 (38%), whereas OGM detected only trisomy 12. The presence of trisomies at different frequencies across multiple loci suggests a possible triploid karyotype. This discrepancy likely reflects the limitation of OGM in detecting ploidy alterations and undersores the importance of standardization and cross-validation between technologies, especially when they are used for clinical decision-making.

Interestingly, in three of our CLL patients, abnormalities detected by FISH had a different prognostic impact than expected. P-2, P-12, and P-19 patients had indications for treatment at follow-up. In P-2, the 13q deletion, which is an indicator of favorable prognosis, was detected by FISH. In the OGM, an additional 22.5 Mb fus(3;3) fusion and 36.6 Mb fus(12;12) were detected. In P-12, FISH detected trisomy 12, an intermediate prognostic marker [50]; monoallelic and biallelic 13q deletions, good prognostic markers in the absence of additional abnormalities depending on the size of the affected region [51]; and monosomy of the IGH locus, a poor prognostic indicator [52]. The overexpression of the IL17D gene localized in the deletion region of 13q has previously been reported to be associated with the progression of CLL [33]. However, according to our OGM data in P-12, deletion of this gene was present. In addition to these abnormalities, approximately 0.6 Mb dup(11), as well as t(8;21) and t(9;10) translocations, were detected by OGM. In P-19, no abnormalities were detected by FISH. However, 20.3 Mb fus(6;6) and approximately 0.6 Mb dup(2) were detected by OGM. While the additional aberrations detected by OGM in these patients have not been previously associated with CLL, their presence raises important questions regarding their potential role in disease progression. These cases illustrate how OGM may uncover additional alterations with potential prognostic relevance, which may otherwise be missed via conventional methods alone. However, owing to the limited patient number and lack of functional correlation, caution is warranted in attributing prognostic weight to these findings.

In CLL, OGM provides genome-wide information beyond the loci analyzed by FISH, enabling broader characterization of known abnormalities as well as novel variants and complex events such as chromoanagenesis. Although OGM can detect SVs at allele fractions of ~ 5% and copy number changes at 10–15%, it cannot yet replace conventional methods. In particular, because prognostically important TP53 deletions may be missed in low-level mosaicism, FISH remains essential for TP53 analysis [53]. In addition structural abnormalities involving repetitive regions or centromeric and telomeric regions and ploidy alterations (e.g., triploidy, tetraploidy) cannot be detected by OGM method [54]. Alternative approaches such as combined targeted DNA/RNA sequencing with SNP arrays currently provide more detailed and cost-effective oncogenomic assessments. As also reported by Akkari et al., this combined strategy represents a powerful alternative for comprehensive oncogenomic profiling in hematological malignancies, including CLL [55].

Our study has several limitations. Although our findings are informative, the relatively small number of patients included may impact the external validity of our observations. Due to budgetary constraints, the abnormalities identified by OGM could not be further confirmed using alternative methods such as array or FISH. A limitation of our study is that conventional chromosome analysis is not routinely performed in CLL unless treatment is indicated, and since interphase FISH is the gold-standard test, OGM findings could not be validated by additional methods. The clinical relevance of several identified SVs remains uncertain because of the absence of functional validation or outcome correlation. There is a need for further prospective studies integrating clinical and molecular analyses.

Conclusions

FISH remains the current standard cytogenetic tool in CLL, offering a cost-effective and widely accessible method for detecting recurrent chromosomal abnormalities with established prognostic value. However, as a targeted technique, FISH lacks the ability to identify complex or novel genomic aberrations. While OGM provides a more comprehensive genomic landscape with higher resolution, it requires specialized equipment, higher costs, and advanced data interpretation, which may currently limit its widespread use in routine clinical practice. Both methods have strengths and limitations. In our study, OGM identified additional chromosomal abnormalities not covered by the FISH panel in three of our patient cohort out of the five patients in whom FISH analysis had not detected any abnormalities. Moreover, among the six patients in whom no additional abnormalities were identified by OGM, none developed an indication for treatment. Importantly, none of these patients presented with advanced-stage disease, as defined by Rai stages 3–4 or Binet stage C. In two patients who required treatment, del(13q) (traditionally associated with favorable prognosis) was detected by FISH. This observation suggests that FISH alone may be insufficient for accurate prognostic assessment in certain cases. These findings support the potential utility of OGM as a complementary tool in the cytogenetic evaluation of CLL. While OGM provides valuable complementary information beyond conventional FISH by uncovering novel structural variants and complex rearrangements, it is not yet ready for routine clinical use in CLL. Further prospective, large-scale studies integrating clinical and molecular data are needed to validate its prognostic significance and define its role in future algorithms.
