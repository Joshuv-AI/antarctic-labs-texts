LncRNA-CTD suppresses metastasis and immune evasion by modulating snail1 and MHC-I expression in colorectal cancer
Authors: Ning Xu, Huisi Qiu, Yuezhang Sun, Hong Jin, Siyu Yan, Fangmei Zhang, Ying Song, Guopei Zheng, Dongjiang Liao, Xiaoli Wei, Liyun Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDistant metastasis and immune evasion are the major obstacles for successful colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment. The link between metastasis and immune evasion, as well as their therapeutic significance, remains unclear.MethodsLong non-coding RNAs from six paired CRC and normal tissues were screened by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). LncRNA-CTD (CTD-2568A17.8) expression levels were determined using in situ hybridization and quantitative PCR analysis. In vitro and in vivo assays were performed to confirm the function of lncRNA-CTD. Flow cytometry was used to analyze the impact of lncRNA-CTD on immune cell infiltration and T-cell function. RNA-seq combined with RNA pull-down and RNA immunoprecipitation assay was used to identify the changes in downstream molecules induced by lncRNA-CTD. The therapeutic value of the combination of lncRNA-CTD and immune checkpoint inhibitors has been evaluated.ResultsIn this study, we identified a novel long non-coding RNA, lncRNA-CTD, which is downregulated in CRC and correlates with both metastasis and immunotherapy response. Mechanistically, the interaction of lncRNA and smad2 prevented the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of smad2, which inhibited the expression of snail1, thereby inhibiting the metastasis of CRC. LncRNA-CTD enhances major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) expression on the cancer cell membrane by interacting with STUB1 to disrupt the interaction of STUB1 with the MHC-I activator NLRC5 and subsequent NLRC5 ubiquitination-mediated degradation, increasing the susceptibility of CRC cells to being killed by CD8+ T cells. TFAP4 overexpression in CRC cells caused lncRNA-CTD downregulation. Moreover, the combination of lncRNA-CTD gene delivery therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors exerted an additive effect on tumor growth inhibition.ConclusionsCollectively, our study reveals the role and mechanism of lncRNA-CTD in CRC metastasis and immune evasion. Overexpression of lncRNA-CTD suppresses CRC metastasis and improves the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors.Cite NowKeywords: Colorectal Cancer, Immunotherapy, MHC class I-related chain A, T cell

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most diagnosed cancer type worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer death.1 There will be about 152,810 new cases and 53,010 deaths in the USA in 2024.2 The landscape of multiple approaches for cancer treatment has evolved apace, as our molecular insight into cancer has deepened.3 Early-stage CRC can be cured by surgery with or without adjuvant chemotherapy, and systemic chemotherapy has been the primary treatment method used to prolong patient survival; however, the survival rate of patients with metastatic CRC (mCRC) remains less than 15%.4,6 Recent studies have shown that immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has been proven to be clinically efficient in mCRC treatment.7 8 However, shortcomings in improving the clinical outcomes and survival of patients with mCRC remain. Therefore, it is imperative and urgent to elucidate the molecular mechanism and develop key therapeutic targets for the effective treatment of CRC.

It is now well known that non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) are widely appreciated as pervasive regulators of multiple cancer. Recent studies have shown that ncRNAs play a major role in CRC progression by rewiring essential signaling pathways. SP1-induced circ_0017552 was originated from NET1 and regulated NET1 expression via sponging miR-22–3 p, thus facilitating colon cancer cell proliferation and invasion.9 Long ncRNAs (lncRNAs) form a class of highly multifunctional noncoding RNAs that consist of more than 200 nucleotides in length. LncRNA dysregulation has been observed in tumorigenesis, metastasis, immune evasion, chemoresistance and angiogenesis.10,14 The fundamental roles of several lncRNAs in the occurrence and development of tumors have been reported. lncRNA FOXD2-AS1 targets the PLOD1/Akt/mTOR pathway by sponging miR-185–5 p to promotes OSCC (oral squamous cell carcinoma) growth and migration.15 RP11-417E7.1 activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling by regulating thrombospondin2 expression to promote CRC metastasis.16 DLGAP1-AS2 promotes CRC tumorigenesis and metastasis by interacting with ElonginA and inhibiting its protein stability by promoting Trim21-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of ELOA.17 However, the possible molecular regulatory mechanisms and precise roles of lncRNAs in mCRC still need to be characterized.

Major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) is also termed the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) in humans, and classical type I HLA molecules include HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C, which present tumor-specific antigens on the cell surface for recognition by CD8+ T cells via the T-cell receptor (TCR).18,20 The MHC-I-mediated antigenic peptide presentation pathway is the predominant initiating factor for anticancer immunity. The MHC-I expression level in CRC is frequently downregulated, which determines the effectiveness of CRC immunotherapies.21 The loss or downregulation of MHC-I is one of the common mechanisms of immune evasion and resistance to ICB therapy.22 The expression of MHC-I molecules is modulated by multiple mechanisms; at the level of genetic transcription, the major regulator of MHC-I genes is the Nod-like receptor (NLR) family CARD domain containing 5 (NLRC5). NLRC5 is an interferon (IFN)-γ-inducible nuclear protein that specifically associates with and activates the promoters of MHC-I genes by generating an MHC class I transactivator enhanceosome complex with other transcription factors.23 The overexpression of NLRC5 can increase the messenger RNA (mRNA) levels of MHC-I genes and contribute to tumor antigen presentation to CD8+ T cells, which further increases antitumor immunity.24 25 In brain metastases (BrMs), Cdk5 suppresses MHC-I expression on the cancer cell membrane through the Stat1-Nlrc5 axis, enabling BrMs to avoid recognition by CD8+ T cells.26 Evidence suggests that the NLRC5-mediated MHC-I antigen presentation pathway is important for tumor immunotherapy.

In this study, we discovered an uncharacterized lncRNA, lncRNA-CTD, that is significantly downregulated in CRC and is correlated with a poor prognosis. Moreover, we found that lncRNA-CTD inhibited CRC cell invasion and metastasis by interacting with smad2 to repress snail1 expression. Furthermore, lncRNA-CTD directly binds with STUB1, increases NLRC5/MHC-I expression, and enhances CD8+ T-cell-dependent immunotherapies. We also found that TFAP4, a transcription factor, decreased the expression level of lncRNA-CTD in CRC cells. In conclusion, our study demonstrated that lncRNA-CTD combination immunotherapy could serve as a potential therapeutic strategy for the treatment of metastatic CRC.

Materials and methods

Human tissue specimens

Human CRC specimens that included cancer and adjacent normal tissue were obtained from patients presenting a pathological diagnosis of colorectal adenocarcinoma at the Guangzhou Institute of Cancer Research, the Affiliated Cancer Hospital, Guangzhou Medical University.

Cell lines

FHC, RKO, HCT116, HT29, SW480, DLD1, SW620, LOVO, HCT8 and CT26 cells line were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, Virginia, USA). All cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Roswell Park Memorial Institute, VivaCell, Germany) supplemented with 10% FBS (Fetal Bovine Serum, VivaCell, Germany) and maintained in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator. Cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination every 6 months. The cell culture supernatant was collected and subjected to PCR amplification targeting mycoplasma-specific DNA sequences using primers as follows: forward primer: 5'- GGGAGCAAACAGGATTAGATACCCT −3', reverse primer: 5'- TGCACCATCTGTCACTCTGTTAACCTC −3'.

Lentivirus packaging and infection

Lentiviral vectors encoding lncRNA-CTD, snail1, and TFAP4, as well as control constructs, were used. STUB1 shRNA (sh-STUB1), NLRC5 shRNA (sh-NLRC5) and control short hairpin RNA (sh-Con) were purchased from Genechem (Shanghai, China). The Virus Packaging Kit (Genechem, Shanghai, China) was used for packaging of virus. The virus containing plasmids infected the adherent CRC cells at 20–30% confluency in 12-well culture plates.

CRISPR-Cas9 for gene knockout

Gene knockout used the lentivirus-mediated CRISPR-Cas9 technology (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats-CRISPR associated protein 9, Genechem, Shanghai, China) for human TFAP4. SgRNA (Single guide RNA) sequences targeting TFAP4 were as follows: 1#, CCACATCAATGTCGTCACCA; 2#, GCGTCTCCGCTCGTTGCTGT; 3#, AGATCGCCAACAGCAACGAG. CRC cells were infected with sgRNA-encoding CRISPR lentivirus. After selection with puromycin and EGFP (Enhanced Green Fluorescent Protein) positive cells, the efficiency of gene knockout was examined by sequencing each cell clone or verification of protein levels through western blotting. Cell clone was subjected to BGI Genomics for Sanger sequencing, and subsequent experiments were performed.

Western blot

Western blot analysis was performed as in previous studies.27 28 Cultured cells were lysed by RIPA Buffer (Radioimmunoprecipitation assay, Thermo Scientific, USA) with PMSF (100×) (Phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, Thermo Scientific, USA). The protein concentration was measured with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA). Proteins were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE (sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Merck Millipore, Schwalbach, Germany). After blocking by 5% skim milk, proteins were then probed with specific antibodies followed by secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The HRP signal was developed by electrochemiluminescence (Tanon, China). The specific antibodies used were anti-smad2 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 5339), anti-p-smad2 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 18338), anti-smad3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 9523), anti-p-smad3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 9520), anti-snail1 (1:1000, Santa Cruz, sc-271977), anti-NLRC5 (1:1000, Santa, sc-515668), anti-STUB1 (1:10000, Abcam, ab134064), anti-TFAP4 (1:500, Santa, sc-376977), anti-Flag (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 14793), anti-Myc (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 2276) and anti-GFP (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 2555).

Colony formation in soft agar assay

Soft-agar assays were used for measuring the ability of CRC cells grow in three dimensions. Prepare 0.7% lower layer gel and 0.35% upper layer gel containing the number of 500, 1,000, and 2,000 cell per well in 6-well culture plates. Culture it in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator after cooling off. Culturing for 2–3 weeks, observe the formation under a microscope and take photos to record. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

MTS assay

The cell suspension was diluted to 1,000 cell per well in 96-well culture plates, then the plate was incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 incubator. MTS (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium) was added to the well and incubated in the incubator for 3 hours. The OD value at 490 nm was measured using an ELISA reader. The obtained OD values were used to calculate the cell proliferation curve.

Plate cloning assay

Cells were inoculated in a gradient of 250, 500, and 1,500 cell per well in a 6-well culture plate and cultured at 37°C, 5% CO2 for about 1 week, and the cells in the 6-well culture plate were washed with PBS, fixed with methanol for 20 min, then stained with crystal violet dye for 20 min and washed with running water. The 6-well culture plate was photographed and plotted. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

In vitro migration assays

Migration assays were carried out using Matrigel Invasion Chamber 24-Well Plate 8.0 micron (Corning, 354480). Add 100 µl serum-free culture medium to the upper chamber containing Matrigel and place it in a 37°C incubator for 30 min to hydrate the basement membrane. Then adding serum-free culture medium diluted cells to the upper chamber. Plates were placed in the incubator for 24–72 hours for migration progression. To detect cells that had passed through the porous membrane, the lower side of the membrane was fixed using methanol, then stained with crystal violet for 20 min. After several washings, migrated cells were photographed and plotted. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Wound healing assay

Cells were plated into 6-well culture plates and incubated until a 90–100% confluency. Then, a 200 µl pipette tip was used to generate linear scratches. Next, cells were washed twice with PBS (Phosphate Buffer Saline). Then, medium with 1% FBS was added to the well. Images were captured every 4 hours after wounding. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

RT-qPCR (Reverse Transcription-quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction) analysis was performed as in previous studies.29 Total RNA was extracted from cells with TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA was reversely transcribed into cDNA using RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RT-qPCR was performed using a PowerUP SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Primer pairs used are shown in online supplemental methods.

H&E staining

Resected tumor tissues were fixed in 10% Neutral Buffered Formalin for 24–48 hours, dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin blocks were then cut into 4 µm sections. After the tissue sections were dewaxed and rehydrated, H&E staining was performed using the H&E Staining Kit (Phygene Biotechnology, China).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry staining was performed according to standard method, including a deparaffinization and rehydration step. Antigens were retrieved in slight boiling citrate buffer (BOSTER, AR0024). According to manufacturer procedures (MXB, KIT-9730, China), incubated with primary antibodies and secondary antibodies sequentially. The antibodies used for staining were anti-smad2 (1:50, Cell Signaling Technology, 5339), anti-snail1 (1:50, Santa Cruz, sc-271977), anti-NLRC5 (1:100, Affinity, China), anti-TFAP4 (1:50, Abcam, ab223771), anti-CD4 (1:50, Santa Cruz, sc13573), anti-CD8 (1:50, BOSTER, A02236-1), anti-CD20 (1:50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-16701), anti-CD56 (1:100, Cell Signaling Technology, 99746) and anti-CD68 (1:150, Cell Signaling Technology, 97778). The stained slides were scanned with Pannoramic Scanner (P250018007, 3D Histech). The IHC (Immunohistochemistry) score of percentage was defined as 0 (<10%), 1 (10–25%), 2 (25–50%) and 3 (>50%). The score staining intensity was defined as 0 (negative staining), 1 (weakly staining), 2 (moderately staining) and 3 (strongly staining). The high expression was defined as scores from 4 to 6, and the low expression was scores of 0–3.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% Paraformaldehyde Fix Solution (Beyotime, China). Then, the cells were treated with Triton X-100 (Beyotime, China) for cell perforation. The fixed cells were incubated with 5% BSA (Bovine Serum Albumin) in PBS, followed by incubation with the indicated primary antibodies and secondary antibody. DAPI (4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) was used to stain the cell nucleus. Images were captured using a Leica confocal laser scanning microscope. The antibodies used for staining were anti-smad2 (1:50, Cell Signaling Technology, 5339), anti-STUB1 (1:200, Abcam, ab134064) and anti-NLRC5 (1:100, Santa, sc-515668).

RNA in situ hybridization

In situ hybridization (ISH) assay was performed to detect the expression of lncRNA-CTD in colon cancer tissues according to manufacturer procedures (Boster biological technology, China). Paraffin sections were routinely dewaxed, and the nucleic acid fragments of tissue sections were exposed with 3% citric acid freshly diluted pepsin. After washing, the sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. After washing, prehybridization solution was added to the sections and prehybridized. After that, the hybridization solution was added directly. After the sections were washed with SCC (Saline Sodium Citrate), they were incubated with blocking solution, and then biotinylated mouse anti-digoxigenin was added directly and incubated. After washing, the DAB kit (3,3'-Diaminobenzidine) was used for color development. The target sequences for the lncRNA-CTD were as follows: 1#, 5’-ATCTGGATCTGGCTGGGAAATCATGATGTGGATAACAGTG-3’; 2#, 5’-AATCTTGGCTGTCTGCAACCCTTGCCTCTCGGGTTCAGGA-3’; 3#, 5’-CTATGATAATGCCATTGCACTCCAG CCTGGGTGACAGAGT-3’.

Isolation of cytoplasmic and nuclear RNA

The PARIS Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) used for isolation of cytoplasmic and nuclear RNA. To isolate RNA from nuclear and cytoplasmic cell fractions, follow the manufacturer’s instructions and use the following procedure: First, prepare separate nuclear and cytoplasmic lysates using Cell Fractionation Buffer and Cell Disruption Buffer. Next, mixing the lysate with Binding Solution and drawing the sample mixture through a Filter Cartridge. Finally, following the wash process, RNA isolation is achieved through elution buffer. In subsequent experiments, GAPDH (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) was performed as an internal reference for cytoplasmic gene expression, while BCAR4 (Breast Cancer Anti-Estrogen Resistance 4) was performed as a positive control for gene expression in the nucleus.

Biotin-labeled RNA pull down

RNA pulldown assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, biotin-labeled RNA was transcribed and purified with T7 RNA polymerase (Ambion) and RNA 3’End Biotinylation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Purified biotin-labeled RNA was then heated and annealed to form secondary structure, mixed with cytoplasmic extract for 1 hour, and incubated with streptavidin Dynabeads for 1 hour. Finally, beads were washed and the extracted protein was subjected to western blot analysis using the smad2 antibody. The full-length or truncated lncRNA-CTD and the indicated antisense probe used for RNA pulldown assay.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

Chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed using the SimpleChIP Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were fixed by 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature to crosslink the protein and DNA. Lysis buffer was added, and the cells were centrifuged to collect the Sample Preparation, which were sonicated to generate DNA fragments. And then incubated with the specific antibody. The antibodies in this study included anti-Flag (1:50, Cell Signaling Technology, 14793), anti-IgG antibody (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology, 2729) and anti-H3 antibody (1:50, Cell Signaling Technology, 4620). The enriched DNA was pulled down by antibody, and RT-qPCR was performed to quantitate. The primers used for ChIP-qPCR (Chromatin Immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative PCR) are shown in online supplemental methods.

RNA immunoprecipitation

Cells cultured in 10 cm dishes were transfected with various plasmids of smad2 (WT-FLAG, MH1-FLAG, Linker-FLAG or MH2-FLAG) for 48 hours. The RNA immunoprecipitation was performed using the EZ-Magna RIP RNA‐binding protein immunoprecipitation kit (Millipore, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First, the cell lysates were incubated with beads coated with anti-Flag (1:50, Cell Signaling Technology, 14793) or anti-IgG antibody (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology, 2729). Then the RNA was purified using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and the enriched mRNA was detected by RT-qPCR.

S1m-aptamer-assisted RNA pull-down

Plasmids overexpressed lncRNA-CTD (lncRNA-CTD-4×S1 m) or Vector were transiently transfected into DLD1 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The cells were washed with PBS and irradiated with 400 mJ cm−2 of energy on ice in an ultraviolet cross-linker (SCIENTZ03-II). Nuclei of ultraviolet cross-linked cells were isolated by suspending the cells in a hypotonic buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 10 mM NaCl and 3 mM MgCl2), added NP-40 to a final concentration of 0.5% and then centrifuged. Nuclei were lysed in lysis buffer (150 mM KCl, 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 5 mM EDTA, 5 mM MgCl2, 1% NP-40, 0.5 mM dithiothreitol, protease inhibitor and 100 U ml−1 RNaseOUT) and debris were cleared by centrifugation. The supernatant containing the nuclear lysate was then cleared with Avidin Agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 20219) to deplete biotin from the lysates. The cleared lysate was incubated with streptavidin C1 Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The beads were collected using a magnet and washed with a wash buffer of the same composition as the lysis buffer, except KCl was increased to 350 mM. To release the proteins, beads were incubated with RNase A and resuspended in 2×LDS sample buffer. The lysates were resolved on a polyacrylamide gel. The position of the pulled-down lysates was observed in the gel by silver staining (Solarbio, China) and then analyzed lysates using mass spectrometry (MS).

Luciferase reporter assay

For promoter activity assays, cells were co-transfected with pGL4-reporter vector (pGl4-WT, pGL4-Del A, pGL4-Del B or pGL4-Del A+B) and pRL-TK Renilla luciferase vector with or without plasmids of TFAP4 using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After 48 hours, the luciferase activity was determined using the luciferase assay kit (Vazyme, China). The relative activity was expressed as the ratio of firefly to renilla luciferase activity.

Flow cytometry

Cells were cultured, digested and resuspended in PBS (with 2% FBS) and then blocked with a blocking agent for 10 min. The cells were then stained for anti-human HLA-A (1:100, Abcam, AB52922), anti-human HLA-B (1:100, Abcam, ab212436), anti-human HLA-C (1:100, NOVUS, NBP2-50419), anti-human MHC-I (5 µl/test, Biolegent, B394406) or anti-IgG (5 µl/test, eBioscience, 12-4739-81), then the cell was detected.

For the CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity analysis, peripheral blood was collected from healthy volunteers. PBMCs (Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells‌) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using the Human Lymphocyte Isolation Solution (GOONIE, 100–306, GuangZhou, China) and Floor-type centrifuge (SCRNSPEEO 1736R, Labogene) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PBMCs were suspended in RPMI-1640 medium (VivaCell, Germany) with 10% FBS. CD8+ T cells were isolated from healthy volunteers‐derived PBMCs using negative magnetic sorting (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11348D) and then stimulated with anti‐CD3/CD28/CD2 antibodies (Stemcell, 10990) 72 hour before the coculture. The stimulated CD8+ T cells were co‐cultured with the CRC cells at an effector/target ratio (E:T ratio) of 10:1 for another 48 hours at 37°C. Then the harvested CRC cells were detected with FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I (Fluorescein Isothiocyanate‌, BD Biosciences, 556547) and stained for Annexin V 5 U/test, and cell apoptosis was detected.

For tumor-infiltrating leukocyte flow cytometry, about 1×105 CT26 control and CT26 lncRNA-CTD cells were inoculated subcutaneously into BALB/c mice. Tumors were collected on day 21 after inoculation, and mechanically minced and digested in DNase I (0.5 mg/mL, Sigma) and collagenase (1 mg/mL, Sigma). The dissociated cells were passed through a 70 µm cell filter (absin, abs7232). The filtered cells were then stimulated with a stimulating protein blocker inhibition mixture (BD Biosciences, 550583) for 4–6 hours at 37°C. Then the cells were stained with Fixable Viability Stain 510 (BD Biosciences, 564406). And the cells were incubated with surface antibodies as anti-CD45 (0.125 µg/test, Invitrogen, 45-0451-82), anti-CD3e (1 µg/test, Invitrogen, 47-0031-82), anti-CD8 (1 µl/test, BD eBioscience, 552877). The cells were then fixed with IC Fixation Buffer (BD eBioscience, 00–8222) and membrane disrupted with Permeabilization Buffer (10X) (BD eBioscience, 00–8333), then stained with intracellular antibodies as anti-Interferon-gamma (INF-γ) (0.5 µg/test, Invitrogen, 48-7311-82) anti-granzyme B (GZMB) (0.125 µg/test, Invitrogen, 50-8898-82). ABD Canto II flow cytometer was used for analysis, and data were then analyzed using FlowJo.

ELISA assay

The conditioned medium of co-cultured CD8+ T cells and cancer cells was analyzed to measure IFN-γ, TNF-α (Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha) or GZMB levels using ELISA kits (R&D Systems, QK285, DTA00D, DY2906-05). The assays were performed in triplicates according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Tumor models

Animal experiments were performed in strict accordance with the relevant ethical guidelines, approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangzhou Medical University. For the Xenograft model in mice, 4-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were obtained from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center, China. CRC cell lines were subcutaneously injected into the left axilla of female BALB/c nude mice (n = 5/group). The tumor size was measured every 7 days, and tumor volume (V) was calculated as V = (length×width2)/2.

For the metastasis assay in vivo, human colorectal cells were resuspended in 100 µl PBS and injected into the tail vein of BALB/c nude mice. The mice were killed after 2 months and lung tissues were stained with H&E dye.

For the CT26 orthotopic tumor model, tumor cell suspension in 40 µl PBS containing 2×106 CT26-luciferase cells were injected into the cecal wall of BALB/c mice. About 5–7 days after tumor cell inoculation, tumor conditions were inspected through a bioluminescence imaging system, tumor-bearing mice were anesthetized using isoflurane and injected intraperitoneally with D-Luciferin (75 mg/kg). After successful gas anesthesia, the animals were transferred to the BRUKER Image Station System (X-RAY SYSTEM) and luminescence was detected. Data were subsequently analyzed using Living Image software. For mice that can develop orthotopic liver metastatic tumors, liver metastasis occurs approximately 4 weeks after inoculation.

For anti-CTLA4 (Cytotoxic T Lymphocyte-Associated Antigen 4) versus anti-PD-L1 (Programmed Cell Death Ligand 1) treatment, we treated one group of the mice models with 10 mg/kg InVivo anti-mouse CTLA4 (Bio X Cell, BP0131) and one group with 10 mg/kg anti-mouse PD-L1-InVivo (Selleck, A2115) from the 7th day after tumor cell inoculation, two times a week. According to the ethical guidelines, all the mice were sacrificed when the average tumor diameter of the control group approached 1.5 cm and then the tumors were resected and weighed, and pretreated for further experiments.

Adeno-associated virus construction and treatment

Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-protein vectors 9 containing lncRNA-CTD were constructed (Genechem, Shanghai, China). The treatment procedures for mouse were as follows. In azoxymethane–dextran sulfate sodium (AOM/DSS)-induced mice model, 5×1010 virus particles of AAV in 100 µl of PBS were injected into the tail veins of 4-week-old C57 mice. Colon and rectum were removed for IHC staining. For mice with subcutaneous grafted tumors, 1×1011 virus particles of AAV in 40 µl of PBS were injected into the tumor, and the graft tumors were then dissected for IHC staining.

AOM/DSS-induced mice model

For generation of the AOM-DSS model, 6-week-old C57 mice were intraperitoneally injected with AOM (10 mg/kg body weight) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) on day 1. In the 2nd, 5th and 8th week, the drinking water of the mice was supplemented with 2% (w/v) DSS. And 11–13 weeks after the AOM injection, the mice were euthanized. And the large intestines were dissected for recording tumor load, tumor size and intestinal weight. Then the intestinal tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for following HE and IHC staining.

Structure prediction

For structure predictions, we used the AF3 server (https://alphafoldserver.com/). The sequences of SMAD2 submitted to the server corresponded to the UniProt entries associated with PDB (Protein Data Bank) structure. Then all molecules were predicted. PyMOL (Python Molecular Viewer) was used for visualization. The predicted binding site of SMAD2 and lncRNA-CTD is shown in online supplemental methods.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated three times independently, and have been performed using GraphPad Prism V.10. Student’s t-tests were employed when the variance between two groups was similar, while Welch’s t-tests were used if the variance was not the same. The subcutaneous tumor growth curves were compared using two-way analysis of variance. Symbols for significance: ns, non-significant; *p<0.05, **p<0.01; ***p<0.001; ****p<0.0001. P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Low expression of LncRNA-CTD is correlated with CRC progression and poor prognoses

To explore lncRNAs associated with CRC clinically, we performed lncRNA-seq in six paired CRC tissues and adjacent normal colorectal tissues and identified 1722 significantly altered lncRNAs (figure 1A). Among the differentially expressed lncRNAs (figure 1B), we confirmed the significant downregulation of lncRNA CTD-2568A17.8 (hereafter termed lncRNA-CTD) in CRC tissues (figure 1C) and CRC cell lines compared with normal colon epithelium FHC cells (online supplemental figure S1A). LncRNA-CTD is composed of two exons with a transcript length of 260 nt and is located on human chromosome 19 (ENST00000594114.1). Because LncRNA-CTD is newly identified in CRC, its biological characteristics and functions need to be elucidated. To determine whether lncRNA-CTD has coding potential, bioinformatics analysis using online software predicted no protein-coding ability of lncRNA-CTD but predicted an open reading frame (ORF) (online supplemental figure S1B). We constructed a series of vectors with a mutation in the initiation codon of GFP that was fused downstream of the full lncRNA-CTD transcript or potential ORF (online supplemental figure S1C). Confocal fluorescence microscopy revealed that no substantial expression of GFP was observed in HEK293T cells transfected with the full-length-GFP mut or ORF-GFP mut construct (online supplemental figure S1D), and a western blot analysis conducted using the anti-GFP antibody further confirmed that lncRNA-CTD lacks protein-coding ability (online supplemental figure S1E).

Low expression of lncRNA-CTD is correlated with CRC progression and poor prognosis. To determine the subcellular localization of lncRNA-CTD, lncRNA-CTD was overexpressed in DLD1 and SW620 cells (figure 1D). lncRNA-CTD was detected in the nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionations, which indicated that the majority of lncRNA-CTD was localized in the cytoplasm (figure 1E). RNA-ISH also confirmed the primary cytoplasmic location of lncRNA-CTD (figure 1F). We further examined the expression levels of lncRNA-CTD in a cohort of 88 CRC tissues and their corresponding normal colorectal tissues. Both the RNA-ISH and RT-qPCR analyses further revealed the downregulation of lncRNA-CTD in CRC tissues compared with the paired normal colorectal tissues (figure 1G,H). Importantly, low lncRNA-CTD expression was correlated with poor overall survival of patients with CRC (figure 1I).

Moreover, to investigate the expression pattern of lncRNA-CTD during colon cancer progression, we established a colitis-associated colon cancer (CAC) mouse model by challenging C57BL/6 mice with an intraperitoneal injection of AOM and provided them with DSS in their drinking water (figure 1J,K). An RNA-ISH analysis revealed a gradual decrease in the expression of lncRNA-CTD during CAC progression, which included normal, inflammation, dysplasia and cancer pathological stages in sequence (figure 1L). These results suggest that the involvement of lncRNA-CTD decreases during CRC progression.

LncRNA-CTD suppresses CRC metastasis and immune evasion

To address the biological significance of lncRNA-CTD in CRC, we constructed lncRNA-CTD-overexpressing human CRC cell lines (figure 1D). A series of assays revealed that lncRNA-CTD has no significant effect on CRC cell proliferation in vitro (figure 2A and online supplemental figure S2A,B) or on xenograft growth in athymic nude mice (figure 2B). However, lncRNA-CTD effectively inhibited CRC cell invasion in vitro (figure 2C) and metastasis in vivo using lung metastasis model established via tail vein injection (figure 2D). Consistently, lncRNA-CTD was overexpressed in CT26 mouse colon cancer cells (figure 2E). lncRNA-CTD had no strong effect on the proliferation of CT26 cells in vitro (figure 2F and online supplemental figure S2C,D) or growth in athymic nude mice with impaired T-cell responses (figure 2G) but inhibited invasion in vitro (figure 2C). Unexpectedly, lncRNA-CTD markedly inhibited xenograft growth in BALB/c immunocompetent mice after the subcutaneous injection of CT26 cells (figure 2H), whereas no strong difference was detected in athymic nude mice. Additionally, to better simulate the microenvironment of CRC progression in vivo, luciferase-labeled CT26 cells overexpressing lncRNA-CTD were injected below the serosa of the cecum in BALB/c mice via surgical orthotopic injection. Remarkably, on the basis of in vivo bioluminescence measurements over time, lncRNA-CTD efficiently inhibited orthotopic CRC growth and the metastatic burden in the liver (figure 2I). These results suggest that lncRNA-CTD has no strong effect on the proliferation of CRC cells but inhibits CRC metastasis and immune evasion.

LncRNA-CTD interacts with smad2 to inhibit snail1-mediated metastasis

To identify the mechanisms underlying the biological effects of lncRNA-CTD in CRC, we employed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to screen differentially expressed genes on the occurrence of lncRNA-CTD overexpression, the potential biases in RNA-seq data were mitigated as in previous studies30 (figure 3A). KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) and GSEA (Gene Set Enrichment Analysis) enrichment analyses revealed that lncRNA-CTD-correlated genes were enriched in focal adhesion and antigen processing and presentation (figure 3B,C). The epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) has been shown to play a critical role in tumor metastasis. Here, we focused on the EMT transcription factor snail1. To assess whether lncRNA-CTD inhibits CRC metastasis via the downregulation of snail1 (online supplemental figure S3A), snail1 was ectopically overexpressed in CRC cells overexpressing lncRNA-CTD (figure 3D). Snail1 effectively disrupted lncRNA-CTD-mediated migration and invasion inhibition in vitro (online supplemental figure S3B,C), as well as metastasis in vivo (figure 3E).

LncRNAs usually affect gene expression via RNA-protein interactions.31 To identify proteins that interact with lncRNA-CTD, the 3′ end of lncRNA-CTD cDNA was fused to four copies of an aptamer with high affinity for streptavidin (4xS1m).32 The lncRNA-CTD-4xS1m transcript or empty control was transiently transfected into DLD1 cells and precipitated with streptavidin beads in ultraviolet cross-linked cells, followed by MS analysis (figure 3F and online supplemental figure S3D). Among the potential proteins interacting with lncRNA-CTD, smad2 was significantly enriched (figure 3G), and the interaction was confirmed by streptavidin precipitation followed by immunoblotting to detect the smad2 protein (figure 3H). LncRNA-CTD reduced the phosphorylation levels of smad2 and smad3 but not the total protein level in CRC cells (figure 3I). IF (Immunofluorescence) assays further revealed that lncRNA-CTD reduced the nuclear translocation of smad2 (figure 3J and online supplemental figure S3E). Additionally, ChIP-qPCR revealed that lncRNA-CTD attenuated smad2 occupancy at the snail1 promoter (online supplemental figure S3F). Consistently, lncRNA-CTD reduced smad2 nuclear accumulation and snail1 expression in in vivo xenografts (figure 3K).

To detail the interaction between lncRNA-CTD and smad2, first, the online AlphaFold WebServer (https://alphafoldserver.com/) was used to predict the interaction regions of lncRNA-CTD and smad2 (figure 3L). A series of truncated mutants of lncRNA-CTD and smad2 were subsequently constructed (figure 3M and online supplemental figure S3G). Related mutants were cotransfected into HEK293T cells. The results revealed that nucleotides 1–100 of lncRNA-CTD are responsible for interacting with smad2 (online supplemental figure S3G) and that the MH1 domain of smad2 is responsible for binding to lncRNA-CTD (figure 3M). These data indicate that lncRNA-CTD interacts with smad2 and prevents smad2 nuclear translocation to suppress snail1 expression, inhibiting CRC cell invasion and metastasis.

LncRNA-CTD restores MHC-I expression in CRC cells and facilitates the T-cell antitumor response

Given that lncRNA-CTD had a stronger effect on tumor growth inhibition in immunocompetent mice than in immunodeficient nude mice (figure 2G,H) and that differentially expressed genes induced by lncRNA-CTD were associated with antigen processing and presentation, the role of lncRNA-CTD in the T-cell antitumor response was determined. The substantial increase in CD8+ TIL in xenografts derived from lncRNA-CTD-overexpressing CT26 cells compared with those in the control group was validated (figure 4A). Consistent with the data from the mouse model, lncRNA-CTD expression in human CRC was positively correlated with CD8+ T-cell infiltration (figure 4B). The mRNA levels of several components in the MHC-I pathway were detected by RT-qPCR, and the analysis revealed that the mRNA levels of several components in the MHC-I pathway, including HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, B2M, CANX, PD1A3, TAP1, TAP2 and TAPBP, were significantly upregulated in lncRNA-CTD-overexpressing CRC cells (figure 4C). Flow cytometric analysis also confirmed the effect of lncRNA-CTD on MHC-I expression (figure 4D).

To validate whether the upregulation of MHC-I expression by lncRNA-CTD affects antitumor immune activity, we performed coculture assays to evaluate the CD8+ T-cell-dependent cytotoxicity of tumor cells and the tumor-specific activation of CD8+ T cells. HLA typing was determined by high-resolution genotyping in DLD1 (HLA-A2+) and SW620 (HLA-A2+) cells (onlinesupplemental tables S1 S2). Next, CD8+ T-cell activation and killing assays were performed. CRC cells were cocultured with HLA-matched CD8+ T cells at a 10:1 E:T ratio. Compared with the vector group, lncRNA-CTD significantly increased the apoptosis of CRC cells induced by CD8+ T cells (figure 4E). ELISA assays examining IFN-γ, TNF-α and GZMB revealed that lncRNA-CTD-overexpressing CRC cells significantly increased the secretion of these immune factors in the supernatants of CD8+ T cells compared with those of the vector group (figure 4F). Similar results were obtained via RT-qPCR, which was used to detect the mRNA levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α and GZMB in CD8+ T cells (figure 4G). To evaluate whether surface HLAs in CRC cells are required for tumor immune activity, an HLA-blocking antibody (W6/32) was also used to block recognition between HLAs and TCRs. Compared with the IgG isotype control, blocking HLA significantly reduced apoptosis in lncRNA-CTD-overexpressing CRC cells via CD8+ T cells (figure 4H). ELISA assays revealed lower levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α and GZMB in supernatants from CD8+ T cells incubated with lncRNA-CTD-overexpressing CRC cells pretreated with the HLA-blocking antibody than in those incubated with the IgG isotype control (figure 4I). Moreover, lncRNA-CTD overexpression increased the cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells (IFN-γ and GZMB) in xenograft tumors derived from CT26 cells in completely immune mice (figure 4J). Taken together, these findings suggest that lncRNA-CTD increases the MHC-I levels in CRC and promotes the recruitment and activation of CD8+ T cells, consequently inhibiting tumor growth.

LncRNA-CTD promotes MHC-I expression by inhibiting STUB1-induced NLRC5 degradation

Since NLRC5 is known to be an MHC-I transactivator, we examined whether NLRC5 was involved in the upregulation of MHC-I expression by lncRNA-CTD. The results revealed that lncRNA-CTD overexpression increased NLRC5 protein levels but not NLRC5 mRNA levels in CRC cells (figure 5A). Further analysis revealed that lncRNA-CTD increased NLRC5 protein levels, possibly by enhancing NLRC5 protein stability (figure 5B). The proteasome inhibitor MG132 restored NLRC5 protein levels in CRC cells (figure 5C). LncRNA-CTD efficiently impaired NLRC5 ubiquitination (figure 5D), suggesting that lncRNA-CTD enhances NLRC5 protein stability by regulating the ubiquitin proteasome degradation system. Among the proteins that interact with the lncRNA-CTD, the ubiquitin ligase STUB1 was identified (figure 5E). The interaction was validated by streptavidin precipitation followed by immunoblotting to detect the STUB1 protein (figure 5F).

Next, whether STUB1 regulates NLRC5 protein stability was determined. Strikingly, immunofluorescence assays revealed that NLRC5 colocalized with STUB1 in the cytoplasm of CRC cells (figure 5G). Co-IP (Co-immunoprecipitation) further confirmed the interaction between STUB1 and NLRC5 (figure 5H). STUB1 knockdown reduced NLRC5 protein ubiquitination and increased the NLRC5 protein levels in CRC cells (figure 5I). Furthermore, lncRNA-CTD overexpression disrupted the interaction between STUB1 and NLRC5 (figure 5J). Moreover, STUB1 knockdown increased MHC-I expression in CRC cells (figure 5K). NLRC5 knockdown reversed the effect of lncRNA-CTD-induced MHC-I expression (figure 5L). Importantly, in clinical CRC tissues, the lncRNA-CTD level was positively correlated with the NLRC5 protein level (figure 5M). The NLRC5 protein level was positively correlated with CD8+ T-cell infiltration (figure 5N). Together, these data suggest that STUB1-mediated NLRC5 degradation is possibly linked to the regulation of MHC-I expression by lncRNA-CTD (figure 5O).

LncRNA-CTD expression is suppressed by TFAP4 in CRC cells

Given that lncRNA-CTD expression was downregulated in CRC cells, we explored the mechanisms underlying lncRNA-CTD downregulation. To identify the transcription factors regulating lncRNA-CTD, the JASPAR database (http://jaspar.binf.ku.dk) was used to predict potential transcription factor-binding sites in the 2 kb region upstream of the transcription start site of lncRNA-CTD. The analysis predicted four potential TFAP4-binding sites in the lncRNA-CTD promoter (figure 6A). An online analysis conducted via UALCAN (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu) revealed that TFAP4 expression was upregulated in CRC tissues compared with normal intestinal epithelial tissues (figure 6B). TFAP4 overexpression was further confirmed at the protein level (figure 6C) and mRNA level (figure 6D) in CRC tissues. Correlation analysis indicated that the TFAP4 level was negatively correlated with the lncRNA-CTD level in CRC tissues (figure 6E). To investigate the potential impact of TFAP4 on lncRNA-CTD expression, TFAP4 was knocked out in CRC cells (figure 6F). TFAP4 knockout significantly increased lncRNA-CTD expression (figure 6G). A flag-tagged TFAP4 expression vector was subsequently constructed to perform ChIP-qPCR assays. The ChIP-qPCR results revealed that TFAP4 could bind to putative binding sites A and B at the lncRNA-CTD promoters in CRC cells (figure 6H).

We next constructed luciferase reporters containing the wild-type lncRNA-CTD promoter or a construct with mutated TFAP4 targeting sites (figure 6I). TFAP4 reduced the reporter activity of the construct with the wild-type lncRNA-CTD promoter (figure 6J). Moreover, the effects of TFAP4 lncRNA-CTD-mediated biological functions were determined. LncRNA-CTD was stably knocked down in CRC cells with TFAP4 knockout (figure 6K). LncRNA-CTD knockdown impaired snail1 downregulation caused by TFAP4 knockout (figure 6K). LncRNA-CTD knockdown blocked the decreased migration and invasion ability of CRC cells in vitro (figure 6L) and the decreased metastasis ability in vivo (figure 6M) resulting from TFAP4 knockout. Knockout of TFAP4 largely compromised the expression of NLRC5 and MHC-I upregulated by lncRNA-CTD knockdown, as well as the nuclear translocation of smad2 (figure 6M). These results suggest that TFAP4 binds to the lncRNA-CTD promoter and transcriptionally suppresses lncRNA-CTD expression in CRC cells.

Targeting lncRNA-CTD enhances antitumor immunotherapy in CRC

To evaluate the translational significance in preclinical models, an AAV-based lncRNA-CTD gene delivery vector (AAV-encapsulating lncRNA-CTD vector, AAV-CTD) was constructed. In the CAC mouse model, AAV-CTD or AAV-Control was administered through the tail vein (figure 7A,B). AAV-CTD treatment successfully increased the lncRNA-CTD level in the lesions (figure 7C) and delayed CAC development and progression (figure 7D). AAV-CTD treatment also resulted in CD8+ T-cell infiltration (figure 7E), increased expression of NLRC5 and MHC-I, and decreased nuclear localization of smad2 in the lesions (online supplemental figure S4A).

Moreover, to determine whether AAV-CTD treatment enhances the effectiveness of immune checkpoint therapy in vivo, CT26 cells were subcutaneously injected into BALB/c immunocompetent mice to establish xenografts. Tumor-bearing mice were treated with AAV-CTD or the AAV-Control 6 days post injection, followed by treatment with anti-PD-L1 or anti-CTLA-4 (figure 7F). AAV-CTD, anti-PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 treatment alone delayed tumor growth (figure 7G) and prolonged the survival time of tumor-bearing mice (figure 7H). Notably, the combined treatment of AAV-CTD with either anti-PD-L1 or anti-CTLA-4 had an additive effect on tumor growth inhibition (figure 7G), improved survival in tumor-bearing mice (figure 7H), and increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration (figure 7I). The increased proportions of IFN-γ+ and GZMB+ CD8+ T cells, as well as the expression of NLRC5 and MHC-I, and the decreased nuclear localization of smad2 were detected in tumor lesions treated with the combination of AAV-CTD and dual ICB therapy (figure 7J,K) (online supplemental figure S4B), indicating that AAV-CTD could potentiate the T-cell response in CRC. These results suggest that combining lncRNA-CTD delivery with immune checkpoint blockade may improve the effectiveness of CRC treatment.

Discussion

Metastasis is the leading cause of poor outcomes for patients with CRC, and the development of novel markers and therapeutic targets for the effective treatment of mCRC is urgently needed. Accumulating evidence has shown that lncRNAs are involved in multiple functions in the tumorigenesis and progression of CRC;33 34 however, the majority of these lncRNAs remain functionally uncharacterized. In the present study, we demonstrated that lncRNA-CTD, a newly identified lncRNA, is significantly downregulated in both CRC cell lines and tissues. Notably, the expression of lncRNA-CTD gradually decreases during mouse CRC development induced by the AOM/DSS model. Additionally, we elucidated the pivotal function of lncRNA-CTD in tumor metastasis and tumor immunity. Mechanistically, we observed that lncRNA-CTD downregulates snail1 at the transcriptional level by binding to smad2 and inhibiting its phosphorylation. In addition, lncRNA-CTD interacts with STUB1 to stabilize the NLRC5 protein, which then enhances MHC-I expression and antitumor immunity, consequently promoting CRC development.

LncRNAs have emerged as pivotal regulators in the process of EMT in CRC development.35 36 EMT is considered a key factor in promoting cancer cell invasion and metastasis. Snail1 is an important EMT-related transcription factor that inhibits the expression of E-cadherin by recruiting a multisubunit inhibitory complex, which is crucial in the occurrence of EMT in tumor cells.37 Our results revealed that lncRNA-CTD inhibits the migratory and invasive capacities of CRC cells via the downregulation of snail1. Many lncRNAs regulate gene expression via RNA-protein interactions. To further elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms responsible for snail1 downregulation by lncRNA-CTD, we investigated proteins that could specifically bind lncRNA-CTD in CRC. Our results verified that lncRNA-CTD could directly bind smad2, an indispensable effector of TGF-β-induced EMT.38 Smad2/3 interact with smad4 to form a heterodimeric complex that regulates the transcription of target genes.39 In HCC, HDACIs upregulate the expression of snail at the transcriptional level by promoting smad2/3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation.40 In this study, we found that lncRNA-CTD could control smad2/3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation to inhibit snail1 expression by binding the MH1 domain of smad2 in CRC cells. A functional study revealed that snail1 overexpression completely reversed the inhibitory effect of lncRNA-CTD on the migration and invasion of CRC cells. These results suggest that the role of lncRNA-CTD in the metastasis of CRC depends on smad2/3-snail1 signaling.

Emerging evidence has indicated that the expression of lncRNAs is specifically modulated by transcription factors.41 42 To explore the underlying molecular mechanisms by which lncRNA-CTD is downregulated in CRC, we analyzed potential transcription factor-binding sites in the lncRNA-CTD promoter region. An experimental validation revealed that TFAP4 was upregulated in CRC and could directly negatively regulate lncRNA-CTD expression. TFAP4 belongs to the basic-helix-loop-helix leucine zipper subgroup and is a transcription factor that represses or activates the expression of target genes.43 TFAP4 directly represses p21 expression induced by TGF-β in human keratinocytes and blocks the upregulation of p21 during monoblast differentiation.44 TFAP4 activated the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by binding with the promoters of DVL1 and LEF1 to increase the tumorigenicity of HCC cells.45 In this study, we found that TFAP4, a transcriptional suppressor of lncRNA-CTD, is involved in regulating the migration and invasion of CRC cells.

CRC is characterized by a highly immune-suppressive microenvironment. The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a complex ecosystem comprising cancer cells, stromal cells, immune cells, extracellular matrix, metabolites and cytokines. Targeting key components of the TME is an important strategy for cancer treatment.46 A20 facilitates CRC immune evasion in a STC1-CRT-dependent manner to block membrane translocation of “eat-me” signal.47 The deregulation of MHC-I molecules has been observed in CRC, the loss or downregulation of MHC-I has been identified as a major factor leading to tumor immune evasion, and increasing MHC-I antigen peptide presentation to CD8+ T cells is a key approach to eliminate tumors.48 49 Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms of MHC-I deregulation in tumor cells is critical for developing effective immunotherapy strategies to enhance immune recognition and the antitumor response. Here, we found that overexpression of lncRNA-CTD upregulated the expression level of MHC-I (including HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C) in CRC cells, resulting in increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration and increased expression of IFN-γ, TNF‐α and GZMB in CD8+ T cells. NLRC5 is well studied as a transcriptional regulator of MHC-I genes in cancer.50 We confirmed that lncRNA-CTD overexpression promoted the expression of NLRC5 at the protein level but not at the mRNA level, with an increase in the surface MHC-I level in CRC cells.

To explore the possible molecular mechanisms responsible for NLRC5 upregulation, we identified STUB1 as an E3 ubiquitin ligase that can bind with lncRNA-CTD. STUB1 (STIP1 homology and U-box-containing protein 1) is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that contains two domains: a tetratricopeptide repeat domain that regulates protein–protein interactions and a U-box domain that controls E3 ligase activity.51 52 A recent study revealed that STUB1 mediates the degradation of the IFNγ-R1/JAK1 complex, sensitizing tumor cells to cytotoxic T cells and responding to anti-PD-1 treatment.53 In cholangiocarcinoma, STUB1 interacts with UHRF1 to facilitate the K63-linked ubiquitination of UHRF1, thereby increasing DNA methylation of the PLA2G2A promoter and promoting the progression of cholangiocarcinoma.54 In our study, we demonstrated that STUB1 and NLRC5 were colocalized in the cytoplasm of CRC cells, and co-IP assays further revealed an interaction between STUB1 and NLRC5 in vitro. lncRNA-CTD binds to STUB1 and inhibits NLRC5 ubiquitination, subsequently increasing the surface MHC-I level. These results suggest that lncRNA-CTD promotes the NLRC5-mediated MHC-I antigen presentation pathway by directly interacting with STUB1 in CRC. Liquid biopsy is usually isolated from peripheral blood samples and comprise circulating tumor cells, circulating-free DNA and cell-free RNA. It is reported that liquid biopsy technology can monitor potential genetic alterations or DNA methylation for early diagnosis of tumors, targeted therapy selection and evaluation of treatment response.55,57 Gene therapy is considered a promising treatment option for human diseases. AAV to deliver circRERE improves the therapeutic effect of immunotherapy in colorectal cancer tumor suppression.58

Finally, our study demonstrated that engineered AAVs loaded with lncRNA-CTD significantly inhibited tumorigenesis and progression in a CAC mouse model. In addition, we found that engineered AAVs loaded with lncRNA-CTD strongly improved the response to ICB treatment and significantly improved overall survival in ICB-treated cohorts. Recent studies have investigated the clinical trials of the combination of immune checkpoint inhibitors and (neo)adjuvant chemotherapy in 5114 patients with early-stage breast cancer.59 The hematopoietic system of the patients was transplanted into MISTRG6 mice, and then patient-derived xenograft tissues were transferred to provide a fully genetically matched model to simulate TME of individual patients with solid tumors.60 61 Collectively, these findings reveal a previously unappreciated molecular mechanism by which lncRNA-CTD can inhibit metastasis and promote antitumor immunity and may provide new insights into targeted treatment strategies in CRC.
