Fourier transform-based single domain generalization for crowd counting
Authors: Lei Song, Tong Li, Zhaoyu Cai, Jinliang Guo, Jingxi He, Junfeng Xie, Yun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate crowd counting is critical for numerous real-world applications. However, domain shift poses a significant barrier to deploying crowd counting models in practical scenarios due to the discrepancy between training and target domains. This paper proposes SinCount, a novel crowd counting framework designed for the Single-source Domain Generalization (SDG) setting, capable of generalizing to unseen domains. SinCount introduces a task-frequency alignment mechanism, directing high-frequency cues toward fine-grained density regression while allocating low-frequency cues to region-level classification to mitigate domain shift. Specifically, we develop a frequency-specific feature extraction module to extract high-frequency and low-frequency features. Subsequently, a dual-attention strategy is devised to embed high-frequency features via spatial attention for the regression branch, while modulating low-frequency features via channel attention for the classification branch. Moreover, an instance normalization mask and an attention consistency loss are incorporated to suppress domain-specific noise and stabilize feature learning. Evaluations across multiple benchmark datasets demonstrate that our method achieves competitive performance compared to state-of-the-art SDG approaches. The code is publicly available at https://github.com/Twiwq/SinCount.

Introduction

Accurately estimating the number of individuals in crowded scenes remains a fundamental yet challenging task in computer vision. Crowd counting serves as a core enabler for real-world applications such as smart city management, public safety surveillance, and large-scale event security, while also providing critical support for higher-level scene understanding and behavioral analysis. In recent years, with the rapid progress of deep learning, density map regression has emerged as the dominant approach for crowd counting. This method maps an input image to a continuous density space and integrates over the predicted density map to estimate the total count, demonstrating superior accuracy and robustness1–5. Despite these advances, real-world deployment remains hindered by domain shift, where changes in camera angles, lighting, or weather cause models trained on one domain to fail in unseen environments.

To address this issue, researchers have explored Domain Adaptation (DA) methods6–10, which aim to transfer knowledge from a labeled source domain to an unlabeled or sparsely labeled target domain by adapting model parameters or feature representations, thereby narrowing the domain gap. Despite their effectiveness, DA methods suffer from two notable limitations in crowd counting scenarios: 1) the adaptation process often requires substantial computational overhead and iterative optimization, which becomes impractical when models must be frequently redeployed across diverse scenes; 2) more critically, target-domain annotations in crowd counting rely on labor-intensive point-level labeling, making target-domain data collection costly or even infeasible in real-world applications. To overcome these limitations, researchers have increasingly turned to Domain Generalization (DG), which aims to learn domain-invariant representations without accessing target-domain data11–16. However, most existing DG methods assume multiple source domains during training, an assumption that is often impractical for crowd counting due to large discrepancies in scene layout, density distribution, and annotation protocols across datasets. Recent progress in single-source domain generalization for crowd counting is exemplified by MPCount17. MPCount extends the conventional density regression paradigm by introducing an auxiliary patch-wise classification branch, thereby forming a dual-branch architecture. Together with an attention memory bank, a content error mask, and an attention consistency loss, MPCount suppresses unreliable features and aligns stable feature patterns to alleviate domain shifts, leading to improved cross-scene generalization performance. Nevertheless, such memory-based mechanisms still exhibit inherent limitations. On the one hand, the attention memory bank grows linearly with feature resolution, which slows training convergence and introduces additional inference overhead. More importantly, MPCount does not explicitly model the intrinsic representation properties preferred by different tasks. Density regression constitutes the core objective of crowd counting and relies on stable local spatial structures and fine-grained geometric contours for precise regression. In contrast, the auxiliary patch-wise classification task emphasizes global distribution patterns and contextual semantics to identify crowd regions reliably. These distinct task objectives suggest that branch-wise decoupling alone may be insufficient, and that a more explicit modeling perspective is needed to align representation properties with task-specific demands.

From this perspective, frequency-domain characteristics provide a natural and intuitive lens for understanding such task differences. As illustrated in Fig. 1, high-frequency components primarily capture head contours and edge details, providing fine-grained and structurally consistent spatial cues that are more compatible with density regression. In contrast, low-frequency components preserve the overall scene layout and energy distribution, offering more robust contextual information for identifying crowd regions, which is better suited to the objective of the patch-wise classification branch. These observations suggest a natural correspondence between frequency components and the objectives of the two branches, motivating frequency-aware feature modeling for dual-branch crowd counting frameworks.

Building on these observations, we propose SinCount, a novel single-source domain generalization framework that systematically injects frequency-specific cues into a dual-branch architecture. Specifically, SinCount explicitly extracts high- and low-frequency embeddings via a Frequency-Specific Feature Extraction (FSFE) module. These frequency-specific embeddings are then routed to the corresponding branches through Frequency-Aware Dual Attention (FADA). Such a frequency-aware design enables each branch to better leverage the distinct properties of its associated frequency band, thereby enhancing cross-domain robustness. In addition, unlike prior memory-based dual-branch frameworks, SinCount removes the attention memory bank from the density branch, reducing inference overhead while maintaining competitive or superior cross-domain generalization performance. Related work

Estimation methods for crowd counting

In the research on crowd counting estimation methods, traditional detection-based methods18,19 often underperform in congested scenes due to severe occlusion. While recent advances like NGA20 enhance robustness by modeling spatial relationships, detection paradigms still struggle with highly overlapped targets. Consequently, occlusion-robust density map regression has become the dominant trend, estimating the total population by integrating pixel-wise density values. Early work such as MCNN1 utilized multi-scale branches to handle density variations, while subsequent innovations like CSRNet3 and L2SM4 leveraged dilated convolutions and normalized maps to refine receptive fields and local estimation. Recently, DM-CRENet2 further improved performance by incorporating a residual estimation module. Although the above methods have achieved remarkable results in single-domain scenarios, their performance is still constrained by the problem of domain shift. In the processing of out-of-distribution data such as cross-scene, cross-view, and complex occlusion, the generalization ability of the model decreases significantly.

Domain adaptation (DA)

Domain Adaptation (DA) seeks to transfer knowledge from a labeled source domain to an unlabeled or partially labeled target domain by aligning their data distributions, thereby mitigating the adverse effects of domain shifts on model performance. This enables more robust generalization to the target domain. Recent advancements in DA have yielded substantial improvements across various tasks. SERC21 harnesses spatial neighborhood information and implements a class distribution matching strategy to tackle significant distribution shifts in hyperspectral image classification. To combat negative transfer, the ELT framework22 dynamically assesses partially transferable features via a rough disentanglement mechanism, while the parameter-free FIA module23 leverages a fuzzy inference system to assess sample transferability based on both class-related and domain-specific information. Architecturally, ECB24 integrates Vision Transformers (ViTs) and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) into a hybrid model to explicitly identify and refine class-specific boundaries. In semi-supervised settings, HiGDA25 constructs hierarchical local-to-global graphs to effectively capture topological structures, and EnSR26 combines data augmentation with graph aggregation to enhance the robustness of cross-domain semantic representations. Despite these advances, DA methods face a critical limitation: their heavy reliance on target-domain data. In many practical deployment scenarios, such data is often entirely inaccessible, making it impossible to perform adaptation. These constraints have driven the exploration of alternative paradigms, fostering increased interest in domain generalization techniques.

Fourier and frequency-domain transformations

Fourier transform provides an effective tool for analyzing frequency-domain characteristics and has been increasingly explored in domain generalization. FAF27 manipulates frequency amplitudes while preserving phase information to synthesize diverse training samples, while TFSA28 further calibrates frequency statistics at test time to mitigate distribution shifts during inference. DFFP29 enforces cross-domain consistency by explicitly aligning frequency-aware representations through dedicated loss functions. Beyond general-purpose modeling, frequency-domain processing and related spectral transformations have also been incorporated into specific vision tasks, such as FSF30 for suppressing background interference in segmentation, TSIAA31 for single-source domain generalization in medical image segmentation via low-frequency component replacement and instance-level adversarial augmentation, DGOD32 for enhancing detection robustness via global–local frequency transformations, FSCMF33 for preserving high-frequency texture details in cross-modality image fusion via discrete cosine transforms, and STWANet34 for accurately capturing complex boundary changes in remote sensing via wavelet feature enhancement. More recently, FFDI35 utilizes an encoder-decoder structure to disentangle high- and low-frequency features in the latent space. It then directly interacts these features via an information interaction mechanism, which effectively combines semantic and structural cues, leading to state-of-the-art performance on multiple domain generalization benchmarks for classification. Nevertheless, frequency-based domain generalization methods for crowd counting remain largely unexplored. Due to the unique characteristics of crowd counting, directly transferring frequency-based techniques from other vision tasks may be suboptimal. Moreover, current frequency-domain approaches typically adopt task-agnostic modeling strategies, where frequency components are manipulated or fused without explicit alignment to task objectives. In this work, we revisit frequency-domain modeling from the perspective of crowd counting and explore how frequency information can be incorporated in a task-aware and architecture-consistent manner to improve domain-generalized crowd counting.

Domain generalization (DG)

Domain Generalization (DG) aims to train a model that remains robust across unseen domains using only source-domain data. When only a single source domain is available, the problem is termed Single-source Domain Generalization (SDG). Within the scope of SDG, numerous cutting-edge technologies have emerged, including data augmentation technologies32,36,37, feature learning optimization technologies38–46, and model structure innovation technologies47–49, etc. While SDG has been extensively studied for classification and segmentation, its application to crowd counting is still in its infancy because density regression not only lacks clear categorical boundaries but also suffers from label noise and spatial ambiguity.

Among the few SDG works tailored for counting, DCCUS50 dynamically partitions source images into multiple pseudo-subdomains and employs a meta-learning framework to disentangle domain-invariant and domain-specific knowledge through two collaborative memory modules. Nevertheless, DCCUS presupposes a sufficiently diverse source distribution to facilitate meaningful subdomain delineation; in scenarios where the source domain is narrowly constrained, this partitioning proves unreliable, leading to instability in the regression memory. In addition, DCCUS does not explicitly handle the inherent noise in point-level annotations. MPCount17 introduces a patch-wise classification branch, explicitly designed to handle the inherent noise in point-level annotations. This branch is integrated with the main density regression branch to form a dual-branch architecture. The regression branch employs a shared memory bank, which removes the need for subdomain clustering and enables stable generalization even when the source data distribution is limited. Nevertheless, these memory-based approaches require accessing the memory during inference, which slows down inference speed. In contrast, SinCount is motivated by the intrinsic frequency characteristics of crowd patterns and their natural alignment with the objectives of a dual-branch architecture. By injecting high- and low-frequency cues into the corresponding branches via attention, SinCount achieves domain robustness without explicit memory and enables robust single-source domain generalization.

Instance normalization mask (INM)

Domain shifts in crowd counting often induce content-inconsistent feature responses, which can hinder the learning of domain-invariant representations. A commonly adopted approach to disentangle content and style information in deep features is instance normalization (IN)51, where channel-wise statistics (e.g., mean and variance) are regarded as style-related factors, while the normalized features primarily preserve content-related information. Building upon this paradigm, prior work has shown that discrepancies between instance-normalized features can effectively reflect the influence of domain shifts on feature content17. Inspired by this, we introduce an Instance Normalization Mask (INM) to suppress content-inconsistent regions during training. Given the original and augmented feature maps \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F^{ori}$$\end{document} , \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F^{aug} \in \mathbb {R}^{H \times W \times C}$$\end{document}, we first apply instance normalization to both features and compute their absolute difference to construct the mask \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{IN} \in \mathbb {R}^{H \times W \times C}$$\end{document}, defined as:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} M_{IN} = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1, & \text {if } | \text {IN}(F^{{ori}}) - \text {IN}(F^{{aug}}) | \le \alpha \\ 0, & \text {else} \end{array}\right. }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} is a predefined threshold. Intuitively, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{IN}$$\end{document} retains locations whose normalized responses remain relatively consistent between the original and augmented features, while suppressing unstable responses. Next, the corresponding feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F^{\chi } \in \{F^{ori}, F^{aug}\}$$\end{document} is refined using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{IN}$$\end{document} to obtain:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{F}^{\chi } = F^{\chi } \odot M_{IN}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{F}^{\chi } \in \{\tilde{F}^{ori}, \tilde{F}^{aug}\}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\odot$$\end{document} denotes the Hadamard product. In this way, INM effectively suppresses domain-sensitive feature responses while preserving content-consistent regions for subsequent learning.

Frequency-specific feature extraction (FSFE)

As discussed in the introduction, high-frequency and low-frequency components exhibit compatibilities with the objectives of the density regression and auxiliary classification branches, respectively. To explicitly model this task–frequency correspondence, we introduce a Frequency-Specific Feature Extraction (FSFE) module to learn branch-specific frequency-aware representations under the supervision of frequency-separated images. Specifically, FSFE adopts an encoder–decoder architecture to extract high-frequency embeddings from the density branch and low-frequency embeddings from the classification branch. The encoder–decoder design follows35, which we adapt to crowd counting for task-aligned frequency representation learning.

First, a 2D discrete Fourier transform is applied channel-wise to the input RGB image \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I^{\chi } \in \{ I^{ori}, I^{aug} \}$$\end{document} to obtain its frequency-domain representation. For each channel, the transform is defined as:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F(u, v) = \sum _{a=0}^{A-1} \sum _{b=0}^{B-1} x(a, b) e^{-j 2 \pi (au/A + bv/B)}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}For notational simplicity, we use \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {F}(\cdot )$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {F}^{-1}(\cdot )$$\end{document} to denote the Fourier transform and its inverse in the following derivations.

To extract high- and low-frequency embeddings, we first construct the low-frequency image and high-frequency image, and use them as fixed supervision targets for FSFE. Specifically, a frequency domain mask \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M(u,v)$$\end{document} is defined to separate the central (low-frequency) region from the periphery (high-frequency) in the frequency space:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} M(u,v) = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1, & (u,v) \in [c_x - r : c_x + r,\; c_y - r : c_y + r] \\ 0, & \text {otherwise} \end{array}\right. }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(c_x, c_y)$$\end{document} represents the frequency center and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r$$\end{document} is a parameter controlling the size of the low-frequency region. Based on this mask, the low-pass filtered image and high-pass filtered image are obtained as:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} LFI^{\chi } = \mathcal {F}^{-1}(\mathcal {F}(I^{\chi }) \odot M), \quad HFI^{\chi } = I^{\chi } - LFI^{\chi }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$LFI^{\chi } \in \{LFI^{ori},LFI^{aug}\}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$HFI^{\chi } \in \{HFI^{ori},HFI^{aug}\}$$\end{document}.

FSFE consists of an encoder \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E(\cdot )$$\end{document} and a decoder \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R(\cdot )$$\end{document}. The encoder takes the density feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{F}^{\chi } \in \{\tilde{F}^{ori},\tilde{F}^{aug}\}$$\end{document} as input to generate the high-frequency embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_H^{\chi } \in \{F_H^{ori},F_H^{aug}\}$$\end{document}, and takes the classification feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z^{\chi } \in \{Z^{ori},Z^{aug}\}$$\end{document} as input to generate the low-frequency embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_L^{\chi } \in \{F_L^{ori},F_L^{aug}\}$$\end{document}. The decoder \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R(\cdot )$$\end{document} then reconstructs the high-frequency image \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{HFI}^{\chi }$$\end{document} from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_H^{\chi }$$\end{document} and the low-frequency image \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{LFI}^{\chi }$$\end{document} from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_L^{\chi }$$\end{document}, respectively. By using the original frequency-separated images \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$HFI^{\chi }$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$LFI^{\chi }$$\end{document} as supervision signals, the two branch encoders are encouraged to learn frequency semantics aligned with their task objectives. High-frequency spatial attention (HFSA)

Although high-frequency embeddings contain rich structural cues that are beneficial to density regression, they cannot be exploited in a naive manner. Given the high-frequency feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_H^{\chi } \in \mathbb {R}^{H \times W \times C}$$\end{document}, we first apply global average pooling (GAP) and global max pooling (GMP) along the channel dimension to generate two spatial descriptors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{avg}^{\chi }, f_{max}^{\chi } \in \mathbb {R}^{H \times W \times 1}$$\end{document}:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} f_{avg,s}^{\chi } = \text {GAP}(F_H^{\chi }), \quad f_{max,s}^{\chi } = \text {GMP}(F_H^{\chi }), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{avg,s}^{\chi }$$\end{document} encodes global spatial statistics, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{max,s}^{\chi }$$\end{document} emphasizes local salient responses. We then concatenate them to form a fused feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{cat,s}^{\chi } \in \mathbb {R}^{H \times W \times 2}$$\end{document}, followed by a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3 \times 3$$\end{document} convolution and a sigmoid activation to generate the spatial attention map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{high}^{\chi } \in [0, 1]^{H \times W \times 1}$$\end{document}:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} A_{high}^{\chi } = \sigma (\text {Conv}_{3 \times 3}([f_{avg,s}^{\chi }, f_{max,s}^{\chi }])), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{high}^{\chi } \in \{ A_{high}^{ori}, A_{high}^{aug} \}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the sigmoid function. Attention consistency loss (ACL)

To ensure that the model consistently focuses on critical regions under different input conditions, we propose the Attention Consistency Loss (ACL). Experiments

In this section, we first describe the evaluation metrics used in our experiments. Then, the datasets are introduced, followed by the implementation details. We present the results compared with state-of-the-art methods, and finally conduct ablation studies and additional analyses.

Datasets

We evaluate the proposed method on four public datasets: ShanghaiTech1, UCF-QNRF52, JHU-Crowd++53, and RGBT-CC54.

ShanghaiTech and UCF-QNRF

ShanghaiTech consists of two parts: SHA (A) and SHB (B). Part A contains 300 training images and 182 test images, primarily sourced from the internet, depicting highly crowded scenes. Part B includes 400 training images and 316 test images, captured from various streets in Shanghai, where the crowd density is generally lower than in Part A. UCF-QNRF (Q) contains 1,201 training images and 334 test images, and is characterized by large variations in crowd density, scene diversity, perspective changes, and complex lighting conditions.

JHU-Crowd++

JHU-Crowd++ is a large-scale crowd counting dataset, consisting of 4,372 images. These are split into a training set of 2,722 images, a validation set of 500 images, and a test set of 1,600 images. Beyond its scale, the dataset provides rich image-level annotations, covering 16 distinct scene categories and 4 different weather conditions. These annotations make JHU-Crowd++ suitable for analyzing crowd counting performance under diverse scene and weather conditions. To facilitate domain-specific analysis, subsets are constructed based on selected scene and weather attributes. For example, 879 images labeled as ”stadium” (SD) and 573 images labeled as ”street” (SR) are chosen to represent two scene-specific domains, while 201 ”snow” (SN) and 168 ”fog/haze” (FH) images are used to define weather-specific domains. In each subset, 80% of the images are used for training and the remaining 20% for testing. Compared to standard datasets, these domain-focused partitions present more challenging and fine-grained distributions, making them suitable for evaluating single-source domain generalization performance.

RGBT-CC

RGBT-CC is a large-scale RGBT crowd counting benchmark, consisting of 1,013 image pairs captured under regular lighting conditions and 1,017 image pairs captured in dark environments (RD), covering diverse real-world scenes such as malls, streets, playgrounds, train stations, and metro stations. A key characteristic of this dataset is that images commonly exhibit blurring artifacts. In our experiments, we exclusively use the RGB modality and focus on the nighttime RD subset, without involving thermal data. This setting allows us to evaluate the robustness of models under common image degradations (e.g., blur) and severe domain shifts (e.g., nighttime conditions), thereby serving as a challenging benchmark for assessing their generalization ability. Representative samples of the RGBT-CC dataset are shown in Fig. 5.

Source-target domain setup

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S \rightarrow T$$\end{document} denote the case where S is the source domain and T is the target domain. The notation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S \leftrightarrow T$$\end{document} indicates that experiments are conducted in both directions between S and T. In our experiments, datasets are used under three different settings: 1) The entire dataset is considered a single domain: A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}B/Q, B\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}A/Q, and Q\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}A/B; 2) The datasets are further divided into subsets based on image-level labels, where each subset is treated as an individual domain: SD\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\leftrightarrow$$\end{document}SR and SN\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\leftrightarrow$$\end{document}FH; 3) Models trained on standard RGB datasets are directly evaluated under the A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}RD and B\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}RD settings to assess robustness against image perturbations and severe domain shifts induced by nighttime conditions.

Implementation details

We adopt VGG16-BN55 as the feature extractor in our model. The density regression head consists of a single convolutional layer with a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 1$$\end{document} filter, while the classification head is implemented using a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 1$$\end{document} convolutional layer. In the FSFE, the high- and low-frequency embedding encoders are both implemented as a single \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 1$$\end{document} convolutional layer. The high-frequency decoder consists of three \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3 \times 3$$\end{document} convolutional layers, while the low-frequency decoder is composed of five \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3 \times 3$$\end{document} convolutional layers. Batch normalization is applied to all convolutional layers except the final output layers. For data augmentation, we employ three types of photometric transformations: color jittering, Gaussian blur, and sharpening. In addition, we randomly crop \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$320 \times 320$$\end{document} patches from the input images and apply random horizontal flipping to both original and augmented samples. We utilize AdamW56 as the optimizer and OneCycleLR57 as the learning rate scheduler, with a maximum learning rate set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\text {e-}3$$\end{document} and the total number of training epochs set to 200. We use the same threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}= 0.5 for both the density features and low-frequency embeddings in the Instance Normalization Mask. The loss weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _{cls}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _{rec}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _{con}$$\end{document} are all set to 10, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _{{aux\_den}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _{{aux\_cls}}$$\end{document} are set to 0.5 and 5, respectively.

We note that in a few challenging scenes, the performance gains over MPCount are relatively modest. This phenomenon may be related to the characteristics of frequency-based representations. As illustrated in Fig. 1, high-frequency responses are not exclusively associated with human head contours, but may also be activated by other fine-grained structures in complex environments, such as tree branches, foliage, or textured backgrounds. In such cases, these non-crowd-related high-frequency patterns may introduce irrelevant responses and slightly affect the density estimation, particularly in scenes with cluttered backgrounds or dense structural details. While earlier methods (e.g., IBN and SW) require fewer parameters, they suffer from significantly higher error rates and lower inference speeds. Compared with memory-based methods such as DCCUS and the state-of-the-art MPCount, SinCount introduces a slight increase in parameters and GFLOPs due to the frequency-specific feature extraction (FSFE) module. However, by avoiding the complex memory-access operations required by these frameworks, SinCount achieves a notably higher inference speed. Theoretically, instance normalization (IN) preserves the underlying content structure by removing the channel-wise mean and variance that primarily encode domain-specific appearance variations. When the discrepancy at a spatial location is small, the feature is regarded as domain-invariant and retained; otherwise, the corresponding element is suppressed. Quantitative Analysis of Frequency-Task Alignment. While the visual examples provide intuitive evidence, we further conduct a quantitative analysis to examine the relationship between model-learned frequency responses and task performance under cross-domain transfer. Specifically, under the B\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}A setting, we analyze the correlation between the magnitude of the generated frequency-aware attention responses and the corresponding task errors across samples. As shown in Fig. 10(a), the magnitude of high-frequency attention exhibits a negative correlation with the absolute density error, indicating that high-frequency structural details are beneficial for precise density regression. Meanwhile, Fig. 10(b) shows that the magnitude of low-frequency attention exhibits a positive correlation with the classification Intersection over Union (IoU), suggesting that low-frequency semantic context facilitates accurate patch-wise classification. These quantitative results empirically support the design motivation of our framework.

Effect of Frequency Mask Radius. To examine the influence of the frequency-domain mask radius r in FSFE, we conduct ablation experiments on A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}B/Q. As shown in Table 6, the model achieves its best performance at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r=30$$\end{document} and maintains stable performance over a broad range (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r \in [30, 90]$$\end{document}). This stability can be attributed to the distinct roles of the dual-branch design. The auxiliary classification branch primarily leverages low-frequency information to suppress unreliable density predictions in non-crowd regions. Since this branch functions as a subtractive filter rather than a primary estimator of density values, the overall performance is relatively insensitive to variations in low-frequency content. In contrast, the final accuracy is mainly determined by the density regression branch, which relies on high-frequency structural cues. When r is excessively small, redundant high-frequency background textures are introduced, which can degrade the regression accuracy. However, as r increases within the range of 30–90, the essential structural signatures of human heads remain well-preserved despite the reduction in overall high-frequency components, thereby ensuring consistently stable performance. These results demonstrate that SinCount is highly robust to the frequency cutoff radius, facilitating steady generalization across diverse scenes without the need for exhaustive hyperparameter tuning.

The density estimation branch requires fine-grained, pixel-wise regression and therefore benefits from spatial attention applied to high-frequency structural cues. In contrast, the auxiliary classification branch focuses on identifying whether a region contains crowd instances, where coarse semantic information is predominantly encoded in feature channels and is more effectively modulated by channel attention on low-frequency semantic cues. Effect of Backbone Architectures. To investigate the impact of backbone selection on the generalization performance of SinCount, we conduct experiments using different backbone architectures, including VGG and ResNet, under identical training and evaluation protocols. All other components and hyperparameters are kept fixed to ensure a fair comparison across multiple cross-domain transfer scenarios. As reported in Table 11, the ResNet backbone achieves the best performance in a limited number of transfer settings, while the VGG backbone consistently yields lower errors in a larger portion of cross-domain tasks. This observation aligns with previous studies showing that VGG-style backbones are more effective for multi-scale density feature fusion and are better suited for crowd counting tasks17.

Conclusion

In this paper, we presented SinCount, a novel frequency-aware framework designed for Single-source Domain Generalization in crowd counting. By leveraging the intrinsic properties of the Fourier frequency domain, our method effectively addresses the challenge of domain shift without relying on complex memory-based architectures. Specifically, the introduced Frequency-Specific Feature Extraction (FSFE) module successfully disentangles high- and low-frequency components, which are further aligned with dual-branch objectives via our Frequency-Aware Dual Attention (FADA) mechanism. We demonstrated that applying high-frequency spatial attention to the density branch and low-frequency channel attention to the classification branch maximizes the synergy between task requirements and frequency cues. Furthermore, the integration of Instance Normalization Mask (INM) and Attention Consistency Loss (ACL) further bolsters the model’s robustness against domain-specific noise. Extensive experiments across multiple benchmarks demonstrate that SinCount achieves competitive cross-domain performance with superior inference efficiency, offering a practical and effective solution for real-world crowd counting tasks.
