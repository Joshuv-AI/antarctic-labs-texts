Association between Color Vision Deficiency and Police Officer Tasks in South Korea: A Prospective Pilot Study
Authors: Young Hee Jung, Kyungsik Kim, Li Lyung Wang, Jimin Kweon, Si Young Kim, Ju-Yeun Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeWe aimed to investigate the association between the results of color vision tests and simulation tests for police tasks among participants with color vision deficiencies (CVDs) and those with normal vision, and to assess the agreement between the results of various color vision tests.Materials and MethodsParticipants with CVDs and healthy controls were recruited. Participants in the CVD group were classified into three subgroups (mild weakness, severe weakness, and color blindness). All participants performed simulation tests (bloodstain identification, traffic light test, and analysis of dashboard camera recordings) and clinical color vision tests. We statistically analyzed the association between color vision tests and simulation tests and quantitatively assessed the agreement between various color vision tests.ResultsWe evaluated 25 participants with normal vision (92.0% male) and 21 with CVDs (95.2% male). Even in the mild CVD group, the scores for the traffic light test and dashboard camera recordings were lower than those of the control group (9.40±0.57 vs. 9.06±0.84, and 1.80±0.40 vs. 1.44±0.77, all p<0.05). However, no difference in the bloodstain identification test scores was observed between the control and CVD groups (p=0.190). Additionally, the association with anomaloscope results was strongest in the following order: Ishihara, Hardy–Rand–Rittler, Farnsworth–Munsell 100 hue, and Farnsworth D-15 tests.ConclusionThis study demonstrated a correlation between the results of clinical color vision tests and police-related simulation tests. Particularly, the score for recognizing traffic signs or analyzing dashboard camera recordings was lower even in those with mild CVD than in those with normal vision.

INTRODUCTION

Color vision deficiency (CVD) is a condition where one has difficulty in distinguishing colors due to abnormalities in the cone cells.1 In color blindness, specific colors cannot be fully perceived due to dysfunction of the cone cells in the retina,12 whereas in color weakness, colors can be detected but with reduced sensitivity due to receptor dysfunction, making it difficult to recognize certain colors.2 There are three types of CVD: type 1 CVD (red weakness, protan), type 2 CVD (green weakness, deutan), and type 3 CVD (blue weakness, tritan)2 (Supplementary Table 1, only online). In South Korea, the prevalence of CVD is reported to be 5.6% among men and 0.2% among women in the general population.3 Compared with the Western population, where the prevalence of CVD is 8% in men and 0.5% in women, South Koreans have relatively lower prevalence of CVD. Notably, most men with CVDs in South Korea have been reported to have type 2 CVD (72.3%).3

The severity and type of CVD are closely related to the level of discomfort experienced in daily life.4 A previous study reported that 66% of patients with color weakness and 99% of those with color blindness experience difficulties in daily life or in the workplace.5 Therefore, various job sectors where employees can be affected by color vision have hiring criteria related to CVDs.6 Sectors such as those for railroad workers, aviators, and mariners do not permit any degree of CVD, regardless of the severity of color weakness. Some institutions, including military academies and the National Fire Agency, classify job duties to differentiate applicant qualifications, and transfer between different job categories is not allowed.67 In South Korea, the recruitment of individuals with CVDs beyond a mild degree is restricted in the police force.8 In most countries, police recruitment standards for color vision are as strict as or stricter than those currently in South Korea.9

Owing to the nature of police work, tasks like recognizing traffic light colors, identifying bloodstains, and interpreting dashboard camera recordings are critical duties where color vision can have a significant impact.101112 However, despite the widely recognized impact of color vision on these tasks, the association between the actual tasks and the degree or type of CVD in police officers has not yet been comprehensively studied. Therefore, we focused on elucidating this relationship to provide scientific evidence. This study aimed to investigate the association between the results of color vision tests and simulation tests for police tasks among participants with CVDs compared with those with normal vision, and to assess the agreement between the results of various color vision tests.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants and ascertainment of study groups

Participants with red-green CVD (protan/deutan) and healthy controls without CVD were prospectively recruited at Myongji Hospital, Goyang, South Korea, from June 2023 to September 2023. All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination, including Snellen chart visual acuity, intraocular pressure measurement, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and fundus photography. Only participants with normal ocular findings were included, defined as best-corrected visual acuity of 1.0 and no abnormal findings on ocular examination, including the macula. Participants with central nervous system disorders affecting color vision, those taking medications that affect color vision, those with ocular diseases, and those unable to undergo the simulation experiment were excluded due to potential interference. Participants were randomly recruited from a pool of police officers and members of the general public. To minimize potential confounding due to job experience, only officers with ≤3 years of service in the police patrol division were included; those from other divisions, including the criminal investigation division, were excluded. Healthy controls were defined as participants who met all eligibility criteria after completing all examinations and had no evidence of CVD.

Based on the results of anomaloscopes, which are a standard tool for diagnosing CVD and determining its severity, all participants with CVD were classified into two types—protan and deutan—and into three subgroups based on severity: mild weakness, severe weakness, and color blindness. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study participation. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Myongji Hospital (IRB # 2023-06-054-006) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All information was anonymized prior to the analysis.

Color vision assessment

Color vision was assessed monocularly in each eye by three trained single-blind researchers (L.W., J.K., and S.K.). When specified, the background environmental lighting was set according to the test manufacturer’s specifications. The type and degree of CVD were assessed using the following tests: 1) HMC-Anomaloscope (Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany);13 2) Farnsworth D-15 test;14 3) Farnsworth–Munsell (FM) 100 hue test;15 4) Hardy–Rand–Rittler (HRR) plates (4th Edition; Richmond Products Inc., Albuquerque, New Mexico, USA);1617 and 5) the Ishihara test for color blindness, using 24 plates1819 (Supplementary Material, only online).

Simulation test

The test consisted of 18 items, categorized into three groups: 1) traffic lights, 2) bloodstains, and 3) interpretation of colored dashboard camera recordings. Each bloodstain was classified into easy- or difficult-to-detect, and this classification was reflected in the score through weighted values. The classification of the traces was determined by experts and researchers. The total duration of the test was 10 minutes, with the allocated time for each item ranging from 1 minute to 3 minutes, depending on its difficulty. The simulation test was conducted with best-corrected visual acuity. All the participants completed their simulation tests in the same environment, location, and duration. Prior to the test, the participants were given a sufficient explanation, and their understanding of the procedure was confirmed. Each test was conducted only once (Fig. 1). The total scores for each category were summed and used in the final analysis.

Quantification of test results and statistical analysis

To define the degree and type of CVD, all color vision test results were quantified and expressed as continuous values. In addition to determining the type of CVD, the scores were defined as the number of chips or plates correctly identified in the Farnsworth D-15 test, HRR, and Ishihara tests. In the FM 100 hue test, the automatically calculated total error score and axis were obtained. Using the anomaloscope, total R, partial R, total anomaly quotient (AQ), and partial AQ values were obtained. Total R referred to the sum of deviations from the mean of the normal R values over the entire range (0 to 73) of the mixture scale. The mean of the normal R values was derived from the results of the controls enrolled in this study. Partial R referred to the total sum of deviations from the mean of normal R values in the range of 10 to 70 on the mixture scale, excluding values at 0 and 73 (this exclusion was applied because the R values at either end were very large and could bias the results). The total and partial AQ values were calculated using the same method.

Primarily, results were compared between the control and CVD groups, followed by comparisons within the CVD groups (mild, severe, color blindness). In particular, the results of the mild CVD group were compared with those of the control group. Descriptive characteristics of the sample were reported as means and standard deviations, as was the duration of the tests. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between study groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test or the Kruskal–Wallis test. When the Kruskal–Wallis test showed statistical significance, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferronicorrected Mann–Whitney U test. Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software (version 24.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A p-value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of participants

We recruited 25 participants with normal vision (92.0% male, age 32.5±11.0 years) and 21 with CVDs (95.2% male, age 27.2±6.6 years). A difference in age was observed between the two groups; however, since CVD is congenital, and both groups were in their 20s and 30s, the likelihood that this age difference significantly affected the study results was considered low. Among participants with CVDs, 16 had mild CVDs, 2 had severe CVDs, and 3 had color blindness. According to the type of CVD, 17 had deutan CVD and 4 had protan CVD; all cases of protan CVD were mild. Demographic information is presented in Table 1.

Comparison of the results of various color vision tests among the groups

In the gold standard anomaloscope test, all CVD groups exhibited significantly poorer performance compared to the control group in terms of total R deviation (1.40±0.92 vs. 14.09±13.78, p<0.001), partial R deviation (1.40±0.93 vs. 12.42±14.79, p<0.001), total AQ deviation (1.06±0.23 vs. 37.50±49.15, p<0.001), and partial AQ deviation (1.06±0.24 vs. 9.13±5.02, p<0.001). Statistically significant differences were also observed between the control and CVD groups in all other color vision tests (all p<0.001) (Table 1).

In the sub-analysis, significant differences were observed between the control and mild CVD groups in all color vision tests (all p<0.001). A significant difference was observed among CVD severity groups in all color vision test results, except for total R deviation and total AQ deviation in the anomaloscope test.

Comparison of the results of the simulation test among the groups

In the traffic light test, participants in the control group correctly identified nearly all items, whereas a significantly lower accuracy rate was noted in the CVD group (score: 9.40±0.57 vs. 7.29±2.90, p<0.001). Even the mild CVD group exhibited a lower accuracy than the control group (9.40±0.57 vs. 9.06±0.84, p=0.007). All participants with protan CVD failed to pass the specific item requiring identification of as many umbrellas of a given color as possible within the time limit, using a rally photo.

The bloodstain identification test yielded the lowest number of incorrect responses. No significant difference in the ability to distinguish bloodstains was observed between the control and the entire CVD group (p=0.190). Additionally, no significant difference was observed between the mild CVD and control groups (p=0.925). Among the participants with CVD, no statistically significant differences were observed in the mean bloodstain identification scores according to the degree of CVD (9.06±1.16 (mild) vs. 8.50±0.70 (severe) vs. 6.33±3.88 (blindness), p=0.050).

In the dashboard camera recordings test, the overall CVD group showed significantly lower scores than the control group (1.80±0.40 vs. 1.24±0.82, p<0.001). In the sub-analysis, the mild CVD group also showed significantly lower scores than the control group (1.80±0.40 vs. 1.44±0.77, p=0.022). Additionally, significant differences were observed in the mean scores for the dashboard camera recordings test according to the degree of CVD (p=0.007). Fig. 2 presents the differences in each test across the CVD severity groups, along with the results of the post hoc analysis.

When comparing CVD subtypes, participants with protan CVD showed significantly lower scores in the traffic lights test than those with deutan CVD (p=0.035). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in the bloodstain test (p=0.127) or the dashboard camera recordings test (p=0.959).

Correlation between simulation test and color vision tests

The results of the correlation analysis between each color vision test and different categories of simulation test are presented in Table 2. Among all the color vision tests conducted in this study, the HRR test demonstrated the highest correlation with the overall score (r=0.631) and with scores for the traffic light (r=0.690), bloodstains (r=0.430), and dashboard camera recordings (r=0.487) categories of the simulation test (all p<0.01). The anomaloscope results showed the second-highest correlation with each category of the simulation test, particularly for the total R deviation value (all p<0.01). Among the simulation test categories, bloodstain scores demonstrated the weakest correlation with color vision test results, while traffic light scores demonstrated the strongest.

Agreement among the different color vision tests

The agreement between the results of the different color vision tests is presented in Supplementary Table 2 (only online). All quantified R deviation and AQ deviation values from the anomaloscope test showed significant correlations with the results of other color vision tests (all p<0.01). When quantified, the highest correlations with anomaloscope results were observed in the following order: Ishihara, HRR, FM 100 hue, and Farnsworth D-15 tests. The strongest correlation was observed between the Ishihara and HRR test results (r=0.818, p<0.001). Overall, the Farnsworth D-15 test results showed relatively low correlation with the results of the other color vision tests.

Additionally, we compared HRR and anomaloscope classifications after categorizing both results into four severity levels (normal, mild, moderate, and strong). Cross-tabulation showed a strong statistical association between the two methods (χ2=119.00, p<0.001), with a substantial level of agreement (Cohen’s κ=0.590). Cross-tabulation revealed that most participants classified as mild by the HRR test (86.5%) were also classified as mild by the anomaloscope. Participants categorized as strong by the HRR test were perfectly matched with the anomaloscope results.

DISCUSSION

In this prospective pilot study, we investigated the association between the results of color vision tests and simulation tests among participants and assessed the agreement between the results of various color vision tests. We found a clear association between police-related simulation tests and color vision tests in individuals with CVD. Additionally, the anomaloscope test, which is considered the gold standard, showed a high correlation with various color vision tests. For police tasks such as recognizing traffic signs or analyzing dashboard camera recordings, even individuals with mild CVD showed significant differences compared to those with normal vision.

Despite the widely recognized impact of color vision on police officers’ tasks, few studies have investigated the association between task performance and the degree or type of CVD in this population. In a previous study, Birch and Chisholm20 conducted a survey of 500 police officers in Northern Ireland to assess the impact of CVD on police work. Over 65% of the respondents reported that good color vision was very important. While color vision is considered less critical than other visual functions such as visual acuity and peripheral vision in performing police duties, it is evident that color vision has a practical impact on police work. However, the clinical correlation between job performance and the severity of CVD has not yet been quantified. In this study, we developed tests that simulated practical duties, improved the accuracy of CVD detection through various color vision tests, and evaluated police task performance according to the degree of CVD. Our first major finding was that participants with CVD (protan or deutan) had significantly lower scores than controls in the dashboard camera recordings and traffic light tests, whereas no significant difference was found in the bloodstain identification test. We also found an association between CVD severity and simulation test performance, indicating that as CVD severity increased, simulation test scores decreased. Our findings can be supported by the results of previous studies related to recognizing traffic lights.21 In a traffic signal recognition study conducted in Japan including 186 participants with CVDs, the highest error rate was reported among those with even mild red weakness (14.6%).22 Atchison, et al.23 examined the impact of CVD on traffic signal reaction time and accuracy, reporting that response time to red lights increased with CVD severity.

Based on this biological mechanism, it can be inferred that even individuals with mild CVD performed significantly worse than those with normal vision in the dashboard camera recordings and traffic light tests. When visual stimuli enter the visual cortex, they are transmitted from V1 to secondary visual areas. After V1, the visual perceptual attributes of color and motion undergo separate primary processing.24 When perceiving objects, color and motion are first perceived independently.24 These pieces of information are then integrated to ultimately recognize the shape and identity of the object. Contrary to the expectation that color blindness would affect color perception and not motion perception, our study found that individuals with mild CVD performed significantly worse than those with normal vision in the dashboard camera recordings and the traffic light tests (which involved flashing signals), where motion perception is crucial. This suggests that individuals with mild CVD may have functional impairments not only in color perception but also in motion perception. Particularly in the dashboard camera recordings and traffic light tests, individuals with mild CVD performed significantly worse than those with normal vision.

In contrast, the bloodstain test primarily involves distinguishing between different colors and forms. The inferotemporal lobe is primarily involved in form perception. Form perception receives input from various sources, including color, direction, and position. Since all of this information contributes to form perception, there have been no reported cases of form blindness. 2425 For this reason, the error rate for the bloodstain test, which involves perceiving form without motion, may be lower even among individuals with CVD.

Our second major finding was that simulation test performance is associated with the results of clinical color vision tests, including the anomaloscope and HRR tests. CVD is a cone cell abnormality in the retina identified by clinical color vision tests, whereas simulation tests assess visual perception from the retina to the visual cortex, integrating color with other visual information such as motion and location. It is crucial to verify whether clinical color vision tests align with simulation tests in evaluating real-world visual tasks. Most tests, including the HRR, showed strong correlations with simulation tasks such as the traffic light and dashboard camera recordings tests, potentially reflecting the impact of CVD on job performance.

Finally, we observed the correlations between various color vision tests. The strongest correlation with the anomaloscope test results was observed in the following order: Ishihara, HRR, and FM 100 hue tests. In addition, the anomaloscope, Ishihara, and HRR tests showed strong correlations with the simulation test results, whereas the FM 100 hue test did not.

Distinguishing CVD severity is challenging with most tests, and relying on a single result may be inaccurate. The anomaloscope is the gold standard but is time-consuming and requires skill, making sole reliance on it problematic. While identifying CVD or color blindness is straightforward, accurately quantifying severity often requires the use of multiple tests. The strength of our study lies in quantifying anomaloscope measurements, enhancing the accuracy of CVD assessment by using R and AQ deviation values rather than relying solely on pattern-based or pictorial assessments. These quantitative measures show potential as future auxiliary indicators. Additionally, by developing simulation tests with experts that reflect practical police tasks, we highlighted the functional limitations of CVD in job performance. Unlike previous studies, our research quantified the results of various color vision tests and identified significant differences between individuals with CVD and those with normal vision.

Our study has several limitations. First, the small sample size, due to the rarity of CVD and a limited research period, may affect the generalizability, though the results are clinically meaningful. Larger studies are needed to confirm these findings. Second, while we collaborated with police agencies to create realistic simulation tests, these may not fully replicate actual police tasks. Future studies should aim to validate the simulations against real-world task performance. Third, incorporating brain imaging, such as fMRI during task performance, could provide insights into the neural correlates of color vision and task performance, which we plan to explore in future research. Finally, the number of participants with protan CVD was relatively small, which may have limited the statistical power to detect differences in task performance between CVD subtypes. Future studies with larger and more balanced samples across CVD types are needed to better assess task-specific functional differences.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrated the association between police-related simulation tests and clinical color vision tests in individuals with CVDs. Additionally, the anomaloscope test, which is considered the gold standard, showed a high correlation with various clinical color vision tests. For police tasks such as recognizing traffic signs or analyzing dashboard camera recordings, even those with mild CVD showed significant differences compared with those with normal vision. Therefore, individuals with CVD showed poor performance in clinical color vision tests and police-related simulation tests. CVD may be considered when assigning duties in related fields.
