Deviations between registered protocols and published systematic reviews in high-impact anesthesiology and pain medicine journals: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Alessandro De Cassai, Burhan Dost, Nicole Asti, Angela Bianco, Maria Bisi, Elena Curmaci, Tommaso Antonio Giacon, Marco Nardelli, Serkan Tulgar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeProtocol registration is essential for preventing selective reporting bias in systematic reviews, yet deviations from registered protocols remain common across medical fields. We sought to evaluate the prevalence of protocol registration and assessed concordance between registered protocols and published systematic reviews in high-impact anesthesiology journals, hypothesizing that undeclared deviations would be prevalent.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of systematic reviews published in 2025 in first-quartile anesthesiology journals according to Journal Citation Reports (Clarivate, London, UK). We searched PubMed® in July 2025 to identify eligible studies. Two reviewers independently assessed concordance between registered protocols and published reviews using a standardized 22-item extraction form covering eligibility criteria; the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study Design (PICOS) framework; search methods; risk assessment tools; and planned analyses. We recorded agreement as binary outcome, with explicit acknowledgement of deviations noted.ResultsOf 172 identified systematic reviews, we included 114 after excluding nonregistered studies and those with post hoc registration. All 114 (100%) reviews reported at least one protocol deviation, but only 18 (16%) provided justifications. The most frequent deviation was authorship change (74%), resulting in 317 additional authors without acknowledgement. PICOS framework variations occurred in 65% of reviews, with primary outcome changes in 15% (disclosed in only two cases). Search strategy deviations occurred in 28% of studies, and 38% added Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) assessments without prior registration.ConclusionsOur study identified at least one deviation from the registered protocol in all of the 114 included systematic reviews published in high-impact anesthesiology journals. Universal protocol deviations with minimal disclosure threaten the integrity of systematic reviews in anesthesiology and pain medicine. These findings show an urgent need for stricter protocol adherence and transparent reporting of deviations by authors, with enhanced oversight by peer reviewers and journal editors.Study registrationOpen Science Framework (https://osf.io/ksezg); first submitted 10 July 2025.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12630-026-03168-6.

High-quality systematic reviews (SRs) and meta-analyses play a pivotal role in synthesizing evidence to inform clinical practice and policy decisions; for this reason, it is of paramount importance that authors make all efforts to improve the overall quality of the manuscript and reduce the possibility of bias whenever it is possible.1 Nevertheless, several studies have suggested that a substantial proportion of SRs are affected by several bias, such as outcome reporting bias,2,3 which arises when the selection of reported outcomes is influenced by the results, with the direct consequence of providing possible misleading conclusions. To minimize this risk, relevant guidelines such as Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA)4 recommend the prospective registration of SR protocols and encourage authors to transparently report and justify any deviations from the original plan, ensuring the credibility and reproducibility of the review.

In fact, registering a protocol before initiating the review acts as a safeguard against selective reporting bias, outcome switching, and other deviations from the planned methodology. Over the years, several databases, such as International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews, have been established and widely adopted as repositories for these protocols, making them publicly accessible and promoting transparency in systematic reviews.

Despite the adoption of protocol registration, discrepancies between registered protocols and published studies are prevalent. A systematic review and meta-analysis encompassing 89 studies found that between 29% and 37% of examined studies exhibited at least one primary outcome discrepancy, with secondary outcome discrepancies ranging from 50% to 75%. These inconsistencies often involved changes in primary outcomes, omission of planned outcomes, or the introduction of new outcomes not specified in the protocol.5

Moreover, the proportion of SRs with registered protocols may be lower than expected. One study investigating SR protocol registration in high-impact medical journals found that, although the rate of registration increased over the years—from 6% in 2009 to 27% in 2015—it remained relatively low.6

Although the implications of such discrepancies are substantial—potentially leading to selective reporting, where only favorable outcomes are published, thereby distorting the evidence base, undermining the credibility of SRs, and misleading clinical decision-making3—there are currently no data on this issue in anesthesiology journals.

Whereas SR methodology is shared across medical disciplines, several characteristics of anesthesiology research suggest that the prevalence and nature of protocol–publication discrepancies may differ from other fields. Anesthesiology SRs frequently synthesize heterogeneous clinical trials spanning perioperative medicine, critical care, pain, and regional anesthesia—areas in which outcomes are often inconsistently defined and measured.7,8 Such variability may incentivize post hoc outcome selection or modification, thereby increasing the risk of selective reporting. In addition, anesthesiology research frequently involves complex, multicomponent interventions—such as combinations of anesthetic agents, monitoring strategies, and perioperative care pathways—which can increase the likelihood that planned analyses evolve during the review process. Moreover, recent evaluations have reported that the methodologic quality of network meta-analyses in anesthesiology is often “critically low,” suggesting that inconsistencies or deviations between protocols and published manuscripts may also be present.9 Taken together, these factors make it plausible that the pattern of protocol–publication discrepancies in anesthesiology systematic reviews differs from that observed in the broader medical literature.

The aim of this study was to evaluate discrepancies between registered protocols and published SRs in high-impact anesthesiology journals, with a focus on differences in primary and secondary outcomes, inclusion criteria, and planned analyses. We hypothesized that, as observed in other fields, published SRs in anesthesiology are also affected by undeclared deviations from their registered protocols. Additionally, in this study, we sought to explore the prevalence of protocol registration and selective outcome reporting and to assess whether deviations from registered protocols are transparently justified.

Methods

This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 reporting guidelines.4

A protocol for the present study was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) repository (reference: ksezg) on 10 July 2025 and is available online for consultation (https://osf.io/ksezg).

We identified target journals from the 2025 release of Journal Citation Reports (JCR; Clarivate, London, UK) as those whose Journal Impact Factor was ranked in the first quartile in the “Anesthesiology” category. While being assigned to the first quartile has no direct relationship with the quality of the journals, it is commonly used in scientometric publications as a proxy of journal quality,10,11 as it means that articles from these journals have received the most citations in the previous 3 years. In 2025, JCR identified 18 journals in the first quartile, the list of which is available in Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) eAppendix 1.

To identify all eligible SRs, we conducted a search of the PubMed® database on 10 July 2025. No restrictions were applied regarding language or publication status, only limiting inclusion to SRs published in 2025. The search strategy was evaluated internally using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies 2015 Evidence-Based Checklist,12 but it was not externally peer-reviewed. Electronic Supplementary Material eAppendix 2 reports the complete search strategy.

Two researchers independently screened the full texts of all retrieved articles according to predefined eligibility criteria. We included preregistered SRs published in 2025 in high-impact anesthesiology journals, defined as journals within the first quartile of the “Anesthesiology” category of the JCR. Only full-length articles were eligible; SRs published in abbreviated formats (e.g., letters to the editor or short reports) were excluded. In addition, SRs were excluded from the final analysis if the registration date occurred after the date specified in the manuscript for the literature search. This criterion was applied to ensure that protocol registration was not influenced by prior knowledge of study results obtained during the initial screening.

To assess concordance between systematic reviews and their registered or published protocols, we developed a standardized data extraction sheet that is available at OSF (https://osf.io/ksezg) (ESM eAppendix 3). It captured 22 items among them: eligibility criteria; search methods; review processes; risk of bias and certainty assessments; specification of Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study Design (PICOS) elements; outcomes; planned analyses; declared deviations; and team composition. For each item, concordance between protocol and review was recorded as a binary outcome (agreement: yes/no); in addition, we noted whether any deviation had been explicitly acknowledged by the authors in the article. The extraction form was built by two members of the research team (A. D. C. and B. D.) and pretested by all the team members.

Agreement for each item was independently assessed by two reviewers, with reasons for discordance between protocol and review recorded. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and when consensus was not achieved, arbitration was sought from a third author (A. D. C.).

Deviations from the protocol

We extracted some additional data beyond what was originally planned. Specifically, we collected information on the country of the authors and the topic of each systematic review to enhance the contextual understanding and representativeness of our sample. These variables allowed us to describe the diversity of topics covered, the range of countries of origin, and the extent of international collaboration among the included reviews (ESM eAppendix 4).

Furthermore, during the peer-review process, we extracted supplementary information, including whether the systematic review reported adherence to any reporting guidelines and the specific version cited and whether a PRISMA checklist or PRISMA for Abstracts checklist was available within the manuscript or its supplementary materials.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was limited to descriptive methods. Categorical variables are summarized as absolute frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges [IQRs] or means with standard deviations, as appropriate. We performed no inferential analyses.

Results

The search, conducted on 13 July 2025, identified 207 articles. We excluded 35 studies with a study design other than a full-text systematic review, leaving 172 systematic reviews for assessment. Of these, three did not report the search date, 16 lacked evidence of protocol registration, and 40 reported a search conducted after protocol registration. We contacted corresponding authors of the 16: eight responded, confirming in seven cases that no protocol had been registered, and in one that registration existed but was omitted from the manuscript. Accordingly, we excluded 15, resulting in 114 systematic reviews included in the final analysis. Study characteristics

Of the 114 systematic reviews included (ESM eAppendix 6), 75 (66%) conducted a pairwise meta-analysis, 11 (10%) performed a network meta-analysis, and 28 (25%) did not include a quantitative synthesis.

The geographical distribution of the studies showed that 19 (17%) originated from Asia, 43 (38%) from Europe, 34 (30%) from North America, 11 (10%) from Oceania, and 7 (6%) from South America. At the country level, the most frequent affiliations of first authors were in Canada (21; 18%), the USA (13; 11%), the UK (13; 11%), Australia (11; 10%), China (11; 10%), and Spain (9; 8%). Notably, 43 (38%) studies were produced through international collaborations.

Among the 114 systematic reviews included, the most frequently investigated topics were chronic pain (31; 27%) and perioperative anesthesia, sedation, and perioperative outcomes (29; 25%). These were followed by surgical and postoperative outcomes (17; 15%) and regional anesthesia (19; 17%). Critical care, intensive care unit, and ventilation accounted for nine (8%) reviews, while health and demographic disparities were addressed in six (5%) reviews. Airway management was the least represented topic, with only three (3%) reviews.

All the 114 included SRs reported at least one deviation from their registered protocol (e.g., changes in the number of authors, PICOS framework, search strategy or restrictions, risk of bias or quality of evidence assessment, and sensitivity or subgroup analyses). Nevertheless, only 18 (16%) SRs provided justifications for some or all of these deviations (ESM eTable).

Reporting guideline adherence

Among the included articles, 63 (55%) cited the primary PRISMA 2020 guideline, 15 (13%) cited the primary PRISMA 2009 guideline, two (2%) cited Preferred Reporting Items for Overviews of Reviews (PRIOR), 1 (0.9%) cited Meta-analyses of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE), and one (0.9%) cited Synthesis without Meta-analysis (SWiM). Regarding PRISMA extensions, 10 (9%) cited PRISMA Network Meta-Analysis, 2 (2%) cited PRISMA Protocols (PRISMA-P), and 1 (0.9%) cited PRISMA Search Extension; among these, 5 (4%) reported two reporting guidelines (one primary PRISMA and one extension). Additionally, 21 (18%) articles declared adherence to PRISMA without providing a proper reference (one referred only to the PRISMA website). The remaining three (3%) articles did not mention any reporting checklist. Only 19 (17%) articles appended a checklist in the manuscript or supplementary material, and among these, only six (5%) included the PRISMA Abstract checklist.

Authorship

Protocols were authored by a median [IQR] of 4 [3–6] authors, while the corresponding published SRs had a median [IQR] of 7 [5–9] authors. The authorship between protocols and SRs matched completely in only 21 (18%) cases. In 84 (74%) cases, additional authors were included in the final SR; in four (3%) cases, the SR had fewer authors than the protocol; and in five (4%) cases, the number of authors was the same but not all original protocol authors contributed to the final SR. Overall, in 14 (12%) cases, at least one protocol author was not listed in the published SR. These modifications from protocol to SR resulted in a total increase of 317 authors across publications; none of these changes were justified in the final papers.

PICOS framework

Within the PICOS framework, 74 (65%) SRs reported variation in at least one element. Namely, 18 (16%) studies focused on different populations and 14 of them by applying stricter inclusion criteria—seven restricted by pathology, four by specific demographic characteristics, and three by including patients undergoing a specific anesthesia technique. In contrast, four studies adopted broader criteria, expanding the population of interest (two by pathology and two by demographic characteristics). One (0.9%) study investigated a slightly different intervention, incorporating additional regional anesthesia techniques beyond what was initially planned. In 10 (9%) studies, we found discrepancies in the control groups: in three studies, additional interventions were included; in four, the description provided in the published paper was more detailed and stricter than in the protocol; and in three, the description was broader. The primary outcome differed from the planned outcomes in 17 (15%) articles, although this protocol deviation was declared in only two. In 11 articles, the reported primary outcome had initially been registered as a secondary outcome, while in the remaining cases, the reported primary outcome had not been planned at all. Regarding secondary outcomes, 48 (42%) articles reported discrepancies compared with the registered protocols: In eight articles, additional secondary outcomes were reported; in 26, some planned secondary outcomes were not performed; and in the remaining articles, we observed a combination of these deviations.

Regarding study designs, most of the studies (94; 82%) were consistent, whereas 20 (18%) included different designs. Of these, 10 incorporated multiple study designs, while nine restricted the analysis to a narrower range of designs (one declared deviation from the protocol).

Search strategy

Only 18 (16%) articles reported a prespecified search strategy in their protocol. Of these, two reported deviations from the declared approach, with one deviation explicitly disclosed.

In 32 cases, we observed deviations in the databases searched: 13 studies had planned but did not perform searches in certain databases, 17 conducted searches in databases that were not originally planned, and in two cases both types of deviations occurred. Overall, the studies registered a median [IQR] of 3 [4–5] databases and reported using a median [IQR] of 3 [4–5] databases in the published article. Restrictions

Of the studies, 23 reported deviations related to language restrictions: four studies applied restrictions to languages different from those specified in the protocol, while the remaining 19 used language restrictions that had not been preregistered.

With regard to date restrictions, nine studies deviated from their protocols: in two studies, a date restriction was planned but not reported in the article; one study applied a different date restriction than planned; and the remaining studies used a date restriction that was not preregistered in the protocol.

Risk of bias and quality of evidence assessment

The tools used for risk of bias assessment matched those specified in the SR protocol in 82 cases (72%). In 16 (14%) cases, only some of the preregistered tools were used, while in another 16 (14%) cases, completely different tools were applied. Risk of bias assessment was conducted by the planned number of reviewers in 96 (84%) cases. In 14 (12%) cases, fewer reviewers performed the assessment than initially planned, whereas in four (3%) cases, more reviewers participated than originally specified. Regarding quality of evidence assessment using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework, it was performed but not prespecified in the protocol in 43 SRs (38%), whereas in two (2%) cases, it was preregistered but not reported in the final publication.

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis

In 11 (10%) SRs, sensitivity analyses were prespecified in the protocol but not conducted. Subgroup analyses were carried out without prior declaration in 22 (19%) SRs. A number of planned subgroup analyses (37 SRs, 32%) and sensitivity analyses (35 SRs, 31%) were not performed, most likely because the authors did not identify a sufficient number of studies to enable meaningful analyses.

Discussion

Our study identified at least one deviation from the registered protocol in all of the 114 included SRs published in high-impact anesthesiology journals. The most common discrepancies involved authorship changes, PICOS framework modifications, and unplanned or omitted subgroup and sensitivity analyses.

Our study provides evidence that, while most SRs in anesthesiology and pain medicine published in high-impact journals in 2025 were preregistered, this practice is not yet universal. Notably, 9% (15 out of 172) of the SRs we examined were not preregistered. While this percentage may appear high, it compares favorably with the estimated 73% of SRs not preregistered in high-quality journals across all fields.6

Among the preregistered SRs, most authors referred to PRISMA guidelines, although sometimes an older version was cited. Moreover, the inclusion of checklists as verifiable material for readers was limited. It is possible that authors completed the checklists but did not submit them to the journal, used them privately to verify their manuscript, or sent them directly to the editor without including them in the supplementary material; our study design cannot verify these scenarios. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that when checklists were provided, the PRISMA Abstract checklist was frequently missing, even if it had been cited in the primary checklist. These gaps highlight an important area for improving transparency and completeness in reporting, particularly to ensure that abstract-level reporting aligns with the main manuscript checklist.

A key finding of our investigation is the common occurrence of deviations from protocols. We found evidence of at least one deviation in all SRs examined. Although this finding may seem extremely high, it aligns with research in other fields.13 Deviations from preregistered protocols are not inherently problematic and may reflect legitimate methodologic refinements. Nevertheless, the failure to disclose or justify them undermines both the reproducibility and trustworthiness of evidence syntheses. Despite the prevalence of these changes, only 18 (16%) SRs provided complete or partial explanations for them, suggesting that either transparency in reporting remains limited or authors do not perceive protocol deviations as problematic.

The most frequent deviation was a change in authorship, with 74% of SRs showing different author lists between protocol registration and the final manuscript. This resulted in a cumulative increase of 317 authors across the corpus, none of whom were acknowledged or explained in the published articles. Such discrepancies raise serious concerns about academic credit, accountability, and the potential for “authorship inflation.”10,14

Moreover, almost two thirds of SRs showed variations within the PICOS framework. In our sample, the reassignment or introduction of primary outcomes occurred in nearly 15% of reviews and was rarely disclosed. While in primary clinical research—where primary outcome switching can directly affect sample size calculations and risks introducing the bias of hypothesizing after the results are known (HARKing)15—the distinction between primary and secondary outcomes in systematic reviews carries a different methodologic weight. The PRISMA 2020 statement4 requires only that review authors “list and define all outcomes for which data were sought” (Item 10a), and PRISMA-P similarly recommends prioritizing outcomes with a rationale, without prescribing a strict outcome hierarchy. Nevertheless, in practice, the designation of a primary outcome in SRs often signals to readers, guideline developers, and clinicians which outcome the authors considered most central at the outset. Undeclared changes to this designation may shift the interpretative emphasis of a review and potentially contribute to selective reporting. Studies performed in other fields suggest that this bias may be even higher, with estimated primary outcome discrepancies of 29–37% (95% confidence interval [CI]) and secondary outcome discrepancies of 50–75% (95% CI).5,16,17 Selective outcome reporting is a well-documented source of bias that can compromise the reliability of evidence syntheses and lead to overestimation of treatment effectiveness.3

Similarly, we observed deviations in search strategies and restrictions, with many reviews omitting preregistered databases or introducing unplanned ones. Only a minority of studies fully reported their search strategy, which limits reproducibility. While the conduct of risk of bias assessments was largely consistent with protocols, discrepancies in the tools used were not uncommon. The use of the GRADE framework for assessing evidence quality was often added post hoc, highlighting the inconsistent application of this critical framework.

Finally, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were frequently planned but not conducted, or conducted without prior declaration. While the reason was likely insufficient data, explicit reporting of such decisions would improve transparency and interpretability.

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, we focused on a restricted time frame and included only journals classified as high-impact within the “Anesthesiology” category of the JCR. Although expanding the time window or including a broader range of journals may have provided a more comprehensive overview, this restriction was necessary owing to feasibility evaluations to ensure a rigorous double-blinded evaluation of all eligible manuscripts. Because the number of preregistered reviews meeting our criteria could not be reliably estimated in advance, widening the scope risked producing an unmanageable data set for us to perform a dual independent assessment. Concentrating on a recent publication year allowed us to capture contemporary practices in protocol registration and adherence, while focusing on high-impact journals ensured that the analysis reflects current standards in both anesthesiology and pain medicine. We also acknowledge that, as perioperative research becomes increasingly multidisciplinary, anesthesiologist-scientists frequently publish in a diverse array of journals. Nevertheless, we chose a defined journal category rather than a topic-based category to avoid introducing selection bias or excessive subjectivity into the topic-inclusion process. Finally, although double-blind, independent assessment reduces bias, the evaluation inevitably involves a degree of judgement, and some subjectivity may remain despite our efforts to standardize the process.

Conclusions

The current findings underscore the urgent need for stricter adherence to and reporting of protocols in systematic reviews. We argue that the responsibility for ensuring such adherence lies primarily with authors, but also with peer reviewers and journal editors, who must require adequate justification for any deviations from registered protocols. This approach would create a more transparent and accountable research environment, ultimately strengthening the integrity and reliability of systematic reviews in anesthesiology and pain medicine.
