Cryptogam biomass estimation using taxonomic and life form models for accurate assessment
Authors: Yoshitaka Oishi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Accurate estimation of cryptogam biomass, encompassing bryophytes and lichens, is crucial for understanding their ecological significance. This estimation is conducted based on the strong correlations between mass and volume of cryptogams. However, mass–volume correlations vary among cryptogams because of their morphological differences. This problem can be solved using models that consider life forms that classify cryptogams based on morphological similarities. In this study, we investigated whether life form models improve cryptogam biomass estimation accuracy. The cryptogam mass–volume correlation of each life form was estimated using Bayesian linear models. The coefficients and intercepts of linear models differed between life forms, which was attributed to the morphological characteristics of each life form. Therefore, life form models can improve the accuracy of estimation models by incorporating morphological differences. However, taxonomic models that consider only the taxonomic difference (bryophytes vs lichens) demonstrated better overall estimation than the life form models, probably because of the ability of taxonomic models to capture systematic differences between bryophytes and lichens. Furthermore, these models may mitigate estimation errors related to morphological variations that cannot be adequately represented by life form types. Based on these results, we propose the appropriate use of estimation models.

Introduction

Cryptogams (bryophytes and lichens) perform crucial ecosystem functions by serving as carbon sinks1, participating in nutrient cycling2–4, contributing to water storage5, and influencing biogeochemical cycles6,7. Cryptogams store about 3.9 Pg of carbon per year globally, which is approximately 7% of the net primary production of terrestrial ecosystems1. Furthermore, cryptogams form symbiotic relationships with cyanobacteria, providing nitrogen to forest ecosystems8. However, the ecological functions of cryptograms may diminish because they are currently at risk of disappearing owing to global changes in climate9,10.

Assessing the ecological functions of cryptogams is the basis for understanding changes in these functions. As these functions largely depend on the cryptogam biomass, estimation methods to quantify cryptogam biomass are required. In particular, non-destructive approaches are desirable for minimizing damage to ecosystems. Cryptogam biomass in open areas (e.g., fen and tundra vegetation) was estimated using remote sensing techniques11,12. However, these techniques are not applied to closed areas (forest interior). Instead, biomass estimations in forests were conducted using mass–volume correlations of cryptogams. The biomass of cryptogams was estimated based on cover and size of fragments13. Specifically, bryophytes demonstrated high correlations between mass and volume14. In lichens, biomass was estimated using cover or depth only as explanatory variables15. However, both cover and depth of lichen communities are needed for a better estimation16,17. One study demonstrated that cover is a fundamental variable for lichen biomass estimation, although the inclusion of depth improved the accuracy of the estimation model18.

These estimation methods using mass–volume correlations may be improved by considering the morphological characteristics of cryptogams, as morphological differences may affect these correlations. For example, the estimation models of bryophytes depend on differences in their colony structures13. Similarly, each lichen taxon demonstrated different mass–volume ratios because of the differences in growth forms16. Considering these results, a life form concept that groups species with similar shoot morphology and colony structure19 may be useful in incorporating morphological characteristics into estimation models. Life forms of bryophytes are classified into seven large groups—turfs, cushions, dendroids, mats, wefts, fans, and pendants—according to Bates’ life form classification (Fig. 1a–g)19. Lichens are classified into three main types based on morphological differences: fruticose, foliose, and crustose (Fig. 1h–j)20. In this study, we aimed to investigate whether the application of life forms can improve the accuracy of cryptogam biomass estimation. Our estimation model (life form model) was compared with biomass estimation models that do not consider cryptogam differences (all-cryptogam model) and those that consider only taxonomic differences (taxonomic model).

The coefficients and intercepts differed among taxonomic groups and life forms. The intercepts of bryophytes were significantly lower (α = − 1.059) than those of lichens (α = − 0.659). In bryophytes, higher values of coefficients were obtained for dendroids (β = 0.930) and fans (β = 0.942), whereas turfs (β = 0.692), mats (β = 0.690), and wefts (β = 0.782) had lower values. By contrast, dendroids and fans demonstrated lower intercept values (α = − 2.038 and − 1.976, respectively) than turfs (α = − 1.133), mats (α = − 1.057), and wefts (α = − 1.481). The coefficient and intercept of dendroids were significantly higher and lower than those of turfs, respectively. For lichens, the coefficients and intercepts between the fruticose (β = 0.620, α = − 0.622) and foliose (β = 0.533, α = − 0.614) forms were close to each other, and significant differences were not observed.

Discussion

We found that the three types of estimation models exhibited good fitness (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${wR}_{B}^{2}$$\end{document}wRB2 > 0.750), confirming that mass–volume relationships of cryptogams are useful for estimating biomass. In bryophytes, mass–volume relationships may differ between the upper green and lower brown parts of each shoot. However, these differences seem to have little influence on the fitness of estimation models, as suggested by the observed good fitness. This finding can be attributed to small differences in biomass and bulk density between green and brown shoot parts21.

The life form model exhibited the lowest \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{w}{R}_{B}^{2}$$\end{document}wRB2 values among the three types of estimation models (all-cryptogam, taxonomic, and life form). Given the variations in morphology within a life form category (e.g., shoot and branch density), the all-cryptogam and taxonomic models that include cryptogam species with different life forms may have reduced the estimation errors related to these morphological variations that could not be reflected in life form types. Consequently, these models demonstrated lower errors than life form models. Moreover, the limited number of samples in certain life forms, such as foliose lichens, may exacerbate the estimation errors associated with the life form models. In the comparison between the all-cryptogam and taxonomic models, the latter yielded higher estimates. This observation can be attributed to the ability of the taxonomic models to account for systematic differences between bryophytes and lichens, such as major substances that constitute the bodies22 and morphology (e.g., absence of leaves in lichens).

When considering model fitness across various life forms, differences were observed in both the coefficient and intercept among life forms. Turfs, mats, and wefts exhibited lower and higher coefficients and intercepts, whereas dendroids and fans demonstrated opposite trends. The morphological characteristics of life forms can explain these results. Mats and wefts form colonies characterized by intricate overlap among their shoots and branches (Fig. 1d, e)19, whereas turf formations shade the lower shoots with the upper parts owing to their erect shoot morphology (Fig. 1a). Such overlapping and shading can decrease the shoot mass in the lower parts owing to reduced light penetration, considering that shoot mass is decreased under low light intensity23,24. These morphological characteristics lower the weight/volume ratio with an increase in colony size, resulting in lower coefficients for the estimation models. By contrast, dendroids and fans form tree-like and fan-like shapes owing to the concentrated branches at the upper shoot (Fig. 1c, f). These shapes with less overlapping and shading may have a minor influence on the shoot mass in the lower parts, which can cause a higher coefficient in the linear model. Moreover, the tree- and fan-like shapes of dendroids and fans create ample space between the canopy (a clump of branches at shoot tops) and the ground (Fig. 1c, f), whereas such space does not exist in turfs, mats, and wefts because they form colonies with loosely or closely packed shoots (Fig. 1a, d, e). This space in dendroids and fans can lower the weight/volume ratio when the colony size is small, resulting in the lowest intercept value.

Wefts exhibited the lowest \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{B}^{2}$$\end{document}RB2 value within each cryptogam category. This result can be explained by the thick and interwoven mats of wefts19 formed by a variety of species with diverse morphologies and community structures. Consequently, the differences in shoot density are more pronounced in wefts than in other life forms, leading to greater variability in mass–volume relationships and decreasing model fitness. These findings suggest that refining the classification of wefts could enhance the fitness of life form models. A similar misalignment between life form categories and mass grouping has been observed in certain lichen species25.

The advantages and limitations of the biomass estimation models employed in this study were explored. The advantages include reduced ecosystem disturbance, high estimation accuracy, and low labor and time costs. While the first advantage is highlighted in the Introduction, the second is supported by the robust fitness of the estimation models. Concerning the third advantage, both cover and depth measurements are deemed necessary for accurate estimation owing to significant differences in cryptogam depth within colonies of similar size. Given that these measurements could be conducted within a few to several minutes per colony in this study, the proposed estimation methods are highly practical. A limitation of these methods stems from the changes seen in cryptogam depth between wet and dry conditions. In most cases, these changes are acceptable because the depth of dominant species is only slightly affected by drought owing to their thick mats or robust stems. However, some epiphytes roll up during drought and their depth changes markedly. This finding was the most pronounced for Pterobryum arbuscula among the collected cryptogam samples, which were 12.3% ± 9.8% taller on average when dry (mean ± SD, n = 20). Therefore, to enhance the accuracy of biomass estimation, we recommend measuring cryptogam depth under standardized conditions, such as avoiding wet conditions during measurement. Alternatively, based on the difference in depth between wet and dry conditions, a correction formula for these species can be developed.

In conclusion, all three types of models (all-cryptogam, taxonomic group, and life form) demonstrated good fitness in estimating cryptogam biomass, demonstrating the effectiveness of the mass–volume relationship in the estimation. Taxonomic models stood out as particularly valuable owing to their ability to account for systematic differences between bryophytes and lichens, thereby reducing estimation errors caused by individual morphological variations that were not reflected in life form types. Conversely, the life form model revealed significant differences in estimation among several life forms (turfs, mats, and wefts versus dendroids and fans) owing to large differences in morphology. These findings suggest the wide applicability of taxonomic models, while highlighting the suitability of life form models in areas where specific life forms dominate but exhibit considerable variation in proportions. This study provides essential insights into the optimal estimation models for cryptogam biomass.

Cryptogam samples were collected from 18 sites in Japan covering the prevalent vegetation types such as alpine, subalpine-subarctic, montane-temperate, and foothill-warm temperate based on the vegetation maps of the Biodiversity Center of Japan26 (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table S1). Sampling

Cryptogams were collected from single colonies using a knife. The sample size (area) varied with the colony size, ranging from 1 cm2 (1 × 1 cm) to 400 cm2 (20 × 20 cm). This sampling method differs from that used in previous studies in which cryptogams of the same size were collected21. The sampling method in this study allowed us to include cryptogams of different sizes in the analysis. For example, the 100 cm2 sampling frame is too small to collect large bryophytes (e.g., Hylocomium spp.) that cover the forest floor in subalpine forests, whereas this frame is large enough to sample single communities of small bryophytes (e.g., Pseudotaxiphyllum spp.) that cover the forest floor with other cryptogams. The sizes of the sampled cryptogams were measured using 20 × 20 cm mesh (marked at 5-cm intervals) and 5 × 5 cm mesh (marked at 1-cm intervals). The depth of the cryptogams was measured by inserting a measuring rod (marked at 1-mm intervals) at five equal divisions of the longitudinal diameter. For cryptogams hanging down from vertical substrates (e.g., tree trunks), the distance from the substrate was measured as depth. Whole shoots, including the dead brown parts, were used in this study. Bryophytes were classified into seven large groups according to Bates’ classification19: turfs, cushions, dendroids, mats, wefts, fans, and pendants (Fig. 1a–g). Lichens were grouped into three growth forms: fruticose, foliose, and crustose (Fig. 1h–j). Samples were collected primarily from the main habitats of each species. Consequently, 423 samples were collected, including 383 bryophytes and 40 lichens. The number of samples for each life form category was 111, 16, 17, 143, 62, 23, 11, 31, and 9 for turf, cushions, dendroids, mats, wefts, fans, pendants, and fruticose and foliose types, respectively (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). Crustose lichens were not sampled because their depths were lower than the minimum depth scale (1 mm).

The collected samples were stored in sealed polyethylene bags and transported to the laboratory. The samples were cleaned of other organic matter and soil and oven-dried at 40 °C for at least 48 h until a constant weight was achieved. All samples were examined under a microscope in the laboratory.

Models for biomass estimation

Cryptogam biomass was estimated based on the strong correlation between mass and volume14,16,21. where W [i] is the dry weight of sample i, V [i] is the volume of sample i, and α and β are the intercept and coefficient, respectively. Default weakly informative prior distributions were adopted for the regression and variance parameters.

Three estimation models (all-cryptogam, taxonomic group, and life form) were constructed according to Eq. 1. The all-cryptogam model estimated the biomass using all the samples. The taxonomic group model was used to estimate bryophyte and lichen biomass. The life form model estimated the biomass of each life form. The 95% credible interval (CI) for the regression coefficients and intercepts were bounded by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the posterior distribution of these parameters, respectively. Significant differences in α and β values were examined based on a Bayesian 95% CI (quantiles between 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles). Differences between the two regression coefficients and intercepts were statistically significant if the CIs did not overlap. This calculation used four chains with a warm-up period of 1,000 samples, followed by 2000 saved iterations.
