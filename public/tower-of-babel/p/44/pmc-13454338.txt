Psychometric validation and measurement invariance of the Polish Multi-Affect Indicator across diverse demographic and occupational groups
Authors: Arkadiusz M. Jasiński, Romuald Derbis, Radosław Andrzej Dziedzic, Thomas Rigotti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present study investigated the psychometric properties and validity of the Polish adaptation of the Multi-Affect Indicator (MAI), a scale designed to measure core affect at work. Specifically, we examined: (a) factor structure; (b) measurement invariance across sex, age, occupational group, and administration mode; (c) intergroup differences in work-related affective well-being; (d) internal, convergent, and discriminant validity; and (e) temporal stability. We conducted three studies with employee samples from diverse occupational sectors (N1 = 1,582; N2 = 326; N3 = 76). Results supported a correlated four-factor model consistent with the original MAI structure. In Study 1, the scale demonstrated scalar invariance across sex, age, and occupational groups, with strict invariance supported for sex and occupational group. In Study 2, the factor structure was replicated, and invariance analyses supported comparability across sex and occupational groups, although occupational-group results should be interpreted cautiously because some absolute-fit indices were elevated. The scale also achieved scalar invariance between the online Study 1 sample and the paper-and-pencil Study 2 sample. The Polish MAI demonstrated strong internal consistency, evidence of convergent and discriminant validity, and moderate one-week temporal stability. Overall, the results support the Polish MAI as a reliable and valid tool for assessing multidimensional affective well-being in occupational settings.

Introduction

Core affect refers to a basic, consciously experienced feeling state in the present moment1. It is commonly understood as a neurophysiological state that gives rise to fundamental affective experiences, such as feeling good or bad, energized or fatigued2. According to the circumplex model of affect3, core affect can be described along two independent dimensions: valence (pleasant-unpleasant) and activation (high-low arousal). The horizontal axis (valence) reflects the pleasentness or unpleasantness of an experience, whereas the vertical axis (activation) indicates the level of physiological or psychological arousal, that is, the degree of readiness for action or engagement. Core affect is considered to underlie more complex emotional experiences. For instance, basic affective states such as calmness or excitement may contribute to more elaborated emotions, such as pride. What distinguishes core affect from more complex emotions is its relatively limited reliance on cognitive appraisal and its direct expression in terms of pleasure and activation. More complex emotions, such as confusion, typically involve more extensive cognitive processing. Thus, emotional experiences can be understood as configurations of valence and activation, or as varying degrees along these two dimensions1. The circumplex model, grounded in both theoretical reasoninng and empirical research, provides a useful framework for classifying affective states and reflects how individuals typically describe their own and others’ emotional experiences4–6.

Previous research has shown that patterns of brain activity correspond to emotions classified along valence and arousal dimensions7,8. For instance, joy and excitement are typically characterized by high arousal and positive valence9. At the same time, the model distinguishes broadly between positive and negative affective states. However, the circumplex model also has limitations. The arousal dimension can be difficult to assess, particularly in the case of ambivalent emotions or low-arousal states, where differences in intensity may complicate emotional differentiation10,11. Despite these challenges, the circumplex model remains widely accepted2. Alternative models, such as the positive-negative affect model12 and the positive-negative activation model13, also provide meaningful representations of affect. Nevertheless, recent work emphasizes the importance of integrating both valence and arousal when assessing complex emotional experiences14. Empirical studies further support the utility of the valence-arousal framework in predicting outcomes such as task-switching performance15, affective priming16, performance satisfaction17, and emotion regulation18. In addition, theoretical models such as the pathways to health model19 and the broaden-and-build theory20 suggest that activities promoting positive emotions may improve coping, physical health, and well-being. Consistent with this view, research has shown a positive association between positive affect and well-being21, as well as a negative association between negative affect and health outcomes22.

In light of the European Union’s strategic priorities, including the prevention of mental health disorders23 and the promotion of well-being to sustain work ability24, there is a growing need for tools to assess core affective states in the workplace across countries. Assessing core affect at work is important for evaluating person-job fit, as job-related affective well-being is a key indicator of employee mental health25.

Warr’s26 original affect measure applied the circumplex model to workplace well-being by distinguishing two bipolar dimensions: anxiety-comfort and depression-enthusiasm. However, this structure may obscure important distinctions between positive and negative affect. Warr et al.‘s27 Multi-Affect Indicator (MAI) extended this approach by distinguishing four affective quadrants: high-activation pleasant affect, low-activation pleasant affect, high-activation unpleasant affect, and low-activation unpleasant affect. This four-quadrant structure improves conceptual precision and psychometric differentiation. The MAI enhances reliability by using multiple items per quadrant and demonstrates robust construct validity by distinguishing between different types of affect. For example, enthusiasm, representing high-activation pleasant affect, is associated with proactive behavior, whereas comfort, representing low-activation pleasant affect, is more closely related to job satisfaction. Similarly, anxiety and depression, representing high- and low-activation unpleasant affect, respectively, may predict distinct patterns of stress and withdrawal28. By capturing the full range of core affect, the MAI provides a more nuanced assessment of workplace emotions than its predecessor.

Despite the strengths of existing instruments for assessing general and job-related affective well-being in Poland, several gaps remain. First, no Polish adaptation of the Multi Affect Indicator (MAI) is currently available. Second, previous validation studies have often relied on smaller or less occupationally diverse samples. Third, existing evidence is limited regarding samples whose sex and age distributions broadly reflect the Polish working population. Fourth, comprehensive psychometric evaluation, including measurement invariance across age and occupational groups, remain insufficiently developed in Polish occupational affect research.

The present study addresses these gaps in several ways. First, it examines the cross-cultural applicability of the MAI by testing whether its concise four-quadrant structure, based on the circumplex model of affect, is replicated in the Polish occupational context. In doing so, the study evaluates whether the MAI offers an efficient alternative to instruments such as the JAWS29, which has been validated in more specific occupational samples, or the PANAS30,31, which focuses primarily on the valence dimension32. Second, by using a large and occupationally diverse sample with a sex and age distribution broadly comparable to the Polish working population, the study provides evidence on the measurement invariance of the MAI across key demographic and occupational groups. These analyses are important because they indicate whether MAI scores can be meaningfully compared across different segments of the workforce.

Beyond the adaptation of the instrument itself, the present study contributes to the conceptualization of affective well-being at work. By testing whether the four-quadrant structure of affect is stable across varied occupational groups, the study provides further evidence for the applicability of the circumplex model of affect in workplace settings3.

Although Polish adaptations of Warr’s26 initial affect scale are available33,34, the development of a Polish adaptation of the MAI addresses an important gap in organizational psychology. It provides a validated Polish-language instrument that ensures linguistic and cultural relevance while enabling cross-country comparisons. A Polish version of the MAI may also support organizations and health professionals in assessing employee well-being across multiple affective dimensions, and countries28,35.

The measurement of affect

The Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS)36 and its expanded version, the PANAS-X37, are widely used to assess affect in Poland and internationally. Both instruments have been validated in Polish samples30,31. However, although these measures are suitable for assessing affect in the general population, they have not been comprehensively evaluated in occupational settings, and their criterion validity in relation to workplace variables, such as job attitudes, performance, and well-being, remains unclear. In addition, several affect measures have been developed by Polish researchers, including the Emotion Questionnaire, the General Mood Scale, the Positive Mood Scale, and the Negative Mood Scale38. However, these instruments were developed using relatively small and non-representative samples, which limits their generalizability to diverse occupational populations.

Several workplace-specific instruments are also available, including the Job Affect Scale (JAS)39, the Job-Related Affective Well-Being Scale (JAWS)40, and the Job-Related Affective Well-Being Questionnaire26. The JAS, which is based on the bifactor model of affect12, has been adapted for use in Poland32. However, it does not include the activation dimension, and key psychometric properties, including temporal stability and measurement invariance, have not yet been examined. Moreover, the Polish adaptation is now more than two decades old. The JAWS, grounded in the circumplex model, captures a broad range of affective experiences at work. Its Polish version includes 20-, 12-, and 8-item formats29, but its validation also has limitations, including a sample composed mainly of male police officers, which restricts generalizability. Furthermore, criterion validity, temporal stability, and measurement invariance were not fully assessed. The Job-Related Affective Well-Being Questionnaire26, also based on the circumplex model3 consists of 12 adjectives representing various work-related feelings. Its Polish adaptation34 has several strengths: it was validated in a diverse sample of 380 employees across multiple sectors, 65% of whom were women; it used a longitudinal design with a two-week test-retest interval; it established factorial, criterion, and predictive validity; and scalar measurement invariance across gender was confirmed33.

Multi affect indicator

Based on Russell’s3 circumplex model of affect, Warr26 proposed a model of work-related affective well-bring organized around two core dimensions: valence, ranging from pleasant to unpleasant, and activation, ranging from high to low arousal. Building on this framework, Warr et al.27 developed the Multi Affect Indicator (MAI), a refined instrument designed to assess four distinct types of core affect at work: high-activation pleasant affect (HAPA; e.g., enthusiasm), low-activation pleasant affect (LAPA; e.g., comfort), high-activation unpleasant affect (HAUA; e.g., anxiety), and low-activation unpleasant affect (LAUA; e.g., depression). Unlike the PANAS36, which includes more complex emotional states such as guilt or shame, the MAI directly reflects the valence-activation structure of affect, thereby enhancing conceptual clarity and psychometric precision. The MAI consists of 16 adjectives, with four items representing each quadrant, and can be adapted to assess affect in specific life domains, such as work or family life, and across different timeframes, such as the past week or past month. This flexibility is important because previous research suggests that shorter recall periods, such as one week, may improve the accuracy of self-report affect41.

A growing body of evidence supports the usefulness of valence and arousal dimensions for describing affective experiences, in addition to approaches based on discrete emotional states42. The MAI has also demonstrated predictive validity for work-related outcomes. Warr et al.27,43 found that the four affect types predict outcomes, such as proactivity, organizational advocacy, task proficiency, perceived autonomy, and task significance. More recent research has shown that high-activation pleasant affect is positively associated with team cohesion and individual thriving, particularly under conditions of high task interdependence44.

Study aims and hypotheses

The primary aim of this study is to adapt and validate the MAI in a large and diverse sample of Polish employees. Specifically, we examine if the factor structure of the Polish version corresponds to that of the original instrument. Previous research supports a four-factor model comprising separate dimensions of enthusiasm, comfort, anxiety, and depression27. This structure has been replicated in studies of English- and Spanish-speaking employees43,45, as well as in Chinese46 and Romanian47 samples. Based on this evidence, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: The MAI consists of four correlated but distinct factors: enthusiasm, comfort, anxiety, and depression.

To date, measurement invariance of the MAI across key sociodemographic and occupational variables, such as sex, age, and occupational group, has not been sufficiently examined. Related research on the Job-Related Affective Well-Being Questionnaire26 has demonstrated invariance across sex33, and cross-cultural research on the MAI has shown invariance between English and Spanish versions48. To address this gap, the present study tested measurement invariance of the MAI across sex, age, and occupational group. Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2: The four-factor structure of the MAI is invariant across sex, age and occupational group.

To assess the criterion validity, we examined associations between the four MAI dimensions and symptoms of depression and generalized psychological distress. Prior research has shown that negative affect, including both high and low activation forms, is positively associated with depressive symptoms and poorer mental health49,50,51. A meta-analysis by Rogers and Farris52 further indicated that negative emotional states are linked to several mental health conditions and show moderate associations with adverse physical health outcomes, including pain catastrophizing, fear of pain, and pain-related disability. Previous studies have also shown that positive affect is negatively associated with stress, whereas negative emotional states are positively associated with psychological distress53,54. To broaden the validity assessment beyond the work domain, we included general measures of depression and stress applicable across life contexts. We therefore expect the MAI anxiety and depression dimensions to correlate positively with depressive and stress symptoms, and the enthusiasm and comfort dimensions to correlate negatively with these indicators.

We expect these association to be moderate, indicating that work-related affect, psychological distress, and depressive symptoms are related but distinct constructs. To assess convergent validity, we also included a Polish-adapted measure of workplace affect based on the bifactor conceptualization of affect12,32, which captures related constructs from a different theoretical perspective. Based on this rationale, we propose the following hypotheses:

H3: Negative affective states are positively associated to psychological stress and depressive symptoms.

H4: Positive affective states are negatively associated with psychological stress and depressive symptoms.

Exploratory research questions

While the primary objective of this study is the psychometric validation of the Polish adaptation of the MAI, we also conducted exploratory analyses to examine whether affective experiences at work differed across demographic groups. These analyses were intended to complement the measurement invariance tests by describing potential group differences after establishing that the scale operates comparably across groups. However, because sex and age differences were not central to the validation aim of the study, they were treated as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Previous research suggests that affective experiences may vary by sex and age, although the size and practical relevance of such differences are often context-dependent. Studies on emotional experience and mental health have reported sex-related differences in affective symptoms and emotion regulation, but these patterns should not be interpreted as fixed or universal differences between women and men55–60. Similarly, lifespan research indicates that affective experience may change with age, with older adults often reporting lower negative affect and greater emotional stability than younger adults61–66. In occupational settings, however, such differences may also reflect work experience, job role, occupational status, and contextual demands.

Accordingly, we examined sex and age differences in MAI scores descriptively and formulated the following exploratory research questions:

RQ1: Do the four MAI dimensions differ by sex in the present occupational sample?

RQ2: Do the four MAI dimensions differ across age groups in the present occupational sample?

Methods

Overview of studies

We report finding from three studies. Studies 1 and 2 used cross-sectional designs, whereas Study 3 used a longitudinal design. Data for Study 1 were collected online as part of a larger, multi-purpose research project. Although selected demographic data from this sample were used in a previous study on individual job performance67, the present manuscript addresses different research objectives. The constructs, theoretical framework, and psychometric analyses of the MAI reported here are unique to this study. There is no overlap with the previous publication in terms of analyses, results, or text, ensuring that the present findings constitute an independent contribution.

Data for Studies 2 and 3 were collected using paper-and-pencil questionnaires. Study 1 examined the factor structure of the Polish adaptation of MAI and tested its measurement invariance across age, sex, and occupational groups. This study was used to test Hypotheses H1 and H2 and to address Research Questions RQ1 and RQ2. Study 2 aimed to replicate the factor structure in an independent sample and evaluate criterion validity, addressing Hypotheses H3 and H4. Study 3 assessed the temporal stability of the MAI.

All studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The research project was reviewed and approved by the Research Quality Assessment Committee of the first author’s institution (University of Opole, no 24/2023). All participants were informed about the purpose of the study and their right to withdraw from participation. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Power analysis

We determined the recommended sample sizes using the A-priori Sample Size Calculator68. For the CFA model, which included four latent variables and 16 observed variables, the recommended sample sizes were as follows. For Study 1, N = 1,022 was identified as the optimal sample size for detecting effects with high precision, assuming f2 = 0.15, power = 0.90, and p < .01. The final Study 1 sample of N = 1,582 therefore exceeded this requiremet and provided sufficient power for model estimation and intergroup comparisons.

For stable model estimation and identification, the calculator indicated a minimum sample size of N = 100. Thus, the Study 2 sample of N = 326 was sufficient for replicating the CFA model. For criterion validity analyses in Study 2, an a-priori power analysis indicated that N = 138 was required to detect a medium correlation of r = .30 with power = 0.95, a requirement also met by the data. However, multigroup analyses in Study 2 should be interpreted cautiously where subgroup sizes were small. In particular, age invariance was not tested in Study 2 because some age groups did not include enough observations for stable MGCFA estimation. For Study 3, the minimum sample size required to detect a test-retest correlation of r = .50 with power = 0.95 was N = 42. The Study 3 sample of N = 76 therefore exceeded this threshold.

Participants and procedure

Study 1 included N = 1,582 employees from three major occupational sectors: professionals, including teachers, physicians, scientists, managers, and self-employed workers (N = 518); service workers, including nurses, midwives, tradespeople, sales staff, and administrative clerks (N = 513); and manual and production workers (N = 551). The sample consisted of 49.6% women (N = 784) and 50.4% men (N = 798). Participants’ mean age was 41.83 years (SD = 11.97), and their mean job tenure was 17.64 years (SD = 11.64).

Workplace affect was assessed using the MAI developed by Warr et al.27. The Polish adaptation followed standard procedures for the cross-cultural adaptation of self-report measures69. First, two bilingual psychologists fluent in Polish and English, including the first author, independently translated the scale into Polish. Second, the two translated versions were synthesized into a single preliminary version. Third, two independent professional translators who were unfamiliar with the original scale back-translated the Polish version into English. Fourth, the preliminary Polish version was pilot-tested in a qualitative study with 15 participants, five from each employment sector, to ensure consistent interpretation of the items across occupational groups. This step was particularly important given the role of semantic clarity in self-report measures of emotional states5. Fifth, the first author prepated the final version of the questionnaire based on the pilot feedback and linguistic reconciliation.

Participants were asked,“In the last week, how often have you felt this way about doing your job?” Responses were provided on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = „almost never” to 5 = „almost every day”. Subscale scores were computed as the mean of the four items belonging to each subscale, resulting in scores ranging from 1 to 5.

Study 2 included N = 326 participants from three occupational groups: social workers (N = 98), paramedics, midwives and nurses (N = 105); and sales and customer service employees (N = 123). Participants’ mean age was 37.29 years (SD = 12.74) and their average work experience was 13.79 years (SD = 12.49). The sample was predominantly female, with women comprising 72.1% of participants.

To assess criterion validity, we used the Job Affect Scale39, in its Polish adaptation by Zalewska32. The scale consists of 20 items, including 10 items measuring positive affect and 10 items measuring negative affect. Respondents answered the question: “How strongly did you experience these emotional states at work in the last two weeks?” using a 7-point Likert scale. Example adjectives include „active” and „scared”. General psychological stress and depressive symptoms were assessed using the relevant subscales of the Copenhagen Psychosocial Questionnaire (COPSOQ II)70, adapted for Polish samples by Baka et al.71. Each COPSOQ II subscale consists of four items, with responses rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 4 = „daily experience of symptoms” to 0 = „no symptoms at all”. Raw scores were converted to a 0–100 scale, where 0 indicates no symptoms and 100 indicates maximum symptom severity. The reliability coefficients for the criterion-validity measures were as follows: positive affect (α = 0.89, ω = 0.89), negative affect (α = 0.86, ω = 0.86), psychological stress (α = 0.86, ω = 0.86), and depressive symptoms (α = 0.83, ω = 0.83).

Study 3 included N = 76 participants from various occupations, including teachers, sales personnel, production workers, healthcare professionals, and service employees. Participants’ mean age was 29.46 years (SD = 8.88), and their average job tenure was 9.46 years (SD = 8.68). The sample was predominantly female, with women comprising 71.1% of participants. To assess temporal stability, participants completed the MAI on two occasions separated by a one-week interval.

Data analysis

Data screening was conducted using standard statistical procedures, including the assessment of skewness, kurtosis and the variance inflation factors (VIF), as well as visual inspection of histograms and residual plots. No substantial violations of normality, linearity, or multicollinearity were detected. All analyses were conducted using SPSS v. 29 and AMOS. In Study 1, confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) was used to test the proposed factor structure of the MAI. Measurement invariance across subgroups was examined using multigroup confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA). Additionally, we conducted ANOVA to test for group differences based on sex (men vs. women), age group (18–34, 35–44, 45–54, ≥ 55 years), and occupational group (three groups). In Study 2, we replicated the CFA model identified in Study 1 and used a parallel procedure to assess measurement invariance. Because Study 1 was conducted online, whereas Study 2 used paper-and-pencil questionnaires, we also tested measurement invariance between the two samples to examine the comparability of MAI scores across administration modes. Finally, criterion validity was evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficiens to examine associations between the MAI dimensions and external variables.

All CFA and MGCFA analyses were conducted using maximum likelihood estimation. This approach was applied because the Likert-scale data were treated as continuous indicators. In both Study 1 and Study 2, we employed the chi-square (χ2) test for model evaluation; however, given its known sensitivity to sample sizes, model fit was evaluated primarily using additional fit indices, including the comparative fit index (CFI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). To compare models and assess measurement invariance, we examined changes (Δ) in fit indices at each step of the invariance testing procedure. Following the recommendations of Hu and Bentler72 and Chen73, we used the following criteria for acceptable model fit: CFI > 0.90, with values > 0.95 indicating very good fit, RMSEA < 0.08, and SRMR < 0.08. For measurement invariance testing, changes of ΔCFI ≤ 0.010, ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015, and ΔSRMR ≤ 0.030 were considered evidence of invariance. Lower BIC values were used to identify the more parsimonious model among non-nested model comparisons.

Common method bias assessment

To assess the risk of common method bias in Studies 1 and 2, we conducted Harman’s single-factor test and additionally compared the hypothesized four-factor CFA model with a single-factor CFA model. In Study 1, Harman’s single-factor test indicated that one factor accounted for 47.41% of the variance, which was below the recommended 50% threshold. The single-factor CFA model also showed substantially poorer fit than hypothesized four-factor model: χ2(104) = 8347.248, p < .001, CFI = 0.60, RMSEA = 0.22, SRMR = 0.20.

Similarly, in Study 2, one factor accounted for 42.24% of the variance, again below the recommended threshold. The single-factor CFA model showed poor fit and performed substantially worse than the hypothesized four-factor model: χ2(104) = 1461.48, p < .001, CFI = 0.59, RMSEA = 0.20, SRMR = 0.14. Together, these findings indicate that the covariance structure of the data is better represented by the intended four-factor structure than by a single common method factor. Nevertheless, because Studies 1 and 2 relied on same-source self-report data, common method bias cannot be ruled out entirely.

The final model, illustrated in Fig. 1, reflectd four distinct but correlated affective dimensions. According to commonly recommended fit criteria72, the goodness-of-fit indices indicated an acceptable to good model fit. Measurement invariance results

Measurement invariance was tested using a sequential model comparison approach. Because the χ² difference test is sensitive to sample size, invariance decisions were based primarily on changes in CFI, which is considered a stringent and relatively sample-size-independent criterion. In Study 1, the results supported strict invariance across sex and occupational groups, and scalar invariance across age groups, Incremental fit indices remained within recommended thresholds, namely ΔCFI ≤ 0.010; ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015, ΔSRMR ≤ 0.03, consistent with Chen73. Intergroup comparisons

Findings indicated no significant sex differences in high-activation positive affect (HAPA), with men (M = 2.80, SD = 0.82) and women (M = 2.73, SD = 0.83) reporting comparable levels. However, men reported significantly higher levels of low-activation positive affect (LAPA; M = 3.23, SD = 0.74) than women (M = 3.06, SD = 0.76; p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.22). In contrast, women reported significantly higher levels of high-activation negative affect (HAUA; M = 2.47, SD = 0.85) than men (M = 2.29, SD = 0.81, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.21), as well as higher levels of low-activation negative affect (LAUA; M = 2.17, SD = 0.84) than men (M = 2.04, SD = 0.83, p = .003, Cohen’s d = 0.15).

Regarding age, older participants reported slightly higher levels of low-activation positive affect (LAPA; r = .07, p = .002). Age was negatively associated with high-activation positive affect (HAPA; r = − .09, p < .001), high-activation negative affect (HAUA; r = − .18, p < .001), and low-activation negative affect (LAUA; r = − .15, p < .001). Internal, convergent and discriminant validity

The reliability coefficients for the MAI subscales in Study 1 indicated good to excellent internal consistency: HAPA (α = 0.90, ω = 0.90, CR = 0.90, AVE = 0.70); LAPA (α = 0.87, ω = 0.87, CR = 0.87, AVE = 0.62); HAUA (α = 0.91, ω = 0.92, CR = 0.91, AVE = 0.72); and LAUA (α = 0.92, ω = 0.93, CR = 0.92, AVE = 0.76). Convergent validity was supported by moderate correlations between the MAI dimensions and corresponding positive and negative affect scores from an alternative validated measure. Discriminant validity was also supported, as correlations between the MAI subscales and measures of general stress and depressive symptoms were sufficiently low, indicating that the constructs assessed by the MAI are empirically distinct from broader indicators of psychological distress. Temporal stability of the MAI

The temporal stability analysis indicated that affect at work, as measured by the MAI, showed moderate stability over a one week interval. Test-retest correlations between the T1 and T2 assessments were statistically significant for all four affective dimensions: HAPA T1 → HAPA T2 (r = .59, p < .001); LAPA T1 → LAPA T2 (r = .49, p < .001); HAUA T1 → HAUA T2 (r = .47, p < .001); and LAUA T1 → LAUA T2 (r = .59, p < .001).

Discussion

This study presents the adaptation and validation of the Multi-Affect Indicator (MAI) for use in the Polish context. The MAI assesses work-related affective well-being as a multidimensional construct comprising four dimensions: high-activation pleasant affect (HAPA), high-activation unpleasant affect (HAUA), low-activation pleasant affect (LAPA), and low-activation unpleasant affect (LAUA). Overall, the results support the MAI as a brief and psychometrically sound instrument, demonstrating strong factorial validity and meaningful associations with established measures of affect at work, psychological distress, and depressive symptoms. The proposed structure was validated in a large and diverse employee sample, including senior staff, white-collar workers, and blue-collar workers, and was replicated in a second independent sample comprising healthcare workers, sales personnel, and social workers. These findings are consistent with the original MAI validation27 and previous international adaptations45–47.

Across all three samples, the MAI exhibited strong psychometric properties. Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values indicated that each factor accounted for a substantial proportion of variance in its respective affective domain. Factor loadings and reliability coefficients further showed that the items reliably assessed their intended constructs, with high internal consistency across all subscales. Correlations among the four factors were moderate, suggesting that although work-related affective dimensions are interrelated, they remain empirically distinguishable. This pattern is consistent with expectations for self-report measures of affect, and supports the orthogonal nature of the dimensions as theorized by Warr et al.27 and discussed by Larsen et al.74.

The test-retest correlations between Time 1 and Time 2 indicated moderate temporal stability, consistent with the view that affective states are dynamic and less stable than enduring traits. This finding aligns with previous methodological and psychometric work, suggesting that excessive stability in affect measures may indicate limited epistemic validity, as affect is expected to fluctuate over time75. More importantly, the results are consistent with theoretical predictions concerning the temporal variability of affect at work76,77 and extend previous evidence on the dynamic nature of emotional arousal in occupational settings78. Together, these findings underscore the MAI‘s ability to capture fluctuations in work-related affect while maintain adequate reliability. Its moderate temoral stability suggests that the instrument is sensitive to context-specific emotional experiences without compromising psychometric robustness.

Hypothesis H2, which proposed that the four-factor structure of the MAI would be invariant across age, sex, and occupational groups, was supported. The results indicate that the MAI assesses work-related affective well-being with minimal influence from demographic or occupational characteristics, supporting scalar measurement invariance. These findings further strengthen the construct validity of the MAI and suggest that the instrument can be used reliably across diverse occupational contexts and demographic groups. Overall, the results provide support for both the circumplex model of affect1,3 and the four-quadrant structure of job-related affective well-being27. The MAI also achieved scalar invariance between the online Study 1 sample and the paper-and-pencil Study 2 sample, suggesting comparability across administrations modes. However, because administration mode was confounded with sample characteristics, this finding should not be interpreted as a definitive experimental test of mode effects.

The criterion variables correlated with the MAI dimensions in the expected directions, providing support for Hypotheses H3 and H4 regarding criterion validity. Work-related affect, as measured by the MAI, was significantly associated with positive and negative affect using an alternative validated instrument based on the bifactor model of affect12,32. As expected, psychological stress, characterized by highly activated negative emotional states, was moderately negatively associated with positive affect and moderately positively associated with negative affect. Importantly, the association between the MAI’s HAUA dimension and psychological distress supports the relatedness, yet distinctiveness, of these constructs. Similarly, depressive symptoms, which are typically linked to low-activation negative affect, showed a moderate positive correlation with the MAI’s depression-related dimension. These associations indicate that, although related, the constructs assessed by the MAI and those captured by general stress and depression scales are conceptually and empirically distinct. Overall, these findings are consistent with previous research on work-related affective well-being measures2932,34, and further support the validity of the MAI. It is important to note that the anxiety and depression-related subscales in this study refer to self-reported affective states experienced within a working population. In line with Warr’s conceptualization of job-related affective well-being26,27, these dimensions are intended to capture occupational affective states and should not be conflated with clinical psychiatric diagnoses.

Sex and age differences in core affect at work

Research Question 1 (RQ1) addressed general differences in affective states between men and women. Men reported higher levels of LAPA, whereas women reported slightly higher levels of HAUA and LAUA. However, no significant sex differences were found for HAPA. These findings are consistent with previous research suggesting that men tend to report higher levels of positive emotions overall79. This pattern may be related to sex differences in positive self-evaluations, including self-concept, cognitive flexibility, and psychological resilience80–82. Such characteristics are often associated with a stronger sense of control and confidence at work, which is supported by evidence showing that men report slightly higher levels of sense of coherence83 and core self-evaluations, both of which are linked to positive work outcomes84. In contrast, women in the present study were more likely to report higher levels of negative affect. These findings align with previous studies indicating that women may experience negative emotions more frequently or intensely59,85,86, and may report greater psychological strain at work than men in comparable roles87.

The observed effect sizes for sex differences were small, with Cohen’s d values around 0.20. Therefore, findings regarding general affective tendencies among men and women should be interpreted with caution. Although previous psychological research has identified significant sex differences in emotion processing59,60,88, other findings suggest that the practical outcomes of psychological interventions, for instance in clinical contexts, are often independent of sex89. However, similar outcomes do not necessarily imply identical underlying processes. Accordingly, research should continue to account for sex-related differences, even when these are not the primary focus of investigation90. From a management perspective, evidence-based practices may benefit from personalized interventions that consider sex as a fundamental social category91. Prior research in organizational psychology suggests that sex differences in behaviors and evaluation are more likely to emerge when sex becomes salient in a given context or task92. Overall, the small effect sizes observed in the present study are consistent with broader evidence indicating that sex differences in emotional experience are generally small but statistically significant93,94. One possible explanation is that broad constructs, such as core affect at work, vary less by sex than more job-specific traits, behaviors, or role-related predispositions.

Research Question 2 (RQ2) explored whether age significantly differentiates employees in terms of their reported positive and negative affect. Older participants reported significantly higher levels of LAPA (comfort) than younger participants. Additionally, employees in the two oldest age groups reported lower levels of HAUA (anxiety) and LAUA (depression) than those in the two younger groups. The findings also suggest that older workers experience affect less intensely, particularly high-activation positive affect, which was reported significantly less often by participants aged 55 and older than by those aged 18–35 years.

These results are consistent with previous research indicating that older individuals are more likely to experience LAPA and less likely to experience negative affect, reflecting the positivity effect, that is, an age-related tendency to focus more on positive and less on negative experiences95,96. One possible explanation is that older adults tend to show more effective emotional regulation, potentially due to improved functional connectivity between the prefrontal cortex and the amygdala97. The present findings suggest that age-related differences in affect at work become particularly evident from approximately age 45 onward, which is consistent with studies showing that emotional differences often emerge during middle adulthood66. Middle-aged and older workers, who typically have more occupational experience, may perceive their work as more comprehensible, predictable, and manageable98. This may reflect professional maturity, characterized by emotional stability, confidence, and a calm approach to work, traits that align closely with the comfort dimension of affect. In contrast, younger workers may experience more energy and enthusiasm, but also higher levels of stress and anxiety. In conclusion, although older workers may not necessarily experience more positive emotions overall, they appear less likely to experience negative affect, especially high-arousal negative states. These findings support the positivity effect61 and suggest that emotional experiences at work change meaningfully with age.

Although the findings indicate significant differences between younger and older employees, the associated effect sizes were very small for pleasant affect and, at most, small for unpleasant affect. This pattern suggests that age-related differences in occupational affective tendencies require further investigation, particularly in contexts beyond the workplace. While the present study shows that such differences exist, their practical relevance may be limited by the small effect sizes and the context-specific nature of the MAI, which focuses specifically on affective experiences at work.

Limitations and future directions

Despite the study’s rigorous methodological design and the use of three independent and diverse samples, including two large samples in Study 1 and Study 2, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relied exclusively on self-report data, which may be susceptible to response biases. However, self-descriptive methods are widely used in affective research because of the inherently subjective and cognitive nature of affective experiences.

Second, the cross-sectional design of Study 1 and Study 2 represents a limitation. Nevertheless, common method bias is unlikely to pose a serious threat to the findings, as the primary aim of Study 1 was scale adaptation and validation, for which cross-sectional data are appropriate. Moreover, common method variance analyses conducted in Study 1 and Study 2 indicated that the results were only minimally affected by this type of bias.

Third, the study did not directly examine predictive validity. Although the MAI captures affective experiences at work, its ability to predict future attitudes, behaviors, or health-related outcomes remains an open question and should be addressed in future longitudinal research. In addition, the sample size in Study 3 was relatively small (N = 76). Therefore, longitudinal associations with other constructs were not examined, and the statistical power of the test-retest assessment should be interpreted with caution. Another limitation of Study 2 is that measurement invariance across age groups could not be tested. Power analysis indicated that the sample size (N = 326) was insufficient for reliable model estimation in multigroup comparisons, particularly because some subgroups included fewer than 100 participants.

Finaly, although the samples were diverse in terms of occupation, age, and sex, selection bias cannot be ruled out due to the voluntary nature of participation. In summary, despite these limitations, the findings concerning sex and age differences in affectivity provide valuable insights for future research on individual differences in emotional functioning, adult development, occupational health, and quality of life.

Conclusions

The 16-item, four-factor MAI is a concise, valid, and reliable instrument for assessing work-related affective well-being. Reflecting the four dimensions of core affect – enthusiasm, comfort, anxiety, and depression – the Polish version of the MAI demonstrated strong psychometric properties across three independent samples, including robust reliability, construct and criterion validity, and moderate temporal stability. The present research supports the conceptualization of core affect as a multidimensional construct, as proposed in the circumplex model3, and shows that the MAI provides a practical operationalization of this model by capturing the fundamental dimensions of valence and activation.

The study also extends previous work on emotions in occupational settings by showing that affective well-being at work, as measured by the MAI, is significantly associated with psychological distress and depressive symptoms. Importantly, the Polish version of the MAI demonstrated scalar measurement invariance across sex, age, and the occupational group in Study 1, and scalar invariance across sex and the occupational group in Study 2, indicating minimal bias related to key sociodemographic variables. The scale also achieved scalar invariance across data collection methods between Study 1 and Study 2. These findings support the suitability of the MAI for use in diverse professional contexts and highlight its value for research and practice in occupational health, well-being, and organizational psychology.
