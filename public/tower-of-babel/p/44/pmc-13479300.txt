Welcome to the jungle: collection and evaluation of quality indicators with the QUALICATOR instrument
Authors: Jonas Backes, Justus Vogel, Alexander Geissler, Michael M Havranek, Sarah P Schladerer, David Ehlig, Swiss Quality Monitoring Research Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid growth in quality indicators (QIs) has increased complexity in selecting those that are effective for monitoring provider quality and patient outcomes. Existing selection methodologies are often insufficiently transparent or standardised and influenced by subjective opinions. To develop an instrument for evidence-based collection and evaluation of QIs that are suitable for quality monitoring, and which can be applied to various healthcare areas (HCAs; defined by care setting). The instrument was developed with HCA-specific experts, who provided feedback on its components and piloted its use in the Swiss context. We conducted a literature search with snowballing to identify prioritisation criteria and weighted these using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP). We developed a template to facilitate data collection and the QI evaluation. The final QUALICATOR instrument consists of five steps: (1) definition of search scope; (2) utilisation of 12 prioritisation criteria across four dimensions (relevance, scientific soundness, usability, feasibility); (3) application of a data collection template objectifying the prioritisation criteria; (4) preselection via knockout criteria and (5) final prioritisation via weighting multicriteria decision analysis. Final criteria weights derived via the AHP varied substantially across HCAs (eg, relevance: 19.16%–57.10%; scientific soundness: 5.50%–39.50%; usability: 13.17%–33.75%; feasibility: 11.78%–45.57%), reflecting HCA-specific priorities. A web-based prototype is available to support a user-friendly application. This QUALICATOR instrument provides a transparent, scalable approach to navigate through a growing body of QIs with further validation needed. It provides a methodological framework and proof of concept, rather than a ready-to-use solution. It shows policymakers, providers and payers a path to make informed decisions about which QIs to prioritise, monitor, invest in and act on.Keywords: Quality measurement, Decision making, Evaluation methodology, Healthcare quality improvement, Quality improvement methodologies

Introduction

The number of quality initiatives (ie, family of indicators from one organisation on a specific theme) and quality indicators (QIs) for measuring healthcare quality has grown considerably, complicating QI selection for healthcare providers, regulators, payers, researchers and several others across healthcare areas (HCAs).1 In this study, HCAs are defined according to the setting in which care is delivered and comprise six areas: inpatient, outpatient, psychiatric, rehabilitative, community and long-term care. This approach builds on prior classifications such as those proposed by Vogel et al.2

In 2023, >500 Swiss-specific outcome, structure and process QIs were collected for the inpatient somatic (ie, care related to physical, non-mental conditions) HCA alone.3 Similarly, the National Quality Forum (NQF) in the USA lists over 1100 QIs,4 and the number of QIs recorded by the NHS England increased from 70 to over 2000 between 1982 and 2020.5

The proliferation of QIs represents a key barrier to quality monitoring. When setting up quality monitoring systems, health system stakeholders are confronted with an overwhelming number of QIs that vary in relevance, scientific soundness, usability or feasibility. Selecting the most suitable QIs and identifying monitoring gaps has become increasingly complex. In doing so, the premise should be to capture all essential quality aspects with the smallest set of QIs possible.

Healthcare administration accounts for an estimated 15%–35% of national healthcare expenditures, with a substantial portion attributed to QI collection and reporting.6 Individual hospitals can spend over 100 000 person-hours annually on external quality assessments, generating excessive documentation and personnel costs exceeding US$5 million.7 With rising healthcare costs in most countries,8 a growing body of research advocates for reducing the number of QIs to improve resource allocation and overall efficiency.4 5 7

To reduce the number of QIs, organisations and institutions such as WHO, the NQF and the Institute of Medicine have urged stakeholders to prioritise QIs that effectively meet information needs.9–11 Yet, the exact criteria and processes used for evaluating QIs are often unclear, and no standardised instrument currently exists.4 5 12 Delphi-based approaches are commonly applied,13 relying on either HCA-agnostic instruments with prioritisation criteria applicable across all HCAs14–20 or HCA-specific and patient-specific instruments tailored to a particular context.21–25 While HCA-specific approaches allow contextual tailoring, they also lead to inconsistencies in data collection, analysis and interpretation. Although Delphi methods remain an important component of QI evaluation, a standardised instrument is needed to ensure that only relevant, scientifically sound, usable and feasible QIs are monitored. Therefore, our aim was to develop a generic and transparent instrument for collecting and evaluating QIs across different HCAs. Although developed to be internationally applicable, we applied the instrument in the Swiss context across six different HCAs.

Compared with prior literature focusing on qualitative, governance-focused methods for QI evaluation,12 13 our research introduces a new, systematic, semi-quantitative, rule-based instrument. It enhances transparency in QI evaluation by integrating a holistic set of prioritisation criteria while allowing for context-specific weighting of those criteria across HCAs. Thus, our approach aligns with the unique priorities of each HCA.

The remainder of this study outlines the methods used to develop and test each component of the instrument, followed by a detailed presentation of the instrument itself, its application to the Swiss context and a discussion of its unique features and limitations.

Methods

Figure 1 guides through the development of our QUALICATOR instrument, showing five subsequent steps. These steps are: (1) definition of search scope; (2) definition of prioritisation criteria; (3) systematic collection of data required for the evaluation; (4) application of knockout criteria and (5) the final prioritisation of QIs. The defined prioritisation criteria directly inform steps 3, 4 and 5. Thus, our instrument follows global frameworks for priority-setting in health research (eg, WHO’s approach for prioritising research development topics), which sequentially define the decision scope, specify prioritisation criteria and apply them systematically to evaluate available options.26 27

QUALICATOR instrument for the collection and evaluation of quality indicators (QIs). This figure illustrates the five-step process of the QI instrument, including: (1) definition of search scope, (2) definition of prioritisation criteria, (3) data collection based on prioritisation criteria, (4) application of knockout criteria and (5) final prioritisation through weighted scoring.

The instrument was developed collaboratively by the authors and six expert groups, each representing a distinct HCA (ie, inpatient and outpatient somatic, psychiatric, long-term, rehabilitative and community care). Each group consisted of multiple senior leads (eg, full or associate professors) with academic and/or clinical expertise in quality measurement. The expert group leads consisted of a ratio of ~40% female to male and represented five native languages. All groups were additionally supported by research staff members assisting with data collection and evaluation. Online supplemental material 1 summarises the profiles of all leads. While the authors developed the overall instrument and methodology, its application to the Swiss healthcare context was conducted independently within each expert group.

Step 1: definition of search scope

To define the QI search scope, expert groups proposed inclusion criteria based on their domain knowledge. These criteria were reviewed by the authors to ensure applicability across HCAs. The criteria aimed to focus the search on relevant QIs and initiatives while excluding non-applicable ones early. An early definition of the search scope is particularly relevant for HCAs with numerous available QIs (eg, inpatient somatic care) to ensure subsequent steps remain manageable.

Step 2: definition of prioritisation criteria

We conducted a literature search using PubMed, Google Scholar and Web of Science to define the prioritisation criteria within our instrument. Starting from the search string “health care quality indicator selection”, we applied a snowballing technique by reviewing citations and references from key publications.28 No restrictions were placed on countries or publication dates. We extracted all prioritisation criteria and consolidated overlapping ones (eg, ‘scientific soundness’ and ‘scientific acceptability’) to establish a holistic set of criteria.

Step 3: data collection

To ensure systematic and objective assessment of QIs against the prioritisation criteria, we developed a QI data collection template grounded in the criteria defined in step 2. The template defined the data required to evaluate each QI. Its purpose was to enhance comparability of QIs’ performance across prioritisation criteria while minimising subjective judgements by experts. An initial version was piloted using mortality, readmission and revision surgery rates from inpatient somatic care. The template was presented to group experts via on-demand online tutorials and refined in two rounds of written feedback. Feedback was minor and included wording adjustments and the addition of a data collection field for patient-reported outcome and experience measures. The template was supplemented with an explanation and a frequently asked questions page.

Step 4: preselection via knockout criteria

Predefined knockout criteria aim for early exclusion of unsuitable QIs to reduce workload in the final prioritisation phase (step 5). Only QIs fulfilling all criteria progress to the final prioritisation step. Knockout criteria were defined by the authors, based on the full set of prioritisation criteria (step 2) and two principles: (1) the criterion must be assessable as a binary pass/fail judgement and (2) failure of the criterion makes the QI fundamentally unsuitable for quality monitoring, regardless of performance on other criteria. Knockout criteria were jointly agreed on between the authors and six expert groups. Criteria not selected for preselection reflect dimensions where partial fulfilment is informative, but binary thresholds would risk premature exclusion of QIs with improvable performance.

Given the selection process of knockout criteria, there is a deliberate overlap between criteria in steps 4 and 5. Step 4 applies a subset of prioritisation criteria in a binary manner. In contrast, step 5 assesses all prioritisation criteria (including knockout criteria) on a graded scale to identify qualitative performance differences.

Step 5: prioritisation of quality indicators

Prior literature shows that HCAs differ in the importance assigned to QI selection criteria.12 To reflect this, a multicriteria decision analysis (MCDA) approach was applied. MCDA supports complex decision-making by breaking down the process into subdecisions and was endorsed by the European Medicines Agency in 2010.29 30 It integrates both quantitative and qualitative criteria, allows flexible adjustments of weights and scores and provides a transparent method for comparing QIs. Thereby, each prioritisation criterion is weighted according to its relative importance within the respective HCA.

Equation 1 illustrates the concept of MCDA, where Sih is the overall score of the indicator i for HCA h, calculated as the weighted sum of the scores cmih for each prioritisation criterion m, multiplied by the respective weight wmh, with m=1,…, n indexing the prioritisation criteria.

To score each prioritisation criterion c, we developed a standardised questionnaire with a 5-point Likert scale. Expert groups assigned scores based on data collected in step 3. Data sources for scoring were defined by the authors based on the objective measurability of each prioritisation criterion. Objectively assessable criteria (eg, discriminatory power via empirical distributions) were scored using step 3 data. The full questionnaire and Likert scale definitions are presented in the ‘Results’ section. More subjective criteria (eg, QI relevance, understandability or ease of implementation) were assessed via a two-round Delphi process including two online surveys and one interim plenary discussion. External experts first rated all QIs passing knockout screening on a 5-point Likert scale; only items with IQR >1 were discussed in the plenary and re-rated in a second survey. If consensus (IQR ≤1) was not reached, mean scores were used and criteria were flagged as non-consensus for transparency.

To determine the relative weights of the prioritisation criteria, the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) was applied,31 using pairwise comparisons to derive the relative importance (wmh) of each criterion (cmih). Within each group, a minimum of two experts independently performed the pairwise comparisons. In line with established collaborative elicitation approaches, differences were resolved through joint discussion. A consistency check was applied to ensure coherent pairwise comparisons.30

After deriving weights, results were compared across HCAs to identify similarities and differences. This comparison was purely analytical; no ex post adjustments were made. Online supplemental material 2 provides the details of the weighting procedure.

Step 2: definition of prioritisation criteria

Thirteen sources informed the definition of the prioritisation criteria, resulting in 12 criteria grouped into four overarching dimensions: (1) relevance, (2) scientific soundness, (3) usability and (4) feasibility (table 1). These dimensions capture the constantly recurring criteria for the assessment of QIs in the literature. Relevance addresses the alignment of QIs with high-impact health issues and stakeholder priorities. Scientific soundness evaluates methodological rigour of QIs, including validity, reliability and discriminatory power. Usability focuses on interpretability and practical utility of QIs for users, while feasibility considers data availability, implementation burden and the potential for subgroup analyses.

Step 3: data collection

We developed a data collection template aligned with the 12 prioritisation criteria defined in table 1. The template operationalises each criterion into measurable data points, thereby enabling standardised data collection across QIs and HCAs (online supplemental material 5). It comprises ~50 data points, each mapped to a corresponding criterion. For instance, discriminatory power is assessed by collecting data on the percentage of providers at both extremes, to identify potential floor or ceiling effects. Data gathered through this template form the empirical basis for the subsequent preselection (step 4) and final prioritisation (step 5).

To ensure consistent scoring, explicit thresholds are defined for each level of the 5-point Likert scale (online supplemental material 6). For instance, for data availability, a score of 1 reflects the absence of national data, while 5 denotes real-time availability with minimal time lag. For criteria where nuanced grading is not meaningful (eg, validity), intermediate scale points were omitted. Likert scale thresholds are intended as initial guidance and should be recalibrated to the specific context. Criteria considered not applicable or not assessable via Delphi are excluded from the scoring, ensuring QIs are not penalised for inapplicable criteria.

Discussion

Contributions and distinctiveness

This study aimed to develop an instrument for the collection and evaluation of QIs. By systematically collecting and evaluating QIs, we support decision-makers in allocating resources towards the most relevant, scientifically sound, usable and feasible QIs. Most existing instruments are exclusively based on expert consensus techniques.12 13 The proposed instrument distinguishes itself in three ways from previous works.

First, it introduces HCA-specific weighting of prioritisation criteria, enabling QI evaluation that reflects distinct needs while maintaining methodological consistency. MCDA has been widely adopted by European HTA bodies for investment and authorisation decisions.33 Despite its methodological rigour, MCDA has not been applied to QI evaluation. To our knowledge, this study is the first to implement a weighted MCDA approach for the evaluation of QIs. Yet, failing to explicitly weight criteria implicitly assumes equal importance. If not justified, this embeds hidden biases towards less important criteria and may lead to rankings conflicting with expert intuition.34 35 Our Swiss context application shows that HCAs may weigh criteria differently, likely reflecting HCA-specific challenges. For instance, inpatient somatic care experts strongly emphasised scientific soundness (40.0%). Hospitals typically have a rich data infrastructure, making feasibility less of a constraint.36 Consequently, prioritisation shifts towards filtering from a wide array of available data to select scientifically sound QIs. By contrast, rehabilitative care experts rated feasibility highest (45.6%), likely reflecting fragmented documentation and high manual data capture burdens.37 For them, the availability of data is a top priority (18.8%).

Second, the instrument incorporates a structured preselection via scope definition and knockout criteria. Early filtration is particularly valuable in HCAs with a high volume of quality initiatives or QIs, such as inpatient somatic care,38 as it reduces the subsequent evaluation burden. In contrast to tools such as QUALIFY,18 which requires assessing each QI against 20 criteria, our instrument enables fast exclusion of QIs that are conceptually weak, poorly defined or statistically immature. This supports a more targeted and resource-conscious evaluation process.

Third, the instrument stands out for its methodological transparency: it provides access to all tools used, including data collection templates, scoring questionnaires, decision trees and Likert scales. This openness facilitates replication in other institutions or national contexts and addresses a recurrent critique in the literature: the lack of clarity in how prioritisation is operationalised in practice.39 40

A persistent challenge is the lack of concrete guidance for scoring complex criteria.12 Terms like ‘validity’ and ‘reliability’ are frequently cited,14–19 yet often remain conceptually vague.18 For instance, how can one determine what an indicator should measure, or at what point consistency is sufficient to justify a high reliability score? Boulkedid et al13 observed that scarcity of empirical evidence often leads to heavy reliance on expert opinion (eg, via Delphi panels). While expert input remains important, our instrument addresses this limitation by offering structured tools to ground evaluations in empirical data. The data collection template specifies data required to assess each criterion, while Likert scales enable standardised scoring. This approach equips evaluators with the tools necessary to move beyond conceptual definitions towards empirically anchored and replicable evaluations.

Stakeholder and practical use

Our instrument targets various stakeholders. Most directly, this includes health authorities and regulators responsible for selecting QIs, such as national quality commissions or ministries. But it is also relevant for payers linking contracting to quality performance, providers reviewing internal QI portfolios or researchers updating QI sets. It is best suited to contexts where a large pool of potential QIs must be systematically narrowed.

However, quality of care is often referred to as an essentially contested concept. Stakeholders may share the goal of high quality of care, yet they may differ in how it is defined and operationalised.41 These differences reflect underlying structural factors, including institutional mandates, incentive structures and the specific decisions QIs are intended to inform.42 Accordingly, a clinician monitoring patient safety, a regulator benchmarking providers, an insurer linking reimbursement to performance or a patient choosing a provider may each prioritise different QI characteristics. Thus, stakeholders may differ in how they evaluate QIs, reflecting their interests, experience and mandates4: patients may prioritise understandability, insurers comparability, clinicians and researchers scientific soundness and hospital managers ease of implementation.

QUALICATOR accommodates this heterogeneity by allowing separate weightings per stakeholder group. For our Swiss piloting, we focus predominantly on academics with experience in quality measurement and evaluation. Future users should clearly define their target audience and, if necessary, consider implementing parallel assessment to accommodate different priorities.

Transferability and context adaptation

Moreover, our empirical findings reflect the perspective of Swiss experts across six HCAs. While the instrument is transferable, its application in other countries or institutional settings requires adaptation. The five-step logic is broadly transferable and context independent. Within steps, adaptations may be required. Some are mandatory: AHP should be re-derived locally to reflect context-specific priorities, and Likert scale thresholds require recalibration to align with national standards. Other adaptations are context dependent: the HCA classification may need adjustment to reflect how healthcare delivery is organised in the target country. Although the data collection logic is transferable, specific data fields (eg, event rates or sample size thresholds) may require recalibration to local data sources and availability. Thus, the instrument offers a methodological template rather than a ‘plug-and-play’ solution and provides all tools to drive context-specific results.

Limitations and outlook

Although limitations of purely qualitative methods have been critically discussed in the ‘Introduction’ section, we deliberately retained a Delphi component to inform criteria that resist objective measurement. Prioritisation criteria such as QI (1.1) high impact issue and (1.2) stakeholder importance cannot easily be reduced to numeric values or thresholds; here, expert judgement remains indispensable. Unlike existing prioritisation methods, our instrument does not use the Delphi as the primary vehicle for prioritisation, but as one component within a broader methodological toolkit.

We acknowledge that our instrument is sensitive to the weights assigned to each criterion. Weights are derived via expert judgement and reflect context-specific assumptions. Changing them could lead to shifts in QI prioritisation, which may impact robustness and reproducibility of QI rankings. Thus, our weights are directional and not definitive measures that universally define the importance of each criterion. Even within a single HCA, different groups may derive different weights, reflecting variability in expert judgement.

While this introduces a bias similar to the subjective process QUALICATOR seeks to improve, there remains a distinction between implicit, unstructured subjectivity (as for most existing instruments) and the explicit and structured subjectivity embedded in our instrument. By making weighting, Delphi-assessed criteria and scoring systems transparent, QUALICATOR enables users to scrutinise, challenge and adapt choices. However, formal validation of weightings (eg, via test-retest reliability studies) and assessment of QIs’ ranking stability remain important paths for future research. Experts from the same HCA and context can be divided into independent subgroups, each applying the instrument to an identical set of QIs, with resulting scores and rankings compared. Divergence would identify instrument steps requiring further refinement.

Moreover, each HCA was represented by a relatively small number of experts (2–4); thus, QUALICATOR steps such as the weighting may be influenced by the respective experience and perspectives of professionals within each group. Experts were predominantly academic with expertise in quality measurement. Thus, not all viewpoints (eg, hospital managers) may fully be captured, and outcomes (eg, the AHP) could differ if other expert profiles had been included. Future users of QUALICATOR are encouraged to expand the range of actors involved in the instrument application. For instance, by running parallel weightings across various stakeholder groups and analysing the sensitivity of QI scoring.

We recognise that applying the QUALICATOR requires familiarity with its underlying logic. First-time users may benefit from experimenting with the instrument on a small number of QIs before broader usage. Reducing barriers to adoption remains an ongoing focus of this research group. Thus, to enhance usability and accessibility, the developed instrument has been transformed into an interactive web-based application (prototype) that guides users through each step.43 Screenshots of the prototype can be viewed in online supplemental material 7. Besides, researchers in other countries may pilot the QUALICATOR instrument, adjust it to local circumstances and use it to cope with QI proliferation. Furthermore, this instrument could be adapted to and tested in other industries (eg, education, manufacturing), where QIs are used for quality and performance tracking.

Overall, QUALICATOR should be seen as a newly developed instrument, which requires further validation and refinement. The Swiss application provides a proof of concept across six HCAs. However, validation (ie, external replication or longitudinal testing of quality of indicator sets) is outstanding. Until such evidence is available, the instrument should be viewed as a structured and transparent guide that formalises QI selection rather than a fully validated decision-analytic tool.

Conclusion

The proliferation of healthcare QIs has created complexity. As measurement burdens grow, so does the risk of undermining the very purpose of quality measurement. We have developed QUALICATOR to support the collection and evaluation of QIs. By focusing on relevant, methodologically sound, usable and feasible QIs, our instrument aims to help systems and stakeholders move from quantity to quality. Rather than expanding QI sets further, the priority must shift towards selecting the most critical QIs that inform action and ultimately support the improvement of the quality of care.
