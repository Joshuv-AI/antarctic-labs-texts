Inflammatory Status Modifies the Association Between Glycemic Traits and Cognitive Impairment in Patients With Coronary Artery Disease
Authors: Lanxin Feng, Jianan Li, Shuwen Yang, Xin Zhao, Jianqiao Song, Xiaoyi Wang, Chenyang Liu, Min Zhang, Xiantao Song, Chenchen Tu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe evidence regarding the modifying role of inflammation in the association between glycemic traits and cognitive impairment remains insufficient. This study aimed to explore the association between glycemic traits and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in patients with coronary artery disease (CAD), with particular emphasis on whether this association is influenced by varying levels of inflammation.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study included 1437 patients with CAD who underwent cognitive function assessment during hospitalization. Cognitive function was assessed using the Mini‐Mental State Examination and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment to identify patients with MCI. The hsCRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein) level>3 mg/L was defined as high inflammatory risk. Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to estimate the associations of glycemic traits and inflammatory risk status with cognitive impairment.ResultsElevated fasting plasma glucose, glycosylated hemoglobin, and diabetes were positively associated with an increased risk of MCI in patients with CAD, with odds ratios ranging from 1.18 (95% CI, 1.10–1.26) to 1.55 (95% CI, 1.09–2.20). A significant interaction was observed between glycosylated hemoglobin and high inflammatory status on the risk of MCI (P for multiplicative interaction <0.001), with the effects of glycosylated hemoglobin on the risk of MCI being significant only in the group with high hsCRP. Additionally, there was evidence of significant additive interaction between high inflammatory risk and diabetes on the MCI risk in patients with CAD (P for additive interaction <0.05).ConclusionsInflammatory status modifies the association between glycemic traits and the risk of cognitive impairment. This finding highlights the importance of incorporating inflammation into the management of blood glucose levels in patients with CAD.Keywords: coronary artery disease, diabetes, inflammation, mild cognitive impairmentSubject Categories: Coronary Artery Disease, Clinical Studies, Inflammation

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

Inflammation significantly modifies the association between glycemic traits (especially glycosylated hemoglobin) and mild cognitive impairment in patients with coronary artery disease, with effects mainly observed in those with elevated high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein levels.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Simultaneous assessment and management of glycemic control and inflammation may enhance early identification and prevention of cognitive decline in patients with coronary artery disease.

Simultaneous assessment and management of glycemic control and inflammation may enhance early identification and prevention of cognitive decline in patients with coronary artery disease.

Coronary artery disease (CAD) and cognitive impairment have gradually become major public health concerns worldwide, imposing significant global disease and medical burdens. 1 , 2 CAD is widely recognized as a major risk factor for cognitive impairment, and the coexistence of these conditions has become increasingly common in clinical practice. 3 However, effective strategies for preventing and managing cognitive decline in patients with CAD remain limited. In recent years, research evidence has increasingly linked diabetes to cognitive impairment, suggesting that optimal diabetes management may reduce the risk of cognitive decline in patients with CAD. 4 , 5

However, there is considerable disagreement regarding the relationship between diabetes and cognitive impairment, and some studies have failed to identify a significant association between diabetes and the progression of cognitive impairment to dementia. 6 , 7 Furthermore, few studies have examined associations extending beyond diabetes status itself, particularly regarding other glycemic traits such as glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and fasting plasma glucose (FPG), leaving their impact on cognitive impairment insufficiently explored.

In recent years, many studies have demonstrated that hsCRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein), a key biomarker of systemic inflammation, not only reflects residual inflammatory risk in patients with CAD but is also strongly associated with the risk of cognitive impairment. 8 , 9 Emerging evidence suggests that the systemic inflammatory state, as indicated by hsCRP, may play a significant regulatory role in the pathogenesis of cognitive impairment. 10 As a complex multifactorial disease, the coexistence of inflammation and abnormal blood glucose homeostasis may be associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment. However, the joint effects of glycemic traits and inflammatory status on the risk of cognitive impairment have not been fully explored. It remains unclear whether managing glycemic traits based on inflammatory status is a more effective strategy for preventing and controlling cognitive impairment in patients with CAD.

Therefore, this study aimed to confirm the association between glycemic traits and cognitive impairment and further explored the joint effects of inflammatory status and glycemic traits on cognitive impairment in patients with CAD through interactions on both the multiplicative and additive scales.

METHODS

The data analyzed in the current study are available from the corresponding authors upon reasonable request.

Study Population

This study included patients with CAD who underwent cognitive function assessments during hospitalization at the Coronary Heart Disease Center of Beijing Anzhen Hospital between August 2023 and September 2024. The diagnostic criteria for CAD included coronary angiographic evidence of >50% stenosis in at least 1 major coronary artery, as well as a history of myocardial infarction or coronary revascularization. In this cross‐sectional study, we excluded patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI; n=186), as the acute phase of AMI is associated with substantial physiological stress, metabolic disturbances, and systemic inflammation, which may transiently alter glycemic parameters and cognitive function and thus introduce potential confounding bias. 11 We also excluded patients with severe mental disease (n=15), prior cerebral vascular accident (n=171), possible dementia (n=326), or missing covariate data (n=49). The present study included 1437 patients without dementia with CAD in the final analysis (Figure). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent.

Flowchart of patient enrollment in the study.

Data Collection and Definitions

All patients had fasting venous blood samples collected within 24 hours of hospital admission, and laboratory tests, including FPG and HbA1c, were conducted following standardized procedures. Diabetes and prediabetes were defined based on the standard diagnostic criteria of the American Diabetes Association. 12 Diabetes was defined as FPG level≥7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL), HbA1c level≥6.5% (48 mmol/mol), self‐reported history of diabetes, or history of glucose‐lowering medication use. Additionally, prediabetes was defined as FPG level of 5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) to <7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL) or HbA1c level of 5.7% (39 mmol/mol) to <6.5% (48 mmol/mol). The hsCRP concentration was measured using the immunoturbidimetric method. According to the recommendations of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the American Heart Association, hsCRP levels were categorized using a cutoff value of 3 mg/L: levels >3 mg/L (high hsCRP) and levels ≤3 mg/L (low hsCRP). 13

In clinical practice at our center, cognitive function assessments are not routinely performed for all patients. Instead, screening is selectively conducted for individuals who either (1) self‐report memory complaints or other cognitive symptoms, or (2) are identified by clinicians as being at high risk for cognitive impairment based on the presence of established risk factors. 14 This targeted approach aims to improve the early identification of cognitive impairment in patients with CAD. Accordingly, the patients included in this study were those who met these criteria and underwent standardized cognitive testing as part of their clinical evaluation. Cognitive function was assessed by trained professionals using the Chinese versions of the Mini‐Mental State Examination and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). The Mini‐Mental State Examination was used to exclude patients with possible dementia, with a score <24 indicating potential dementia. 15 Because the Mini‐Mental State Examination is not sufficiently sensitive for detecting early cognitive decline associated with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), the MoCA was used for screening. 16 , 17 The MoCA has a total score of 30 points, with an additional point added for individuals with ≤12 years of education, as recommended by the developers. 18 In this study, an education‐adjusted MoCA score of <26 was used as the cutoff to identify individuals with suspected MCI. These participants subsequently underwent a secondary evaluation by an independent neurologist to confirm the clinical diagnosis.

Statistical Analysis

The Wilcoxon rank‐sum test and Pearson's chi‐square test were used to compare differences in continuous and categorical variables between groups, respectively. Multivariable logistic regression models were fitted to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs of inflammatory risk status and glycemic traits on the risk of cognitive impairment. Adjusted restricted cubic splines were used to evaluate the dose–response relationship between FPG, HbA1c, and hsCRP levels with the risk of cognitive impairment. To assess the potential modifying effect of inflammation on the association between glycemic traits and MCI, we first conducted multiplicative interaction analyses. Cross‐product terms between hsCRP status (≤3 versus >3 mg/L) and each glycemic trait (eg, HbA1c, FPG) were included in multivariable logistic regression models to evaluate interactions on the multiplicative scale.

Furthermore, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of effect modification, we also assessed additive interactions, which capture interactions on the absolute risk scale and are considered complementary to multiplicative interactions. Additive interactions can offer insights into potential biological or public health synergy between risk factors. Given its clinical relevance as a categorical marker of glycemic traits, diabetes status was selected for additive interaction analysis with hsCRP. Additive interactions between high hsCRP and diabetes status on the risk of cognitive impairment were also assessed using the relative excess risk due to the interaction and the attributable proportion due to the interaction. Stratified analyses by sex were also conducted to assess potential effect modification. Finally, a sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the association between glycemic traits and the risk of cognitive impairment under different inflammatory risk statuses after excluding patients taking antidiabetic medications. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A 2‐sided P‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To further explore the potential influence of sex on our study findings, we performed sex‐stratified analyses. As shown in Table S1, the interaction between HbA1c and elevated hsCRP on the risk of MCI remained significant in both male and female patients (P for interaction=0.016 and <0.001, respectively), supporting the consistency of our results across sexes. DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that elevated levels of FPG and HbA1c were associated with an increased risk of MCI in patients with CAD. Notably, among patients with high inflammatory risk status (hsCRP >3 mg/L), the associations between FPG, HbA1c, diabetes status, and MCI risk were stronger than those observed in patients with lower inflammatory risk. We additionally identified a multiplicative interaction between hsCRP and HbA1c levels, as well as an additive interaction between high hsCRP and diabetes on MCI risk. Our findings suggest that inflammatory risk status modifies the association between glycemic traits and cognitive impairment in patients with CAD.

Evidence from multiple studies indicates that cardiovascular disease is a major risk factor for cognitive impairment and its progression to dementia. 19 , 20 Patients with CAD are at an increased risk of cognitive impairment, with mechanisms such as oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction playing key roles. 21 , 22 Findings from a population‐based cohort study indicated that a higher cardiovascular risk burden may contribute to accelerated cognitive decline. 23 Patients with CAD and cognitive impairment face a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular events and a worse clinical prognosis. 24 However, the lack of targeted treatments for cognitive impairment currently exists in clinical practice, highlighting the importance of early identification of modifiable risk factors to prevent and mitigate cognitive decline in patients with CAD. 25 Diabetes and chronic low‐grade inflammation are recognized as potential risk factors for cognitive impairment. 5 However, whether inflammatory status and glycemic traits can improve the management of cognitive function in patients with CAD remains insufficiently explored.

Epidemiological evidence has shown that glycemic traits are associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment. A Swedish longitudinal population‐based study shows that diabetes is associated with the accelerated progression of cognitive impairment when glycemic control is poor. 26 Additionally, a study conducted in Chinese rural communities also demonstrated that diabetes status (prediabetes and diabetes) is a statistically significant risk factor for cognitive impairment. 27 Consistent with our findings, a previous meta‐analysis of 144 prospective studies demonstrated that diabetes confers a 1.25‐ to 1.91‐fold increased risk of cognitive impairment. 28 Despite epidemiological evidence indicating an increased risk of cognitive impairment and dementia in patients with diabetes, the causality of this association remains debated. Some studies have reported no significant association between diabetes and cognitive impairment risk. 7 , 29 A population‐based study also found no association between diabetes and the risk of progression from cognitive impairment to dementia. 6 These heterogeneous findings may be partially attributed to self‐reported diabetes, self‐reported cognitive impairment, or differences in participant characteristics. In addition, unlike other typical end‐organ complications of diabetes, such as diabetic nephropathy and diabetic retinopathy, there is currently no clear evidence that the increased risk of cognitive impairment in patients can be solely attributed to glycemic control. 5 Cognitive impairment, as a complex multifactorial disease, arises from the interplay of multiple risk factors and exhibits substantial individual heterogeneity. In this study, we found that the coexistence of diabetes and a high inflammatory state significantly heightened the risk of cognitive impairment in patients with CAD. Moreover, the association between glycemic traits and cognitive impairment is influenced by the broader context of cardiometabolic disorders.

Inflammation, a key driver of cardiovascular disease, is increasingly recognized as closely linked to cognitive impairment. The relationship between inflammation and cognitive impairment is complex and multifaceted, involving multiple biological pathways and risk factors. Chronic inflammation can contribute to vascular pathologies, such as endothelial dysfunction, which are known to accelerate cognitive decline and progression to dementia. 30 Systemic inflammation is also considered an early event in the course of Alzheimer's disease, as it contributes to β‐amyloid accumulation. 31 Additionally, recent studies have shown that circulating inflammatory biomarkers are associated with a higher risk of cerebrovascular events and cognitive decline in older adults, further highlighting the complex relationship between inflammation and cognitive impairment. 32 , 33 Inflammaging, the chronic low‐grade inflammation associated with aging, is increasingly recognized as a crucial framework for understanding the interconnections between inflammation, diabetes, and cognitive decline. 34 However, little research has explored the joint association of inflammatory risk states and glycemic traits with cognitive impairment risk. In this study, we evaluated patients' inflammatory status using hsCRP and assessed the interaction between inflammatory status and glycemic traits on cognitive impairment risk in patients with CAD. As expected, hsCRP was positively associated with the risk of cognitive impairment. Moreover, the association between HbA1c levels and cognitive impairment risk became stronger with increasing hsCRP, suggesting that the combined effect of high inflammatory status and elevated HbA1c levels exceeded the product of their individual effects. Additionally, we found that the risk of cognitive impairment in patients with CAD who had both diabetes and high inflammatory status was greater than the sum of the risks associated with each factor individually, providing evidence of a significant interaction on an additive scale.

It is noteworthy that this study is the first to evaluate the interaction between inflammatory status and glycemic traits (FPG and HbA1c levels, diabetes status) in relation to the risk of cognitive impairment, a finding of significant importance. From a public health perspective, these results may help identify patients with CAD most susceptible to cognitive impairment and those who would benefit the most from glycemic control. Therefore, for patients with CAD with high inflammatory status, particularly those with diabetes, close monitoring and management of blood glucose levels are essential. This approach not only reduces the risk of cardiovascular events but may also slow cognitive decline to some extent. Adopting a comprehensive management strategy, including inflammation control and glycemic optimization, may enhance overall health and clinical outcomes in patients with CAD.

The mechanisms underlying the interaction between inflammation, diabetes, and cognitive impairment remain unclear. Hyperglycemia can directly induce the synthesis of advanced glycation end products, which exert neurotoxic effects that contribute to the progression of cognitive decline. 35 Additionally, hyperglycemia can activate a substantial number of microglia in the central nervous system through stress damage, leading to neuronal apoptosis mediated by proinflammatory factors. 36 Insulin resistance, particularly in the hippocampus, is recognized as a major contributor to cognitive impairment. 37 Chronic inflammation can drive both peripheral and central insulin resistance, thereby exacerbating the risk of cognitive impairment. 38 Moreover, inflammatory mediators may alter enzyme activity in key metabolic pathways, providing another potential connection between inflammation and cognitive decline. 39 Further research is essential to elucidate these mechanisms, which could enhance the development of more effective prevention, management, and treatment strategies for cognitive impairment.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, due to its cross‐sectional design, a causal relationship between glycemic traits and cognitive impairment cannot be firmly established, and the possibility of reverse causation cannot be ruled out. Second, as a single‐center study, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Although extensive confounder adjustments and sensitivity analyses were performed, residual confounding factors may still influence the results. Future large‐scale multicenter studies are necessary to validate these findings. Third, although cognitive impairment was assessed using the MoCA, which is a well‐validated and widely used screening tool for MCI, it remains a general measure and may not capture subtle deficits in specific cognitive domains such as memory, executive function, or attention. Similarly, the inflammatory status was assessed solely using hsCRP, which reflects systemic inflammation but does not provide information on specific inflammatory pathways. The use of more comprehensive neuropsychological assessments and a broader panel of inflammatory biomarkers, such as IL‐6 (interleukin‐6) or TNF‐α (tumor necrosis factor alpha), may provide deeper insights and should be considered in future studies. Finally, cognitive assessments in this study were selectively administered to patients who reported subjective cognitive symptoms or were deemed at high risk for cognitive impairment based on clinical risk factors. As such, the study population may have been enriched with individuals more likely to exhibit cognitive dysfunction, introducing potential selection bias and limiting the generalizability of the findings to all patients with CAD.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, this study provides evidence that the association between HbA1c levels and cognitive impairment is significantly modified by inflammatory risk status on a multiplicative scale. Additionally, diabetes was significantly associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment, and high inflammatory risk may interact additively with diabetes on cognitive impairment risk. Our findings highlight that combining the management of glycemic traits with inflammatory risk may offer a more effective approach to preventing cognitive impairment or arresting its progression to dementia in patients with CAD.
