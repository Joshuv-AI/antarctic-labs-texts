It Takes Guts: Interactions of Intestinal Stearoyl CoA Desaturase 1 and Bile Acids
Authors: Jessica M Ferrell
Published: 2024

Stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (Scd1) is the rate-limiting enzyme that converts saturated fatty acids to monounsaturated fatty acids, which can then be incorporated into de novo lipogenesis. Scd1 is widely expressed in the liver, skin, adipose tissue, and intestine, and its inhibition protects against diet-induced obesity, steatosis, and symptoms of metabolic syndrome.1, 2, 3, 4 The mechanism of action may involve altered bile acid physiology; in addition to their roles as physiological detergents, bile acids bind metabolic receptors to promote lipid and energy metabolism. Bile acids also shape the gut microbiota composition, and the microbiota reciprocally alters bile acid composition. These alterations can then affect host physiology and can be further dysregulated by metabolic disease.

Key Findings

Burchat et al5 used an intestine-specific Scd1 knockout mouse model (iKO) maintained on standard chow or high-fat diet for 12 weeks to study energy and lipid metabolism. In chow-fed iKO mice, circulating bile acids were drastically induced and composition was shifted to taurine-conjugation, indicating reduced hydrophobicity and toxicity. Expression of the intestinal bile acid reuptake transporter Asbt was significantly induced in ileum, while expression of hepatic bile acid synthesis and reuptake genes were reduced, and fecal bile acid excretion was also reduced, providing a potential route to explain the increased serum bile acids. The authors note that transcriptional regulators of Asbt were also induced in iKO ileum, although further studies are required to determine the mechanism of activation.

Simultaneously, markers of hepatic injury and inflammation were not induced, nor was activation of the bile acid receptor farnesoid X receptor. However, the data indicated that another bile acid receptor, Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (Tgr5), may be involved in mediating some of the energy metabolism effects seen in iKO mice. Although Tgr5 gene expression was not changed, downstream targets of Tgr5 were induced in iKO mice, including plasma glucagon-like peptide-1 and deiodinase 2 gene expression in brown adipose tissue. When fed a high-fat diet, iKO mice exhibited further metabolic effects indicative of Tgr5 activation, including reduced body weight, and increased respiratory exchange ratio, heat production, and locomotor activity. Plasma bile acids remained nonsignificantly elevated in iKO mice, although it is noted that the Tgr5 agonists lithocholic acid and ursodeoxycholic acid were both significantly induced.

Given the localization of Scd1 in the ileum and the bidirectional relationship between bile acids and the microbiome, the authors next analyzed the gut microbiome in chow-fed iKO mice. Intestine-specific Scd1 deletion significantly reduced alpha diversity and induced changes in the populations of 74 microbial variants identified in iKO mice. Plasma bile acids were associated with alterations in variants of Clostridiales, Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Muribaculaceae, and several of these genera are implicated in the formation and maintenance of the circulating bile acid pool.6,7 Although the variants identified in this study were not directly correlated to these functions, these results provide insight into how an altered intestinal environment can contribute to bile acid metabolism, and rationale for the continued study of Scd1 inhibition for the prevention of metabolic disease.8

Conclusions

The prevalence of metabolic syndrome in the United States is estimated to be approximately 30%–40% and this number is increasing yearly, especially in young adults.9,10 Development of novel therapeutics is critical to halt this climb, and intestinal physiology is often overlooked as a contributor to overt metabolic disease or liver steatosis. Burchat et al5 present intriguing data that support the inhibition of intestinal Scd1 to improve symptoms of metabolic syndrome through the modification of bile acids. These results warrant further investigation into how Scd1 interacts with bile acids and the microbiome to affect energy metabolism.
