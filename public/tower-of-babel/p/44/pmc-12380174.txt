Conversion of natural cytokine receptors into orthogonal synthetic biosensors
Authors: Hailey I. Edelstein, Amparo Cosio, Max L. Ezekiel, William K. Corcoran, Aaron H. Morris, Joshua N. Leonard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Synthetic receptors enable bioengineers to build cell-based therapies that perform therapeutic functions in a targeted or conditional fashion to enhance specificity and efficacy. Although many synthetic receptors exist, it remains challenging to generate new receptors that sense soluble cues and relay that detection through orthogonal mechanisms independent of native pathways. Here we co-opt natural cytokine receptor ectodomains into modular extracellular sensor architecture (MESA) receptors to form natural ectodomain (NatE) MESA receptors. We generated multiple functional, orthogonal synthetic cytokine receptors, identified design principles and constraints and propose guidance for extending this approach to other natural receptors. We demonstrate the utility of NatE MESA by engineering T cells to sense an immunosuppressive cue and respond with customized transcriptional output to support chimeric antigen receptor T cell activity. Lastly, we multiplex NatE MESA to logically evaluate multiple cues associated with the tumor microenvironment. These technologies and learnings will enable engineering cellular functions for new applications.

Main

Engineered cell therapies enable the targeted treatment of complex diseases by leveraging the intrinsic properties of mammalian cells1. These advances often use synthetic receptors, which direct cells to sense and respond to specific environmental cues2. Extending engineered cell therapies to address unmet medical needs will require suitable synthetic receptors for application-specific sensing and control.

Existing synthetic receptors differ in mechanism and capabilities2. Some receptors transduce extracellular sensing into natural, co-opted intracellular signaling pathways. Key examples include chimeric antigen receptors (CARs)3,4, synthetic cytokine receptors5 and the generalized extracellular molecule sensor6. These receptors can regulate custom transcriptional output using engineered promoters responsive to natural transcription factors (TFs). A potential limitation of this approach is the possibility of crosstalk with native signaling and regulation2. To circumvent this challenge, other synthetic receptors signal in a manner that is orthogonal to (independent of) endogenous signaling. Key examples include synthetic Notch7,8 and synthetic intramembrane proteolysis9 receptors, programmable antigen-gated G-protein-coupled engineered receptors10 and the modular extracellular sensor architecture (MESA)11,12. Because orthogonal receptors are self-contained (that is, they embody the required ligand recognition and signal transduction mechanisms), they can be deployed across many cell types13. Orthogonal receptors may be multiplexed to implement information processing, such as conditioning output gene expression in the presence of some cues and absence of others14,15. These properties make orthogonal synthetic receptors particularly useful for implementing diverse engineered cellular functions.

Building synthetic receptors for new extracellular ligands remains challenging. First, subtle amino acid changes may adversely affect surface expression in unpredictable ways16,17. Receptor trafficking can be influenced by domain-specific post-translational modifications16. Design choices that boost surface expression may not be optimal for receptor performance17. Second, rationally designing receptor signaling mechanisms that couple ligand binding to subsequent signaling is nontrivial, even with powerful, emerging protein design tools. Choice of ligand-binding domain and the resulting ligand-binding complex size and affinity can strongly influence performance6,17–19.

Guided by these insights, we investigated a new approach—conversion of natural cytokine receptors into orthogonal, self-contained synthetic receptors using MESA, a class of synthetic receptors that can sense soluble ligands11,12,17,20. In MESA receptors, ligand-binding-induced dimerization drives intracellular reconstitution of a split protease and trans-cleavage to release a TF to drive target gene expression. MESA receptors can use chemically induced dimerization domains, antibody single-chain variable fragments or nanobodies as ligand-binding ectodomains (ECDs) to sense target ligands including small molecules and proteins. We hypothesized that natural receptor ECDs could also comprise MESA ligand-recognition domains. While other synthetic receptors have incorporated natural receptor ligand-binding domains using the aforementioned chimeric designs, this approach has generally not been extended to engineer orthogonal synthetic receptors21–23. One study implemented a mechanism similar to MESA to use receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) ECDs to achieve autocrine, internal ligand sensing, suggesting that this general approach could be feasible for sensing exogenous ligands24. Because there exist multiple options for tuning MESA performance, including split protease reconstitution propensity20 and TF properties25,26, MESA is well suited for exploring general strategies for integrating natural receptor ECDs into synthetic receptors.

In this study, we converted natural human cytokine receptors into orthogonal biosensors by pairing a natural receptor ECD (NatE) with MESA intracellular mechanisms to yield NatE MESA receptors. We investigated four natural receptor candidates and identified ligand-inducible NatE MESA receptor designs for three of the four, although performance varied substantially across receptors. We then synthesized learnings to propose design principles for extending this approach to co-opt other natural receptors. Lastly, we demonstrate the utility of NatE MESA receptors by using them to build therapeutically motivated sense-and-respond programs in T cells and logic gates that integrate multiple cues.

Results

Development of biosensors using human cytokine receptors

To begin engineering NatE MESA receptors, we first explored the general feasibility of natural receptor conversion. We selected a panel of human cytokine receptors representing various receptor classes and signaling mechanisms that—if successful—could be useful for cellular engineering. These receptors included human vascular endothelial growth factor receptors (VEGFR1 and VEGFR2)27,28, interleukin 10 receptors (IL-10Rα and IL-10Rβ)29–31, tumor necrosis factor receptors (TNFR1 and TNFR2)32,33 and transforming growth factor-β receptors (TGFβR1 and TGFβR2)34,35 (Supplementary Table 1). VEGF, IL-10 and TGFβ are upregulated in the tumor microenvironment (TME) and TNF and IL-10 have immunoregulatory roles, generally driving inflammatory and immunosuppressive states, respectively. Additional description is provided in subsequent sections and in Supplementary Notes 1 and 2 (for TNFR and TGFβR, respectively). These receptors include heteroassociative (different receptor chains associate) and homoassociative (like receptor chains associate) ligand-binding-induced signaling mechanisms involving two or more receptor chains, making them promising for coupling to MESA’s mechanism (Supplementary Fig. 1). We chose a validated set of MESA intracellular signaling domains, including the split tobacco etch virus protease (TEVp) mutant 75S;190K and a minimal zinc finger (ZF) domain-based synthetic TF (synTF)17,20,25. We included designs retaining the native transmembrane (TMD) and juxtamembrane (JMD) domains for each selected natural receptor because those domains influence surface expression, ligand-independent receptor chain association and ligand-induced signaling events36. We also evaluated designs that replace the native TMD and JMD with domains validated in prior MESA receptors, including a truncated murine cluster of differentiation 28 (CD28) TMD and a glycine–serine-rich intracellular linker20. Lastly, we used either the native signal sequence or commonly used variants derived from the human CD8a T cell receptor or the human immunoglobulin variable heavy chain (IgG VH)7,9,37. This directed survey of design space (Supplementary Fig. 2) evaluates general prospects for natural receptor conversion.

Conversion of VEGFR

We first investigated biosensors detecting VEGF. VEGF has a key role in neovascularization and its dysregulated activity is related to solid tumor growth and development38. VEGF is upregulated in many tumors and has been harnessed as a TME marker and therapeutic target39. VEGFR1 and VEGFR2 belong to the RTK family and can homodimerize or heterodimerize in the absence of VEGF but they do not signal without VEGF27,28,40–42. All VEGF isoforms exist mainly as homodimers43. Ligand binding to the receptor’s extracellular domains induces a conformational change, which leads to the activation and autophosphorylation of the intracellular kinase domains, activating downstream signaling (Fig. 1a)44. To investigate conversion of VEGF receptors to NatE MESA receptors, we first evaluated surface expression across various design choices (Supplementary Fig. 2). All variants expressed well and trafficked to the cell surface (Fig. 1b). We verified that full-length receptors were expressed by western blot (Supplementary Fig. 3a). VEGFR2-based receptors exhibited higher surface expression than VEGFR1-based receptors. Signal sequence and TMD choice did not meaningfully affect trafficking to the cell surface (Fig. 1b). These observations suggest that ECD choice is the primary determinant of receptor surface expression. Because all signal sequences comparably influenced expression and would not impact signaling (signal peptides are cleaved during translation and trafficking), native ECD signal sequences were carried forward.

We next evaluated candidate VEGF NatE MESA function. First, to quickly identify receptor configurations that are incapable of signaling, we coexpressed the ligand (in an autocrine fashion) with the receptor chains (Fig. 1c). This strategy maximizes receptor–ligand contacts within the secretory pathway and at the cell surface; failure to signal is strong evidence of nonfunction. We tested both the VEGFA165 isoform, which is most abundant and can be bound to the extracellular matrix, and the VEGFA121 isoform, which is exclusively soluble43. Multiple receptors exhibited ligand-inducible signaling, including both homodimeric and heterodimeric configurations (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3b). A high ligand-independent signal (that is, background) and resulting low fold induction were observed when both chains contained a CD28 TMD. This is consistent with previous observations with MESA receptors and CARs, as the CD28 TMD has a high propensity to aggregate17,45,46. Receptors in which the CTEVp chain contained a VEGFR2 ECD and TMD signaled only with VEGFA121. We speculate that this could be attributable to differences in binding geometry across the VEGFA isoforms, which could affect the interaction of intracellular MESA domains. We also tested and confirmed our assumption that changes in signal sequence would not affect receptor function, finding only variations in output magnitude that may be attributable to receptor expression level (Supplementary Fig. 3a,c). We subsequently focused on detecting VEGFA165, which is most relevant as a TME feature47–49. The most promising receptor configuration (VEGFR2 N-terminal component of split, mutant TEVp (NTEVp) and VEGFR1 C-terminal component of split, mutant TEVp (CTEVp)) signaled in a manner that increased with dose of the ligand-encoding plasmid (Fig. 1d). The decline in receptor output at the highest VEGFA165-encoding plasmid doses might be attributable to resource competition and/or saturation of ligand-binding sites with excess ligand (reducing receptor chain dimerization). Overall, these findings support the fundamental signaling competency of VEGF NatE MESA receptors.

We next evaluated whether VEGF NatE MESA can detect exogenous (external) VEGF. Some inducibility was observed but the magnitude of the response to exogenous ligand was modest compared to that induced by coexpressed ligand (Supplementary Fig. 3d). We hypothesized that the intracellular split TEVp could be tuned to improve performance. We constructed new receptors containing alternative split TEVp mutants that varied in reconstitution propensity (interfacial energies)20, focusing on mutants with reduced interfacial energies to promote split TEVp reconstitution. We first verified that the new split TEVp variants did not ablate surface expression (Supplementary Fig. 3e). We then tested receptor performance with either coexpressed or exogenous VEGF and identified a promising configuration including a wild-type (WT) NTEVp domain and 190K CTEVp domain (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 3f). Decreasing split TEVp interfacial energy generally increased signaling (both background and ligand-induced) and this generally reduced fold induction. Interestingly, choice of NTEVp mutant modulated the magnitude of reporter expression, whereas choice of CTEVp mutant modulated the inducibility. While additional tuning could potentially further improve performance, because these receptors already conferred substantial ligand inducibility (hereafter, ‘functional’), they were carried forward for further development.

Transient receptor expression is suitable for prototyping but stable genomic expression is desirable for applications; thus, we investigated how such deployment impacts receptor performance. We stably integrated VEGF NatE MESA receptor expression cassettes into HEK293FT cells using PiggyBac transposons. We evaluated two strategies; in the first, the whole system (inducible reporter, NTEVp receptor chain and CTEVp receptor chain) was contained in a single transposon and, in the second, the receptor expression cassettes were delivered by transposons into cells previously engineered with an inducible reporter in a safe harbor locus (Supplementary Fig. 3g). Stable expression of receptors improved sensing of exogenous ligand (Fig. 1f) compared to experiments in which receptors were expressed transiently (Supplementary Fig. 3d). Interestingly, the receptor that included the WT NTEVp domain, which exhibited higher background and induced signal when expressed transiently, exhibited the best performance when expressed stably. The ‘all-in-one’ transposon strategy yielded better overall performance (magnitude of background and induced signal) than the receptor-only transposon with a preintegrated reporter. We hypothesize that stable expression comprises lower copy numbers of both receptors and reporter genes; thus, performance is benefitted by choices that increase signaling. At low copies, the benefits of a high on state outweigh the costs of elevated background signaling. Similarly, the all-in-one transposon potentially inserts multiple copies of the reporter cassette, whereas the receptor-only system included a single, preintegrated copy of the reporter. Thus, the strategy used for receptor and reporter integrations impacts the performance of VEGF NatE MESA.

Conversion of IL-10R

We next explored biosensors for detecting IL-10. IL-10 dysregulation is implicated in many diseases50,51 and IL-10 is a pleiotropic molecule conferring immunosuppressive and immunostimulatory effects in different contexts52. Many cancers are associated with high local concentrations of IL-10 in the TME, including melanoma, ovarian cancer and several lymphomas53. IL-10 is a homodimeric ligand that signals through IL-10Rα and IL-10Rβ, which are class II cytokine receptors50. Signaling occurs when a pair of high-affinity IL-10Rα receptor chains bind to the homodimeric ligand, eliciting a conformational change that causes two low-affinity IL-10Rβ chains to bind and form a full heterotetrameric complex that signals through the Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription proteins pathway (Fig. 2a)29,31,54,55. Signaling is also induced by monomeric IL-10 mutants, suggesting that some signaling may be induced by a single α and β chain complex31,56. While IL-10Rα–IL-10Rβ complexes may preassemble in the absence of ligand to some extent30, addition of ligand induces an increase in homoassociation and heteroassociation of the two receptor chains56. We first evaluated expression across the full panel of design choices (Supplementary Fig. 2). All variants were highly expressed on the cell surface (Fig. 2b). Choice of signal sequence modestly affected surface expression and this was most pronounced for receptors with an IL-10Rβ ECD and an IL-10Rβ signal sequence. For all others, surface expression was similar across NTEVp and CTEVp receptors. All variants were expressed primarily as full-length receptor chains (Supplementary Fig. 4a).

We next evaluated biosensor function (Fig. 2c). We again used coexpressed ligand (human IL-10) for initial receptor profiling (Fig. 2a). Receptor configurations in which both chains used the same ECD demonstrated minimal inducibility. Background and ligand-induced signal were greatest for receptor configurations in which both ECDs were IL-10Rα, suggesting that these ECDs may mediate some preassociation. Receptor configurations pairing different ECDs (that is, IL-10Rα on one chain and IL-10Rβ on the other chain) demonstrated inducible signaling. The highest performing configuration (IL-10Rβ NTEVp and IL-10Rα CTEVp) demonstrated the greatest inducibility (fold induction) and reporter output; thus, we selected this configuration for further analyses. Signaling required both receptor chains (Supplementary Fig. 4b,c). Because we observed substantial differences in expression for IL-10Rβ chains as a function of signal sequences, we generated and evaluated variants of this promising receptor configuration incorporating alternative TMD and signal sequences (Supplementary Fig. 4d). All such receptor variants demonstrated ligand-inducible signaling and the magnitude of induced signal and fold induction could be increased by the choice of signal sequences on the IL-10Rβ NTEVp chain. We proceeded with the CD8a signal sequence on the IL-10Rβ NTEVp chain and the IL-10Rα signal sequence on the IL-10Rα CTEVp chain because the overall magnitude of signal was highest; we hypothesized that this property would be useful when implementing these receptors through genomic integration. As observed with the VEGF NatE MESA receptors, background signal was highest for receptors in which the CD28 TMD was included on both chains (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 4d); thus, we proceeded with the respective native TMD for the ECD on each chain.

We next evaluated whether the most promising IL-10 NatE MESA receptor could sense exogenous ligand. As with VEGF NatE MESA receptors, we initially observed minimal response to exogenous ligand when IL-10 NatE MESA receptors were expressed transiently (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 4e). Through post hoc analysis, we found that cells with the highest transgene expression (quantified using a coencoded fluorescent protein) exhibited the greatest levels of reporter induction (Supplementary Fig. 4e). Given the improvement in performance observed when VEGF NatE MESA receptors were expressed stably and the observed correlation between IL-10 NatE MESA receptor expression level and performance, we stably expressed these receptors using PiggyBac and Sleeping Beauty transposons (Supplementary Fig. 4f). Excitingly, we then observed strong inducibility with exogenous ligand across all designs tested. Output was greatest when the reporter was encoded in the same transposon as the receptors (consistent with VEGF receptors) (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 4g). Biosensor-expressing cells responded differentially to IL-10 concentrations down to 16 ng ml−1, with response saturating around 250 ng ml−1 (Fig. 2f). The biosensor-expressing cells also responded to paracrine IL-10 secreted by other cells (Supplementary Fig. 4h). Ligand-induced reporter expression was clearly detectable by fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 2g). Lastly, we explored how various strategies used to generate stable cells impact performance (Supplementary Note 3). Notably, fold induction was relatively conserved when expressing receptors transiently or stably, suggesting that our functional assays captured intrinsic performance characteristics of the receptors and that the IL-10 NatE MESA biosensor is robust to genetic context and expression level. Comparing dose–response curves (fitted to Hill equations), we found that, across genomic integration and sorting strategies, the half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) values were somewhat conserved and sorting the transposon-modified population for high expressers slightly decreased ultrasensitivity (Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Fig. 4r). Altogether, these findings validate the function and identify performance characteristics of this IL-10 NatE MESA receptor.

Construction of therapeutically motivated cellular functions

To evaluate the utility of NatE MESA receptors for building engineered cellular functions, we constructed a model testbed inspired by a function that could address an unmet need in cancer immunotherapy. In this proof of principle, we engineered T cells that sense IL-10 through a NatE MESA receptor and respond by activating pathways that enhance T cell cytotoxicity. First, we used the same PiggyBac transposons used in Fig. 2e,f to implement the biosensor, reporter and constitutive fluorescent protein marker in Jurkat T cells. The resulting cells sensed exogenous IL-10 with a detection threshold of 32 ng ml−1 (Fig. 3a,b). Mirroring observations in HEK293FT cells (Supplementary Note 3), sorting Jurkats for the subpopulation exhibiting greatest expression of the constitutive transposon marker led to higher background and induced signal across all doses of IL-10 and did not change fold induction but did lower the detection threshold (down to 2 ng ml−1 IL-10) compared to the unsorted parental population (Fig. 3c). The EC50 was similar in the presort and sorted populations. We next constructed and tested a circuit representing a candidate therapeutic modality—IL-10-induced expression of a second-generation pan-anti-ErbB CAR, T1E28z (ref. 57). We validated the circuit in HEK293FT cells; either coexpressed or exogenous IL-10 led to a substantial increase in CAR expression (Fig. 3d). When this circuit was implemented in Jurkats, CAR signaling was activated synergistically in the presence of IL-10 (recombinant or produced in trans) and antigen engagement (HER2 on cocultured SKOV3 cells) (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 5a,b). This demonstrates that NatE MESA receptors can be combined in series with other synthetic receptors (for example, CARs) to integrate detection of soluble and surface-bound cues. We also evaluated NatE MESA-driven expression of an engineered cytokine, membrane-bound IL-15 (mbIL-15), and observed increased mbIL-15 expression upon exposure to IL-10 (Supplementary Fig. 5c,d)58. These results illustrate two general strategies for using the IL-10 NatE MESA, which—if validated in preclinical models—could ultimately improve the specificity and safety of CAR T cell therapies by restricting expression of receptors and cytokines59 to sites enriched in IL-10.

An attractive feature of engineered orthogonal receptors is that output can also comprise natural TFs that regulate multiple endogenous genes. As a motivating application, we selected using natural TFs to boost T cell activation and prevent exhaustion to improve CAR T cell therapies60,61. Multiple TFs have been identified for such uses, including cJun, which confers resistance to exhaustion and increased production of IL-2 (refs. 60,62), basic leucine zipper TF ATF-like (BATF), which confers resistance to exhaustion and promotes CD8+ T cell differentiation into effector T cells63–66, and T-box expressed in T cells (Tbet), which increases the proinflammatory antitumor response and promotes CD4+ T cell differentiation into a T helper 1 phenotype67,68. Overexpressing these TFs constitutively carries oncogenic risk (for cJun, in particular69) and restricting their activity to the TME could improve safety. We evaluated whether IL-10 NatE MESA receptors could be used to sequester and release these native TFs in a TME-induced manner. We replaced the synTF with each natural TF on receptors and confirmed surface and whole-cell expression (Supplementary Fig. 5e,f). Surface expression generally decreased as TF size increased. We next developed and validated a synthetic reporter for each TF by engineering binding site arrays with a YB_TATA minimal promoter driving DsRed-Express2 (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 5g). Reporters were inducible by coexpressed TFs and background (leak) varied by construct; HEK293FT cells express basal levels of cJun and BATF but not Tbet and some reporters are subject to crosstalk with other expressed TFs (https://v23.proteinatlas.org/)70. Reporter expression increased with coexpression of IL-10 for receptors with BATF, Tbet and cJun with a cJun dimer-specific reporter (Fig. 3f). Reporter expression decreased with coexpression of IL-10 for the receptor with cJun and an activator protein 1 (AP1)-inducible reporter, which is responsive to other AP1 family TFs, and this result was consistent with constitutive overexpression of free cJun (Supplementary Fig. 5g). We hypothesize that overexpressed cJun interacts with the AP1 proteins (cJun and cFos) basally expressed in HEK293FT cells, shifting the distribution of functional dimeric TFs to more cJun homodimers than cJun–cFos heterodimers, which bind to the AP1 response element more efficiently than cJun homodimers71. When implemented stably in Jurkats using PiggyBac transposons, modest ligand-induced increases in reporter expression were observed for cJun and BATF but not Tbet constructs (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 5h–j), potentially because of lower receptor and reporter expression in Jurkats (compared to HEK293FT cells) and transcriptional silencing72. These proof-of-principle results demonstrate that NatE MESA receptors can regulate gene expression through the release of native TFs. Whether release of natural TFs from NatE MESA receptors provide adequate signal magnitude to confer meaningful functional effects should be evaluated in an application-specific context; opportunities for further improvement include the addition of circuit layers to amplify receptor output26, mimicking strategies used in nature.

Logical integration of multiple TME cues

Engineering cell-based therapies to integrate multiple inputs enables new functions. These include recognizing patterns of TME signatures to increase safety by reducing on-target, off-tumor toxicity14,15,73. Other synthetic receptors can perform logical evaluation of several inputs14,15,73 and receptors using synTF output are particularly well suited for this purpose; however, such evaluation has not yet been implemented for soluble ligands such as those found in the TME. We evaluated whether the VEGF and IL-10 NatE MESA receptors could be multiplexed to perform logical evaluation of cytokine inputs. Multiplexing an early generation of MESA receptors did not yield synergistic output74 and subsequent work suggested that this is attributable to both receptor and synthetic promoter characteristics2. We hypothesized that combining NatE MESA receptors with a minimal ZF domain-based synTF and cognate promoter25 could confer robust logical evaluation of inputs. We selected two promising receptor chain pairs for each NatE MESA receptor: for VEGF, the 190K CTEVp chain and both the 75S and the WT NTEVp chains with native signal sequences and TMDs; for IL-10, the 190K CTEVp chain with the native signal sequence and TMD and the 75S NTEVp chains with the human CD8a and IgG VH signal sequences and native TMDs.

We first probed the cross-reactivity of VEGF and IL-10 NatE MESA receptors. Most chains showed no cross-reactivity but the VEGFR NTEVp chains showed some activity when paired with the IL-10R CTEVp chain and this was most pronounced for the VEGFR WT NTEVp (Supplementary Fig. 6a). This cross-reactivity was reduced in the presence of ligand, potentially indicating that the association between matched receptor chain pairs bound to ligand could sequester chains to inhibit transient interactions between mismatched receptor chain pairs. Because cross-reactivity between most pairs was minimal, we concluded that VEGF and IL-10 NatE MESA receptors are likely amenable to multiplexing. We chose to evaluate each subsequent logical function with all selected VEGF and IL-10 NatE MESA receptor chain variants, including the VEGFR WT NTEVp chain, to evaluate how receptor pairs with varying degrees of orthogonality versus cross-reactivity can be used for multiplexing.

We first built an IL-10–VEGF OR gate—a circuit producing output when either (or both) of the inputs is present and no response when neither is present (Fig. 4a). To implement OR logic, both receptors were designed to release the same synTF and regulate the same output promoter. As desired, reporter expression was induced by either or both ligands (Fig. 4b). The magnitude of reporter expression depended on the ligand, with IL-10 inducing greater output. This trend is consistent with observations of the individual NatE MESA receptors. Choice of IL-10R pair did not affect the function of the OR gate, whereas choice of VEGFR pair affected the magnitudes of the no-ligand and VEGF-only conditions (Supplementary Fig. 6b). As observed previously (Supplementary Fig. 3f), VEGFR including an NTEVp domain with greater propensity to reconstitute the split TEVp increased the total signal without affecting reporter fold induction. We next built an IL-10–VEGF AND gate, defined as exhibiting synergy, where the output when both inputs are present is larger than the sum of the outputs in response to each individual input. In our first design, each receptor releases a different synTF that binds to a hybrid promoter containing binding sites for both synTFs (Fig. 4c)74. We designed several promoter architectures on the basis of previous work25, genomically integrated them into an HEK293FT landing pad and evaluated synergistic activation using synTFs (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Although all designs exhibited synergy, we moved forward with designs that exhibited both high synergy and high reporter output. To evaluate the contributions of each receptor to performance, we created multigene expression vectors (MGEVs) for each receptor, including each chain driven by a separate constitutive promoter and a constitutive fluorescent protein (Supplementary Fig. 6d). MGEVs were evaluated by the transfection of reporter cell lines. All configurations of receptors, synTFs and cognate promoters yielded synergistic output (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 6e). In general, promoter P4 exhibited greater synergy than P2. We hypothesize that this is because of the mechanism by which synTFs cooperatively recruit transcriptional machinery25; because P2 has more synTF-binding sites than P4, each input (and corresponding synTF) can induce more transcriptional activation. This phenomenon is most pronounced for the receptor that releases the ZF6 synTF, for which binding sites are closer to the minimal promoter. Interestingly, variations upon receptor design had little effect upon AND gate performance, which was mostly driven by synTF choice and promoter design (Supplementary Fig. 6e). Altogether, NatE MESA can implement AND logic and tuning at the level of synTF and promoter design rather than receptor design was most impactful.

We next investigated an alternative AND gate strategy to reduce output in response to single ligands (and improve synergy). We used split inteins to separate a synTF into its activation and DNA-binding domains26, such that each receptor releases a different synTF (ZF1 or ZF6), which then induces the expression of one half of the split-synTF (Fig. 4e). We engineered a reporter cell line incorporating the intermediate synTF-responsive promoters driving expression of the split-synTF halves and validated its function (Supplementary Fig. 6f). In functional tests, this circuit design exhibited synergy in some cases, albeit generally less than observed for the hybrid promoter AND gate (Fig. 4f). Moving to a split-synTF design successfully reduced output for the IL-10-only condition but not for the VEGF-only condition. We hypothesize that the higher ligand-independent background signal generally conferred by IL-10 NatE MESA (compared to VEGF NatE MESA) causes some of the associated split-synTF component to always be available to complement the half released from the VEGF NatE MESA. Thus, reducing background from the IL-10 NatE MESA receptors could increase AND gate synergy. An additional consideration is that the promoter controlling the reporter is driven by a single synTF, such that even a small amount of reconstituted synTF can bind near the minimal promoter and cooperatively activate transcription, whereas, in the case of the hybrid promoter in Fig. 4d, any one synTF is not capable of such cooperative activation. We again observed that variations upon receptor design had little effect upon AND gate performance compared to downstream circuit design choices (Supplementary Fig. 6g). These results underscore that level matching between receptor output levels (both background and induced signal) is tunable and important for circuit function74. These insights can guide the design and optimization of NatE MESA circuits for diverse applications.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the conversion of natural receptors into synthetic orthogonal biosensors that sense and respond to soluble extracellular cues. Notably, not all receptor conversions were straightforward or yielded functional biosensors at all. By focusing on receptors with well-understood biophysical mechanisms, we can interpret these outcomes aided by prior knowledge. Conversion success was driven primarily by compatibility between natural receptor and MESA mechanisms. Heteroassociative receptors were most successfully converted; MESA signaling is regulated by the association of two different receptor chains. Ligand-independent association of receptor chains with the same ECD was undesired and generally led to high background signaling (for example, IL-10Rα, TGFβR1 and TNFR1). However, for receptors that preassociate and then undergo a conformational change upon ligand binding, preassociation may be tolerated or favorable, such as the VEGFRs, where ligand-independent association did not result in ligand-independent signaling. We hypothesize that the same mechanism that prevents VEGFR kinase domain autophosphorylation in the absence of ligand42,75 also prevents MESA intracellular interactions, which are then enabled by the conformational change induced by ligand binding. In general, exploring alternative N-terminal signal peptide sequences sometimes improved receptor expression and trafficking, in turn increasing signaling, but no change in signal peptide rescued noninducible receptors. TMD choice, on the other hand, modulated receptor behavior. The best-performing NatE MESA receptors generally contained natural TMDs. Other TMD choices seemingly produced chain associations not observed in nature; by using a CD28 TMD in TNF NatE MESA, a low-affinity receptor variant was able to produce an inducible signal (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 7)32. Other design choices generally modulated induced signal magnitude without much effect on fold inducibility. While any one receptor may greatly benefit from application-specific optimization, our observations suggest that exploring downstream circuits to amplify or process outputs from a functional receptor can be a promising avenue for improving overall performance26.

Although the VEGFR and IL-10R conversions were most deeply characterized here, we also evaluated the conversion of TGFβR and TNFR, which were less successful but yielded useful lessons (Supplementary Notes 1 and 2 and Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). The TGFβ NatE MESA exhibited low expression, even with alternative signal sequences and extended JMDs, and no tested configuration yielded inducible signaling. The TNF NatE MESA produced minimally inducible biosensors when chains contained a CD28 TMD or split TEVp variants with high reconstitution propensity. Improvement of TNF NatE MESA into a higher-performing biosensor may require tuning the interactions between intracellular domains (potentially by introducing rotation by adding amino acids6 or other intracellular linker variations) to bring parts together in a geometry compatible with split TEVp reconstitution. Alternatively, improvements observed with the more easily reconstituted split TEVp variants (Supplementary Fig. 7) would likely require introduction of a mechanism to reduce background signal. These examples highlight the importance of achieving high expression (TGFβ) and exploring alternative receptor design choices (TNF). With the advancement of computational tools for predicting structures of chimeric proteins, it may be possible to gain insights into the structural basis of such challenging receptor conversions and identify opportunities for improvement76. We summarize the experience gleaned through this study in Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Note 4, which can guide future synthetic receptor engineering.

The NatE MESA receptor characterizations in this study add new knowledge that complements prior efforts to engineer orthogonal synthetic receptors. For example, Baeumler et al. used VEGFR ECDs to transduce VEGF binding into TF release, validating this fundamental concept, but that initial exploration did not validate responsiveness to exogenous VEGF or explore receptor design variations that could be required to achieve such functionality24. Here, we systematically and comparatively evaluate synthetic receptor conversion across a range of receptor families, providing insights into how performance is driven by design choices including receptor configuration (for example, homotypic versus heterotypic ECD pairings), genomic integration method and deployment across multiple cell types. The success in converting IL-10 NatE MESA and failure with TGFβ NatE MESA teaches that even natural receptors with similar mechanisms have nuances that may yield different outcomes in a synthetic receptor context. A key learning is that beginning by evaluating receptor expression and sensing autocrine ligands can identify whether the conversion is likely to yield inducible (VEGF and IL-10), minimally inducible (TNF) or noninducible (TGFβ) synthetic receptors. While efforts to tune and optimize receptor performance were beneficial for the successful conversions, such work minimally improved the less successful conversions. These insights can inform the design of future screen-based and iterative approaches to receptor engineering. Lastly, NatE MESA performance is robust to variations in receptor expression level, obviating the need for fine-tuning receptor expression stoichiometry as was needed for first-generation MESA12. This property makes NatE MESA user-friendly and portable across applications and implementation methods.

We also validated NatE MESA utility for constructing useful genetic programs through a series of proof-of-principle demonstrations. We showed that mammalian cells can be engineered to integrate the sensing of multiple soluble, environmental protein ligands using multiplexed synthetic receptors. We also built genetic circuits to process these inputs by implementing Boolean logic (a framing that aligns with a clinical understanding of microenvironmental features) and rewiring transcriptional output to yield immunotherapy-relevant activities. NatE MESA receptors can be multiplexed in parallel (by integrating synTF outputs using logic) and in series with other synthetic receptors (such as CARs). Using small, three-ZF synTFs in NatE MESA yields a compact genetic design and confers both high induced and low background signal because these synTFs bind together closely and exhibit transcriptional cooperativity25. These parts also enable signal integration and/or Boolean logic because promoters responsive to multiple small ZFs are rather simple to design and tune, especially compared to classical Gal4 and tTA synTFs74. Learnings from previous work74, the aforementioned fact that NatE MESA receptors are robust to variations in stoichiometry compared to earlier designs12 and the availability of customizable toolkits of transcription parts25 enabled us to achieve AND gate behavior without extensive tuning. These demonstrations exemplify a general trend—using synthetic receptors to achieve cellular engineering goals is enabled by integration with technologies for downstream information processing2.

We identified opportunities for future development of NatE MESA for translational applications. Firstly, it will be important to evaluate whether the robust performance observed in immortalized cell lines (Supplementary Notes 3) will hold across additional cell types (for example, primary cells) and delivery modalities. Secondly, although NatE MESA ECDs are derived from human cells, which could minimize immunogenicity, this requires evaluation77. Complementary ongoing work includes creating orthogonal human proteases78 and humanization of exogenous sequences through protein engineering79. Evaluating immunogenicity is nontrivial and may be best addressed within a given application. Thirdly, NatE MESA receptors can be used to implement illustrative, proof-of-principle circuits that could ultimately support and enhance CAR T cell function. Future avenues for investigation include implementing NatE MESA-CAR circuits in primary T cells and evaluating efficacy in vitro and in preclinical animal models to identify and, if needed, address needs for increasing and shifting bioactivity. For example, if a given dose of cytokine induces an inadequate level of CAR expression to achieve cytotoxicity, such circuit output can be increased through promoter engineering25 and/or genetic amplification26. Fourthly, although NatE MESA receptors signal through orthogonal pathways, there remains potential for interaction with the native receptors from which NatE MESAs are derived. Potential solutions include a careful matching of NatE MESA and cell type to avoid such problems or systematically mutating NatE MESA domains to prevent interactions with endogenous receptors but maintain interactions with MESA receptor partners. Fifthly, managing transcriptional silencing is a general challenge for engineered cell therapies72. We evaluated how silencing may impact the performance of NatE MESA receptors (Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Fig. 10). Similar to prior studies72, subclones with higher basal transgene expression showed more resistance to silencing, preserving reporter expression magnitude and inducibility over time. The genomic integration strategy also impacted NatE MESA performance; integration locus and copy number are both important considerations. Lastly, although the NatE MESA receptors developed here transduce ligand sensing into changes in gene expression, split TEVp could be replaced with other split enzymes20 to transduce ligand sensing into other enzymatic activity. Altogether, this NatE MESA receptor conversion study may inform the development of novel, tunable biosensors for programming cellular responses to diverse physiological targets of interest.

Cloning MESA receptors

In most cases, MESA receptors were first cloned into polytransfection backbones, which are modified versions of pcDNA, to confer high expression in HEK293FT cells and include a secondary transcriptional unit encoding constitutive expression of a fluorescent protein95. These plasmid backbones are derived from a modified version of the pcDNA3.1/Hygro(+) mammalian expression vector (Thermo Fisher, V87020), which was modified by our laboratory in previous work (pPD005; Addgene, 138749)25 and further modified here to change the antibiotic resistance marker from ampicillin to kanamycin and to add the additional expression cassette containing a cytomegalovirus promoter, fluorescent protein gene and polyadenylation sequence. Receptors with the CTEVp domain were cloned into a polytransfection backbone with a constitutively expressed mTagBFP2 gene, while receptors with the NTEVp domain were cloned into a polytransfection backbone with a constitutively expressed mNeonGreen gene using a restriction-enzyme-based approach. Selected receptor constructs were moved into TUPV backbones by restriction-enzyme-based cloning to facilitate transposon vector assembly using mammalian modular cloning (mMoClo)83. In some cases, receptors were assembled directly into TUPV backbones.

Cloning ligands for coexpression and secretion

Ligands were cloned into a pcDNA backbone to confer high expression in HEK293FT cells (Addgene, 138749)25. PCR was used to amplify the coding region of each ligand and append a secretion signal sequence if necessary and restriction-enzyme-based cloning was used to insert the products into pcDNA. Selected ligands were moved into a second-generation pGIPZ lentiviral vector by restriction-enzyme-based cloning to enable generation of stable ligand-secreting HEK293FT and SKOV3 cell lines.

Cloning reporters

Golden Gate assembly was used to construct all synTF-responsive reporters in a TUPV backbone to facilitate assembly into transposon or landing pad integration vectors96. The specific TUPV backbone used for Golden Gate reporter assembly (pGGB022) includes a pair of BsaI restriction sites upstream of a YB_TATA minimal promoter and a DsRedExpress-2 reporter gene97. Promoter inserts containing TF-binding sites were synthesized as 15–100-bp oligonucleotides by Integrated DNA Technologies or Life Technologies (Thermo Fisher). The coding and reverse strands were synthesized separately and designed to anneal, resulting in double-stranded DNA with a 4-nt sticky end overhang on each side. The coding and reverse oligonucleotides were mixed (6 µl of H2O, 1 µl of T4 ligase buffer, 1 µl of T4 PNK (10 U per µl; NEB) and 1 µl of each 100 µM oligonucleotide) and phosphorylated at 37 °C for 1 h. They were then denatured at 95 °C for 5 min and cooled slowly to room temperature (approximately 22 °C) to allow for annealing. The mix was then diluted 500-fold to make a 20 nM stock and included in the Golden Gate reaction. Golden Gate reaction mixtures comprised 1 µl of T4 ligase buffer, 1 µl of 10× BSA (1 mg ml−1), 0.5 µl of BsaI-HF (20 U per µl; NEB), 0.5 µl of T4 ligase (400 U per µl; NEB), 10 fmol of vector, 1 µl of each insert (diluted to 20 nM) and water to a total volume of 10 µl. The reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, 55 °C for 15 min and 80 °C for 20 min and then cooled to room temperature. Then, 3 µl of the reaction was immediately transformed into 50 µl of chemically competent TOP10 E. coli.

Natural TF-responsive promoters were constructed using restriction-enzyme-based assembly. Promoters were synthesized using the same oligo annealing approach described for synTF-responsive promoters. These inserts were diluted 1:500 to generate a 20 nM stock. The backbone used was a TUPV plasmid containing a different array of binding sites, a YB_TATA minimal promoter and a DsRedExpress-2 reporter gene. The backbone was cut upstream of the binding site array using BglII (NEB) and in the YB_TATA minimal promoter using SpeI-HF (NEB). The natural TF-binding site array inserts were designed to match these overhangs and replace the part of the YB_TATA minimal promoter that was removed when linearizing the backbone. The ligation reactions comprised 2 µl of T4 ligase buffer, 1 µl of T4 ligase (400 U per µl; NEB), 1 µl of backbone (typically around 50 ng), 3 µl of each insert (diluted to 20 nM) and water to a total volume of 20 µl. The specific binding sites used for each natural TF were as follows: cJun, reporter 1 AP1-binding site TGAGTCA and reporter 2 cJun dimer-binding site TTACCTCA98,99; BATF, BATF/IRF-binding site GAAATGAGTCA100; Tbet, palindromic Tbet-binding site AATTTCACACCTAGGTGTGAAATT101; nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT; NFAT-binding site ACGCCTTCTGTATGAAACAGTTTTTCCTCC)102.

Cloning transposon vectors and landing pad integration vectors

Both transposon vectors (for genomic integration of receptors and sometimes reporters) and landing pad integration vectors (for genomic integration of reporters) were assembled through a BbsI-mediated Golden Gate reaction based on the previously published mMoClo system83. Each 20-μl reaction comprised 2 µl of 10× T4 ligase buffer, 2 µl of 10× BSA (1 mg ml−1 stock), 0.8 µl of BbsI-HF (NEB), 0.8 µl of T4 DNA ligase (400 U per µl stock), 20 fmol of integration vector backbone and 40 fmol of each transcription unit and linker plasmid to be inserted. The backbone for PiggyBac transposon vectors was pHIE426, for Sleeping Beauty transposon vectors was pHIE430 and for landing pad integration vectors was pPD1178. Transposon vector assemblies typically included transcriptional units encoding receptors, constitutive fluorescent markers, antibiotic selection markers and sometimes a fluorescent reporter regulated by a synTF-responsive promoter. Landing pad integration vector assemblies typically included transcriptional unit(s) encoding fluorescent reporters regulated by synTF-responsive promoters, constitutive fluorescent markers and antibiotic selection markers. The Golden Gate reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min and then subjected to 55 iterations of thermocycling (37 °C for 5 min, 16 °C for 3 min, repeat), followed by 37 °C for 15 min, 50 °C for 5 min and 80 °C for 10 min to terminate the reactions. Next, the mixture was cooled to room temperature and placed on ice before immediate transformation into NEB Stable E. coli. Because all three backbones used here contain the crt operon, colonies that were transformed with unmodified backbone appeared red after 24 h of growth and were discarded. Additional information on landing pad integration vector design is provided in Supplementary Fig. 11.

Plasmid preparation

In most cases, TOP10 or NEB Stable E. coli cells were grown overnight in 100 ml of Luria–Bertani (LB) medium with the appropriate selective antibiotic. The following morning, cells were pelleted at 3,000g for 10 min and then resuspended in 4 ml of a solution of 25 mM Tris pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA and 15% sucrose. Cells were lysed for 15 min by the addition of 8 ml of a solution of 0.2 M NaOH and 1% SDS, followed by neutralization with 5 ml of 3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2). The precipitate was pelleted by centrifugation at 9,000g for 20 min. The supernatant was decanted and treated with 3 µl of RNAse A (Thermo Fisher) for 1 h at 37 °C. Then, 5 ml of phenol chloroform was added and the solution was mixed and centrifuged at 7,500g for 20 min. The aqueous layer was removed and subjected to another round of phenol chloroform extraction with 7 ml of phenol chloroform. The aqueous layer was then subjected to an isopropanol precipitation (41% final volume isopropanol, 10 min at room temperature, 9,000g for 20 min) and the pellet was briefly dried and resuspended in 420 µl of water. The DNA mixture was incubated on ice for at least 12 h in a solution of 6.5% PEG 20,000 and 0.4 M NaCl (final volume: 1 ml). DNA was precipitated with centrifugation at maximum speed for 20 min. The pellet was washed once with ethanol, dried for several hours at 37 °C and resuspended for several hours in TE buffer (10 mM Tris and 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0). DNA purity and concentration were confirmed using a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher). In some cases, TOP10 or NEB Stable E. coli cells were grown overnight in 50–100 mL of LB with the appropriate selective antibiotic and DNA was prepped using a ZymoPURE II plasmid midiprep kit (Zymo, D4201) by following the manufacturer’s instructions. In a given experiment, all receptor variants being compared were prepped using the same method. For some transposon vectors and reporter landing pad integration vectors, 10 ml of NEB Stable E. coli cells were grown overnight in LB with the appropriate selective antibiotic and DNA was prepped using a ZymoPURE plasmid miniprep kit (Zymo, D4210) by following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transient cotransfection of HEK293FT cells

Transient cotransfection of HEK293FT cells was conducted using the calcium phosphate method. Cells were plated at a minimum density of 1.0 × 105 cells per well in a 24-well plate in 0.5 ml of DMEM, supplemented as described above. For western blot and surface staining experiments, cells were plated at a minimum density of 2.0 × 105 cells per well in a 12-well plate in 1 ml of DMEM. After about 24 h, by which time the cells had adhered to the plate and grown to about 50% confluency in the well, the cells were transfected. Plasmids (up to 500 ng of DNA for 24-well plates and up to 1,000 ng of DNA for 12-well plates) were mixed in H2O and 2 M CaCl2 was added to a final concentration of 0.3 M CaCl2. This mixture was added dropwise to an equal-volume solution of 2× HEPES-buffered saline (280 mM NaCl, 0.05 M HEPES and 1.5 mM Na2HPO4) and gently pipetted up and down four times. After 4 min, the solution was mixed vigorously by pipetting ten times. Next, 100 µl of this mixture was added dropwise to the plated cells in 24-well plates (or 200 µl to 12-well plates) and the plates were gently swirled. All receptor plasmid masses were calculated by normalizing to a copy number of 4.22 × 109 for 24-well plates and 8.44 × 109 for 12-well plates, approximately 35–40 ng per 24-well and 70–80 ng per 12-well plate. These numbers were determined empirically from prior experiments involving rapamycin-sensing receptors17 and the plasmid masses scaled with well size. For conditions that received coexpressed ligands, 20 ng of ligand-expressing plasmid was included per 24-well plate. The total mass of all samples in an experiment was held constant by supplementing with empty vector filler DNA (pHIE298). A complete set of plasmid masses used in this study can be found in Supplementary Data 3. The next morning, the medium was aspirated and replaced with fresh medium.

Transient polytransfection of HEK293FT cells

Transient polytransfection of HEK293FT cells was conducted using the calcium phosphate method. Cells were plated at a minimum density of 5.05 × 105 cells per well in a six-well plate in 2 ml of DMEM, supplemented as described above. After about 24 h, the cells were transfected. Complexes for the two receptor-encoding plasmids being transfected were complexed and added to cells separately. Plasmids (up to 1 µg of DNA per mix for two mixes per well) were mixed in H2O, and 2 M CaCl2 was added to a final concentration of 0.3 M CaCl2. This mixture was added dropwise to an equal-volume solution of 2× HEPES-buffered saline (280 mM NaCl, 0.05 M HEPES and 1.5 mM Na2HPO4) and gently pipetted up and down four times. After 4 min, the solutions were mixed vigorously by pipetting ten times. Next, 200 µl of each mixture (to make 400 μl of total mixture) was added dropwise to the plated cells sequentially and the plates were gently swirled. All receptor plasmid masses were calculated by normalizing to a copy number of 16.88 × 109 for six-well plates, approximately 140–160 ng per six-well plate. These numbers were determined empirically from prior experiments involving rapamycin-sensing receptors17 and the plasmid masses scaled with well size. The total mass of all samples in an experiment was held constant by supplementing with empty vector filler DNA (pHIE298). A complete set of plasmid masses used in this study can be found in Supplementary Data 3. The next morning, the medium was aspirated and replaced with fresh medium. An overview of data collection and analysis strategy for polytransfected samples can be found in Supplementary Fig. 12.

Signaling assays with coexpressed ligand

Cells were transfected as described in the section on transient cotransfection of HEK293FT cells. The morning after transfection, the medium was aspirated and replaced with fresh medium. Time course microscopy signaling assays with exogenous ligand

For time course microscopy experiments, cells were plated at a density of 0.25 × 105 cells per well in 48-well plates. The next morning, IL-10 was diluted in serum-free (incomplete) DMEM and added to the side of each well to make the final concentration 250 ng ml−1. For untreated wells, an equivalent volume of serum-free DMEM was added. Plates were immediately placed in the microscope (Keyence BZ-X800E), well positions were chosen using the bright-field channel and the time course was started. Plates were held in a stage-top incubator (TOKAI HIT, INU-KIW-F1) to maintain humidity, a temperature of 37 °C and CO2 concentration of 5%. Images were taken every 2 h for 48 h. A BZ-X GFP filter (excitation, 470/40 nm; emission, 525/50 nm; dichroic, 495 nm) was used to measure mNeonGreen fluorescence and a BZ-X Texas red filter (excitation, 560/40 nm; emission, 630/75 nm; dichroic, 585 nm) was used to measure DsRed-Express2 reporter fluorescence. The following exposure times were used: 1/5 s for mNeonGreen, 1/40 s for DsRed-Express2 and 1/7,500 s for bright-field imaging using 25% transmitted light with oblique illumination. Images were acquired using BZ Series Application software version 01.01.00.17 and a PlanApo ×4 objective with a numerical aperture of 0.2. Images were not modified or corrected in any way. Images were processed using custom software in MATLAB (R2020b 9.9.0.1467703) to extract the fluorescence in the green channel per pixel, discard nongreen pixels and extract the fluorescence in the red channel for green pixels only (Supplementary Software 1). We then calculated the average DsRed-Express2 fluorescence across the selected pixels for each field of view at each time point.

CAR-mediated NFAT signaling assays

To assess CAR expression and activity in Jurkat cells, cell lines modified with various CAR-expressing circuits were cocultured with target cancer cells and/or IL-10. Target cells used here were SKOV3 cells, which express the CAR T1E28z target antigen HER2 (ref. 57). Either unmodified SKOV3 cells or IL-10-secreting SKOV3 cells (cells transduced with lentiviral vector pHIE730 encoding constitutive secretion of human IL-10 and puromycin resistance and selected with 1 μg ml−1 puromycin) were plated at a density of 3 × 104 cells per well of a 48-well plate in 300 μl of complete DMEM and left to adhere overnight. About 20 h later, 100 μl of medium was removed from each well to leave about 150 μl of remaining medium per well. Then, 150 μl of complete RPMI containing Jurkat cell lines at a density of 2 × 105 cells per ml was added such that the final Jurkat cell concentration in each well was 1 × 105 cells per ml. The Jurkat cell suspensions also contained either 500 ng ml−1 IL-10 (such that the final concentration per well was 250 ng ml−1 IL-10) or an equivalent volume of vehicle (water). Conditions without SKOV3 cells were prepared by adding 150 μl of complete DMEM to empty 48-well plates and then supplementing with the same Jurkat ± IL-10 suspensions added to SKOV3-containing wells. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for approximately 48 h before harvesting for flow. To harvest Jurkat cells and minimize harvested SKOV3 cells, the full volume of medium in each well was pipetted gently 4–5 times and added to 1 ml of FACS buffer containing 3 μM DAPI (PBS pH 7.4, 2–5 mM EDTA, 0.1% BSA and 3 μM DAPI (Thermo Scientific, 62247)). Cells were spun at 125g for 5 min, the supernatant was decanted and fresh FACS buffer was added before flow analysis. Generally, the larger size of SKOV3 cells compared to Jurkats enabled distinction of the two cell types by forward scatter (FSC) versus side scatter (SSC). For engineered Jurkat cell lines that express a constitutive mNeonGreen fluorescent protein, only mNeonGreen+ engineered Jurkat cells were evaluated, adding an additional gating layer to exclude any SKOV3 cells harvested.

Analytical flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was run on a BD LSR Fortessa special order research product (Robert H. Lurie Cancer Center Flow Cytometry Core). Lasers and filter sets used for data acquisition are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Approximately 3,000–10,000 single, transfected cells were analyzed per sample in transfection experiments. Transfected cells were identified using a separate, single transfection control fluorescent protein or multiple fluorescent proteins encoded on receptor plasmids (for example, mNeonGreen and mTagBFP2). In cases where the transfected cells were landing pad engineered cells, a constitutive miRFP720 gene was included in landing pad cargo such that cells were first identified by miRFP720 expression before setting transfection gates. In cases where the cells were engineered with transposons, a constitutive mNeonGreen gene was included in the transposon such that engineered cells were identified by mNeonGreen fluorescence.

Samples were analyzed using FlowJo version 10 software. Fluorescence data were compensated for spectral bleedthrough. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 13a, the HEK293FT cell population was identified by SSC-A versus FSC-A gating and singlets were identified by FSC-A versus FSC-H gating. To distinguish transfected from nontransfected cells, a control sample of cells was generated by transfecting cells with a mass of pcDNA (empty vector) equivalent to the mass of DNA used in other samples in the experiment. For the single-cell subpopulation of the empty-vector-only sample, a gate was made to identify cells that were positive for the constitutive fluorescent proteins used as a transfection control in other samples, such that the gate included no more than 1% of the nonfluorescent cells (Supplementary Fig. 13b). A similar approach was used to identify genomically modified HEK293FT cells expressing miRFP720 or mNeonGreen. The Jurkat cell population was identified by SSC-A versus FSC-A gating and singlets were identified by FSC-A versus FSC-H gating. Live cells were identified by inclusion of a DAPI viability stain such that DAPI+ (dead) cells were excluded from analysis. Genomically engineered Jurkats were identified by mNeonGreen expression by drawing a gate on unmodified Jurkats such that the gate included no more than 1% of the nonfluorescent cells.

To generate HEK293FT cell lines, from exponentially growing cells, 5 × 104 cells were plated per well (0.5 ml of medium) in 24-well format and cells were cultured for 24 h to allow cells to attach and spread. Next, 50 μl of this transfection mix was added dropwise to each well of cells and mixed by gentle swirling. Cells were cultured until the well was ready to split (typically 3 days), without any medium changes.

To begin selection of HEK293FT cells that successfully integrated the transposon vector, cells were harvested from the 24-well plate when confluent by trypsinizing and transferring to a single well of a 12-well plate in 1 ml of medium supplemented with 1 μg ml−1 puromycin (Invivogen, ant-pr) or 200 μg ml−1 hygromycin (Millipore, 400053) depending on the vector. Cells were cultured in medium supplemented with puromycin through exponential expansion until reaching a confluent 10-cm dish, upon which cells were frozen. Drug selection was maintained when culturing these cells but not during experiments.

For HEK293FT sorting experiments, cells were harvested by trypsinizing, resuspended at approximately 107 cells per ml in presort medium (DMEM with 10% FBS, 25 mM HEPES (Sigma H3375) and 100 µg ml−1 gentamycin (Amresco, 0304)) and held on ice until sorting was performed. The second sorting strategy required an additional gate for exclusion of reporter (DsRed-Express2)-positive cells to isolate low-background cells and the same octile sorting strategy was applied to this mNeonGreen+ DsRed-Express2− population. The top four mNeonGreen octiles were sorted. A total of 50,000 cells were collected in postsort medium (DMEM with 20% FBS, 25 mM HEPES and 100 μg ml−1 gentamycin); cells were held on ice until they could be centrifuged at 150g for 5 min, resuspended in 0.5 ml of complete medium supplemented with 100 µg ml−1 gentamycin and plated in one well of a 24-well plate. Cells were cultured in medium supplemented with puromycin until the six-well plate was confluent and cells were exponentially growing. Cells were then selected with 6 μg ml−1 blasticidin (Alfa Aesar, Thermo Fisher, J61883) for 7 days. Cells were cultured in both puromycin and blasticidin to maintain selective pressure until flow sorting (Supplementary Fig. 11b).

To sort, cells were harvested by trypsinizing, resuspended at approximately 107 cells per ml in presort medium (DMEM with 10% FBS, 25 mM HEPES (Sigma, H3375) and 100 µg ml−1 gentamycin (Amresco, 0304)) and held on ice until sorting was performed. Cells were sorted using a BD FACS Aria four-laser special order research product (Robert H. Lurie Cancer Center Flow Cytometry Core) using BD FACS Diva software (version 8.0.3). The sorting strategy was as follows. Single cells were first gated to exclude all EYFP-positive cells (as EYFP-positive cells still have an intact landing pad locus, suggesting that a misintegration event occurred) and to include only miRFP720+ cells. EYFP expression was measured using the FITC channel and miRFP720 expression was measured using a modified APC–Cy7 channel (details on channel configurations in Supplementary Table 4). Then, a gate was drawn on miRFP720 expression to capture the 88th to 98th percentile of miRFP720-expressing cells (the top 2% were excluded to exclude cells suspected to possess two or more integrated copies of the cargo vector). A total of 50,000 cells were collected in postsort medium (DMEM with 20% FBS, 25 mM HEPES and 100 μg ml−1 gentamycin); cells were held on ice until they could be centrifuged at 150g for 5 min, resuspended in 0.5 ml of complete medium supplemented with 100 µg ml−1 gentamycin and plated in one well of a 24-well plate. Cells were maintained in gentamycin for 7 days after sorting during expansion before banking. Additional information is depicted in Supplementary Fig. 11c.

Quantification of reporter output

MESA signaling was quantified by measuring the expression of a downstream fluorescent reporter protein, DsRed-Express2, regulated by a synTF-inducible promoter. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) for each relevant channel (as defined in Supplementary Table 3) of the single-cell transfected population (positive for transfection control marker) or the single-cell transposon-modified population (mNeonGreen+) was calculated and exported for further analysis. To calculate reporter expression, MFI in the PE–Texas red channel was averaged across three biological replicates. The MFI was converted to molecules of equivalent PE–Texas red (MEPTRs); as shown in Supplementary Fig. 13c, to determine conversion factors for MFI to MEPTRs, UltraRainbow calibration particles (Spherotech, URCP-100-2H) were run with each flow cytometry experiment. These reagents contain nine subpopulations of beads, each with a known number of various fluorophores. The total bead population was identified by FSC-A versus SSC-A gating and bead subpopulations were identified through two fluorescent channels. MEPTR values corresponding to each subpopulation were supplied by the manufacturer. A calibration curve was generated for the experimentally determined MFI versus the manufacturer-supplied MEPTRs and a linear regression was performed with the constraint that 0 MFI = 0 MEPTRs. The slope from the regression was used as the conversion factor and error was propagated. Fold differences were calculated by dividing reporter expression with ligand treatment by the reporter expression without ligand treatment. Standard error was propagated through all calculations.

Western blotting

For western blotting to detect MESA receptor expression, HEK293FT cells were plated at 2 × 105 cells per well in 1 ml of DMEM in 12-well plates 24 h before transfection and transfected as above, using 200 μl of transfection mix per well (the reaction scaled with the volume of medium). At 36–48 h after transfection, cells were lysed with 250 μl of radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate and 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate) with protease inhibitor cocktail (Pierce, Thermo Fisher, A32953) and incubated on ice for 30 min. Lysate was cleared by centrifugation at 14,000g for 20 min at 4 °C and supernatant was collected. A BCA assay was performed to determine protein concentration and, after a 10-min incubation in Laemmli buffer (final concentration: 60 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 10% glycerol, 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 100 mM dithiothreitol and 0.01% bromophenol blue) at 70 °C, protein (10–25 μg, the maximum amount of protein that could be loaded to keep protein mass constant across all samples on a gel) was loaded onto a 4–15% Mini-PROTEAN TGX precast protein gel (Bio-Rad) and run at 50 V for 10 min followed by 100 V for at least 1 h. Wet transfer was performed onto an Immuno-Blot PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad) for 45 min at 100 V. Ponceau-S staining was used to confirm protein transfer.

For detection of MESA receptors using the N-terminal 3×FLAG epitope tag, membranes were blocked for 30 min with 3% milk in Tris-buffered saline pH 8.0 (TBS; 50 mM Tris, 138 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl and HCl to pH 8.0), washed once with TBS pH 8.0 for 5 min and incubated for overnight at 4 °C in primary antibody (mouse anti-FLAG M2 (Sigma, F1804, RRID: AB_262044); http://antibodyregistry.org/AB_262044), diluted 1:1,000 in 3% milk in TBS pH 8.0. The primary antibody solution was decanted and the membrane was washed once with TBS pH 8.0 and twice with TBS pH 8.0 with 0.05% Tween for 5 min each. Secondary antibody (horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-mouse (Cell Signaling Technologies, 7076, RRID: AB_330924); http://antibodyregistry.org/AB_330924), diluted 1:3,000 in 5% milk in TBST pH 7.6 (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, HCl to pH 7.6 and 0.1% Tween), was applied for 1 h at room temperature and the membrane was washed three times for 5 min each with TBST pH 7.6. After primary and secondary staining and washing, the membrane was incubated with Clarity western enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Bio-Rad) for 5 min and then imaged using an Azure c280 (Azure Biosystems). Images were cropped with Illustrator CC (Adobe). No other image processing was used.

For detection of mbIL-15 using the C-terminal Myc epitope tag, membranes were blocked for 30 min with 5% milk in TBST pH 7.6, washed once with TBST pH 7.6 for 5 min and incubated overnight at 4 °C in primary antibody (mouse anti-Myc (Abcam, ab32, RRID: AB_303599); https://antibodyregistry.org/AB_303599) diluted 1:1,000 in 5% milk in TBST pH 7.6. Primary antibody solution was decanted and the membrane was washed three times with TBST pH 7.6 for 5 min each. Secondary antibody (HRP-conjugated anti-mouse (Cell Signaling Technologies, 7076, RRID: AB_330924); http://antibodyregistry.org/AB_330924), diluted 1:3,000 in 5% milk in TBST pH 7.6, was applied for 1 h at room temperature and the membrane was washed three times for 5 min each time with TBST pH 7.6. After primary and secondary staining and washing, the membrane was incubated with Clarity western ECL substrate (Bio-Rad) for 5 min and then imaged using an Azure c280 (Azure Biosystems). Images were cropped with Illustrator CC (Adobe). No other image processing was used.

Statistical analyses

Analyses of variance (ANOVAs) and Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) tests were performed using RStudio. Tukey’s HSD tests were performed with α = 0.05. Pairwise comparisons were made using a two-tailed Welch’s t-test, which is a version of Student’s t-test in which the variance between samples is treated as not necessarily equal. Two-tailed Welch’s t-tests were performed in GraphPad and Excel. To decrease the false discovery rate, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to each set of tests per figure panel; in all tests, after the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, the null hypothesis was rejected for P values < 0.05. The outcomes for each statistical test are provided in the figure captions and in more detail in Supplementary Notes 6–8. Ligand dose responses were log-transformed and fitted to a standard Hill equation using GraphPad Prism 10.4.1; results are summarized in Supplementary Table 5.
