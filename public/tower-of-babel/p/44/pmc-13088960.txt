Randomized Clinical Trial on the Efficacy and Safety of Preoperative Enema in the Prevention of Peritoneal Dialysis Catheter Dysfunction
Authors: Lijuan Zhao, Jin Zhao, Yan Xing, Ming Bai, Guoshuang Xu, Xiayin Li, Meilan Zhou, Yan Yu, Jun Yang, Shiren Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCurrent clinical practice guidelines do not universally recommend bowel preparation for peritoneal dialysis (PD) catheter insertion. This randomized controlled trial aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of preoperative enema in preventing PD catheter dysfunction.MethodsIn this prospective, single-center trial, 136 patients undergoing PD catheterization were randomly assigned to receive either preoperative enema within 24 hours (enema group, n = 68) or no bowel preparation (control group, n = 68). The primary end point was the incidence of catheter dysfunction requiring intervention within 3 months postoperation. Secondary outcomes encompassed the incidence of catheter dysfunction requiring intervention in 6 months, catheter event free survival time, the incidence of PD-associated peritonitis, and other catheter-related complications.ResultsOne hundred thirty-four participants completed the 6-month follow-up after excluding 2 patients (1 with scrotal leakage and 1 with acute kidney injury). Baseline characteristics showed no significant intergroup differences. The enema group demonstrated significantly lower incidence of catheter dysfunction requiring intervention at 3 months (10/68 [14.7%], unadjusted risk ratio (RR): 0.297, [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.129–0.682], P = 0.004; adjusted RR 0.299 [95% CI: 0.121–0.737], P = 0.009). Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed longer catheter survival time in the enema group versus controls (log-rank P = 0.0015). Only 1 control patient (1.5%) required surgical revision. There was no statistically significant difference between the 2 groups in terms of other infection and noninfection complications.ConclusionPreoperative enema significantly reduces early PD catheter dysfunction risk without increasing complication rates. These findings support incorporating bowel preparation into standard preoperative protocols for PD catheter placement.Keywords: bowel preparation, catheter survival time, enema peritoneal dialysis, peritoneal dialysis catheter dysfunction

End-stage kidney disease (ESKD) has emerged as a significant global public health challenge,1,2 with PD serving as the primary renal replacement therapy for affected patients.3 The long-term success of PD critically depends on maintaining a functional and durable peritoneal catheter access, with mechanical complications representing a leading cause of technical failure, particularly during the initial treatment year.4,5 Catheter dysfunction, often necessitating catheter removal, accounts for 20% to 30% of early PD discontinuations according to registry data.6, 7, 8, 9

Current preventive strategies emphasize the following 3 key elements: appropriate catheter selection, meticulous surgical technique, and comprehensive postoperative care.10 Current guidelines from the International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis (ISPD)11 and the Chinese standard operating procedures for PD12 specifically recommend bowel management for constipated patients before catheter insertion. This recommendation primarily stems from 2 observational studies conducted in 199613 and 2004,14 which reported substantially higher rates of initial dialysis dysfunction in constipated patients than their nonconstipated counterparts (68% vs. 13%). However, several knowledge gaps persist. First, the reported prevalence of constipation in CKD or ESKD populations ranges modestly from 14.2% to 28.9%,15,16 leaving uncertainty about optimal management for the majority without baseline constipation. Second, existing evidence derives from small-scale observational studies lacking standardized protocols for bowel preparation. Third, no randomized controlled trials have evaluated the efficacy and safety of routine preoperative bowel preparation in contemporary PD practice.

Our preliminary single-center retrospective analysis17 revealed a significant association between preoperative bowel preparation and reduced catheter dysfunction risk (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.384, 95% CI: 0.254–0.581, P < 0.001). However, retrospective designs inherently limit causal inference and may introduce selection bias. To address these limitations and provide higher-level evidence, we conducted this prospective randomized controlled trial evaluating the efficacy and safety of preoperative enema in preventing catheter dysfunction among patients undergoing PD.

Methods

Patients and Study Design

This single center, prospective, randomized trial (registration number: NCT05258552) was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Xijing hospital (KYKY20222006-F-1). The participants provided their written informed consent at the time of enrolment. The study was designed and conducted in compliance with the principles of Good Clinical Practice guidelines of the International Conference on Harmonization.

We enrolled consecutive patients who needed catheter insertion for PD in our center. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) patients ≥ 18 years; (ii) requirement of maintenance PD for ESKD; (iii) patients undergoing first-time open surgical PD catheter insertion; (iv) patients in whom only straight double-cuff Tenckhoff catheters were used; and (v) patients willing to participate in this study and who signed the informed consent form. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) patients having known contraindication to PD or enema; (ii) patients who refused enema; (iii) patients with an expected survival time < 3 months; and (iv) patients on whom cathartic agents such as senna, lactulose, or colonic dialysis and enema were used 3 days before PD surgery. Recruitment took place from January 2022 to December 2023.

Patients underwent randomization and were assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either enema group or control group. Randomization was performed using a block randomization scheme with a fixed block size of 4 to ensure allocation concealment. The random allocation sequence was generated by an independent statistician using computer-generated random numbers. Implementation of the allocation sequence was carried out by research personnel not involved in patient recruitment or outcome assessment. Treatment allocation was blinded to operators and investigators. The schematic of the study design is presented in Supplementary Figure S1. Constipation was defined by Rome III questionnaire.18

Enema Procedure

Patients emptied their bladder before the procedure. The steps of the enema procedure were as follows: (i) The patient was assisted to adopt the left lateral position, with the knees pulled up to the chest. (ii) The enema bag was placed 30 to 46 cm above the rectum. (iii) The lubricated enema tube was inserted into the rectum approximately 7 cm to 10 cm. (iv) The clamp was released and the fluid was allowed to fill inside till all the fluid entered the rectum. (v) The nozzle was removed once the bag was empty. The patient was kept in the lying down position for a minimum of 5 minutes and to a maximum of 1 hour before going to the bathroom and expelling the fluid. (vi) The materials were cleaned and records were prepared. The operation process was completed by the nursing staff using 0.1% to 0.2% soap-suds enema.

PD Program

The patients were inserted by nephrologists using open dissection surgical technique as previously reported.17 Before catheter insertion, the patient’s urinary bladder was emptied, skin preparation was done using a povidone-iodine solution, and standard draping was performed. Prophylactic intravenous cefazolin was routinely administered, except in patients with penicillin or cephalosporin allergies. An open approach through a small paramedian paraumbilical incision was performed in all patients. Only straight double-cuff Tenckhoff with straight tip catheters were used. An arc-shaped subcutaneous tunnel was established from the outer top to the outer bottom of the incision, and the catheter was pulled out from the outer lower outlet of the incision.

PD was initiated as early as 24 hours after catheter placement. The fill volume per exchange was gradually increased: starting from 300 ml, it was progressively raised to 2000 ml per exchange. By the 7th day, the patient was receiving three exchanges per day, each with a fill volume of 2000 ml.

End Points and Definitions

All patients and catheters were followed up for at least 6 months. We examined mechanical and infectious catheter complications. Catheter survival was calculated from the day of catheter insertion. Catheter event free survival refers to the time that a PD catheter can be used without catheter dysfunction. PD-related peritonitis and exit-site infection were defined in the conventional manner. Data were censored at the time of kidney transplantation, recovery of kidney function, or the end of the study.

The primary outcome of this study was the incidence of catheter dysfunction requiring intervention within 3 months. Catheter dysfunction refers to drainage failure, as defined by the inability to drain peritoneal dialysate effluent reliably within 45 minutes. Following PD catheter insertion, the PD drainage was assessed with every exchange. The patient or their caregiver was educated to observe the drainage. In case of any drainage problems, they were advised to come to the hospital immediately. The investigators (JY and YY), who were blinded to the treatment group, evaluated any issues. Mechanical kinking of the catheter tubing was excluded. If the catheter met the definition of dysfunction, it was counted as an event in the analysis. An abdominal upright plain film was obtained to determine whether catheter migration had occurred.

The interventions of catheter dysfunctions included moderate physical activity (walking on stairs and jumping slightly), intestinal and bowel relaxation, manual reduction, fibrinolytic therapy, second operation (either catheter repositioning or reinsertion by an open surgical method), and extubation. The specific procedures for handling PD catheter dysfunction are detailed in the supplementary material.

Secondary outcomes included the incidence of catheter dysfunction requiring intervention in 6 months, catheter event free survival time, the incidence of PD-associated peritonitis, and other catheter complications.

Catheter tip migration can be recognized by a plain abdominal radiograph. Ideally, the PD catheter tip should be located in the deep pelvic area to minimize the risk of omental entrapment and maximize the hydraulic function. A high PD catheter, defined as higher than the pelvic brim on a lateral abdominal radiograph, is associated with significantly higher rates of clinically important drainage problems.

Statistical Analysis

Sample size calculation was based on previous data indicating a 30% incidence of catheter dysfunction with migration following peritoneal catheter insertion, consistent with rates reported in studies using open surgical techniques. The trial was designed to detect a clinically meaningful reduction in the complication rate to 10%, with 80% power and a 2-sided alpha of 0.05. To account for potential loss to follow-up, the calculated sample size was increased by 10%. Thus, a total of 68 patients per group were planned.

The primary and secondary outcomes were analyzed based on the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, which included all participants who underwent random assignment. For sensitivity analysis, these outcomes were also assessed using a per-protocol (PP) population, excluding 1 patient per group who did not receive PD after PD catheter insertion.

Time-to-event outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with between-group comparisons performed using the log-rank test. Treatment effects for binary primary and secondary outcomes were estimated via modified logistic regression models, with results expressed as risk ratios (RRs) and 95% CIs. For time-to-event end points, hazard ratios with 95% CIs were derived from the Cox proportional hazards models. All models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, history of abdominal operation, constipation, surgeon, and serum potassium. Safety outcomes were compared between groups using chi-square tests. No missing data were encountered, and all patients completed follow-up.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the homogeneity of the treatment effect on the primary outcome. These analyses used a modified logistic regression model (or Cox regression for time-to-event outcomes) that incorporated treatment assignment, subgroup variables, and their interaction terms. Prespecified subgroups included sex, age, body mass index, serum potassium, surgeon, and constipation. Consistency of treatment effects across subgroups was evaluated using interaction P-values (detailed methods are provided in the Supplementary material).

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS software package (version 24.0, IBM Corp.) and R Project for Statistical Computing (version 4.3.0, R foundation). A 2-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 136 patients were randomly assigned to the enema group or control group after excluding 23 patients (Figure 1). Sixty-eight patients were randomized to the enema and control groups. The study was stopped after the inclusion of the planned number of patients. The ITT population included all patients who underwent randomization. One patient randomized to the enema group was excluded from the final analysis because this patient’s renal function recovered within 20 days after randomization and did not receive PD. One patient randomized to the control group was excluded from the final analysis because of scrotal leakage on the first day after PD catheter insertion. Therefore, 67 patients were included in each group from the PP population, respectively.

Flow diagram of the number of patients recruited, randomly assigned, followed up, and analyzed.

The 2 groups were comparable in baseline characteristics (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1). There were no significant differences between the 2 groups in terms of sex, age, diabetes mellitus, and other primary diseases, body mass index, blood urine nitrogen, serum creatinine, blood urine acid, serum albumin, serum potassium, and hemoglobin. Of the included patients, 10 (14.7%) and 11 (16.2%) had constipation in the enema and control groups, respectively. And 16.9% of the included patients had previous abdominal surgery before catheter insertion. The mean interval between enema administration and PD catheter insertion was 9.96 hours, ranging from 4 to 16 hours. No patients were lost to follow-up during the trial.

Primary Outcome

In the ITT analysis, there were 35 (25.7%) episodes of catheter dysfunction requiring intervention at the end of the 3-month follow-up. Patients randomized to the enema group had a significantly lower catheter dysfunction rate than the control group (14.7% vs. 36.8%, unadjusted RR 0.297 [95% CI: 0.129–0.682], P = 0.004; adjusted RR 0.291 [95% CI: 0.121–0.737], P = 0.009; Table 2). Similar results were also obtained in the PP analysis (14.9% vs. 37.3%, unadjusted RR 0.295 [95% CI: 0.128–0.679], P = 0.004; adjusted RR 0.291 [95% CI: 0.118–0.722], P = 0.008; Supplementary Table S2).

Secondary Outcomes

After 6 months, catheter dysfunction was more frequent in the control group, 28 of 68 patients (41.2%) compared with 11 of 68 patients (16.2%) in the enema group (P = 0.002). Catheter tip migration was the most common cause of catheter dysfunction. As shown in Table 2, 10 of 68 (14.7%) patients receiving enema had catheter tip migration in the enema group versus 18 of 68 (26.5%) in control group within 3 months. There was a slightly higher incidence of catheter tip migration in the control group, but the difference between the groups was nonsignificant. Catheter dysfunction required surgical intervention only in 1 patient in the control group. No statistical differences were observed between the 2 groups in terms of leakage, PD-associated peritonitis, and exit or tunnel infection.

Safety and Adverse Events

Preoperative cleansing enema was administered once in the enema group, with good tolerability and no adverse reactions reported. All surgeries were completed without serious complications. No cases of PD-associated peritonitis occurred within 30 days after PD catheter insertion in either group. Two deaths were reported as follows:

patient #11 (male, 53 years, enema group) died on day 165 because of heart failure, unrelated to the trial, with no catheter-related complications. Patient #37 (female, 57 years, control group) died on day 136 because of autoimmune pernicious anemia and multiorgan failure, unrelated to the trial, with no catheter-related complications during the follow-up.

Subgroup Analyses

The results of subgroup analyses are included in Figure 3. There were no significant interactions between subgroups and efficacy except for the subgroups of constipation. In the nonconstipated subgroup, enema significantly reduces the risk of PD catheter dysfunction (RR, 0.16 [95% CI: 0.06–0.47], P = 0.001). The proportional effect of enema on PD catheter function was consistent across other prespecified subgroups (all interaction P-values ≥ 0.05), including by sex, age, body mass index, serum potassium, and by surgeon.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial provides the first prospective evidence that preoperative cleansing enema significantly reduces catheter dysfunction incidence (P = 0.004) and prolongs unimpeded catheter survival in urgent-start patients undergoing PD, while maintaining comparable peritonitis rates between the intervention and control groups. Notably, the 14.7% residual dysfunction rate in the enema group suggests that single-dose bowel preparation might not fully resolve fecal impaction, indicating potential benefits of intensified bowel management protocols.

The evolving role of PD as a primary renal replacement modality, currently serving > 20% of China’s ESKD population, underscores the clinical imperative for reliable catheter function optimization. Our observed 29.1% catheter dysfunction rate aligns with reported global ranges (9.5%–62.4%),19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 reflecting center-specific variations in surgical expertise and procedural protocols. The identified risk hierarchy— diabetic status (36% vs. 20% dysfunction rate), preoperative constipation (68% vs. 13%), and inadequate postoperative rest (< 6 hours)— corroborates pathophysiological mechanisms linking metabolic dysregulation and mechanical obstruction to catheter malfunction.

Given our findings, the current guideline recommendations for selective bowel preparation,11,12 based on limited observational data,13,14 appear insufficient. Three key insights emerge, as follows: first, the 15.6% constipation prevalence (Rome III criteria) in our cohort confirms underdiagnosis of gastrointestinal dysmotility in uremic populations. Second, colonic transit studies demonstrate 30% to 40% slower motility in ESKD versus healthy controls,25 suggesting universal rather than selective bowel preparation merits consideration. Third, catheter dysfunction typically occurs within the first few days after PD insertion. Adequate preoperative bowel preparation can ensure proper placement of the PD catheter into the Douglas pouch during the procedure. Positioning the catheter in this most dependent part of the abdominal cavity enhances its stability and helps prevent catheter-related dysfunctions, such as displacement. Our retrospective data revealed striking bowel preparation disparities between dysfunctional and functional catheter groups (56.6% vs. 86.7%, P < 0.001), further supporting proactive intervention.

The safety profile of preoperative enema deserves particular emphasis. Despite theoretical concerns about intestinal perforation risk,26 we observed no procedure-related adverse events. The 16.4% overall leakage rate, was comparable to urgent-start PD reports,27,28 all occurring within 1 to 3 days postoperatively and resolving after 3 days of dialysis cessation. Catheter dysfunctions resolved spontaneously with conservative management in 99.3% of cases, affirming this approach’s feasibility. Importantly, enema administration did not increase peritonitis risk, addressing a major clinician concern regarding bowel preparation safety.

Although bowel preparation was performed, catheter dysfunction still occurred in 14.7% of patients in the enema group. As is well recognized, catheter dysfunction is influenced by multiple factors, including catheter type,29,30 surgical approach,31 surgeon experience, and patient-related variables—both modifiable and nonmodifiable (age19 and BMI32). This underscores the importance of a multifaceted clinical approach, encompassing appropriate catheter selection, optimal surgical technique, standardized training for surgeons, and refined, individualized perioperative management, all of which contribute significantly to improving catheter outcomes. Furthermore, the incidence of catheter dysfunction was notably higher in the constipation group (42.9%, 9/21) compared with the nonconstipation group (22.6%, 26/115). Subgroup analysis revealed that in nonconstipated patients, preoperative enema significantly reduced the rate of postoperative catheter dysfunction. However, within the constipation subgroup, the results were likely biased because of the small sample size (36.4% in the enema group [4/11] vs. 50% in the control group [5/10]). This suggests that a single enema may be insufficient to fully address bowel issues in all patients, particularly those with refractory constipation, who might require more intensive and prolonged bowel preparation. Future studies should focus on developing reliable methods to assess intestinal gas and stool burden, along with comprehensive, multimodal, and sustained perioperative bowel management protocols, with the goal of reducing the incidence of PD catheter dysfunction. Results from other parameters in the subgroup analysis were consistent, showing odds ratio < 1, which further supports the potential protective effect of enema against catheter dysfunction. Increasing the sample size in future research may help to obtain statistically significant positive outcomes.

Some principal limitations warrant consideration. First, single-center design and modest sample size limit generalizability, particularly regarding rare complications. Further multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed to support these findings. Second, given intervention, blinding was not possible for the participants. However, outcome assessors and surgeons were blinded which guaranteed the reliability of our conclusions. Additionally, the use of straight double-cuff Tenckhoff catheters, open surgery, and experienced surgeons were the predetermined characteristics of this RCT. Although this standardization minimizes performance bias and ensures the observed outcomes, it inevitably limits the generalizability of our findings. Moreover, standardized postoperative care protocols, may not reflect real-world practice variability while minimizing confounding. Future multicenter trials should incorporate the following: (i) Objective bowel content quantification (e.g., CT volumetry)33; (ii) Extended follow-up assessing long-term patency; (iii) Comparative evaluation of different bowel preparation regimens such as oral laxative.

Conclusion

We designed an RCT to further investigate the effectiveness and safety of preoperative enema in preventing catheter dysfunction in urgent-start PD. The 6-month follow-up data analysis showed that preoperative enema is beneficial in reducing the incidence of catheter dysfunction and prolonging unimpeded PD catheter survival time.
