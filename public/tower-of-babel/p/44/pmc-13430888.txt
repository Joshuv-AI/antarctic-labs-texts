Epigenetic brain reprogramming rejuvenates neuro-immune circuits to reverse Alzheimer’s disease pathology and systemic bone loss
Authors: Mei-Dan Wan, Xi-Xi Liu, Teng-Fei Wan, Yi-Wei Liu, Ya-Ling Jiang, Jing-Tao Zou, Bin Jiao, Qian-Qian Liu, Ling Jin, Ran Duan, Zun Wang, Chun-Gu Hong, Xin Wang, Xin-Yue Hu, Xin-Xin Liao, Jia Cao, Lu Shen, Hui Xie, Zhen-Xing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by progressive neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation, and systemic comorbidities, yet disease-modifying therapies remain elusive. Here, we show that partial epigenetic reprogramming via brain-restricted expression of Oct4, Sox2, and Klf4 (OSK) restores neuronal and neuroimmune homeostasis without loss of cellular identity. In APP/PS1 mice, OSK reprogramming improves cognitive performance across disease stages, reduces amyloid-β deposition, attenuates microglial activation, preserves synaptic integrity, and limits neuronal apoptosis. Mechanistically, reduced representation bisulfite sequencing reveals widespread reversal of AD-associated DNA methylation patterns, which is dependent on Tet2-mediated demethylation, establishing epigenetic rejuvenation as a key driver of functional recovery. Unexpectedly, brain-restricted OSK reprogramming also ameliorates systemic bone loss by reshaping brain-derived extracellular vesicle signaling, including modulation of miR-483-5p, thereby restoring osteogenic capacity. Together, these findings identify partial epigenetic reprogramming as a strategy to rewire neuro-immune circuits and link central nervous system rejuvenation to peripheral tissue homeostasis, providing a conceptual framework for targeting both neurodegeneration and its systemic consequences in AD.Graphical abstract Localized OSK-mediated epigenetic reprogramming in the hippocampus rejuvenates neuronal and microglial epigenomes via Tet2-dependent DNA demethylation, thereby suppressing Aβ deposition, neuroinflammation and apoptosis to restore cognition in AD, while also mitigating associated bone loss through modulation of brain-derived EV cargo Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03854-8.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia and represents a growing global health and socioeconomic challenge. Its core neuropathological features—extracellular β-amyloid (Aβ) plaques and intracellular tau tangles—drive progressive neuronal loss, cognitive decline, and chronic neuroinflammation mediated by activated glial cells [1–4]. Beyond the central nervous system, AD is increasingly recognized as a systemic disorder, associated with accelerated bone loss and elevated fracture risk [5–9]. Consistent with this view, we have identified a functional brain–bone axis mediated by extracellular vesicles (EVs): osteocyte-derived EVs exert neuroprotective effects, whereas EVs from AD brains impair bone marrow mesenchymal stem/stromal cell (BMSC) fate and promote skeletal degeneration [10–13]. These findings highlight the need for therapeutic strategies that simultaneously target neurodegeneration and its systemic consequences. However, despite decades of effort, interventions directed at Aβ or tau have achieved only modest clinical benefit [14].

Epigenetic dysregulation has emerged as a central feature of AD pathogenesis. Age-associated alterations in DNA methylation are closely linked to AD risk and progression [15, 16], contributing to dysfunction in neuronal circuits and microglial activation states [17–20]. These observations have prompted growing interest in epigenetic rejuvenation strategies aimed at reversing age-related molecular signatures while preserving cellular identity [21]. Reprogramming with Yamanaka factors can reset epigenetic landscapes [22, 23]; however, full induction of OSKM (Oct3/4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc) carries substantial risks, including loss of cell identity and tumorigenesis [24, 25]. In contrast, partial reprogramming using OSK (excluding c-Myc) has been shown to restore youthful epigenetic states without inducing pluripotency [26, 27], offering a potentially safer approach for tissue rejuvenation, particularly in the central nervous system.

Here, we test the hypothesis that brain-restricted epigenetic reprogramming can restore neuroimmune homeostasis and modify both central and systemic manifestations of AD. We show that localized hippocampal expression of OSK improves cognitive function, attenuates amyloid pathology and neuroinflammation, and preserves neuronal integrity in both early- and late-stage APP/PS1 mice. Mechanistically, these effects depend on Tet2-mediated DNA demethylation, supporting a causal role for epigenetic remodeling in functional recovery. Moreover, we find that OSK reprogramming reshapes brain-derived EV signaling, including miR-483-5p, thereby restoring osteogenic capacity and alleviating AD-associated bone loss. Together, our findings position partial epigenetic reprogramming as a strategy to couple central nervous system rejuvenation with peripheral tissue homeostasis in AD.

Methods

Animals and ethics statement

All animal procedures complied with the guidelines of the World Organization for Animal Health and the Chinese National Guideline for Animal Experiments. Protocols were approved by the Ethical Review Board at Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (approval no. 202101002).

Viral vector construction and production

To generate AAV-OSK, mouse Oct4, Sox2, and Klf4 cDNAs were cloned into a plasmid under the control of the EFS promoter, yielding a polycistronic construct (Oct4-P2A-Sox2-T2A-Klf4; Figure S1A). The control vector (AAV-Mock) contained the EFS promoter and an eGFP reporter for monitoring transduction efficiency (Figure S1B). Both AAV-OSK and AAV-Mock were packaged into AAV serotype 9 capsids (AAV9) at titers exceeding 1 × 10¹² genome copies per mL (Haixing Biosciences).

For in vitro experiments, an adenoviral vector (Ad5 serotype) was employed. Mouse Oct4, Sox2, and Klf4 cDNAs were cloned into a CMV-driven pAV plasmid fused with eGFP, generating Ad-OSK (Figure S1C). The corresponding control vector (Ad-Mock) consisted of the empty CMV-eGFP pAV plasmid. Both adenoviral vectors were constructed and packaged by Haixing Biosciences.

Animals and treatments

APPswe/PS1dE9 (APP/PS1) mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (Stock No. 034829-JAX). Male APP/PS1 and wild-type (WT) mice were housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Mice were randomized to treatment groups, and experiments were performed blinded to genotype and treatment where possible.

AAV-OSK or AAV-Mock (2 µl per side) was stereotaxically injected into the bilateral hippocampus of APP/PS1 or WT mice under anaesthesia. Using a stereotaxic frame (SA-100, Yuyan), injections were targeted at 1.8 mm posterior to the bregma, 1.5 mm lateral to the sagittal suture, and 1.5 mm below the cortical surface (Figure S3A). The injection rate was 0.4 µL/min, and the needle was left in place for 2 min before slow withdrawal to facilitate viral diffusion. Control mice received equivalent injections of AAV-Mock. For the early-stage APP/PS1 mice, injections were initiated at 4 months of age and repeated one month later; behavioral testing was conducted, and brain samples were collected at 6 months. For late-stage APP/PS1 mice, injections were initiated at 6 months of age, followed by behavioral testing after one month and brain collection at 7 months.

Behavioral analysis

Morris water maze (MWM): The MWM test was performed as previously described [10]. A white circular pool with a diameter of 140 cm was filled with water maintained at of 22 ± 0.5 °C. The pool was divided into four quadrants, and a hidden escape platform with a diameter of 10 cm was placed 1 cm below the water surface in the middle of one quadrant. A distal clue was also included to guide the mice. A commercial animal video-tracking analysis system (AVTAS ver4.0, AniLab) was used. The training for the MWM test was performed over 5 days, and each trial lasted for 60 s or until the animal could locate the escape platform. On the sixth day, memory tests (probe trials) were performed with the platform removed, and the mice were allowed to explore freely for 60 s. The time spent in the target quadrant and the platform crossing times were quantified.

Y maze: The Y maze test was performed as previously described [10]. The apparatus was a symmetrical Y maze with arms 50 × 10 cm (20-cm-high sidewalls), each arm labeled with different shapes. Mice were placed in start arm of the maze with a closed arm (novel arm) and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. The remaining arm was named the familiar arm. After a 2-hour interval, all three arms were opened and allowed to explore the maze freely for 5 min. The times spent in each arm were recorded.

Novel object recognition task (NORT): The NORT was conducted in a square polymethyl methacrylate box (30 × 30 × 30 cm) with external cues for memory tasks [28]. Mice were habituated to the testing room for at least 30 min and allowed to freely explore the arena for 10 min. The arena was cleaned between trials to eliminate olfactory cues. During the training phase, two identical objects were fixed in the open field. Each mouse was placed in the release corner facing the wall and allowed to explore the objects for 10 min. For the test phase, one object was replaced with a novel object while the other remained as the familiar reference. After a 20-min interval, mice were returned to the arena and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. Exploration times for each object were recorded and analyzed.

Tissue processing

Brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h. Following fixation, the samples were dehydrated by immersion in 30% sucrose in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at 4 °C for 24 h. The brains were then embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT) and quickly frozen at − 80 °C. Brain sections, including the hippocampal regions of interest, were subsequently cut on a Cryostat (ThermoFisher) to a thickness of 10 μm.

Femur tissues were fixed in 4% PFA for histological evaluations. After fixation, decalcification of the femur tissue was achieved through immersion in 18% EDTA for one week. The tissues were then embedded in paraffin, allowing for ultra-thin sectioning with a thickness of 5 μm for histological analysis.

Immunofluorescence (IF) and immunohistochemical staining

Following sectioning, brain slices were baked at 37 °C to promote adhesion to glass slides, and residual OCT was removed by rinsing in PBS for 10 min. Sections were permeabilized with PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with 10% goat serum in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C. Sections were then incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen). For Thioflavin S staining, sections were incubated in 0.05% Thioflavin S in 50% ethanol for 12 min, followed by two washes in 50% ethanol and a final wash in PBS for 5 min. Sections were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Apotome, Zeiss). Microglial morphology was analyzed by skeletonized analysis of IBA1 positive cells.

For bone sections, samples were baked at 65 °C, dewaxed in xylene, and dehydrated through graded ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed with citric acid. Osteocalcin (OCN) immunostaining and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining (G1050, Servicebio) were used to assess osteogenic and osteoclastic activity, respectively. Antibody dilution ratios are provided in Supplementary Table S8. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed for histological evaluation.

TUNEL staining

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining was performed using a commercially availablekit (40307ES20; Yeasen). After brain tissue sections were baked at 37 °C, OCT was washed by rinsing the sections in 0.01 M PBS for 10 min. The processes were then carried out in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell culture and transductions

Primary microglia were isolated from the hippocampi and cortices of mouse pups at postnatal days P0–P2. Briefly, tissues were dissected in cold Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS), meninges removed, and digested with 0.025% trypsin at 37 °C for 20 min. Digestion was stopped with pre-warmed HBSS, followed by gentle trituration through pipettes and filtration with a 70 μm nylon strainer. Cells were seeded into poly-D-lysine (PDL)-coated T-75 flasks and maintained in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 20 ng/mL granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) to establish mixed glial cultures. At confluence (day 12), microglia were harvested by shaking at 120 rpm for 30 min and reseeded onto PDL-coated plates for transfection.

Primary neurons were prepared from the hippocampi and cortices of embryonic day 16.5 (E16.5) mouse embryos. Dissected tissues were digested with 0.025% trypsin at 37 °C for 20 min with gentle agitation every 5 min. After enzymatic dissociation, cells were triturated ten times with pipettes, filtered through a 70 μm strainer, counted, and plated on PDL-coated plates. Neurons were maintained in Neurobasal medium supplemented with 2% B27 and 2 mM GlutaMAX.

BV2 microglial cells (Procell), HT22 hippocampal neurons (BLUEFBIO), and SH-SY5Y cells (Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology) were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (PS; Solarbio) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. SH-SY5Y-APPswe stable cell lines were generated by lentiviral transduction with the pathogenic APPswe mutation (NM_201414.3: c.1785G > C p. K595N; c.1786 A > C p. M595L).

For primary microglia and neurons, cells were infected with Ad-OSK or Ad-Mock. In some experiments, OSK-infected microglia were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 200 ng/mL; Sigma, Cat. No. 559763), and conditioned medium was applied to neurons. Neurons were also treated with CCCP (Yeasen, Cat. No. 40333ES60) to induce apoptosis.

For BV2 cell infection, cells were infected with AAV-OSK or AAV-Mock. For SH-SY5Y-APPswe cell experiments, cells were treated with BV2-conditioned medium for 48 h. Supernatants were collected for Aβ42 measurement by ELISA. HT22 cells were infected with AAV-OSK or AAV-Mock, followed by CCCP treatment to induce apoptosis.

Mouse BMSCs were isolated from the femoral and tibial marrow of 3-week-old wild-type mice, as previously described [11]. Cells were cultured in α-Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM; HyClone) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PS.

Isolation and characterization of EVs

EVs were isolated from the cerebral cortex and hippocampus of AD or WT mice treated with either AAV-OSK or AAV-Mock, as described in our previous study [11]. Briefly, brains were dissected and placed in cold Hibernate-A medium (Cat. No. A12475–01, Life Technologies). Following PBS perfusion to remove residual blood cells, tissue samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and passed through a 0.22 μm filter (Millipore) to remove remaining cells and debris. The filtrate was then transferred to Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Units (10 kDa; Millipore) and concentrated by centrifugation at 4,000 × g at 4 °C. EVs were subsequently isolated from the concentrated filtrate using bottom-up OptiPrep™ density gradient centrifugation according to a previously established protocol [29]. Particle size and concentration were measured by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA).

BMSC differentiation assays

Osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs was performed as previously described [11]. Briefly, BMSCs were seeded into 48-well plates and cultured for 24 h prior to induction. The culture medium was then replaced with either osteogenic or adipogenic differentiation medium (Cyagen), supplemented with EVs derived from brain tissues. After 8 days of osteogenic induction, cells were stained with alizarin red S (ARS) solution (Cyagen) to assess matrix mineralization. For adipogenic differentiation, cells were induced for 12 days and subsequently stained with oil red O (ORO) solution (Cyagen) to detect lipid droplet formation.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of Aβ42 in the cell culture supernatants were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (E-EL-H0543c; Elabscience). All procedures were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK8) assay

SH-SY5Y-APPswe cells were seeded in 96-well plates. After 24 h, cultures were treated with conditioned medium from LPS-activated BV2 cells with or without AAV-OSK pretreatment. Cell proliferation was assessed after 48 h using the CCK-8 assay (Abcam, ab228554).

Flow cytometry

Apoptosis in HT22 cells was assessed by flow cytometry following CCCP treatment. Cells were harvested by trypsinization (T1300; Solarbio), stained with PE Annexin V and 7-AAD using the PE Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I (BD, 559763), and analyzed on a FACSCanto™ II flow cytometer (BD, San Jose, CA, USA) within 1 h.

RNA extraction and qRT‒PCR analysis

Total RNA from cells was extracted using the TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit (TransGen) and reverse-transcribed with the NovoScript Plus All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Novoprotein). qRT–PCR was performed on an FTC-3000 system (Funglyn) using SYBR Green Master Mix (Bimake), with Gapdh as the reference gene. RNA from brain-derived EVs was isolated with the SeraMir Exosome RNA Purification Kit (System Biosciences), and miRNAs were reverse-transcribed with the miRNA First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Sangon). qRT–PCR was performed using FastStart Universal SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara) with universal reverse and specific forward primers (Table S9).

Immunocytochemistry staining

pAV-infected primary cells (microglia and neurons) and AAV-infected cell lines (BV2 and HT22) were characterized by immunofluorescence (IF) staining. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) containing 4% sucrose, permeabilized with Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. They were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen), and images were acquired by fluorescence microscopy.

Western blot

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (P0013B; Beyotime) containing a protease and phosphatase inhibitor mixture on ice for 30 min. Protein was obtained by centrifuging the lysate at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (70-PQ0012; Multi Sciences). The protein samples were heated in 5 × SDS sample loading buffer for 5 min at 95 °C to denature the proteins and then loaded onto SDS‒PAGE gels (Epizyme). The proteins were separated based on their molecular weight and transferred to PVDF membranes (IPVH00010; Millipore). Then, the membrane was incubated with specific antibodies. After incubation with appropriate HRP–conjugated secondary antibodies, the detected protein bands were visualized using ECL western blot detection kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The ChemiDoc XRS Plus with Image Lab Software (Bio-Rad) was employed to quantify the intensity of the protein bands. The dilution ratios of the primary are listed in Table S8. The uncropped raw Western blot images were shown in Figure S16.

µCT analysis

To analyze the changes in bone structure in femurs, we followed the protocol detailed in our previous work [30]. In brief, femurs were fixed with 4% PFA overnight and then scanned using vivaCT80 (SCANCO Medical AG) with a resolution of 11.4 μm per pixel, a voltage of 55 kV, and a current of 145 µA. The obtained images were recons tructed and visualized using NRecon and µCTVol v2.2 software. The analysis of trabecular bone focused on the areas between 3% and 8% proximal to the distal growth plate of the femurs. The parameters of trabecular bone included trabecular bone volume (Tb BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb. Th), trabecular number (Tb. N), and trabecular separation (Tb. Sp). In contrast, the analysis of cortical bone focused on the areas between 20% and 30% of regions proximal to the distal growth plate in the femoral mid-diaphysis. The parameters of cortical bone included cortical bone volume (Ct. BV.TV), periosteal perimeter (Ps. Pm), endosteal perimeter (Es. Pm), and cortical thickness (Ct. Th).

DNA extraction, bisulfite treatment, library preparation and sequencing

Total DNA was extracted from samples using the Blood/Cell/Tissue Genomic DNA Extraction kit (TIAGEN Biotech Co., Ltd, Cat.No. DP304) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, yielding over 2 µg of DNA. An aliquot of extracted DNA was electrophoresed on a 0.8% agarose gel to assess DNA quality and size. The resulting clear bands indicated no degradation or minimal degradation.

Approximately 2–3 µg of the extracted DNA was digested with MspI (NEB, R0106) for 30 min. The digested DNA was then subjected to bisulfite conversion using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold™ Kit (Zymo Research, Cat. No. D5005). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Accel-NGS® Methyl-Seq DNA Library kit (Swift BiosciencesTM, Cat. No. 30024) following the manufacturer’s protocol. PCR products ranging from 300 to 500 bp were enriched, quantified and sequenced on Novaseq 6000 sequencer (Illumina) with PE150 model.

Raw reads were processed with SOAPnuke (version 2.0.5) [31] to remove the residual adaptor sequences and low-quality reads. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome, and duplicate reads were removed using Bismark (version 0.20.1) [32]. The depth and coverage on chromosomes were calculated with samtools (version 1.4) [33] and bedtools (version 2.26.0) [34]. Genome-wide methylation detection was performed using Bismark, which calculates the proportion of methylated reads at each genomic site. The differentially methylated regions (DMRs) between different groups were identified with metilene (version 0.2–7.2) [35]. The pathway enrichment analysis of genes annotated to DMR was conducted with KOBAS (version 2.1.1) [36].

Statistical analysis

Quantitative statistics were described as the mean and standard deviation. The statistical significance between different groups was measured by Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc test. Two-way ANOVA was used to assess statistical differences between AD and WT mice across different treatment conditions, with genotype and treatment as the two independent factors (with Bonferroni post hoc test). In vitro experiments, “n” refers to independent experiments using separately prepared cell cultures. In vivo experiments, “n” refers to the individual mice that are independent. The statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. P < 0.05 was considered significant. The level of significance is indicated by asterisks as follows: * P ≤ 0.05, ** P ≤ 0.01, *** P ≤ 0.001, **** P < 0.0001.

Results

OSK suppresses microglial inflammatory activation while preserving cellular identity

To deliver Oct4, Sox2, and Klf4, we generated polycistronic OSK constructs in adeno-associated virus (AAV) for cell lines and adenoviral vectors for primary cultures (Figure S1). Given the role of microglial dysfunction in AD pathology [37], we first assessed the impact of OSK expression in BV2 cells and primary microglia. OSK was robustly expressed without inducing c-Myc or Nanog, indicating the absence of pluripotency or oncogenic reprogramming (Fig. 1A–B, upper panel; Figure S2). We next examined whether OSK modulates inflammatory responses. Upon lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation, proinflammatory genes (iNos, Il-1α, Il-1β, Il-6) were markedly upregulated in AAV-Mock controls but significantly suppressed by OSK in BV2 cells, while a trend toward lower levels in Tnfα and Tnfβ (Fig. 1 C and E–F). In primary microglia, OSK strongly reduced iNOS and IL-1β expression and attenuated Tnfβ levels, with a trend toward lower Tnfα and Il-1α levels (Fig. 1D–F). Under basal conditions, OSK modestly reduced Tnfα, iNos, and Il-6 levels in BV2 cells (Figure S3A). In primary microglia, OSK led to a mild decrease in Tnfα and iNos expression. However, compared with controls, the protein levels of iNOS and IL-1β were not significantly reduced (Figure S3B–D).

Consistent with these findings, dual immunofluorescence staining revealed that OSK pretreatment reduced LPS-induced microglial activation, as evidenced by decreased IBA1 and CD68 intensities in both BV2 and primary microglia (Fig. 1G–J). OSK expression alone did not alter microglial activation under resting conditions (Figure S3E–F).

Together, these data demonstrate that OSK reprogramming maintains microglial identity while effectively suppressing inflammatory activation, especially under stimulated conditions, establishing a neuroprotective microglial phenotype.

OSK preserves neuronal identity and protects against apoptosis

We next examined the effects of OSK on neuronal identity and survival in HT22 hippocampal cells and primary neurons. OSK expression was robustly induced without activation of pluripotency-associated factors (c-Myc, Nanog), thereby avoiding dedifferentiation (Fig. 2A–B, upper panel; Figure S4). Instead, neuronal markers including Dcx, Map2, and Tubb3 were upregulated, and immunofluorescence revealed enhanced MAP2 intensities, confirming preservation of neuronal identity (Fig. 2A–B, lower panel; Fig. 2C–F). Since synaptic integrity is central to learning and memory [40, 41], we assessed dendritic protrusion density at DIV18, a critical stage of synapse maturation. OSK significantly increased protrusion density (Fig. 2G–H) and increased neurite branching complexity (Figure S5A–B). Functionally, OSK reduced carbonyl cyanide metachlorophenyl-hydrazone (CCCP)-induced apoptosis in HT22 neurons, lowering both early- and late-apoptotic populations while increasing viability (Fig. 2I–J). In primary neurons, OSK suppressed cleaved caspase-3 and modestly upregulated Bcl-2 upon apoptotic challenge, with minimal effects under basal conditions (Fig. 2K–L; Figure S5C–D). Moreover, OSK significantly increased the expression of synaptophysin (SYP) and PSD95 in primary neurons (Fig. 2K–L; Figure S5C–D).

Indirectly, conditioned medium from OSK-pretreated LPS-activated microglia reduced neuronal cleaved caspase-3 levels (Fig. 2M–N), while medium from basal OSK-microglia showed no effect (Figure S5E–F). Interestingly, conditioned medium from OSK-pretreated microglia increased SYP and PSD95 in primary neuron (Fig. 2M–N; Figure S5E–F). In APPswe-mutant SH-SY5Y cells, conditioned medium from OSK-pretreated BV2 microglia slightly enhanced neuronal viability and markedly reduced secretion of the pathogenic Aβ42 isoform (Figure S5G–H; Figure S6A–B).

Collectively, these results show that OSK reprogramming maintains neuronal identity, enhances synaptic structure and marker expression, and protects neurons both intrinsically and via modulation of microglial inflammatory signaling.

Discussion

Here, we show that brain-restricted expression of OSK restores neuroimmune homeostasis and ameliorates both central and systemic manifestations of Alzheimer’s disease in APP/PS1 mice (Graphical abstract), a model that recapitulates key pathological features of AD. Notably, OSK-mediated reprogramming reverses disease-associated phenotypes without inducing pluripotency or compromising cellular identity. The observed reduction in Aβ burden, attenuation of microglial activation, and preservation of neuronal and synaptic integrity collectively support a model in which partial epigenetic reprogramming functionally reconstitutes the diseased brain.

Epigenetic dysregulation is increasingly recognized as a central driver of neurodegeneration, impairing adaptive stress responses and limiting endogenous repair capacity [49]. Importantly, such alterations remain reversible, providing a mechanistic entry point for intervention [50]. While pluripotent reprogramming via OSKM resets the epigenome, its clinical translation is constrained by dedifferentiation and tumorigenic risk [25, 51, 52]. Recent studies indicate that epigenetic rejuvenation can be uncoupled from loss of somatic identity [53], and that transient or partial reprogramming is sufficient to restore cellular function and reverse aging-associated phenotypes [23, 54, 55]. In the nervous system, partial reprogramming has been shown to improve cognitive performance and neuronal resilience [56]. Our findings extend this framework by demonstrating that sustained OSK expression, in the absence of c-Myc, is sufficient to restore both neuronal and microglial function in a disease context. Additionally, a modest attenuation of astrocyte activation was observed based on GFAP in vivo; however, given additional indicators were not assessed, the extent of astrocytic involvement requires further investigation using additional markers.

Mechanistically, our data identify Tet2-dependent DNA demethylation as a central mediator of OSK-induced functional recovery. RRBS revealed reversal of AD-associated hypermethylation at genes involved in synaptic plasticity, axon guidance, and PI3K/Akt signaling (including Rbfox3/NeuN, Sema6b, Sptbn4, and Mest) [57–60]. Our data indicate that Rbfox3 mRNA and NeuN protein levels were increased following OSK treatment, consistent with the established role of Rbfox3 as a marker of neuronal identity and its relevance to AD. This was accompanied by downregulation of DNMT3a and upregulation of Tet2, and loss-of-function experiments in vitro demonstrated that Tet2 is required for the anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects of OSK. These findings support a model in which OSK reprogramming restores functional gene expression programs through active DNA demethylation. However, the requirement for Tet2 in vivo remains to be established. Future studies employing inducible or cell-type–specific Tet2 deletion will be necessary to determine whether epigenetic remodeling is causally required for the behavioral and neuropathological improvements observed.

An unexpected finding of this study is that brain-restricted OSK reprogramming exerts systemic effects on skeletal homeostasis. Our prior work has suggested that the brain–bone axis is mediated, at least in part, by extracellular vesicle (EV) signaling [10–13]. Consistent with this concept, we show that OSK reshapes the cargo of brain-derived EVs, including reduction of miR-483-5p, thereby restoring osteogenic differentiation and suppressing adipogenic commitment in BMSCs. These results extend recent reports demonstrating that neuronal epigenetic reprogramming can reverse age-associated decline in cognitive function and cellular phenotypes [61], and suggest that such interventions may also affect microenvironment and propagate across organ systems. This cross-tissue effect highlights a previously underappreciated systemic dimension of epigenetic reprogramming in neurodegenerative disease.

From a translational perspective, OSK-mediated partial reprogramming may offer a safer alternative to full OSKM induction, enabling rejuvenation of degenerative cells while minimizing the risk of dedifferentiation and tumorigenesis [62]. Advances in mRNA-based delivery systems provide a potential route for temporally controlled and tissue-specific expression of reprogramming factors [63], raising the possibility of developing precision epigenetic therapies for AD. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the present study focuses on relatively short-term outcomes following OSK induction; the durability of therapeutic effects, as well as potential delayed adverse events such as cellular identity drift or inflammatory rebound, remain to be determined. Second, we did not assess the impact of OSK in aged wild-type animals, and thus cannot distinguish disease-specific rescue from broader rejuvenation effects. Future studies in aged mice and larger animal models will be necessary to determine the generalizability and translational potential of OSK-mediated reprogramming. Finally, whether transient, inducible, or intermittent OSK expression is sufficient to achieve sustained benefit represents an important question for future investigation.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings demonstrate that brain-restricted OSK reprogramming restores neuroimmune function and improves both central and systemic manifestations of Alzheimer’s disease. Through Tet2-dependent DNA demethylation, OSK reactivates youthful epigenetic programs, leading to enhanced neuronal integrity, reduced neuroinflammation, and improved cognitive performance. Notably, this intervention also modulates the brain–bone axis via extracellular vesicle signaling, thereby alleviating AD-associated bone loss. Together, these results position partial epigenetic reprogramming as a strategy to couple central nervous system rejuvenation with peripheral tissue homeostasis, and highlight its potential as a disease-modifying approach for Alzheimer’s disease and related comorbidities.
