The Influence of Pre‐Exercise Snacking on Glycemic Control, Physical Activity, Sleep, and Subjective Mood and Hunger During a 30‐h Fast: A Randomized Crossover Study
Authors: Landon S Deru, Katelynn E Hales, Elizabeth Z Gipson, Hennessey Chan, Bruce W Bailey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGlycemic control is vital to health and disease prevention. A randomized crossover design assessed the effects of fasting alone, fasting with exercise, and fasting with a pre‐exercise snack on beta‐hydroxybutyrate (BHB) and glucose concentrations, hunger, mood, physical activity, and sleep in 30 participants (16 male). BHB concentrations were elevated in the exercise condition compared to the control at 24 h (p < 0.01), continuing through the end of the intervention (p < 0.01). BHB concentrations were also elevated in the exercise condition compared to the pre‐exercise snack condition at 22 h (p < 0.01) and 24 h (p = 0.01) with no differences in BHB between the pre‐exercise snack condition and control. There was no main effect of condition on glucose concentrations during the 30‐h fast (p = 0.48). However, a condition by time interaction (p < 0.01) revealed elevated glucose levels immediately after exercise (p < 0.01) and lower glucose levels at hour 24 in the exercise‐only condition compared to control (p < 0.05). Hunger ratings were lower at 21 h in both exercise conditions immediately following the bout (p < 0.01), but similar at all other time‐points. Moderate physical activity was greatest during the pre‐exercise snack condition (p = 0.02). Sleep and mood parameters did not differ between conditions. Snacking before exercise produced comparable levels of BHB to a water‐only fast. After fasted exercise, BHB decreased, and glucose increased compared to water‐only fasting. Hunger was lower following exercise. Snacking before exercise could be a viable alternative for maintaining glycemic control while temporarily reducing subjective hunger compared to a water‐only fast.Keywords: intermittent fasting, ketosis, metabolic health, physical activity, tolerability

Introduction

The prevalence of lifestyle‐related chronic diseases is currently estimated to account for nearly two‐thirds of deaths worldwide. (Prevention CfDCa) Conditions such as Alzheimer's disease (now commonly referred to as Type 3 diabetes) (Kalra et al. 2021), cardiovascular disease (Murray and Rosenbloom 2018), type 2 diabetes (Drury et al. 1941), and obesity (Balasse et al. 1978) exhibit a strong connection with poor glycemic control. Kalra et al. (Kalra et al. 2021) argues that the loss of metabolic flexibility, the ability to switch between oxidation of glucose and fatty acids, plays a central role in the development of several common chronic diseases.

In response to this health challenge, numerous lifestyle intervention strategies have emerged to enhance metabolic flexibility and promote improved overall well‐being. Intermittent fasting is one such strategy, involving complete caloric restriction for certain periods of time using a variety of timing strategies. This approach leads to lower circulating glucose and liver glycogen levels within 10–14 h, stimulating the upregulation of fatty acids for metabolic fuel (Murray and Rosenbloom 2018).

Lipolysis, the process of obtaining energy from adipose tissue (Drury et al. 1941; Balasse et al. 1978; Sherrill et al. 1998), begins by breaking triglycerides into their two components: fatty acids and glycerol (Betts and Desaix 2017). The fatty acids undergo beta‐oxidation to generate acetyl CoA which then enters the Krebs cycle to produce ATP (Betts and Desaix 2017). However, when the amount of acetyl CoA produced exceeds the availability of oxaloacetate—derived from glucose or other glucogenic precursors—entry into the Krebs cycle is limited. As a result, excess acetyl CoA is diverted toward ketogenesis, leading to the formation of ketone bodies. This metabolic shift allows the resulting ketones to be transported in the blood to various target organs where they can be used for energy (Betts and Desaix 2017). Beta‐hydroxybutyrate (BHB) is one of the commonly measured ketones in the blood because it is the most abundant, providing an initial indication of the metabolic transition from glucose to fatty acid oxidation. BHB concentrations ≥ 0.5 mmol/L typically indicate a minimum threshold for this transition, a state referred to as ketosis (L. Deru et al. 2020).

Despite the potential benefits, adhering to a fasting regimen can be difficult, as some individuals may experience negative side effects (Lee et al. 2020; Malinowski et al. 2019). Heilbronn et al. (Heilbronn et al. 2005) found that hunger on fasting days did not decrease over time and suggested that a small meal on fasting days may be a more sustainable option. However, even a small meal may interrupt the beneficial metabolic changes that take place during fasting (Mattson et al. 2018).

Breaking a fast with mixed macronutrient caloric intake typically causes cells of the body to revert to primarily utilizing glucose for energy rather than ketones (Longo and Mattson 2014; de Toledo et al. 2019). However, there is some evidence that exercise may allow a fasted‐like metabolic state to be maintained even with the ingestion of some food. Morrison et al. (Morrison et al. 2018) demonstrated that both acute and chronic endurance exercise increased glucose clearance and produced marked reductions in postprandial insulin, indicating improved glycemic control. These findings align with research suggesting that glucose uptake during exercise can occur independently of insulin (Reichkendler et al. 2013), and that glucose clearance during exercise increases (Richter et al. 2001). Additionally, physical activity accelerates the depletion of glycogen stores, facilitating a more rapid metabolic switch from glucose to ketones (L. S. Deru, Bikman, et al. 2021a). However, maintaining a state of ketosis during and after fasted exercise and evaluating whether ketosis can be quickly recovered by exercise following a mid‐fast snack has not been evaluated thoroughly. Therefore, this study aimed to examine how exercising in a fasted state influences capillary BHB and glucose levels as well as subjective ratings of hunger and mood states. It also sought to describe how consuming a modest mid‐fast snack followed by exercise influences these biomarkers as well as subjective hunger, mood, and objective physical activity and sleep patterns.

Methods

Design

A randomized crossover design with counterbalanced treatment conditions was used to compare the influence of fasting alone (control), fasting with moderate‐intensity exercise (exercise), and fasting with the addition of a snack immediately prior to moderate‐intensity exercise (snack/exercise) on capillary blood BHB and glucose levels, as well as on subjective hunger and mood. Approval from the University's Institutional Review Board was obtained prior to initiating any aspect of this study.

Participants completed three treatment conditions, with a 5–7‐day washout period between each session. Using randomizer.org, the condition order was randomly assigned to participant numbers before the study commenced. Participant numbers were assigned chronologically based on the time they joined the study. Prior to each laboratory session, participants were screened for any contraindications to participation. The following outcome variables were measured: capillary BHB ketone levels, capillary glucose levels, and rate of perceived exertion (RPE) during exercise, subjective hunger and mood ratings, and objective physical activity and sleep measurements.

Participants

Healthy adults aged 18 and older who had been weight stable (±3% body weight) for the past 3 months were recruited for this study. Participants were required to participate in moderate physical activity without restrictions for the study. Any “yes” answer to the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR‐Q) (Thomas et al. 1992) resulted in exclusion. Other exclusion criteria included having a known chronic or metabolic disease; taking medications that altered metabolism or appetite (Verhaegen and Van Gaal 2017); pregnant, lactating, or postmenopausal women (Morrow et al. 1981); orthopedic impairments; practicing a restrictive diet; fasting more than once per week; eating disorders; food allergies; irregular sleep patterns; an underweight or obese BMI; and caffeine consumption greater than 60 mg per day (Johnston et al. 2003).

Screening and Orientation

Potential participants for the study were sent an online survey to ensure they met inclusion criteria. Candidates were asked to report any food allergies, complete a food preference questionnaire, and the PAR‐Q (Warburton et al. 2011). The food preference questionnaire was used to ensure that participants would eat standardized meals and snacks. Qualifying candidates were invited to participate in the study. Prior to participation in any aspect of this study, potential participants were informed of the main purpose of the study and familiarized with testing procedures. Informed consent was given by all participants. Training for proper portable ketone/glucose meter use took place in accordance with manufacturer guidelines (Abbott Laboratories, Abington, UK), and participants were given a copy of these testing instructions. Using Qualtrics' online survey software (Qualtrics.com), participants logged their capillary ketone and glucose blood levels and filled out the questionnaires relating to mood and hunger.

Treatment Sessions

All treatment conditions consisted of a thirty‐hour water‐only fast beginning at 5:00 p.m. and ending at 11:00 p.m. the following day. Participants arrived at the laboratory having not eaten for the previous 4 hours. During the first visit, height and weight were assessed and recorded. Baseline capillary BHB and glucose measurements and a hunger/mood survey were taken and a standardized meal was then provided regardless of the type of intervention, which initiated the thirty‐hour fast. Participants were instructed to stay hydrated throughout the fast and were only allowed to drink water. Chewing gum and non‐caloric beverages were not allowed.

After 20 h of fasting, participants returned to the laboratory where capillary ketone and glucose measurements were taken, and mood and hunger ratings were assessed. Following these measurements, participants proceeded with either the exercise, snack/exercise, or control condition. During the control condition, participants received a DXA scan in place of exercise. 1 hour after arriving in the laboratory (21 h of fasting), the participants again took capillary ketone and glucose measurements along with a hunger/mood survey. They were then allowed to return to their normal daily activities until the termination of their fast at 11:00 p.m. that evening. For the snack/exercise condition, the pre‐exercise snack was ingested 15 min prior to exercise. Exercise took place at the same time for both the exercise and snack/exercise conditions. After exercising, participants took capillary ketone and glucose measurements again (at 21 h of fasting) along with a hunger/mood survey. Finger pricks and surveys were taken again at 22, 24, 27, and 30 h in each fasting condition, for a total of 7 times.

Standardized Meal and Pre‐Exercise Snack

The standardized meal used to initiate each fast contained 25% of the participants' estimated daily caloric requirements. Daily caloric requirements for each participant were estimated using the following equation: Basal metabolic rate × 1.55. (National Institutes of Health) Meals were standardized based on macronutrient content: 48% CHO, 36% fat, and 16% protein. The same pre‐fast meal was given for each intervention and participants were instructed to consume all the food provided. The pre‐exercise snack provided 10% of participants' estimated daily caloric requirements and consisted of whole fat chocolate milk with a similar macronutrient profile to the standardized meal.

Exercise Protocol

The exercise intervention consisted of a brisk walk or a light jog on a treadmill at a grade and speed that brought the participants' heart rate reserve (HRR) to 60% (Solheim et al. 2014). Participants exercised in this manner until an equivalent number of calories were expended as would be consumed in the standardized snack, regardless of whether a snack was consumed before the exercise (Hess et al. 2016). Hunger and Mood Surveys

Mood and energy levels were assessed using the validated Brunel mood scale (BRUMS) (Rohlfs et al. 2005). The 24‐item BRUMS measures six mood states (tension, depression, anger, vigor, fatigue, and confusion) through a self‐report inventory on a 5‐point Likert scale. Hunger, thirst, and stomach discomfort were also assessed using the Likert scale. Surveys were administered at 0, 20, 21, 22, 24, 27, and 30 h of fasting during all conditions.

Objective Physical Activity and Sleep

The ActiGraph GT3X (ActiGraph, Pensacola, FL) accelerometer was worn on the non‐dominant wrist and used to objectively measure physical activity and sleep patterns (Plasqui and Westerterp 2007; Masse et al. 2005). Participants were required to wear the accelerometer for the duration of the intervention, only taking it off for bathing. Non‐wear time was conservatively defined as 20 or more minutes of consecutive zeros. Data were collected in 60‐s epochs. Vigorous activity was defined by observing > 5999 activity counts/minute, moderate activity was defined by observing 2020‐5999 activity counts/minute, combined moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity (MVPA) was defined by observing 2020 to > 5999 counts/min, light activity (LIGHT) was defined by observing 100–2019 counts/min, and sedentary activity (SED) was defined by observing 0–99 counts/min (Matthew 2005).

The ActiGraph GT3X accelerometer was also used to objectively measure variables related to sleep. “Bedtime” was defined as the time the participant went to bed with the intention to sleep. “Wake time” was defined as the time the participant got out of bed with the intention to start their day. Sleep log data was used to verify sleep and wake times. Actigraph data were used for data analysis. Data were collected in 60‐s epochs, and the sleep log and Actigraph data were used to determine sleep onset time and final wake time. Bedtime was said to occur after the participant had taken no steps and had registered no activity velocity for a period of 5 min. The data were then searched for 2 h longer to verify that the participant was truly asleep. Wake time was said to occur when the participant registered over 5 steps per minute and registered activity velocity for a period of 5 min. This activity needed to continue at regular patterns for wake time to be verified. Sleep was analyzed using ActiLife software version 6.3 (Ancoli‐Israel et al. 2003). Specific sleep measurements being reported and evaluated include efficiency, wake after sleep onset, latency, and total sleep time.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size for this study was calculated a priori by estimating the effect size for the difference in area under the ketone curve between conditions to be 0.66 (L. S. Deru, Bikman, et al. 2021a). Using this estimate, and setting alpha at 0.05 and beta at 0.2 (80% power), a minimum of 22 participants was required for a paired design. To ensure adequate power and account for potential dropout, 30 participants were enrolled, which provided approximately 88% power. Participants were recruited until 30 people completed the study which resulted in 31 total participants beginning the interventions because of one drop out. Analyses were completed using SAS statistical software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc. Cary NC). Participant data are reported as means and standard deviations. A repeated measures mixed‐effects ANOVA was utilized to assess the main and interactive effects of condition and period (or time of day) on glucose, BHB, mood, physical activity and sleep. Post hoc t‐tests were performed if significant interactive effects were observed. Similarly, a mixed‐effects ANOVA was used to analyze the impact of fasting and exercise on glucose, BHB, physical activity, and sleep within each intervention period. An alpha level of 0.05 was used for this analysis.

The length of time spent exercising during the exercise conditions was 32.55 ± 5.17 min. Glucose

Table 2 displays the concentrations of glucose over time in each condition. There was no significant main effect of condition for overall glucose concentrations over the 30‐h fast (p = 0.4749, F = 0.75). However, there was a significant condition by time interaction (p < 0.0001, F = 6.15; see Figure 2). Follow‐up analysis demonstrated that there was no difference between conditions in glucose concentration except for two time points. First, immediately after exercise glucose concentrations were elevated in both the exercise and snack/exercise conditions compared to control (p < 0.001, t = 5.44 and p < 0.001, t = 5.61, respectively). In addition, at hour 24 of fasting, glucose concentrations were significantly lower in the exercise only condition compared to control (−4.08 ± 0.36 mg/dL, p = 0.049, t = 1.90).

Subjective Hunger/Thirst/Mood Ratings

There was no significant main effect of subjective hunger observed between conditions (p = 0.3426, F = 1.08), but there was a significant main effect of time with hunger increasing over the course of the fast (p < 0.0001, F = 132.40). As depicted in Figure 4, there was a significant condition by time interaction (p = 0.0106, F = 2.20) that occurred at 21 h of fasting when subjective ratings of hunger were significantly lower (compared to control) post exercise in both the exercise and snack/exercise conditions (p = 0.0043, t = 2.87 and p = 0.0003, t = 3.68, respectively) with no difference between the exercise and snack/exercise conditions (p = 0.4234, t = 0.80). The suppression of subjective hunger after exercise disappeared by hour 22 of the fast (about an hour after exercise) and there was no difference between conditions for the rest of the 30‐h fast.

Similar to hunger, there was no main effect of condition for thirst (p = 0.2078, F = 1.60); however, there was a significant main effect of time (p = 0.0011, F = 3.83). There was also a significant condition by time interaction (p = 0.0056, F = 2.37), with subjective thirst increasing after exercise in the exercise condition (3.35 ± 1.99 cm) compared to the control condition (1.65 ± 1.37 cm) (p < 0.0001, t = 5.33) and the snack/exercise condition (2.42 ± 1.64 cm, p = 0.0227, t = 1.94).

Stomach discomfort (p < 0.0001, F = 39.34), anger (p < 0.0001, F = 18.56), confusion (p < 0.0001, F = 7.72), depression (p < 0.0001, F = 14.87), and fatigue (p < 0.0001, F = 55.40) all increased over the course of the fast, but did not differ significantly between conditions (p = 0.3088, F = 1.19; p = 0.1910, F = 1.69; p = 0.7753, F = 0.26; p = 0.7698, F = 0.26; p = 0.6044, F = 0.51, respectively). In contrast, there was no difference in subjective vigor between conditions (p = 0.9598, F = 0.04), but vigor did decrease significantly over the course of each fast (p < 0.0001, F = 41.72). Finally, there was no difference in subjective measures of tension between conditions (p = 0.7116, F = 0.34) or over time (p = 0.0563, F = 2.06).

There was no condition by time interactions for stomach discomfort (p = 0.1545, F = 1.42), anger (p = 0.0714, F = 1.66), confusion (p = 0.7540, F = 0.70), depression (p = 0.6065, F = 0.84), fatigue (p = 0.3137, F = 1.15), or vigor (p = 0.6234, F = 0.83). There was a condition by time interaction for tension (p = 0.0463, F = 1.79) which occurred at 27 h of fasting. At this timepoint, tension increased in the snack/exercise condition to a rating of 1.61 ± 2.60 above the exercise condition which was rated at 0.57 ± 1.03 (p = 0.0417, t = 0.13). Tension returned to not being different between these two conditions by the 30 h timepoint (t = 0.92, p = 0.36).

Physical Activity and Sleep

Objective physical activity measurements showed no difference between conditions for total kilocalories expended each day (p = 0.0531, F = 3.10), total sedentary time (p = 0.4440, F = 0.82), total light physical activity time (p = 0.0654, F = 2.88), and total vigorous physical activity time (p = 0.3982, F = 0.93) (Table 4). The total moderate physical activity time was different between conditions (p = 0.0219, F = 4.10) being highest in the snack/exercise condition (99.47 ± 5.62 kcal or 416.18 ± 23.51 kJ), followed by the exercise condition (90.96 ± 5.88 kcal or 380.58 ± 24.60 kJ), and finally by the control condition (84.35 ± 5.61 kcal or 352.92 ± 23.47 kJ). This contributed to a significant difference in total moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity between conditions (p = 0.0106, F = 4.94) with the highest outcome in snack/exercise condition (104.56 ± 5.93 kcal or 437.48 ± 24.81 kJ), followed by the exercise condition (94.92 ± 6.24 kcal or 397.15 ± 26.11 kJ), and finally by the control condition (85.76 ± 5.92 kcal or 358.82 ± 24.77 kJ).

Heart Rate (HR) and Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE)

The average HR in the exercise condition was 166.9 ± 13.8 and in the snack/exercise condition was 168.2 ± 23.2 representing no difference in heart rates between these conditions (F = 0.22, p = 0.6435). Perceived exertion increased over the course of the exercise beginning at an average of 10.61 ± 1.77 and increasing to a peak average of 13.82 ± 2.52 (p < 0.0001, F = 16.01) (Table 5). The average RPE during exercise was 12.70 ± 2.32 in the exercise condition, and 12.25. ± 2.27 in the snack/exercise condition representing no difference between conditions (p = 0.2576, F = 1.31).

Discussion

The results suggest that exercising after consuming a modest snack during a fast lead to similar capillary BHB and glucose levels to those seen with a water‐only fast. The exercise session following the snack prevented the usual suppression in ketone production and utilization that typically occurs after refeeding; however, the pre‐exercise snack did not affect the subjective experience of the exercise or the remainder of the fast. There were no differences in perceived exertion, hunger, or mood with the addition of the snack. Additionally, while fasted exercise did increase BHB production beyond the levels achieved by fasting alone, this difference only became apparent 3 hours post‐exercise.

We hypothesized that exercise could mitigate the impact of a modest snack on BHB concentrations during a fast by minimizing the insulin response to the food and utilizing the ingested carbohydrates for energy, rather than allowing them to be taken up by the liver and inhibit BHB production. Our findings supported this hypothesis, showing that a fasted‐like ketone state can be rapidly reestablished when a fast is briefly interrupted with a modest mixed‐macronutrient snack quickly followed by exercise.

Koeslag et al. (Koeslag 1982) suggested that ketones tend to remain elevated for several hours after exercise, a phenomenon known as post‐exercise ketosis. During prolonged exercise, increased lipolysis leads to greater availability of fatty acids which are partially oxidized and diverted toward ketogenesis when the availability of glucogenic precursors is limited. However, these ketone substrates are also readily utilized by the body during the activity, resulting in only modest increases in ketone concentrations during exercise itself (Koeslag 1982). However, after exercise cessation, a sustained higher rate of ketone body formation is observed, leading to what is known as post‐exercise ketone excursion (Johnson and Walton 1971). It is important to note that the study by Koeslag et al. (Koeslag et al. 1985) did not examine the impact of exercise on ketone production in the context of fasting. This is a critical consideration when evaluating whether a fast‐like metabolic state can be maintained when a fast is interrupted with energy intake immediately followed by exercise.

Participants in both exercise conditions experienced a rise in glucose levels immediately following the exercise bout, although this increase from fasting levels was short‐lived. About an hour after exercise, glucose levels across the three conditions were no longer different. These results were somewhat surprising given that glucose utilization tends to balance hepatic glycogenolysis during exercise, resulting in relatively constant blood glucose concentrations (Coggan 1991; Adams 2013). The rise in glucose levels we observed may have been influenced by the metabolic state of our participants. Though one study reports unchanged glucose levels post‐exercise after an overnight fast (Morrison et al. 2018), a meta‐analysis reported a non‐significant increase in glucose concentrations post‐exercise after an overnight fast (Frampton et al. 2021). They suggested this may be because of lower glucose levels after an overnight fast. Our participants began exercise after 20 h of fasting which may have contributed to the significantly higher glucose levels seen after exercise. Although the same meta‐analysis reported decreased glucose concentrations after post‐prandial exercise (Frampton et al. 2021), it is possible that the 15‐min period between snack ingestion and exercise in our study was too short to meaningfully influence the fasted metabolic response, as insulin release may have been blunted by both the short postprandial period and by catecholamine‐mediated suppression during exercise (Coggan 1991; Gyntelberg et al. 1977). Given that participants were 20 h into a fast at the time of exercise, liver glycogen stores were likely diminished significantly, indicating that gluconeogenesis likely played a more significant role in sustaining elevated plasma glucose levels during exercise. The lactate generated by the exercise may have been a major gluconeogenesis precursor (Brooks et al. 2021).

The change in glucose concentrations appeared to mirror the self‐reported ratings of hunger. We hypothesized that exercise would suppress subjective hunger for a period of time following the activity. If exercise reduces hunger, it could be a useful strategy for making fasting more tolerable. Consistent with our hypothesis, we observed that exercise did reduce feelings of hunger during a fast; however, this effect was short‐lived, disappearing within an hour after the exercise bout. This transient suppression of hunger following exercise has been documented in several studies (Kawano et al. 2013; Bailey et al. 2021). The reduction in hunger may be influenced by elevated catecholamine levels in the blood and a temporary rise in blood sugar. Al‐Zubaidi et al. suggested that while increased blood glucose does not promote satiety, low blood glucose levels (< 85 mg/dL) are associated with increased hunger (Al‐Zubaidi et al. 2019). Therefore, even low‐intensity exercise might alleviate some of the discomfort associated with fasting, though the effect is brief. Given that hunger typically increases after 16 h of a 24‐h fast (Tinsley et al. 2018), incorporating exercise during periods of heightened hunger cues could help individuals extend their fasting duration.

Surprisingly, adding a modest snack before exercise did not improve feelings of hunger beyond what was achieved with exercise alone. Hunger ratings were significantly lower only immediately after participants exercised, regardless of whether they had consumed a snack beforehand. This suggests that exercise, rather than the snack, was the primary factor in reducing hunger. It is important to note that the results might have differed if a solid snack had been used instead of a liquid based where solid foods tend to suppress postprandial hunger more compared to liquid (Tieken et al. 2007).

In contrast to hunger, it is interesting to note that subjective ratings of mood and stomach discomfort did not differ between any of the conditions. The addition of a snack or exercise neither improved nor worsened these ratings compared to the control condition. These findings suggest that combining a snack with exercise may not enhance the tolerability of fasting.

Interestingly, we observed significant differences in moderate physical activity between conditions, with the highest levels seen in the snack/exercise condition, followed by the exercise condition, and then the control. It was interesting to observe the difference between the snack/exercise and exercise conditions, where the snack appeared to have a small impact on increasing overall activity levels after exercise.

Whereas underfeeding is typically associated with decreases in non‐exercise activity thermogenesis (NEAT) (Levine 2002), this does not fully explain why a relatively small snack during a fast would lead to significant differences in activity. Previous studies, such as one by Klempel et al. (Klempel et al. 2010) and Deru et al. (L. S. Deru et al. 2024) found no significant differences in step counts between fasting and feeding days in an alternate‐day modified fasting protocol. Therefore, more research is needed to understand the relationship between fasting and spontaneous physical activity.

We also hypothesized that adding a snack prior to exercising in a fasted state would make the exercise more tolerable. Surprisingly, there was no difference in the self‐reported difficulty between the fed or fasted exercise. This may be because the exercise intensity was light enough that energy could be produced at a rate that was sustainable by fat oxidation, existing glycogen stores, and gluconeogenesis in the liver. Similar studies using continuous aerobic exercise protocols have also reported nonsignificant differences in RPE between fed and fasted participants (Aziz et al. 2010; Trabelsi et al. 2012). However, differences in RPE may emerge at higher exercise intensities, where glucose becomes more crucial as a substrate for ATP production. For instance, greater RPE in a fasted state compared to a fed state has been observed during protocols requiring maximal effort, such as sprint‐type exercises (Chtourou et al. 2011).

These findings align with research suggesting that continuous aerobic exercise lasting less than 90 min often does not benefit from carbohydrate intake, as the body's glycogen stores are typically sufficient to sustain the activity (Pochmuller et al. 2016). Although participants in the current study likely began exercise with glycogen stores somewhat depleted because of fasting, most had not reached the ketosis threshold, indicating that some liver glycogen was still available. As a result, skipping a snack shortly before continuous aerobic exercise during a fast does not appear to worsen the subjective experience of the exercise.

Our laboratory demonstrated that initiating a fast with exercise can induce a state of nutritional ketosis approximately 3.5 h sooner than fasting alone (L. S. Deru, Bikman, et al. 2021b). This finding underscores that metabolic substrate switching associated with fasting occurs more rapidly when exercise is included at the beginning of the fast, which prompted our current study to examine the effects of exercising later in the fast on glycemic control. Additionally, our previous work indicated that subjective hunger and mood ratings remained similar regardless of whether exercise was incorporated at the start of the fast, suggesting that the metabolic benefits of fasting can be enhanced without increasing perceived hunger or difficulty.

Finally, the objective sleep parameters of sleep latency, sleep efficiency, total minutes in bed, total sleep time, wake after sleep onset, number of awakenings, and the movement index were not different between conditions. Recent work from our laboratory supports these findings and demonstrated that alternate‐day modified fasting also had no significant impact on these same sleep parameters (L. S. Deru et al. 2024). Many studies have shown that intermittent fasting (IF) does not appear to have a significant impact on the specific aspects of sleep quality being measured. Research examining alternate‐day fasting and time‐restricted eating has assessed both objective and subjective sleep outcomes in individuals with poor sleep quality, and no significant differences were observed when compared to non‐fasting groups (Mulas et al. 2023; Gabel et al. 2019). Although intermittent fasting can be implemented in various forms, the current body of evidence suggests that it does not lead to measurable improvements in sleep quality.

When combined with the current study, these results suggest that exercise may temporarily mitigate the drive to consume food during a fast, a finding supported by other research (Bhutani et al. 2013; Martins et al. 2015). These outcomes provide valuable insights for individuals practicing intermittent fasting and time‐restricted eating, indicating that exercise during fasting may preserve the metabolic benefits of fasting with minimal impact on subjective hunger, even when the fast is interrupted by a snack and followed by exercise. As a result, this strategy may allow some metabolic benefits of fasting to be achieved in a shorter duration or to a greater extent.

Limitations and Strengths

Although the results from this study are informative and help to understand several aspects of fasting, there are limitations to consider. First, our results are limited to acute fasting, and we are unable to describe how these results may change with repeated fasts over time. Second, each fast was completed under free living conditions, which lacks the control of a laboratory environment. Although this introduces some variability between fasts, the results are more generalizable since behaviors were not constrained. Condition order was randomized and any differences in behavior between conditions should be random and equal between conditions. Third, we did not control menstrual cycle phases of the women in the study. This may have resulted in more variability in appetite hormones since the hormone concentrations change with each phase of the menstrual cycle (Brennan et al. 2009). Future work should include tracking of the menstrual cycle. Additionally, glucose and BHB were the only physiological measurements taken in this study. Collecting blood samples would better describe the time course of metabolic changes during the fast, especially within the first few hours after exercise. Finally, this study is limited by the absence of a fed‐state exercise‐only group (e.g., exercise 2 hours after a meal), which reflects a common real‐world scenario. Including this condition would have allowed for direct comparison with the fasted exercise + snack group, which showed the greatest ketone response. As such, it remains unclear how much greater this response is compared to exercise in a typical fed state.

Despite these limitations, this study adds to the current literature on fasting and exercise. Whereas most research has evaluated the impact of fasting or exercise on glucose and BHB separately, this study is one of the first to evaluate these behaviors in combination and with the variable of adding food. Although much of the existing literature captures the acute impact (30 min–3 h) of exercise while fasting on glucose and BHB, this study evaluated the impact 10 h after the initiation of the exercise. This enabled us to describe the prolonged effects of exercise. Finally, the practice of intermittent fasting and time restricted eating are very popular because of their potential to improve body composition and metabolic health. This study adds to the current body of literature by describing how exercise could be used in conjunction with fasting to potentially enhance the benefit of this behavior while transiently mitigating the negative effects of hunger.

Conclusion

Consumption of a snack immediately before exercise during a fast produced comparable BHB levels to a water‐only fast without exercise. BHB was significantly higher in the exercise condition than control from 24 h to the end of the study, but the snack/exercise condition was not significantly different from control at any time point. In the conditions that exercised, glucose was significantly higher immediately following the bout, but only for a couple hours. Hunger ratings were transiently lower following exercise regardless of whether a snack was consumed. Exercising without a snack while fasting may be the best protocol to maximize ketone production. These findings suggest that a snack followed by exercise during a fast may be a viable alternative to a traditional water‐only fast. However, the fact that hunger, mood, and stomach discomfort ratings were not different between any conditions calls into question the assumption that a snack/exercise fasting protocol is in fact easier to adhere to. Further research is needed to determine the effect of these interventions on other blood markers beyond ketones and glucose, such as satiety and hunger hormones, glycemic control hormones, as well as inflammatory markers. Additionally, the nature of the snack is also important to consider.
