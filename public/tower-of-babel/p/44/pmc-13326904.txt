Associations Between Serum Uric Acid Levels and Glaucoma: Results From the LifeLines Cohort and UK Biobank
Authors: Wei Liu, Ruru Guo, Mengxue Song, Zekai Chen, Siqi Wang, Valeria Lo Faro, Wenbo Zhang, Nigus Gebremedhin Asefa, Mark Eijgelsheim, Harold Snieder, Nomdo M. Jansonius
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to investigate the association between serum uric acid (UA) levels and primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), and to explore potential genetic links and causal relationships using large population-based cohorts.MethodsWe analyzed data from 7443 participants in the LifeLines cohort and 90,107 participants in the UK Biobank, all aged ≥55 years. Glaucoma status was determined based on questionnaires and International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes. Logistic regression was used to assess the association between serum UA levels and glaucoma, adjusting for demographic, clinical, and lifestyle covariates. Meta-analysis was performed to combine results across cohorts. Genetic correlation, polygenic risk score (PRS), and two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses were conducted to evaluate shared genetic architecture and causality.ResultsHigher serum UA levels were significantly associated with increased odds of glaucoma in the UK Biobank (per 0.1 mM increase, odds ratio [OR] = 1.06, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.02–1.11, P = 0.007 in the fully adjusted model), with consistent findings in meta-analysis. No association was observed in the LifeLines cohort. Genetic analyses revealed no evidence of shared genetic correlation or causality between UA and glaucoma. The UA PRS was not associated with glaucoma odds, and the glaucoma PRS showed no association with serum UA levels.ConclusionsElevated serum UA levels were phenotypically associated with glaucoma, but genetic analyses did not support a causal link. UA may reflect systemic oxidative stress rather than directly contribute to glaucoma pathogenesis. Further studies are needed to clarify underlying mechanisms and clinical relevance.

Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) is a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, characterized by progressive optic disc cupping and visual field loss, and its global burden has been increasing over recent decades.1 Due to extensive research, the etiology of POAG has been uncovered as complex, with the underlying mechanisms still largely unclear. Several factors, including increased intraocular pressure (IOP), genetic predisposition, and vascular dysfunction, have been implicated in its pathogenesis.2,3 Among these, oxidative stress has emerged as a key contributor to the development of POAG, with growing evidence suggesting that oxidative damage to retinal ganglion cells and the optic nerve plays a significant role in the progression of the disease.4–6

Uric acid (UA), a well-known antioxidant, has recently gained attention for its potential role in glaucoma pathophysiology. As an endogenous antioxidant, UA can scavenge free radicals and protect cells from oxidative damage.7,8 However, the exact relationship between UA levels and the risk of glaucoma remains controversial, as discussed in our recent review.9 Some studies have reported that serum UA levels are lower in patients with POAG compared with controls,10,11 whereas other research has found the reverse.12,13 These conflicting findings may reflect UA’s dual potential role in pathogenesis. On the one hand, a high constitutive level of UA could provide a persistent antioxidant defense, neutralizing free radicals, and thereby exerting a protective effect against glaucomatous damage. On the other hand, elevated UA might instead be a secondary response—recruited as an endogenous defense mechanism in the face of heightened oxidative stress—making it merely a proxy marker for the underlying pathological process, which would manifest as a harmful association in epidemiological analyses. Elucidating which of these mechanisms predominates is critical, and our study aims to help clarify this ambiguous relationship.

In this study, we conducted large-scale population-based analyses using data from the LifeLines Cohort Study and the UK Biobank to examine the association between UA levels and POAG. Given the complexity and genetic basis of both conditions,14–17 we also performed genetic correlation, polygenic risk score (PRS), and Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses to assess shared genetic architecture and potential causality. These analyses aim to clarify the role of UA in POAG and provide insights into its potential as a biomarker or therapeutic target for the disease.

Methods

Study Design and Samples

LifeLines is a multi-disciplinary prospective population-based cohort study examining in a unique three-generation design the health and health-related behaviors of 167,729 persons living in the North of the Netherlands. It uses a broad range of investigative procedures in assessing the biomedical, socio-demographic, behavioral, physical, and psychological factors that contribute to the health and disease of the general population, with a special focus on multi-morbidity and complex genetics. Baseline data were collected between 2006 and 2013, and follow-up visits are scheduled every 5 years for at least 30 years. During the first follow-up visit between 2014 and 2017, all participants aged 18 years and older (n = 110,759) completed a questionnaire covering the diagnosis and treatment of eye conditions. This included the National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire-25 (NEI-VFQ-25) along with specific questions related to common eye diseases. Detailed information on the study design, cohort structure, and data collection methods can be found in previous publications.18,19 The ethics committee of the University Medical Center Groningen (approval no. 2007/152) approved the LifeLines data collection, which adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

The UK Biobank is a large-scale prospective cohort study that recruited over 500,000 participants aged 37 to 73 years between 2006 and 2010 across the United Kingdom. It aims to improve the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of a wide range of diseases by collecting detailed touchscreen questionnaire data, various physical measurements, and biosamples following electronic informed consent. Between 2009 and 2010, a substudy focusing on eye health and vision was conducted in approximately 175,000 participants to complement the baseline dataset.20 The UK Biobank received approval from the National Health Service North West Multicentre Research Ethics Committee (06/MRE08/65) and the National Information Governance Board for Health and Social Care, and this research adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Further details about the UK Biobank, including the full study protocol and testing procedures, are available online (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk).

Data were extracted only from participants aged 55 years and over in both cohorts, as POAG is much more prevalent above that age.21,22

Glaucoma Phenotyping

In the LifeLines cohort, glaucoma was defined using a validated algorithm that combines self-reported glaucoma diagnosis, treatment history, and NEI-VFQ-25 scores.21 Participants were classified into four categories: definite, probable, possible glaucoma cases, or unaffected. Definite cases included individuals who had undergone incisional surgery for glaucoma, whereas probable cases were identified based on self-reported glaucoma (including the use of IOP-lowering medication or a history of glaucoma laser treatment) alongside a glaucoma-specific complaints pattern exceeding a predefined threshold in the NEI-VFQ-25. Possible cases included those who either self-reported glaucoma or exhibited a glaucoma-specific complaints pattern. The algorithm was applied to participants during their first follow-up visit with available eye-related questionnaire data. To focus on primary glaucoma, participants with self-reported macular degeneration or a history of (laser) surgery for diabetes or retinal detachment were excluded. We were not able to formally exclude angle closure glaucoma and pseudoexfoliation glaucoma, but these entities are relatively rare in the Dutch population.21

In the UK Biobank, glaucoma cases were identified based on self-reported diagnoses (collected via touchscreen questionnaires or verbal interviews), a history of surgical or laser treatment for glaucoma in either eye, or the presence of an International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code for POAG (ICD-9: 365.1; ICD-10: H40.1) in their linked hospital records at any time before or up to 1 year after the baseline assessment.23 To ensure specificity, participants with ICD codes for primary angle-closure glaucoma (ICD-9: 365.2; ICD-10: H40.2) or secondary glaucoma (ICD-9: 365.3–365.6; ICD-10: H40.3–H40.6, H42.*) were excluded from the analysis. Cases with an ICD code for glaucoma suspect (ICD-9: 365.0; ICD-10: H40.0) were classified as possible glaucoma cases.

Uric Acid Measurement

Serum UA levels were measured at baseline in both cohorts. In LifeLines, UA was quantified spectrophotometrically based on the uricase-peroxidase (Trinder) reaction, in which UA is oxidized by uricase to produce hydrogen peroxide, which then reacts with 4-aminophenazone to form a quinoneimine dye. In the UK Biobank, UA was assessed using an enzymatic uricase PAP method on a Beckman Coulter AU5800 analyzer (Beckman Coulter, USA).

Covariates

Age and age2 were added as covariates, with age2 calculated by subtracting the mean age from each participant’s age and squaring the result. Body mass index (BMI) was determined by dividing weight (kg) by height squared (m²). Hypertension was defined as having a systolic blood pressure of ≥140 millimeters of mercury (mm Hg), a diastolic blood pressure of ≥90 mm Hg, or the use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes was considered present if participants self-reported the condition, used antidiabetic medications, had a fasting blood glucose level ≥7.0 mM, or an HbA1c level ≥6.5%. Hyperlipidemia was defined as a total cholesterol level ≥6.2 mM or self-reported use of lipid-lowering medications. Chronic kidney disease was defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine equation. Education level was categorized differently in the LifeLines Cohort and UK Biobank: in LifeLines, education was classified into high (university education or higher vocational education), middle (secondary vocational education, senior general secondary education, or pre-university secondary education), and low (junior general secondary education, lower or preparatory secondary vocational education, primary education, or no education). In the UK Biobank, high education included college or university degrees, middle education included A levels/AS levels, National Vocational Qualification (NVQ) or Higher National Diploma/Higher National Certificate (HND/HNC) or equivalent, or other professional qualifications, whereas low education was defined as O levels/General Certificate of Secondary Education (GCSEs) or equivalent, Committees on Special Education (CSEs), or no qualifications. Smoking status was categorized as smoker (current or ever smoker) and nonsmoker.

Genetic Data

In the LifeLines cohort, genotypic data were collected in two stages. The first group of 15,638 participants was genotyped using the Illumina CytoSNP 12 version 2 array (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The second group, consisting of 38,030 participants, underwent genotyping with the Infinium Global Screening Array (GSA) MultiEthnic Disease Version. Both datasets underwent rigorous quality control procedures and were subsequently imputed using the Haplotype Reference Consortium panel r1.1 (http://www.haplotype-reference-consortium.org) via the Sanger imputation service. Further details on the genotyping, quality control, and imputation processes in LifeLines are available in previously published studies.19,24

In the UK Biobank, genetic data from 488,377 participants were obtained using 2 different genotyping arrays. The Affymetrix UK BiLEVE Axiom Array was used for 49,950 individuals, whereas the Affymetrix UK Biobank Axiom Array was used for the remaining 438,427 participants. Because 95% of the genetic markers were shared between the 2 arrays, quality control and imputation were carried out together. Imputation was performed using genetic information from reference panels including the 1000 Genomes Project, UK 10K, and the Haplotype Reference Consortium. Further details on the genotyping, imputation process, and quality control can be found in a previous publication.25

Linkage Disequilibrium Score Regression Analysis

Linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSR) was conducted to assess the bivariate genetic correlations between UA and glaucoma, using the open-source LDSC software version 1.0.1 (https://github.com/bulik/ldsc). The analysis utilized linkage disequilibrium (LD) scores required by the method, and based on the European ancestry subset of the 1000 Genomes Project reference panel.26 We focused on well-imputed single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from the HapMap3 dataset. To avoid potential pleiotropic effects, SNPs located within the major histocompatibility complex region were excluded. Additional filtration criteria included a minor allele frequency greater than 0.01 and an imputation score (INFO) above 0.9, where applicable in the summary dataset.

We used the genomewide association study (GWAS) summary statistics of UA from the CKDGen consortium, which included European ancestry data (n = 288,649) from LifeLines as well as other cohorts, but excluded the UK Biobank. For the outcome data, we used the GWAS summary statistics for POAG from a large-scale meta-analysis (n = 1,451,742), which incorporated both LifeLines and the UK Biobank. Additionally, we included GWAS summary statistics for IOP (n = 139,555) in our LDSR analysis, as it is the most critical and heritable continuous endophenotype for glaucoma. The specific GWAS summary data utilized for LDSR is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Polygenic Risk Scores Calculations

PRS were calculated using the SBayesRC method, which combines GWAS summary statistics with functional genomic annotations to enhance the polygenic prediction of complex traits.27 This method fits all imputed SNPs simultaneously and accounts for variations in both the proportion of causal variants and the magnitude of their causal effects across different annotations.

The GWAS summary statistics used to calculate the PRS for UA in the UK Biobank were the same as those used in the LDSR analysis. For the LifeLines cohort, we first extracted the effect specific to this cohort from the GWAS summary statistics using the R-package “Metasubtract,”28 before proceeding with the PRS calculation. Additionally, we excluded the LifeLines cohort and UK Biobank data separately and recalculated the POAG GWAS summary statistics, as described by Lo Faro et al.29 (see Supplementary Table S1), before calculating the PRS for POAG within each cohort. Genomic annotation data and LD reference for Europeans from the UK Biobank were used for the analysis. The PRS was standardized to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation (SD) of 1 before further analysis.

Two-Sample Mendelian Randomization Analysis

We performed MR analyses to investigate the potential causal relationship between UA and POAG. The analyses were carried out in R software version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the TwoSampleMR package (https://mrcieu.github.io/TwoSampleMR/). SNPs with genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10-8) were selected as instruments from GWAS data for both UA (as the exposure in forward MR) and POAG (as the exposure in reverse MR; see Supplementary Table S1). These SNPs were then clumped with an LD threshold of r2 < 0.001 within a 10 Mb window. The primary approach for causal effect estimation was the random-effect inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, which accounts for potential heterogeneity among the genetic instruments, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05. In addition, we used MR Egger and weighted median methods to assess pleiotropy effects. Cochran’s Q statistic was used to evaluate heterogeneity for both MR Egger and IVW methods. The intercept term in MR Egger regression was used to examine directional horizontal pleiotropy. The detailed MR analysis procedure that we applied has been described previously.30 We also performed a reverse MR analysis to explore the causal impact of POAG on UA to assess any reverse causality.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± SD, whereas skewed variables were summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were reported as counts (percentages). Logistic regression was used for binary outcomes (glaucoma), and linear regression was applied for continuous outcomes (UA). To better capture varying effects across different levels, continuous predictor variables (UA and PRSs) were categorized into tertiles. To evaluate dose-response relationships across tertiles, a formal trend test was performed by assigning the median value of each tertile as a continuous variable in the logistic regression model. Analyses were initially conducted on all glaucoma cases, followed by a secondary analysis excluding the possible cases, where glaucoma diagnosis was less certain. To cope with the relatively small sample size of the LifeLines cohort, we also performed a meta-analysis with the results from the UK Biobank and LifeLines, using the R package “metafor.” As no substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 0) was detected between the datasets, a fixed-effects model was applied.

Two regression models with different levels of covariate adjustment were used. Model 1 included adjustments for age, age2, and sex. Model 2 incorporated the same adjustments as model 1, along with additional covariates being BMI, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease, education level, and smoking status. For the PRS analysis, the model used in the UK Biobank included age, age2, sex, and the first 10 principal components, whereas in LifeLines, we further adjusted for the SNP array (CytoSNP or GSA).

Data analyses were conducted using R software version 4.0.3. A P value of < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Participants’ Baseline Characteristics

In total, our study included 7443 participants from LifeLines and 90,107 participants from the UK Biobank for phenotypic analysis (Fig. 1). In the LifeLines dataset, 28 participants (0.38%) were identified as having definite glaucoma, 81 (1.09%) as probable, and 519 (6.97%) as possible cases. The UK Biobank included 4948 individuals (5.49%) with glaucoma, of whom 449 (0.50%) were classified as possible cases. Table 1 provides a comparative overview of participant characteristics stratified by glaucoma status in both cohorts. Among the LifeLines participants, glaucoma was more prevalent in female subjects, whereas a slight male predominance was observed in the UK Biobank. Across both populations, those with glaucoma tended to be older, and were more frequently affected by comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and chronic kidney disease. Educational attainment was generally lower among glaucoma cases. Serum UA levels were higher in patients with glaucoma than in controls in the UK Biobank (0.323 ± 0.080 mM vs. 0.314 ± 0.080 mM), with a similar but much smaller difference observed in LifeLines (0.309 ± 0.072 mM vs. 0.308 ± 0.072 mM).

Associations of Serum Uric Acid With Glaucoma

In the LifeLines cohort, no significant association was observed between serum UA levels and glaucoma, regardless of whether all cases were included or possible cases were excluded (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S2). In contrast, in the UK Biobank, higher serum UA levels were significantly associated with an increased odds of having glaucoma. Specifically, in the full sample, the odds ratios (ORs) were 1.06 (95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.02–1.10, P = 0.004) in model 1 and 1.06 (95% CI = 1.02–1.11, P = 0.007) in model 2, per 0.1 mM increase in serum UA level. Similar associations were observed after excluding the 449 possible cases (model 1: OR = 1.05, 95% CI = 1.01–1.10, P = 0.02; model 2: OR = 1.05, 95% CI = 1.00–1.10, P = 0.04; see Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S2), suggesting a consistent relationship between higher UA levels and glaucoma. Meta-analysis confirmed the results (see Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S2), but was almost entirely driven by the UK Biobank findings due to the large difference in sample size between cohorts.

Associations of Serum Uric Acid Tertiles With Glaucoma

To explore potential nonlinear associations, we examined the relationship among serum UA levels, categorized into tertiles, and glaucoma odds (Table 2). In the LifeLines cohort, using the lowest tertile as the reference group, no significant differences in the odds of glaucoma were observed across the serum UA tertile categories. In contrast, findings from the UK Biobank showed that individuals in the highest UA tertile had a significantly elevated odds of glaucoma. This association remained consistent whether all glaucoma cases were analyzed (OR = 1.13, 95% CI = 1.04–1.22, P = 0.005 in model 2) or after excluding possible cases (OR = 1.11, 95% CI = 1.01–1.21, P = 0.03 in model 2). A formal test for trend showed a significant dose-response relationship (P for trend = 0.006 for all cases; P for trend = 0.03 after excluding possible cases in model 2).

Linkage Disequilibrium Score Regression Analysis

No significant genetic correlation was found between POAG and UA (rg = −0.04, P = 0.11); similarly, there was no significant genetic correlation between IOP and UA (rg = 0.00, P = 0.85).

Polygenic Risk Scores Analysis

For the PRS analysis, we included 4759 individuals from LifeLines and 88,597 from the UK Biobank with available genetic data (see Fig. 1). Strong associations were observed between each trait (UA and POAG) and their corresponding PRSs across both cohorts, with all P values < 2 × 10⁻¹⁶ (Supplementary Table S3), demonstrating the robust predictive power of the PRSs derived using SBayesRC. The PRSs accounted for 11.4% and 1.3% of the variance in UA and POAG, respectively, in the LifeLines cohort, and 7.4% and 2.4% in the UK Biobank.

Our study did not observe any significant overall association between the UA PRS and POAG in either cohort (Supplementary Table S4). When examining POAG odds across UA PRS tertiles, no clear associations were observed in either cohort, except for one nominally significant finding in LifeLines after excluding possible cases, where the middle tertile showed higher odds of POAG (OR = 2.21, 95% CI = 1.18–4.13, P = 0.01; Supplementary Table S5). However, given the limited sample size, this result should be interpreted with caution.

Similarly, no significant association was observed between the POAG PRS and serum UA levels in either LifeLines (b, 0.0014, 95% CI = −0.0004 to 0.0032, P = 0.13) or the UK Biobank (b, −0.0002, 95% CI = −0.0007 to 0.0002, P = 0.31). Additionally, UA concentrations did not differ meaningfully across tertiles of the POAG PRS (Supplementary Table S6).

Two-Sample Mendelian Randomization Analysis

To investigate potential causal relationships between UA and POAG, we performed a two-sample MR analysis. For UA, 73 independent SNPs (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) were selected as instrumental variables. The results showed no statistically significant causal effect of genetically predicted UA on POAG. Although substantial heterogeneity was detected, there was no evidence of directional pleiotropy (Supplementary Table S7).

A reverse MR analysis using 74 independent SNPs associated with POAG also did not support a causal effect of POAG on UA. Similar to the forward analysis, significant heterogeneity was present, but no clear indication of directional pleiotropy was found (see Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

In this study involving data from the LifeLines cohort and the UK Biobank, we observed a significant positive association between serum UA levels and the odds of POAG in the UK Biobank and in the combined meta-analysis. No such association was found in the LifeLines cohort. Genetic correlation and PRS analyses did not reveal shared genetic architecture between UA and POAG, and MR analyses provided no evidence for a causal relationship in either direction.

Several studies have explored the relationship between serum UA levels and POAG, but the findings have been inconsistent. Some studies, including those by Li et al.10 and Serra et al.,11 reported lower serum UA levels in patients with POAG compared with controls, with further reductions observed in more advanced disease stages. In contrast, Bouchemi et al.12 and Elisaf et al.13 found higher UA levels in patients with POAG, suggesting a possible elevation of UA in association with the disease. In our study, we found a significant positive association between higher serum UA levels and glaucoma in the UK Biobank cohort, and this finding was confirmed by meta-analysis combining data from both cohorts. However, no such association was observed in the LifeLines cohort, which might be attributed to its relatively small number of glaucoma cases, as well as potential differences in phenotyping, population characteristics, selection bias, or residual confounding. The biological mechanisms underlying the association between elevated UA levels and glaucoma remain speculative. UA is known to possess both antioxidant and pro-oxidant properties.31 One possible explanation is that elevated UA levels reflect a compensatory response to increased oxidative stress in glaucoma; as UA is consumed during the neutralization of reactive oxygen species, systemic levels may increase in an attempt to maintain redox balance. Alternatively, high UA levels might contribute directly to glaucomatous damage by promoting oxidative stress and impairing trabecular meshwork function,12 leading to increased IOP and optic nerve damage. Interestingly, recent literature suggests that UA may exhibit a U-shaped association with various diseases, including cardiovascular, renal, and pulmonary conditions, where both low and high serum UA levels are associated with increased risk.32 This duality may also apply to glaucoma, implying that both insufficient and excessive UA could be detrimental, depending on the oxidative and inflammatory context. Such a U-shaped relationship might partially explain the conflicting findings across previous studies and highlights the need for further research to elucidate optimal UA levels in the context of glaucoma risk.

Despite the positive association between higher serum UA levels and glaucoma observed in our phenotypic analyses, our genetic analyses did not support a shared or causal genetic basis for this relationship. This apparent inconsistency between phenotypic and genetic findings suggests that the observed association may be driven by non-genetic factors, such as environmental exposures, comorbid conditions, residual confounding, or medication use. It is also possible that elevated UA represents a biological marker or downstream consequence of disease rather than a causal factor. Another potential explanation is limited statistical power in the genetic analyses, particularly in MR, which relies on strong and valid instrumental variables. Although the GWAS datasets used were large, the proportion of variance in UA explained by the selected SNPs may still be modest, potentially limiting our ability to detect weak causal effects.33 In our MR analyses, although we observed significant heterogeneity, which may indicate horizontal pleiotropy, the consistent null findings across multiple sensitivity methods (weighted median and MR Egger) and the nonsignificant MR Egger intercept argue against directional pleiotropy biasing our results. Further studies integrating longitudinal data, tissue-specific expression, metabolomics profiling, and larger or more powerful genetic datasets including those that can better address pleiotropy (e.g. MR-PRESSO) may help clarify the mechanistic links between UA metabolism and glaucoma pathophysiology.

This study has several notable strengths. First, we utilized two large, well-characterized population-based cohorts with detailed phenotypic, biochemical, and genetic data, allowing for robust and comprehensive analysis. Second, the association between higher serum UA levels and glaucoma was observed in the UK Biobank and remained statistically significant in the meta-analysis. Notably, whereas the UK Biobank contributed the majority of weight to the meta-analysis, the direction of effect was concordant across the analyses. This pattern, although requiring cautious interpretation regarding consistency, supports further investigation into this association. Third, by incorporating multiple analytical approaches (including logistic regression, LDSR, PRS, and bidirectional MR), we were able to explore both phenotypic and genetic relationships between UA and glaucoma from complementary perspectives. However, some limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the phenotyping of glaucoma relied on self-reported diagnosis and registry-based codes, which may introduce misclassification bias, particularly for possible glaucoma cases. Second, the LifeLines cohort had a relatively small number of confirmed glaucoma cases, which may have limited the power to detect associations in that dataset. Third, our analysis focused on individuals of European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations. Fourth, serum UA levels were measured only at baseline and may not reflect long-term exposure or within-individual variability over time, which could lead to non-differential misclassification and potentially attenuate the observed associations. Nevertheless, our genetic analyses, which capture lifelong UA exposure and are not susceptible to single-measurement bias, yielded consistent null findings, supporting that UA is unlikely to be causally related to glaucoma. Fifth, the UK Biobank is not a truly random population sample due to volunteer bias, and our additional inclusion criteria may have introduced selection bias. However, such bias, if present, is likely non-differential with respect to exposure and outcome, which would tend to attenuate rather than inflate the observed associations.

Our findings suggest a potential association between elevated serum UA levels and the presence of POAG. This raises important considerations for the clinical evaluation of patients with glaucoma and highlights the potential role of systemic metabolic factors in disease pathogenesis. Although UA has traditionally been studied in the context of gout and metabolic syndrome, its dual antioxidant and pro-oxidant properties imply that abnormal UA levels—whether elevated or reduced—could have broader implications for ocular health. From a clinical perspective, monitoring serum UA levels may offer insight into a patient’s systemic oxidative stress burden. We acknowledge that the observed effect size is modest, and its clinical significance should not be overstated. More importantly, given the inconsistency of previous findings and the lack of supportive evidence from genetic analyses in our study, UA should not be considered a causal biomarker or therapeutic target for glaucoma at this stage. Instead, it may reflect broader metabolic disturbances that warrant further attention. Future studies are needed to determine whether UA plays a contributory or compensatory role in glaucoma development, and to clarify whether targeting UA levels could have any clinical utility in glaucoma prevention or management.

Conclusions

In this large population-based study, we observed a significant association between higher serum UA levels and the presence of POAG in the UK Biobank and in meta-analysis, whereas no such association was found in the LifeLines cohort, possibly due to limited statistical power. Genetic analyses—including LDSR, PRS, and MR—did not support a shared genetic basis or causal relationship between UA and POAG. These findings suggest that although elevated UA may be linked to glaucoma phenotypically, it is unlikely to be a direct causal factor. Further research is warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and to explore whether UA represents a modifiable marker of systemic oxidative stress in the context of glaucoma.
