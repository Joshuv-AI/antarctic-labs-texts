Empowering Saudi pharmacy students
Authors: Fahad M. Alzahrani, Bsmah S. Bojan, Haifa A. Fadil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:To promote healthy habits among pharmacy students through curriculum-embedded challenges. Student well-being is a growing concern in Saudi pharmacy education, with high rates of stress and poor health behaviors reported among students. Traditional interventions often fall short in bridging the gap between awareness and action.Methods:A quasi-experimental design was conducted from September to December 2024 at Taibah University. Four month-long challenges targeting smartphone use, physical activity, sleep, and gratitude were integrated into a required hospital pharmacy course. The intervention was grounded in behavioral economics principles (salience and inertia) and delivered via Blackboard LMS with minimal extrinsic incentives. A reflective survey assessed participation, motivations, baseline behaviors, and post-intervention intentions.Results:Of 198 enrolled students, 155 (78.3%) engaged in at least one challenge. Participation was highest in the smartphone challenge (64.1%) and lowest in the gratitude challenge (28.8%). Key motivators included extra credit and interest in the challenging outcomes. A large majority (89.7%–98.7%) reported intentions to sustain the positive behaviors post-intervention, particularly among those who had not previously adopted them. Intrinsic motivation, such as the desire for personal well-being, showed a significant positive correlation with the intention to continue.Conclusion:Embedding nudge-based challenges into pharmacy curricula is a feasible and culturally responsive strategy for enhancing student well-being. Further research should evaluate long-term impacts and scalability across educational settings.

The well-being of pharmacy students is a growing concern in Saudi Arabia, with evidence indicating higher levels of stress and psychological distress among these students compared to the general student population.1-3 This trend aligns with broader shifts in healthcare education, where mental health is recognized as essential to academic success and professional growth.4 In response, the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCHS) introduced the Da’em framework to support training environments that promote learner well-being and prevent burnout.5

Saudi universities have implemented support programs, including counseling, mindfulness, and stress management initiatives. One remarkable example is the Mindful Jeddah Training Program (MJTP), a 3-week mindfulness-based intervention piloted at a private university.6 However, few studies have evaluated the long-term impact of such programs. In Saudi Arabia, pharmacy students face academic pressure alongside cultural and social obligations, leading to persistent challenges such as poor sleep, limited physical activity, and ineffective stress management.7-10 Although awareness of the importance of mental and physical well-being is increasing among students, it has not yet translated into substantial or sustained behavioral change. This persistent gap between knowledge and action underscores the need for targeted interventions that promote healthier habits within the specific cultural and educational context of Saudi pharmacy programs.11

Behavioral science offers a range of promising tools to encourage positive lifestyle changes among students, with nudging emerging as a particularly effective approach. Nudging involves the use of subtle environmental or contextual cues that guide individuals toward better decisions without restricting their freedom of choice.12,13 Within this framework, concepts such as salience and inertia are especially relevant in the student context. Salience refers to the effectiveness of drawing attention to certain choices or behaviors; students are more likely to act when healthy options are made more visible or accessible. Inertia, on the other hand, reflects the tendency to maintain the status quo; without structured prompts or embedded reminders, students may struggle to initiate or sustain positive behavioral changes, despite good intentions.14,15

Evidence from recent studies supports the efficacy of nudging interventions. For example, a randomized controlled trial in Australia demonstrated that nudges improved students’ life satisfaction by 0.49 units on a standardized scale and enhanced their resilience and engagement in well-being-related behaviors.16 Moreover, other studies have found that students exposed to nudging strategies tend to show higher levels of participation and engagement compared to those who are not.17

Despite these global successes, research on the application of nudging within Saudi educational settings remains limited. Pharmacy students in Saudi Arabia, in particular, face unique cultural and institutional challenges that hinder the adoption of well-being practices. Traditional awareness campaigns have often failed to produce meaningful behavioral change.18,19 This study aims to investigate whether incorporating optional, structured well-being challenges into academic coursework can help overcome behavioral inertia and promote healthier habits tailored to the specific needs of this population.

Methods

The intervention was conducted within 2 mandatory 2-credit-hour hospital pharmacy courses (I and III) at Taibah University, Al Madinah Al Munawwarah, Saudi Arabia, involving students from both lower and upper academic levels. In accordance with cultural norms and institutional policies, the study was conducted separately but concurrently for the male and female sections, using identical protocols. Eligibility was limited to students enrolled in the specified courses during the Fall 2024 semester who provided informed consent. Participants who did not complete at least one challenge survey or declined consent for data use were excluded from the analysis.

Four month-long well-being challenges (Figure 1) were embedded within the course curricula from September through December 2024. The challenges addressed included smartphone use management, promoting physical activity, optimizing sleep behavior, and expressing gratitude.20

- Requirements for well-being challenge requirements implemented to nudge students toward adopting protective health behaviors.

The primary design focus was to make the 4 protective behaviors highly visible, ensuring they captured students’ attention. This was achieved by integrating the behaviors into the course as assignments within the Blackboard Learning Management System (Blackboard Inc., Washington, D.C., US) and reinforcing them with automated announcements about each challenge. Additionally, the “challenge” format, along with a small incentive (one extra credit point for completing each challenge), was used to encourage students to overcome inertia and engage in the desired behaviors.21 The incentive, equivalent to just 0.2% of the final grade per challenge, was deliberately minimal to align with nudging principles, ensuring that no significant disadvantage would be incurred by non-participants.22

On the first day of class, students were introduced to the well-being challenges via a lecture discussing organizational wellness initiatives in healthcare. The lecture emphasized the proven benefits of the targeted behaviors in alleviating stress, anxiety, and depression. Following the minimal intervention approach, students were informed that participation was entirely voluntary, with the sole extrinsic reward being 0.5 extra credit points for completing each challenge and its reflective survey.

Data collection involved a 5-question reflective survey administered through Blackboard at the end of each challenge, with students given 3 days to complete it. The survey was available in both Arabic and English, measuring participation rates, motivations for participation, pre-existing behaviors, and future behavioral intentions.

Two faculty members reviewed the survey to ensure the questions aligned with the study’s objectives and were relevant to the target audience. Prior to the official survey, 12 pharmacy students tested the questionnaire for clarity and to identify any ambiguous or poorly worded questions, confirming that no changes were necessary.

The strategy’s effectiveness in encouraging students to adopt better well-being behaviors was assessed based on their short-term participation and long-term intentions to maintain these behaviors. Short-term success was characterized by at least 50% of the class (n=99) engaging in 2 or more challenges. Long-term success was identified when 50% of participants from each challenge expressed intentions to sustain their behavioral changes after the challenge concluded. Due to significant variations in the number of students exhibiting the targeted behaviors before the course, the benchmarks for long-term success were differentiated between the smartphone and gratitude challenges compared to the sleep and exercise challenges. The success of the smartphone and gratitude challenges was evaluated by the number of students who, having not previously displayed those specific behaviors, planned to continue them after the challenge. In contrast, for sleep and exercise, very few participants had not previously met the requirements. Hence, success was gauged by whether those who had not consistently practiced these well-being behaviors intended to carry them forward after the challenge.

The study was conducted with ethical approval from the Scientific Ethics Committee of the College of Pharmacy at Taibah University, Al Madinah AL Munawwarah, Saudi Arabia, under reference number COPTU-REC-126-2025114. All participants received and signed a consent form prior to their involvement, ensuring they were informed about the study’s purpose, procedures, and their rights.

Data analysis

The data was coded and entered into a computer for analysis using SPSS for Windows version 27.0. The analysis involved descriptive statistics, with results presented as frequencies and percentages. Chi-square tests were used to examine the associations between gender, academic year, and participation rates in challenges. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was conducted to assess the monotonic relationships between reasons for participating in well-being challenges and participants’ intentions to continue the behavior. The strength of Spearman’s correlation coefficients was interpreted using standard thresholds: 0.10–0.29 = weak, 0.30–0.49=moderate, and <0.10=very weak, as recommended by Cohen.23

Results

Out of 198 students enrolled in the course, 78.3% (n=155) participated in at least one well-being challenge and consented to have their results included in the study for analysis. The study included 155 participants, with a higher representation of females (56%). The mean age of participants was 19.9 years (SD = ±1.58). Most students were in the upper division (59.3%). The strategy demonstrated high success in engaging students, with 62 (40%) pharmacy students completing one challenge, 41 (26.4%) students completing 2 challenges, 23 (14.8%) students completing 3 challenges, and 29 (18.7%) students completing all 4 challenges. The smartphone challenge had the highest participation rate, with 36.5% of participants (n=127). In contrast, the gratitude challenge was the least attempted, with only 23.7% of participants (n=56) taking part.

While earning extra credit (points) was a common motivator across all challenges (54.4%-72.4%), interest in the effects (64.3%-75.6%) and potential increases in personal productivity (39.3%-64.5%) were also significant factors. Before the course, 46.4% of participants never or rarely met the criteria for the smartphone challenge, while 42.8% had never or rarely met the criteria for the gratitude challenge. In comparison, 26.4% of participants never or rarely met the criteria for the sleep challenge, and 26.8% did not meet the criteria for the exercise challenge (Figure 2).

Regarding the long-term effectiveness of the challenges, a significant number of participants (between 89.7% and 98.7%) expressed their intention to either maintain or sustain their behavior after completing the challenge. Within the smartphone challenge, 80% of the 20 participants who had not previously engaged in these behaviors aimed to either try to continue (n=13) or definitely continue (n=3) them post-challenge. In the gratitude challenge, all 16 participants who had not previously shown the behaviors intended to either attempt to continue (n=13) or certainly continue (n=3) their behaviors following the challenge. Among the 40 participants in the sleep challenge who did not consistently exhibit the behaviors before, 55% (n=10) stated they would definitely maintain them. Similarly, 50% (n=20) of the 40 participants in the exercise challenge, who had not consistently practiced the behaviors prior to the challenge, indicated that they would certainly continue them afterward. Most participants reported feeling more capable of enhancing their personal well-being after completing the challenges, with percentages for exercise (74.4%), gratitude (71.4%), sleep (57.4%), and smartphone use (56.6%). Remarkably, very few participants felt less capable than they did before the challenges, with responses ranging from 1.7% to 8.6% across all challenges. The analysis revealed a significant association between gender and participation in the 4 challenges, χ²(3) = 8.783, p=0.03, with females showing notably higher engagement in the gratitude challenge. However, no significant association was found between academic level (lower division versus upper division) and participation, χ²(3)=1.87, p=0.56, indicating similar behavior across year groups. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between students’ reasons for participating in well-being challenges and their intentions to continue the behavior after the challenge. A moderate, statistically significant positive correlation was found between the reason “needed for personal well-being” and intention to continue (rs=0.31, p<0.001), suggesting that students who joined the challenge for well-being purposes were more likely to sustain the behavior. A smaller but still significant positive correlation was observed between “interested in the effect” and intention to continue (rs=0.17, p<0.001), indicating that curiosity about outcomes also influenced future behavioral intentions. “Increase personal productivity” showed a weak but statistically significant association (rs=0.11, p=0.04), while “points (extra credit)” was not significantly correlated with intentions to continue (rs= –0.06, p=0.221). These findings highlight the stronger influence of intrinsic health-related motivations over external incentives in promoting sustained behavior change. A full summary is presented in Table 4.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into the application of nudge-based strategies to enhance the well-being of pharmacy students within the cultural and academic context of Saudi Arabia. The high participation rate (78.3%) and substantial completion rates across multiple challenges suggest that structured wellness interventions can successfully engage healthcare students, aligning with previous research that has shown the efficacy of wellness programs in academic settings.24

The smartphone challenge attracted the highest participation, whereas the gratitude challenge had the lowest. This contrast likely reflects students’ perceptions of which wellness domains are most relevant to their lives. Prior research suggests that managing digital device use is a pressing concern for many college students.25 The higher engagement in the smartphone challenge could also be attributed to students’ recognition of problematic device use, as prior studies have shown that university students often acknowledge their excessive smartphone dependency.26 In contrast, the lower participation in the gratitude challenge may be due to the perception that gratitude practices are time-intensive and demand sustained emotional effort.27

The analysis of motivational factors reveals a complex interplay between extrinsic and intrinsic motivators. While extra credit was a common motivator, the significant correlation between personal well-being needs and the intention to continue well-being behaviors supports the premise of the Self-Determination Theory, which reveals that intrinsic motivation leads to more sustainable behavior change.28 Furthermore, as mentioned by Al-Ghalib and Salim, interventions that resonate with students’ personal goals and values show higher engagement rates in Saudi educational settings.6 This finding is significant for designing future wellness interventions in academic settings.

The observed behavior change can be further understood through the lens of the COM-B model, which emphasizes that behavior results from an interaction between capability, opportunity, and motivation.29 The well-being challenges in this study were designed to enhance students’ psychological and physical capability (such as self-monitoring), provide structured opportunities within the course, and activate both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation through reflection and incentives. Moreover, the progression in students’ intentions to maintain the behaviors aligns with the transtheoretical model, suggesting that many participants may have advanced from contemplation or preparation stages toward action and maintenance.30

The gender disparity in challenge participation, particularly in the gratitude challenge, aligns with previous research indicating that female students tend to exhibit higher engagement in wellness activities.31 However, it is important to note that wellness education is relevant and impactful at every stage of academic development.32

The high intention rates for maintaining behaviors after the challenge are especially promising. This finding is consistent with research on habit formation, which suggests that structured interventions can effectively initiate lasting behavioral changes when implemented with adequate support and duration.33 The notably high continuation rates among participants who had never exhibited these behaviors before (80-100%) indicate the potential of such challenges to catalyze new healthy habits.34

The moderately high participation in exercise-related challenges, despite environmental and cultural constraints, suggests that appropriately designed interventions can overcome traditional barriers. This finding supports research by Al-Momani on health-promoting lifestyles among Saudi medical students, indicating that structured support can facilitate positive health behaviors despite cultural or environmental limitations.7

Study limitations

This study has several significant limitations. External factors, such as academic stress, exams, and other concurrent interventions, may have influenced students’ engagement and outcomes. These uncontrolled variables limit our ability to isolate the effects of the intervention. The single-institution design at Taibah University limits the generalizability of findings at other Saudi pharmacy schools. The self-reported nature of participation and behavioral changes may introduce social desirability bias. The one-month duration for each challenge might be insufficient to establish lasting behavioral changes, and the timing within the academic semester could have influenced participation rates. The voluntary nature of participation may have resulted in selection bias, with more health-conscious students being more likely to participate. Moreover, the lack of a control group and the inability to track actual behavioral changes beyond the challenge periods limit our understanding of the intervention’s long-term effectiveness. These limitations suggest opportunities for future research through multi-institutional studies with more extended intervention periods and more robust measurement of behavioral changes.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the effectiveness of embedding structured well-being challenges within pharmacy education curricula in Saudi Arabia. The high participation rate and intention to continue behaviors suggest that nudge-based interventions can successfully promote well-being behaviors among pharmacy students. The varying engagement levels across different challenges highlight the importance of cultural adaptation in wellness interventions, particularly in the Saudi educational context. The combination of extrinsic motivators and intrinsic factors proved effective in driving participation, while the increased self-efficacy among participants suggests potential for sustainable behavioral change. These findings contribute valuable insights into the implementation of behavioral science applications in Saudi healthcare education. However, future research should focus on long-term follow-up studies to assess the sustainability of these behavioral changes and explore additional culturally appropriate interventions for promoting the well-being of Saudi pharmacy students.
