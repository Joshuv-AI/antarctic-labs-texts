How to make one line in the FDA Commissioner's new drug review program into a force for affordable access for patients
Authors: Sarah K Emond, Daniel A Ollendorf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe FDA Commissioner's new National Priority Voucher program seeks to accelerate drug approvals for products meeting certain criteria. Interestingly, the program intends to increase affordability of new drugs. With few specifics available as to how the program will achieve that goal, this paper proposes a framework for leveraging independent value assessments to achieve affordable access while incentivizing evidence development and innovation.Keywords: FDA, value assessment, prescription drugs

The US Food and Drug Administration recently announced a new Commissioner’s National Priority Voucher1 pilot program aimed at speeding the time to market for newly developed drugs. Drawing on a “tumor board” approach in which a team of clinicians often make a decision about a patient's treatment in a single-day, multidisciplinary meeting, this program is meant to speed the review and approval of a new medicine from 6 months or longer to just 1-2 months. The program is intended to allow for more frequent communication between the sponsor and the FDA, and draw on FDA staff reviewers from across disciplines, all while maintaining the FDA's usual standards for safety and efficacy. While the mechanics of just how this speedy review will take place and the specifics of which drugs will be granted this designation are still being worked out, there is one stated goal of the new program and requirement of participating companies that is worth unpacking now:

Increasing affordability. This could include a company that lowers the US price of a drug or drugs consistent with Most Favored Nation pricing or reduces other downstream medical utilization to lower overall healthcare costs.

This is a marked and notable departure for the FDA, an agency that has been clear in the past that drug costs are not its purview or responsibility. However, as consumers and drug pricing experts, we know that drug costs remain high, and addressing high drug costs has the distinction of one being one of the few truly bipartisan issues in the country. The data here are clear: More than a quarter of patients chose not to fill a drug prescription because of cost,2 while some patients forego basic needs or go into debt to afford their medicines.3 Without any additional specifics from the FDA as to how this new focus on drug affordability will be applied as part of the Commissioner's National Priority Voucher program, we would like to propose an innovative solution to bridging the aspirations of more prescription drug affordability with the reality of our convoluted drug pricing system.

In short, drugs approved through the pathway should be required to be priced to value. Pricing to value—or ensuring that a price charged society by the drug manufacturer is tied to how much better it makes patients feel—is a commonly used approach outside the United States for ensuring there is some balance between a company's ability to leverage its government-granted monopoly while ensuring affordable access for patients. US citizens have granted drug manufacturers a reprieve from competition through patent laws for the first few years after a drug is approved by the FDA. But we also allow them to price drugs however they want, regardless of how well the drug works. This imbalance can be corrected through value assessment. A value assessment approach means companies can charge more for drugs that work really well, and have to charge less when there is uncertainty in how well the drug works, or data that it does not work that well.

This is the approach that our organization, the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review (ICER), has been taking for nearly 20 years.4 Our nonpartisan, independent orientation allows us to call “balls and strikes.” Our value assessments have judged some new medicines for rare conditions fairly priced at millions of dollars given the tremendous benefits they bring to patients, while also calling out pharmaceutical companies that charge society and patients much more for a drug than the benefits deserve. Importantly, our approach does not rely on foreign pricing mechanics; our assessments are based on US values and financial considerations. Our commonsense approach would fit nicely into the Commissioner's new program—offering an evidence-based way to ensure we are getting the health we want for the price we pay.

How it could work

While the Commissioner's statement uses “Most Favored Nation” pricing to define what a fair drug price might be, this is problematic because the United States is frequently where new drugs are launched first, so in most cases, there would not be any other prices to reference. Instead, one of ICER's cornerstones of value assessment, cost-effectiveness analysis, can summarize the costs a new drug would introduce to the system, the savings it might provide by keeping patients out of the hospital or emergency room, and how it might improve both survival and quality of life during treatment. This type of analysis can suggest a fair and affordable price for the benefits a drug provides, which aligns with the spirit of the Commissioner's statement.

It is quite possible that the National Priority Voucher may be applied in situations where a drug has much potential for a life-threatening or serious condition, but traditional randomized controlled trials (RCTs) may take years to run. The FDA's Accelerated Approval pathway is available for these situations and allows manufacturers to conduct smaller, short-term studies using surrogate or intermediate endpoints, but also to commit to those longer-term RCTs to gain full FDA approval.

The National Priority Voucher program could support a similar pathway, but with a clear “reimbursement with evidence development” mandate. Drugs receiving the voucher could get an initial approval based on surrogate or intermediate endpoints, but the findings of any value assessment would be more uncertain as a result—and the suggested fair price should reflect that uncertainty (eg, the range of possible effects of improved glycemic control in diabetes on long-term outcomes). But this also provides an incentive for manufacturers to commit to developing evidence that confirms the benefit and reduces the uncertainty (eg, reduced rates of myocardial infarction, stroke, and other diabetes-related complications). If benefits in the confirmatory study are greater than first estimated, the fair price could actually increase.

What needs to change

There are significant changes that we would need to see from the typical way drugs are priced in order to make this approach possible. First, more consensus and alignment on which dimensions of value should be measured and counted in determining value to ensure prices are reflecting what patients care about is needed. The Institute for Clinical and Economic Review, along with other leading health technology assessment organizations, is supporting an initiative that features an expert working group to tackle these and other pressing methodological questions in an independent manner.5

Second, the recent enactment of inflationary penalties in Medicare presents a challenge to allowing drug makers the opportunity to raise prices later if they demonstrate larger benefits for patients after more evidence is generated. Congress would need to amend the Medicare inflationary penalty to allow drugs approved through the Commissioner's National Priority Voucher program to be exempt from the inflationary penalty if confirmatory trials completed in a timely manner demonstrated more benefit for patients than the initial studies. Alternatively, Congress could give the FDA power to assign certain drugs to a list that the Centers for Medicaid and Medicaid Services (CMS) could reference to know which drugs are exempted from the inflationary penalty because their initial pricing is felt to be value based; such a provision would be available for a limited time period, in order to incentivize the competition of additional studies.

Third, in order for patients to feel the benefit of value-based pricing, cost-sharing should be waived for drugs that are priced based on a third-party assessment of value. Right now, our system of rebates, utilization management, and cost-sharing is often divorced from whether or not a price is fair. We must tie value-based pricing to reducing cost-sharing barriers for patients. Only then can we achieve affordability for patients and society.

Conclusion

Much is changing in the drug approval and pricing space, and in order to ensure affordability for patients and society, policymakers should pay close attention to the merits of a US-based value assessment approach. In the context of the FDA's new Commissioner's National Priority Voucher program, an opportunity to use independent assessments of value exists to ensure fair pricing for drugs approved under such a pathway while also incentivizing the completion of confirmatory trials so that patients, their families, and prescribers have the information they deserve to make informed decisions about their care.
