Use of Rituximab in the Management of a Nasopharyngeal Fibrosing Inflammatory Pseudotumor
Authors: Erika Hazan, Samuel Bellavance, Philippe Lavigne, Apostolos Christopoulos, Paul Tabet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFibrosing inflammatory pseudotumor (FIP) of the nasopharynx can mimic malignancy and IgG4‐related disease (IgG4‐RD), complicating diagnosis. This corticosteroid‐dependent case responded well to rituximab despite not meeting IgG4‐RD criteria. This highlights the diagnostic challenges and suggests rituximab as a potential steroid‐sparing treatment for FIP.Keywords: corticosteroid‐sparing therapy, fibrosing inflammatory pseudotumor, IgG4‐related disease, rituximab, steroid‐induced side effects

Introduction

Fibrosing inflammatory pseudotumor (FIP) of the nasopharynx is an uncommon disease. It typically presents with facial pain and cranial neuropathies and can resemble malignancy or infection, leading to a wrong initial diagnosis [1]. Similar diagnoses include IgG4‐related disease (IgG4‐RD), Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)‐associated inflammatory pseudotumor, and inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor [2].

The literature on FIP is limited, mainly consisting of case reports and small case series. This scarcity of data makes diagnosis and treatment difficult. This case study provides an overview of the presenting symptoms, imaging characteristics, treatment options, and outcomes of patients suffering from nasopharyngeal pseudotumor.

Case History/Examination

A 37‐year‐old female presented with left ear pain, progressive hearing loss, and left otitis media with effusion. Her history includes depression and anxiety. She is an active tobacco smoker, consumes two alcoholic drinks daily, and uses cannabis occasionally. A 2 cm mass was found in the left nasopharynx during endoscopy.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

The differential diagnosis following initial examination includes malignant disease (nasopharyngeal carcinoma, lymphoma, sinonasal undifferentiated carcinoma), benign disease (Thornwaldt cysts, inverted papillomas, hypertrophic adenoids) or inflammatory conditions (FIP, IgG4‐RD, EBV‐associated inflammatory pseudotumor, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor).

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed an infiltrative left‐sided nasopharyngeal mass involving the soft palate, medial pterygoid muscle, and parapharyngeal space. The mass presented T1 hyperintensity and slight T2 hypointensity, suggesting hypercellularity (Figure 1). Positron emission tomography demonstrated a hypermetabolic mass in the left nasopharynx with a standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 9.1, raising suspicion of malignancy. Mild uptake (SUVmax 3.8) was also noted in a 6 mm left level IIA lymph node (Figure 2).

The patient underwent a biopsy of the left nasopharyngeal mass under general anesthesia. The histopathological analysis included hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Epstein–Barr encoded RNA (EBER) in situ hybridization, and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Results showed no neoplasia or EBER, and IHC demonstrated a polyclonal lymphoid population without evidence of lymphoma.

Given the high suspicion of malignancy, biopsies were repeated. IHC quantified IgG4+ plasma cells and calculated the IgG4+/IgG+ ratio for suspected IgG4‐RD. Significant lymphocyte infiltration with fibrosis was noted without neoplasia or lymphoma. Three IgG4+ plasma cells per high‐powered field and an IgG4+/IgG+ ratio of 10% were reported. Typical IgG4‐RD features, such as storiform fibrosis and obliterative phlebitis, were absent (Table 1). Bacterial, fungal, and mycobacterial cultures were negative.

Two months later, the patient developed severe left frontotemporal pain, temporomandibular joint pain, and trismus. A repeat MRI showed significant growth of the nasopharyngeal mass, extending into the left masticator space, lateral pterygoid muscle, cavernous sinus, and dura mater around the left temporal lobe. Enhancement was noted along the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve. The previously identified lymph node demonstrated a reduction in size and was deemed benign.

High‐dose oral prednisone alleviated symptoms, and Botulinum toxin infiltration into the left masticator space improved trismus. Although symptoms diminished, corticosteroid side effects included a cushingoid appearance, weight gain, acne, myopathy, and hyperthyroidism. Of note, no treatment was started after the initial biopsy because malignancy was still considered possible and had not been confirmed histopathologically. High‐dose oral prednisone was administered only after two negative biopsy results, when the diagnosis of malignancy was reconsidered.

Due to the diagnosis's uncertainty, the patient returned to the operating room for another biopsy. The authors preferred an endonasal, trans‐pterygoid approach to the masticatory space, which provides access to the central aspect of the lesion. A surgical clip was placed at the center of the biopsied area to confirm, through postoperative imaging, that the biopsy was performed in a representative region. As anticipated, the procedure increased hypoesthesia of the left palatine nerve. IgG4 serologies were ordered, showing a level of 107.7 mg/dL. Although this level did not meet the official criteria threshold of 135 mg/dL [3], IgG4‐RD was considered because the level exceeded the upper limit of normal (N: 86.5 mg/dL) despite high‐dose corticosteroid therapy. The patient did not, however, exhibit additional manifestations of IgG4‐related disease, such as thyroid or ophthalmologic involvement. No other family member showed similar lesions or IgG4‐related disease.

Conclusion and Results

An attempt to taper oral prednisone from 50 to 30 mg daily led to a recurrence of pain and trismus. High doses were maintained for 5 months to control symptoms until rituximab initiation. An MRI during high‐dose corticosteroid therapy showed improvement in tumoral infiltration. Enhancement was markedly reduced, though minor residual enhancement persisted in the same region.

Rituximab was initiated per IgG4‐RD guidelines (two 1000 mg doses, 2 weeks apart) [4]. Prednisone was tapered from 50 to 7.5 mg daily without symptom recurrence. Six‐month follow‐up MRI showed significant improvement in enhancement abnormalities, particularly in the left infratemporal fossa. No evidence of new lymphadenopathy was observed (Figure 3). Serum IgG4 levels normalized to 50.8 mg/dL.

Discussion

This is the first documented case of a corticosteroid‐dependent patient with FIP of the nasopharynx responding to rituximab despite not meeting IgG4‐RD criteria. Nasopharyngeal FIP usually presents at an average age of 51 [5]. Most present with facial pain, headache, or cranial nerve involvement, particularly the trigeminal nerve [5].

MRI typically shows gadolinium enhancement in nasopharyngeal FIP, with lesions appearing more ill‐defined than encapsulated. Typical findings include T2 hypointensity from dense fibrosis [1]. Skull base involvement is frequent, often affecting the clivus and petrous part of the temporal bone [5]. Other affected regions include the occipital condyle, pterygoid plates, sphenoid wing, and orbital apex. Over half of the cases show internal carotid artery (ICA) encasement, potentially causing obstruction and stroke [5].

Corticosteroids are the primary treatment for nasopharyngeal FIP and effectively relieve symptoms [5]. In this case, corticosteroids were beneficial but caused side effects. Rituximab, a CD20‐targeting monoclonal antibody, was introduced to reduce steroid dependence while managing symptoms. Its success in IgG4‐RD and other inflammatory conditions suggests potential efficacy in nasopharyngeal pseudotumors [4].

This patient did not meet the IgG4‐RD criteria, which are often considered in nasopharyngeal pseudotumors. IgG4‐RD typically presents with an IgG4+/IgG+ plasma cell ratio > 40% and features such as storiform fibrosis and obliterative phlebitis, none of which were present [3]. This case raises questions about the current criteria for diagnosing IgG4‐RD. Even though the patient's IgG4 level was below the 135 mg/dL threshold, it still exceeded the normal range. The 135 mg/dL cutoff, determined through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, may exclude patients with lower IgG4 levels who could benefit from treatments like rituximab [3]. Prior high‐dose corticosteroid treatment, which could lower IgG4 levels, is not considered in the current IgG4‐RD diagnostic criteria.

Some FIP cases have shown IgG4 positivity in specific tumor areas, suggesting an IgG4‐RD diagnosis might have been possible if different tumor regions were sampled [5]. This underscores the limitations of current IgG4‐RD criteria. Further research may reveal that some FIP cases belong to IgG4‐RD, supporting a flexible approach that considers treatment responses, such as rituximab.

This case highlights the challenges in diagnosing and managing FIP, especially in corticosteroid‐dependent patients. The successful use of rituximab offers a promising option for symptom control with less steroid exposure. Further research may clarify rituximab's role in FIP and improve criteria to distinguish it from IgG4‐RD.
