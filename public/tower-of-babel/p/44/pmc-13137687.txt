Biomechanical comparison of four different internal fixation constructs applied to stabilise Letenneur type I Hoffa fractures: a finite element analysis
Authors: YM Cha, XD Jian, Bo Chang, Peng Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo simulate and compare the biomechanical characteristics of different internal fixation constructs for treating Letenneur type I Hoffa fractures via finite element analysis to establish a theoretical foundation for selecting a fixation method in clinical practice.MethodsOn the basis of computed tomography (CT) data acquired from a healthy adult male volunteer, a finite element model of Letenneur type I Hoffa fracture—characterized by a fracture line oriented parallel to the posterior femoral cortex—was developed. Four different internal fixation constructs were constructed: anteroposterior cannulated screw fixation (AP group), posteroanterior cannulated screw fixation (PA group), posteroanterior cannulated screws with a posterior plate (PA + P-plate group), and posteroanterior cannulated screws with a lateral plate (PA + L-plate group). Under a vertical load of 2100 N, the displacement distribution, stress distribution, and peak values of the internal fixation and femur were analyzed.ResultsUnder a 2100 N axial load, the AP and PA groups presented the highest and comparable peak displacement values in the femur and internal fixation (3.80 mm, 3.46 mm, and 3.84 mm, 3.41 mm, respectively). The PA + P-plate group presented the lowest peak displacement value (1.85 mm and 1.58 mm), followed by the PA + L-plate group (2.25 mm and 2.19 mm). Similarly, the AP and PA groups presented the highest peak stresses in the internal fixation (535.65 and 574.39 MPa, respectively), whereas the PA + P-plate group presented the lowest peak stress (287.21 MPa). The stress distribution patterns in the femur were similar across all groups, with concentrations primarily on both sides of the fracture line, the posterolateral region, and the anterior aspect.ConclusionsThe mechanical stability of Anteroposterior (AP) and posteroanterior (PA) screws is comparable. While the addition of a plate significantly enhanced fixation stability, the posterior plate demonstrated superior biomechanical performance compared with the lateral plate.

Introduction

Hoffa fracture is a rare type of coronal plane fracture of the femoral condyle, accounting for approximately 8–13% of all distal femoral fracture [1–3]. As intra-articular fractures, they are prone to displacement due to muscular traction and shear forces from the tibial plateau, therefore, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) is recommended as the preferred treatment strategy [4–7].

Currently, cannulated screws are widely employed for internal fixation, with insertion directions classified as anteroposterior (AP) and posteroanterior (PA) [8, 9]. While cannulated screws can provide interfragmentary compression, their resistance to shear forces is limited, particularly in osteoporotic or comminuted fractures [10, 11]. To enhance fixation strength, supplementary plates positioned either posteriorly or laterally are often utilized [12, 13]. Despite these strategies, no consensus has been reached regarding which internal fixation construct offers optimal biomechanical performance [14].

In this study, finite element analysis was used to construct a validated model of a Letenneur type I Hoffa fracture and compare the biomechanical characteristics of four distinct internal fixation constructs: anteroposterior cannulated screws, posteroanterior cannulated screws, posteroanterior cannulated screws with a posterior plate, and posteroanterior cannulated screws with a lateral plate. By analyzing displacement and stress distribution, we aim to provide a theoretical foundation for selecting the optimal internal fixation construct in clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Finite element model establishment

This study received review and approval from the author’s institutional ethics committee. A healthy adult male volunteer (aged 35 years, weighing 70 kg) with no history of femoral fracture, arthritis, or congenital deformity was enrolled. Continuous thin-slice computed tomography scanning of the femur (from the femoral head to the knee joint) was performed via a Siemens CT scanner, with a slice thickness of 0.625 mm. The acquired image data in DICOM format were imported into Mimics 21.0 software to reconstruct a three-dimensional femoral model, which was subsequently exported in the STL format. The STL file was then processed in Geomagic Wrap 2021 for model optimization, including denoising, surface smoothing, mesh refinement, and solid filling, to generate a high-quality geometric solid model. The refined model was imported into SolidWorks 2022 in STEP format for further processing. To simplify the computational calculations, the proximal third of the femur was removed. On the basis of the Letenneur classification [1], a type I Hoffa fracture model of the lateral femoral condyle was created via segmentation and combination tools, with the fracture line oriented parallel to the posterior cortex of the femur (Fig. 1A).

The cannulated screw models were constructed on the basis of the parameters of 6.5 mm partially threaded cannulated screws, featuring a thread diameter of 6.5 mm, a thread length of 16 mm, a pitch of 1.8 mm, and an inner cannulated diameter of 2.8 mm. On the basis of anterior‒posterior diameter measurements of the femoral lateral condyle, the screw lengths were set at 60 mm and 55 mm respectively. A 6-hole 3.5 mm locking compression plate (LCP) and five 3.5 mm locking screws were also modeled. To simplify the models and reduce the calculation time, the locking screws were modeled without threads.

The implant models were then assembled with the validated Letenneur type I Hoffa fracture model to establish the following four internal fixation constructs (Fig. 1B):AP group: two parallel cannulated screws inserted in the anteroposterior direction.PA group: two parallel cannulated screws inserted in the posteroanterior direction.PA+L-plate group: PA screw with a lateral LCP plate.PA+P-plate group: PA screw with a posterior LCP plate.

In all the models, cannulated screws were inserted perpendicular to the fracture line and positioned at the upper and lower thirds of the cross-sectional length of the lateral posterior condyle along the longitudinal axis [15]. When screw heads pose a risk of articular surface impingement, they are countersunk to avoid joint intrusion. The plates were contoured to match the anatomical curvature of the posterior or lateral aspect of the femoral lateral condyle, ensuring optimal bone implant contact. For the posterior plate, the distal end was positioned proximal to the posterior articular margin to avoid articular surface impingement. In the lateral plate group, the distal end was placed on the lateral surface. The two distal locking screws of the plate were placed in the femoral condylar fragment (with a sufficient length of the extra-articular cortex of the condylar fragment), and the remaining three locking screws were placed in the proximal femur.

Material properties and contact settings

All the finite element analyses were performed via ANSYS Workbench 2017. On the basis of previously published literature [16], all the materials were defined as continuous, homogeneous, and isotropic linear elastic (Table 1). A surface-to-surface contact behavior was adopted to all contact pairs, the locking screws were fully bonded to the plate and bone, the threaded portions of the cannulated screws were bonded to the bone, while the screw shafts were set as nonseprable contacts. The interfaces between the plate and the two fracture ends are considered friction contact, and the friction coefficients between bone-bone, bone-plate are 0.46 and 0.30 [15], respectively. All the models were meshed with ten-node tetrahedron elements, The mesh size was determined by the mesh convergence test and the deviation was less than 5% adhering to the convergence criterion, resulting in element sizes of 3 mm for cortical and cancellous bone, 2 mm for the plate, 1.5 mm for the cannulated screws, and 1.0 mm for the locking screws. Boundary and loading conditions

The proximal end of the femur model was fully constrained to simulate single-leg standing conditions. A vertical load of 2100 N, equivalent to three times the body weight of a 70 kg adult male, was applied along the mechanical axis of the femur to the articular surface of the femoral condyle (Fig. 1C).

Evaluation criteria

In the context of finite element analysis, this study focused on the following observation indicators: (1) Displacement distribution and peak displacement of the femur; (2) Stress distribution and peak stress of the femur; (3) Displacement distribution and peak displacement of the internal fixation. (4) Stress distribution and peak stress of the internal fixation. For the femur, the peak displacement was measured at the articular surface of the distal femoral condylar fragment. For the internal fixation, the peak displacement value refers to the maximum movement of any point on the implant, which in our models was consistently located at the most distal aspect of the cannulated screw.

Results

Displacement distribution and peak value of the femur

As shown in Fig. 2, under axial loading conditions, the femoral condylar fragment exhibited proximal displacement. Displacement distribution analysis revealed that the peak displacement value was localized at the load area and progressively decreased toward the proximal shaft of the femur in all groups. The peak displacement values were comparable between the AP group (3.80 mm) and the PA group (3.84 mm), and cannulated screws with plate fixation substantially reduced displacement. The PA + L-plate group presented a peak displacement value of 2.25 mm, whereas the PA + P-plate group presented the lowest displacement value of 1.85 mm.

Stress distribution and peak stress of the femur

As shown in Fig. 3, the stress distribution patterns were consistent across all the groups. The condylar fragment was subjected to a proximally directed force, which was subsequently transmitted to the proximal shaft of the femur. The stress was primarily distributed on both sides of the fracture surface, as well as in the posterolateral and anterior aspects of the femur. The peak stress values were comparable between the AP group (143.92 MPa) and the PA group (147.33 MPa), with both groups exhibiting peak stresses adjacent to the fracture ends. In the PA + P-plate group, the peak stress was 134.92 MPa and was localized at the most proximal locking screw hole of the femur. The PA + L-plate group demonstrated a further reduction in peak stress to 127.04 MPa, which was located at the second distal locking screw hole within the femoral condylar fragment.

Displacement distribution and peak value of internal fixation

As shown in Fig. 4, the peak displacement consistently occurred at the distal cannulated screws in all the groups. In the PA, PA + P-plate, and PA + L-plate groups, the displacement gradually increased from the threaded portion to the cap of the cannulated screws, and the peak displacement was localized to the cap end of the cannulated screws. In contrast, the displacement in the AP group gradually decreased from the cap toward the threaded portion, whereas the peak displacement occurred at the threaded end of the screw. The peak displacements of the AP and PA groups (3.466 mm and 3.413 mm, respectively) were comparable. The addition of a plate substantially reduced the peak displacement of the distal cannulated screw. The PA + P-plate group exhibited the lowest peak displacement at 1.580 mm, followed by the PA + L-plate group at 2.192 mm.

Stress distribution and peak stress of internal fixation

As shown in Fig. 5, the stress distributions of the cannulated screws in the AP and PA groups were comparable and were mostly concentrated at the middle of the cannulated screws adjacent to the fracture surface. The peak stresses in the AP (535.65 MPa) and PA (574.39 MPa) groups were comparable. The PA + P-plate group presented the lowest peak stress at 287.21 MPa, whereas the PA + L-plate group presented a peak stress of 516.00 MPa. Compared with cannulated screw fixation alone, the addition of plates substantially altered the overall stress distribution pattern and introduced localized stress concentration phenomena. Specifically, peak stress in the PA + L-plate group occurred at the second distal locking screw of the lateral plate, whereas in the PA + P-plate group, it was localized to the first distal locking screw of the posterior plate. Discussion

Hoffa fractures are relatively rare coronal plane fractures of the femoral condyle. Due to traction forces from surrounding soft tissues and shear stresses exerted by the tibial plateau, these fractures are prone to displacement [1, 17]. Consequently, open reduction and internal fixation has been established as the preferred treatment strategy. Although cannulated screw fixation remains a mainstay, the optimal configuration—particularly the choice between anteroposterior (AP) and posteroanterior (PA) screws—continues to be debated [18]. In this study, we confirmed that screw orientation does not significantly affect the biomechanical stability of fixation in Letenneur Type I Hoffa fractures. However, the addition of a plate significantly enhanced fixation stability. More importantly, we reported the distinct biomechanical advantages of a posteriorly placed plate compared to a lateral plate via finite element analysis.

From a biomechanical perspective, PA screws should provide superior resistance to fracture displacement under axial loading. Jarit et al., in a cadaveric femur study, reported that PA screws offered greater stability than AP screws [19]. Conversely, Tang et al. revealed that there is no significant difference in fracture stability between AP and PA screws via finite element analysis [15]. Our results are consistent with the latter, demonstrating comparable fragment displacement and similar screw stress distribution under axial loading. This discrepancy may be attributable to fragment size, as suggested by Liu et al., who proposed that AP screws confer greater stability in larger fragments, whereas PA fixation is more advantageous for smaller fragments [10]. Thus, our findings further support that in the management of simple Letenneur Type I fractures, the choice of screw direction may be guided by surgeon preference and soft tissue conditions, after all, compared to the PA screw, the AP screw can reduce soft tissue dissection and decrease damage to the articular cartilage [9].

To enhance fixation stability in Hoffa fractures—particularly comminuted or osteoporotic variants—supplemental plate fixation is often employed. Cao et al. reported that lateral plate fixation not only provides reliable stability but also facilitates early postoperative mobilization, contributing to satisfactory functional outcomes [13]. Similarly, Zhu et al. compared screw fixation alone with screw fixation combined with a posterior plate and found that the combined approach yielded superior stability and improved functional recovery [12]. However, current evidence on plate fixation for Hoffa fractures is predominantly derived from retrospective clinical studies and the systematic biomechanical data is absent. Our results demonstrated that both lateral and posterior plates significantly reduce fragment displacement compared to screw fixation alone, thereby corroborating these clinical observations. The addition of a plate transforms the construct from one relying solely on interfragmentary screw compression to one that shares and neutralizes applied loads.

In our study, the posterior plate exhibited superior biomechanical performance, limiting fragment displacement to 1.85 mm compared to the lateral plate. This finding is consistent with fundamental principles of internal fixation. As Sun et al. demonstrated in their biomechanical analysis of Letenneur Type I fractures, the posterior plate functions as a true buttress plate, resisting proximal migration of the femoral condylar fragment under axial loading [11]. In contrast, due to the anatomy of the femoral condyle, the lateral plate functions more as a neutralization plate and do not directly oppose proximal fragment displacement [20]. Stress analysis further supports this mechanical distinction. In the lateral plate group, stress was concentrated in the plate region adjacent to the fracture site, with a peak stress of 516.00 MPa localized at the locking screw–plate interface. In the posterior plate group, stress was more effectively distributed along the plate, with a significantly reduced peak stress of 287.21 MPa observed at the most distal screw within the condylar fragment.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly compare the biomechanical performance of plates placed in two distinct positions for Hoffa fractures using finite element analysis. Our findings suggest that, compared to lateral plating, posterior plating not only provides more reliable fracture fixation stability but also reduces the risk of implant failure by optimizing stress distribution and minimizing stress concentration.

However, from a clinical perspective, posterior plate placement is technically more demanding than lateral plate application, primarily due to soft tissue constraints imposed by the gastrocnemius muscle, the plate may instead be positioned posterolateral [21, 22]. Ultimately, the selection of plate location requires comprehensive consideration by the surgeon, weighing biomechanical benefits against anatomical and technical feasibility.

This study has several limitations. First, the finite element model did not incorporate surrounding soft tissue structures such as muscles and ligaments, thereby omitting their potential dynamic stabilizing effects on fracture fragments. Second, the fracture geometry was modeled as a simple, noncomminuted pattern, which does not reflect the comminution or impaction often encountered clinically—factors that may substantially influence fixation stability. Third, the analysis was confined to Letenneur Type I fractures; thus, the applicability of these findings to other fracture types requires further validation. Finally, although finite element analysis provides valuable biomechanical insights, it cannot fully capture the clinical risks associated with different surgical approaches. For instance, the more extensive soft tissue dissection required for posterior plating may increase surgical trauma and potentially elevate complication rates in clinical practice.

Conclusion

In summary, this finite element analysis demonstrated that for Letenneur type I Hoffa fractures, the insertion direction of cannulated screws—either anteroposterior (AP) or posteroanterior (PA)—does not significantly influence fracture fixation stability. Compared with cannulated screws only, combined plates, either posteriorly or laterally, substantially enhance mechanical stability. Furthermore, the posterior plate demonstrates superior biomechanical performance in reducing femoral condylar fragment displacement and dispersing stress.
