Wound complications and clinical efficacy of the direct anterior approach versus the posterolateral approach in total hip arthroplasty: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Wangjie Fu, Yong Deng, Jiongnan Xu, Keyi Chen, Luolong Wang, Songyu He, Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe direct anterior approach (DAA) and the posterolateral approach (PLA) are commonly used surgical techniques in total hip arthroplasty (THA). However, whether DAA is associated with different wound complication rates compared with PLA remains controversial. This meta-analysis aimed to compare wound complications and clinical outcomes between DAA and PLA in THA based on randomized controlled trials.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted across six major electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Chongqing VIP Information (CQVIP). The search strategy encompassed studies indexed from database inception to 31 December 2025. The primary outcomes were wound complications, including incisional infection, hematoma, and delayed wound healing. Predefined eligibility criteria were applied during study screening. The risk of bias of the included studies was assessed using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2). The PLA group was used as the control group. Data were extracted from the included randomized controlled trials (RCTs). A pooled statistical analysis was performed to estimate the rate of wound complications and to compare the clinical effects of DAA and PLA. The meta-analysis was conducted with Review Manager 5.3 and Stata 15.ResultsA total of 18 eligible RCTs, comprising 1,485 patients, were included. Compared with PLA, DAA was associated with decreased rates of incisional infection (RR = 0.48, P = 0.03, I2 = 0%) and a lower overall rate of wound complications (RR = 0.58, P = 0.04, I2 = 0%). No evidence of a significant difference between groups was observed for hematoma incidence, poor wound healing, or overall surgical complications. Regarding perioperative indicators, DAA demonstrated several advantages. DAA was associated with a shorter incision length (MD = -3.44, P < 0.001, I2 = 97%), less intraoperative blood loss (MD = -76.74, P < 0.001, I2 = 97%), and a shorter hospital stay (MD = -2.15, P < 0.001, I2 = 95%). In terms of functional recovery, DAA demonstrated better early postoperative function. The DAA group had higher HHS at 1 month (MD = 5.87, P < 0.001, I2 = 15%), 3 months (MD = 5.10, P < 0.001, I2 = 95%), and 6 months (MD = 2.94, P < 0.001, I2 = 92%). No significant difference was observed at 12 months. For pain outcomes, DAA was associated with lower VAS scores in the postoperative period, including at 1 day (MD = -0.84, P < 0.001, I2 = 59%), 3 days (MD = -0.77, P < 0.001, I2 = 76%), 7 days (MD = -0.85, P < 0.001, I2 = 88%), 1 month (MD = -0.49, P = 0.002, I2 = 90%), 3 month (MD = -0.67, P = 0.02, I2 = 96%), and 6 month (MD = -0.12, P = 0.04, I2 = 75%).ConclusionCompared with the PLA, the DAA is associated with improved perioperative outcomes following THA. Specifically, the DAA demonstrates a lower incidence of incision infection, fewer overall wound complications, shorter incision length, reduced intraoperative blood loss, and a shorter length of hospital stay. Early postoperative functional outcomes and pain, assessed by HHS and VAS, are also superior with the DAA. However, owing to heterogeneity and limited robustness of certain outcomes, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further high-quality randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm these results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12893-026-04010-y.Keywords: Direct anterior approach, Posterolateral approach, Total hip arthroplasty, Incisional infection

Introduction

As demographic aging progresses, the prevalence of hip osteoarthritis continues to rise, leading to considerable socioeconomic challenges [1, 2]. THA is widely considered the preferred intervention for advanced osteoarthritis [3]. Hip pain can be effectively relieved, and hip function is significantly improved after THA, as reported in previous studies [4, 5]. Recent bibliometric and meta-analytic studies have highlighted the evolving research landscape in arthroplasty, as well as the importance of perioperative optimization and complication assessment in hip and knee replacement surgery [6–10].

Postoperative wound complications remain a significant clinical challenge in total hip arthroplasty (THA) and are associated with considerable morbidity. Wound-related adverse events, including surgical site infections and delayed healing, can impair recovery, prolong hospitalization, and increase healthcare costs. Therefore, a clear understanding of the incidence and characteristics of wound complications associated with different surgical approaches in THA is essential. Such evidence may help inform clinical decision-making and optimize patient outcomes.

At present, multiple surgical approaches are used in THA. These approaches include the anterolateral approach (ALA), PLA, direct lateral approach (DLA), and DAA [11–15]. In the ALA, access is obtained through the gluteus medius, vastus lateralis, and external rotator muscle groups [15]. The posterior approach remains a widely adopted technique in routine total hip replacement practice. Increased postoperative creatine kinase levels have been reported in patients undergoing the PLA, and creatine kinase is considered a biomarker of muscle injury and inflammation [16, 17]. In comparison, the DAA is regarded as a favorable surgical approach. A limited skin incision is created, and the procedure is performed through the intermuscular plane between the sartorius, rectus femoris, and tensor fascia lata, thereby minimizing damage to surrounding soft tissues [18–20]. In recent years, the DAA has demonstrated advantages over other approaches due to its minimally invasive characteristics. Reduced postoperative pain and accelerated functional recovery have been reported, and the DAA is now widely adopted in clinical practice. Meanwhile, surgical techniques have been refined, and dedicated instruments have been improved, further optimizing clinical outcomes associated with the DAA [21–23]. In addition, optimized perioperative management, including the use of tranexamic acid to reduce blood loss and aspirin or other anticoagulants for thromboprophylaxis, has been shown to further improve outcomes following total hip arthroplasty [24–26]. Although these studies did not directly compare surgical approaches, they provide important context for understanding perioperative risk and functional recovery.

However, the risk of postoperative complications associated with the DAA remains controversial. It has been reported that the DAA may be associated with a higher incidence of postoperative complications [27–29]. Previous meta-analyses comparing surgical approaches for THA have not provided a detailed assessment of wound complication rates between the DAA and PLA. Furthermore, an in-depth analysis of common wound complications has been lacking [30–33]. Therefore, a meta-analysis comparing the DAA and PLA was performed. Primary outcomes were defined as wound complications, including incisional infection, hematoma, and delayed wound healing. Secondary outcomes included surgical parameters, such as overall surgical complications, incision length, and intraoperative blood loss. Postoperative functional and pain outcomes were also evaluated, including hip scores and visual analog scale scores. The objective of this study was to determine differences in wound complication rates and clinical efficacy between the DAA and PLA after THA, based on high-quality randomized controlled trial evidence.

Methods

Reporting guideline and protocol registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines. The protocol of this study was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261332627).

Selection strategy and eligibility criteria

Study eligibility criteria were defined according to the PICOS framework. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty (THA); (2) randomized controlled trials (RCTs); (3) studies comparing the direct anterior approach (DAA) with the posterolateral approach (PLA); and (4) studies reporting postoperative complications or relevant clinical outcomes after THA.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) duplicate publications; (2) review articles, conference abstracts, bibliometric studies, or other non-original research articles; (3) studies with unavailable or insufficient data for extraction; and (4) non-randomized controlled trials.

Search strategy

Two reviewers independently conducted a systematic literature search in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CNKI, and CQVIP from inception to December 31, 2025. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords were used to ensure comprehensive retrieval of eligible studies. The detailed search strategy for each database is provided in Supplementary Table S1. All retrieved records were imported into EndNote for duplicate removal and screening.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data from the 18 included studies, recording study characteristics (first author, year, design, patient age, sex distribution, BMI, and follow-up duration) and clinical outcomes (wound- and surgery-related complications, incision length, blood loss, VAS, and HHS). These study-level characteristics were subsequently used for prespecified subgroup and meta-regression analyses to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Outcome definitions are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. In the included randomized controlled trials, patient-level data were not available and most studies did not explicitly report whether individual wound-related events were mutually exclusive; therefore, potential overlap among different wound-related outcomes at the patient level could not be determined. In this meta-analysis, each wound-related outcome was analyzed independently using aggregate data as reported in the original studies, without assuming mutual exclusivity or performing any adjustment or redistribution of potentially overlapping events across outcomes. Accordingly, independence in the present study is defined at the outcome level rather than the patient level, and possible co-occurrence of different wound events within the same patient does not affect the estimation of effect sizes for each individual outcome.

Quality assessment

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool. Each study was evaluated at the outcome level across five domains: bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, and bias in selection of the reported result. An overall risk of bias judgment (low risk, some concerns, or high risk) was then assigned for each outcome according to the RoB 2 algorithm [34].

Two reviewers independently conducted the assessments, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion to reach consensus. If consensus could not be achieved, a third reviewer was consulted to make the final decision.

Assessment of heterogeneity

Both fixed-effect and random-effects models were used for meta-analysis. The fixed-effect model (Mantel–Haenszel method) was applied when heterogeneity was low (I2 ≤ 50%), whereas the random-effects model was used when heterogeneity was substantial (I2 > 50%). The between-study variance (τ2) was estimated using the DerSimonian and Laird method. In addition, to assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses using a random-effects model were performed for all outcomes.

Publication bias was assessed for outcomes with ≥ 10 studies using funnel plots, Begg’s test, and Egger’s test; trim-and-fill analysis was applied when potential bias was detected. Subgroup analyses were prespecified based on clinically relevant variables, including age, sex, BMI, and follow-up duration, chosen to explore whether differences in patient characteristics or study follow-up might contribute to heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses were performed using standard meta-analytic methods, with each subgroup analyzed separately. These analyses are considered exploratory, as no formal statistical tests for subgroup differences (i.e., interaction tests) were conducted, and findings should be interpreted with caution.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative synthesis was conducted with Review Manager and Stata software. For binary outcomes, treatment effects were calculated as risk ratios (RRs). Continuous variables were synthesized using weighted mean differences (WMDs) were used when all included studies applied identical measurement scales and units. In cases where different instruments or non-comparable units were employed, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were adopted instead. Pooled effect sizes were reported together with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-tailed P value ≤ 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Certainty of evidence (GRADE assessment)

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) approach. The assessment was performed at the outcome level considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The overall certainty of evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low according to the GRADE framework, and summarized in the Summary of Findings table.

Results

Study selection and basic characteristics

The comprehensive database retrieval process initially identified 4,541 potentially relevant records. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 685 articles were retained for initial assessment. After applying publication type restrictions, 45 studies remained. A total of 45 studies were then assessed in full text according to the established eligibility criteria. In the end, 18 studies satisfied the inclusion criteria and entered the final analysis (Fig. 1). All included investigations were randomized controlled trials. In all selected trials, 1,485 patients were recruited; 740 received DAA, whereas 745 underwent PLA. Key features of these studies are presented in Table 1.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological rigor of the included randomized controlled trials was assessed using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2), and the detailed results are presented in Figs. 2 and 3. All studies included in this meta-analysis were randomized controlled trials. Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies was considered acceptable, with most studies demonstrating relatively adequate study design and implementation. Quantitatively, 7 studies were rated as low risk of bias, 9 as having some concerns, and 2 as high risk of bias according to the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. The domains most commonly associated with potential bias were the randomization process and the measurement of outcomes, mainly due to insufficient reporting of allocation concealment and blinding of outcome assessors. Nevertheless, most studies showed a low risk of bias regarding deviations from intended interventions and missing outcome data. These findings indicate that while most domains showed acceptable methodological quality, limitations in randomization and outcome reporting should be considered when interpreting the pooled results. According to the GRADE assessment, the certainty of evidence varied across outcomes, with three outcomes rated as moderate and three as low. Most downgrades were due to inconsistency, imprecision, and potential publication bias (Table S3).

Primary outcomes: wound complications

Incisional infection

Twelve included studies (n = 1008; 500 experimental, 504 control) were analyzed for incisional infection [29, 35, 37–42, 46, 47, 49, 51]. In the DAA group, the incidence of incisional infections was lower than that of the PLA group. (RR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.25 to 0.92, P = 0.03, I2 = 0%, Fig. 4). Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of the funnel plots (Fig. S1) and statistically by Egger’s test (P = 0.367), and Begg’s test (P = 0.244) (Figs. S2-S4). Sensitivity analysis using a leave-one-out approach revealed that exclusion of most individual studies did not materially alter the pooled effect. However, in several cases, removal of specific studies caused the 95% CI to cross the null value (RR = 1), indicating that the statistical significance of this outcome was influenced by particular trials. These findings suggest that while the DAA may be associated with a reduced risk of incisional infection, the robustness of this result is limited and should be interpreted with caution (Fig. S5; Table S4).

Hematoma

Six included studies (n = 468; 234 experimental, 234 control) were analyzed for hematoma [38, 41, 43, 44, 46, 48]. The comparison revealed no statistically significant difference between the study arms (RR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.22 to 1.63, P = 0.32, I2 = 0%, Fig. 5). Findings from the sensitivity analysis indicate that the overall stability of the pooled results was good (Fig. S6).

Wound healing

Two included studies (n = 210; 105 experimental, 105 control) were analyzed for wound healing [36, 45]. No significant disparity was identified between the study arms (RR = 2. 33, 95% CI: 0. 35 to 15. 52, P = 0. 38, I2 = 0%, Fig. 6).

Forest plot of the effect of DAA versus PLA on poor wound healing

Overall wound complications

Eighteen included studies (n = 1,485; 740 experimental, 745 control) were analyzed for total wound complications [29, 35–51]. Overall wound-related complications were less frequent in participants undergoing DAA compared with PLA (RR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.35 to 0.98, P = 0.04, I2 = 0%; Fig. 7). Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots (Fig. S7) and statistically using Egger’s test (P = 0.087) and Begg’s test (P = 0.034) (Figs. S8–S10). The results were inconsistent, with Begg’s test suggesting potential publication bias whereas Egger’s test did not detect significant asymmetry. A trim-and-fill analysis was subsequently performed, and the adjusted pooled estimate remained largely unchanged (Fig. S11). However, the possibility of publication bias cannot be excluded and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Sensitivity analyses further showed that although the direction of effect remained unchanged, exclusion of certain studies resulted in the 95% confidence interval crossing the null value, suggesting limited robustness of the pooled estimate (Fig. S12; Table S5).

Forest plot of the effect of DAA versus PLA on total wound complications

Secondary outcomes: Surgery-related outcome indicators

Surgical complications

Eighteen included studies (n = 1,485; 740 experimental, 745 control) were analyzed for surgical complications [29, 35–51]. No significant difference was observed between the two groups (RR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.34–1.10, P = 0.10, I2 = 55%; Fig. 8). Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot inspection as well as Egger’s test (P = 0.291) and Begg’s test (P = 0.096) (Figs. S13–S15). Neither test provided strong evidence of publication bias, although the results should be interpreted cautiously given the limited power of these methods. Sensitivity analysis indicated that the study by Yan had a notable influence on the pooled estimate; its exclusion rendered the result statistically significant and reduced heterogeneity. These findings suggest that the pooled effect was driven, to some extent, by this individual study, and should therefore be interpreted with caution (Figs. S16–S17; Table S6).

Forest plot of the effect of DAA versus PLA on surgical complications

Incision length

Eleven included studies (n = 1,186; 593 experimental, 593 control) were analyzed for incision length [29, 36–45, 48, 51]. The pooled analysis indicated that the DAA was associated with a shorter incision length than the PLA (MD = − 3.44, 95% CI: − 4.71 to − 2.17, P < 0.001; I2 = 100%; Fig. 9). Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plot inspection (Fig. S18), Egger’s test (P = 0.326), and Begg’s test (P = 0.30) (Figs. S19–S21), none of which suggested significant publication bias. To investigate the observed heterogeneity, subgroup analyses and meta-regression were conducted according to age, BMI, sex distribution, and follow-up duration; however, none of these variables significantly explained the between-study variability (Fig. S22; Table S7). Sensitivity analyses yielded broadly similar pooled estimates (Fig. S23; Table S8). Nevertheless, the extremely high level of heterogeneity remained largely unexplained, indicating substantial variability across studies and limiting the robustness, interpretability, and generalizability of the pooled effect estimate.

Intraoperative blood loss

Twelve studies (n = 911; 456 experimental, 455 control) were included in the analysis of intraoperative blood loss [29, 35–39, 41, 42, 44, 48, 51]. The pooled analysis indicated that intraoperative blood loss was lower in the DAA group compared with the PLA group (MD = − 76.74, 95% CI: − 94.20 to − 59.27, P < 0.001; I2 = 97%; Fig. 10). Potential publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots (Fig. S24) and statistically using Egger’s test (P = 0.718) and Begg’s test (P = 1.000) (Figs. S25–S27). Neither test indicated evidence of publication bias. Subgroup analyses and meta-regression were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity; however, none of the examined variables showed a significant association with the pooled effect estimates (Fig. S28; Table S9). Sensitivity analyses suggested that the overall findings were generally consistent across studies, although the substantial heterogeneity should be considered when interpreting the results (Fig. S29; Table S10).

The pooled analysis indicated that hospital stay was shorter in the DAA group compared with the PLA group (MD = − 2.15, 95% CI: − 2.83 to − 1.47, P < 0.001; I2 = 95%; Fig. 11). Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots (Fig. S30) and statistically using Egger’s test (P = 0.202) and Begg’s test (P = 0.631) (Figs. S31–S33). Eighteen studies (n = 1,485; 740 experimental, 745 control) were included in the analysis of total complications [29, 35–51]. No significant difference was observed between the DAA and PLA groups (RR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.35 to 1.02, P = 0.06; I2 = 65%; Fig. 12). Secondary outcomes: Postoperative functional and pain assessment indicators

Harris Hip Score

HHS was assessed at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. For HHS at 1 month, four studies involving 314 participants compared outcomes between the DAA and PLA groups [37, 41, 44, 51]. The pooled analysis showed significantly higher HHS in the DAA group (MD = 4.18, 95% CI: 3.23 to 5.13, P < 0.001; I2 = 45%; Fig. 13A). For HHS at 3 months, nine studies involving 775 participants were included [35–38, 41, 44, 45, 48, 49]. Patients in the DAA group had significantly higher HHS than those in the PLA group (MD = 5.10, 95% CI: 2.13 to 8.08, P < 0.001; I2 = 95%; Fig. 13B). For HHS at 6 months, data from 11 studies involving 1,032 participants were pooled [29, 36, 37, 39–41, 44, 45, 48, 49, 51]. HHS remained significantly higher in the DAA group (MD = 2.94, 95% CI: 1.35 to 4.53, P < 0.001; I2 = 92%; Fig. 13C). For HHS at 12 months, four studies involving 268 participants were included [36, 46, 48, 49]. No significant difference in HHS was observed between the DAA and PLA groups (MD = 0.05, 95% CI: − 0.58 to 0.68, P = 0.87; I2 = 0%; Fig. 13D).

Discussion

Key findings

The present study aimed to provide evidence-based guidance on wound complications and perioperative outcomes in patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty via the DAA versus the PLA. Eighteen RCTs were included. Overall, the DAA was associated with a lower incidence of surgical site infections and a reduced overall frequency of wound-related complications compared with the PLA. However, no statistically significant differences were observed for individual wound complications such as hematoma or delayed wound healing.

In terms of perioperative and clinical outcomes, the DAA was generally associated with shorter incision length, lower intraoperative blood loss, and reduced postoperative length of hospital stay. No significant difference was observed in overall surgical complications. Early postoperative functional recovery appeared to be more favorable with the DAA. Nevertheless, some outcomes exhibited substantial between-study heterogeneity, and these results should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Comparison with previous literature

The present study showed that the DAA correlates with a reduced incidence of incisional infection and overall wound adverse events. This observation aligns with the outcomes reported by Wang et al. [52]. In contrast, opposing results were reported in a multicenter retrospective study published in 2024 by Harold Salmons et al. [53]. A total of 17,111 patients were included and were divided into a DAA group and a lateral approach group, which consisted of lateral and posterolateral subgroups. The findings demonstrated that the wound complication rate in the DAA group was 3.6%, which was significantly higher than the 2.6% observed in the lateral approach group. In addition, some studies have indicated that no meaningful statistical disparity was observed regarding the incidence of wound-related adverse events across the two surgical approaches [54, 55].

Current evidence on wound complication rates among different surgical approaches shows considerable inconsistency, and several factors may account for this variation. First, a clear learning curve effect is observed in DAA procedures. Previous studies [56, 57] have shown that after 50 to 100 cases are completed and sufficient surgical experience is gained, the complication rate decreases markedly. Second, differences in surgical technique exist among surgeons. Variations are also present in clinical practice standards and perioperative protocols across institutions. These differences may influence reported outcomes. Third, methodological limitations are present in some relevant studies. As an illustration, in the investigation conducted by Miller et al. [55], a considerable share of the enrolled trials adopted a retrospective design. Such studies are inherently subject to bias, and the reliability of their findings may therefore be reduced. Furthermore, data on poor wound healing were reported in only two of the included studies, and the sample sizes were limited. The strength of this evidence is therefore restricted. These findings need to be confirmed by larger, high-quality prospective clinical studies.

Regarding surgical-related indicators, it was shown in our analysis that DAA procedures were associated with shorter incision lengths, less intraoperative blood loss, and shorter postoperative length of stay. The present results are in agreement with those described in earlier investigations [30, 52, 55, 58–63]. With respect to postoperative functional and pain assessment outcomes, it was found that patients treated with the DAA achieved significantly better early postoperative functional recovery. No significant difference was observed in late functional recovery. The present observations are in accordance with evidence documented in earlier research [64–66]. The favorable early outcomes of the DAA are likely related to its minimally invasive characteristics. The DAA approach is performed through the intermuscular interval delineated by the sartorius, rectus femoris, and tensor fasciae lata muscles. This interval is used without detaching major muscles. As a result, muscle integrity is preserved, and hip external rotation function is maintained [20]. Early functional recovery was also associated with a shorter length of stay, and the overall financial burden on patients was therefore reduced [67, 68]. However, certain limitations are present in previous studies. As illustrated in the systematic review performed by Wang et al. [30], a retrospective study by Zhang et al. [69] was included. This inclusion introduced methodological heterogeneity and may have affected the reliability of their results.

It should also be noted that substantial heterogeneity was observed for several secondary outcomes in the present meta-analysis, with I2 values approaching or exceeding 90% in some analyses. Although subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed, the sources of heterogeneity could not be fully explained. Differences in patient demographics, surgical expertise, perioperative management protocols, rehabilitation strategies, and outcome assessment methods may have contributed to the observed variability. Consequently, the pooled effect estimates for these outcomes should be interpreted cautiously, as the magnitude of the reported benefits may not be consistent across different clinical settings.

Implications for clinical practice and future research

The present study suggests that the DAA may be associated with a lower incidence of postoperative wound infections following THA, providing supportive evidence for its potential clinical benefits. However, given the modest statistical significance and high heterogeneity for several outcomes, this finding should be interpreted with caution. The DAA was also associated with improved early postoperative functional recovery compared with the PLA, as reflected by slightly higher HHS and lower VAS scores in the first few months after surgery. While these differences reached statistical significance, their magnitude was relatively small and may represent only modest improvements in patient-perceived function and pain. No differences were observed in longer-term outcomes, indicating that early benefits may not persist over time. The shorter incision length observed in the DAA group may further reduce soft tissue trauma and potentially contribute to a lower risk of wound-related complications [70]. Similarly, the observed reduction in intraoperative blood loss (approximately 76.74 mL) is unlikely to be clinically meaningful. No differences were observed in longer-term functional outcomes, indicating that the early benefit may not persist over time. These findings suggest that although the DAA offers certain perioperative advantages, the overall clinical impact may be limited. Overall, these findings suggest that the DAA may offer certain perioperative advantages; however, the substantial heterogeneity observed for several outcomes limits the certainty and generalizability of these findings. However, definitive conclusions should be drawn cautiously, and further high-quality, large-scale randomized controlled trials are required to confirm these observations.

Advantages

To our knowledge, this study represents the first quantitative synthesis of randomized controlled trials directly comparing the DAA and PLA with respect to postoperative wound complications and clinical outcomes following hip arthroplasty. The analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines, enhancing methodological rigor and transparency. Unlike previous meta-analyses that included non-randomized studies or indirect comparisons, only randomized controlled trials were considered, thereby minimizing potential bias, clarifying causal associations, and providing a higher level of evidence. By specifically focusing on wound-related complications, this study offers robust data to support evidence-based clinical decision-making [71]. A comprehensive literature search was performed across six major electronic databases—PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CNKI, and CQVIP—using controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) without restrictions on publication date or language. This approach maximized the identification of relevant studies and enhanced the reproducibility and reliability of the findings.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, substantial heterogeneity was observed in several outcomes, with I2 values exceeding 75%. This may reflect clinical and methodological differences across studies, including variations in patient characteristics, surgeon experience, perioperative management, rehabilitation protocols, and follow-up duration. Although subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, considerable variability remained. Therefore, the pooled estimates for these outcomes should be interpreted with caution, and their robustness and generalizability may be limited.

Second, the random-effects analyses were conducted using the DerSimonian–Laird method. More robust approaches, such as the Hartung–Knapp adjustment, have been recommended to improve the reliability of pooled estimates and provide more conservative confidence intervals in the presence of substantial heterogeneity [72].

A methodological limitation of this study is that most included randomized controlled trials did not provide sufficient information to determine whether individual wound-related complications were mutually exclusive at the patient level; therefore, potential overlap among wound-related outcomes such as incisional infection and delayed wound healing cannot be excluded. However, this does not affect the validity of the analyses, as each outcome was independently assessed using study-level aggregate data without cross-outcome aggregation or double counting. Accordingly, the results reflect outcome-specific risk estimates rather than patient-level composite incidences of wound complications.

Conclusion

Compared with the PLA, the DAA may be associated with a lower risk of incisional infection, a reduced incidence of wound complications, shorter incision length, less intraoperative blood loss, shorter postoperative hospital stay, and improved early postoperative functional recovery following THA. These findings suggest potential perioperative advantages of the DAA; However, they should be interpreted in the context of existing heterogeneity and limited robustness of certain outcomes. Further high-quality randomized trials are required to confirm these results.
