Implementation of spirometry screening for post-tuberculosis lung disease (PTLD) among adolescents and adults enrolled within the National Tuberculosis Control Program of Carmelo Hospital in Chókwè District, Mozambique: A hybrid type III effectiveness-implementation study protocol

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant public health problem and a leading cause of death from infectious disease worldwide [1, 2]. There is an increasing body of evidence suggesting that after completion of TB treatment, despite adequate chemotherapy, a significant proportion of TB survivors are left with post-TB sequelae, in part due to damage of lung tissues during the period of active TB disease [3]. Chronic inflammation, tissue damage, and destructive healing responses as a result of persistent TB infection are pathophysiologic effects of TB disease that are known to result in damaged airways, lung parenchyma, and pleura that persists after completion of TB treatment [4, 5].

Current TB treatment programs around the world provide well-structured care up to the completion of TB treatment but frequently provide no structured care thereafter [6]. In recent years, the global scientific community has convened to collaboratively examine the problem of sequelae from pulmonary TB and noted the paucity of quality research on individuals surviving TB. Expert panels have developed consensus definitions for post-tuberculosis lung disease (PTLD) and specific clinical standards for the assessment and management of PTLD [7]. They highlighted the need for increased awareness about PTLD, and the urgent need for research to generate evidence to guide the development of solutions for people formally living with TB who have sequelae [7, 8]. Experts have recently included PTLD as a natural progression of TB and are advocating for its integration into national TB guidelines as a key component of comprehensive care [9].

PTLD is a leading, underrecognized cause of chronic lung disease in persons with and without HIV. Worldwide, 10.6 million people fell ill with TB in 2022, and 6.3% were among people living with HIV (PLHIV) [10]. Between a third to a half of TB survivors are estimated to go on to develop PTLD [11–16]. PTLD is defined by the presence of chronic respiratory abnormalities, with or without symptoms, attributable at least in part to previous TB infection [8]. It is characterized subjectively by persistent symptoms after TB treatment completion, and objectively by either abnormalities in chest imaging or lung function, often measured by spirometry. Individuals with PTLD often have chronic respiratory symptoms including cough, dyspnea, wheeze and lower quality of life [12, 17, 18]. PTLD increases the risk for subsequent pulmonary infections including recurrent TB [19].

PTLD management options are those used for chronic respiratory diseases (CRD). They consist of optimizing pharmacotherapy, as well as individually tailored pulmonary rehabilitation interventions designed to overcome the deconditioning associated with CRDs. For those patients in which spirometry indicates an obstructive impairment, referral for bronchodilator and steroid therapy may be warranted, whereas individuals with restrictive patterns may benefit from pulmonary rehabilitation [7]. Current evidence is mainly limited to adult patients in high-income countries, [20] and the design of a basic pulmonary rehabilitation package to offer to persons with TB completing treatment in LMICs is still pending.

A rising national HIV prevalence from 2% in 1990 to 12.5% in 2021 has fueled the TB epidemic in Mozambique [21]. Mozambique is one of the World Health Organization´s (WHO) 30 high TB burden countries and faces many challenges in its successful control, especially among PLHIV [22, 23]. In 2021, the WHO estimated Mozambique’s TB prevalence to be 361/100,000 people. However, only 84% of estimated cases were diagnosed and reported to the National Tuberculosis Control Program (NTCP) [24]. It is estimated that nearly 60% of people with TB in Mozambique are co-infected with HIV and that TB is the most common opportunistic infection in PLHIV [25].

In this effectiveness implementation study protocol of the roll-out of symptom and spirometry screening for PTLD among those who have completed TB treatment in Chókwè District, Mozambique, we describe the different implementation outcomes we aim to evaluate, our spirometry implementation evaluation measures, and the conceptual frameworks that guided protocol development.

Methods/design

Aims and objectives

The overall objective of this study is to evaluate the introduction of a new screening program that utilizes spirometry for diagnosing PTLD among adolescents and adults (≥ 15 years of age) that have completed TB treatment. The research protocol includes three complementary components: 1) defining the prevalence of PTLD in patients enrolled in the NTCP of Carmelo Hospital in Chókwè District, Mozambique (PTLD will be defined by abnormal spirometry per the American Thoracic Society / European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) standard interpretation) [26, 27], 2) evaluating anticipated implementation outcomes through identification of the site-, provider-, and individual-level determinants that either promote or inhibit the successful adoption, implementation, and maintenance of the spirometry screening program through the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), and 3) evaluating actual implementation outcomes/processes, in real time, in order to provide practical evidence-based key indicators of successful implementation of the spirometry screening program as defined by the five domains of the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance) implementation science conceptual framework. The reporting of this protocol adheres to the SPIRIT checklist [28].

Study setting

The introduction of a new intervention into a clinical setting is a complex process and requires support and buy-in at each facility. This study will use a hybrid type III effectiveness-implementation design. In a hybrid type III design, the primary focus is on testing of an implementation strategy, while simultaneously continuing to observe and gather information on the effectiveness of a clinical intervention, under real world conditions, for which effectiveness data has already largely been determined [29, 30].

Carmelo Hospital of Chókwè (CHC) is a reference hospital for TB and HIV in the district of Chókwè, a large rural district in Gaza Province in southern Mozambique (Fig. 1). CHC is a rural hospital administered by the Daughters of Charity, Saint Vincent de Paul, Catholic missionaries since 1995 in partnership with the local governmental health authorities (public–private partnership with free health care services). Chókwè has a surrounding catchment population of approximately 200,000 inhabitants. Agriculture is the predominant source of local income for roughly 60% of the population, while approximately 40% of the population migrates seasonally to South Africa, seeking work in the mines and good living conditions [31]. Per the latest national HIV prevalence study in 2021, Gaza Province had the highest provincial HIV prevalence in the country at 20.9% [21]. CHC was chosen for this study due to its 1) established TB and HIV care and treatment services; 2) being a geographic location with a high TB and HIV prevalence; 3) on-site presence of the GeneXpert platform for TB diagnosis; and 4) established research capacity for implementation science.

In addition to CHC, which will serve as the spirometry screening pilot site for much of this study, we have selected three additional health facilities near CHC in Chókwè District which have the physical and human resource conditions for the possible future expansion of spirometry screening (Centro de Saúde da Cidade, Centro de Saúde de Chalucuane, and Centro de Saúde Urbano). Semi-structured in-depth interviews with the administrators and clinicians of CHC and these additional facilities will be conducted as part of our evaluation.

The study team at CHC consists of the Medical Director (a physician) who will oversee study personnel consisting of a Medical Technician (Clinical Officer equivalent), a Nurse, and a Data Manager. This team will be responsible for the day-to-day implementation of the spirometry screening program at CHC. This will include screening for eligibility, seeking informed consent/assent, collecting initial clinical and sociodemographic data, conducting the spirometry screening at enrollment (TB treatment completion) and at six months post-TB treatment, active engagement in the study´s spirometry quality assurance program, and overseeing study follow-up procedures. Experienced investigators both locally and internationally will be responsible for the oversight of qualitative interviews conducted and data collected with both health system employees as well as study participants as a part of this study. This includes coordination of interview times and places; conducting and recording of the interviews; as well as processing of the data recordings post interview, transcribing of the interviews, and working with the broader study team on analysis of the interview findings and report drafting.

Study design and conceptual frameworks

To assess the implementation outcomes, we will conduct a mixed-methods evaluation employing both quantitative and qualitative methods. This study will be guided by two different conceptual frameworks commonly used in implementation research: the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), and the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework.

CFIR is a comprehensive framework composed of constructs associated with effective implementation [32]. CFIR’s 39 constructs are organized into five domains: Intervention Characteristics; Outer Setting; Inner Setting; Characteristics of Individuals; and Implementation measures (Process) [33]. CFIR can be used at multiple phases of implementation and facilitates multi-level analysis (including individual, organizational, environment and process). RE-AIM provides practical information across five domains: Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance that can improve the translation of evidence-based interventions into practice and their public health impact [34].

Understanding which constructs, or sets of constructs promote or inhibit adoption, implementation, and maintenance can inform development during planning of tailored and testable implementation strategies [35]. Examining the presence or absence of CFIR constructs can explain “why” implementation was or was not successful, while RE-AIM describes outcomes in terms of “who, what, where, how, and when” [36].

Intervention

To document the prevalence of PTLD in patients enrolled at CHC, we will conduct a prospective observational study in which we introduce spirometry and symptom screening within the NTCP at baseline (completion of six-months of TB treatment) and at six-months after completion of TB treatment. Patient enrollment into the spirometry screening program will continue for approximately three months following study initiation. Selection of study participants will be by convenience, wherein all patients of the NTCP who meet the inclusion criteria during the three-month enrollment window will be approached for study participation.

Subjects will be eligible for study participation if they meet all of the following criteria at the time of enrollment: 1) are ≥ 15 years of age, 2) are enrolled in the NTCP at CHC with a GeneXpert positive sputum, 3) have successfully completed six-months of TB treatment and considered TB cured, and 4) have the ability to understand and comply with study procedures.

Participants will be required to sign written informed consent to be enrolled in the study. If participants are between 15–17 years of age, then additional signed informed assent will be required by the participant as well as an informed consent from their parent or guardian. Subjects will be considered ineligible for study participation if they present with active respiratory symptoms at the time of triage for the study that are indicative of a new acute respiratory illness (fever, cough, chest congestion, or sputum production in the last week despite TB therapy).

Enrolled participants will complete standardized respiratory symptom and quality of life questionnaires at each visit. Spirometry will be performed using the ultrasound-based ndd EasyOne® Spirometer. Forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) and Forced Vital Capacity (FVC) will be measured in all participants according to ATS/ERS standards [26]. Per these criteria, we will use spirometers that meet the International Organization for Standardization (IOS) standards. Spirometers will be calibrated daily and spirogram quality monitored by a board certified pulmonologist [26]. Each participant will perform a minimum of three flow-volume loops at the baseline visit. At the six-month follow-up visit, participants will complete a minimum of three flow-volume loops before and after administration of a standard dose of an inhaled bronchodilator via disposable spacer. Each participant will realize up to a maximum of eight attempts to achieve the three acceptable measurements. In-person training will be provided to all study personnel prior to study launch on how to perform spirometry. Ongoing quality control and oversight will include real-time verification by the site clinical supervisor, as well as remote review and quality scoring of spirograms by the US-based board-certified pulmonologist. Twice monthly quality control meetings will be held with the study personnel for ongoing virtual spirometry training, tutorials on administration of spirometry, and feedback on spirograms to ensure study staff are able to perform and interpret high-quality spirometry tests.

Ongoing feedback provided to the research staff will highlight corrective actions that are needed and reinforce high quality maneuvers. Highest FEV1 and FVC from acceptable efforts for each participant will be recorded (Fig. 2). Race-neutral Global Lung Initiative (GLI) reference equations, will determine predicted values [27, 37–39]. In primary analyses, we will model z-scores for FEV1, FVC and their ratio as continuous measures; results will also be characterized as normal vs. abnormal indicating PTLD, using standard criteria to define obstruction and restrictive patterns. The severity of airflow limitation will be based on the degree of impairment in FEV1 and/or FVC per ATS/ERS standards [26, 40]. We will compare respiratory symptoms and quality of life in those with and without impaired spirometry.

Spirometry process flowchart

Following the completion of the six-month spirometry screening exam, participants will be asked a series of questions related to their experience participating in the screening. Possible responses will include yes/no or the selection of a choice on a three-point Likert scale consisting of Agree, Disagree, and Neutral.

Intervention sample size

Between 2006 and 2017, the NTCP at CHC registered an average of 60 patients per month, of whom nearly 90% completed a full six-month course of TB treatment [31]. However, since the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic, average enrollment numbers in the NTCP at CHC have been closer to 35 patients per month. Based on these numbers we anticipate that approximately 32 patients per month for each of the three months of our enrollment period will be completing TB treatment (total N = 96 people treated for TB enrolled). Among them, we anticipate that 80% will be retained six months after completing TB treatment. This will lead to a total of 96 questionnaires completed at enrollment and 76 questionnaires completed at six months after TB treatment.

With 96 participants enrolled, we expect to estimate an underlying prevalence of 50% with a margin of error of 10%; if the true prevalence is lower, e.g., 30%, the margin of error will be approximately equal to 9%. Having slightly fewer patients (e.g., 90) at the first post-spirometry questionnaire will not have a large impact on the precision; the margin of error will still be under 10.5% to estimate an underlying prevalence of 50%. At the second post-spirometry questionnaire, 6-months after TB treatment completion, we expect a margin of error not larger than 11.5% for a 50% underlying prevalence (Fig. 3).

Key informant interviews

We will conduct semi-structured key informant interviews with representatives of the NTCP at the level of the Ministry of Health (MOH), hospital administrators at CHC, and clinicians that work within both the NTCP and HIV care and treatment services of health facilities in the District of Chókwè. In addition, every participant enrolled in the study will complete a post-spirometry questionnaire related to their experiences being screened.

We will employ purposive sampling of health workers who have knowledge of the functioning of the NTCP at different levels of the health system. We will conduct a total of approximately 16–19 semi-structured key-informant interviews which will include a minimum of two participants at the central level of the NTCP in Maputo (Mozambique’s capital city), two administrators of CHC, and one administrator at each of three selected peripheral health facilities. In addition, we will conduct interviews with the three clinicians (1 physician, 1 medical technician, and 1 nurse) responsible for implementing the spirometry screening program at CHC, as well as all clinicians responsible for treating TB at each of the three selected peripheral health facilities (we estimate there will be between two to three clinicians at each of the three health facilities).

Our sample size and selection methods related to the implementation outcomes objectives were determined by the research questions, the range of responses we expect to collect from key informants with diverse knowledge of the functioning of the NTCP at different levels of the health system, and the mechanisms and resources that are available for accessing the key informant population. We determined that using a purposive sampling method would allow us to approach and solicit the input of a wide range of experts who are knowledgeable about the specific issues related to the functioning of the NTCP and have a broad range of perspectives.

Anticipated and actual implementation outcomes

We aim to explore anticipated implementation outcomes through identification of the site-, provider-, and individual level determinants that either promote or inhibit the successful adoption, implementation, and maintenance of the spirometry screening program. To do this, we will evaluate constructs from the CFIR, including perceptions about the intervention characteristics (e.g., facilitators, potential challenges, eligibility, and cost); outer setting factors influencing use (e.g., perception of patient needs and resources, demand creation, commitment); inner setting factors impacting uptake and fidelity to the intervention (e.g., resources needed, workload, standard operating procedures, infrastructure); characteristics of individuals (e.g., knowledge, competency, belief it can be done with quality); and process factors of implementation (e.g., fidelity, execution, and evaluation) within each health facility. We will explore how these factors influence uptake and fidelity throughout implementation (Fig. 4).

Consolidated framework for implementation research: domains and factors that impact implementation

We will evaluate actual implementation outcomes/processes, in real time, in order to provide practical evidence-based information based on key indicators of successful implementation of the spirometry screening program. The RE-AIM model was developed to increase the impact of health promotion interventions by assessing domains considered most relevant for real-world implementation, such as their ability to reach underserved populations and be adopted in diverse settings. The Reach domain refers to the percentage and characteristics of individuals who receive the intervention; Effectiveness refers to the impact of the intervention, including expected and unanticipated outcomes; Adoption concerns the percentage and representativeness of environments that adopt the intervention; Implementation refers to the consistency and cost of delivering the intervention; and Maintenance refers to the long-term sustainability at both the facility and the individual level.

Data management

Intervention data will be collected by study clinicians at CHC utilizing a paper-based instrument and then uploaded into an online Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) database. Clinical data will be extracted from routinely collected data in the patient´s medical record that is maintained in the NTCP. A new form will be created for each respondent, and forms will be submitted immediately upon completion of the spirometry screening visit. Risk of information sharing will be minimized through a password-protected desktop account with restricted device access. Data will be exported to Excel and “R” statistical software for further analysis and the files will only be shared with relevant staff members. Data quality control will be conducted by study investigators who will review all completed paper-based study instruments and confirm the accuracy of data entered into REDCap.

Semi-structured interviews with key informants will be conducted face-to-face in a quiet and private location and audio recorded. The CFIR and RE-AIM framework will be employed to develop an interview guide to generate a robust description of the social, facility culture, personnel factors, and intervention characteristics that influenced successes and/or challenges with implementation.

The semi-structured interviews will be recorded with participants’ consent so they can be transcribed and translated to English. The transcript will be typed into a word processing document on a password protected computer. All potentially identifying proper names of people, places, and/or organizations will be redacted from the electronic transcripts. The transcripts will be sent electronically via a secure file exchange site on a regular basis for analysis and archiving. All transcripts will be password protected prior to sending. Audio-recordings will be destroyed after the completion of data analysis.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics, persistent respiratory symptoms, and self-reported quality of life will be summarized in tables and figures. Categorical variables will be presented in terms of frequency tables and percentages; continuous variables will be presented in terms of median and interquartile range. Chi-square tests will be used to compare categorical variables between patients with and without PTLD and the nonparametric Mann–Whitney-Wilcoxon test will be used to compare continuous variables.

Prevalence will be calculated as the proportion of patients with an abnormal spirometry, indicative of PTLD, divided by the total number of patients with at least one spirometry result. A logistic regression will also be used to estimate factors associated with PTLD. The model will adjust for TB treatment length, age, sex, and HIV status. Continuous variables will be modeled using restricted cubic splines to alleviate linearity assumptions. Results will be presented in terms of odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals.

Sensitivity analysis will later be used to estimate the impact of non-response in the estimated prevalence of PLTD and associated risk factors. All analyses will be performed in the statistical software R version 4.4.0 or the latest.

Using data captured during implementation of spirometry screening for PTLD, we will evaluate intervention fidelity as a function of 1) Completion Rate: the proportion of enrolled patients who successfully completed the base-line and the 6-month screening spirometry visit; 2) Test Result Notification Rate: the proportion of participants determined to have PTLD who were notified of their result and referred for further management; and 3) Treatment Rate: the proportion of screen positive patients in which a treatment currently exists and who were initiated on treatment.

Qualitative data analysis

Immediately following each interview, the audio files will be uploaded into a REDCap database. The audio files will be transcribed verbatim, translated to English, and a set of a priori codes derived from CFIR constructs will be applied by two researchers from the US-based study team. Analysts will be blinded to the level of implementation adoption and fidelity to avoid bias. Using NVivo 15, a software program for qualitative and mixed-methods data analysis, we will prepopulate all deductive codes and definitions to facilitate coder inter-reliability. We will categorize and organize the data using a hybrid approach of qualitative methods of thematic analysis, allowing us to identify recurrent themes and explore interview responses. Deductive codes will be used to form main themes, while inductive subthemes will be data-driven and incorporated into the analysis as they emerge. A rigorous consensus-based coding process will be implemented with inter-coder reliability assessments conducted after each analyst has coded five interviews. Coding discrepancies will be discussed, and if consensus cannot be achieved, the study’s leadership team will engage to arbitrate disputes.

Dissemination plan

With the realization of this study, we expect the following: 1) to characterize the prevalence of PTLD in our cohort of patients who are categorized as TB cured following completion of six months of TB treatment; 2) To elucidate the site- provider- and individual-level determinants that contribute to the successful roll-out of spirometry screening at CHC. We will be able to comment on the acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility, implementation climate, and implementation readiness of CHC for spirometry screening; and 3) To provide a comprehensive evaluation of spirometry screening implementation in real world conditions which will elucidate strengths and weaknesses in the implementation process. We expect our findings will aid decision makers in optimizing the interventions impact and in translating our research findings into practical and evidence-based programming.

The study findings will be disseminated locally to stakeholders at CHC; the three selected peripheral health facilities: Centro de Saúde da Cidade, Centro de Saúde de Chalucuane, and Centro de Saúde Urbano; and to the NTCP/Ministry of Health and stakeholders in Maputo. In addition, results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication in the medical literature and through presentations at both domestic and international scientific conferences.

Discussion

Significant challenges persist regarding TB occurrence worldwide, especially in LMICs, including undetected cases, inadequate response and control of TB and HIV co-epidemics, and slow uptake of new tools and resources [6]. Well-structured, evidence-based care for people with former TB infection experiencing PTLD symptoms will reduce chronic respiratory symptoms, improve quality of life, and potentially reduce risks for additional pulmonary infections including recurrent TB. A large gap remains in the scientific literature regarding the implementation of best practices of HIV and TB health services delivery that could help Mozambique overcome its challenges in diagnosis, treatment, and continuity of care.

This study will evaluate the effectiveness and implementation of a new screening program that utilizes spirometry and questionnaires for diagnosing symptomatic PTLD among adolescents and adults who have completed TB treatment within the NTCP of CHC in rural Mozambique. The hybrid type III effectiveness implementation design of this study will allow us to test our spirometry implementation strategy while we simultaneously gather information on the intervention’s impact on outcomes under real world conditions. Findings from this study could be scaled-up to additional regional health facilities of Chókwè District which have the physical and human resource conditions for spirometry screening, and eventually nationwide.

This study will contribute to the literature on PTLD and implementation science by providing information on how spirometry can be implemented under practical circumstances in resource constrained areas such as rural Mozambique. By determining the prevalence of PTLD in this population, we aim to highlight the need for PTLD treatment and management options in Mozambique and LMICs, including pharmacotherapy and/or a pulmonary rehabilitation package. The knowledge gained from this study will be used to assist decision makers in determining a course of action for increasing spirometry screening coverage, optimizing the intervention’s impact, and translating our findings into evidence-based programming.
