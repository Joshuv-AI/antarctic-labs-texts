Non-covalent Lysine–docosahexaenoic acid amphiphilic complexes as interfacial stabilizers for emulsions and lutein delivery
Authors: Jiaqi Shang, Zhenyuan Wang, Yuanyuan Li, Xiaohui Zheng, Dan Cheng, Mi Wang, Jiaheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-covalent amphiphilic complexes offer potential as interfacial stabilizers in emulsion-based delivery systems. However, the relationship between their molecular composition, interfacial assembly, and emulsion performance remains inadequately understood. Lysine–docosahexaenoic acid (Lys-DHA) complexes with varying mass ratios were synthesized to investigate how the Lys/DHA ratio influences oil–water interfacial behavior and lutein delivery. Analytical techniques and computational simulations revealed that Lys and DHA interacted through electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals interactions. Higher DHA content increased hydrophobic exposure and facilitated interfacial adsorption. The Lys-DHA (1:3) complex exhibited optimal interfacial properties, characterized by the lowest interfacial tension, highest surface pressure, fastest diffusion rate, and a predominantly elastic interfacial film. Emulsions stabilized by Lys-DHA (1:3) displayed smaller droplet sizes, lower ζ-potential, and more excellent physical stability, with an average droplet size of 317.97 nm and ζ-potential of −51.37 mV. Regarding lutein delivery, these emulsions achieved encapsulation efficiencies exceeding 80%, retained over 70% of lutein after 14 d, and enhanced lutein bioaccessibility to 44.9%. These results highlight the potential of Lys-DHA complexes as functional lipid-containing amphiphilic interfacial stabilizers, offering insights into the correlation between non-covalent molecular interactions and interfacial/emulsion characteristics.Keywords: Lysine, Docosahexaenoic acid, Interfacial properties, Emulsion, Delivery

Introduction

Emulsion-based delivery systems are widely utilized for encapsulating and conveying bioactive compounds in various industries, such as food, nutraceuticals, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals. The stability and formation of these systems primarily rely on the adsorption of amphiphilic molecules at oil–water interfaces. These molecules work to lower interfacial tension and create protective layers (Dickinson, 2011; Kharat and McClements, 2019). Traditional emulsifiers, which encompass phospholipids, proteins, polysaccharides, and synthetic surfactants, are commonly used to control droplet formation, interfacial viscoelasticity, and overall physical stability (Cai et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024). Phospholipids are attractive interfacial components because their amphiphilic structures allow directional organization at interfaces (Andersson et al., 2025; Luchini and Vitiello, 2021). Nevertheless, systems based on phospholipids, like liposomes or structured lipid emulsions, often necessitate intricate processing methods and precise compositional management, because bilayer packing, membrane compactness, lipid phase behavior, and emulsion stability are strongly affected by parameters, such as cholesterol ratio, lipid concentration, hydration, and emulsification conditions (Mendanha et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2025). Consequently, the pursuit of uncomplicated, food-compatible amphiphilic systems that can be easily prepared through mild and accessible techniques remains a crucial goal in emulsion engineering.

Non-covalent interaction offers a promising approach to develop amphiphilic interfacial stabilizers without the need for chemical modification. Biomolecules with complementary charged, polar, and hydrophobic moieties can interact via electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions, leading to the formation of complexes that can adsorb at oil–water interfaces (Ren et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2023). Previous research has shown that proteins, polysaccharides, peptides, and fatty acids can be combined to adjust interfacial adsorption behavior and emulsion stability (Du et al., 2021; Nooshkam and Varidi, 2021; Xv et al., 2024). However, many existing systems involve macromolecular structures or multi-component compositions, and the correlation between molecular composition, interfacial adsorption kinetics, interfacial viscoelasticity, and emulsion performance remains incompletely understood in simplified small-molecule models.

Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA; 22:6n-3) is a long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acid with six double bonds and a terminal carboxyl group, making it suitable for interfacial assembly (Guesnet and Alessandri, 2011; Nguyen et al., 2014). The extended hydrocarbon chain allows for hydrophobic anchoring in the oil phase, while the carboxyl group can engage in electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding with positively charged or polar molecules (Fattahi et al., 2020; Mendanha et al., 2024). Lysine (Lys), an amino acid with α-amino, ε-amino, and carboxyl groups, offers multiple hydrophilic and positively charged sites under appropriate conditions (Slobodian et al., 1962). Lys presents amine-containing polar groups that can interact with fatty acids, leading to the formation of an amphiphilic structure. Therefore, the combination of Lys and DHA provides a simple model for constructing small-molecule non-covalent amphiphilic complexes, in which DHA can function as both a nutritional lipid component and potentially participate in interfacial stabilization.

In this study, we hypothesized that Lys and DHA could form small-molecule non-covalent amphiphilic complexes at various ratios, and these complexes could function as lipid-containing interfacial stabilizer. To test this hypothesis, Lys-DHA complexes with varying mass ratios were synthesized and characterized. Their molecular interactions were investigated using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and computational analysis. Subsequently, the interfacial adsorption behavior of the complexes was assessed by measuring interfacial tension, surface pressure (π), adsorption kinetics, and dilatational rheological behaviors. Moreover, the emulsifying performance of Lys-DHA complexes was evaluated by analyzing droplet morphology, particle size distribution, ζ-potential, rheological properties, and stability. To assess the delivery potential of Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsions under simulated digestion conditions, lutein was employed as a model hydrophobic bioactive compound. By linking connections between molecular interactions, interfacial arrangements, emulsion stabilization, and lutein bioavailability, this work aims to enhance comprehension regarding the modulation of oil–water interfaces by small-molecule non-covalent amphiphilic complexes. The findings of this study may facilitate the rational development of sample food-grade interfacial stabilizers for emulsion-based delivery systems.

Materials and methods

Materials

The Lys used in this study was L-lysine. L-lysine (98% purity) and Nile red were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Poole, UK). Free DHA used for the preparation of Lys-DHA was purchased from Aladdin Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Algal oil as the oil phase was supplied by Runke Bio-engineering Co., Ltd. (Fujian, China). Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) was purchased from Adamas Co Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Lutein was purchased from Macklin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation and characterization of Lys-DHA

Preparation of Lys-DHA

Lys solution (20%) was mixed with the DHA at different Lys/DHA mass ratios. After stirring at 25 °C for 30 min, the mixture was freeze-dried immediately. The mass ratios of Lys and DHA dry matter in the complex were 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3, respectively. To minimize DHA oxidation, all preparation procedures were performed under nitrogen protection and light-protected conditions.

FTIR analysis

FTIR patterns of Lys, DHA, and Lys-DHA were recorded by a Nicolet iS20 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA) with a scanning range of 4000–400 cm−1.

Computational analysis

In the computational analysis, Lys was modeled with two protonated amino groups and a deprotonated carboxyl group, while DHA was modeled with a deprotonated carboxyl group. Molecular docking simulation of Lys-DHA was carried out using AutoDock Vina, with hydrogen bonding interactions then being visualised through PyMOL. Gaussian 16 was used to conduct electrostatic potential (ESP), independent gradient model based on the Hirshfeld partition (IGMH), and atoms-in-molecules (AIM) analyses, with density functional theory (DFT) calculations being the basis of these. Post-processing was then performed in VMD and Multiwfn. The solvation model based on density implicit solvation model was used to account for the water solvation effect.

Contact angle

The contact angle was measured according to a previously reported method (Mao et al., 2025). The Lys-DHA was pressed into 2 mm thick, 13 mm diameter pellets, which were then placed in an optical glass cuvette filled with algal oil. 2 μL of ultrapure water was applied to the surface of pellet to assess water contact angle. The oil contact angle was determined by applying 2 μL of algal oil to the pellet surface. The contact angle images were obtained using a high-speed camera and the quantitatively assessed by an OCA 20 AMP analyzer (Data physics Instruments, Filderstadt, Germany).

Interfacial properties

The interfacial tension was measured at 25 °C over a period of 3600 s. In the tip of the U-shape, a 4 μL drop of algal oil was formed and immersed in Lys-DHA (1 mg/mL) solution. The shape of oil was automatically analyzed by an interface rheometer (Teclis Tracker, Civrieux d’Azergues, France). The measurement of dilatational rheology of algal oil at the Lys-DHA solutions was carried out according to a previously reported method (Hsieh et al., 2021). The oil droplet was located at the top of the U-shape with a sinusoidal oscillatory deformation of 20% every 20 s for 10800 s.

Preparation and characterization of emulsions

Preparation of emulsions

Firstly, 80 mL of 10 mg/mL Lys-DHA solutions were mixed with 20 mL of algal oil. Then, the mixture was emulsified using an ultrasonic cell crusher for 5 min (2 s on, 2 s off) and cooled with ice water to prevent the heat affecting. The ultrasound power was 100 W, frequency was 20 kHz and amplitude was 40%.

Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM) observation

The CLSM images of the Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsions were obtained using a TCS SP 8 CLSM (Leica Camera AG, Wetzlar, Germany). 10 mL of sample was mixed with 400 μL of 0.1% Nile red and stained in the dark for 12 h, and the mixture was observed at 488 nm.

Particle size and zeta potential

The droplet size distributions and Zeta potential of the emulsions were measured using a Zetasizer Nano-ZS90 instrument (Malvern Panalytical Ltd, Worcestershire, UK). The emulsions were diluted 200 times with ultrapure water to avoid multiple scattering effects.

Rheological characteristics

Rheological behavior of the emulsions was measured using a MCR 302e rotational rheometer (Anton Paar Co., Graz, Austria) with a 35 mm diameter cone plate at 25 °C. Steady-state flow tests was carried out at shear rate of 0.01 to 1000 s−1.

Emulsifying activity (EAI) and emulsifying stability (ESI)

EAI and ESI were measured according to a previously reported method (Zhao et al., 2025, Zhao et al., 2025). 50 μL of emulsion was diluted to 5 mL with 0.1% SDS. The absorbance at 600 nm of the mixture was measured immediately and after 30 min by a UV-2600 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan).

Storage, heat, pH, salt and centrifugal stability (Ke)

The samples were stored at 4 °C for 30 days. The photographic images of samples were recorded on 0, 7, 14 and 30 d and the microscopic morphology of the emulsions were observed using an Olympus IX73 inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Fresh emulsions were heated at 30, 50, 70, and 90 °C for 30 min and observed to evaluate the thermal stability of emulsions. Moreover, the pH of emulsions was adjusted to be 2.0 to assess the pH stability of emulsions. The salt stability was evaluated under 150 mM NaCl condition. Centrifugal stability (Ke) was measured according to a previously reported method (Li et al., 2025). The absorbance of fresh emulsion and supernatant of the emulsion after centrifugation at 1000g for 10 min was determined at 600 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Preparation and characterization of emulsions loading lutein

Referring to Section 2.4, the lutein content in the oil phase was 15 mg/ml, Lys-DHA (1:3) was selected as emulsifier, and the Lys-DHA concentrations were 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 mg/mL, respectively. The lutein-encapsulation efficiency in Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsions was calculated according to the total and free lutein concentrations. 0.5 mL of sample was diluted with 4.5 mL of ethanol, then the mixture was centrifuged at 10000 g for 10 min to collect the supernatant. The free lutein concentration was evaluated by measuring the absorbance of the supernatant at 425 nm. The measurement of particle size and Zeta potential of emulsions loading lutein was based on Section 2.5. Release rate of lutein in the Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsion during simulated digestion was evaluated by measuring the concentration of lutein in collecting digestion phase at the end of the oral digestion, 15-min intervals during gastric digestion, and 30-min intervals during intestinal digestion. The chyme was centrifuged at 10000 g for 30 min after simulated intestinal digestion. The middle micellar phase was collected, and the concentration of the lutein was measured to calculate the bioaccessibility of the lutein.

Statistical analysis

The results were collected by three independent determinations and expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Significant differences between groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance, followed by Duncan's test using SPSS 25.0 statistical software. Differences were deemed statistically significant with a p-value <0.05.

Results and discussion

Interactions between Lys and DHA

Fig. S1 shows images of Lys, DHA, and an aqueous solution containing 5% Lys-DHA. The complex exhibited great water-soluble, indicating an interaction between Lys and DHA. Furthermore, the water solubilities of fatty acids may be effectively enhanced by promoting combinations of amino and fatty acids. FTIR spectroscopy was employed to elucidate the interaction between Lys and DHA. Fig. 1a and Table S1 present the FTIR spectra and detailed band assignments of Lys, DHA, and Lys-DHA complexes. For Lys, broad bands at 3356 and 3289 cm−1 were associated with O–H stretching, while the bands at 1574 and 1509 cm−1 were assigned to COO− asymmetric stretching and NH3+ asymmetric deformation. In addition, the band at 1403 cm−1 was related to COO− symmetric stretching, and the band at 1019 cm−1 was attributed to C–N stretching. Free DHA exhibited characteristic bands at 3012, 2925, and 2853 cm−1, which were assigned to the olefinic C–H stretching, methylene (CH2) stretching, and methyl (CH3) stretching, respectively. The band at 1708 cm−1 was attributed to the C=O stretching. The relatively low wavenumber of C=O stretching suggested that the carboxyl groups of free DHA were involved in hydrogen-bonded self-association, possibly in the form of carboxylic acid dimers or aggregates (Smak et al., 2024).

Blue regions represent negative electrostatic potential; red regions represent positive electrostatic potential. (d) The IGMH and (e) AIM of of Lys-DHA. After the formation of Lys-DHA complexes, the disappearance of the C=O band (1708 cm−1) of DHA and the emergence of a new band at 1738 cm−1 indicated that the microenvironment and hydrogen-bonding state of the DHA altered after association with Lys (Albrecht et al., 2010). Meanwhile, disruption of the original DHA self-associated carboxylic acid dimers was observed, as evidenced by the shift of the COO− asymmetric stretching band from 1574 cm−1 in Lys to 1577-1578 cm−1 in Lys-DHA, and the shift of the COO− symmetric stretching band from 1403 cm−1 to 1404-1407 cm−1. The NH3+ asymmetric deformation band was also retained at around 1508 cm−1 in the complexes. These changes indicated the involvement of both the carboxylate groups and protonated amino groups of Lys were involved in the interaction process, suggesting electrostatic interaction contributed to the association. The combined changes in the C=O, COO−, and NH3+ vibration regions implied that the interaction between Lys and DHA was driven by a combination of electrostatic ion-pair formation and hydrogen bonding.

To further investigate the interaction between Lys and DHA, molecular docking, ESP, and IGMH analyses were performed. According to the FTIR results, the carboxyl and amino groups experienced deprotonation and protonation during the interaction. Consequently, the amino groups in Lys were protonated, while the carboxyl groups in both Lys and DHA were deprotonated to evaluate the local interactions in Lys-DHA complexes. As shown in Fig. 1b, two hydrogen bonds were found between Lys and DHA, with lengths of 1.7 Å. These short contact distances indicated that the protonated amino groups of Lys acted as hydrogen-bond donors and interacted with the deprotonated carboxyl group of DHA. The ESP distribution further supported the electrostatic interaction between Lys and DHA (Fig. 1c). The positive electrostatic potential was mainly distributed around the protonated amino groups of Lys, whereas negative potential regions were located around deprotonated carboxyl groups. Charge distribution suggested that electrostatic attraction facilitated the approach and association between Lys and the DHA. As shown in Fig. 1d and e, apart from hydrogen bonding, Lys-DHA was stabilized by various non-covalent interactions, such as electrostatic interactions and van der Waals forces. Together with the FTIR results, the computational analysis supported a multicomponent non-covalent interaction mechanism between Lys and DHA.

Lys-DHA contact angles

The contact angles of Lys-DHA complexes are shown in Fig. 2a and Fig. S2. All Lys-DHA samples exhibited water and oil contact angles of less than 90°, suggesting that complexes possessed amphiphilic wetting characteristics. The hydrophobic groups of amphiphilic molecules were exposed to the oil phase during emulsification, whereas their hydrophilic groups were arranged on the oil surface (Tao et al., 2024a). The water contact angle increased with increasing DHA content, with a maximum value of 60.7° recorded at a Lys/DHA ratio of 1:3. This trend indicated that increasing the DHA proportion enhanced the hydrophobic contribution of the complexes.

Adsorption behavior at the oil–water interface

The contact angle results only provided qualitative information on the wettability of Lys-DHA complexes, dynamic interfacial measurements were further performed to evaluate the behavior of Lys-DHA complexes at the oil–water interface. Interfacial tension is the main factor that influences emulsification capacity and emulsion stability (Wei et al., 2023). The temporal evolution of interfacial tension at oil–water interface for each Lys-DHA complex was determined using the Yang–Laplace equation. All Lys-DHA samples exhibited interfacial tensions that initially declined rapidly and then plateaued (Fig. 2b), which is similar to the adsorption trend observed for β-lactoglobulin modified by enzymatic hydrolysis, glycation, and fibrillation (Zhang et al., 2025). Lys-DHA rapidly formed an interfacial film when adsorbed at the oil–water interface, leading to a rapid reduction in interfacial tension. Once saturated, the Lys-DHA at the interface rearranged, which resulted in a slow reduction in interfacial tension. The interfacial tensions of all samples were observed to descend in the order: 3:1 > 2:1 > 1:1 > 1:2 > 1:3. A Lys/DHA ratio of 1:3 led to an excellent ability to reduce interfacial tension, which was possibly ascribable to the flexible Lys-DHA structure obtained at this ratio that enabled rapid adsorption and unfolding at the oil–water interface.

π can be used to evaluate the adsorption behavior of Lys-DHA (Baldursdottir et al., 2010). As shown in Fig. 2c, all samples exhibited two-stage changes in π, in which the adsorption rate was observed to increase markedly within the first 400 s, followed by an interfacial saturation stage (400–3600 s) during which π increased slowly. This behavior is similar that observed for ultrasound-assisted cross-linked myofibrillar proteins at the oil–water interface (Tao et al., 2024b). Comparing with π values of the ultrasound-assisted cross-linked myofibrillar proteins at different oil-water interfaces, the π value of Lys-DHA was lower, which might be due to the difference in the oil phase. The rapid increase in π observed for all samples during the initial stage was ascribable to the adsorption and aggregation of Lys-DHA at the bare interface, with steric hindrance and the electrostatic energy generated by the initially adsorbed Lys-DHA hindering subsequent adsorption, thereby lowering the increasing rate of π (Cho et al., 1997). The various Lys-DHA complexes exhibited final equilibrium π of 5.42 mN/m (3:1), 5.72 mN/m (2:1), 6.26 mN/m (1:1), 6.77 mN/m (1:2) and 7.36 mN/m (1:3). The hydrophobic groups became more exposed with increasing DHA content, which enhanced Lys-DHA/oil interactions and promoted the rapid formation of interfacial membranes that resulted in higher π.

Dynamic interfacial biopolymer adsorption is divided into three steps: diffusion, permeation, and rearrangement (Dickinson, 2011). π is linearly related to the square-root of time (t1/2) during the diffusion-dominated period, with the diffusion rate (Kdiff) determined from the slope of the π–t1/2 plot (Perez et al., 2009). As shown in Fig. 2d, the linear region ended at 20 s1/2 for all Lys-DHA samples. The Kdiff value (before 20 s1/2) for each sample was calculated by linear regression (Table 1), with the highest value (0.144 mNm−1s−1/2) observed for Lys-DHA (1:3), which indicated that this sample was more inclined to adsorb at the oil–water interface than the other Lys-DHA complexes. The diffusion of Lys-DHA (1:3) was promoted by more exposed hydrophobic groups. Highly hydrophobic emulsifiers have low adsorption-energy barriers and diffuse quickly at the oil–water interface as a consequence (Wierenga et al., 2003). Therefore, Lys-DHA (1:3) exhibited the fastest diffusion, promoting the formation of an interfacial film. In addition, the charge and solubility of the complexes, as well as residual or weakly bound DHA, may also improve interfacial and emulsifying properties.

The first-order phenomenological equation can be used to calculate the rates of emulsifier penetration and rearrangement (Graham and Phillips, 1979), the results of which are shown in Fig. 2e–i. The plots show two linear regions with slopes corresponding to the penetration rate (KP) and rearrangement rate (KR), which were shown in Table 1. Lys-DHA (1:3) exhibited a significantly higher KP value than the other samples (p < 0.05). The KP and KR values showed opposite trends, with Lys-DHA (1:3) exhibiting a lower KR than the other samples (p < 0.05), which was attributable to stronger lateral intermolecular interactions in Lys-DHA (1:3) that resulted in the formation of larger aggregates at the interface, thereby impeding Lys-DHA rearrangement at the oil–water interface.

Interfacial dilatational modulus

Amphiphilic molecules impede oil droplets from flocculating by forming a robust viscoelastic film capable of withstanding mechanical pressure while also providing static repulsion and hindrance. The mechanical strength and deformation resistance of the interfacial film can be assessed using the interfacial dilatational modulus (E), which consists of two components: the elastic modulus (Ed) and viscous modulus (Ev) (Wang et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 3a, all samples exhibited E values with similar temporal dependences, with values increasing sharply at the start of the adsorption process and then increasing more slowly to completion. All complexes exhibited similar changes in Ed (Fig. 3b), with E > Ed for each sample, which suggested that the interfacial film was significantly strengthened during the initial adsorption stage owing to the interfacial packing of Lys-DHA. The interfacial film subsequently aged gradually as adsorption approached saturation (Tang and Shen, 2015). Ed was largely influenced by the proportion of DHA in the Lys-DHA complex and was observed to increase with increasing DHA content, which suggested that a higher proportion of Lys-DHA resulted in stronger lateral intermolecular interactions while promoting the formation of more cohesive interfacial film. All groups exhibited Ed values that were considerably higher than Ev (Fig. 3c), which indicated that the interfacial film formed by Lys-DHA complex was predominantly elastic (Zhang et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2022).

(a) Interfacial dilatational, (b) elastic, and (c) viscous moduli as functions of time, and (d) elastic moduli as functions of surface pressure for Lys-DHA complexes containing various proportions of DHA.

The slope of the E–π plot is indicative of the strength of the interaction between adsorbed molecules (H. Zhao et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 3d, E increased with increasing π, with the slope increasing with increasing DHA content in the Lys-DHA. The various E–π plots exhibited slopes of 1.110 (3:1), 1.224 (2:1), 1.300 (1:1), 1.380 (1:2), and 1.935 (1:3); these slopes were all greater than 1.0, which suggested that the formed interfacial films were highly mechanically strong and had the ability to resist droplet deformation (Shen et al., 2023). Based on the adsorption behavior presented above, we concluded that Lys-DHA exhibited stronger lateral intermolecular interactions at the oil–water interface with increasing DHA content (Fig. 4), which facilitated the formation of more elastic and more cohesive interfacial layer. We subsequently prepared emulsions to better illustrate the relationships between properties of interfaces and emulsions.

Emulsion microstructure

CLSM can be used to analyze the type of formed emulsion (Cai et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 5a, all emulsions contained green oil droplets that were surrounded by water, indicative of typical O/W emulsions. While all emulsions exhibited large oil droplets, that containing Lys-DHA (1:3) contained the fewest, and its droplets were the most uniform in size; in addition, its droplets did not aggregate because the self-aggregated Lys-DHA formed an interfacial film that increased spatial resistance and prevented clustering.

(a) CLSM images, (b) droplet-size distributions, (c) droplet sizes, (d) Zeta potentials, (e) apparent viscosities, and (f), EAI and (g) ESI values for Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsions prepared via ultrasonication. Scalebar: 10 μm.

Droplet size and Zeta potential

Fig. 5b and c show droplet size distributions and average sizes of the Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsions. All emulsions exhibited single peak distributions with droplets 100–1000 nm in size. Droplet size was observed to decrease with increasing DHA content in the Lys-DHA, with the smallest droplet size of 317.97 nm recorded for Lys-DHA (1:3), in agreement with the CLSM results. Smaller droplets are indicative of a more physically stable emulsion (Li et al., 2020). A higher absolute Zeta potential is indicative of greater electrostatic repulsion between droplets and a lower probability of aggregation, thereby increasing the stability of the solution (Ren et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 5d, all emulsions had negative Zeta potentials. Except for Lys-DHA (3:1), all emulsions exhibited Zeta potentials that were more negative than −30 mV, which indicated that they were stable and that Lys-DHA effectively stabilized the emulsion. Lys is positively charged, whereas DHA carries a negative charge; therefore, the Zeta potential of the stabilized emulsion decreased with increasing DHA content in the Lys-DHA complex.

Rheological behavior

Fig. 5e shows apparent viscosities of various Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsions. All emulsions exhibited downwardly concave viscosity curves that were different to those associated with the shear-thinning behavior observed for liquid–liquid systems (Li et al., 2023; L. Zhang et al., 2022), which indicated that the emulsions stabilized by Lys-DHA were dilatant fluids, also known as shear-thickening fluids. Inter-droplet collisions increased with increasing shear rates, which accelerated Lys-DHA interactions at the interface; consequently, the space between droplets decreased, whereas viscosity increased as these interactions increased. This shear-thickening behavior was similar to that observed for Pickering emulsions stabilized with modified corn-wax starch (Santos et al., 2024).

EAI and ESI

EAI and ESI reflect the capacity of amphiphilic molecule to bind at the oil–water interface and maintain emulsions stability (Dong et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 5f, the EAI of the sample was observed to increase from 54.40 to 57.34 m2/g with increasing DHA content, while the ESI increased from a minimum value of 3.00 min to a maximum value of 4.28 min. Hence, a higher proportion of DHA improved the dispersibility of Lys-DHA at the oil–water interface and reduced the interfacial energy such that the droplets were evenly dispersed, consistent with the dynamic adsorption behavior results presented in Sections 3.3, 3.4.

Stability

Centrifugal settling is an important method for evaluating emulsion stability. A smaller Ke value corresponds to a more stable emulsion with better dispersed droplets (Han et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 5g, the Ke value decreased with increasing DHA content, from 28.26% (3:1) to 18.37% (1:3), which indicated that the emulsion stabilized by Lys-DHA (1:3) was more physically stable toward stratification and flocculation. Emulsion samples stored at 4 °C for 30 d were evaluated for storage stability, with photographic and optical microscopy images displayed in Fig. 6a and b. All emulsions retained stable when stored for 30 d. Droplet size of emulsion was observed to decrease with increasing DHA content, with Lys-DHA (1:3) exhibiting the smallest droplets because Lys-DHA lowered the π at the oil–water interface and enhanced the electrostatic repulsion between droplets, consistent with the droplet size and Zeta potential results presented in Section 3.6.

(a) Photographic and (b) optical microscopy images of Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsions prepared via ultrasonication. (c) Photographic images of Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsions prepared via ultrasonication heated at various temperatures. Scalebar: 20 μm.

Thermal stability is also important for emulsions intended for use in the food industry (Wang et al., 2024). Emulsions stabilized by Lys-DHA were heated to 30, 50, 70, and 90 °C, and their stabilities were observed. As shown in Fig. 6c and Fig. S3, the Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsions did not change significantly during heating for 30 min, which was attributable to Lys-DHA improving lateral intermolecular interactions that ultimately enhanced the thermal stability of each emulsion. To further evaluate stability of Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsions under conditions that may weaken non-covalent interactions, the emulsions were observed under acidic pH (pH 2.0) and high ionic strength (150 mM NaCl) conditions. Fig. S4 revealed that emulsions stabilized by Lys-DHA complexes showed some large droplets under acidic conditions or 150 mM NaCl, suggesting partial destabilization and droplet coalescence. This occurrence might be associated with the electrostatic association between DHA and Lys was weakened, reducing electrostatic repulsion between droplets. The DHA-rich samples, particularly Lys-DHA (1:3), exhibited relatively uniform and smaller droplets in comparison to the other samples. These observations demonstrated that acidic pH and physiological ionic strength could affect the stability of Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsions. However, the emulsions did not completely disassemble under these challenging conditions. The Lys-DHA (1:3) system displayed greater stability, supporting the notion that DHA-rich complexes might exhibit enhanced adsorption due to increased hydrophobic anchoring. Moreover, the ratio of Lys to DHA could alter the charge and solubility of the complexes, thereby influencing their diffusion and interfacial packing. The residual or weakly bound DHA might contribute to a reduction in interfacial tension. The 1:3 ratio potentially provided an optimal balance between aqueous dispersibility and hydrophobic interfacial anchoring.

Characterizing emulsions loaded with lutein

Combined with the data from the above sections, emulsions loaded with lutein were prepared using Lys-DHA (1:3) to assess its potential for stabilizing delivery system of hydrophobically active compounds. As shown in Fig. 7a, the Lys-DHA-stabilized lutein emulsions remained stable during storage without any obvious changes. The Lys-DHA-stabilized lutein emulsions exhibited single-peak distributions in the 100–900 nm range, consistent with droplets of uniform size (Fig. 7b and c). The lutein emulsions exhibited average droplet sizes of 336.97 nm (2 mg/mL), 335.39 nm (4 mg/mL), 326.63 nm (6 mg/mL), 322.34 nm (8 mg/mL), and 317.98 nm (10 mg/mL). In addition, the lutein emulsions had negative Zeta potentials, which were observed to decrease from −45.71 to −52.77 mV with increasing Lys-DHA (1:3) concentration, which suggested that increasing the Lys-DHA (1:3) concentration in the interfacial membrane enhanced the electrostatic repulsion between emulsion droplets. As shown in Fig. 7d, all Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsions exhibited lutein-encapsulation efficiencies greater than 80%; moreover, encapsulation efficiencies of 70% were recorded after 14 d of storage, which indicated that the interfacial Lys-DHA (1:3) membrane effectively minimized the accumulation of oxygen-induced factors and prevented lutein degradation.

(a) Photographic images of emulsions loaded with lutein stabilized by different concentrations of Lys-DHA (1:3) after preparation and after 14 d of storage. (b) Droplet-size distributions, (c) droplet sizes and Zeta potentials, and (d) lutein-encapsulation efficiencies of various emulsions. (e) Release rates and (f) bioaccessibilities of lutein in emulsions stabilized by Lys-DHA.

Fig. 7e shows the release rates of lutein in the Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsions. The emulsion stabilized with 10 mg/mL Lys-DHA (1:3) exhibited the lowest lutein release rate of 1.74% after hydrolysis in simulated saliva. However, lutein release rates of 34.25% (2 mg/mL), 31.60% (4 mg/mL), 28.41% (6 mg/mL), 24.48% (8 mg/mL), and 23.26% (10 mg/mL) were recorded after 60 min of gastric digestion. The higher lutein release rate observed during gastric digestion was ascribable the high ionic concentration and acidity (pH 1–3) in the stomach, which resulted in oil-droplet separation and emulsion flocculation (Hu et al., 2022). The emulsions were then hydrolyzed in simulated intestinal fluid for 60–180 min, resulting in final lutein release rates of 73.34% (2 mg/mL), 70.44% (4 mg/mL), 65.53% (6 mg/mL), 60.95% (8 mg/mL), and 57.20% (10 mg/mL). Bile salts and lipases in intestinal fluids promoted oil-droplet hydrolysis that led to the steady release of lutein. The retention of lutein in the emulsion was observed to increase with increasing Lys-DHA (1:3) concentration, which was possibly ascribable to the superior stabilities of emulsions stabilized by high concentrations of Lys-DHA (1:3). These findings are consistent with emulsions loaded with β-carotene stabilized by starch nanocrystals, in which more β-carotene is retained in emulsions stabilized by starch nanocrystals with increasing levels of octenyl succinic anhydride substitution, which is mainly due to enhanced emulsion stability during simulated digestion at elevated substitution levels (Zhu et al., 2025). Emulsions stabilized with high Lys-DHA (1:3) concentrations retained more nutrients during saliva and gastric digestion, which ensured that more lutein was delivered to the simulated intestinal fluids. The bioaccessibility of lutein increased with increasing Lys-DHA (1:3) concentration in the emulsion (Fig. 7f). The highest lutein bioaccessibility (44.9%) was recorded for the emulsion stabilized with 10 mg/mL Lys-DHA (1:3). In summary, Lys-DHA-stabilized emulsions effectively served as encapsulation and delivery vehicles for lutein. DHA is a high-value polyunsaturated lipid, as a component of interface stabilizer, it is more suitable for functional emulsions or nutrient delivery systems, where DHA can serve as a bioactive lipid component.

Conclusion

Lys and DHA formed non-covalent amphiphilic Lys-DHA complexes, and lower interfacial tensions, higher π, diffusion rates, and penetration rates were exhibited at the higher proportion of DHA in Lys-DHA. These observations were ascribable to the greater hydrophobic exposure in the Lys-DHA with higher proportion of DHA, which strengthened the lateral intermolecular interactions within the interfacial films. The viscoelasticity of these films—dominated by elasticity—effectively prevented droplet deformation. Emulsions stabilized by Lys-DHA had droplet sizes of 317.97–533.03 nm and Zeta potentials between −22.83 and −51.37 mV, ensuring emulsion stability. The emulsions exhibited excellent stability under storage, centrifugation, and heating conditions, and the emulsions did not completely disassemble under acidic pH (pH 2.0) and high ionic strength (150 mM NaCl). Notably, emulsions stabilized with Lys-DHA (1:3) retained over 70% of lutein after 14 d and improved lutein bioaccessibility to 44.9%. This work provides a simple model system for understanding how small-molecule amphiphilic complexation regulates interfacial stabilization. The system may serve as a simple food-relevant interfacial stabilizer, but further work is needed on benchmark comparison, environmental stability, and real food matrices.
