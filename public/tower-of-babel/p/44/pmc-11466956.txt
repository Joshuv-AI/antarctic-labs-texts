The mediating effect of circulating inflammatory proteins on the relationship between gut microbiota and FD: a bidirectional Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Li Li, Minhan Fu, Feiyi Chen, Huijie Ji, Guowei Zhou, Lu Chen, Hao Geng, Jing Guo, Lixia Pei, Jianhua Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFunctional dyspepsia (FD) is known to be influenced by gut microbiota (GM) and circulating inflammatory proteins (CIPs), however, the causal relationship between GM, CIPs and FD haven’t been investigated. This study employed two-sample Mendelian Randomization (TSMR) to investigate their associations using data from Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS). In this study, Inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was employed as the primary analysis, with supplementary approaches including weighted median, weighted mode, simple mode, and MR-Egger. Heterogeneity and pleiotropy were assessed using the Cochrane Q test, MR-Egger intercept test, and MR-PRESSO global test. Totally, 196 GM traits and 91 CIPs were analyzed, and the results uncovered the causal impact of 12 GM taxa and 5 proteins on functional dyspepsia (FD). 9 GM genera were linked to a reduced risk of FD, while 3 GM genera were associated with an increased risk of FD.Additionally, reverse analysis revealed no FD-GM causation. Furthermore, IL-12, IL-10, CXCL10, CXCL9 and VEGFA were significantly correlated with FD, with CXCL9 and VEGFA acting as mediators in the association between GM traits and FD. Taken together, our findings established a link between specific GM and CIPs in the pathogenesis of FD, offering novel insights for its diagnosis and treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74717-6.Keywords: Mendelian randomization, Functional dyspepsia, Gut microbiota, Circulating inflammatory proteins, Mediation analysisSubject terms: Diseases, Gastroenterology, Medical research

Introduction

As is well documented, Functional dyspepsia (FD) manifests as pain or discomfort in the upper abdomen, often without any physical abnormalities1, and is commonly linked to unhealthy dietary habits. Its causes are multifaceted and include low-grade inflammatory changes in the gastrointestinal tracts and destruction of mucosal functional integrity, visceral and somatic hypersensitivity, psychological factors, changes in microbial flora, and impaired digestive function2. Familial dysautonomia impacts a significant proportion of the population and persists in patients over an extended period, significantly impacting their quality of life. According to epidemiological studies3, FD affects 25–40% of the adult population and 3–27% of children in their lifetime, and its symptoms are complex. Moreover, FD frequently relapses and co-exists with gastrointestinal disorders like irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)4, which also aggravates the anxiety and depression of patients5, leading to a significant rise in the financial and emotional burden on both patients and society. Despite extensive researches, its pathophysiology remains elusive due to its complex nature. Ascribed to the intricate development and manifestations of FD, it is challenging to effectively alleviate discomfort using conventional single-target pharmacological treatments6.

The intricate relationship between gut microbiota (GM) and FD has garnered increasing attention in recent years, underscoring the potential role of microbial dysbiosis in the pathophysiology of this condition. Notably, variations in GM composition have been observed in FD patients, suggesting a distinct microbial profile that may contribute to symptomatology7. Furthermore, the interventions conducted to modulate GM, such as the administration of probiotics, have demonstrated promising results in alleviating FD symptoms, with a significant proportion of patients achieving clinically meaningful improvements8. Additionally, further microbiota studies have indicated that the increase of Streptococcus is associated with upper gastrointestinal symptoms across all upper intestinal groups9, which can exacerbate intestinal inflammation and induce symptoms such as early satiety and abdominal bloating. Moreover, in-depth mechanistic studies have revealed significant differences in the upper gastrointestinal mucosal microbiota between FD patients and healthy individuals through high-throughput sequencing10.The cumulative evidence suggests that specific microbial taxa may play a pivotal role in the manifestation of FD symptoms. Therefore, further investigation into the causal relationships between GM and FD is essential for elucidating the underlying mechanisms and identifying potential therapeutic targets for precision medicine in FD patients.

The inflammatory response is also reported to be one of the main pathologies of FD. Intestinal damage and inflammation conduce to a hypoxic environment, thereby dysregulating immune cell function. Genetic modifications generate lipid molecules that activate pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IFN-γ, IL-10, and IL-8, consequently exacerbating immune and inflammatory responses11. Elevated levels of inflammatory markers within cells have been linked to the severity of symptoms experienced by individuals with FD. Studies have reported a substantial rise in TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-10 levels in peripheral monocytes of FD patients, correlating with symptoms such as delayed gastric emptying, nausea, and vomiting12. Additionally, an increase in the levels of eosinophils, macrophages, and intraepithelial lymphocyte infiltration has been observed in FD patients following infectious gastroenteritis13. Meanwhile, animal research demonstrated that a sterile diet low in lipopolysaccharide (LPS) contributes to the proliferation of B and T cells in Peyer patches and mesenteric lymph nodes compared to germ-fed mice14.

Despite the findings of prior research establishing the relationship between GM and FD, the specific function of these microbes and their precise cause-and-effect connection with FD has not been fully understood. MR, which is based on Mendelian’s second law and randomly distributes confounding factors, can predict the effect of variables on disease susceptibility15. Notably, it is not subject to confounders, and the causality is unidirectional, which addresses the primary challenge and establishes epidemiological causality16. Therefore, this study employed MR methods to analyze aggregated data from shared genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to elucidate the relationships between GM, circulating inflammatory proteins (CIPs) and FD, which may further determine their specific mechanism and provide valuable insights for the prevention and treatment of FD.

Methods

Study design

The study flowchart is presented in Fig. 1. Initially, published GWAS summary data, including characteristics of the GM, CIPs and FD, were collected (Table S1). Two-sample MR analyses were used to identify causal connections among the GM, CIPs and FD. Finally, MVMR analyses were employed to ascertain the mediating role of CIPs in the relationship between GM and FD.

Flow chart of the MR study.

Data sources

The MiBioGen consortium has conducted the most extensive meta-analysis to date on gut microbial composition using genome-wide datasets17. The groundbreaking research involved 18,340 individuals across 24 groups, with the majority being of European ancestry, totaling 13,266 participants. Researchers used direct taxonomic classification to investigate microbial composition by examining the variable regions V4, V3-V4, and V1-V2 of the 16 S rRNA gene. In order to identify genetic differences in hosts associated with gut microbiome bacterial abundance, researchers performed an analysis termed Microbial Quantitative Trait Loci (mbQTL) mapping. To perform MR analyses, this research included GWAS summary data for 196 gut microbiome components. The Olink Target Inflammation panel was used to assess GWAS data for 91 CIPs across 11 cohorts, totaling 14,824 participants of European ancestry18. The GWAS data of FD were obtained from the latest version (R11) of FinnGenn, released in June 2024, with 8,875 patients and 320,387 controls were included. FD patients were defined according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes K30 (ICD-10), 5368 A (ICD-9), or 5361 (ICD-8). The majority of cases were defined by the ICD-10 code19. All GWASs were approved by the relevant local ethical review committee, and informed consent was obtained from each participant.

The selection of instrumental variables

The selection of instrumental variables (IVs) must fulfill three assumptions: significantly correlated with exposure factors, independent from confounders, and mediation of outcomes only through exposure factors without direct association. In order to adhere to the independence assumption, we screened Phenoscanner database (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) to identify any potential pleiotropic confounders. The IVs were meticulously selected via a rigorous screening process, selecting single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with a significance level of P < 1 × 10−5when few whole-genome significant loci were available20. SNPs associated with outcomes (P< 0.05) were excluded, and a clumping process was used for linkage disequilibrium. Subsequently, linkage disequilibrium (LD) was employed to evaluate the existence of gene linkage among these SNPs, employing an r2 value of 0.001 and a window size of 10,000 kb. The formula F = beta2exposure/se2exposure and R2 = 2 × (1-MAF)×MAF×beta2were utilized to assess the statistical significance of SNPs, and the SNPs with weak associations (F < 10) were then eliminated21.

Statistical analysis

Mendelian randomization

In this research, a bidirectional TSMR analysis was employed to investigate the causal relationship between the GM, CIPs and FD by five common MR approaches, namely inversevariance-weighted (IVW), weighted median, weighted mdoe, simple mode and MR-Egger regression. The IVW analysis method served as the primary analysis in the current study22. Furthermore, a series of sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure the validity and reliability of the findings. Pleiotropy and outliers were identified by the MR-PRESSO test. SNPs were ranked by MR-PRESSO P-values, and iterations continued until the absence of pleiotropy (MR-PRESSO global P> 0.05)23. Steiger filtering was performed to eliminate IVs that were more strongly linked to outcomes than exposures24. Cochran’s Q test was used to examine variation in the estimates, and if significant diversity was detected, a random-effects IVW model was utilized for the main analysis25. Meanwhile, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted on the significant outcomes to ascertain whether a single SNP was responsible for the causal relationship observed in the TSMR analysis17.

Reverse Mendelian randomization analysis

A reciprocal MR analysis was executed to investigate the causal influence of FD on the detected GM (PIVW < 0.05). In this analysis, SNPs associated with FD were utilized as IVs, FD was considered the exposure variable, and GM taxa were regarded as the dependent variable. For the reciprocal MR analysis, the research participants, the fundamental method, and the procedures for data examination were identical to those employed in the direct MR analysis.

Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis aims to evaluate the pathway from exposure to outcome through a mediator, which allows for the decomposition of the overall effect into indirect and direct effects and helps explore the potential mechanisms by which exposure affects outcome. The total influence of GM on FD was further divided into two components: (1) the direct influence of genus on FD, and (2) the indirect influence of genus on FD, mediated by CIPs. The proportion of the mediation effect was determined by dividing the indirect effect by the total effect, with the concurrent calculation of the 95% confidence interval (CI).

Results

The associations of GM with FD

Following the exclusion of SNPs associated with potential risk factors for FD, a total of 2091 IVs were ultimately incorporated to investigate the causal impacts of the gut microbiota on FD. The F-statistics of all IVs were > 10, indicating no evidence of weak instrument bias existed (Table S2). As presented in Figs. 2 and 3A and Table S3, 12 bacterial genera were identified significantly correlated with FD, comprising class Deltaproteobacteria, class Gammaproteobacteria, family Acidaminococcaceae, genus Blautia, genus Lachnospiraceae FCS020 group, genus Family XIII AD3011 group, genus Haemophilus, genus Ruminococcaceae UCG013, genus Ruminococcus gauvreauii group, genus Sutterella, genus Terrisporobacter and phylum Cyanobacteria were associated with FD in at least one MR method. On the one hand, 9 genera were negatively correlated with the risk of FD: class Deltaproteobacteria (OR = 0.867, 95% CI [0.754 − 0.996], P = 0.043), class Gammaproteobacteria (OR = 0.807, 95% CI [0.652 − 0.999], P = 0.049), family Acidaminococcaceae (OR = 0.834, 95% CI [0.717 − 0.971], P = 0.019), genus Lachnospiraceae FCS020 group (OR = 0.866, 95% CI [0.754 − 0.996], P = 0.044), genus Family XIII AD3011 group (OR = 0.852, 95% CI [0.738 − 0.983], P = 0.028), genus Ruminococcaceae UCG013 (OR = 0.854, 95% CI [0.736 − 0.992], P = 0.039), genus Ruminococcus gauvreauii group (OR = 0.857, 95% CI [0.750 − 0.978], P = 0.022), genus Sutterella (OR = 0.839, 95% CI [0.733 − 0.960], P = 0.011) and genus Terrisporobacter (OR = 0.836, 95% CI [0.715 − 0.977], P = 0.024). Meanwhile, 3 genera were positively correlated with the risk of FD: genus Blautia (OR = 1.494, 95% CI [1.004 − 2.222], P = 0.047), genus Haemophilus (OR = 1.160, 95% CI [1.017 − 1.325], P = 0.028), phylum Cyanobacteria (OR = 1.167, 95% CI [1.035 − 1.316], P = 0.011). Pleiotropy and heterogeneity tests between GM and FD resulted in P > 0.05. MR-PRESSO showed no evidence of pleiotropy or heterogeneity in the relationship between the GM and FD (overall test P > 0.05, Fig. 2, Table S4-S6). The scatter plots, leave-one-out plots and funnel plot for sensitivity analysis can be found in FigS1-3.

Forest plots of MR results for 12 gut microbiota on functional dyspepsia. Abbreviation: MR, Mendelian Randomization; NSNP, number of SNPs; OR, odds ratio.

MR analysis of gut microbiota and functional dyspepsia (A) Circular heatmap of the causal relationship between GM and FD. (B) Circular heatmap of the causal relationship between FD and GM.

To explore the causal effect of FD on gut microbes, a reverse MR analysis was performed. As demonstrated on Fig. 3B, Table S8&S13, we concluded that there was no reverse causal relationship between the GM and FD. To ensure the validity of the findings, horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity were assessed using MR-Egger’s intercept, the MR-PRESSO test, and the heterogeneity test (Table S9-S16). As shown in Fig. 3B, the detection results of all GM on FD indicate that the GM is categorized into 5 levels: order, phylum, class, family, and genus. The shading of colors reflects the size of the p-values within the circles, with all p-values being greater than 0.05, indicating that there is no reverse causal relationship.

The associations between circulating inflammatory proteins and FD

A total of 280 SNPs were employed as IVs for the relationship between c and FD, and the F statistics for each SNP ranged from 29.21 to 1848.48, suggesting the absence of instrument bias (Table S17). CIPs that lowered the risk of FD were IL-12 (OR = 0.918, 95% CI [0.870–0.968], P = 0.001) and IL-10 (OR = 0.802, 95% CI [0.688–0.937], P = 0.005). On the other hand, inflammatory proteins that increased the risk of FD included C-X-C motif chemokine 10 levels (OR = 1.139, 95% CI [1.008–1.288], P = 0.038), CXCL9 (OR = 1.178, 95% CI [1.022–1.358], P = 0.024) and Vascular endothelial growth factor A levels (OR = 1.061, 95% CI [1.001–1.124], P = 0.045). To assess the reliability of these results, both the MR-Egger (Table S18) and MR-PRESSO tests (Table S19) were conducted on the studied SNP loci. Neither test indicated the presence of potential horizontal pleiotropy (P > 0.05). Moreover, the modified Cochran’s Q test determined that there was no significant variability in the impacts of the analyzed SNPs (P > 0.90). Additionally, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the influence of SNP loci on the overall causal connection. After systematically excluding individual SNPs and reanalyzing the data, the analysis showed no significant differences in the observed causal relationship, indicating that the estimated effects were not linked to any particular genetic factor. The results of the MR analyses are depicted in Table S18 and Fig. 4, while the findings from the heterogeneity test and horizontal pleiotropy analysis are detailed in the Table S20&S21. FigS4-6 presents a scatter plot illustrating the exposure-outcome correlation and assesses the reliability of findings using the leave-one-out technique.

The link between GM and circulating inflammatory proteins

In this study, we employed MR analysis to elucidate the associations between specific microbial genera and FD-related CIPs (Table S23). As shown on Fig. 5, the presence of genus Family XIII AD3011 group was associated with an increase in CXCL9 (OR = 1.123, 95% CI [1.006–1.254], P = 0.039), while genus Haemophilus was linked to a decrease in VEGFA (OR = 0.906, 95% CI [0.825–0.994], P = 0.038). In subsequent MVMR analysis, the associations between the genus Family XIII AD3011 group, CXCL9, genus Haemophilus, and FD remained significant. However, the previously observed association between VEGFA and FD was no longer significant. Besides, the percentage of the genus Family XIII AD3011 group to FD through CXCL9 was 11.8%, while that of genus Haemophilus to FD through VEGFA levels was 3.93%. These findings indicated a consistent alignment of the overall, indirect, and direct impacts, as presented in Figs. 5 and 6.

Discussion

Prior investigations have validated the influence of the intestinal microbiome on the overall well-being of humans. Indeed, intestinal microbiome not only mediates interactions between gut commensal bacteria and the development of the human immune system26but also affects the psychological and neurological functions of the host27. Furthermore, it plays an essential role in the maintenance of regular intestinal function and overall well-being28. This research involved gathering the most extensive GWAS dataset available on the GM and FD and conducting a comprehensive assessment of the causal connections between the GM, circulating inflammatory proteins, and FD using MR analysis. Three sets of microorganisms were positively correlated with FD, whereas nine genera displayed a negative relationship, with eight at the genus level, two at the class, one at the family level, and one at the phylum level. Analysis of established inflammatory proteins in circulation uncovered that IL-12, CXCL10, CXCL9 along with VEGFA are implicated in the onset and progression of FD. Furthermore, the findings from the mediation analysis confirmed the mediating role of circulating inflammatory proteins in the relationship between GM and the development of FD.

Taken together, the findings of this study suggest that an increased abundance of bacteria such as class Deltaproteobacteria, class Gammaproteobacteria, family Acidaminococcaceae, genus Lachnospiraceae FCS020 group, genus Family XIII AD3011 group, genus Ruminococcaceae UCG013, genus Ruminococcus gauvreauii group, genus Sutterella and genus Terrisporobacter may exert a beneficial impact against the onset and progression of FD.

Ruminococcaceae, commonly found in the rumen of ruminant animals, are bacteria that can degrade complex carbohydrates and provide energy through fermentation for ruminants29. This family produces butyrate, which enhances gut health by improving the barrier function of the intestinal mucosa, reducing permeability, and thus lowering the risk of inflammation and infection30. Research indicates that the stool of individuals with constipation contains factors that inhibit the growth of Ruminococcaceae. A reduction in Ruminococcaceae levels decreases butyrate production, leading to increased epithelial permeability and contributing to constipation31,32. Additionally, the actions of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), like butyrate in the gut suggest that they may reduce bacterial translocation, maintain intestinal integrity, and suppress inflammation by stimulating mucin synthesis33,34. In our study, genus Ruminococcaceae UCG013 and genus Ruminococcus gauvreauii group were shown to be protective factors for FD, which is in agreement with studies reported in the literature.

Firmicutes is a large group of Gram-positive bacteria characterized by their thick cell walls and is a crucial component of the GM, encompassing many different genera and species such as Blautia, Lachnospiraceae, family Acidaminococcaceae, Family XIII AD3011 group, and others35. These bacteria play key roles in a variety of biological processes, including the production of SCFAs, the maintenance of the intestinal barrier function, and the regulation of immune responses36. Lachnospiraceae is a family of bacteria belonging to the phylum Firmicutes. Notably, most bacteria in this family are anaerobic Gram-positive and typically rod-shaped or spiral-shaped. They are commensal bacteria associated with healthy intestinal states and play a complex and multifaceted role in gut health and disease37. Earlier studies have pointed out that a rise in the abundance of Lachnospiraceae is a significant indicator of the initial stages of Chron’s disease38. Lachnospiraceae FCS020 group, a member of the Lachnospiraceae family, plays a critical role in intestinal well-being39. A clinical study revealed a notable correlation between an increased abundance of Lachnospiraceaeand a reduction in colonic inflammation, as well as an enhancement in quality of life, in individuals diagnosed with ulcerative colitis40. Our results showed that Lacertidae FCS020 group, the Family XIII AD3011 group, family Acidococcus, group Ruminococcus gauvreauii and the genus Terrisporobacter were positively associated with the onset and progression of FD. The Acidococcaceae familyis known for producing butyric acid41. Several studies have shown that an increase in butyrate production usually improves intestinal epithelial function and immune function in relation to it42,43. In one study, the Family XIII AD3011 groupwas demonstrated to reduce inflammatory levels while also modifying the GM in young, healthy men44. The group Ruminococcus gauvreauii and the genus Terrisporobacter also belong to the Firmicutes. Prior research has indicated that the Ruminococcus torques groupmay possess probiotic properties, potentially contributing to the suppression of inflammatory processes in colitis45. The genus Terrisporobacteris associated with the content of SCFAs and oxidative indicators, and may be involved in carbohydrate metabolism in the gut, which could have an impact on intestinal health46. The specific functions of these two bacteria are not well understood, and further mechanistic exploration is still needed. Nevertheless, in our study, genus Blautia was found to be significant, indicating a risk factor for FD. The present study corroborates the positive correlation between genus Blautiaand inflammation47, which was previously identified in our research, and its potential mechanisms require further investigation.

The observed association between certain Gram-negative bacteria and FD highlights the intricate interactions between the GM and host metabolic processes. Notably, the genera Haemophilus, Deltaproteobacteria and Gammaproteobacteria were negatively associated with FD in our study, suggesting that an increase in these bacterial populations may be associated with a reduction in FD symptoms. This finding is consistent with previous studies, which have shown that certain bacterial taxa can modulate gastrointestinal function and influence the metabolic state of the host48, In contrast, Sutterella was positively associated with FD, suggesting that higher levels of this genus might exacerbate dyspeptic symptoms. This observation is particularly intriguing as Sutterellais thought to be associated with a variety of gastrointestinal disorders and may contribute to inflammation through mechanisms such as influencing intestinal motility and the production of metabolites49,50. The contrasting relationship of these bacterial genera with FD highlights the potential of targeting microbiota modulation as a therapeutic strategy.

Interestingly, our study found a positive correlation between cyanobacteria and FD. Cyanobacteria, a group of photosynthetic bacteria, are known to influence the composition and function of the GM, which in turn affects host health. Cyanobacteria have been less studied in the gut, and understanding the metabolic pathways associated with them may elucidate their functional role in FD.

Findings from our reverse Mendelian randomisation study strongly suggest against the hypothesis of reverse causality between GM and FD. The lack of a significant association between genetically determined GM composition and FD symptoms suggests that alterations in the GM do not precede or contribute to the development of FD. Although the GM may play a role in a variety of gastrointestinal conditions, it does not appear to be directly inversely causally related to FD.

Furthermore, our investigation focused on the mediating impact of inflammatory proteins in the connection between GM and FD, revealing that CXCL9 and VEGFA play a vital role in this relationship. CXCL9 has emerged as an important biomarker in a variety of pathological conditions, including inflammatory diseases and cancer. This is because it attracts T cells and other immune cells to sites of inflammation51,52, thereby helping to modulate the immune response53. Dysregulation of the immune response may lead to altered gastrointestinal dynamics and changes in sensitivity, which are important features of FD54. In addition, the association of CXCL9 with microbial dysbiosis, particularly with specific bacterial taxa, highlights the potential of this chemokine as a link between the GM and host health. Furthermore, mediation analysis demonstrated that CXCL9 acted as a mediator in the association between the genus Family XIII AD3011 group and FD, accounting for 11.8% of the mediation. VEGFA is a pivotal pro-angiogenic cytokine that plays a crucial role in the proliferation of endothelial cells and the regulation of vascular permeability. A notable study revealed that Clostridium butyricumcan suppress the expression of vimentin and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 in colorectal cancer (CRC) cells, effectively impeding the epithelial-mesenchymal transition that characterizes intestinal carcinogenesis55. Additionally, the study highlighted a direct neuroprotective effect of VEGF on the enteric nervous system, as evidenced in a cellular assay56. These findings suggest that VEGF may be intricately involved in the pathophysiology of FD, highlighting its potential role in the gastrointestinal system beyond its angiogenic properties. Our study revealed that the relationship between genus Haemophilus and FD is influenced by the VEGFA, with a mediation proportion of 3.93%.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to employ a comprehensive Mendelian randomization framework to analyze the causal relationship between GM, CIPs and FD. Furthermore, the two-sample analysis and mediation analysis of GM and FD established a pathway linking GM to FD. Additionally, to enhance the reliability of the Mendelian randomization findings, this research utilized multiple sensitivity analyses.

However, our study also has several limitations that cannot be overlooked. Firstly, demographic information such as age and gender was not collected, which restricted the depth of our analysis. Exploring the deeper mechanisms and related factors is also one of the future directions. Secondly, the GWAS was focused on Europeans, limiting the applicability of its findings to other populations. Furthermore, Our results did not pass the stringent Bonferroni correction. Nevertheless, the Bonferroni correction is a conservative method that can result in an increased likelihood of false negatives.

Conclusion

In summary, our study utilized Mendelian randomization techniques and identified 12 GM causally linked to FD. Additionally, reverse Mendelian randomisation showed that there was no reverse causality between FD and GM. Furthermore, two mediating relationships were identified between GM and FD causal pathways. This analysis provides evidence for the causal impact of GM on FD. Additionally, mediation analysis demonstrated that CXCL9 and VEGFA mediate the connections between GM and FD. Finally, the identified GM can serve as indicators for the diagnosis and treatment of FD, as well as facilitating research into the mechanisms underlying FD.
