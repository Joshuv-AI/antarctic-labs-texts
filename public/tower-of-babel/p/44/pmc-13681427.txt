Dietary Patterns Modulate Ozone-Induced Reproductive Injury in C57BL/6J Mice
Authors: Yuzhi Guo, Shen Chen, Jiahao Zhang, Miao Li, Xinhang Jiang, Yahan Yang, Lizhu Ye, Zhaohuan Gui, Guanghui Dong, Daochuan Li, Wen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dietary patterns orchestrate systemic metabolism and reproductive health, yet whether specific dietary patterns modulate susceptibility to ozone-induced male reproductive toxicity and its intergenerational consequences remains unclear. Here, male C57BL/6J mice were fed ad libitum (AL), a 30% calorie-restricted diet (CR), or a 45% high-fat diet (HFD), followed by ozone exposure (1 ppm, 4 h/day, 5 days/week for 3 months) to recapitulate real-world condition. We found that ozone exposure exacerbated reproductive injury under both CR and HFD, each diet perturbing metabolism through distinct pathways. Under CR, ozone further suppressed glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and amino acid metabolism, resulting in severe testicular energy depletion and impaired spermatogenic differentiation. In contrast, HFD amplified ozone-induced metabolic overload, inflammatory activation, and mitochondrial stress, accompanied by dysregulated lipid metabolism. Integrated metabolomic analyses identified disruption of the glucose-lactate-TCA axis as a central mechanism underlying testicular energy failure. Furthermore, exposed males (F0) were subsequently mated with unexposed females to generate offspring (F1 and F2) for assessment of next-generation health risks. The results showed that paternal dietary regimen and ozone exposure exclusively perturbed metabolic homeostasis in F1 offspring, indicating a reversible metabolic reprogramming rather than stable transgenerational inheritance. Collectively, these findings establish systemic metabolic status as a critical determinant of ozone-induced reproductive toxicity and reveal that paternal nutritional and environmental challenges jointly prime metabolic susceptibility in the next generation (F1).

Introduction

Air pollution is the fourth leading risk factor for premature mortality and disability worldwide. Among air pollutants, ozone-related mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) have been increasing annually, with attributable deaths rising by 46% from 2000 to 2019. Epidemiological studies have shown that each 10 μg/m3 increase in ozone concentration is associated with a 3% increase in nonaccidental mortality. Emerging evidence indicates that ozone exposure is associated with respiratory, metabolic, reproductive, and multisystem injuries. Epidemiological studies suggest that ozone may reduce sperm quality by affecting telomere length; however, toxicological evidence that clarifies the mechanisms is limited. Furthermore, previous studies indicate that parental nutrition and environmental exposures during germ cell development may affect offspring health through epigenetic mechanisms , and may even exert transgenerational effects. , Nevertheless, the impact of parental ozone-induced reproductive injury on offspring health and its potential to propagate across generations remains largely unexplored.

The health risks associated with ozone exposure can be influenced by external and internal factors, such as diet, lifestyle, and pre-existing metabolic disorders. Epidemiological and clinical studies have shown that dietary composition, nutritional status, and lifestyle modulate the body’s response to environmental pollutants. − Calorie restriction (CR) and high-fat diet (HFD) represent two extreme dietary patterns commonly observed in human populations. The National Institutes of Health defines CR as a daily caloric intake below normal levels without causing malnutrition or essential nutrient deficiencies. Extensive evidence indicates that CR reduces oxidative damage, enhances autophagy, suppresses inflammation, and mitigates DNA damage, , thereby maintaining metabolic homeostasis and extending lifespan. Nonetheless, CR may also have adverse effects, such as delayed wound healing, impaired bone health, and reduced immune function. , Prior studies indicate that severe caloric restriction may impair reproductive function, although the magnitude and direction of these effects depend on sex, species, and the extent of restriction. − In contrast, HFD epitomizes the pervasive nutritional excess characteristic of modern societies. High-fat or high-calorie diets reprogram metabolism, trigger oxidative stress, inflammation, and insulin resistance, and constitute a primary driver of metabolic syndrome and obesity risk. These metabolic perturbations amplify vulnerability to environmental insults and may correlate with reduced male fertility. , Yet, despite the ubiquity of both CR and HFD regimens, how their distinct systemic metabolic signatures modulate ozone-driven reproductive injury remains unresolved.

In the physiological energy metabolism of germ cells, Sertoli cells convert glucose to lactate via aerobic glycolysis and supply this lactate to adjacent germ cells, sustaining spermatogenesis. Within germ cells, lactate is oxidized to pyruvate, which enters the TCA cycle to generate ATP. , Although dietary patterns reprogram systemic metabolism, how these changes reconfigure testicular metabolic fingerprints, alter substrate uptake (fats, glucose, amino acids), and ultimately govern nutrient allocation within the testis remains largely undefined. Our previous studies demonstrated that gestational ozone exposure induces developmental toxicity in a mouse model, including placental trophoblast dysfunction and low birth weight, with additive effects under high-temperature conditions. However, the extent to which ozone disrupts male reproductive function, and whether such effects are reshaped by underlying metabolic status, remain poorly understood. Comparative studies across defined metabolic states are therefore essential to dissect the mechanisms of ozone-induced male reproductive toxicity.

Our early work showed that 2 ppm ozone induces overt toxicity, whereas 0.5 ppm produces only marginal effects. Accounting for interspecies extrapolation and the summer ambient levels recorded in Chinese cities (∼200 μg/m3), 1 ppm was selected as the environmentally relevant and biologically informative dose. In this study, we subjected CR and HFD male mice to three months of environmentally relevant ozone exposure to test whether diet-induced metabolic states modulate male reproductive vulnerability. Integrative analyses were used to identify shared core metabolic pathways and state-specific pathways. By mating exposed males with unexposed females, we further generated F1 and F2 offspring to assess the persistence and potential transgenerational legacy of ozone-amplified reproductive injury.

Material and Methods

Animal Use

8-week-old C57BL/6J male mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). All animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 50% relative humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to water and standard chow. Mice were acclimatized for 2 weeks before experimental procedures.

Establishment of Mouse Models with Different Dietary Regimens and Ozone Exposures

A total of 150 male C57BL/6J mice (10 weeks old) were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 50 per group): ad libitum (AL), calorie restriction (CR), and high-fat diet (HFD). Mice in the AL group were fed the AIN-93G standard diet. CR was achieved by reducing daily food quantity by 30%, based on the average daily chow intake of the ad libitum group, without altering the diet composition. For the HFD, the composition was 24% protein, 41% carbohydrates, and 24% fat by weight, corresponding to an energy contribution of 45.4% from fat, 20.2% from carbohydrates, and 34.5% from protein. All mice first underwent a 4 week dietary intervention to establish the respective dietary models. At the 4th week, 10 mice from each group were randomly selected for dissection to assess baseline metabolic status. Subsequently, mice in each dietary model were randomly assigned to either a 3 month ozone exposure group (1 ppm, 4 h/day, 5 days/week) or an air-filtered control group (n = 20 per group). Body weight and food intake were recorded weekly. Following three months of ozone exposure, mice were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital at 100 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection, euthanized, and blood samples were collected from the inferior vena cava. All animal procedures were conducted according to the guidelines of the Animal Care and Protection Committee of Guangzhou Center for Disease Control and Prevention (No. 2024113).

The ozone exposure system was adapted from an individually ventilated cage (IVC)-based platform, into which an ozone delivery module was integrated. This module consists an oxygen generator coupled with an ozone generator, allowing continuous ozone supply to the exposure chambers to achieve the target concentration. Ozone concentrations within the exposure chambers were monitored using an online detection system at multiple time points to ensure stability and accuracy. The target ozone concentration was reached within 15–30 min and maintained within the predefined range throughout the exposure period.

Mate and Offspring Generation

After the 3 month exposure, male mice were mated with untreated females (10 weeks old) at a 1:2 ratio, and vaginal plugs were checked the following morning (recorded as gestational day 0.5). The fertility rate for each group was calculated as the proportion of males that successfully sired offspring. To determine pregnancy rate, plugged females were housed individually and pregnancy outcomes recorded. Litter size and birth weight were assessed after delivery. After F1 males reaching sexual maturity, they were mated with untreated females following the same protocol to generate the F2 offspring, and litter size and birth weight were again documented.

Sperm Count Assessment

Sperm motility and concentration were assessed using an automated sperm analyzer (Hamilton Thorne, TOX IVOX, USA) in mice from all experimental groups, including AL, CR, and HFD groups exposed to either filtered air or 1 ppm ozone. The left cauda epididymis was minced and incubated in prewarmed human tubal fluid (HTF; Millipore, MR-070-D) at 37 °C for 5 min to allow complete sperm release. The suspension was then diluted with prewarmed HTF, and 10 μL of the diluted sample was loaded onto a slide for sperm parameter analysis.

Histopathological Analysis

About 8 mice per group were randomly selected for histological evaluation. The testis, inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT), and ovarian white adipose tissue (oWAT) were collected for pathological examination. Testicular tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, G1101), while iWAT and oWAT were fixed in a specialized adipose tissue fixative (Servicebio, G1119). Paraffin-embedded samples were sectioned at 5 μm thickness and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Whole-section images were acquired using a TissueFAXS system (Tissuegnostics). Seminiferous tubule diameter and adipocyte area were quantified using ImageJ (NIH, USA).

Immunofluorescence Staining for C-Kit

C-kit is a well-established marker of differentiating spermatogonia in the testis. Testicular sections (5 μm) were mounted on slides, deparaffinized, and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in 0.1 mol/L citrate buffer (pH 5.8). After cooling, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-c-Kit primary antibody (R&D, AF1356, USA), followed by incubation with a fluorescent secondary antibody for 30 min at 37 °C and counterstaining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min at room temperature. Images were captured using a laser-scanning confocal microscope (Leica, Germany). For quantification, c-Kit-positive cells were counted in 20 randomly selected seminiferous tubules per section in each group, and the mean number of positive cells per tubule was calculated.

Hematological, Hormonal, and Biochemical Analyses

Peripheral blood was collected by inferior vena cava exsanguination into EDTA-coated tubes (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA). Hematological parameters were measured using an automated hematology analyzer (HemaVet 950FS, DREW, USA). Plasma was obtained by centrifugation at 2500g for 10 min at room temperature. Plasma total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were quantified using an automated biochemical analyzer (Pointcare M3, MNCHIP, Tianjin, China). Plasma malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were determined using MDA assay kit (Beyotime, China). Plasma luteinizing hormone (LH) and testosterone were measured with ELISA kits (Elabscience, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Quantification of Testicular Lactate, Pyruvate, and Citrate

Testicular lactate, pyruvate, and citrate were quantified with commercial kits from Beyotime (lactate, cat. S0227S; pyruvate, cat. S0299S; Shanghai, China) and Solarbio (citrate, cat. S0335S; Beijing, China) according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

Quantification of Hepatic Triglycerides, Total Cholesterol, and ATP

Hepatic triglycerides, total cholesterol, and ATP were quantified with commercial kits from Beyotime (TG, cat. S0219S; T-CHO, cat. S0211S; Shanghai, China) and Solarbio (ATP, cat. S0026; Beijing, China) according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

Glucose and Insulin Tolerance Tests

For glucose tolerance tests (GTT) and insulin tolerance tests (ITT), mice were fasted for 14 or 4 h with free access to water. Mice were administered d-glucose (2 g/kg body weight) or insulin (0.4 U/kg body weight) by intraperitoneal injection. Blood glucose was measured from tail-tip blood at 0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min after injection using an automated glucose monitor (Abbott, USA).

Open-Field Test and Y-Maze Test

Open-field test: The open-field test was conducted using a 40 cm × 40 cm × 30 cm white acrylic chamber. Mice were acclimated to the behavioral testing room for 1 h before testing. Each mouse was placed in the center of the chamber and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. The chamber was divided into a central area and a peripheral area. Locomotor activity, including the distance traveled and time spent in the central zone, was recorded and analyzed using the EthoVision XT automated tracking system to assess anxiety-like behavior. Animals were always placed in the chamber from the same direction. After each test, the chamber was cleaned with 75% ethanol to remove residual odors and maintain consistency.

Y-maze test: spatial working memory was assessed using a Y-maze apparatus (arm length: 40 cm, base width: 10 cm, wall height: 15 cm). The three arms were designated A, B, and C in a clockwise orientation. Mice were acclimated to the behavioral testing room for 1 h before testing. Each mouse was introduced into arm A and allowed to explore the maze freely for 5 min, during which locomotor trajectories were recorded using the EthoVision XT automated tracking system. The number of sequential entries from arm A into arms B and C (or C and B) was quantified. The alternation ratio was calculated as the number of triad sequences (A–B–C or A–C–B) divided by the total number of arm entries minus two. The maze was cleaned with 75% ethanol between trials to eliminate odor cues.

Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol and purified by isopropanol precipitation. RNA concentration and integrity were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Reverse transcription was performed using the Advantage RT-for-PCR Kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan), and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was carried out using the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Toyobo, Tokyo, Japan). β-Actin served as the internal control. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method.

RNA-Seq Transcriptomic Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from testis tissue (n = 3 per group) using TRIzol reagent and purified by isopropanol precipitation. RNA concentration and integrity were evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. mRNA was isolated using oligo (dT)-attached magnetic beads and fragmented into short fragments. First- and second-strand cDNA synthesis was performed sequentially, followed by end repair, A-tailing, and ligation to RNA index adapters. The resulting libraries were amplified by PCR, purified, and assessed for quality using the Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Single-stranded circular DNA libraries were generated, amplified into DNA nanoballs (DNBs), and sequenced on the BGIseq500 platform. Clean paired-end reads were aligned to the reference genome using STAR software. Gene expression levels were quantified with FeatureCounts, and differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package (v1.20.0), with a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Plasma Metabolomics Analysis

Untargeted metabolomic profiling was conducted using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). In total, 1164 metabolic features in positive ion mode and 743 features in negative ion mode were detected. Quality control (QC) samples were generated by pooling equal volumes of all study samples and were injected before, during, and after sample acquisition. The first three QC injections were used to assess instrument performance and chromatographic-mass spectrometric equilibration, followed by three additional QC injections for metabolite annotation. QC samples were periodically interspersed throughout the analytical sequence to evaluate system stability and support data quality control. Raw MS data were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard and processed with XCMS for peak detection, retention-time alignment, feature grouping, and quantification. Metabolite annotation was performed by matching features to high-quality MS/MS spectral databases using predefined mass-accuracy tolerances and adduct information. Only features with a coefficient of variation <30% in QC samples were retained for downstream statistical analyses. Metabolite intensities were expressed as peak areas and normalized to the total ion signal. Features with >50% missing values in any group were excluded. Final metabolite identities were assigned using an in-house metabolite database. Differential metabolites were identified using thresholds of |FC| > 1.2 and P < 0.1. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/).

Statistical Analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test was used for comparisons among multiple groups. Statistical analyses and figure preparation were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0, with significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Establishment of Calorie Restriction and High Fat Diet Mouse Models under Ozone Exposure

To model extreme dietary patterns observed in human populations, we generated 30% calorie restriction (CR) and 45% high-fat diet (HFD) models in C57BL/6J mice as described in the Methods and illustrated in Figure A. Before ozone exposure, CR mice weighed 24.9% less and had a 39.0% lower fat-to-lean mass ratio than ad libitum (AL) controls (all P < 0.05, Figure C,D). Hepatic ATP levels were 40.6% higher (Figure E), while serum TG, TC, and LDL-C reduced by 9.9–26.9% (Figure G–I). Glucose, hemoglobin, and essential amino acids (tryptophan, valine, leucine, and phenylalanine) remained unchanged (Figures F and S1C–G) and no lean-mass loss was observed. Compared with AL mice, HFD mice gained 5.1% more body weight and 35.8% more fat-to-lean mass ratio (Figure C,D). They also exhibited 2.5–22.0% higher glucose, TC, and LDL-C, and a 44.2% rise in TG (Figure F–I), whereas hepatic ATP, hemoglobin, and essential amino acids were unaltered (Figures E and S1C–G). These data confirm that CR and HFD models were successfully established, producing lean, metabolically efficient animals and obese, dyslipidemic animals, respectively.

To simulate realistic human ozone exposure, we delivered a stable 1 ppm ozone exposure system throughout the exposure period (Figure C). The choice of this concentration is justified at the end of INTRODUCTION. Ozone raised white blood cell counts by 32.5% in AL mice and by 52.0% in CR mice (both P < 0.05; Figure S1B), whereas HFD mice showed almost no change. In addition, CR itself lessened the ozone-induced rise in WBCs by 23.8%. Taken together, these findings verified the successful establishment of two distinct dietary mouse models and a stable ozone exposure platform for subsequent analyses.

Calorie Restriction and High Fat Diet Amplified Ozone-Induced Testicular Injury

To determine how the two dietary regimens modulate ozone-induced male reproductive injury, we monitored oxidative stress, testicular histology, sperm quality, mating success, and germ cell differentiation. In the absence of ozone, CR markedly compromised reproductive function: testicular weight declined 12.8% (Figure S2A), malondialdehyde dropped 48.5% (Figure S2B), testosterone declined 68.3%, seminiferous tubule diameter shrank 4.6%, sperm concentration decreased 43.8%, sperm motility reduced by 38.5%, and the mating rate was 40.0% lower compared to AL mice (Figures and C). HFD likewise impaired reproduction, though less severely, raising MDA 62.5% (Figure S2B) while lowering testosterone 64.1%, tubule diameter 9.4%, sperm concentration 17.9%, motility 21.0%, and mating rate 13.0% (Figures and C). Thus, both calorie restriction and high-fat diet already undermined male reproductive performance, with CR exerting the more severe impact.

Ozone exposure amplified reproductive injury in every dietary group, but the extent of damage differed by diet. In AL mice, ozone raised MDA by 70.0%, lowered testosterone by 39.1%, reduced sperm concentration and motility by 36.6% and 26.2%, and decreased pup birth weight by 5.63% (Figures and C). In CR mice, ozone increased MDA levels by 72.4%, reduced seminiferous tubule diameter by 7.5%, and decreased sperm motility by 37.0%, although litter size and pup weight were unchanged (Figures and C). HFD mice exhibited a 69.5% rise in MDA and a 27.6% reduction in sperm motility (Figures and C). Analysis of germ cell differentiation revealed that, even before ozone exposure, both CR and HFD had already elevated the expression of undifferentiated spermatogonia marker Plzf but suppressed the differentiation markers, C-kit, Smc3, and Acrv1 (Figures A,B and S2D–G), indicating early blockade of spermatogenesis. Ozone further intensified this blockade across all dietary regimens.

Because male reproduction is governed jointly by the hypothalamic-pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis and local testicular activity, we measured circulating luteinizing hormone (LH). Neither diet nor ozone altered LH levels (Figure C), whereas testosterone was markedly reduced, pointing to primary testicular rather than central endocrine failure. Consistently, the Leydig-cell steroidogenic genes Cyp11a1, Cyp17a1, Hsd17b7, and Star were markedly downregulated in CR mice and further suppressed by ozone exposure, whereas HFD mice showed only modest changes (Figure S2H–K). Together, these observations indicate that dietary pattern dictates testicular function and, consequently, vulnerability to ozone-driven reproductive injury, with CR-evoked deficits in intratesticular testosterone synthesis and spermatogenesis conferring the greatest susceptibility to oxidative stress.

Diet-Dependent Pathway Disruption in Ozone-Induced Testicular Injury

To address how diet modulates ozone-induced reproductive injury, we performed RNA-seq and ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) on testes. Metabolic profiling revealed that both dietary regimens suppressed carbohydrate, amino acid, lipid metabolism, and mitochondrial ATP synthesis. CR blocked inflammatory and hormonal signaling primarily by down-regulating micronutrient-related pathways (e.g., iron, zinc, and retinol), thereby inhibiting autophagy and cellular renewal, ultimately leading to reproductive dysfunction. In contrast, HFD promoted inflammatory responses and disrupted hormonal networks, including insulin-like growth factor (IGF) and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathways, to disrupt germ cell development (Figures A,B and S3).

In AL mice, ozone exposure simultaneously triggered inflammatory activation, disrupted energy metabolism: immune pathways were up-regulated, while the Warburg effect and NAD signaling were down-regulated, and mitochondrial efficiency declined (Figure C). In response to ozone, CR animals displayed metabolic compensation with restricted structural repair pattern, which was featured by PPAR- and FXR-driven lipid oxidation balanced energy demand, perturbed Sertoli-cell junction and GnRH pathways, repressed phagosome formation and wound-healing functions, and structural renewal stalled (Figure D). HFD mice suffered “metabolic overload, amplified inflammation, and endocrine disruption”: ozone accelerated lipid turnover and recruited signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1), T helper 1 (Th1) and alternative macrophage pathways, creating a self-perpetuating metabolic-inflammation loop, while Ca2+, gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), and oxytocin signaling were perturbed, indicating that chronic inflammation and enhanced cellular stress disrupted the local neuroendocrine regulatory network within the testis (Figure E). Taken together, ozone exposure leads to testis injury through diet-tuned energy imbalance, it lowers baseline efficiency, blocks repair when calories are scarce, and overloads mitochondria through lipid-driven inflammation.

Plasma Metabolomics Reveal Energy Deficiency as a Driver of Testicular Dysfunction

To uncover how testicular energy metabolism becomes disrupted, we profiled plasma metabolites. In both CR and HFD mice, pyruvate oxidation, the TCA cycle and the pentose-phosphate pathway were markedly suppressed, revealing diminished NADPH supply, impaired glucose-driven ATP generation and reduced NADPH biosynthesis (Figure A,B).

In AL mice, ozone exposure reprogrammed metabolism, which was characterized by activation of amino acid metabolic pathways and enhanced glutathione metabolism, indicating activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms (Figures C and S4A). In CR mice, ozone exposure exhibited a pattern of energy metabolic suppression with compensatory lipid activation, reflecting a pronounced disruption of energy supply. Broad suppression of energy and amino-acid routes (BCAA, aromatic AA, alanine-aspartate-glutamate, TCA, pentose-phosphate, CoA) coupled with up-regulation of unsaturated-fatty-acid and arachidonic-acid catabolism, indicating that a compensatory shift to lipid oxidation when glucose is scarce (Figures D and S4B). In HFD mice, ozone exposure induced a metabolic profile characterized by excessive activation of energy metabolism and dysregulated lipid oxidation. The metabolic signature was characterized by activated amino-acid and nucleotide pathways, along with accelerated TCA and glyoxylate cycles. Unsaturated-fatty-acid and steroid metabolism oscillated in both directions, revealing runaway energy flux and a lipid oxidation synthesis imbalance that amplifies oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage (Figures E and S4C). Taken together, dietary regimens dictate the systemic metabolic footprint of ozone in the testis: it boosts antioxidant amino-acid flux in AL mice, forces a glucose-to-lipid energy switch in CR mice, and triggers hyperactive TCA and lipid oxidation in HFD mice, collectively disrupting energy balance and amplifying oxidative damage.

Glucose-Lactate-TCA Axis Disruption Underlies Testicular Energy Failure

To further determine whether disruption of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle contributes to testicular dysfunction, we quantified TCA-cycle intermediates and the expression of key metabolic genes in the testis. Compared with the AL group, blood glucose levels remained unchanged in the CR group (Figure C), whereas testicular lactate levels showed a downward trend, and pyruvate and citrate levels were significantly reduced by 13.7% and 34.6% (Figure D–F). In contrast, the HFD group exhibited a 70.7% (Figure C) elevation in blood glucose, accompanied by decreasing trends in testicular pyruvate (29.0%, P < 0.05) and citrate levels (Figure D–F). Following ozone exposure, the AL group showed significant reductions in lactate (26.7%), pyruvate (29.0%) and citrate (28.1%; all P < 0.05; Figure D–F). These effects were further amplified in the CR group, where significant reductions in testicular lactate and pyruvate were observed, whereas only modest, nonsignificant changes were detected in the HFD group. Gene expression profiling revealed that CR enhanced testicular glucose uptake (Slc2a1, Slc2a3) but suppressed glycolysis (Hk1, Gpi1), TCA cycle entry (Mpc2, Pdha1), and lactate metabolism (Ldha, Ldhb, Slc16a3, Slc16a7). In contrast, HFD reduced glucose uptake and strongly inhibited glycolysis and lactate metabolism, exhibiting even greater suppression than CR. Under AL conditions, ozone primarily inhibited lactate metabolism (Ldhb, Slc16a3) and TCA cycle entry (Pdha1). In the CR group, ozone further impaired TCA-cycle entry (Pdha1) and lactate metabolism. In the HFD group, ozone predominantly suppressed mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (Cox6b1, Mpc2) and markedly reduced AMPK signaling (Prkaa2) (Figure A,B).

Taken together, diet and ozone jointly fracture the testicular glucose-lactate-TCA axis: CR already lowers pyruvate and citrate while up-regulating glucose uptake yet down-regulating glycolysis, TCA entry and lactate handling; HFD raises blood glucose and suppresses these pathways even more strongly. Ozone amplifies these lesions-depleting lactate, pyruvate and citrate most in CR, and in HFD inhibits oxidative phosphorylation and AMPK signaling-culminating in energy failure within the testis.

Metabolic Alterations in F1 Offspring Following Paternal Dietary and Ozone Exposure

Given the observed reduction in sperm quality and offspring birth weight following paternal dietary manipulations and ozone inhalation, we next assessed whether these metabolic and environmental insults impose lasting consequences on offspring health. We therefore monitored F1 offspring conceived and reared under standard conditions for assessment of neurological, reproductive and metabolic phenotypes (Figure A). Open-field and Y-maze analyses revealed no paternal effect on anxiety-like behavior or spatial learning in either sex (Figure S5), and sperm motility and mating success of F1 males remained unchanged (Figure S6), indicating that paternal exposures did not overtly compromise neurobehavioral or reproductive end points in the next generation. Metabolic profiling, however, uncovered diet-specific alterations that were more evident in female offspring (Figures and S7). Fasting glucose and insulin tolerance tests showed no statistically significant group differences, yet paternal HFD tended to improve glucose and insulin handling, whereas paternal CR exerted little influence (Figure S9). Ozone exposure, across all diets, produced a consistent upward trend in both glucose and insulin tolerance, although the increments did not attain statistical significance (Figure S9).

Lipid metabolism showed the clearest paternal imprint. Fathers on CR reduced their daughters’ fat/lean mass by 18.0% (Figure S9F) while raising plasma LDL by 37.0%, TC by 18.1% and triglycerides by 127.0% (all P < 0.05, Figure E–H); hepatic TG and TC also trended upward (Figure I,J). Adipocytes in both iWAT and oWAT were markedly smaller (≈34%, Figure B–D). Paternal HFD produced only modest, nonsignificant increases in circulating and hepatic lipids and in adipocyte size. Ozone modified these traits in a diet-specific manner. In AL offspring, ozone increased fat/lean mass by 22.8% (P < 0.05; Figure S9F), reduced hepatic TG by 44.0% (Figure I), and enlarged iWAT and oWAT adipocytes, while plasma TC and hepatic TC trended downward. CR amplified every change: fat/lean mass rose 23.6%, plasma LDL, TC and TG declined by 21.2%, 12.8%, and 39.1%, respectively, and adipocytes in iWAT and oWAT expanded 69.2% and 79.7% (Figure B–H). Thus, paternal ozone suppresses offspring lipid mobilization in a diet-dependent fashion, with CR enhancing and HFD suppressing the effect. When F1 males were bred to unexposed females, F2 offspring showed no group differences in glucose or lipid profiles (Figure S8), demonstrating that the F1 metabolic signature was not transmissible to the next generation.

Together, these findings demonstrate that paternal dietary regimens retune the F1 offspring’s lipid-handling response to ozone; however, the effect is transient and confined to the first generation, implying metabolic reprogramming rather than true transgenerational inheritance.

Discussion

Epidemiological and experimental studies indicate that ozone exposure adversely affects the male reproductive system. , However, how dietary heterogeneity and associated metabolic states modulate susceptibility to ozone-induced reproductive toxicity remains poorly understood. Here, we employed CR and HFD to model energy deficiency and energy excess, respectively, in combination with ozone exposure. The results demonstrate that ozone-induced reproductive toxicity is strongly diet dependent and manifests as distinct, context-specific injury patterns. Under CR conditions, ozone exposure was associated with alterations in pathways related to glycolysis, TCA cycle, and amino acid metabolism, suggesting aggravated limitations in testicular energy availability that may impair spermatogenic differentiation. In contrast, under HFD conditions, ozone exposure was more closely linked to lipid metabolic dysregulation, inflammatory activation, and mitochondrial dysfunction, consistent with a reproductive injury profile driven by metabolic overload. These findings identify diet-induced metabolic status as a critical biological context programming both the phenotype and mechanistic pathways of ozone-induced reproductive toxicity.

Dietary habits are key external regulators of metabolic homeostasis and environmental stress responses, exerting profound effects on susceptibility to multisystem disorders. , In recent years, extreme weight control and prolonged energy restriction have become increasingly prevalent among young populations and have been associated with female reproductive endocrine dysfunction, including reduced ovarian reserve. − In contrast, the potential risks of energy restriction to male reproductive health, particularly under conditions of environmental pollution, have received limited attention. In the present study, CR as an independent metabolic state was associated with impaired male reproductive parameters and markedly exacerbated ozone-associated reproductive toxicity. Although our previous work showed that CR attenuated PM2.5-induced lung injury via anti-inflammatory and antioxidative mechanisms, such protective effects did not extend to the reproductive system. Under CR, testicular energy balance is already constrained, and ozone-induced metabolic disturbances may further destabilize this fragile equilibrium, compromising spermatogenic maintenance. Consistent with prior studies of CR-associated impairments in wound healing, skeletal health, and immune function, , our findings support the organ-specific nature of CR effects rather than a universally protective role. From a public health perspective, these results suggest that prolonged or extreme energy restriction may heighten male reproductive vulnerability under air pollution exposure, with potential implications for population fertility.

In contrast to CR, HFD represents a prevalent state of chronic energy excess in modern societies. Extensive evidence indicates that excessive lipid intake induces metabolic disturbances characterized by lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, and low-grade chronic inflammation, which can compromise multiple organ systems, including cognitive function reduction, cancer and reproductive function. Consistent with prior studies, our results show that HFD alone reduced sperm motility and testosterone levels. Notably, additional ozone exposure in HFD-fed mice resulted in only modest further deterioration. One plausible explanation is that pre-existing metabolic and inflammatory stress under HFD conditions partially constrains additional ozone-induced effects, as overlapping pathogenic pathways may limit the extent of detectable phenotypic worsening. These findings suggest that under metabolic overload, environmental reproductive toxicity may not accumulate linearly but instead be embedded within the existing metabolic and inflammatory milieu, implying a concealed yet persistent reproductive health risk in populations with obesity and metabolic syndrome.

Testis is a highly sensitive organ to disruptions in energy homeostasis. Spermatogenesis does not rely solely on the intrinsic glycolytic capacity of germ cells but depends critically on lactate supplied by Sertoli cells as the primary energy substrate. Lactate is subsequently converted to pyruvate and enters the TCA cycle to support meiosis and spermatogenic differentiation. Accordingly, perturbations in substrate availability or mitochondrial efficiency can disproportionately impair spermatogenesis. Integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses indicate that ozone exposure does not induce a uniform reproductive toxicity phenotype but instead interacts with diet-specific metabolic vulnerabilities by altering energy flux and metabolic efficiency. Under AL, ozone primarily increased inflammation and oxidative stress with modest energetic impairment, while compensatory mechanisms partially preserved testicular function. Under CR, ozone further disrupted the glucose-lactate-TCA axis, leading to energy insufficiency and failed compensation, rendering the testis particularly vulnerable to energy depletion. In contrast, under HFD, ozone exacerbated metabolic overload, inflammatory amplification, and mitochondrial stress without inducing substrate deficiency, resulting in modest and nonadditive deterioration of reproductive function.

Although CR and HFD represent different metabolic states, their adverse reproductive outcomes are both associated with compromised mitochondrial metabolic efficiency, albeit with distinct trajectories, effect magnitudes, and phenotypic manifestations. Under energy deficiency, spermatogenesis is highly sensitive to limited substrate availability, whereas under chronic metabolic overload, persistent mitochondrial stress and oxidative damage disrupt spermatogenic differentiation. These findings indicate that testicular function may depends not on absolute energy levels but on the stability and efficiency of energy metabolism. Overall, our results demonstrate that ozone-induced reproductive toxicity is strongly influenced by metabolic context, which acts as a critical biological determinant rather than a mere modifying factor.

Previous studies have shown that parental environmental exposures increase the risk of adverse offspring outcomes, including preterm birth and low birth weight, , which are associated with long-term metabolic dysregulation. , While most evidence focuses on maternal ozone exposure during pregnancy, , the contribution of paternal exposure remains less well characterized. In this study, paternal ozone exposure was associated with metabolic alterations in F1 offspring, particularly in lipid metabolism, whereas no persistent metabolic abnormalities were observed in the F2 generation. Together with evidence that maternal HFD increases offspring susceptibility to metabolic dysfunction, our findings suggest that ozone-diet interactions primarily influence intergenerational metabolic programming rather than stable transgenerational inheritance. A plausible explanation is that paternal metabolic status and environmental exposure alter metabolic or epigenetic signals during sperm maturation, thereby resetting early developmental metabolic trajectories with no evidence of stable germline epigenetic reprogramming. These effects may therefore be context-dependent and reversible in the absence of continued exposure. Collectively, these findings suggest that environmental pollution and adverse dietary patterns can influence offspring metabolic status via paternal pathways in a context-dependent and potentially reversible manner. This underscores the importance of incorporating paternal metabolic status and lifestyle factors into assessments of reproductive and intergenerational health risks, thereby informing more precise population-level risk evaluation and intervention strategies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that diet modulates the testis’s vulnerability to ozone. CR limits glycolysis and TCA substrates, so ozone worsens the energy deficit and may impair spermatogenesis. HFD creates lipid overload and inflammation, and ozone then amplifies mitochondrial damage and metabolic stress. Thus, CR imposes “energy shortage,” HFD imposes “metabolic excess,” and ozone magnifies both. Fathers exposed to these diets and ozone are associated with a sex-skewed lipid phenotype in F1 daughters, but the effect is no longer observed in F2, indicating inter-, not trans-generational impact. Metabolic status therefore governs ozone-driven reproductive toxicity and paternal exposures sculpt offspring metabolic risk.
