Ectopic gastric squamous metaplasia with concomitant well-differentiated adenocarcinoma: a case report
Authors: Kenichi Takahashi, Satoshi Osawa, Rei Ishikawa, Ryoji Kushima, Yusuke Asai, Natsuki Ishida, Mihoko Yamade, Moriya Iwaizumi, Yasushi Hamaya, Takanori Yamada, Kazuya Shinmura, Ken Sugimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastric squamous metaplasia is a rare condition whose clinical significance and malignant potential remain unclear. Most reported cases are located in the cardia and are continuous with the esophageal squamous epithelium. Ectopic gastric squamous metaplasia, defined as lesions discontinuous from the esophagus, is exceedingly rare, and its association with gastric neoplasms has not been well characterized.Case presentationAn 81-year-old woman with a history of Helicobacter pylori eradication underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy for gastric cancer screening. A 20-mm discolored, slightly depressed lesion was identified on the greater curvature of the upper gastric body. The lesion was discontinuous from the esophageal mucosa and appeared as an iodine-unstained area. Magnifying endoscopy with narrow-band imaging revealed intrapapillary capillary loop–like vascular dilation with background coloration. Biopsy specimens suggested squamous metaplasia with reactive atypia, and en bloc resection was performed by endoscopic submucosal dissection.Postoperative histopathological examination identified mature stratified squamous epithelium consistent with gastric squamous metaplasia, and incidentally identified a well-differentiated adenocarcinoma confined to the mucosa adjacent to the metaplastic lesion without lymphovascular invasion.Immunohistochemical analysis revealed a linear arrangement of p63-positive cells along the basal layer of the metaplastic epithelium with a subset of Ki-67–positive cells indicating proliferative activity at the basal layer. p53 expression showed a wild-type pattern.ConclusionsWe report an extremely rare case of ectopic gastric squamous metaplasia associated with well-differentiated adenocarcinoma. Although a direct histogenetic relationship between the two lesions remains uncertain, their coexistence raises important considerations regarding the pathogenesis of gastric squamous metaplasia and its potential clinical significance. The immunohistochemical findings may support a possible role of competitive epithelial replacement in the development of gastric squamous metaplasia. Careful endoscopic and histopathological evaluation is warranted when this rare lesion is encountered.

Introduction

Metaplasia is an adaptive process in which one differentiated cell type is replaced by another in response to chronic injury or inflammation. In the stomach, intestinal metaplasia associated with Helicobacter pylori infection is well recognized as a precancerous condition. In contrast, squamous metaplasia of the gastric mucosa is a rare entity, and its pathogenesis and clinical significance remain poorly understood. [1–2]

Gastric squamous metaplasia has been reported predominantly in the cardia and lesser curvature of the proximal stomach, often in continuity with the esophageal squamous epithelium. This distribution has led to the hypothesis that squamous epithelium may extend from the esophagus into the gastric mucosa under conditions of chronic mucosal injury. However, cases of squamous metaplasia arising independently within the gastric mucosa, without continuity with the esophageal epithelium (ectopic gastric squamous metaplasia), are exceedingly rare. [3–4]

The relationship between gastric squamous metaplasia and neoplastic lesions remains unclear. While some reports have described an association with squamous cell carcinoma or adenosquamous carcinoma, evidence regarding its association with conventional gastric adenocarcinoma is extremely limited [5–8].

Herein, we report a rare case of ectopic gastric squamous metaplasia associated with well-differentiated adenocarcinoma in the upper gastric body, with detailed endoscopic and histopathological findings, including immunohistochemical analysis.

Case presentation

An 81-year-old woman with no significant family history was referred to our hospital for further evaluation of a gastric lesion detected during a screening esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD). She had a history of Helicobacter pylori eradication. Her past medical history included surgery for lumbar disc herniation, pulmonary sarcoidosis, meningioma, and chronic constipation, with no prior history of gastric ulcers. She had no history of smoking or alcohol consumption. Her daily medications were acetaminophen, sulpiride and laxatives. She had no history of using proton pump inhibitors or potassium-competitive acid blockers.

The patient underwent a screening EGD at a previous clinic, which revealed a flat, depressed lesion in the stomach. A biopsy was performed targeting the central area of this lesion, and the histopathological examination revealed stratified squamous epithelium with mild cytological atypia, mimicking neoplastic changes. She was subsequently referred to our hospital for further management.

Endoscopic examination revealed a discolored, slightly depressed lesion measuring approximately 20 mm in diameter on the greater curvature of the upper gastric body (Fig. 1A, B). Chromoendoscopy with indigo carmine clearly delineated the depressed morphology, and iodine staining demonstrated an unstained area corresponding to the lesion (Fig. 1C). The lesion was located away from the esophagogastric junction and showed no continuity with the esophageal squamous epithelium.

Magnifying endoscopy with narrow-band imaging (ME-NBI) revealed intrapapillary capillary loop (IPCL)-like vascular dilation and background coloration within the depressed area, mimicking esophageal squamous intraepithelial neoplasia (Fig. 2A-C). On the proximal side of the lesion, a reddish, slightly elevated component was observed, showing mild surface irregularity and an irregular vascular pattern on ME-NBI (Fig. 2D, E). No distinct demarcation line was identified. Therefore, the lesion did not fulfill the MESDA-G criteria of gastric cancer and was interpreted as a benign hyperplastic or regenerative change associated with the main lesion. Endoscopic ultrasonography demonstrated that the lesion was confined to the mucosal layer.

Regarding her screening history, the patient had undergone periodic EGDs at a previous clinic. A retrospective review of the endoscopic images revealed that the flat, depressed lesion was already present 2 years before her referral, although it had not been clinically recognized by the examiner at that time. On EGD images from earlier check-ups (5 and 7 years before referral), the lesion was not evaluable due to the lack of focused photographs of that specific anatomical area, making it difficult to assess any morphological changes over time.

Histological examination of biopsy specimens revealed squamous epithelium with reactive atypia, suggesting squamous metaplasia in the stomach. To ensure complete removal of the primary depressed lesion with a sufficient horizontal margin, this proximal elevated area was included within the planned ESD resection line without a preoperative biopsy.

The resected specimen measured 61 × 45 mm and contained a 21 × 17 mm flat, depressed lesion with a slightly elevated component at the proximal margin (Fig. 3A, B). The specimen was serially sectioned at 2-mm intervals for histopathological examination (Fig. 3A). Histopathological examination revealed mature stratified squamous epithelium replacing the gastric mucosa within the depressed area, consistent with gastric squamous metaplasia (Fig. 3E). Notably, as an incidental finding, adjacent to the proximal margin of the metaplastic lesion, an atypical glandular lesion with a mixed gastric and intestinal phenotype was identified within the mucosa. This lesion was diagnosed as intramucosal well-differentiated tubular adenocarcinoma based on marked glandular crowding and the presence of intraglandular necrotic debris (Fig. 3C, D).

We created a histopathological reconstruction map of the ESD specimen and illustrated the respective distributions of squamous metaplasia and adenocarcinoma in Fig. 3A. A low-magnification view encompassing the submucosal layer beneath the adenocarcinoma component revealed prominent inflammatory cell infiltration and lymphoid follicle formation within the submucosa, without any signs of invasive growth (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, on a single, identical histological section, the non-neoplastic stratified squamous epithelium and the well-differentiated adenocarcinoma were observed directly adjacent to each other, forming a distinct, sharp interface without any intervening normal or non-metaplastic gastric mucosa (Fig. 3F and G). No lymphovascular invasion was observed, and both the horizontal and vertical margins of the ESD specimen were negative, indicating curative resection.

Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated a linear distribution of p63-positive cells along the basal layer of the metaplastic squamous epithelium. A subset of these cells showed Ki-67 positivity, suggesting proliferative activity at the basal layer. In contrast, p53 expression showed a wild-type pattern within the metaplastic epithelium (Fig. 4).

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient has been followed up without evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

Gastric squamous metaplasia is a rare histological finding, and its pathogenesis and clinical significance remain incompletely understood. Previous reports have described this condition predominantly in the cardia and proximal stomach, often in continuity with the esophageal squamous epithelium. In such cases, it has been hypothesized that squamous epithelium may extend from the esophagus into the gastric mucosa under conditions of chronic mucosal injury. In contrast, ectopic gastric squamous metaplasia, defined as a lesion arising within the gastric mucosa without continuity with the esophageal epithelium, is exceedingly rare, and only a limited number of cases have been reported [8].

The etiology of gastric squamous metaplasia has not been fully elucidated. Accumulating evidence suggests that chronic inflammation and mucosal injury may play an important role in its development. Gastric squamous metaplasia has been reported in association with various conditions, including peptic ulcer disease, corrosive injury, infection, and chronic gastritis [3]. However, these associations are based primarily on isolated case reports. In the present case, the patient had a history of Helicobacter pylori infection and exhibited marked atrophic gastritis with intestinal metaplasia, suggesting that a chronic inflammatory environment may have contributed to the development of squamous metaplasia [5, 6].

The relationship between gastric squamous metaplasia and neoplastic lesions remains controversial. Previous reports have described cases in which squamous metaplasia was associated with squamous cell carcinoma or adenosquamous carcinoma, raising the possibility that squamous metaplasia may serve as a precursor lesion in certain contexts [9–11]. However, the available evidence is limited to case reports, and a definitive causal relationship has not been established. Furthermore, the coexistence of gastric squamous metaplasia with conventional gastric adenocarcinoma, as observed in the present case, has rarely been reported.

In the present case, a well-differentiated adenocarcinoma was identified adjacent to the area of squamous metaplasia. Although the spatial proximity of these lesions raises the possibility of a biological relationship, a direct histogenetic link cannot be established based on the current findings. It is conceivable that both lesions developed independently within a shared background of chronic mucosal injury. Alternatively, alterations in the local mucosal microenvironment associated with metaplastic change may have contributed to the development of adjacent neoplastic lesions. Further accumulation of similar cases will be required to clarify this relationship.

An additional noteworthy feature of the present case was the lack of iodine staining in the metaplastic lesion. In previously reported cases, gastric squamous metaplasia has generally been described as strongly iodine-positive, reflecting the presence of glycogen-rich squamous epithelium [5, 12]. Although mild cytological atypia was initially noted in the biopsy specimen and the lesion appeared as an iodine-unstained area, these features are considered to reflect the immature nature of the squamous metaplasia rather than neoplastic changes. Immature metaplastic squamous cells undergoing active regeneration often contain less glycogen, resulting in reduced iodine uptake, and can exhibit transient regenerative atypia. This non-neoplastic nature was further confirmed by the wild-type p53 pattern and the restricted Ki-67 expression in the resected ESD specimen.

From a clinical perspective, the combination of iodine unstaining, IPCL-like vascular patterns on magnifying endoscopy with narrow-band imaging, and histological atypia raised the suspicion of superficial squamous neoplasia [8, 13]. Based on these findings, endoscopic submucosal dissection was performed for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. The prominent appearance of IPCLs in the central area of the lesion might have been enhanced by the tissue remodeling and regenerative angiogenesis secondary to the mechanical stimulation of the prior biopsy. This case highlights that gastric squamous metaplasia may occasionally mimic squamous neoplasia both endoscopically and histologically, representing a potential diagnostic pitfall. Therefore, when encountering iodine-unstained lesions with squamous features in the stomach, careful diagnostic evaluation, including endoscopic resection, should be considered to exclude neoplastic lesions.

An additional notable finding in this case was the distinct immunohistochemical profile observed in the metaplastic epithelium. In particular, p63-positive cells were distributed in a linear pattern along the basal layer, with a subset showing Ki-67 positivity, indicating proliferative activity at the basal compartment. p63 has been identified as a key determinant of proliferative potential in stem cells of stratified epithelia, where it is characteristically expressed in the basal compartment [14]. This pattern may suggest the presence of progenitor-like cells with squamous differentiation potential. The absence of aberrant p53 expression supported the non-neoplastic nature of the metaplastic lesion.

These findings, representing a lineage alteration entirely independent of the adjacent adenocarcinoma, may be consistent with a model of epithelial remodeling in which cell populations with different differentiation programs compete for occupancy of the basal niche. A conceptually analogous mechanism—competitive replacement between epithelial lineages driven by chronic mucosal injury—has been proposed in experimental models of metaplasia in the upper gastrointestinal tract, although in that context the process involves the emergence of columnar rather than squamous epithelium [15]. Although the present findings do not provide direct evidence for such a mechanism, they offer histological support for the possibility that competitive epithelial replacement may contribute to the development of gastric squamous metaplasia, albeit through a distinct directionality.

This case has several limitations. First, as a single case report, it does not allow definitive conclusions regarding the pathogenesis or clinical significance of gastric squamous metaplasia. Second, the absence of longitudinal observation limits our ability to determine the temporal relationship between metaplasia and neoplasia. Finally, although immunohistochemical findings provide suggestive insights, they do not establish a causal mechanism.

In conclusion, we report a rare case of ectopic gastric squamous metaplasia associated with well-differentiated adenocarcinoma. This case highlights not only the possible diversity of epithelial alterations in the gastric mucosa under chronic inflammatory conditions but also the potential diagnostic challenges posed by this rare entity. Further studies are needed to clarify its biological behavior and clinical significance.
