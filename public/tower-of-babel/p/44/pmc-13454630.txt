Active vibration sensing unveils material features with a gentle touch
Authors: Danissa Sandykbayeva, Valeriya Zhanuzakova, Zhanat Kappassov, Bakhtiyar Orazbayev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wave propagation is widely used in engineering and biology, because even small changes along a propagation path encode valuable information about the traversed medium. In this paper, we employ this concept in the context of vibrotactile object perception, a cornerstone problem in safe and effective human-robot interaction. We developed a robotic system that analyzes properties of grasped objects by injecting a controlled vibration through the grasped object, while measuring vibrations on both fingers and computing spectral residual changes. We investigate object-modulated responses in the frequency domain using spectral and statistical analysis techniques and build compact spectral and statistical descriptors. We validate the method across various material properties: elasticity (rigid to elastic: PLA 100% infill, TPU, FLEX, SortaClear 40, SortaClear 18, SortaClear 12), porosity (PLA infills of: 100%, 80%, 60%, 40%, 20%, hollow), and applied grip force (1 - 5 N). The results show consistent, property-dependent spectral signatures of elasticity, porosity, and grasp force across repeated probing. Finally, we demonstrate gentle-touch grasp analysis, enabling the tactile system to assist with efficient grasping in practical scenarios (e.g., strawberry ripeness tracking) and to provide invaluable tactile information on wave-propagation behavior across various environments. The proposed method has strong potential for precision agriculture and safe human-robot interaction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54766-9.

Introduction

Vibrational phenomena affect everything from communication between living organisms to the structural integrity of natural and manmade landscapes1–3. In this day, there are myriads of application scenarios that are heavily reliant on patterns of wave propagation: finding cracks in beams3 and detecting conditional changes in industrial machines4,5, assessing structural stability6, investigating animal behavior and patterns through bioacoustics7–9, seismological analysis10,11, environmental health monitoring12,13, etc. Recent prominent developments in human-robot interaction (HRI), however, have shed new light on the use of vibration as a direct descriptor of our physical interactions with the environment14. In this context, for humans, interacting with objects often involves active tactile exploration with procedures that involve lateral motion, squeezing, holding, and enclosing15 (Fig. 1). These actions help identify varying object properties, such as texture, hardness, weight, and shape, respectively16. However, replicating such passive exploratory procedures in robots takes time and space that are not always available in the operating environment. Active vibration, on the other hand, implies that the information is extracted directly from the signal generated by the system itself, which travels to the receiver after being affected by the propagation medium17–19.Fig. 1Conceptual representation: (a) Human touch as an example of passive vibrational sensing: the signal is generated due to the arising friction between surfaces of the fingertip and the object during exploratory touch; (b) Artificial (robotic) touch in the context of active vibrational sensing: the signal is generated and controlled by the system’s own actuator, and both the transmitted (green) and received (red) signals are recorded for future comparison and analysis.

Most prior work on vibrational sensing focuses on interpreting passive haptic cues generated by dynamic physical contact with an object20,21. Since vibrational setups are notoriously difficult to sufficiently isolate in experimental scenarios, external interference and encounters with unknown or poorly generalizable objects pose major obstacles for effective robotic tactile perception. As a result, recent trends in vibrational touch sensing are shifting rapidly toward active sensing22–25. The major difference introduced by this approach is the inclusion of the system’s own controlled signal emitter, which, in theory, enables it to reliably filter out all noise unrelated to interpretable vibrational cues. However, the majority of exploratory work in the field relies heavily on the increasingly demanding computational requirements of complex machine learning algorithms26–28. This leads to setbacks in the process of generalizing patterns that can later be extrapolated to everyday objects and practical scenarios. Therefore, in addition to active signal generation, we employ a unique analytical approach based on spectral residuals, complemented by theoretical simulations that investigate the effects of varying physical properties, such as elasticity and porosity, as well as the grasp-force state, on acoustic propagation patterns. Moreover, from a practical point of view, existing approaches in perceiving the physical properties of objects through touch predominantly rely on discriminative models that assign each object to one of a predefined set of categories (for example, “soft” or “rigid”29–31), which limits their ability to sufficiently quantify the underlying material attributes. The system proposed in this paper also places the results of those dependencies in the context of robotics tactile sensing, where information about object properties directly affects the efficiency and success of grasping and subsequent tactile interactions. Lastly, we present one of the practical use cases of the approach in the context of precision agriculture, utilizing fine manipulation to distinguish fruit ripeness and general fruit condition for the fruit-picking task.

Methods

The design of the proposed experimental setup builds on our previous VibroTouch sensor for gripper-object and gripper-human detection32. In that project, we inferred the contact state by injecting vibrations into the system and observing how its resonant modes change with increasing load, providing a robust signature of contact. That study was primarily focused on gripper-object interactions, without explicit inquiry into the properties of the grasped object. As such, an eccentric rotating mass (ERM) vibration motor and a single receiver accelerometer were sufficient to capture shifts in resonant peaks due to contact. Because of the ERM motor’s narrow frequency range (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_0 = 120$$\end{document} Hz), it was unable to adequately display the spectral responses of objects with different materials and internal structures, whose highly frequency-dependent responses may extend beyond the ERM motor’s range.

In this work, however, we extend the sensor’s capabilities to approximate object properties, such as stiffness and porosity, which are essential for a stable and safe robotic grasp. Here, both gripper fingers are equipped with independent accelerometers that record transmitted and received excitations, yielding an image of an object-modulated response.Fig. 2Overview of the proposed active vibro-tactile system, where colored labels for parts in (a) match their corresponding representatives in the simulated setup in (b): (a) Exploded view and a photograph of the real-life setup; (b) Mass-spring-damper representation of the setup used for simulation; (c) General experimental procedure, from left to right: fingers slowly approaching the object; desired contact force established, propagating and recording of the signal started.

This dual configuration allows us to decouple frequency-response features arising from the setup’s state from those arising from intrinsic object properties. A voice-coil actuator drives the system with a linear-sweep sine wave over a specific frequency range, largely constrained by the hardware’s operational limitations. This method provides confidently repeatable, controlled broadband excitation, in contrast to the largely noisy responses generated by impulse-based methodologies33. With this modified sensor setup, we explicitly focus on revealing the object’s distinctive spectral signature and examine how its properties evolve under varying physical and grasp conditions.

Experimental platform

The experimental setup, showcased in Fig. 2a, is loosely inspired by the anatomy of the human fingertip, which consists of a rigid bone, soft tissue with mechanoreceptors, and an elastic skin layer. We designed two custom artificial fingers composed of a rigid plastic support (Ultimaker PLA) and a silicone tip (Formlabs Elastic 50 A resin), emulating the rigid and soft layers, respectively. One finger, housing a vibrotactile actuator (Haptuator Mark II), serves as a vibration emitter, generating a 100–800 Hz sweep signal to be propagated through an object’s medium. Analogous to the tactile receptors in human fingers, both soft fingertips contain an analog three-axis accelerometer (ADXL335) that measures both signals emitted by the vibromotor and received on the opposite side of the setup. The receiving fingertip also includes an F/T sensor (Wittenstein HEX-21 force/torque sensor) for the force-controlled grasping required for experiments. The fingers are mounted on a two-finger parallel-robot gripper (Schunk EGN 100) that continuously communicates with the force sensor via the Robot Operating System (ROS) framework. Accelerometers and a vibromotor are connected to the data-acquisition module within a microcontroller unit (STM32F407 Discovery board). A more detailed description of the data-acquisition pipeline is provided in Supplementary Fig. S1. Sampling frequency of 8 kHz provided sufficient temporal resolution for accurate reconstruction of the sweep without exceeding the available buffer memory.

Experimental objects for grasping were manufactured as 30 mm side cubes. For the elasticity experiment, the cubes can be classified into two broad groups: soft and rigid. The experimental procedure, presented in Fig. 2c, starts with a gradual gripper squeezing until a desired force of 1–5 N (with a step of 1 N) is reached. Once the required state is achieved, the vibration motor generates a broadband chirp from 100 to 800 Hz, thereby propagating the vibration through the squeezed medium. Upon reaching the receiving accelerometer, the collected data of 50 trials per state (force, material/porosity) is saved for further analysis.

Since not all statistical and analytical measures can be obvious to the naked eye, the obtained Fast Forier Transform (FFT) of the signals then undergo an estimation of a compact set of parametric features, such as Kurtosis, peak amplification (PeakAmp), min amplification (MinAmp), peak (resonant) frequency (PeakFreq), min frequency (MinFreq), and area under the curve (AUC) within the complete 0–800 Hz band. In the next section, we describe this compact set of parameters that form our feature extraction framework.

Feature extraction framework

The proposed active vibrotactile sensing approach is based on the premise that, once the gripper closes on an object, an additional mechanical transmission path is established between the excited transmitter (Tx) finger and the opposite receiver (Rx) finger. This vibration coupling modifies the propagation of the injected vibrations according to the effective mechanical properties of the squeezed object and the associated contact transmission conditions. To provide a reduced-order mechanical interpretation of this cross-finger transmission, we consider the lumped mass-spring-damper (MSD) model shown in Fig. 2b. The model is organized as two terminal subsystems corresponding to the Tx and Rx fingers, coupled through an intermediate object-mediated transmission path. The vibro-actuator is treated as an external excitation F(t) applied on the transmitter side and is not modeled as a passive elastic element in the transmission chain. Each compliant connection is represented by a Kelvin–Voigt element, i.e., a spring and damper acting in parallel. Having defined the spectral residual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _V(f)$$\end{document}, we now describe how it is computed in our semi-analytical MSD model and in the experiment. First, we evaluate our MSD model using commercial numerical software, such as MATLAB (in-depth information on the state-space representation of the system in MATLAB can be found in Supplementary Equations S2-S7). The coupling parameters (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_i,c_i$$\end{document}) of the system are selected to reproduce the qualitative trends described in the experimental platform section and the Supplementary Table S2. After implementing the MSD model shown in Fig. 2b, we compute the sensors time responses \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ddot{x}(t)$$\end{document} at the accelerometers degrees of freedom (DOF) (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_{acTX,acRx}$$\end{document}) for the different conditions of the contact/object coupling parameters (see Supplementary Tables S3 and S4 for more details). Then, after performing the FFT on the simulated time signals, we obtain the spectra \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S^{(a),sim}_{Tx,Rx}$$\end{document} for the transmitter and receiver sides, which are used to calculate the spectral residual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _a(f)$$\end{document}. Examples of the obtained spectral responses are shown in Fig. 3a; additional examples are available in the Supplementary materials. It should be noted that our analytical MSD model was not fitted to reproduce the experimental spectral responses exactly, but rather to capture the key trends in vibration propagation and coupling mechanisms.

In the experiment, to obtain an accurate representation of the frequency response, the time evolution of the accelerometer output voltages is first captured over 50 trials. Then, the FFT spectra \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S^{(V)}_{Tx,Rx}(f)$$\end{document} are used to compute the spectral residual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _V(f)$$\end{document} (full spectral residuals can be seen in Supplementary Fig. S4-S15), averaged across trials for the same tested subject’s physical property. An additional moving average filter also highlights prominent features and suppresses noise arising from combining a large number of trials.

Band-integrated spectral residual features

Although the spectral residual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _{V}(f)$$\end{document} can indicate the frequency-dependent cross-finger coupling, its direct use for robust inference of the system’s properties is limited due to the high sensitivity to trial-to-trial variability, changes in excitation, and narrowband noise (from external vibration sources). Therefore, we define the band-integrated features by calculating the AUC and Kurtosis from acceleration/voltage spectral residuals:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AUC=\int _{B}\Delta _{V}(f)df$$\end{document}where B=[100,800]Hz is the full measured band, and\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Kurtosis=\frac{1}{N}\sum _{i=1}^{N}(\frac{\Delta _{V_i}-\mu ^{(\Delta _V)}}{\sigma ^{(\Delta _V)}})^4$$\end{document}where N is the number of trials, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu ^{(\Delta _V)}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma ^{(\Delta _V)}$$\end{document} are the mean and standard deviation of the spectral residual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _V$$\end{document}, respectively.

Moreover, we define peaks/troughs of the spectral residual and the corresponding frequencies:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PeakAmp=\max _{f\in B}\Delta _V(f),\quad PeakFreq =\arg \max _{f\in B}\Delta _V(f),$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MinAmp=\min _{f\in B}\Delta _V(f),\quad MinFreq=\arg \min _{f\in B}\Delta _V(f).$$\end{document}The AUC and Kurtosis features indicate how the broadband energy is redistributed between the transmitter and receiver, while the peak/min values quantify the dominant amplification/absorption and corresponding frequencies. Together with statistical analysis, they become powerful metrics for inferring the properties of the squeezed object and the contact information.

Statistical analysis

The features described in previous sections capture complementary characteristics of the spectral shape. The amplification peak and min frequencies and amplitudes define the location and strength of the dominant resonances/antiresonances in the spectral residual. The area under the curve, which is sensitive to the curve’s signed position relative to the x-axis, shows how the relative translated vibrational energy is distributed across different frequency bands. Kurtosis, as a measure of tailness, captures the overall shape of the spectral residual by reflecting the narrowness of the peaks and the prominence of sidebands.

For each investigated object state/property, a corresponding primary feature was selected. This assignment of a unique feature to a corresponding distinct object property bestows the system with the ability to sufficiently distinguish the effects of one property (e.g., elasticity) from the other (e.g., porosity). Such decoupling of features also tests the idea of specific changes in the object’s structure, resulting in traceable, explicit differences in spectral response. To select a feature that best represents the investigated physical parameter, multiple correlation tests (Pearson, Kendall, and Spearman) are performed to ensure adequate coverage of both linear and general monotonic dependencies (see Supplementary Fig. S16 and S17). Pearson correlation focuses on testing for the presence of a linear relationship between two variables, while Spearman and Kendall cover a broader spectrum of generally monotonic relationships42. Datasets of varying features and object properties are assessed on their respective normality with the Shapiro-Wilk test, showcasing a wide range of results with p-values ranging from 1.0 (a maximum value with p>0.05 being significantly normal distribution), down to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2.991\times 10^{-26}$$\end{document} (strong deviation from the normality), see more details in Supplementary Table S6. Therefore, we report both parametric (Pearson) and nonparametric (Spearman and Kendall) correlation analyses to ensure adequate coverage of possible relationships between an object property/state (e.g., elasticity, porosity, or grasping force) and a chosen frequency response variable (e.g., AUC, PeakFreq, etc.). To further confirm the significance of established correlations, we also apply the Kruskal-Wallis test (a nonparametric test for multiple groups), which is appropriate given that most of the data remain non-normal (see Supplementary Tables S7-S9 and Supplementary Fig. S19-S21). In the following, we use AUC-based features as our primary descriptors and employ peak-based and shape-based statistics as auxiliary features when needed.

Property inference: elasticity, porosity, and contact force

In this work, we propose constructing simple feature maps (or physically interpretable indices) from previously selected spectral-residual descriptors, rather than fitting a full MSD model to each trial. For each experimental observable (material, porosity level, and contact force), we compute the features per trial from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _V(f)$$\end{document} and summarize across repeated probes.

Elasticity correlated stiffness index. As previously mentioned, each material is assigned a nominal Young’s modulus E (values shown in Table 1). Since our datasets include both material changes and force levels, we assess stiffness-related features while accounting for force using partial correlation analysis (for the detailed results, see Supplementary Tables S2-S4). The analysis shows that PeakFreq remains strongly associated with E even after removing the effect of force and is stronger than the corresponding AUC correlations. We, therefore, can map the stiffness index by calibrating the Young’s modulus E as a monotonic function of PeakFreq. We report the resulting PeakFreq distributions and the corresponding stiffness index trends in the Results section (see Fig. 3).

Porosity index classification. To approximate the internal structure changes, particularly PLA cubes with different infill, we use the AUC and MinAmp. The more porous objects (lower infill) demonstrate more negative AUC and more pronounced absorption (more negative MinAmp), while denser infills shift AUC toward positive values and decrease MinAmp. For a quantitative comparison of different porosity levels, we relate these descriptors to the Bulk moduli shown in Table 2, and the resulting monotonic trends are shown in Fig. 4.

Contact Force extraction. Estimating the pressure force acting on different materials is more challenging than estimating intrinsic material properties such as elasticity or porosity, because force corresponds to an operating condition of the coupled finger–object system rather than to a fixed material parameter. In contrast to elasticity and porosity, which primarily reflect inherent object properties, grip force modifies the transmission state and therefore changes the measured frequency response in a material-dependent manner. In our reduced-order model, the force dependence is captured by allowing the effective object-transmission-path parameters to depend on the grip force \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_g$$\end{document}. Specifically, for each force load level, we treat the system as a linearized MSD model with force-dependent effective coupling: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{obj}=k_{obj}(F_g),$$\end{document} which, over the experimentally explored range, is approximated by a first-order dependence:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{obj}(F_g)=k_{obj,0}+\gamma F_g,$$\end{document}where γ are the empirical preload sensitivity parameters identified from the force-dependent experimental response. Thus, each force level is interpreted as a distinct linear operating point, with changes in grip force altering the effective stiffness and damping of the coupled transmission path, thereby modifying the measured transmissibility spectrum. Because this force dependence is also material-dependent, force estimation was performed conditionally within each material class or stiffness group identified from the elasticity analysis. To infer contact force levels and based on observed force-dependent trends, we use two descriptors: AUC and MinAmp, conditioned on each material class or stiffness group (defined from the elasticity test). For the fixed material, increasing force produces a distinct trajectory in the (AUC, MinAmp) plane (see Figs. 5,6). Therefore, in an investigation of frequency responses under varying forces, generating bivariate distributions of the best-suited parametric features provides a more comprehensive representation than a single correlation plot. The simulated features with the highest correlations were compared across the results of the Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall tests applied to the experimental data, selecting parametric features that exhibited strong mutual correlations in theoretical and practical scenarios and were graphically represented as bivariate distribution maps. By computing the median for each force level, we can build reference force maps that will allow inferring (or assigning to the closest reference force level) an unknown force level from values (AUC, MinAmp).

Force level

Starting with a group of relatively more rigid materials, the AUC, PeakFreq, and MinAmp features showed strong force correlations of 1.0. Interestingly, although the values for TPU and FLEX are 1.0, PLA shows a strong negative correlation (−1.0) between applied force and the aforementioned features (see Supplementary Fig. S17 for all correlation analysis results). A closer look at PLA suggests that the MSD model anticipates a pronounced antiresonance in the cross-finger transmissibility, indicating that the system’s antiresonant mode strengthens with increasing applied force and absorption. The experimental data partially follow this behavior: while MinAmp decreases with increasing force, PeakAmp shows a partial opposite trend. This signifies a growing involvement of the object in the amplification of the response from transmitter to emitter, while the decrease in MinAmp supports the semi-analytical trend of the increased system’s absorbance under strain. It’s also worth noting that although AUC showed a strong negative correlation in the analytical model, the measured data showed little to no correlation, with a maximum coefficient of 0.090 (Pearson). We attribute these major discrepancies between theory and experiment to their different modal structures (resonant and antiresonant modes), arising from the inability of the low-order MSD model to accurately represent all the complexities of a largely rigid (∼3GPa) 3D-printed PLA experimental object.

For TPU, the illustrated experimental features of MinAmp and AUC had corresponding correlation coefficients of 0.837 (Pearson) and 0.87 (Spearman), respectively, strongly supporting the theoretical predictions. While the experimental data appear less linearly confident around intermediate forces of 2-4N, the general pattern of a positive AUC slope and MinAmp indicates that amplification gains an advantage over absorption. Interestingly, the semi-analytical model even accurately captures the exponential decrease in both features, jumping from a 1-2N gap to a 4-5N gap, highlighting the object’s increasing stiffness and decreasing compliance under higher forces. The third parameter, PeakAmp, with a strong coefficient of 1.0 in the simulation, did not yield strong correlations in the experiment, reaching a maximum of −0.21 (Pearson), indicating no major changes in amplification patterns. A similar picture emerges for the FLEX, with strong positive correlations across the same features. Switching to more elastic materials, the frequency responses seem less predictable. In order to more efficiently infer the force-dependent behavior of materials with different elasticity, we extract the linear sensitivity coefficient γ in the force-dependent stiffness coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{obj}(F)=k_{obj,0}+\gamma F$$\end{document} from observed linear decrease in the silicone object’s dimension along the squeezing axis vs. the force applied along the same axis (see Supplementary Table S1). The obtained sensitivity parameters are used for the MSD model for the object’s stiffness at each force load level. Starting with SortaClear 40, both of the prominently correlated features, while showing a strong connection to applied pressure, exhibited polarly opposing trends in simulation and experiment. The experimental data also show less linear behavior in both features, peaking at around 2 N and decreasing thereafter. The theory suggests that both MinAmp and AUC approach zero as pressure increases, likely indicating that the material gradually loses its elastic nature and vibration-attenuating properties under high forces. Experimental results, on the other hand, demonstrate that as pressure increases, silicone 40 A enhances its absorption properties. The two remaining types of silicone, SortaClear 18 and 12, while demonstrating almost identical behavior of the chosen feature variables in simulation, yield ambiguous experimental results. In this regard, the only prominent behaviors present in both theoretical and practical cases are a considerably higher amplification (PeakAmp) and an AUC closer to zero as force increases. It is likely that simulation has steered away from the practice due to the increasingly complex viscoelastic nature of softer silicones.

The observed deviations between the low-order MSD model and the experiment are not uniform across materials and arise from distinct physical causes. For instance, in the rigid PLA specimen, the discrepancy is attributed mainly to an insufficient representation of the coupled system’s modal structure: because the object softness is small, the measured transmissibility becomes highly sensitive to distributed resonances and antiresonances of the gripper–object assembly, whereas the present MSD model captures only a limited number of effective modes. For soft silicone materials, the mismatch is more likely associated with preload-dependent contact impedance and frequency-dependent viscoelastic dissipation, which are approximated by effective Kelvin–Voigt couplings.

Discussion

From a vibration transmission perspective, the gripper–object–gripper system behaves as a coupled, force-dependent MSD network, whose frequency response is governed by effective coupling (stiffness and damping) and contact impedance. Because the object dimensions are much smaller than the characteristic elastic wavelength (deeply subwavelength regime), we interpret the object’s reshaping of the injected excitation via coupled resonances and attenuation pathways, rather than via the time-of-flight method. We observe two consistent physics trends that emerge from the semi-analytical analysis and experiments. First, softer objects introduce stronger viscoelastic losses and impedance mismatch, resulting in a residual spectrum dominated by absorption. Second, the increase in effective stiffness shifts the dominant resonances upward. The stiffer objects support higher coupled resonance frequencies (PeakFreq increases with increasing Young’s modulus), indicating that they can be used to estimate object elasticity (Supplementary Fig. S16 and S19).

When the objects have the same base material and external geometry but different internal structures, changes in response are due to reduced effective bulk moduli and increased internal damping, as indicated by AUC and absorption extrema. The case of the hollow cube, with distinct changes in the spectral residual and indicators of a thin-shell structure, can be used to detect structurally fragile objects before applying large forces.

Grip-force estimation is challenging due to strongly coupled finger dynamics and nonlinear contact impedance. Nevertheless, for a fixed material, the proposed approach exhibits repeatable, monotonic trajectories in the feature maps, confirming the possibility of inferring applied force levels and object properties. The discrepancies between the semi-analytical and experimental results point to the future improvements that can be introduced so that the model provides a more accurate representation of the real system. In particular, an extended attention would be required to improve the distributions of modes and internal object development under increased applied pressure. The major deviation in force estimation for the PLA material, in particular, shows that the model predicts a reduced amplification of the generated vibration, which might be more closely related to the representation of soft fingertip behavior under excessive rigid loads, rather than to a structural difference in the PLA object itself. This assumption of the dynamic elastic behavior also, in part, results in the differences observed in the force estimation of the silicone objects. In the context of practical application, real soft objects also include a variety of other physical properties besides elasticity that, in combination with it, contribute to the changes in an object’s vibrational response, e.g., roughness, density, brittleness, texture looseness, etc. Extending the MSD model in the future would increase the generalisability of the proposed approach, allowing the system to adjust its expectations and predictions to the new objects with the shared properties.

An additional advantage of the active differential approach is that the proposed system relies on the relative transmission between Tx and Rx, reducing sensitivity to absolute measurement values, including platform-specific differences (sensor sensitivity, assembly tolerances, etc.). This greatly increases the adaptability of the sensor for different robotic implementations.

For future work, it is important to extend the analysis beyond the cubic specimens used in this work. While the cube geometry was deliberately chosen to minimize geometric variability and improve inference of elasticity, porosity, and normal force levels, future experiments should examine how object shape, internal structure, and asymmetry affect transmissibility. It will demonstrate the generalization of the proposed sensing principle to less regular objects in robotic manipulation. Luckily, many objects in the world are semi-symmetric, or symmetric enough, where the slight differences in the shape wouldn’t have a considerable effect on a more general wave propagation pattern. In the following, we describe one potential application arising from the results of our study: how active vibration sensing can reveal a strawberry’s ripeness.

Application

Application in precision agriculture

Global population growth is placing increasing strain on the world’s food supply. Traditional farming practices can be inefficient in their use of irrigation water, occupation of arable land, and reliance on manual labor. One way to relieve the pressure is through precision agriculture, which uses technologies to perform various farming practices, such as watering and harvesting, as efficiently and precisely as possible. The proposed active tactile sensor is a promising candidate for precision agriculture applications. In fact, the most conspicuous advantage of active vibrotactile application is the ability to classify stiffness from subtle touch.

Previous works explore non-destructive tactile sensing methods for assessing ripeness of mangoes43, peaches44, avocados45, tomatoes and apples46. These approaches are usually passive, meaning they rely on mechanical indentation of fruit surfaces to enable usable sensing. This requirement can be problematic when handling delicate specimens because even small indentations may exceed the bruise threshold of overripe or small fruits, such as strawberries, and therefore lead to their rupture (Fig. 7c). One study used a commercial vision-based tactile sensor – DIGIT – mounted on a robotic gripper to estimate the ripeness of various berries (strawberries, blueberries, raspberries)47. By progressively squeezing a berry sample, the authors achieved a comprehensive characterization of the surface deformation. However, this passive sensing approach relied on mechanical deformation of a sample, which can be unsuitable for large-scale use. Conversely, vibration analysis can be used to assess the hardness of a fruit, which is closely correlated with its ripeness33, under a light touch32.

We applied the proposed sensing method to track strawberry ripeness, as it fits our setup and exhibits relatively pronounced changes in firmness during ripening. Thirty export-ready strawberries of the Allegro cultivar were monitored over four consecutive days as they ripened under room conditions. Each day, every strawberry was positioned between the two fingers of the setup and gently squeezed so that it was not deformed (the industrial force/torque (F/T) sensor has reported readings comparable to force sensor’s noise peak level 0.14 N, within which the F/T signal can be attributed to sensor noise). For each berry and day, we recorded 50 trials of transmitted and received vibration signals under swept-sine excitation over the 100–800 Hz band, and retrieved the feature set from the trial-averaged residual spectra, as in the main experiments.

Across the 120 repeated observations from 30 strawberry samples measured over four days, Kurtosis showed the strongest negative correlation with storage day, with a coefficient of −0.716 (Pearson) (Table 5, and Supplementary Fig. S18 for additional information). As the berry ripens, its internal structure becomes more viscous and dissipative, redistributing energy across the bands and altering the overall spectral shape. Kurtosis is particularly sensitive to these changes in tailedness and peakedness and, thus, can capture these signatures of stiffness shifts due to ripening. Embodied tactile perception in human-robot interaction

Embodied tactile perception explains how an agent can infer material properties through touch, a process fundamentally shaped by the agent’s own physical structure. In this view, perception is inseparable from action – active tactile sensing is essential to embodied perception as the tactile information is acquired through deliberate exploratory procedures (e.g., sliding, tapping, squeezing, or injecting vibrations) that amplify informative cues.

Most conventional tactile sensors – such as capacitive, force-torque, or magnetic – primarily report only local contact conditions. To extend perception beyond a single contact site, researchers design electronic skins with dense sensor arrays distributed across multiple body parts (fingers, palms, arms, torso, etc.), thereby increasing spatial resolution. In contrast, active vibro-tactile sensing generates controlled signals that can be captured not only at the point of contact but also expressed throughout the coupled finger-hand-arm (or finger-link-gripper) structure as they propagate through the body and distal links. Serendipitously, human perception studies indicate that the loss of distal cutaneous sensation – whether due to injury or anesthesia – has a negligible impact on roughness discrimination during sliding across the surface49. This persistence in tactile sensitivity suggests that mechanical vibrations are not confined to the fingertip but propagate proximally through the limb, reaching as far as the forearm50. Consequently, these signals activate deep-seated mechanoreceptor populations situated remotely from the initial point of contact51. Since an object’s elasticity and porosity shape the force-dependent state of the coupled body–object system, active vibro-tactile exploration (implemented as injected vibrations) yields characteristic signatures that can be detected beyond the fingertip along the distal links. Motivated by this principle, our proposed model treats the coupled gripper-finger-object mechanics as a part of the sensing process: injected vibrations are reshaped by the finger and object structure as well as the force-dependent contact state – this reshaping is observable on the transmitting and receiving fingertip measurements (Fig. 2 b). Extending this sensing to distal links is a natural next step towards embodied, whole-body tactile perception, where contact may occur away from the fingertips or be mediated through the grasped object.

The distributed sensing perspective is especially important in HRI, where detecting direct physical contact with a human is essential. In real interactions, the contact may occur anywhere on the robot (not just at the fingertips), and is often mediated through a grasped object during handovers or co-manipulation. In such cases, the propagated vibro-tactile cues can provide whole-body contact awareness for safety and richer interpretation of the information – including the detection and localization of contact through held objects, prediction of grasp stability during handover, and discrimination of contact types (sliding, tapping, pushing, etc.)23. Furthermore, unlike industrial force-torque or capacitive sensors, which rely on absolute measurements and can be sensitive to ambient conditions, active vibrotactile sensing can provide robust signatures with a high signal-to-noise ratio32, supporting reliable contact inference during unpredictable HRI events.

Conclusion

We applied the active vibro-tactile probing and modal (frequency-domain) analysis to quantify the mechanical response of soft and rigid objects in the sub-wavelength gripper-object-gripper system. In both simulations and experiments, we observed two robust physical descriptors: strong absorption and a PeakFreq shift in compliant materials, and dominant band shifts to higher frequencies in stiffer materials. These trends confirmed that active vibration sensing enables material characterization, ushering in applications in precision agriculture and HRI with a gentle touch.

Experimentally, we evaluated the object features of rigid-to-elastic materials made of PLA with 100%, 80%, 60%, 40%, 20%, and hollow infill, and of TPU, FLEX, Silicone40, Silicone18, Silicone12, and soft objects with rigid inclusions. These findings are relevant to the design of tactile sensors and haptic interfaces, which require a holistic understanding of the bulk mechanical properties of the contacting extremities.

We address the challenge of hardware heterogeneity: whereas absolute measurements demand tuning for every sensor, our differential sensing strategy makes the system sensor-agnostic. This robustness to manufacturing tolerances is essential for scalable deployment across diverse robotic environments, as, for example, in grasping ripe strawberries shown in the Application in Precision Agriculture.

The implications of this work go beyond advances in the fundamental physics of matter. By enhancing tactile perception through induced vibration in a controllable, macroscopic model system, the research provides an analog tool for exploring similar phenomena in other fields of science. Notably, the study offers novel insights into the human somatosensory system. This is because, in a manner analogous to haptics, it allows rendering the material-specific transfer function of complex structures.
