Prevalence of sensory impairments and associations with symptoms of anxiety and depression in US older adults
Authors: Chunsu Zhu, Shengmei Li, Zhiwei Lian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSensory impairments are common in older adults and may contribute to mental health problems. However, nationally representative data on sensory impairments, especially objective measured impairments, and their associations with emotional symptoms remain limited. This study aims to evaluate the age-specific prevalence of sensory impairments and investigate their short-term prospective associations with depression and anxiety among older adults living in the United States.MethodsData were from the round 12 (2022) and 13 (2023) of the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS), a nationally representative survey of Medicare beneficiaries ≥ 65 years in the United States. We included 5,020 participants with objective vision/hearing tests and mental health questionnaires. Age-specific prevalence of objective and self-reported sensory impairments was calculated. Cox proportional hazards regression models, accounting for the complex survey design, were used to evaluate the short-term prospective associations between sensory impairments and depression and anxiety symptoms, measured via Patient Health Questionnaire-4.ResultsOf the 5,020 participants (weighted: 54.8% female; 79.3% White), the prevalence of objectively measured vision impairment (VI), hearing impairment (HI), and dual sensory impairment (DSI) was 30.9%, 20.9%, and 8.6%, respectively. However, the self-reported prevalence was substantially lower, with only 7.1%, 16.8% and 1.5% reporting VI, HI and DSI, respectively. Longitudinal analyses included 4,453 participants without baseline depressive symptoms and 4,519 without baseline anxiety. Compared with those without impairments, objective VI alone (adjusted hazards ratio[aHR], 2.29; 95% CI, 1.41–3.72), HI alone (aHR, 2.14; 95% CI, 1.24–3.68) and DSI (aHR, 2.40; 95% CI, 1.17–4.93) were significantly associated with an increased risk of anxiety symptoms over a median follow-up of 10.1 months. No significant associations were observed for depressive symptoms.ConclusionsObjectively measured sensory impairments were highly prevalent among US older adults and were associated with an increased risk of anxiety, but not depressive symptoms over a short follow-up duration. Self-reports considerably underestimated true prevalence of mild- to- moderate impairment, emphasizing the need for routine objective assessment in clinical practice and research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-08019-z.Keywords: Vision impairment, Hearing impairment, Depressive symptoms, Anxiety symptoms, Prospective study

Introduction

Both vision and hearing impairment (VI and HI) are highly prevalent among older adults and are major contributors to disability [1], reduced quality of life [2], and the global burden of chronic conditions [3, 4]. Globally, approximately 5.5% of the population is estimated to live with combined vision and hearing impairment, with the prevalence increasing with age and projected to rise by 27.2% by 2050 [3]. Although often treatable, these impairments can lead to substantial functional decline and psychosocial issues when left unaddressed [5]. While previous studies have estimated the prevalence of VI and HI [6, 7], most have relied on self-reported measures [8], which may underestimate the true burden of sensory impairment. For instance, previous studies have reported that self-reported hearing impairment varies by sociodemographic characteristics and may underestimate audiometrically measured hearing loss in older adults [9]. In addition, few studies have reported the objectively measured prevalence of dual sensory impairment (DSI) [3], despite its potential to exert a compounded impact on physical and mental well-being. Self-reported assessments of sensory function may have limited concordance with objective evaluations [10] and may underestimate its contribution to health burden. This is particularly relevant as mild-to-moderated impairments, although less likely to be self-reported, may still contribute to psychological distress. These highlight the need for more robust, performance-based estimates to better identify at-risk populations and inform intervention strategies.

Mental health, including depression and anxiety, is another critical determinant of well-being in older adults [11]. Emerging evidence suggests that individuals with VI or HI are more likely to experience poorer mental health, including increased psychosocial difficulties [12, 13]. Sensory impairments may affect mental health through multiple pathways, such as reduced communication, mobility limitations, loss of independence, decreased confidence, and social withdrawal [14]. Compared with single sensory impairment, DSI may impose a greater psychological burden because the combined loss of vision and hearing can further restrict social interaction, daily functioning, and access to environmental information [15]. However, prior studies on the associations between sensory impairment and mental health have been limited by cross-sectional design [16], selected populations (e.g., cancer patients) [17], or reliance on self-reported sensory assessments [18, 19], resulting in mixed results [19, 20]. Evidence based on objectively measured VI, HI, and especially DSI in nationally representative older populations remains limited.

We used data from the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS), a nationally representative survey of Medicare beneficiaries aged ≥ 65 in the United States, to: 1) investigate the age-specific prevalence of both objectively measured and self-reported sensory impairments and 2) examine their short-term prospective associations with incident depressive symptoms and anxiety. Because self-reported and objective assessments may capture different aspects and severity thresholds of sensory impairment, we examined both measures to better characterize potential discrepancies in prevalence estimates and to explore whether their associations with mental health outcomes differ by assessment method.

Methods

Study population

NHATS is an ongoing, nationally representative annual survey of US Medicare beneficiaries aged ≥ 65 years. The detailed design of this project is provided elsewhere [21]. This study used data from rounds 12 (2022) and 13 (2023), as objective assessments for visual and hearing function were not conducted until 2021, with 2022 wave incorporating a sample replenishment. Interviews were performed in person or by phone, while objective tests for visual and hearing function were conducted in person at participants’ home. Of the 6,327 individuals surveyed in 2022, 5,044 completed both vision and hearing tests. After excluding those with missing data on symptoms of depression and anxiety, the final analytic sample included 5,020 participants.

Vision measures

The NHATS introduced three tablet-based tests by Ridgevue to assess presenting binocular vision with habitual correction: distance visual acuity, near visual acuity and contrast sensitivity (CS). These tests have demonstrated good validity compared to clinical gold standards (ETDRS, MNRead, and Pelli-Robson) [22, 23]. For distance acuity, participants sat five feet from the tablet and read five letters per screen, with letter size decreasing progressively. A score was calculated based on correctly identified letters, adjusting for reading distance and reported as the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (log MAR) [23]. For near acuity, participants held the tablet at their usual reading distance and followed a similar procedure. Distance and near VI were defined as log MAR > 0.30 (20/40) [24]. For CS, participants also sat five feet away and read two letters per screen as contrast gradually decreased. Testing ended after 16 screens or if no letter was correctly identified. The results were recorded as log CS, with CS impairment (CSI) defined as log CS < 1.55 [24, 25]. Any objective VI was defined as impairment in at least one of the three tests.

Self-reported vision status was assessed prior to objective testing through self- or proxy-report. Participants were defined as having VI if they reported being blind or, even with glasses, were unable to: (1) recognize someone across the street or watch television across the room (self-reported distance VI), and/or (2) read newspaper print (self-reported near VI).

Hearing measures

Participants were offered pure-tone air conduction audiometric testing using a tablet-based portable audiometer (SHOEBOX Ltd) [7, 23]. The pure tone averages (PTA) for both right and left ears were recorded by the interviewers. We classified participants into two groups based on the PTA of the better ear: no/mild HI (PTA, ≤ 40 dB), and moderate or worse HI (PTA, ≥ 41dB), per the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations [26].

Participants were classified as having self-reported HI if they reported being deaf, using a hearing aid, or being unable to hear well enough to use the telephone or hold a conversation in a room with background noise (e.g., a television or radio playing).

DSI

Participants were categorized into four mutually exclusive groups based on their sensory impairment status at baseline: no sensory impairment, only VI, only HI, and DSI.

Depressive and anxiety symptoms

The Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4), a validated screening tool for depression and anxiety, was administered annually. It consists of two subscales: the PHQ-2 for depression and the generalized anxiety disorder-2 (GAD-2), each comprising two items [27, 28]. Depressive symptoms were measured by asking how often a person experienced “little interests or pleasure in doing things” and “feeling down, depressed, or hopeless” during the past month. Answers were recorded on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 6. A cut-off of ≥ 3 indicated clinically significant depressive symptoms, with a sensitivity of 0.87 and specificity of 0.78 for major depressive disorder, and a sensitivity of 0.79 and specificity of 0.86 for any type of depressive disorder [29]. Anxiety was assessed by asking how often participants “felt nervous, anxious, or on edge” and “were unable to stop or control worrying” in the last month. Responses were recorded using the same 4-point Likert scale, and a total score ≥ 3 indicated clinically significant anxiety symptoms. This threshold has a sensitivity of 0.76 and a specificity of 0.81 for generalized anxiety disorder [30], and also performed well in detecting social anxiety, panic disorder and posttraumatic stress disorder [28].

Covariates

Demographic characteristics included age (both continuous and categorized), sex, education (below college/college and above), marital status (married or living with a partner/separated, divorced, widowed, or never married), and race (non-Hispanic white/non-Hispanic black/others non-Hispanic/ Hispanic). Lifestyle related-factors included smoking status (never/ current or past), vigorous exercise (yes/no), and obesity (body mass index≥30 kg/m2). The number of self-reported chronic medical conditions was also included (containing heart attack, heart disease, high blood pressure, arthritis, osteoporosis, diabetes, lung disease, cancer, and stroke). Survey design factors included a dichotomous indicator of respondents.

Statistical analysis

All analyses accounted for the complex design of NHATS by incorporating NHATS sampling weights. Covariates with missing values were imputed using Monte Carlo Markov Chains multiple imputations based on five repetitions.

To estimate the age-specific prevalence of sensory impairment among older adults, we calculated the weighted prevalence and distribution of both objectively measured and self-reported VI and HI, stratified by age group. We also described the weighted distribution of baseline characteristics according to objective DSI status. To estimate the short-term prospective associations between concurrent VI and HI and depressive and anxiety symptoms, we fitted multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models, using those with no sensory impairment as the reference group. We selected Cox proportional hazards models to account for variation in follow-up time between baseline and follow-up assessments. Because the exact onset dates of depressive symptoms and anxiety were unavailable, the follow-up interview date was used as a proxy for event date. Follow-up time was calculated from baseline interview date to the follow-up date and expressed in months. Two models were specified: model 1 was adjusted for demographic characteristics, model 2 was further adjusted for lifestyle-related factors, the number of chronic medical conditions, proxy respondents, and baseline depressive (for anxiety analyses) or baseline anxiety symptoms (for depressive symptoms analyses). These covariates were included as potential confounders reflecting baseline health status, lifestyle, and psychological condition. Participants with the outcome of interest at baseline were regarded as prevalent cases and were excluded from the corresponding analysis. Individuals who did not experience the outcome event during the follow-up, were lost to follow-up or died were classified as censored cases. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residual methods, and no evidence of violation was detected (p = 0.253). Additionally, we investigated the associations of each type of sensory impairment (objective and self-reported VI and HI) with symptoms of depression and anxiety. To test the robustness of this study, we reran our analyses under the following conditions: 1) excluding participants aged 90 years or more; 2) excluding data provided by proxies. All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4, with statistical significance defined as a two-sided p < 0.05.

Results

We included 5,020 participants, representing approximately 45.2 million older adults nationwide. Weighted estimates showed that 54.8% were female, 79.3% identified as White, 8.8% as Black, and 7.8% as Hispanic. Objective assessments indicated that 30.9% had VI and 20.9% had HI (eTable 1). In contrast, self-reported rates were lower, with only 7.1% reporting VI and 16.8% reporting HI. The prevalence of both impairments increased with age. The prevalence of any objective VI was 23.7% (95% CI, 19.1%-28.4%) in the group aged 65–69 years, increasing to 65.2% (95% CI, 60.5%-69.9%) in those aged ≥ 90 years. Similarly, the prevalence of objective HI rose from 7.0% (95% CI, 4.4%-9.6%) in the 65–69 age group to 75.5% (95% CI, 70.9–80.2%) among those aged 90 or older. Self-reported sensory impairment also increased with age but was consistently lower than objectively measured results.

Among objectively measured impairments, 22.3% (95% CI, 20.8%-23.9%) of participants have VI only, 12.3% (95% CI, 10.9%-13.7%) have HI only and 8.6% (95% CI, 7.6%-9.7%) have DSI (Fig. 1A). The prevalence of HI only and DSI increased with age. Specifically, the prevalence of HI only was 5.1% (95% CI, 2.7%-7.6%) in the group aged 65–69 years and rose to 27.6% (95% CI, 23.2%-32.1%) in the group aged ≥ 90 years. The prevalence of DSI increased from 1.9% (95% CI, 0.8%-2.9%) to 47.9% (95% CI, 42.5%-53.3%) across the same age groups.

In self-reported measures, 5.5% (95% CI, 4.6–6.5%) of individuals reported VI only, 15.3% (95% CI, 13.7–16.8) reported HI only, and 1.5% (95% CI, 1.2%-1.9%) reported DSI, the prevalence of HI only and DSI were also increased with age (Fig. 1B). The prevalence of self-reported HI only increased from 7.5% (95% CI, 4.9%-10.1%) among participants aged 65–69 years to 37.6% (95% CI, 32.3%-43.0%) among those aged ≥ 90 years. The prevalence of self-reported DSI was below 1% among participants aged 65–69 years but increased to 6.5% (95% CI, 4.0%-9.0%) among those aged ≥ 90 years.

Compared to individuals without sensory impairment (Table 1), those with VI only, HI only or DSI tend to have lower education, be widowed/separated/never married, and have a higher prevalence of chronic conditions: heart attack (no impairment: 9.6%[95% CI,8.5%-10.6%)] vs. DSI: 18.5%[95% CI,14.8%-22.3%]), heart disease (14.5%[95% CI,13.0%-16.1%] vs. 27.3%[95% CI,21.6%-32.9%]), high blood pressure (62.7%[95% CI,59.8%-65.6%] vs. 73.05%[95% CI,67.8%-78.2%]), diabetes (22.4%[95% CI,20.1%-24.6%] vs. 34.7%[95% CI,29.6%-39.9]), stroke (7.4%[95% CI,6.4%-8.5] vs. 16.2%[95% CI,11.65–20.8%]), dementia (3.5%[95% CI,2.9%-4.2%] vs. 11.0%[95% CI,8.1%-13.9%]), symptoms of depression (8.4%[95% CI,6.8%-9.9%] vs. 18.0%[95% CI,13.8%-22.3%]) and proxy respondent use (0.2%[95% CI,0.01%-0.3%) vs. 4.2%[95% CI,2.0%-6.4%]).

A total of 4,453 participants without depressive symptoms at baseline and 4,519 without anxiety were included in the short-term prospective analyses. Over a median follow-up of 10.1 months (interquartile range: 8.2–11.7 months), 294 participants developed incident depressive symptoms and 233 participants developed incident anxiety (eTable 2). The objective VI alone (adjusted hazards ratio[aHR], 2.29; 95% CI, 1.41–3.72), objective HI alone (aHR, 2.14; 95% CI, 1.24–3.68) and objective DSI (aHR, 2.40; 95% CI, 1.17–4.93) were associated with an increased risk of anxiety symptoms, after adjusting for a wide range of covariates, including baseline depressive symptoms (Table 2). In contrast, no significant associations were detected between objective DSI and incident depressive symptoms. Among self-reported sensory impairments, only DSI remained significantly associated with anxiety after adjustment (aHR, 3.57; 95% CI, 1.01–12.67), although this estimate was imprecise, as reflected by the wide confidence interval (Table 2).

Compared to participants without impairment, any objective VI was significantly associated with an increased risk of anxiety symptoms (aHR: 1.90; 95% CI, 1.28–2.83) in full adjustment model (eTable 3). However, the association between any objective VI and depressive symptoms was attenuated and became marginal after full adjustment. All self-reported sensory impairments were not significantly associated with symptoms of depression or anxiety.

Sensitivity analyses (eTable S4-S5) yielded similar results. However, in a sensitivity analysis excluding participants aged ≥ 90 years, any objective VI (aHR:1.40; 95% CI, 1.00-1.96, eTable S4) and objective DSI (aHR: 1.66; 95% CI, 1.03–2.67, eTable S5) were significantly associated with an increased likelihood of depressive symptoms.

Discussion

In this nationally representative cohort of older US adults, we found that 30.9% had objective VI, 20.9% had objective HI, and 8.6% had DSI. The self-reported prevalence of sensory impairments was substantially lower, with only 7.1%, 16.8% and 1.5% reporting VI, HI and DSI, respectively. Among individuals without anxiety at baseline, objective sensory impairments were significantly associated with an increased risk of anxiety symptoms. The strongest association was observed among those with DSI, who had a 2.40-fold higher risk compared to those without impairment. Although a similar trend was observed for depressive symptoms, with the highest risk in the DSI group, this association did not reach statistical significance after full adjustment.

Building on prior nationally representative studies that have incorporated of objective vision and hearing impairment [31], this study presents contemporary prevalence estimates of objectively measured and self-reported sensory impairments in older US adults and evaluate their short-term prospective associations with mental health. We found that over one-fifth of participants had HI, nearly one-third had VI, and approximately one in ten had DSI. These prevalences increased with age, consistent with prior studies [8, 31]. Data from the National Health and Nutritional Examination Surveys (NHANES) reported an age-related increase in prevalence, with a DSI prevalence of 11.3% among those aged ≥ 80 years [31], closely aligning with our findings in the 80–84 age group. Importantly, our study also revealed a substantial discrepancy between objectively measured and self-reported impairments. Self-reported sensory impairments consistently underestimated the prevalence of visual and dual impairments. While self-reports and objective measures of HI appeared similar in those under 75 years, self-reports significantly underestimated HI in those aged ≥ 75 years. This age pattern is consistent with prior studies showing that self-reported hearing trouble may underestimate audiometrically measured hearing loss in older age groups [9]. These discrepancies underscore the limitations of relying solely on self-reported data and highlight the importance of incorporating objective assessment methods into both research and clinical practices to avoid underdiagnosis and ensure appropriate intervention.

Despite the high prevalence of sensory impairments in older adults, there has been limited research examining the associations between objective sensory impairments, particularly the objective DSI, and symptoms of depression and anxiety. In our study, both objectively and subjectively measured DSI were associated with an increased risk of anxiety, consistent with prior studies [16, 17, 20]. However, we did not observe significant associations between sensory impairments and depressive symptoms, which contrasts with several previous studies [32, 33]. For example. A cohort study in China reported a 1.27-fold increased risk of depressive symptoms associated with self-reported DSI over four years [34], while the Health Aging and Body Composition (HABC) study found an association with chronic but not transient depression [20]. In contrast, findings from the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSA) suggested that the link between DSI and depressive symptoms disappeared after the adjustment of health indicators [35]. In our sensitivity analysis excluding participants aged ≥ 90 years, objective DSI was significantly associated with depressive symptoms. This may reflect reduced measurement variability and increased statistical precision in a more homogeneous subgroup, as the oldest individuals often experienced greater variability in both sensory and depression measurements (e.g., due to cognitive decline, reporting bias, survivor effects) [36]. However, other explanations are also possible. Participants aged ≥ 90 years represent a highly selected survivor population, and survivor bias may have reduced the association because individuals most vulnerable to the adverse psychological effects of sensory impairments had already died or were less likely to remain in the study. Additionally, higher competing mortality in this age group may have attenuated the likelihood of observing incident depressive symptoms. Moreover, the absence of a significant association for depressive symptoms in the full sample could also be partly attributed to the short follow-up duration and limited number of depressive symptoms events, which reduced the statistical power to detect associations. It is also possible that sensory impairments exert differential effects on mental health over time. In the short term, the onset or progression of sensory impairments may heighten anxiety due to immediate functional limitations, uncertainty and reduced social participation. Over longer durations, however, the cumulative burden of sensory loss, such as persistent social isolation, may contribute more strongly to depressive symptoms. Our relatively short follow-up may therefore have captured primarily the early phase of this trajectory, in which anxiety responses may be more apparent than depressive symptoms. Future investigations with longer follow-up are warranted to further clarify these temporal dynamics.

The mechanisms underlying the associations between sensory impairments and symptoms of depression and anxiety were not directly examined in the current study but are likely multifactorial. Sensory impairments may reduce communication, mobility, and social participation [37], thereby increasing social isolation [38], and diminishing social support [39], which are established correlates of emotional distress. Sensory impairments may also contribute to reduced physical activity [40], which may further affect mental health. Although we adjusted for vigorous physical activity in our analyses, the results remained similar. In addition, participants with DSI in our study had a higher burden of chronic conditions, which may compound psychological burden. Furthermore, shared biological pathways, such as systemic inflammation and microvascular changes, may also contribute to both sensory decline and affective symptoms [41, 42]. Future studies with repeated assessments of potential mediators are needed to clarify the mechanisms liking these associations.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s strengths include its nationally representative sample and use of objectively measured sensory impairments. It also has several important implications for clinical practice, policy and future research in the context of healthy aging and longevity. First, the high prevalence of objectively measured sensory impairments, coupled with the considerable under-recognition of visual and dual impairments, highlights the need for routine objective assessments, rather than reliance on self-reports in clinical practice and research settings. Second, the observed association between sensory impairments and emotional symptoms underscores the importance of integrated care models that combine sensory health with mental health service. Improving access to timely vision and hearing care, as well as assistive technologies, may not only enhance functional independence but also reduce the psychological burden faced by older adults. Finally, longer-term longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the causal pathways and potential modifiers of the relationship between sensory impairments and mental health outcomes. Such evidence will inform preventative strategies of care that target sensory health as a modifiable risk factor, ultimately contributing to the promotion of healthy aging and the extension of healthy lifespan.

This study also has several limitations. First, while the PHQ-2 and GAD-2 are validated tools with high sensitivity and specificity for detecting clinical depression and anxiety disorders [29, 30], they do not provide clinical diagnoses. Second, the relatively short follow-up period limited our ability to assess the long-term or delayed effects of sensory impairments on mental health. Although Cox models accounted for differences in follow-up time, the findings should be interpreted as short-term prospective associations rather than long-term risk estimates. Nevertheless, the short follow-up may have captured more immediate psychological responses to sensory impairments and reduced confounding from unrelated life events over time. In addition, some covariates included in the fully adjusted models, such as physical activity, chronic conditions, and baseline emotional symptoms, may lie on the causal pathway between sensory impairment and mental health outcomes. Therefore, adjustment for these variables may have attenuated the observed associations because of overadjustment, which may partly explain the non-significant associations observed for depressive symptoms. Third, the discrepancy between self-reported and objectively measured sensory impairments may arise because objective testing can detect mild-to-moderate impairments that may not yet be perceived or reported by participants. However, the differences may also partly reflect methodological differences in impairment definitions and severity thresholds between self-reported and objective assessments. Fourth, participants who died during the follow-up were censored in the Cox models. Since sensory impairments may be associated with mortality, this approach could introduce informative censoring and potentially overestimate the associations if death acts as a competing event. However, given the very small number of deaths and the short follow-up duration, the potential influence of competing mortality on the main estimates was likely limited, although it cannot be completely excluded. Fifth, our results are generalizable to Medicare population, which includes approximately 98.9% of older adults in the US in 2022 [43], but may not extend to those outside this system. Finally, despite adjusting for a broad range of covariates, residual confounding cannot be fully eliminated.

Conclusion

In this nationally representative sample of US older adults, objectively measured VI, HI and DSI were common in older adults. Older adults with sensory impairments, particularly DSI, were at increased risk for incident anxiety during short-term follow-up. Self-reported sensory impairments significantly underestimated the true prevalence of mild- to- moderate impairment, highlighting the need for routine objective assessments.
