Somatic DICER1 pathogenic variants detected in a well-differentiated fetal lung adenocarcinoma diagnosed in a man with familial adenomatous polyposis
Authors: Afrida Ahmed, Paul S Thorner, Anne-Laure Chong, Naciba Benlimame, David Szlachtycz, George Chong, Sophie Camilleri-Broët, William D Foulkes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWell-differentiated fetal lung adenocarcinoma (WDFLAC) is a rare pulmonary carcinoma characterized by its resemblance to fetal lung. It is thought to be caused by somatic pathogenic variants (PVs) in the genes CTNNB1 or APC, which lead to the nuclear accumulation of beta(β)-catenin and down-stream activation of key oncogenes. Recent studies, however, have shown that germline and/or somatic PVs in DICER1 are also implicated in the etiology of lung tumors with fetal-type morphologies. Here, we report the second case of WDFLAC in a male non-smoker with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), and the first case with confirmed inactivating PVs in both APC and DICER1. A review of the literature also showed that out of the 17 WDFLAC cases for which sequencing of DICER1, CTNNB1 and/or APC was performed, 13 had PVs in DICER1 and either CTNNB1 or APC, with one patient having all three genes mutated. Taken together, this suggests that the development of WDFLAC may be the summative effect of WNT and DICER1 dysfunction. It may thus be beneficial to test for DICER1 variants in diagnostically challenging cases and, if a germline DICER1 PV is found, additional screening for DICER1-associated tumors could be pursued.

Background

Well-differentiated fetal lung adenocarcinoma (WDFLAC) is a rare type of pulmonary malignancy known for its resemblance to fetal lung at ten to fifteen weeks’ gestational age [1]. In 2002, Nakatani et al [1]. postulated, based on nuclear accumulation of β-catenin in this tumor, that continued upregulation of the wingless-type MMTV integration site family (WNT) signaling pathway played a role in WDFLAC tumorigenesis. Nuclear expression of β-catenin is known to cause the activation of key oncogenes such as MYC and Cyclin D1 [2].

Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) is an autosomal dominant disease caused by deleterious mutations in the adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) gene [2]. In the canonical WNT pathway, APC binds β-catenin and recruits it to the β-catenin destruction complex, leading to the protein’s degradation [3]. Individuals with FAP are typically heterozygous for APC predicted truncating pathogenic variants (PVs), which, when accompanied by a “second hit” on the wild-type allele, leads to the development of adenomatous colorectal polyps at an early age, in turn conferring risk of early-onset colorectal cancer (CRC) [2]. Though FAP contributes to <1% of CRC cases, somatic APC PVs are found in >80% of colorectal tumors [3]. FAP also increases the risk of desmoid tumors, thyroid carcinoma and hepatoblastoma [2], but rarely is it implicated in lung cancer. However, in 1995, WDFLAC was reported in a single patient with FAP [4].

Over a decade after Nakatani et al.’s finding, our group [5] discovered an association between WDFLAC and DICER1, a gene involved in other types of rare lung tumors associated with an embryonic lung phenotype [6]. We now report here a second case of WDFLAC found in a young male non-smoker with FAP, and the first with molecular analysis that confirms the role of pathogenic variants in both APC and DICER1. We also review the existing literature on the molecular genetic alterations found in WDFLAC.

Case presentation

Prior to the patient’s lung cancer diagnosis, he was being followed in the Gastroenterology service for his FAP. The patient had a strong family history on his maternal side of polyposis, as well as young-onset colorectal cancer (Fig. 1a), which led to his genetic testing at age 11. He was found to carry the familial APC PV NM_0.000038.5: c.3379 C > T (p.Q1127*). After his first colonoscopy the following year revealed the presence of 21 polyps, he underwent a proctocolectomy and J-pouch reconstruction at age 13. Post-operatively, the patient had semi-regular duodenoscopies and pouchoscopies, several of which resulted in the removal of tubular adenomas. He had been otherwise stable until age 26, when he presented to the emergency room with left chest and shoulder pain. This was accompanied by dyspnea and a persistent cough, still present after finishing a round of antibiotics for left upper-lobe pneumonia. A PET-CT scan found an FDG-avid lung mass, and a subsequent biopsy revealed a cancer. Following a left upper lobectomy and further histopathological examination, the tumor was officially diagnosed as a pT3N0 WDFLAC. The patient received one cycle of cisplatin (161 mg or 75 mg/m2) & pemetrexed (1075 mg or 500 mg/m2) prior to self-discontinuing due to adverse symptoms. He has been in remission to date.

Materials and methods

Immunohistochemical testing

To determine if expression of the mismatch repair (MMR) proteins was retained in the WDFLAC, immunohistochemical staining for the four proteins was carried out using the U Optiview DAB IHC Kit v6 and its related protocols on the Benchmark ULTRA IHC/ISH instrument. The following antibodies were used: 1:30 diluted mouse monoclonal anti-MLH1 (Clone G168-5, BIOCARE), pre-diluted mouse monoclonal anti-MSH2 (Clone G219-1129, ROCHE), pre-diluted rabbit monoclonal anti-MSH6 (Clone SP93, ROCHE) and pre-diluted rabbit monoclonal anti-PMS2 (Clone EP51, DAKO). The slides were incubated with the proteins for 60, 8, 12 and 40 min, respectively.

The β-catenin IHC was performed as described in [6] with a prediluted mouse monoclonal anti- β-catenin (Clone 14, ROCHE) on the normal lung FFPE and tumoral lung FFPE sample.

Clinical molecular testing

DNA and RNA were extracted from a formalin-fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) block of WDFLAC and libraries were prepared for sequencing in the AmpliSeq 52-gene Solid Tumor Illumina Focus Panel at a local molecular diagnostics laboratory, using standard clincal-grade methods and analytical tools. Briefly, the test is an amplicon-based targeted resequencing assay designed to detect clinically relevant somatic DNA and RNA alterations in 52 genes (full list in Supp. Note 1). RNA and DNA samples were tested in parallel, and paired-end massively parallel sequencing of 150-bp fragments was performed using an Illumina MiSeq. The CTNNB1 DNA sequence was aligned and compared to the reference genome GRCh37 to identify single-nucleotide variants and insertion-deletions. The results were validated as per recommended guidelines [7] and categorized based on their clinical impact [8].

As neither DICER1 nor APC were included in the clinical panel, DNA from the patient’s blood lymphocytes, normal lung FFPE tissue and tumoral lung FFPE tissue were newly extracted as described previously [5] and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq to look for variants in both genes. The Roche KAPA HyperPlus hybrid capture-based protocol was used to enrich for targeted regions. The following transcripts were used in this analysis: DICER1 (NM_030621.3) and APC (NM_0.000038.5).

The loss of heterozygosity in APC was confirmed by analyzing all of the patient’s germline variants in chromosome 5 in DNA extracted from blood lymphocytes and normal lung FFPE tissue and then comparing their allelic frequencies (AFs) to the variants present in the DNA of the FFPE WDFLAC.

Literature review

To strengthen our argument that mutations in DICER1 and either APC or CTNNB1 lead to the development of this tumor, we conducted a literature review including English language peer-reviewed reports of all WDFLACs tested for DICER1 and CTNNB1 and/or APC. To find the articles, we searched PubMed and Google Scholar using keywords “WDFLAC” and “low-grade fetal lung adenocarcinoma”. For one article, the whole exome sequencing (WES) results were validated using the enrichment analysis tool on the STRING database [9].

Molecular testing

The Ampliseq Solid Tumor Panel revealed that no pathogenic variants of were found in the WDFLAC, including for CTNNB1.

Bioinformatics analysis revealed a copy number-neutral loss of heterozygosity (CN-LOH) for the entire APC gene at variant allele frequency (VAF) 0.87 (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 1) in the tumor. The region of CN-LOH extends for a region of at least 0.13Mbp (Chr5: 112043072-112178212) in chromosome 5, but the extent of deletion beyond this region could not be determined (Supp. Note 2, Supplementary File 1). The blood lymphocytes and normal lung tissue only showed the presence of the germline APC mutation, at VAF 0.47 and 0.49, respectively. Two DICER1 hits were found solely in the lung tumor: one in the RNase IIIb domain (NM_030621.3: c.5425 G > T (p.G1809W); VAF 0.27), and the other a truncating loss-of-function mutation (NM_030621.3: c.3697delG (p.D1233Mfs*4); VAF 0.55) (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 2).

We also reviewed the recent study by Sun et al. [14], but we noticed some inconsistencies in their findings. Firstly, their reported WDFLAC patient demographics (male, >50 years old) were atypical, as WDFLAC is mostly diagnosed in females under 40 [1]. Their WES results also showed that BLTP1 was the only commonly mutated gene across four of the eight cases, a gene which has not been previously linked to WDFLAC. Two of the BLTP1 variants were silent, and all the variants were sub-clonal mutations with a VAF < 0.20. Lastly, our enrichment analysis of their data revealed that only cigarette-smoking-affiliated genes were enriched in the set, which coincides with the reported patient histories and suggests that most of the reported variants are unrelated to the etiology of WDFLAC. Given the inconclusive results of the study, we chose to exclude it from our review.

Discussion

The patient’s APC germline PV at amino acid 1127 lies upstream of the first 20 amino-acid repeat that comprises one of the main β-catenin binding sites in the protein. It is unusual for a predicted truncating germline PV at this position in APC to be accompanied by LOH at the same locus, as there is often a selective pressure to retain at least one of the 20 amino-acid repeats between residues 1265 and 1400 for effective tumor formation in the colon [15]. The only other reported patient with FAP and WDFLAC never underwent germline or somatic genetic testing [4], so their tumor’s second APC somatic hit cannot be determined. While none of the WDFLAC cases previously presented by our group [5, 6] had confirmed co-diagnoses of FAP, our recent article did report one case with a clear loss of heterozygosity for APC at p.G871* (VAF 0.77), which, similar to our current patient, would also produce a protein missing its key β-catenin binding sites. Due to these findings, we postulate that LOH of the wild-type APC allele at residue 1127, accompanying the germline pathogenic variant, predisposed this patient to develop a WDFLAC.

A new report investigating biallelic inactivation of tumor suppressor genes (TSGs) across multiple cancer databases showed that amongst rare, mutated TSGs found in non-canonical primaries, biallelic loss of APC was commonly present in prostate adenocarcinomas and pulmonary adenocarcinomas [16]. Though the percentage of pulmonary adenocarcinomas with LOH in APC was not always significantly higher than those with wild-type APC, β-catenin protein levels were significantly elevated in the former. There was also a degree of mutual exclusivity in mutations of WNT pathway genes implicated in this type of cancer [16]. This is in line with our finding of wild-type CTNNB1 in our patient’s tumor, but our earlier paper [6] did note a patient with both mutated CTNNB1 and APC. However, this patient did appear to retain one wild-type copy of APC, so the CTNNB1 mutation may have played a more significant role in tumor formation. These results, nonetheless, do lead to the question of why there are not higher rates of lung cancer in FAP patients. It points to the possibility of spatiotemporal factors, or that other pathways take precedence over WNT signaling in pulmonary adenocarcinoma pathogenesis.

The DICER1 NGS results corroborate our earlier findings on WDFLAC [5, 6] and that of several other groups (Table 1 [10–13]). The two pathogenic DICER1 hits in the patient’s tumor, as well as its “fetal”-type morphology, are characteristic of other DICER1-related tumors [17]. An interaction between the two does appear to be exist in other types of tumors. Our group [6] analyzed sequencing data to show that DICER1 and CTNNB1 pathogenic mutations often co-occur in the related tumor, pulmonary blastoma. To et al. [18]. were able to establish directionality in the interaction within ovarian cancer metastases, showing that β-catenin downregulates the expression of DICER1 mRNA in highly metastatic ovarian cancer lines. Contrastingly, in Illiou et al.’s [19] study where they generated DICER1-impaired CRC cell lines, these lines were shown to have greater nuclear accumulation of β-catenin compared to their wild-type counterparts, though it must be noted that they already had deleterious mutations in CTNNB1 or APC.

Taken together with the results of our current case, DICER1 and WNT pathway PVs appear to play a synergistic role in the tumorigenesis of several tumors and influence the cell morphology of non-small cell lung cancers. We believe the absence of full-length APC in this patient’s lung tumor may have set the stage for development of a WDFLAC, driven by somatic DICER1 PVs. As such, in diagnostically challenging cases, testing for DICER1 variants in the tumor could provide value by helping confirm the diagnosis. Furthermore, if a somatic DICER1 variant is found, subsequent germline DICER1 PV analysis should be performed to see if the patient potentially has DICER1-related tumor predisposition. If the patient possesses a germline PV, screening for the associated tumors [20], which often present in childhood, could be instigated.
