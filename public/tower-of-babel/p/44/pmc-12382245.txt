Drivers of the unintended use of emptied chemical containers by smallholder farmers
Authors: Tewodros Mulugeta, Haimanot Teklemariam, Takele Taye Desta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo increase agricultural productivity, each year, an estimated two million tonnes of various pesticides are used worldwide. Thus, indiscriminate pesticide use and household use of empty pesticide containers are jeopardizing human health and the environment. Pesticide containers should be disposed of in a controlled container management system after dispensing their contents. This study assesses farmers’ practice of pesticide container usage at home, the reasons behind, handling practices, and the status of empty pesticide container management schemes in the study area. The findings revealed that a significant proportion of the farmers used empty pesticide containers for various unintended household purposes (χ2 = 15.4, p < 0.0001; 68%), such as storage of liquid and solid foods, food preparation utensils, and water tanks. Farmers prefer empty chemical containers ascribed of their durability, ease of access, and affordability, and the significant majority of the farmers used them for more than five years (96%; χ2 = 83.6, p < 0.0001). Washing containers was a common practice by the significant majority (87%; χ2 = 53, p < 0.0001) using hot water, followed by fumigation with Olea africana wood splits. The majority of the respondents disagree that empty chemical containers are harmful to human health, and argue that cleaning can make them safe for household use (63%; χ2 = 15.4, p < 0.0001). A significant proportion of the respondents were not aware of the means of disposing of empty chemical containers (97%; χ2 = 130, p < 0.0001). Discarding empty chemical containers was significantly associated with age, family size, and educational background. Farmers with a lower level of education have the highest rate of using empty chemical containers compared to the respondents with advanced literacy. Introduction

Due to global population growth, the demand for agricultural products is estimated to increase by 70–100% in 2050 [46]. Population growth together with increasing standards of living inevitably cause the expansion and intensification of agricultural land use to fulfill growing demands for food, biofuel, and other commodities because it is unlikely that production increases will not be attained from the currently cultivated land [8, 10, 37]. Globally, the cropland is expanding, and the greatest expansion occurs in South America and sub-Saharan Africa [21]. Consequently, forestlands, grasslands, wetlands, and other features of ecological importance are diminishing [20]. However, food security cannot be achieved only through land expansion, but also by improving production per unit area of land.

According to the land-cover analysis, highland areas are the most cultivated regions in Ethiopia. For instance, the highlands of Amhara, central Oromiya, and northeastern Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples (SNNP) have already utilized 60, 50, and 70 percent of the total land dedicated to crop production, respectively. In contrast, the lowlands have a very small area dedicated to crop production [39]. Agricultural expansion and productivity are coupled with the expansion of pesticide usage [38]. Currently, worldwide pesticide consumption stands at 4 million metric tons. According to The Business Research Company [41], the global pesticide and agricultural chemicals market was valued at approximately $117.5 billion in 2024 and is projected to grow to $131.8 billion in 2025 with annual growth rate of 12.1%. Chemical pesticide use in Ethiopia has significantly increased from 1440 to 4586 tons between 2001 and 2013 [33]. As a result of the increased use of pesticides, the volume of empty containers generated has reached to unmanageable level [16].

Empty pesticide containers pose a threat to humans and the environment unless properly managed. Chemical pesticide containers are often made of plastic. Consequently, discarding these containers randomly could influence human health, and the impact is due to the presence of the active substance in the containers [30, 36]. The amount of active substance in emptied pesticide containers can be above the hazard limits, depending on the type of active ingredient [14]. The triple rinsing is one of the recommended means of rendering containers less toxic; however, it may not necessarily work to free the containers from hazardous compounds [14]. Several studies have shown the mismanagement and misuse of emptied containers. When discarded, they are simply thrown in the fields, into irrigation canals or streams, or discarded with other general wastes [3, 9, 40, 45].

Empty containers are used to fuel, store food, animal products, and water, which could cause pesticide poisoning [7, 9, 28]. There is a risk that empty pesticide containers may cause when used for home use purposes without adequate control, resulting in contaminated food and water supplies and public health problems. Despite their meticulous cleanliness, pesticide containers are not suitable for storing food items. Besides, the improper discarding can harm non-target organisms in addition to the contamination of soil and groundwater [6]. As a result, a container management scheme –triple rinsing before discarding or burning- should be in place to prevent the unsafe use of pesticide containers [12]. According to Aschale et al. [4], there is no established system in Ethiopia.

To the best of our knowledge, no information is available on the number of empty containers generated annually in Ethiopia. However, studies have shown that farmers use empty chemical containers for household uses [1, 26, 27]. These studies have only shown that farmers utilized empty containers, but several issues, such as why farmers use empty chemical containers, their preference for these containers compared to other containers, such as clay pots, and farmers’ knowledge about empty chemical containers on their health have never been studied. Getting answer to this questions would provide insight how to design the means to reduce farmers reliance on pesticide containers. Therefore, this study investigated the status of empty chemical container usage for household purposes, motivations, and socio-demographic factors influencing such practices as well as the local awareness of associated health risks and disposal options. Based on prior evidence and field observations, we hypothesize that there is high unintended use of empty pesticide containers which is associated with low education levels, inadequate public awareness, and the lack of proper container management systems.

Methodology

The study site

The study (Fig. 1) was conducted in the Chole district, which is located in the Arsi zone of Oromia National Regional State in south-eastern Ethiopia. The sampled villages are located at an elevation of 2700 m above sea level. Chole district covers a total area of about 766 square kilometers. The climate is temperate, with daily temperature ranges of 15 to 25 °C. The mean annual rainfall is 1000 mm. Subsistence farmers typically make a living through mixed crop-livestock agriculture.

Map of the study site

Methods of sampling and data collection

Three rural villages that are locally called kebeles were randomly selected from eighteen kebeles that are found in the study district. From each kebele, fifty volunteer respondents were selected. The sampling was done in a field-based approach while walking in the sparesly scattred houses in the rural areas. The interviews were conducted in the field while the respondents were working on their fields. None of the farmers refused to participate in the interview. The data was gathered by the authors in January 2023. This was a cross-sectional study that involved general informant interviews of 150 smallholder farmers. Due to the scaterred nature of the houses in the rural setting and data saturation the sample size was limited to 150 participitants [13]. Verbal consent was obtained from each respondent before commencing the face-to-face interview. The questionnaire was translated from English to Amharic, one of the local language before, administered. Dichotomous (yes or no), multiple choice, and open-ended items were used to gather data on the usage and handling of emptied chemical containers, perception of safety and health risks, awareness and training on safe disposal, and willingness to change behavior.

Variables such as whether or not the respondents bought empty containers, cleaned empty chemical containers before use, and preferred empty chemical containers over clay or other utensils were measured using dichotomous (Yes/No) questions. Similarly, whether the respondent believes or not that using empty containers is harmful to health, cleaning the containers can reduce health risk, and they should stop using these containers were measured using dichotomous questions. Whether the respondent knows how to dispose of emptied chemical containers, has received training or been informed on disposal methods, has advised others not to use emptied containers, and whether the respondent would stop using the containers if told to stop were measured by dichotomous questions. The current use of emptied chemical containers, the purpose for household use, the disposal means and location, reasons for using emptied containers, and cleaning methods of empty containers before use were measured using structured multiple-choice questions. Reasons for preferring empty chemical containers, perceived health effects of using such containers, source of training or information received on disposal of empty containers, and reasons for willingness or unwillingness to stop use were measured using open-ended questions.

Data analysis

The responses were organized and coded for analysis. The data were analyzed using frequency counts and the Chi-square test to check for disparities between expected and observed proportions of the responses. Logistic regression, considering a dependent variable (the usage of empty pesticide containers), was used for analysis. The explanatory variables were household size, age of the respondent, educational status, duration of use of emptied containers, awareness of health risks, training or information on disposal, and reason for use of empty chemical containers To refine the model, the non-significant predictors were removed from the model stepwise, and significant values were reported (household size and age). The correlation between educational level and the usage of empty pesticide containers was analyzed using the Pearson chi-square test.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents

The majority of the study participants (96.7%, 145/150) were male and 3.3% (5) were female respondents. The maximum and minimum age range was 20 and 74 years old, and the avergae age was 50.2 years. The majority of the respondents were married (97.3%), and family size ranges between 1 and 18 individuals and the mean family size was six. Farming was reported as the major livelihood by the majority (98.7%); whereas, only 1.3% had side jobs. About a third (34.7%) of the respondents had completed primary school. 28.7 and 10.7% of the respondents had no education nd non-fromal education, respectively. Only 4% of the respondents had college diploma and 22% attended secondary school.

The tradition of using emptied chemical containers for household use

A significantly higher proportion of farmers (99/150; 66%) used pesticide containers or fertilizer bags after they had been emptied (χ2 = 15.4; p < 0.0001). The containers were used for various household purposes, such as the storage of liquid and solid foods, food preparation utensils, and water tanks. Liquid foods such as food oil, milk, yogurt, and local beverages were reported as food items that are commonly stored in pesticide containers. Empty containers are used to store solid foods like wheat flour, roasted barley flour (locally called Beso), powdered red pepper (locally called Berbere), powdered peas (locally called Shiro), salt, coffee beans, honey, cottage cheese, and lentils. The remaining 34% (51/150) of the farmers discarded empty chemical containers and they never used empty pesticide or fertilizer containers as a home appliance. Among farmers who have discarded chemical containers: 2% (1/51) dumped empty containers at their home premises, 86% (44/51) threw them randomly, and 12% (6/51) burned the containers (χ2 = 65; p < 0.0001). None of them ever washed containers before disposal.

A significantly higher proportion of the farmers (95/99; 96%) used chemical containers for more than five years (χ2 = 83.6; p < 0.0001). Four percent (4/99) of the farmers used pesticide containers for more than ten years. In addition to the containers emptied from pesticides and fertilizers they used, a significant proportion of the farmers also bought empty chemical containers from street vendors (71/99; χ2 = 18.7; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2).

Preference toward the use of empty chemical containers

Farmers mentioned various reasons to use emptied chemical containers, such as durability, ease of access, and affordability. The durability of the containers was mentioned as the main reason by the majority of the respondents (84%, 83/99). Accessibility and affordability were also mentioned by 15% (15/99) and 1% (1/99) (χ2 = 117; p < 0.0001). All the respondents reported that they rinsed the chemical containers before use. However, they randomly disposed of the rinsate after washing the empty chemical containers (Fig. 3).

The reason behind farmers’ preference for the use of empty chemical containers over clay pots

All the respondents who used pesticide containers washed them before use. Most of these respondents (86/99; 87%) used hot water to wash the empty pesticide containers and fumigated with the burnt splits of olive wood (Olea europaea) (χ2 = 53; p < 0.0001). They claimed that in addition to cleaning the container, fumigation improved the aroma and taste of the food. Empty pesticide containers are filled by byproducts of local drinks such as Tej () and Tela () and are kept for three or more days to rinse the containers by 13% of the respondents (13/99). Those farmers who used empty chemical containers for household purposes preferred empty chemical containers over clay pots. Conversely, farmers who did not use empty chemical containers preferred clay pots over chemical containers (χ2 = 15; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3). Durability, ease of handling, and lighter weight were reasons for inclined preference toward empty chemical containers (Fig. 3).

A significant proportion of the respondents (99/150) (χ2 = 15.4; p < 0.0001) disagreed with the notion that empty chemical containers are harmful to human health. They further added that cleaning them using traditional methods can make them safe for household use. However, the remaining 34% acknowledged the harmful effect of empty chemical containers on human health and they reported that no cleaning method can guarantee their safe use. The possible health problems related to chemical container usage raised by the respondents who never used empty chemical containers were asthma, lung, skin, eye infection, and miscarriage. Accordingly, 34% of the respondents warned that all chemical containers should be discarded, whereas the remaining 66% recommended that all the containers should be reused.

Proposed ways of discarding empty chemical containers

A significant proportion of the respondents (145/150) (χ2 = 130; p < 0.0001) reported that they were not aware of the means of discarding empty chemical containers. Only 3% (5/150) said that they knew the means to dispose of empty chemical containers (Fig. 4). All study participants had never received training on the disposal of empty chemical containers (150/150). Farmers who use empty chemical containers for domestic service said that they would stop using chemical containers if they were told about the health risks (99/99). Out of those who never used empty chemical containers, (22/51) said they have advised others not to use empty chemical containers, whereas 57% (29/51) of the respondents have never advised fellow farmers not to use empty chemical containers (χ2 = 0.96; p > 0.5) (Fig. 4).

Discarding empty chemical containers

Results from the multivariate logistic regression showed that discarding empty chemical containers were significantly associated with the age (p = 0.0003) and family size of the respondents (p = 0.005). Households with large family sizes were 1.5 times more likely to use empty chemical containers (odds ratios (OR) = 1.5) than those with a smaller family size. However, the younger the age, the more likely it is to use empty chemical containers for home purposes (OR = 0.96). The Pearson chi-square results revealed that empty chemical containers usage was significantly influenced by respondents’ educational status. The highest empty pesticide container usage was observed in the group of farmers who never had formal education (28%), followed by primary education recipients. How respondents (n = 51) discarded empty chemical containers was significantly associated with their educational level. Accordingly, 7.84% of the respondents who burned empty chemical containers had a college diploma, which was the highest level of education among the respondents (Table 1).

No container management scheme in the study area aligns with the international code of conduct for the distribution and use of pesticides, so, farmers had no chance to return empty chemical containers to the distributors. Those who never used empty chemical containers never rinsed them before discarding them. They have never stored empty chemical containers once their content was removed. Respondents used empty chemical containers and washed them whenever they wanted, whether the contents were dry or not, and discarded the rinse water at random.

Discussion

The expansion of agriculture is putting an extra burden on the environment due to the stockpiles of empty chemical containers and their mismanagement. Containers abandoned in the environment can lead to the pollution of soil and groundwater [12]. The safe and environmentally sound management of empty chemical containers is pivotal [12]. The present study aims to assess the scope of empty chemical containers’ usage for household purposes and the associated risk factors. The result revealed that a higher proportion (66%) of the farmers used one-way chemical containers for household purposes after removing their contents. Studies have reported the use of empty chemical containers by smallholder farmers [5, 7, 19, 23, 32]. Farmers mentioned durability, ease of access, and affordability as reasons for using empty chemical containers. Similarly, Agmas & Adugna [2] reported that 56 percent of the empty chemical containers were reused around Lake Tana in northwest Ethiopia.

According to Ibitayo [17], 80% of Egyptian farmers did not dispose of empty containers; rather, they used them at home. A study conducted on Nigerian cocoa farmers revealed that more than 35% of the farmers used empty pesticide containers at home [42]. In contrast to this study, Loha et al. [23] reported a lower proportion of use (29%) of these containers in the Central Rift Valley (CRV) region of Ethiopia. According to a study conducted in Jimma Zone (southwestern Ethiopia), only 12.6% of farmers reused empty chemical containers in their homes [15]. Mergia et al. [29] found a much lower household container usage rate of 8.1% among Ziway small-scale vegetable producers. In Pakistan, Mubushar et al. [31] reported that only 1.5% of the farmers used empty chemical containers at home. However, pesticide containers should not be used for other purposes because the residues of pesticides are still inside them [12]. The apparent usage of containers might be owing to farmers’ misunderstanding of the adverse impacts of chemical containers on their health and the environment. From another point of view, areas where a relatively lower proportion of farmers’ empty chemical container usage was reported, is due to farmers’ better understanding of the negative impact of these containers resulting from the residual effect of pesticides [7].

Farmers used the containers in different ways and for several storage purposes, such as storage of solid food items (flours of wheat, barley, peas, pepper, salt, coffee beans, honey, cottage cheese, and lentils) and liquid food items (food oil, milk, yogurt, and local beverages), food preparation utensils, and for collection of water and storage tanks (Fig. 5). Studies have found that empty pesticide containers can be used to store palm oil, and food grains, fetch water, provide drinking water to children, and water domestic animals [5, 17, 32, 42]. Thirty-five percent of the respondents never used chemical containers at home; however, this could not make them safe because the results revealed that the means of disposing of empty containers was unsafe. Pesticide containers were randomly thrown away at home or in the open field by 88% of the respondents, and only 12% burned containers. This might be due to the lack of awareness among farmers of the potential risk of chemical containers. A study conducted in Jimma Zone reported that 23% of the farmers throw pesticide containers in the open field [34]. Farmers who never used pesticide containers at home randomly dumped empty containers either at their home premises or in the field, and a few burned the containers [2]. Similarly, Lekei et al. [22] reported burying, burning, and dumping empty pesticide containers on the farm as means of disposal. In Pieria, in Greece, Damalas et al. [9] reported that randomly throwing containers into irrigation canals or streams, abandoning them in the field, or disposing of them with other wastes are the most common means of discarding pesticide containers by farmers. On-site burning, burying, and selling are the other means reported to be used to discard containers, in contrast to the present study.

Chemical containers that are disposed of improperly pollute the environment. Empty containers should be disposed of at a licensed incinerator. According to Mengistie et al. [27], findings from a study conducted in Ethiopia, all the respondents randomly threw containers in the field. In contrast to this finding, Loha et al. [23] reported that farmers’ perceptions and attitudes toward the proper disposal of containers have been improved. A study conducted in Iran reported that 32.8 percent of the farmers randomly dump empty pesticide containers in the fields [5].

Rinsing empty chemical containers can be economically and environmentally advantageous. Triple container rinsing is suggested before disposal, especially in the absence of washing equipment [12, 25]. However, farmers’ activity contradicts this; none of them ever rinses empty containers before disposal. In contrast to the present study, a study in Bolivia showed that 43% of the farmers performed the triple rinse [16]. Rinsing of containers intended for disposal could be perceived as a waste of time and water for farmers, which may seem like the right decision unless observed from an environmental perspective. In the study area, farmers has no direct access to water, they travel a few kilometers to get access to water; thus, using water to wash empty containers before disposal might appear something luxury.

According to the findings, a sizable proportion of farmers believed that empty chemical containers were not harmful to human health and that washing them could make them safe. This showed the gap in farmers understanding, which was further observed in the lack of access to training where none of the repondents had traning on safe pesticide container disposal. In agreement to this, a study by Sosan et al. [40] has shown that more than half of the cocoa farmers in Nigeria who participated in the study never took training on the disposal of pesticide containers. However, studies have reported farmers’ understanding of pesticides’ impact on human and animal health and the environment [11, 15, 18].

Findings further revealed that respondents’ age, household size, and educational status were associated with discarding empty chemical containers. The bigger the family, the more likely to use empty chemical containers; younger respondents are more likely to use empty pesticide containers than older ones; and the more educated, the less likely to use chemical containers at home. Similarly, a study revealed that the age of the farmer had a positive relationship with pesticide container usage [35]. Okoffo et al. [35] further reported that gender, farming experience, and age positively correlate with burning as the means of disposing of chemical containers. In contrast, Huici et al. [16] reported that the knowledge and practice of handling empty pesticide containers had no relation to the age and educational status of the respondents. In agreement with this study, other studies have shown the impact of age, educational background, and traning in safe usage of pesticides and understanding of pesticide impacts [43, 44].

The human, legal, technical, and economic conditions necessary to implement pesticide container management schemes are generally lacking in developing nations [16]. In agreement with this study, Loha et al. [24] reported that there is a policy and implementation gap in Ethiopia, as there is neither a standard system that accepts and collects expired, unwanted, or unused pesticides and pesticide containers nor established recycling systems for disposal. There is no institute or governmental body to control the disposal of pesticide containers. In addition, no disposal or storage facilities are available [24]. This study have shown not only farmers used pesticides container for unintended household purposes and practiced unsafe disposal but also why, the drving factors that forced them to use pesticide containers and discard container randomly. Therfore, educating farmers to raise awareness on the dangers of using empty pesticide containers to store food and water would minimize the impact. Besides, availing other safer containers in an affordable price would reduce the use of pesticide containers. An innovative approach would be to give them safer containers in exchange for empitied pesticide containers, which would need government or other stakeholders intervention. The government should consider adopting a container management scheme and customize it to suit the conditions in the study area. Adopt puncturing or cutting containers to discourage household use could also be part of the scheme. The government could also restrict the sell of pesticide containers in local markets or streets or devise collection methods. Alternatively, focusing on the use of water-soluble packs to avoid the need to recycle or dispose of the contaminated container.

Conclusion

One of the major pollutants that continues to endanger the safety of people, animals, and the environment is the improper use and disposal of empty pesticide containers. It was discovered that the majority of farmer use pesticide containers for a variety of domestic uses. The respondents claim that the use of pesticide containers is associated with some exceptional qualities of the containers, such as durability, accessibility, and affordability compared to standard utensils. Farmers’ perceptions of the effects of chemical containers on the environment, the health of people and animals, and other factors significantly from reality, and they claim cleaning the containers removes the toxic substance. In general, farmers are keen to use empty pesticide containers, thus to minimize the negative impact of pesticide containers use educating farmers about the dangers of using pesticide containers is necessary.
