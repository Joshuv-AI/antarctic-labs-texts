Sexual self-care in fertile and infertile women: a comparative study
Authors: Mahnaz Mohammadnezhad, Sohrab Iranpour, Pouran Akhavan Akbari, Samira Shahbazzadegan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSexual self-care is an emerging concept in reproductive health. In addition to harm reduction, self-care plays a key role in promoting physical, mental, and social health, especially in societies where sexual taboos exist. This study aims to compare the sexual self-care among fertile and infertile women in Ardabil city.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 100 fertile women visiting health centers and 100 infertile women from the Ardabil Infertility Treatment Center, aged 15–49 years, between May 2024 and February 2025. Samples were selected through convenience sampling based on inclusion criteria. Data were collected using a demographic questionnaire and Female Sexual Health Self-care (FSHS) questionnaire, and analyzed in SPSS version 26 using chi-square test, independent t-test, and ANOVA.ResultsFemale and male factors were reported as causes of infertility in 59% and 41%, respectively. The mean self-care score of the fertile group was 57.93 ± 15.42 and for the infertile group was 60.62 ± 13.14. There was no significant difference in overall sexual self-care scores between the two groups, but when source of health-related information was considered as a confounding factor, the results related to total score of the instrument became significant (p-value = 0.04). The highest score in both groups was related to the domain of sexual health promotion, with a higher mean score of infertile women (76.13 ± 15.66) compared to the fertile group (62.09 ± 19.83), and there was a significant difference between the two groups (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe findings of this study indicated that there is no significant difference in sexual self-care between fertile and infertile women, despite variations in sources of health-related information. Therefore, it can be concluded that sexual self-care is a multidimensional phenomenon influenced by various factors and cannot be explained solely based on fertility status.Keywords: Fertility, Infertility, Sexual self-care

Introduction

Reproductive and sexual health are important aspects of women’s health [1]. Sexual health is essential for the overall health and well-being of individuals, couples, families, as well as for the social and economic development of communities and countries [2]. According to the World Health Organization, sexual health is the ability to enjoy sexual behaviors and manage them, prevent and control sexually transmitted infections, and cope with psycho-sexual disorders [3]. To achieve this, individuals must practice sexual self-care [4]. Sexual self-care is an emerging concept in reproductive health [5]. There is a direct relationship between sexual self-care and the sexual quality of life in women, and women with high levels of sexual self-care experience a better quality of sexual life [6]. Fertility is considered the most fundamental aspect of women’s lives in most cultures [7]. Fertility and the survival of generation in society require ensuring the marital and sexual health of couples [8]. In contrast to fertility, infertility has been raised as an important reproductive health problem, Approximately one in six people in the world experiences infertility at some stage of their lives [9]. Infertility affects women’s health in emotional, psychological, sexual and physical aspects [10]. In most cases, infertility eliminates the voluntary and pleasurable aspects of sexual function by relegating it to a means of “procreation” [11]. The psychological and social consequences of infertility reduce life satisfaction and increase marital problems [12]. Infertility can affect women’s sexual behavior through medical treatments, assisted reproductive technologies, timing of intercourse, and psychological stress, which may reduce sexual satisfaction and shift sexual activity toward conception rather than pleasure, highlighting the need for sexual and psychological counseling for infertile women [13]. however some studies have shown that infertility does not cause marital dissatisfaction [14] and infertile couples even experience greater intimacy due to being together during the treatment process and getting to know each other better [15, 16]. Most studies have identified infertility as a risk factor for marital conflict and divorce [17, 18]. Given that infertility is categorized as a chronic condition, self-care holds a pivotal role in its management and treatment [19]. Importantly, self-care is not limited to individuals with illnesses; it also applies to healthy populations by enhancing awareness and knowledge of physical and psychological status, thereby contributing to overall health promotion [20]. Considering the significance of improving fertility rate according to population policy makers in Iran and the substantial costs associated with infertility treatment in infertile couples, self-care interventions may serve as an effective and cost-efficient approach [19]. According to the most recent WHO guidelines, the scope of self-care in relation to sexual health encompasses improving antenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum care; ensuring the provision of high-quality services for family planning—particularly in the context of infertility; eliminating unsafe abortion; preventing and managing sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV; addressing reproductive tract infections and cervical cancer; and advancing sexual health [4].

In general, sexual self-care has 4 subgroups: prevention of sexually transmitted diseases and genital infections, prevention of women’s cancer, promotion of sexual health, and prevention of unwanted pregnancy [21]. “The increasing prevalence of sexually transmitted infections such as HPV highlights the need for public education, screening, and vaccination [22], which are part of the sexual self-care that individuals need. According to Yazdani et al. study (2023), the mean total score of sexual self-care in reproductive age women in Sari was 66.70 ± 12.52, and factors such as age, history of HPV disease and vaccination, source of sexual information and method of contraception were among the factors affecting women’s sexual self-care [4]. Infertile individuals may focus more on managing their reproductive health through medical consultations and treatments, which can affect their self-care behaviors [23]. Overall, sexual self-care is critical for improving individual health and promoting informed decision-making [24]. However, research on the self-care in sexual and reproductive health is limited [25, 26]. Considering the importance of sexual health for infertile women, who due to infertility treatments require more sexual counseling and sexual self-care recommendations [8]. Considering the inconsistent findings of comparative studies on fertile and infertile women, further research can help expand our understanding of sexual self-care in these two groups and clarify their specific sexual health needs. Since self-care plays an important role in maintaining individual and social health, examining the status of sexual self-care among fertile and infertile women is essential for raising awareness and supporting reproductive and sexual health. Accordingly, the present study was designed to compare the sexual self-care status of fertile and infertile women attending health centers in Ardabil. A better understanding of sexual self-care in these groups can provide valuable insights for developing targeted strategies to improve sexual and reproductive health.

Materials and methods

In this study, 100 fertile women from those referring to health centers and 100 infertile women visiting the infertility center of Ardabil University of Medical Sciences were studied through convenience sampling. The inclusion criteria for both groups included: age range of 15 to 49 years, no experience of a stressful event in the past three months, monogamous husband, and no known psychological or physical illnesses. For fertile women, not being pregnant and having at least one child over the age of six months was considered, and for infertile women, not having adopted children was considered. Exclusion criteria included incomplete questionnaire completion and withdrawal from the study. The sample size, based on Karaka et al. [27], considering 95% power and 5% error level, was calculated using the following formula for each group as 88 individuals, which was calculated as 100 people for each group, considering the probability of miscarriage.

For collecting data from fertile women Ardabil, city was first divided into five regions, and five clusters from different areas of the city were selected to ensure sample diversity and from each cluster, one health center was chosen. The study samples from each center were selected through convenience sampling. The research environment for infertile women was the Infertility Center of Ardabil University of Medical Sciences, and the women were selected by using convenience sampling. After explaining the objectives to the participants and obtaining informed consent, a checklist containing of personal and midwifery information and sexual self-care instrument were completed through interviews for each participant. The Female Sexual Health Self-care (FSHS) Contains 40 items that assess sexual self-care in four domains: prevention of sexually transmitted diseases and genital tract infections, prevention of women’s cancers, prevention of unwanted pregnancy, and promotion of sexual health. Each question is set on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from never to always. The total score for the instrument (40 questions) ranges from a minimum of 40 to a maximum of 200. To better understand the scoring and its comparability, the scores of each factor were converted to a 0–100scale. The psychometric properties of the Persian version of the questionnaire were assessed by Yazdani and colleagues. The content validity index of the instrument was 93%and the relative reliability was 94% with Cronbach’s alpha [21]. The obtained data were analyzed using SPSS software version 26 and the statistical tests of Chi-square, independent t-test, and ANOVA.

Results

The mean age and standard deviation of fertile and infertile women were 34.69 ± 5.81 and 34.31 ± 6.07, respectively, and the duration of marriage of fertile women was 10.87 ± 5.79 and infertile women were 7.97 ± 5.35. Demographic information of the participated women is presented in Table 1. Independent t-test and chi-square statistical tests showed that, except for the duration of marriage, emotional problems with the spouse, place of residence, and sources of health information, there were no statistically significant differences between the two study groups in terms of other demographic and obstetric variables. The most important source of health information for fertile women was cyberspace (37%) and for infertile women it was healthcare centers (56%), and the two groups had a statistically significant difference (p-value < 0/001). In terms of the type of infertility, 84% had primary infertility and 16% had secondary infertility. Also, the cause of infertility in the infertile women studied was reported as 41% male factor and 59% female factor. Review of women’s sexual self-care status based on the information in Table 2 shows that the two groups do not have a significant difference in terms of average sexual self-care (p-value = 0.185), However, when controlling for the source of health-related information as a confounding variable, sexual self-care showed a significant difference between the two groups (p-value = 0.04).

Discussion

The present study, conducted to compare the sexual self-care status of fertile and infertile women, demonstrated that Comparing of the sexual self-care status of fertile and infertile women showed that, the overall sexual self-care scores were higher among fertile women compared to infertile women. Consistent with our findings, Yazdani et al. also reported a relatively high overall sexual self-care score among women [4]. Lower sexual self-care scores in our study compared with Yazdani et al. study could be due to various factors such as different samples including fertile women and context of study, also it seems that Lower access to sexual health education and services, as well as traditional cultures in Ardabil city, where sexual issues are still considered taboo can be considered as another cause of different results.

In our study there was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of sexual self-care, although considering “source of sexual information” as a confounding factor, the results approached at the threshold of significance. In this regard, our findings are consistent with the research of Yazdani et al. (2021) that demographic factors, sources of sexual information, and contraception were among the most important factors affecting sexual self-care [4]. This indicates the key role of targeted education and information dissemination in promoting sexual self-care behaviors. Studies have confirmed women’s interest in and need for receiving sexual information [28, 29]. Several studies have highlighted the importance of specialized counseling in enhancing sexual self-care among infertile women, suggesting that targeted interventions can effectively improve their sexual health behaviors and overall well-being [6, 23, 30]. Also Infertile individuals may focus more on managing their reproductive health through consultations and medical treatments, which can affect their self-care behaviors [23]. In the study by Lotfollahi et al. (2021), there was no significant difference between the two groups of fertile and infertile women in terms of sexual self-concepts, such as self-schemas, which is consistent with our findings regarding to sexual self-care which both variables are related concepts in sexual health issue [31]. In contrast to our study, Ozturk et al. (2019) showed that infertile women often have lower sexual function scores compared to fertile women, which included decreased sexual satisfaction and increased discomfort during intercourse [32]. Although the limited number of studies in the field of sexual self-care, especially when comparing fertile and infertile groups, makes discussing and interpreting the study findings difficult, given that sexual self-care is a complex and multidimensional issue [21] and that various variables can affect it [4], sexual self-care status cannot be attributed solely to the fertility status of individuals. Educating infertile women, as well as referring them to sexual counseling during the infertility treatment process, can affect their sexual knowledge and self-care. Additionally, access to sexual health information and related education for both fertile and infertile women should be ensured. Essentially, conducting is accompanied with limitations.

This study has several inherent limitations. Convenience sampling of infertile participants, achieving cross sectional study and conducting the study within the traditional cultural context of Ardabil, may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Another limitation was unavailability of comparable studies related to fertile and infertile comparison, which constrained opportunities for broader comparisons. Future research should explore larger and more diverse samples, consider cultural contexts, and employ longitudinal or mixed-method designs. Developing targeted educational and health interventions for infertile women is also recommended.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of the present study, when the source of sexual information is not considered, there is no significant difference in sexual self-care between fertile and infertile women. Given that fertile women primarily obtain information from cyberspace and infertile women from health centers, this highlights the importance of access to accurate and evidence-based sexual information through the health system, specialized education, and supportive policy-making. Therefore, it is recommended that health centers, sexual health professionals, and policymakers provide targeted educational and counseling programs to promote sexual self-care, make reliable sexual information accessible, and offer practical strategies to improve healthy sexual behaviors and prevent sexual health problems. Overall, sexual self-care is a multidimensional issue influenced by various factors and cannot be attributed solely to an individual’s fertility status.
