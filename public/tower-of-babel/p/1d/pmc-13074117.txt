High prevalence of maturity‐onset diabetes of the young in the Czech Republic: A 25‐year nationwide registry‐based study
Authors: Petra Dusatkova, Marketa Pavlikova, Michaela Cermakova, Klara Vesela, Katerina Kolarova, Lenka Elblova, Zdenek Sumnik, Jan Lebl, Stepanka Pruhova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsMaturity‐Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) results from a single‐gene defect. This study aimed to determine the minimal prevalence, screening indicators, as well as clinical characteristics of MODY in the Czech Republic based on data from 25 years of the nationwide registry.MethodsClinical and genetic data were collected from individuals suspected of having MODY referred for genetic testing and registered in the National Registry for Monogenic Diabetes between 1999 and 2023. Prevalence estimates were calculated using Czech national census data. The cluster analyses of probands with the absence of a detected monogenic cause of diabetes were performed with the Mclust library.ResultsIn total, 1879 probands with suspected MODY were registered, and 728 (39%) received a confirmed diagnosis (in total, 1473 individuals). GCK, HNF1A and HNF4A gene variants accounted for 93% of all diagnoses. The average prevalence of MODY was 136 per million inhabitants, peaking at 240 per million in the northeast region of the country. If the highest recorded minimum prevalence is applied to the total population in the Czech Republic, approximately 44% of cases remain undiagnosed. A cluster statistical analysis identified five overlapping, clinically defined subgroups within the probands with no identified monogenic cause of diabetes.ConclusionsThis study provides one of the highest national prevalence rates of MODY to date. Nevertheless, approximately half of the expected cases remain undetected. The negative group included several different subtypes of glucose tolerance disorders, which should guide future research into the pathogenesis of different types of diabetes.

INTRODUCTION

The term monogenic diabetes encompasses several subtypes of impaired beta cell function disorders that result from one or more defects in a single‐gene or chromosomal locus. 1 Outside the neonatal period, Maturity‐Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) and diabetes associated with extra‐pancreatic features (syndromic diabetes) represent the most common subtypes of monogenic diabetes. 2 , 3 Three genes implicated in MODY diagnosis prevail: GCK, HNF1A and HNF4A; all representing medically actionable genes. Hyperglycaemia caused by loss‐of‐function variants in the glucokinase (GCK) gene does not require treatment 4 outside pregnancy. People with diabetes having causal variants in the genes encoding transcriptional factors hepatocyte nuclear factor 1‐alpha (HNF1A) and 4‐alpha (HNF4A) are sensitive to the therapy, stimulating insulin secretion from the beta cells like low‐dose sulfonylurea 5 or alternatively GLP‐1 analogues. 6 In contrast to GCK‐MODY, there is a high risk of chronic microvascular complications in persons with HNF‐MODY. 7 Thus, straightforward treatment recommendations, genetic counselling in the respective families and early detection of potential comorbidities are the main reasons why searching for rare monogenic diabetes is of high importance.

Well run registers represent an essential prerequisite for studies aiming at establishing the correct diagnoses. 8 Based on the mainly nationwide registries of children with diabetes, the estimated prevalence of monogenic diabetes among them ranges between 0.2% and 5.5%. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Only three nationwide studies searching for people with MODY originating from both paediatric and adult populations with diabetes for a significantly long period of time were published, showing a wide spectrum of observed overall prevalence: 30, 58 and 500 cases per million, respectively. 3 , 15 , 16

The present study aimed to determine the minimal prevalence, screening indicators, as well as clinical characteristics of MODY in the Czech Republic with an emphasis on three major subtypes based on data from 25 years of the National Registry for Monogenic Diabetes.

METHODS

Investigation setup and indication criteria

The search for the monogenic cause of diabetes in the first Czech families began in 1999. One person with hyperglycaemia from each referred family is designated as the proband and undergoes genetic testing. If a variant is found, samples from other available family members of the proband are tested for the specific variant. Initially, only families with an extended family history of diabetes have been recruited each year in collaboration with paediatric diabetologists. The genetic testing was performed at the collaborating workplace at the Steno Diabetes Centre and Hagedorn Research Institute in Denmark 17 and, since 2006, has been ongoing in the Laboratory of Molecular Genetics, Department of Paediatrics, 2nd Faculty of Medicine, Charles University and University Hospital Motol in Prague, the Czech Republic. Between 2008 and 2010, a nationwide campaign promoting knowledge about MODY and its clinical implications was conducted among diabetologists across the Czech Republic. Blood samples of participants with suspicion of MODY are referred continuously, collected into EDTA tubes, accompanied by signed informed consent for genetic testing, completed clinical questionnaires and sent to the laboratory. Genetic testing is fully financially covered by research funding. Our laboratory has always been the dominant and is currently the leading provider of testing for MODY of diabetes in the Czech Republic and at the same time manages the National Registry for Monogenic Diabetes.

The indication criteria for genetic testing of people with suspected MODY have evolved over the years: from a strong emphasis on a positive family history of diabetes in each generation and an age at diagnosis of less than 25 years 18 , 19 to the current recommendation of the documented absence of pancreatic autoantibodies in individuals who manifested diabetes between the age of 6 months and 18 years only 1 because of the low prevalence of type 2 diabetes in children and adolescents in the Czech Republic. 20 For those who were diagnosed with diabetes in adulthood, the required conditions for genetic testing are age at diabetes diagnosis less than 40 years, absence of pancreatic autoantibodies and at least one additional factor: positive family history of diabetes, absence of signs of metabolic syndrome and/or detectable C‐peptide several years after diagnosis.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee of the 2nd Faculty of Medicine, Charles University and University Hospital Motol, Prague, the Czech Republic (EK‐544.11/23) issued on 14 June 2023.

Genetic investigation

Similar to the indication criteria, methods used for molecular genetic investigation have been evolving during the duration of the study. However, given the three predominant subtypes of MODY and time and cost efficiency, 21 the main method has been direct (Sanger) sequencing since the beginning of investigation 22 till now. DNA samples of referred people with persistent, mild hyperglycaemia were first investigated for the GCK gene variants, and those with reported progressive hyperglycaemia requiring pharmacological treatment were first tested for the HNF1A and HNF4A gene variants, respectively. Detection of possible larger genetic alterations, such as deletions and duplications were performed by Multiplex Ligation Probe‐dependent Amplification (P241 kit, MRC Holland, Amsterdam, Netherlands). As of 2018, these molecular genetic approaches are complemented by next‐generation sequencing involving 52 genes, and since 2022, 63 genes associated with the monogenic forms of pancreatic beta cell disorders. 21 Of note, samples of probands with a strong suspicion of MODY (i.e. with positive family history of diabetes) without detected variants in the GCK or HNF1A and HNF4A genes in the early period of investigation were retrospectively tested using a next‐generation sequencing panel as well. The causality of observed variants was re‐evaluated based on the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics 23 and Monogenic Diabetes Variant Curation Expert Panel.

National population data

The Czech Republic is a middle European country with approximately 11 million inhabitants and represents mostly a uniform, non‐Finnish European population. Epidemiological data containing the number of inhabitants and proportion of women and men for each of 14 regions of the Czech Republic were obtained from the publicly available database of the Czech Statistical Office. 24 Minimal prevalence was calculated as the number of persons with genetically proven MODY divided by the total population of the Czech Republic or the total population of each of the Czech regions, all on the date 31 December 2023. The map of observed prevalence in the Czech regions was generated using Datawrapper. 25

Statistical analyses

Data were summarized as medians with interquartile range (difference between the third and first quartiles) for continuous variables and as absolute and relative frequencies in the case of categorical variables. Weight parameters of probands whose samples were referred in their paediatric age were displayed as percentiles according to the Czech normative data. 26 Differences between studied groups were explored using a two‐sample t‐test or Wilcoxon's two‐sample test for continuous characteristics and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables; in case of multiple groups, ANOVA F‐test/Kruskal–Wallis test were used. The percentage of missing data was similar between studied clinical or biochemical parameters for the two compared groups (Table 1); therefore, the probability of influence on results because of missing data was low. Post‐hoc analysis of clinical characteristics of probands with GCK‐MODY, HNF1A‐MODY, HNF4A‐MODY, rare subtypes and those without an observed genetic cause of diabetes, respectively, was performed using pairwise testing with Holm correction. The cluster analyses of probands with the absence of a detected monogenic cause of diabetes were performed with the Mclust library for R, using a model‐based approach with age at detection of hyperglycaemia, HbA1c and therapy at investigation as independent variables. The level of statistical significance was set to 0.05. Statistical language and environment R, version 4.4.3, were used throughout the analysis. 27

RESULTS

Referral and detection rate

In total, 1879 families, each represented by a single proband and a variable number of family members, have been investigated for suspicion of MODY from 1999 to 2023. The referral rate (Figure 1a) started to increase since 2009 after a nationwide campaign promoting MODY, reaching 148 new probands referred in 2019, followed by a 41% decrease in 2020 due to the Covid‐19 pandemic. The diagnosis of MODY was genetically confirmed in 728/1879 probands (39%); including family relatives in a total of 1473 persons. The detection rate of genetically confirmed MODY (Figure 1b) has shown that original emphasis on positive family history of diabetes and age at diagnosis below 25 years brought with it a high detection rate exceeding 50% in the first 10 years of investigation and has been subsequently moving steadily between 27% and 40% since 2010.

Referral rate (a) and detection rate (b) of samples of probands sent for genetic testing of MODY. (a) Number of probands referred for genetic investigation because of clinical suspicion of MODY for the respective year. (b) Percentage of probands for each year in whom MODY was genetically proven.

Prevalence of MODY

The average minimal prevalence of MODY in the Czech Republic was 136 per 1 million inhabitants (women: 155/1,000,000; men: 116/1,000,000). The highest minimum prevalence of MODY was observed in the northeast region of the Czech Republic (240/1,000,000) and in the capital city of Prague (192/1,000,000) (Figure 2). When applying the highest observed minimum prevalence value (240/1,000,000) to the total population in the Czech Republic (10,900,555 in 31 December 2023), the estimated minimum number of people with MODY was 2616. Since the diagnosis of MODY was already established in 1473 persons, at least 44% remain unrecognized.

The prevalence of MODY observed in the Czech Republic (number of cases per 1 million inhabitants). Minimal prevalence was calculated as the number of persons with genetically proven MODY divided by the total population of each of the 14 Czech regions, all on the date 31 December 2023.

Causal genetic variants were observed in 13 different genes. While ultra‐rare subtypes of MODY (e.g. variants in the HNF1B, MT‐TL1, INS, WFS1 and other genes) were observed in 7% of probands, (likely) pathogenic variants in the GCK (69%), HNF1A (15%) and HNF4A (8%) genes accounted together for 93% of all intercepts.

Clinical characteristics of major monogenic diabetes subtypes

The clinical characteristics of the investigated probands were summarized in Table 1. Compared to people without detected causal genetic alteration, people with genetically confirmed diagnosis of MODY were more often women than men (p = 0.025) and were referred for testing at a younger age; their hyperglycaemia was observed at a younger age; their weight percentile in the case of children and BMI in the case of adults was lower (p < 0.002); and diabetic retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy were described less frequently (p < 0.012). Positive family history in each generation was reported significantly more often in people with MODY than in tested probands without a pathogenic variant (p < 0.001). Finally, non‐pharmacological treatment was predominantly observed in probands with MODY (p < 0.001).

The subsequent post‐hoc analysis aimed to identify which of the most prevalent subgroups (GCK‐, HNF1A‐ and HNF4A‐MODY) are responsible for the significant differences observed between confirmed cases of monogenic diabetes and negative probands. Age of hyperglycaemia detection and BMI were significantly lower in probands with either GCK‐MODY or HNF1A‐MODY (p < 0.001) but not in probands with HNF4A‐MODY (p > 0.336) compared with the negative group. Only probands with GCK‐MODY (p < 0.003) but not those with HNF1A‐ and HNF4A‐MODY (p > 0.086) were reported to have significantly lower HbA1c levels, weight percentiles, representation of diabetic complications, as well as non‐pharmacological treatment in comparison to probands without confirmed MODY. All three main subtypes of MODY accounted for a statistically significant difference in reported positive family history of diabetes between positive and negative findings (p < 0.001). Interestingly, women significantly outnumbered men in the HNF1A‐MODY and HNF4A‐MODY cohorts.

Cluster analysis of people with diabetes without an observed monogenic cause of diabetes

Because of the wide spectrum of reported HbA1c levels and type of treatment in people with diabetes without observed causal variant (Table 1), we performed a cluster analysis aiming to identify possible groupings within this dataset. Considering age at hyperglycaemia detection, HbA1c and type of treatment at referral for genetic testing, five overlapping clusters were identified (Figure 3). The clusters differ in both the age at detection of hyperglycaemia and HbA1c levels, with some groups partially overlapping (upper panel). Moreover, the results of the cluster analysis illustrated the differences in therapeutic approaches between clusters and their relationship to the age of detection of hyperglycaemia (bottom panel). There were people with diabetes who were diagnosed in paediatric age with HbA1c below approximately 6.7% (50 mmol/mol) and without treatment in cluster A. Cluster B represented a group of people with diabetes diagnosed also in paediatric age but displaying generally higher levels of HbA1c compared with cluster A and having insulin treatment. In group C were predominantly clustered people with diabetes diagnosed in adulthood, treated by diet or oral hypoglycaemic agents, and with HbA1c approximately ranging between 5.4% and 7.2% (35–55 mmol/mol). Insulin treatment, HbA1c levels generally above 6.3% (45 mmol/mol) and diagnosis at adolescent or early adulthood were most often observed in cluster D. Finally, the cluster E included people with diabetes with the widest range of HbA1c (predominantly very high levels), treated with a combination of insulin and oral hypoglycaemic drugs, who were diagnosed at different ages.

DISCUSSION

This is a registry‐based study summarizing 25 years of efforts of searching, genetically diagnosing and clinically characterizing people with MODY in the Czech Republic. We observed one of the highest observed average minimal prevalence (136 per million inhabitants) of this condition reported so far.

There are a limited number of studies describing the prevalence of MODY in the total population of the country. In Poland, a neighbouring country to the Czech Republic, the observed prevalence among only children reached 75.2 cases per million. 28 The prevalence of 21.0 cases per million was reported in the U.S. population study of persons younger than 20 years. 29 Genetic investigation of both paediatric and adult populations led to a prevalence of 30.0 cases per million in the Netherlands. 15 The observed overall prevalence of MODY was reported to be 57.8 cases per million in Great Britain. 3 However, considering the prevalence only from the Exeter area in England, where there is the most testing and the most awareness of monogenic diabetes, the prevalence reached 248 cases per million. This finding is similar to the highest observed prevalence in the northeast region of the Czech Republic (240 cases per million). Extrapolating these observations to a number of persons from the whole country suggests that 77% remain undiagnosed in Great Britain 3 and 44% in the Czech Republic. However, a recent study from Greenland reported a prevalence more than twice as high (500 cases per million) 16 as our and the British study. Importantly, the Greenland population was isolated for thousands of years, leading to a limited gene flow and therefore a distinct genetic architecture compared with other populations. 30 This is confirmed by recurring variants in the dataset, with a promoter variant in the HNF4A gene observed in 45% of families. Given the smaller size of the local population, systematic research is easier to conduct and more comprehensible. Therefore, this study might show that the true prevalence of monogenic diabetes could be higher than currently observed, also in other populations.

The most prevalent subtype observed in the Czech national registry of monogenic diabetes was GCK‐MODY (69% of all genetic findings). This is in line with other studies, which were predominantly or fully based on referrals from the paediatric population. 9 , 12 , 13 , 28 Because the HNF‐MODY manifests most commonly in adolescence and early adulthood, 31 this subtype prevailed in registries based on referrals of adult people with diabetes. 3 In total, the three main subtypes of monogenic diabetes detected outside the first six months of life (GCK‐, HNF1A‐ and HNF4A‐MODY) accounted for the majority of reported genetically confirmed cases: 93% in the present study and approximately 70%–97% in other studies/reports. 3 , 9 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 16 , 28 It is, however, important to note that most of the investigation, including ours, was performing a phenotype‐driven approach to genetic testing of the respective three genes, at least in the past decades. Therefore, some people with other forms of diabetes were most likely unrecognized. With the advent of next‐generation sequencing techniques and subsequent bioinformatics steps, including, for example, search for copy number variants, the number of ultra‐rare subtypes of MODY is being observed more often. 3 For the illustration, ultra‐rare forms of monogenic diabetes were detected in one proband in 2013 (2% of all genetically confirmed cases) compared with ten probands (19% of all genetically confirmed cases) in 2023 in the present study. Detection of ultra‐rare forms is particularly clinically relevant in cases of syndromic diabetes subtypes. 32 , 33

Differences, for example, in HbA1c or the proportion of non‐pharmacological treatment in probands with positive and negative genetic findings, which were further explained by the domination of GCK‐MODY, were consistent with its known pathophysiological mechanisms and clinical manifestations. 34 On the contrary, a significantly higher prevalence of MODY was observed in women compared with men. Fasting blood glucose testing is routinely performed in all pregnant women in the Czech Republic. Although the representation of women with GCK‐MODY among women with gestational diabetes is usually considered, 35 in our cohort, women outnumbered men, especially in the HNF1A‐MODY group. We speculate that blood glucose screening in pregnancy, as well as the greater overall health care services utilization among women compared with men, 36 may represent the main factors for this observation.

The people with no identified monogenic cause of diabetes represented the largest group, which was highly heterogeneous in terms of HbA1c, treatment and other parameters, as shown by our descriptive statistics. Moreover, a recent nationwide study from Japan described similar observations. 37 Therefore, we performed a cluster statistical analysis to identify possible clinically similar subgroups within this group. Five overlapping subgroups were identified (Figure 3). Those who were diagnosed with hyperglycaemia in childhood could be divided into a group with a GCK‐like phenotype (cluster A) and children with clinical type 1 diabetes without positive pancreatic autoantibodies (cluster B). The remaining three subgroups (C, D, E) consisted mainly of individuals in whom hyperglycaemia was detected in (early) adulthood and whose type of treatment more or less corresponded to their HbA1c level (i.e. at the highest HbA1c levels, treatment with a combination of insulin and oral antidiabetic drugs). Of note, we confirmed our previous observation that if a person with diabetes is found to have mild hyperglycaemia in adulthood, diet or treatment with oral antidiabetic drugs is more likely to be recommended than if mild hyperglycaemia is observed in childhood. 38 Taken together, it is likely that the negative group includes a number of different subtypes of glucose tolerance disorder, and further investigation is needed in the regulatory areas of known genes causing MODY, the search for possible new genes whose disruption causes MODY, but also focusing on possible pathophysiological variations of the major forms of diabetes (type 1 and type 2 diabetes). In support of this, common genetic variants predisposing to type 2 diabetes were found at a higher rate in individuals with presumed MODY without a detectable pathogenic variant compared with controls. 39

Our long‐term, real‐world study brings along several limitations. First, the clinical indications for genetic investigation of MODY change over time due to the evolving level of knowledge. Moreover, the nationwide registry of monogenic diabetes in the Czech Republic is dependent on the referrals from the attending health care professionals of people with diabetes, mainly diabetologists. The clinical indications were not always followed by all, and the clinical ‘intuition’ could also play a significant role in referral for testing. Thus, it is probable that an unknown proportion of people with diabetes, although fulfilling the current indication criteria, would not have been sent for genetic testing. However, the increasing referral rate with a stable detection rate documented the awareness of MODY in the Czech Republic. Second, the clinical questionnaires were not completed in full for all probands. Missing data may therefore have influenced the statistical analysis of the data. On the contrary, the percentage of missing data was approximately the same in all groups compared. Third, similarly to changes of indication criteria, the availability of genetic testing methods has markedly evolved during the 25‐year duration of the present study. It is crucial to state that not all of the study participants underwent the same genetic testing that could introduce bias in the results. Nevertheless, the three major causal genes accounted for the majority (>80%) of monogenic diabetes cases outside the first 6 months of age in all European populations studied. Moreover, DNA samples of those people with diabetes with strong clinical suspicion of MODY are being genetically re‐investigated after the introduction of novel techniques such as next‐generation sequencing, including samples from the Czech registry. Finally, in a certain number of individuals, the diagnosis of MODY was not confirmed in the laboratory of the authors of this study. However, according to our anonymous, unpublished questionnaire survey among Czech diabetologists, 2.5% of them sent samples from patients suspected of having a MODY to a laboratory other than ours, and 1% did not remember where they sent the samples. Therefore, we describe the minimum prevalence of MODY in the Czech Republic, noting that the actual prevalence is very likely to be higher for this reason as well.

In summary, the estimated minimal prevalence of MODY in the Czech Republic was 240 per one million inhabitants, with GCK‐MODY representing the most prevalent subtype. Despite 25 years of experience in examining Czech people with diabetes suspected to have MODY, approximately 44% of them have not yet been referred to genetic investigation, do not have a correctly determined form of diabetes and probably do not receive optimal treatment.
