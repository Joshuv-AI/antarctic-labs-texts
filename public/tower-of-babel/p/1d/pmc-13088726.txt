The impact of appearance anxiety on loneliness among college students: an integrative model
Authors: Jianbao Chang, Dehua Guo, Shiqin Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe widespread use of social media has made appearance anxiety increasingly prevalent among college students and has been linked with mental health concerns. While prior research has mostly emphasized its influence on self-perception, it has paid less attention to how such anxiety may foster loneliness through interpersonal mechanisms. Guided by self-discrepancy theory and social comparison theory, this study presents a serial mediation model to evaluate whether appearance anxiety is connected with loneliness via interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety, and whether these pathways differ by gender.MethodsThis study employed the Appearance Anxiety Scale-Brief Version, the Interpersonal Sensitivity Scale, the Interaction Anxiousness Scale, and the UCLA Loneliness Scale to measure appearance anxiety, interpersonal sensitivity, social anxiety, and loneliness among 1255 college students. Based on the data, we constructed a serial mediation model to test its validity and further analyzed gender differences within the model.ResultsAppearance anxiety increased loneliness both directly and through the serial mediation of interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety. Students reporting higher appearance anxiety tended to be more interpersonally sensitive, which was related to greater social anxiety, ultimately contributing to elevated loneliness. Multi-group comparisons revealed that female students had a greater predictive path from interpersonal sensitivity to social anxiety.ConclusionsThe results highlight gender-specific differences and show the serial mediating effects of social anxiety and interpersonal sensitivity in the relationship between appearance anxiety and loneliness in college students. This research provides theoretical implications for mental health interventions in higher education, with practical relevance for reducing appearance anxiety, improving social adaptation, and mitigating loneliness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04269-z.Keywords: Appearance anxiety, Loneliness, Interpersonal sensitivity, Social anxiety, Gender differences

Introduction

Loneliness is a distressing emotional experience that arises when individuals perceive their social relationships as insufficient to meet desired emotional needs [1]. Loneliness has drawn more attention as a public health issue across all age groups, especially among college students [2]. Due to social comparison, academic pressure, interpersonal transition difficulties, and frequently diminished family support after moving away from home, college students are particularly susceptible to loneliness. Empirical evidence indicates that loneliness is highly prevalent among college students in both China and Western countries [3–5]. Previous studies have demonstrated that loneliness among college students is associated with a number of detrimental consequences. These include depressive and anxiety symptoms [6, 7], elevated suicide risk [8], and social withdrawal [9]. These findings highlight the importance of identifying psychological factors related to loneliness and informing targeted mental health support in university settings.

Previous studies have linked loneliness to multiple psychological factors, such as negative emotional states, social anxiety, and perceived social support [10–12]. However, the ways in which these factors interact within a theoretically grounded, sequential cognitive-affective process remain insufficiently clarified, particularly among college students. Furthermore, evidence regarding gender differences in these pathways is limited, which may hinder a more comprehensive understanding of loneliness. Guided by self-discrepancy theory (SDT) and social comparison theory (SCT) [13, 14], the present study investigates how appearance anxiety contributes to loneliness through interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety. According to SDT, disparities between a person’s real self and ideal norms might cause negative feelings, which may exacerbate loneliness [15]. From the perspective of SCT, appearance anxiety prompts upward social comparisons, which are posited to first heighten interpersonal sensitivity, a cognitive-affective state of heightened vigilance and distress in social contexts. We theorize that this increased interpersonal sensitivity serves as a critical prerequisite, which in turn amplifies social anxiety, specifically the behavioral and emotional manifestation of fear in social situations. This social anxiety hinders social engagement, making individuals more prone to loneliness.

Accordingly, the findings suggest a major serial mediation paradigm in which loneliness is indirectly influenced by appearance anxiety through the sequential enhancement of social anxiety and interpersonal sensitivity. This model integrates SDT’s cognitive-emotional pathway and SCT’s explanation of interpersonal processes, revealing the dynamic process from appearance anxiety to loneliness. Gender differences in the extent and particular paths of this process are also taken into account. By evaluating this model, the study hopes to identify the fundamental causes of loneliness in college students and offer focused intervention techniques to lessen loneliness.

Theoretical framework and research hypotheses

Theoretical framework

According to SDT by Higgins [13], negative feelings like dissatisfaction and anxiety are caused by differences between the “actual self,” “ideal self,” and “ought self.” This theory has been widely applied in areas such as psychological health, teacher development, and online behavior [16–18]. In the context of appearance concerns, discrepancies between one’s perceived appearance and internalized standards may elicit appearance-related anxiety and undermine emotional well-being [19]. From this perspective, appearance anxiety reflects an internal self-evaluative process rooted in unmet psychological needs and self-discrepancies.

Festinger [14] introduced SCT. This theory has been applied in areas such as psychological health, social media use, and user behaviors [20, 21]. It complements this framework by emphasizing how individuals evaluate themselves through comparisons with others, particularly in socially salient domains such as physical appearance. Appearance-based upward comparisons may heighten vigilance toward social evaluation and increase interpersonal sensitivity [22], which in turn is associated with avoidance tendencies in social interactions [23]. These processes are especially salient in contemporary digital environments where appearance norms are highly visible.

By integrating SDT and SCT, the present study conceptualizes loneliness as emerging from the interplay between internal self-discrepancies and externally driven comparison processes. Specifically, appearance anxiety may be associated with loneliness through heightened interpersonal sensitivity and subsequent social anxiety, forming a sequential pathway that links self-evaluative concerns to social withdrawal.

Appearance anxiety and loneliness

Appearance anxiety, as defined by Cash [24], is the excessive concern about how others perceive one’s appearance. This anxiety is closely linked to loneliness, as college students with higher appearance anxiety often experience self-doubt and social discomfort, leading to reduced social engagement [11, 25]. SCT provides a framework for understanding this link, suggesting that individuals who compare their appearance to others often feel uncertain about their social standing, which can increase feelings of social disconnection and loneliness [14]. Societal pressure to conform to physical attractiveness standards further amplifies these comparisons, leaving students more vulnerable to negative evaluations and deepening their loneliness [22]. For instance, Papapanou et al. [11] found that appearance anxiety was positively associated with loneliness through social media use. Similarly, Kovan et al. [26] confirmed that social appearance anxiety significantly predicted loneliness among college students. These findings indicate that appearance-related concerns contribute to reduced social engagement and heightened loneliness. In light of this, the current study puts up the following theory:

H1: Appearance anxiety is positively associated with loneliness among college students.

H1: Appearance anxiety is positively associated with loneliness among college students.

The mediating role of interpersonal sensitivity

Interpersonal sensitivity is defined as a stable cognitive and affective tendency characterized by anticipatory anxiety, overinterpretation, and avoidance behaviors in response to perceived interpersonal rejection [27]. Prior studies have confirmed that interpersonal sensitivity correlates positively with loneliness [28–30]. For instance, Zhao et al. [28] found that interpersonal sensitivity significantly predicted higher levels of loneliness among college students. Similarly, Leighton and Rudenstine [29] found that higher interpersonal sensitivity was associated with greater loneliness, which may reflect heightened vigilance to social feedback and negative interpretations of interpersonal cues that reduce social engagement.

Empirical studies also indicate that appearance anxiety can intensify interpersonal sensitivity among college students [22, 31]. From the perspective of SDT [13], discrepancies between one’s actual appearance and internalized ideal standards may elicit appearance anxiety, which heightens vigilance to social evaluation and increases sensitivity to perceived rejection. Consistent with this view, Maftei [22] reported a positive association between appearance anxiety and interpersonal sensitivity. Although prior research has suggested that interpersonal sensitivity may show complex associations with anxiety related conditions in adult samples [32], limited research has examined interpersonal sensitivity as a mechanism linking appearance anxiety to loneliness, particularly among college students. To address this gap, the present study incorporates interpersonal sensitivity as a mediator to clarify how appearance anxiety may be associated with loneliness. Based on this reasoning, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2: Interpersonal sensitivity mediates the relationship between appearance anxiety and loneliness among college students.

H2: Interpersonal sensitivity mediates the relationship between appearance anxiety and loneliness among college students.

The mediating role of social anxiety

Social anxiety refers to anxious emotions that emerge in real or imagined social interactions when individuals anticipate negative evaluation by others [33]. According to earlier studies, social anxiety and loneliness are positively correlated [34–36]. College students, especially during identity formation, are sensitive to interpersonal acceptance, and social anxiety can lead to social avoidance, limiting their ability to form close relationships [37]. The transition to university life further increases these challenges. Students with social anxiety may avoid face-to-face interactions, turning to shallow online communication, which can worsen loneliness through social comparison [36]. According to research by O’Day and Heimberg [34], social anxiety causes social media addiction, which exacerbates loneliness. Similarly, Oren-Yagoda et al. [35] showed that social anxiety significantly contributes to loneliness.

Furthermore, there is a favorable correlation between appearance anxiety and social anxiety [38–40]. Students concerned about their appearance often engage in excessive self-monitoring and worry about others’ judgments [41]. Cultural pressures and social media amplify these concerns, leading to more social fear and avoidance [40]. Gao et al. [42] found a high correlation between appearance anxiety and social anxiety among Chinese undergraduates, suggesting that appearance stress could contribute to elevated social anxiety. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that social anxiety mediates the connection between loneliness and appearance anxiety [43, 44]. Mo and Bai [43] discovered that the relationship between loneliness and height dissatisfaction in teenagers was mediated by social anxiety. Based on this, the current study suggests that among college students, appearance anxiety and loneliness are mediated by social anxiety. Therefore, the following theory is proposed:

H3: Social anxiety mediates the relationship between appearance anxiety and loneliness among college students.

H3: Social anxiety mediates the relationship between appearance anxiety and loneliness among college students.

The serial mediation roles of interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety

Prior studies consistently indicate that interpersonal sensitivity positively relates to social anxiety [37, 45, 46]. For example, Li. et al. [47] reported that, interpersonal sensitivity is closely associated with social anxiety in the context of interpersonal stressors. From the perspectives of SDT and SCT, appearance anxiety reflects concerns arising from perceived self-discrepancies and unfavorable social comparisons, which are associated with heightened vigilance to social evaluation and greater interpersonal sensitivity [13, 14]. In turn, interpersonal sensitivity is defined by a cognitive orientation toward negative self-comparisons and social appraisal, and it is strongly linked to higher levels of social anxiety and a greater dread of negative judgment in academic contexts [46]. Persistent social anxiety is commonly associated with reduced social engagement and avoidance of interpersonal interactions, which are linked to increased feelings of loneliness [48]. Taken together, interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety can be understood as sequentially related cognitive and affective processes through which appearance anxiety is associated with loneliness. Based on the above theoretical and empirical considerations, the present study posits the following hypothesis:

H4: Interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety function as serial mediators in the relationship between appearance anxiety and loneliness among college students.

H4: Interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety function as serial mediators in the relationship between appearance anxiety and loneliness among college students.

Method

Sample and data collection

This study received approval from the Ethics Review Committee of Guangxi College for Preschool Education (Approval Number: GCPE-005, Approval Date: April 17, 2025). All procedures strictly followed the international ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were recruited between June and July 2025 from several universities located in Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region and neighboring provinces in southern China. Data were collected using the online survey platform Wenjuanxing. Faculty members at the participating universities distributed the survey link to undergraduate students through institutional communication channels. Participation was voluntary, and electronic informed consent was obtained prior to survey completion. Due to practical limitations, convenience sampling was used, which could restrict how broadly the results can be applied.

This study employed a 93-item instrument in accordance with the Kline [49] sample size guideline, which suggests at least 10 responders per questionnaire item. We chose 1,116 participants as the desired sample size, taking into account a 20% attrition rate. We sent out 1,300 questionnaires and got 1,300 answers in order to reach our goal. Two factors led to the exclusion of 45 responses during data cleaning. First, responses with over 20% missing answers were removed. Second, responses with more than 80% extreme responses were excluded. Choosing the strongest endpoint choice on a Likert scale—strongly agree or strongly disagree—was considered an excessive reaction. For reverse-scored items, responses were recoded prior to applying this criterion to ensure the judgment was based on the intensity of the expressed attitude. The 80% threshold was implemented to identify a potential response set, such as straight-lining, which is indicative of inattentive or non-genuine responding, consistent with established methods for identifying careless responses in survey data [50]. Such patterned responses can introduce significant bias like floor or ceiling effects and compromise data quality. In the end, the final analysis had 1,255 valid questions.

Measurement instruments

There are two sections to the scales used in this study: the first section gathers the respondents’ demographic data, and the second section contains the four constructs that were investigated in this study: loneliness, social anxiety, appearance anxiety, and interpersonal sensitivity.

Appearance anxiety

The Appearance Anxiety Scale-Brief Version, created by Keelan et al. [51], is used in this study to gauge an individual’s level of concern regarding their physical appearance. The scale has 14 items (four reverse-scored), rated from 1 (Never) to 5 (Always) on a Likert scale. Both positive and negative comments are covered, such as " I’m really happy with how I look” and “I feel nervous about my appearance.” Higher scores reflect greater appearance anxiety. The scale exhibits good reliability and validity in Chinese samples [42], with a Cronbach’s α of 0.948, composite reliability of 0.954, and average variance extracted (AVE) of 0.596. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) confirmed good model fit: ꭓ²/df = 1.918, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.994, TLI = 0.992, SRMR = 0.009, RMSEA = 0.027, [90% CI (0.020, 0.034)].

Interpersonal sensitivity

Boyce and Parker [52] created the 36-item Interpersonal Sensitivity Measure (IPSM), to evaluate emotional vulnerability, heightened sensitivity, and anxiety in social contexts. The measure has 36 items (such as “I feel upset when I say goodbye to people”) divided into five domains: interpersonal awareness, separation anxiety, weak self-esteem, need for approval, and timidity. A 4-point Likert scale, with 1 denoting “not at all true” and 4 denoting “very true,” is used to rate responses; higher totals suggest better interpersonal sensitivity. Previous studies have established the reliability and validity of the IPSM for use with Chinese college students [53]. In the current sample, the scale displayed strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.970), composite reliability = 0.972, and AVE = 0.516. CFA indicated satisfactory model fit: ꭓ²/df = 2.772, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.959, TLI = 0.956, SRMR = 0.005, RMSEA = 0.038 [90% CI (0.035, 0.040)].

Social anxiety

Leary [54] created the Interaction Anxiousness Scale (IAS), which consists of 15 items (five of which are reverse scored) intended to measure anxiety in this study, namely avoidance actions and fear of unfavorable appraisal (e.g., “I often feel nervous even in casual gatherings”). On a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not at all true”) to 5 (“very true”), participants scored their answers; higher scores denoted more social anxiety. The IAS has shown strong validity and reliability in Chinese student groups [55]. In this study, the scale yielded a Cronbach’s α of 0.955, a composite reliability of 0.959, and an AVE of 0.612. CFA supported good model fit: ꭓ²/df = 1.646, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.995, TLI = 0.995, SRMR = 0.008, RMSEA = 0.023 [90% CI (0.016, 0.029)].

Loneliness

The UCLA Loneliness Scale was used in this study to gauge participants’ subjective symptoms of loneliness [56]. The reverse-scored statement, “I feel in tune with the people around me,” is one of the 20 elements on the scale. There are eleven reverse-scored things. Each item is rated by participants on a 4-point scale, with 1 denoting “never” and 4 denoting “always.” Higher scores indicate more intense emotions of loneliness. The scale has proven reliable and valid among Chinese adolescents and young adults [57]. In this study, it showed a Cronbach’s α of 0.961, a composite reliability of 0.964, and an AVE of 0.575. CFA indicated good model fit: ꭓ²/df = 2.415, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.985, TLI = 0.984, SRMR = 0.004, RMSEA = 0.034 [90% CI (0.029, 0.038)].

The translation and cultural adaptation of all scales followed a standardized forward and back translation protocol. Initially, two bilingual experts, one specializing in mobile learning research and the other in applied linguistics, independently translated the original instruments into Chinese. Subsequently, two additional professionals, one with domain-specific knowledge and another with translation expertise, performed blind back-translation into English. The research team carefully compared the original and back-translated versions, resolving inconsistencies through iterative discussion until consensus was achieved. A preliminary Chinese version was pilot tested with 20 university students to enhance linguistic clarity and cultural relevance, and their feedback helped refine the version before finalization.

Data analysis

We used SPSS 27.0 to analyze the data. Given the observed non-normality in key variables, Spearman rank correlation, as a nonparametric method, was employed to examine bivariate relationships, as it does not assume normality and is robust to distributional violations [58]. Descriptive statistics including means and standard deviations were calculated. After using CFA to assess the measurement model’s fit and validate its validity, we used the PROCESS macro [59] to test the chain mediation model (Model 6), using 5,000 bootstrap samples to generate 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Effects were deemed significant if the CI excluded zero at a significance level of α = 0.05.

Common Method Bias (CMB)

Harman’s single factor test was employed in this study to look for common technique bias. Only 32.417% of the variance was explained by the greatest predictor, significantly below the 40% limit [60]. While these results suggest that CMB may not be a major concern, it is important to note that the test does not completely rule out the possibility of CMB affecting the findings. Therefore, the potential influence of common method bias should be interpreted with caution.

According to bootstrap analyses (Table 6), appearance anxiety had a significant indirect impact on loneliness through interpersonal sensitivity (effect = 0.075, 95% CI [0.040, 0.115]). The pattern of results aligns with the statistical phenomenon of suppression, also known as inconsistent mediation. The total effect was significantly positive, while the direct effect was non-significant and its point estimate was negative. This suggests that the significant positive indirect pathways fully explain the total relationship between appearance anxiety and loneliness. Accordingly, H2 and H4 were supported, whereas H3 was not supported.

Discussion

The direct connection between appearance and loneliness and anxiety

Among college students, appearance anxiety shows a positive relationship with loneliness, which confirms H1. This outcome echoes the findings of Papapanou et al. [11], who identified a similar link in young populations. According to SDT [13], appearance anxiety may enlarge the gap between one’s actual self and ideal self. When students perceive a discrepancy between their appearance and internal or external standards, they may experience shame or inferiority, which discourages social engagement and increases loneliness [22]. On a practical level, appearance anxiety often creates fears of negative evaluation, for instance being ridiculed, which leads students to reduce social contact [64]. In addition, the strong emphasis on appearance in online platforms such as social media feeds and short videos tends to distort normative standards. Students with high appearance anxiety may be especially vulnerable to upward social comparisons, which weakens their sense of belonging and reinforces loneliness [11]. These findings suggest that universities should provide targeted mental health programs, such as cognitive restructuring to reduce maladaptive appearance concerns, and cultivate a more tolerant campus climate to lessen the pressure of appearance-based comparison.

The interpersonal sensitivity mediating role

Interpersonal sensitivity mediated the relationship between appearance anxiety and loneliness, supporting H2. According to SDT, appearance anxiety often stems from discrepancies between the actual self and idealized standards, which heightens sensitivity to others’ evaluations and increases expectations of rejection [31]. This heightened interpersonal sensitivity may discourage social engagement and gradually intensify loneliness [13]. SCT further clarifies this mechanism [14]. In contemporary media-driven contexts, frequent upward comparisons with peers or influencers may amplify appearance dissatisfaction and fear of criticism, reinforcing a cycle of hypersensitivity and social withdrawal [31]. Individuals high in interpersonal sensitivity may also misinterpret neutral social cues as rejection, reducing their willingness to engage in authentic interactions [37]. These findings imply that universities may adopt a dual strategy: cognitive behavioral approaches to correct distorted self-perceptions and group counseling to provide supportive contexts for practicing social skills, thereby reducing both interpersonal hypersensitivity and loneliness.

The mediating role of social anxiety

H3 was not supported in this study, and social anxiety did not buffer the relationship between appearance anxiety and loneliness among college students. This differs from Mo and Bai [43], who identified a significant mediating role of social anxiety in adolescent samples. This discrepancy suggests potential developmental or population-specific variations in the pathway from appearance concerns to loneliness. From the standpoint of SDT [13], this theory suggests that appearance anxiety, which results from the perceived discrepancy between the real and ideal selves, may cause internalized anguish that does not immediately cause the fear of unfavorable social assessment that is at the heart of social anxiety. The pattern observed here aligns better with SCT [14]. According to this view, appearance anxiety drives upward comparisons, which first cultivate interpersonal sensitivity [65]. It is through this heightened sensitivity to social evaluation that appearance concerns subsequently amplify into broader social anxiety, which in turn exacerbates loneliness. Therefore, social anxiety operates not as a primary or independent conduit, but as a conditional, downstream intensifier within a sequential affective-cognitive process [66]. Its significant mediating role was evident only when it was preceded by interpersonal sensitivity in the serial mediation model.

The serial mediating role of interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety

This study supported H4 by confirming that the relationship between appearance anxiety and loneliness was serially mediated by interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety. This discovery is essential to comprehending the precise function of social anxiety in the model. From the perspective of SDT [13], appearance anxiety draws attention to the discrepancy between the real and ideal selves, resulting in emotions of inferiority and humiliation. These emotions first enhance interpersonal sensitivity, which then amplifies social anxiety, leading to social withdrawal and loneliness. SCT [14] further explains this mechanism, as frequent upward comparisons on social media intensify appearance dissatisfaction and promote anxious avoidance in social interactions. Over time, such avoidance patterns can solidify into feelings of loneliness [67]. The findings imply that social anxiety is a situational factor in this model. It is not an independent direct mediator of the relationship between appearance anxiety and loneliness but rather an emotional and behavioral response activated after interpersonal sensitivity in the cognitive-affective sequence [66]. This specific conditional mechanism accounts for, and provides a clear explanation for, the rejection of H3. In summary, this study delineates a sequential affective-cognitive pathway where interpersonal sensitivity is a critical precursor that activates social anxiety, which in turn leads to loneliness. This refined model clarifies the conditional role of social anxiety and underscores the need for interventions that systematically target this specific sequence to disrupt the cycle from appearance concerns to social isolation.

Multi-group analysis

Multi-group analysis revealed a high and positive correlation between interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety in both the male and female groups. Notably, this association was stronger among female students. According to SCT [14], individuals evaluate their self-worth through social comparison. High interpersonal sensitivity female college students may be more sensitive to social criticism and outside assessments, especially in appearance-focused online settings, which could exacerbate anxiety. In addition, gender role socialization often emphasizes relational awareness and emotional attunement among females, increasing their susceptibility to interpersonal stressors [31]. In contrast, male students may be more likely to repress their emotions or avoid receiving critical feedback due to traditional gender role expectations, which may somewhat mitigate the link between social anxiety and interpersonal sensitivity [68]. Overall, these findings underscore the necessity of including gender-sensitive considerations into psychological therapies aiming at lowering social anxiety associated with interpersonal sensitivity.

Implications

Based on SDT and SCT, this study constructed a mediation model to rigorously analyze the pathways connected to loneliness among college students. The findings demonstrated the direct and indirect connections between appearance anxiety and loneliness, as well as the mediating roles of social anxiety and interpersonal sensitivity. These results not only extend the research boundaries of SDT and SCT but also provide valuable insights for addressing loneliness among college students.

Theoretical implications

First, this study integrates SDT and SCT to explain loneliness by linking self-discrepancies with social comparison processes. From an SDT perspective, appearance anxiety reflects discrepancies between the actual and ideal self, which frustrate basic psychological needs and increase vulnerability to loneliness. SCT further explains how upward appearance-based comparisons, especially in digital contexts, intensify interpersonal vigilance and avoidance, thereby translating appearance anxiety into loneliness.

Second, this study advances existing loneliness models by empirically distinguishing parallel and serial mediation pathways. Specifically, interpersonal sensitivity emerged as a key proximal mechanism linking appearance anxiety to loneliness, while the serial pathway through interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety further explains how cognitive hypersensitivity evolves into behavioral avoidance. This layered process highlights that loneliness is not merely an outcome of social anxiety but may originate earlier at the level of interpersonal threat perception, thereby refining theoretical understandings of the temporal ordering among these constructs.

Finally, by incorporating multi-group analysis, this study extends theoretical discussions of gender differences in emotional socialization within the SDT–SCT framework. The stronger association between interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety among female students suggests that gendered patterns of social evaluation and emotional processing shape vulnerability pathways to loneliness. This result emphasizes how crucial it is to include gender as a moderating factor in theoretical models of loneliness and distress related to appearance rather than just using it as a demographic control variable.

Practical implications

The current findings offer a number of specific and practical intervention techniques to lessen the detrimental correlations between appearance anxiety and loneliness among college students. First, given the direct association between appearance anxiety and loneliness, campus counseling programs can incorporate structured cognitive-behavioral modules that specifically target appearance-related automatic thoughts. For example, counselors may help students identify distorted beliefs about physical appearance and engage in cognitive reframing through written thought records or brief interventions grounded in cognitive behavioral therapy. Behavioral role-play exercises, such as simulated peer interactions or mock presentations, may further help students translate self-acceptance into active social engagement.

Second, given that the indirect pathway involving interpersonal sensitivity and subsequent social anxiety showed a relatively larger effect size than the pathway through social anxiety alone, interventions targeting interpersonal sensitivity may represent a higher priority for reducing loneliness. Because interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety jointly mediate the association between appearance anxiety and loneliness, interventions should address both cognitive bias and behavioral avoidance. At the cognitive level, group-based psychoeducation sessions can help students recognize and challenge hypersensitivity to perceived rejection. At the emotional regulation level, mindfulness-based stress reduction or brief acceptance-based training can be used to reduce anxiety reactivity in social situations. At the behavioral level, universities can create low-risk social practice opportunities, such as facilitated peer support groups, cooperative learning tasks, or structured extracurricular activities that encourage gradual exposure to social interaction in supportive settings.

Lastly, the gender disparities that have been found point to the necessity of gender-sensitive intervention strategies. For female students, programs may emphasize cognitive restructuring to reduce over-interpretation of social feedback and strengthen emotional regulation skills. For male students, interventions may focus on promoting emotional expression, reducing avoidance of vulnerability, and encouraging help-seeking behaviors, thereby counteracting tendencies toward emotional suppression. Tailoring intervention components to these gender-specific patterns may enhance the effectiveness of loneliness prevention programs on college campuses.

Limitations and future directions

This study sheds light on the relationship between appearance anxiety and loneliness. However, it is important to recognize a few restrictions. First, people were chosen by convenience sampling, which may limit the findings’ generalizability to different age groups, educational environments, or cultural backgrounds. Future studies should include more diverse populations, such as older adults or other socially vulnerable groups, as well as samples from different cultural or national contexts, to enhance external validity. Second, the cross-sectional design constrains causal interpretation and prevents conclusions about the temporal ordering of appearance anxiety, interpersonal sensitivity, social anxiety, and loneliness. Future research could adopt multi-wave longitudinal designs to examine whether appearance anxiety prospectively predicts subsequent changes in interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety, which in turn lead to changes in loneliness across time. In addition, intervention-based or randomized controlled studies could test whether reducing appearance-related cognitive biases or interpersonal sensitivity through targeted programs results in measurable decreases in social anxiety and loneliness. Third, although standardized translation and cultural adaptation procedures were employed, pilot testing was conducted with a relatively small sample, which may limit the robustness of preliminary validation. Finally, response bias like CMB may be introduced if self-report measurements are relied upon. Harman’s single-factor test did not completely rule out the possibility of CMB, even though it indicated that it was unlikely to have a significant impact on the results. To better evaluate and reduce CMB, future studies may use multi-source data or sophisticated statistical methods. A more thorough evaluation of social and emotional processes might also be achieved by include behavioral markers, peer reports, or experimental tasks.

Conclusion

Drawing on SDT and SCT, this study proposed a mediation model and tested it with data from 1,255 college students. The findings demonstrated that appearance anxiety was positively related to loneliness. Mediation analysis further indicated that interpersonal sensitivity and social anxiety independently mediated this relationship and formed a serial mediation pathway, whereby appearance anxiety increased interpersonal sensitivity, which heightened social anxiety and, in turn, loneliness. Multi-group analysis further showed significant gender differences in the pathway from interpersonal sensitivity to social anxiety. These findings provide new insights into the psychological underpinnings of loneliness among college students and widen the applicability of self-discrepancy and social comparison theories. Given the limited sample and reliance on self-reports, future studies should include more diverse groups and cross-validate the model with multiple data sources.
