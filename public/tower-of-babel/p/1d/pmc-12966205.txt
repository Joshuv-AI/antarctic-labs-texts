Reduced dose versus standard melphalan conditioning for autologous stem cell transplantation in multiple myeloma: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Yifan Yao, Shan Zhang, Ziying Li, Lili Luo, Xuan Lu, Huafang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This retrospective, single‑center study evaluated the efficacy and safety of a reduced‑dose melphalan conditioning regimen (140–180 mg/m2) for autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) in patients with multiple myeloma (MM) ineligible for the standard dose (200 mg/m2). Clinical outcomes were compared between patients receiving reduced‑dose versus standard‑dose melphalan over a five-year period, with pre‑specified subgroup analyses based on age and renal function. The results demonstrated that despite less favorable baseline characteristics in the reduced-dose group, both overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) met comparable criteria relative to the standard group. The reduced-dose regimen was associated with a significantly improved safety profile, characterized by lower rates of mucositis, fewer febrile episodes, and accelerated platelet engraftment. In conclusion, for MM patients unable to tolerate standard-dose conditioning, particularly those with compromised baseline status, a reduced-dose melphalan regimen offers a clinically effective and safer alternative that maintains therapeutic efficacy while reducing treatment-related toxicity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-026-06909-1.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) remains an incurable hematologic malignancy that imposes substantial healthcare utilization and economic burden [1, 2]. In this time of new agents, autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) continues to be the standard of care for transplant-eligible patients with MM. Evidence from phase II/III clinical trials including FORTE [3], DETERMINATION [4], EMN02/HOVON-95 [5], and IFM2009 [6, 7] demonstrates that ASCT provides superior outcomes compared to chemotherapy-only regimens in terms of progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and minimal residual disease (MRD) negativity.

For patients with normal renal function, the standard pre-transplantation conditioning regimen for ASCT remains melphalan 200 mg/m2 (Mel200). This dose has demonstrated proven superiority over both 280 mg/m2 and 100 mg/m2 dosing regimens [8, 9]. Although various conditioning regimens have been investigated, such as high-dose melphalan plus bortezomib [10], 8 Gy total body irradiation plus 140 mg/m2 melphalan [11], and busulfan and cyclophosphamide [12], none have shown a superior comprehensive profile in efficacy or safety relative to melphalan monotherapy [13].

However, for elderly or comorbid patients, the toxicity of the standard dose often renders it unsuitable, and there remains a lack of clear standards for reduced-intensity conditioning prior to transplantation. Melphalan at 140 mg/m2 (MEL140) is broadly adopted in clinical treatment, but studies on its efficacy have yielded inconsistent results. A post-hoc subgroup analysis of two randomized controlled trials (RCTs) by Straka et al. [14] suggested that cumulative melphalan dose affects both OS and PFS after transplantation. Several retrospective studies have reported inconsistent results about the impact of reduced-dose melphalan on prognosis. For instance, the study by Brioli et al. [15] indicated that MEL200 benefits longer OS and PFS. Sharma et al. [16] found that although dose reduction might reduce toxicity, it could compromise OS. Conversely, Auner et al. [17] and Srour et al. [18] observed no significant differences in OS or PFS between MEL140 and MEL200. More recently, retrospective analyses by Brown et al. [19] and Kort et al. [20] confirmed comparability between the MEL140 and MEL200 regimens in terms of efficacy, toxicity, or even rates of MRD negativity.

Based on current evidence and clinical experience regarding melphalan dose reduction, our center has adopted a stepwise dose reduction strategy. Guided by patient-specific factors, including age, organ function, and comorbidities, an appropriate reduced dose is selected from three predefined levels: 180 mg/m2 (MEL180), 160 mg/m2 (MEL160), and 140 mg/m2 (MEL140). Given the persistent uncertainty surrounding the impact of dose reduction on transplantation outcomes, this study aims to evaluate the safety and efficacy of these different melphalan dose levels in MM patients undergoing ASCT, thereby providing an evidence-based foundation for individualized conditioning therapy.

Methods

Patient selection and study design

157 MM patients who received ASCT at our center were enrolled in this retrospective study from January 2019 until June 2024. Data were collected retrospectively up to the end of follow-up on June 30, 2025, including diagnosis, prior therapies, pre-transplant disease status, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score, stem cell mobilization, conditioning regimens, survival outcomes, and relapse. The ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. This study is in accordance with ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all procedures compliant with the ethical standards.

Treatment strategy

All patients received melphalan (Evomela®) as the sole preparative regimen, with the cohort split by the actual administered dose into the standard-dose group (200 mg/m2) and the reduced-dose group (140–180 mg/m2). Specifically, the reduced-dose group followed a stepwise dose adjustment protocol with three levels: 180 mg/m2 (MEL 180), 160 mg/m2 (MEL 160), and 140 mg/m2 (MEL 140).

The individualized melphalan dosage was determined through a multidimensional clinical assessment. The primary evaluation framework incorporated four core parameters: age, chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage, ECOG score, and the Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation–Comorbidity Index (HCT‑CI). Additional considerations included the depth of pre‑transplant disease remission, the presence of high‑risk cytogenetic abnormalities, and active infection status. Within this framework, the final dose was jointly decided by two clinicians, with no single factor being exclusively determinative. Specifically, doses were titrated upward for patients with high‑risk cytogenetics or suboptimal responses (failure to achieve VGPR) to induction therapy, and downward for those with severe active infections. Based on this comprehensive evaluation, patients were stratified into four conditioning intensity levels: standard dose (MEL200), mild reduction (MEL180), moderate reduction (MEL160), or significant reduction (MEL140).

Prior to transplantation, all patients received standard preparatory care in laminar airflow rooms, including hydration, urine alkalinization, prostaglandin E administration, cytomegalovirus (CMV) prophylaxis, and antifungal prophylaxis [21, 22]. Melphalan was administered as a single intravenous infusion, with the time interval to stem cell reinfusion individually adjusted under the patient’s clinical assessment. The reinfusion procedure was performed within a standardized window of 24 to 72 h following the completion of melphalan infusion [23–25].

After transplantation, all patients received standardized and individualized supportive care, including intravenous immunoglobulin (total dose 20 g). Throughout the treatment course, additional supportive measures, such as thrombopoietin (TPO), granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), and blood component transfusions, were administered as needed, guided by the patients’ hematological parameters. Anti-infective therapy was also provided according to the patient’s symptoms and infection markers. Following successful engraftment, maintenance therapy was initiated according to individualized risk stratification.

Related definitions

Neutrophil engraftment was designated as the initial occurrence of three consecutive days with an absolute neutrophil count at or above 0.5 × 109/L. Platelet engraftment was characterized as the first day from which a platelet count exceeding 20 × 109/L was sustained for seven straight days in the absence of transfusion support. In this study, relevant scoring systems, line of therapy, efficacy evaluation, and treatment-related toxicities in MM patients undergoing ASCT were all determined according to the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) criteria. Renal impairment was assessed based on the pre-transplant glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and classified using the chronic kidney disease (CKD) staging criteria. High-risk cytogenetic abnormalities were defined as the presence of one or more of the following: (t(4;14), t(14;16), t(14;20), del(17p), TP53 mutation, or gain of 1q21). Extramedullary disease was defined as the presence of plasmacytomas or myeloma cell infiltrates in organs or tissues outside the bone marrow [26–28].

Statistical analysis

We performed all statistical analyses utilizing the software packages SPSS 27.0, R 4.3.3, and GraphPad Prism 9.5. Descriptive statistics are presented as medians and ranges. Group comparisons involved the Pearson chi-square test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon test for continuous measures. OS was measured from transplantation to any-cause death or last follow-up, and PFS to disease progression or death. Kaplan-Meier estimates were generated for survival probabilities, with log-rank testing for group differences. Identification of independent prognostic factors for OS and PFS was accomplished through a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model. Statistical significance was defined as a p value of < 0.05. As this is a retrospective observational study comparing outcomes between groups, it was not designed or powered as a formal non-inferiority trial.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

157 patients with MM who underwent ASCT were recruited and stratified into two groups: a reduced-dose (n = 57) and a standard-dose (n = 100) group. In the reduced-dose group, there were 24 patients receiving MEL140, 15 receiving MEL160, and 18 receiving MEL180. The baseline characteristics of the two cohorts are presented in Table 1, with detailed characteristics of the reduced-dose group provided in Table S1. Baseline characteristics were comparable between groups, with the exception of two significant differences. Patients in the reduced-dose group demonstrated a significantly older age distribution, with a higher proportion aged ≥ 60 years at transplantation compared to the standard-dose group (31.6% vs. 15.0%; p = 0.014). Specifically, among patients aged ≥ 65 years, those in the MEL140 subgroup accounted for 10.5% (with the oldest patient being 72 years old), whereas in the MEL200 subgroup this proportion was 5.0% (with the oldest patient being 67 years old). Renal function also varied notably: although no patients in either group had stage 4–5 renal impairment, the reduced-dose group had a higher combined prevalence of stage 2 (28.1% vs. 13.0%) and stage 3 (10.5% vs. 7.0%) renal impairment (overall p = 0.035).

Stem cell mobilization, collection, and transplant procedure

Stem cell mobilization was uniformly conducted with G-CSF. Adjunct mobilization agents were utilized in a subset of patients, including plerixafor (19.7%), etoposide (19.1%), and cyclophosphamide (7.0%). Following melphalan conditioning, autologous stem cells were infused within 0–2 days. The CD34⁺ cell infusion doses were comparable between two groups, with median values of 5.91 × 106/kg (reduced-dose group) and 5.72 × 106/kg (standard-dose group), respectively (p = 0.923).

Treatment-related toxicity

Regarding hematologic toxicity, a significantly shorter platelet engraftment time was demonstrated in the reduced-dose group against the standard-dose group (median 12 vs. 13 days, p = 0.038). However, no significant differences were observed between the two cohorts in terms of time to neutrophil engraftment, rates of engraftment failure, or transfusion requirements for red blood cells and platelets during hospitalization. While all patients experienced non-hematologic toxicities of varying grades, the overall incidence was lower in the reduced-dose group. This difference reached statistical significance for three specific adverse events: fever (65.0% vs. 82.0%, p = 0.016), oral mucositis (31.6% vs. 52.0%, p = 0.013), and diarrhea (28.1% vs. 52.0%, p = 0.004). The incidence of other types of non-hematologic toxicities has not shown statistically significant differences in the two cohorts (Table 2). The main treatment-related toxicity of the reduced-dose group is presented in Table S1.

Survival and recurrence

The day 100 post-transplant evaluation showed generally similar treatment responses in both groups. Specifically, 145 patients (92.4%) across both cohorts had achieved a response of very good partial response (VGPR) or better status (92.0% in the standard-dose group vs. 93.0% in the reduced-dose group, p = 0.824). The rate of conversion from MRD-positive to negative after treatment was also similar between the two groups (80.0% in the standard-dose group vs. 80.0% in the reduced-dose group, p = 1.000). After a median follow-up of 25.37 months, the median OS had not been reached in either group, with no statistically significant difference observed (p = 0.197; Fig. 1A). The estimated 2-year OS rate was 0.886 (95% CI: 0.803–0.977) in the reduced-dose group and 0.889 (95% CI: 0.822–0.962) in the standard-dose group.

Multivariate Cox regression analysis

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to investigate independent prognostic factors in OS and PFS. In the multivariate analysis for OS, older age (HR 3.162, 95% CI: 1.234–8.100; p = 0.016), high-risk cytogenetics (HR 3.366, 95% CI: 1.361–8.325; p = 0.009), and renal function stage ≥ 3 (HR 4.493, 95% CI: 1.458–13.847; p = 0.009) were independent adverse factors. For PFS, high-risk cytogenetics (HR 4.278, 95% CI: 2.001–9.147; p < 0.001), and positive MRD status (HR 4.785, 95% CI: 1.361–16.822; p = 0.015) emerged as independent risk factors. However, the melphalan dose wasn’t the influential factor for OS (p = 0.204) or PFS (p = 0.725) (Table 3). In subgroup analyses of patients with MRD-positive status or high-risk cytogenetic abnormalities, no significant differences in OS and PFS were observed across the conditioning intensity groups (Figure S1). Similarly, within the reduced dose cohort, OS and PFS did not differ significantly among the MEL140, MEL160, and MEL180 subgroups (Figure S2).

Discussion

In clinical practice, reduced-dose melphalan is frequently utilized as a conditioning regimen for elderly or comorbid MM patients undergoing ASCT [29–32]. However, its long-term prognostic impact remains incompletely defined. This study evaluated the efficacy and safety of an individualized stepwise reduced-dose melphalan regimen (140–180 mg/m2) as conditioning for ASCT. The findings indicate that this approach maintained comparable survival outcomes to the standard dose, demonstrated consistent efficacy across subgroups stratified by age and renal function, and was associated with a significantly improved safety profile.

Although the sample size limited subgroup comparisons among the specific reduced-dose tiers (MEL140, MEL160, MEL180), the reduced-dose cohort (140–180 mg/m²) overall achieved comparable OS and PFS. This is consistent with a growing body of recent retrospective evidence. Studies by Auner et al. [17] (MEL140, n = 245; MEL200, n = 1719; OS: p = 0.73; PFS: p = 0.57) ,Srour et al. [18] (MEL140, n = 76; MEL200, n = 621; OS: p = 0.17; PFS: p = 0.50), Brown et al. [19] (MEL140, n = 240; MEL200, n = 82; Relapse: p = 0.3661; PFS: p = 0.2335), and Kort et al. [20] (MEL140, n = 82; MEL200, n = 151; OS: p = 0.3; PFS: p = 0.31) all confirmed that melphalan dose (MEL140) was not associated with prognosis. These collective findings align with our results, suggesting that reduced-intensity conditioning might not compromise intermediate-term survival outcomes in this patient population.

Conversely, some studies have reported a significant impact of melphalan dose. Straka et al. [14] (MEL140, n = 124; MEL200, n = 216) found that the cumulative melphalan dose affected both OS (p = 0.019) and PFS (p = 0.0141) in overall cohort level. In the bortezomib consolidation treatment group, there was no statistically significant difference in PFS or OS, whereas in the observation group, MEL200 still showed significantly better PFS (p = 0.0015) and OS (p = 0.0315) than MEL100/140. This key finding suggests that effective post‑ASCT consolidation therapy may mitigate the potential negative impact of dose reduction, offering a rationale for dose adjustment in patients planned for such therapy. Although the study designs differ, both their work and ours support the overarching principle that overall treatment strategy, including both conditioning and subsequent therapies, constitutes a modifiable and compensatory framework.

The study by Brioli [15] (MEL200, n = 191; MELRed, n = 122) also found that the MEL200 group demonstrated significantly longer OS (p = 0.001) and PFS (p < 0.001). Similarly, the study by Sharma et al. [16](MEL140, n = 127; MEL200, n = 803) indicated a link between melphalan dose and OS (p = 0.04). Notably, after adjustment for baseline imbalances, the association between melphalan dose and OS or PFS was attenuated to varying degrees in both studies. Brioli et al. [15] suggested that MEL200 associated merely with prolonged PFS, while Sharma et al. [16] found that MEL200 continued to demonstrate a survival benefit only in younger patients with normal renal function and poor response to induction therapy.

The discrepancy between their adjusted results and our null finding for dose effect may be attributed to our prospective, multi-parameter dose-selection strategy. By individually tailoring the dose within the 140–180 mg/m2 range based on age, CKD stage, and comorbidity scores, we may have better optimized the efficacy-toxicity balance for each patient, allowing those with poorer baseline status to achieve outcomes comparable to the standard-dose group. Furthermore, the profound baseline disparities (older age, worse renal function, more comorbidities) in the reduced-dose groups of earlier studies may exert a residual confounding effect on long-term outcomes that is difficult to fully eliminate through statistical adjustment.

In the contemporary management of MM, advanced age, high-risk cytogenetics, and renal impairment are well-established adverse prognostic factors for OS, while high-risk cytogenetics, extramedullary disease, and pre-transplant MRD positivity reliably predict shorter PFS [14–20]. The role of melphalan dose intensity as an independent prognostic factor remains ambiguous. The conflicting results across studies, including those identifying it [14, 15] as significant and those refuting it [17–20], likely stem from heterogeneity in treatment eras, evolving supportive care, patient selection criteria for dose reduction, and importantly, whether dose reduction was applied uniformly or through an individualized, risk-adapted approach as in our study.

Regarding safety, our findings corroborate earlier reports [15, 17–19]. The reduced-dose regimen significantly lowered the incidence of key non-hematologic toxicities, particularly oral mucositis and fever, attributable to decreased systemic drug exposure. The earlier platelet engraftment observed in our reduced-dose cohort, a finding not commonly emphasized, may be influenced by center-specific supportive care protocols. Crucially, as melphalan is renally cleared, dose reduction in patients with pre-existing renal impairment offers a logical pharmacokinetic rationale for renal protection [33, 34], potentially explaining the comparable post-ASCT renal injury rates between our groups despite worse baseline renal function in the reduced-dose cohort.

Our study has limitations. Its retrospective design introduces potential selection bias and unmeasured confounding. The median follow-up of 25.37 months is relatively short for assessing long-term survival in MM. The sample size within each reduced-dose tier precluded a powered comparison of outcomes across MEL140, MEL160, and MEL180. Furthermore, although our dose-selection strategy is structured around core clinical parameters, it operates as a qualitative clinical tool without a fully validated quantitative scoring system. This methodological limitation may constrain its generalizability across treatment centers and necessitates prospective validation in future studies.

In conclusion, an individualized, stepwise reduced-dose melphalan regimen (140–180 mg/m2) for ASCT conditioning achieved similar survival outcomes while significantly reducing treatment-related toxicity compared to the standard dose. Prospective studies are warranted to validate the prognostic implications of stratified dose reduction and to explore its impact in broader patient populations and in combination with modern consolidation and maintenance therapies.
