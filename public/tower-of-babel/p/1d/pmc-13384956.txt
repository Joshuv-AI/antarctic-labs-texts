Efficacy of vascularized lymph node transfer for lower extremity lymphedema: A systematic review and meta-analysis of 395 patients from 25 peer-reviewed studies
Authors: Pushti Shah, Bruno Pillari, Nicole Margiotta, Nitya Devisetti, Alex K. Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackground:Lower extremity lymphedema (LEL) is a debilitating disease causing limb enlargement secondary to fluid accumulation. Although vascularized lymph node transfer (VLNT) has emerged as a promising option for refractory cases, data are limited by small samples and inconclusive results. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to assess metrics of symptom improvement after VLNT and the impact of donor sites on outcomes.Methods:This study was performed per the PRISMA guidelines and registered on PROSPERO (#CRD420251243270). The PubMed, Scopus, and CENTRAL databases were searched until February 2026. Studies on lower limb VLNT were included and combined techniques were excluded. Outcomes included circumference reduction rate (CRR), limb volume, and complications. Descriptive statistics, chi-square, and meta-analyses were performed. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle Ottawa and Joanna Briggs Institute ScalesResults:Twenty five studies were included and comprised 395 unique patients (70.28% female). Above and below knee CRR were 24.79% and 29.50% with high reduction in flaps using the gastroepiploic donor site (p = 0.002, p < 0.001, respectively). Limb volume was decreased by 25.32% and cellulitis incidence was significantly decreased postoperatively (p < 0.001). Adverse events were low at 12.22%.Conclusion:VLNT is a safe and effective technique for LEL treatment with substantial CRR, limb volume reduction, and cellulitis incidence. Outcomes were significantly improved with gastroepiploic flaps, suggesting that they should be most used for lymphedema treatment. Limitations include potential reporting bias and retrospective nature of studies. Future prospective studies should further evaluate outcomes based on different donor and recipient sites.

Lymphedema is a chronic, progressive disorder characterized by impaired lymphatic transport, interstitial fluid accumulation, inflammation, and fibroadipose tissue deposition, ultimately leading to limb enlargement and long-term functional decline.1 Lower extremity lymphedema in particular presents unique therapeutic challenges relative to upper extremity disease, including greater fibrosis, massive limb enlargement, and high cellulitis burden.2,3 Among patients with extremity lymphedema, chronic swelling, recurrent infections, and venous comorbidities have been well described.4 Etiologies of LEL include oncologic lymph node dissection or radiation, trauma, infection, and congenital lymphatic dysplasia. As disease progresses, conservative therapy becomes increasingly ineffective owing to progressive fibrosis and structural lymphatic damage, prompting interest in reconstructive approaches to help restore physiologic lymphatic function.5,6

Conservative management of lymphedema includes use of compression garments, manual lymphatic drainage, complete decongestive therapy, and pneumatic compression. However, these treatments primarily provide symptomatic relief without correcting the underlying lymphatic dysfunction.7 Complete decongestive therapy is considered the gold standard nonoperative treatment and consists of two phases with the first phase aimed at mobilizing increased fluid and reducing volume and second phase encompassing patient long-term management to maintain therapeutic results such as compression garments.8–10 Several patients with longstanding or severe LEL are refractory to conservative therapies and experience persistent swelling and cellulitis, necessitating operative techniques such as lymphovenous bypass or lymphaticovenular anastomosis (LVA), vascularized lymph node transfer (VLNT), and combined techniques. LVA involves creating a shunt between higher pressure lymphatic vessels and low pressure veins to facilitate limb drainage, leading to symptom improvement in 23.3–100% of patients.11–13 LVA can be effective in early-stage disease with identifiable, functional lymphatic channels, but its benefits diminish in advanced stages of lymphedema characterized by lymphatic fibrosis or obstruction.13,14 VLNT involves transplanting functional lymphatic tissue to re-establish lymphatic drainage pathways and improving regional immune function (Figure 1).15,16

Lower extremity disease is often more refractory to conservative therapy and may respond less predictably to physiologic microsurgical procedures. VLNT has become an increasingly used strategy for advanced or treatment-resistant LEL, demonstrating the growing recognition of the need for physiologic reconstruction in this population. Despite this, existing evidence is limited by small sample sizes, variable follow-up, and inconsistent reporting across studies. Furthermore, no consensus exists regarding expected circumference and volume reduction, optimal donor site selection, and safety profiles. Current studies in the literature overwhelmingly focus on upper extremity lymphedema and have evaluated the impact of different donor sites, optimal treatment algorithms, rehabilitation, and optimal imaging for surgical planning.3,17–20 LEL outcomes in prior studies are limited by the lack of patient-reported outcomes and reporting circumference reduction and volume reduction rates as a combined value rather than specific metrics.21,22 The purpose of this study was to evaluate the clinical effectiveness and safety of vascularized lymph node transfer for lower extremity lymphedema using precise clinical outcome metrics to establish more standardized benchmarks for VLNT in lower extremity lymphedema and guide patient selection, surgical planning, and perioperative counseling.

Methods

Search strategy

This study has been performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and registered a priori on PROSPERO (#CRD420251243270). A search was conducted on the PubMed, Scopus, and CENTRAL databases for studies published up to February 2026 on VLNT performed for lower extremity lymphedema (Table 1).

Inclusion criteria

Screening of studies was performed by two independent reviewers to include randomized controlled trials, prospective cohort studies, retrospective cohort studies, and case series (> 3 patients) on VLNT for lower extremity lymphedema of any etiology. Only studies with patient outcomes were included, whereas technique papers, reviews, book chapters, and conference abstracts were excluded. Studies without human subjects or those using cadavers were excluded. Studies with upper extremity lymphedema patients without individual lower extremity outcomes were excluded. Studies with combined techniques such as VLNT with lymphovenous anastomoses or any other combination with either physiologic or excisional procedures were excluded. Papers not in English were excluded.

Extractions and outcomes measures

Data were extracted independently by two separate reviewers, with a third reviewer resolving discrepancies. Demographics, etiology of lymphedema, and duration of symptoms were collected. Additionally, surgical information including donor site, recipient site, number of lymph node transfers, and laterality (unilateral or bilateral) were collected. The primary outcomes of interest included the circumference reduction ratio (CRR) and volume reduction. Secondary outcomes included imaging outcomes, cellulitis incidence, patient-reported outcomes, and adverse events. Outcomes were collected at the final follow-up.

Risk of bias

Risk of bias was assessed by two independent reviewers using the Newcastle Ottawa scale (NOS) and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for case series. Studies were deemed a cohort study if both arms included patients with LEL. Low risk of bias was established as a score of 7 or higher out of 9 on the NOS and 8 or higher out of 10 on the JBI scale. High risk of bias was established as a score of 3 or lower on the NOS and 4 or lower on the JBI scale. Studies with a high risk of bias were excluded from this review.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize results with data reported as weighted mean ± weighted standard deviation for continuous variables and n (%) for dichotomous variables. Student t-test and ANOVA were used for continuous variables and the Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used as appropriate for dichotomous variables. For articles with overlapping patient populations, descriptive statistics and meta-analyses were performed using the article with a greater patient population. Meta-analysis was performed on R Studios using a random effects model for variables with three or more included papers. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed for two arm meta-analyses with heterogeneity > 75%. Funnel plots were prepared for all meta-analyses to assess publication bias (Supplementary digital content 1–3). A p-value < 0.05 was determined as significant.

Results

Study characteristics

A total of 501 studies were found after the initial search on the PubMed, Scopus, and CENTRAL databases with 305 articles included in title-abstract screening. Among those, 101 articles were screened for full text eligibility of which 25 articles were deemed appropriate to be included in this review (Figure 2).4,5,16,23–44 All studies were retrospective in nature and consisted of 3 cohort studies and 22 case series. Risk of bias was found to be low in all studies (Tables 2 and 3). Average study follow-up time was 26.1 ± 13.1 months.

Patient demographics

A total of 407 unique patients were included in this review, 70.28% of them were female, and had a mean age and BMI of 53.4 ± 6.54 years and 26.6 ± 1.49 kg/m2, respectively. Etiologies of lymphedema were predominantly secondary, with 314 (92.4%) patients compared to 26 (7.65%) with primary lymphedema. Secondary causes included 148 (47.1%) patients with gynecological cancer, 15 (4.78%) patients with urological cancer, 5 (1.59%) with trauma, and 27 (8.60%) other causes. Causes of secondary lymphedema were unspecified in 119 (37.9%) patients. Average duration of symptoms prior to VLNT was 6.60 ± 2.84 years. Flap donor sites were submental for 143 (55.0%) patients, gastroepiploic for 77 (29.6%), supraclavicular for 19 (7.31%), and other sites for 21 (8.08%) patients. Flap recipient site was primarily in the ankle or distal lower limb (N = 217, 81.6%) with other sites including the thigh and groin area (N = 48, 18.0%), and lower abdomen (N = 1, 0.38%). In studies using lymphoscintigraphy or imaging for lymphedema assessment, patients demonstrated either partial or total obstruction of dermal backflow. Incidence of cellulitis per patient was reported to be 3.70 ± 8.85 events per year preoperatively (Table 4).

Circumference and volume reduction rate

Above-knee circumference reduction rate was described in 10 studies (N = 151).4,16,31–35,39,40,44 In meta-analysis, the average CRR was found to be 24.9% (95% CI; 14.8, 35.1) above the knee (Figure 3a). When comparing across donor sites, studies using the gastroepiploic donor site yielded a significantly higher reduction rate of 26.1% ± 14.3% compared to the submental site at 17.7% ± 4.97% (p < 0.001).

Below-knee CRR was reported in 10 studies (N = 151) and found to be 29.6% [12.7, 46.6] in meta-analysis (Figure 3b).4,16,31–35,39,40,44 Circumference reduction was found to be significantly higher in studies using the gastroepiploic donor site at a rate of 35.3% ± 17.4% compared to the submental site at 21.5% ± 3.73% (p < 0.001).

Above-ankle CRR was reported in 4 studies (N = 39) and found to be 15.8% (9.26, 22.4) in a meta-analysis (Figure 3c).34,39,40,44 The average circumference reduction rates for the entire limb were reported in 7 studies and 6 unique patient populations (N = 130) and found to be 25.4% (16.3, 34.4; Figure 3d).26,31,36,37,40,42,44

Limb volume reduction was reported in 6 studies (N = 119) with average volume decreases ranging from 12.7 mL to 4004 mL (Table 5).5,23,27,28,31,35 Average percent volume decrease was found to be 25.3% (18.1, 32.6; Figure 4).

Imaging outcomes

In the 9 articles that mentioned imaging outcomes (N = 118), 7 mentioned lymphoscintigraphy, 2 mentioned indocyanine green lymphangiography (ICG), 1 mentioned ultrasonography, 1 mentioned magnetic resonance angiography, and 1 mentioned L-Dex scores using bioimpedance spectroscopy.5,16,24,25,32,34,35,43,44 Although most studies using lymphoscintigraphy mentioned improved lymphatic uptake, one mentioned that only 3 of 7 flaps showed uptake into lymph nodes.24 Another study reported that 7 of 13 patients reported an improved disease stage using ICG or lymphoscintigraphy.43 Use of magnetic resonance angiography showed a patent vascular pedicle in all flaps and enhancing lymph nodes in 92.3% of flaps.24 Extracellular fluid measurements with l-Dex showed significant reductions (p < 0.001) by 59.8% ± 8.7%.5 Finally, imaging with ultrasonography revealed an average of 3.9 ± 1.9 lymph nodes per flap (Table 5).35

Patient-reported outcomes

Patient-reported outcomes were reported in 6 studies overall (N = 84) with several reporting scales.5,26–28,35,40 The Lymphedema Life Impact Scale (LLIS) was used in 2 studies showing an average reduction in scores by 54.2% ± 8.05%. Other reported scales, such as the visual analog scale (9.01 ± 1.0), lower extremity functional scale (67.1% ± 12.9% reduction), and lymphedema quality of life questionnaire (overall QoL: 7.1 ± 0.3), showed high quality of life metrics (Table 5).5,26,35

Cellulitis incidence

Cellulitis outcomes were reported in 12 articles (N = 221).4,25,26,30,32,35,36,38,39 The number of patients with cellulitis significantly decreased from 80 (89.9%) patients preoperatively to 10 (28.6%) postoperatively (p < 0.001). Meta-analysis comparing the annual rates of cellulitis per patient showed significantly lower incidence postoperatively (p < 0.001; MD: −1.88; 95% CI: −2.48, −1.28; Figure 5). This remained significant after sensitivity analysis.

Adverse events

The overall rate of adverse events was reported in 19 studies (N = 282) and was low at 46 (16.3%) complications.4,16,23–27,30,32–35,38–44 Adverse events primarily consisted of venous congestion or obstruction (7.44%), edema or hematoma (1.77%), flap loss (2.12%), numbness (0.71%), and other or unspecified causes (4.25%). Complication rates did not differ between gastroepiploic or submental donor sites (p = 0.42), with 10 (16.4%) and 17 (12.1%) complications, respectively.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to evaluate the safety and efficacy of VLNT for LEL by examining circumference reduction rates, limb volume changes, imaging-based findings, patient-reported outcomes, infection rates, and adverse events. Although a prior study evaluated clinical outcomes, circumference and volume measurements were pooled and the level of circumference measurement was not assessed. Although the assessed 39.53% clinical improvement with VLNT is a valuable metric, we aimed to establish further specific outcomes for a more comprehensive understanding of the applicability of VLNT in LEL.21

Circumference and volume reduction rate

Above and below knee circumference reduction rates were 24.79% and 29.40%, respectively. The greater proportional reduction below the knee suggests higher responses to VLNT in specific limb regions and may reflect differences in lymphedema burden. The CRR are similar to values in the literature reporting a reduction of 21.98% in mean circumference. This was much lower than the 42.7% CRR observed with upper-extremity disease.45 Similarly, Moon et al. corroborated this disparity by demonstrating significantly higher CRR at the elbow than at the knee.27 These differential responses to treatment likely reflect the greater gravitational effect on lymphatic fluid collection,46 more advanced disease stage at presentation, and increased fibrofatty deposition typically observed in LEL.47 The current literature thoroughly investigates the effect of gravity on fluid balance and suggests that it facilitates further limb swelling, which requires compression garments and elevation.48,49 A prior study noted that approximately 42% of patients could discontinue compression garments 3 years postoperatively with an average discontinuation times of approximately 13 months.50 Use of compression garments for at least 6 months is recommended, but no study analyzed the ideal cutoff for discontinuing compression garment use. Future studies should assess optimal postoperative regimens for VLNT including physical therapy, elevation, and compression garment use.

Circumference reduction rates were higher when associated with gastroepiploic donor sites compared to submental sites. These findings are supported by another meta-analysis demonstrating that gastroepiploic donor flaps had a 43.48% pooled clinical improvement (including CRR and volume) which was significantly higher than submental flaps with a 19.80% improvement.21 These findings were also consistent with the upper extremity where gastroepiploic flaps were superior in reducing limb circumference and cellulitis episodes.20 These improved outcomes may reflect the higher density of lymph nodes and more robust vasculature in the area.51,52 Additionally, the gastroepiploic flap facilitates the transfer of larger lymphatic tissue volumes, which may promote better lymphangiogenesis in fibrotic lower-extremity environments.53 Given that edema reduction was greater with gastroepiploic flaps and adverse events were similar, it is reasonable to assume that gastroepiploic donor sites are preferred over other options when possible. Future studies should encompass randomized controlled trials to evaluate the impact of donor site on outcomes and provide further analysis of these results.

Volume-based analysis revealed a mean percent volume reduction of 25.32%, supporting VLNT’s physiologic efficacy and suggesting improvements in limb heaviness and overall functional capacity.54 Unlike CRR, volume reductions are found to be similar between the upper and lower extremities.27 These similarities suggest that volume-based metrics may not be adequate to effectively capture the physiologic success of VLNT treatment across anatomically distinct regions, although circumference metrics can account for regional tissue composition. Further, although limb size reductions translate to greater quality-of-life, no standardized threshold of circumference or volume reduction has been established to define clinical success. Instead, change can be interpreted using the LLIS, with a minimal detectable change of 11–12 points and minimally clinical important difference of 7.31.55 VLNT was found to have positive patient-reported outcomes, with one study reporting an LLIS scores and showing an improvement of 22 points and another showing a 61.6% reduction in LLIS scores.5,28 Correlations between volume or CRR and patient-reported outcomes are warranted to assess which metric is more accurate for assessing patient symptom improvement.

Cellulitis and adverse events

Cellulitis is a lymphedema-associated morbidity caused by impaired immune surveillance and stagnant lymphatic fluid. Therefore, cellulitis incidence remains a critical marker of VLNT efficacy. Aligning with the existing literature, meta-analysis of studies showed decreased postoperative cellulitis incidence, reflecting improved lymphatic drainage.56,57 Compared with LVA, VLNT has shown significantly greater decreases in cellulitis events in the upper and lower extremities,45 suggesting that procedures that employ lymph node transfer may offer improved immunological protection than those that simply reroute drainage pathways, especially in advanced cases characterized by the loss of native lymphatic systems. Emerging evidence also shows that lymphatic system transfer (LYST) may provide similar or enhanced reductions in cellulitis incidence, potentially owing to immediate lymphatic drainage through transferred afferent vessels, though data in this area remain limited.56

Reported adverse events were low at 12.22%, consistent with the current literature, with hematoma formation and temporary sensory changes among the most common, and with no major donor-site complications.52 Chun et al.’s analysis of upper-extremity VLNT showed a pooled postoperative complication rate of approximately 12.9%, comparable to the rate found for lower extremity VLNT in this study. Complication rates for LVA were lower at 8.9%, suggesting that LVA may be preferable for milder forms of lymphedema.58 Donor-site morbidity is known to vary by location, with lower complication rates for omental and submental flaps and higher tendency of seroma formation in groin sites.59 These findings together reinforce the strength of intraabdominal sites, including the gastroepiploic flap, as safe and effective options for lower-extremity VLNT.

Limitations and future directions

This study is limited by the inherent constraints of a systematic review and meta-analysis, including heterogeneity in metrics for measuring patient-reported outcomes and modalities for imaging. Another potential limitation is reporting bias, with risks of only studies with positive results being published. Additionally, most of the available literature on VLNT and related topics is derived from single-center and retrospective cohort studies, underscoring the need for further investigation using a multicenter, prospective approach. Meta-analysis yielded high heterogeneity—likely owing to variations in the procedure and patient population—which was partially assessed with subgroup analysis by donor site. Despite the heterogeneity, this study establishes thresholds for VLNT outcomes for surgical planning and patient counseling. Future directions should incorporate standardized outcome measures, particularly for PRO and disease burden metrics, to establish more uniform benchmarks of clinical success.

Conclusion

Vascularized lymph node transfer is a highly effective technique for refractory LEL treatment, with considerable circumference reduction rates, volume reduction, and decrease in cellulitis incidence. VLNT offers a safe approach for lymphedema management, with an adverse event rate of 12.22% and no significant differences in adverse events based on the donor site. Gastroepiploic donor sites are associated with greater circumference reduction and thus offer a more effective approach for lymphedema management. Future studies should incorporate standardized patient-reported outcomes and prospectively evaluate the impact of donor sites on symptom reduction.
