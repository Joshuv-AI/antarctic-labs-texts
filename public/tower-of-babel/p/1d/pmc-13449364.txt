Cost-effectiveness of a prosthetic venous valve versus compression therapies and wound care for treatment of infra-inguinal deep vein reflux
Authors: Jennifer Benner, Roy Arguello, Peter Pappas, Mark Meissner, Kush Desai, Josh Epstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with chronic venous insufficiency secondary to infra-inguinal deep vein reflux have few treatment options available to them, given the focus-to-date has been directed toward obstruction rather than reflux. The SAVVE trial was a prospective, non-blinded, single arm, multicenter study designed to assess the safety and efficacy of VenoValve, a bioprosthetic replacement venous valve that is surgically implanted into the deep venous system for treatment of patients with severe infra-inguinal deep venous reflux (Clinical, Etiological, Anatomical, and Pathophysiological classification C4b-C6). This study was designed to estimate the cost-effectiveness of VenoValve compared to the current standard of care for patients with severe infra-inguinal deep vein reflux.MethodsA semi-Markov cohort model was developed to simulate clinical and economic outcomes among patients with deep venous chronic venous insufficiency through health states defined by revised Venous Clinical Severity Score. Patients could experience deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, ulcer healing, and ulcer recurrence. Key input values were sourced from the SAVVE trial and publicly available sources. Outcomes were compared over 5 years.ResultsTreatment with VenoValve resulted in an average of 2.2 ulcers avoided per-patient over 5 years. These clinical benefits translated into savings of $32,153 per patient, $14,779 per ulcer avoided, and a cost per revised Venous Clinical Severity Score point change of $4065; VenoValve was dominant relative to standard of care (ie, lower costs, higher quality-adjusted life years). Results were robust in one-way sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsIn our model, VenoValve yields substantial economic benefits and improved health outcomes for patients suffering from severe infra-inguinal chronic venous insufficiency.Keywords: Cost-effectiveness analysis, Chronic venous insufficiency, Chronic venous disease, Chronic lower extremity venous reflux, Venous leg ulcer

Infra-inguinal chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) related to superficial venous reflux and iliofemoral venous obstruction can be treated with a variety of superficial venous interventions and iliofemoral venous stenting respectively. Currently, there are no effective treatments for infra-inguinal deep venous reflux. Open reconstructive procedures (including valve repair, transposition, transplantation, or neovalve creation) remain treatment options; however, they are technically demanding, confined to a limited number of expert centers, and available evidence demonstrates variable and often limited long-term durability, particularly in post-thrombotic disease.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Moreover, its applicability in broader clinical practice is limited, as few surgeons are trained in the technique. Although several endovascular techniques have been proposed, there are currently no FDA-approved devices. Management focuses on reducing discomfort and edema, enhancing skin health, and healing ulcers.6 Conservative measures include a variety of venotonic agents and compression with stockings, bandages, or Unna boots, but require a high rate of compliance and can be contraindicated in patients with severe arterial disease or heart failure.7,8

The VenoValve, a novel bioprosthetic replacement valve designed to correct deep venous reflux, is currently being evaluated for approval by the FDA. The surgically implanted VenoValve is a device intended to treat patients with lower limb CVI of the deep venous system by restoring valvular function where valvular incompetency has developed. The SAVVE study was a prospective, multicenter trial that enrolled 75 patients with deep venous CVI due to valvular incompetence (CEAP classification C4b-C6). All patients failed at least 3 months of standard of care (SOC) and were free of superficial reflux; the VenoValve was successfully implanted in 97% of patients.9 Results demonstrated that 85% of patients experienced a ≥3-point improvement in Revised Venous Clinical Severity Score (rVCSS) at 12 months post-implant compared to baseline.

Economically, CVI imposes a substantial burden on the healthcare system and patients. In the US, estimates indicate that venous leg ulcers account for 1% of total healthcare expenditures, with total general CVI care accounting for 2% of total healthcare expenditures.10,11 High costs are attributed to ongoing treatment and management of complications such as venous ulcers, which can lead to significant outpatient costs, inpatient hospitalization-related costs, averaging $27,000 (2022 US dollars) for infected ulcers.12 Additionally, CVI imposes personal financial burdens including out-of-pocket expenses and loss of work productivity. Patients with venous leg ulcers miss approximately 29% more workdays than those without.13 To better understand the potential economic benefits associated with the VenoValve, a cost-effectiveness analysis was conducted comparing the VenoValve to the current standard of care (SOC) of compression therapies and wound care therapies for those patients with venous leg ulcers.

Methods

Model overview

Informed by the SAVVE study, a semi-Markov cohort model was developed in Microsoft Excel to assess the cost-effectiveness of the VenoValve vs the current SOC of compression and wound care therapy from the US payer perspective. The model assumed 6-month cycles for the first year, to match the SAVVE trial, followed by annual cycles.9,14,15 Costs and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) were discounted at 3% in accordance with economic modeling guidelines.16 Results were presented over a 5-year time horizon given recent trends in the duration of health plan enrollment in the US.17 This study did not require Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval as it was based solely on previously published data and did not involve human subjects or identifiable personal information.

Model structure

The model structure and patient flow were designed de novo to reflect the clinically meaningful 3-point improvement in rVCSS as assessed in the SAVVE study. Three-point intervals were selected to align with the clinically meaningful improvement in rVCSS of 3-points, as indicated by the FDA.18 All patients entered the model in one of 11 mutually exclusive health states based on the rVCSS distribution at baseline within the SAVVE study (Fig 1).9,14,15 At the end of each cycle, patients could either remain in the same rVCSS health state, transition to a different rVCSS health state (more severe or less severe), or die. Within each health state, patients could experience an incident or recurrent ulcer, DVT, or PE. Patients experiencing an ulcer could also experience ulcer improvement or ulcer infection in subsequent model cycles.

Beyond 12 months, the model applied constant transition probabilities, assuming that the 12-month trial data best reflected the long-term effects of the VenoValve. Treatment effects were assumed independent of age and event history due to a lack of evidence suggesting otherwise.

Population

The model population reflected the SAVVE study, which was comprised of patients aged 63 years (mean, standard deviation: 9.97 years); all patients had a CEAP classification of C4b or higher (86.3% C5/C6) and 85% had post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS), or evidence of a prior DVT, at baseline.

Since all patients received anticoagulation post-implantation of the VenoValve and the majority of patients had PTS, the model conservatively assumed anticoagulation use in both treatment arms throughout the modeled time horizon to provide a consistent comparison between treatment strategies.9,14,15 Although long-term anticoagulation may be associated with bleeding complications, anticoagulation-related bleeding events were not explicitly modeled, as they were not expected to materially affect incremental differences between VenoValve and SOC.32,33

rVCSS health state distributions

The starting distribution of patients in each rVCSS health state was derived from a post-hoc analysis of the patient-level SAVVE data that estimated the baseline distribution of patients by 3-point intervals in the rVCSS score. The baseline rVCSS distribution was applied to both the VenoValve and SOC arms of the model.

For the VenoValve arm, a similar approach was used to capture the distribution of patients in each rVCSS health state at months 6 and 12. From month 12 onward (ie, cycle 3+), the model assumed durability of the 12-month treatment effect, without further improvement of rVCSS. This assumption was informed by the longest-term data available from the SAVVE study and by 3-year follow-up data from the first in-human VenoValve study demonstrating sustained improvement in rVCSS through year 3.34 In the base case, the model assumed that SOC patients remained within their initial rVCSS health states over time due to limited availability of longitudinal rVCSS transition data for conservative management and evidence demonstrating poor long-term adherence to compression therapy.35,36

Risk of DVT and PE

The underlying risk of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE) events were derived from the SAVVE study for the VenoValve arm and from the literature for the SOC arm.9,14,15,21,22,37 With respect to DVTs, outcomes reported in the SAVVE study primarily reflected ipsilateral target vein thromboses involving the implanted femoral vein.

For the SOC arm, the probability of experiencing a DVT or PE event was estimated using data from a retrospective study of patients with primary chronic venous disease (C0-C6). The DVT rate was defined to include both DVTs and DVT with PE.21 Annual rates of DVT and PE were then converted to 6-month probabilities for use in the model. While the SOC arm of the model is intended to represent a patient population on anticoagulants potentially receiving wound care, the study did not explicitly report rates of anticoagulant use or wound care. Given the lack of evidence on DVT and PE events by rVCSS, the model assumed no difference in risk of DVT or PE events across rVCSS health states, irrespective of treatment arm.

As DVT and PE events are associated with long-term consequences, the model assumed that patients who experienced DVT or PE events incurred an excess risk of future subsequent DVTs or PEs. These subsequent risks were derived from a large population-based cohort study of patients with a first venous thromboembolic event, which demonstrated that recurrent DVT occurred more frequently than recurrent PE over long-term follow-up.22

Ulceration

The underlying risk of ulceration for each rVCSS health state was estimated via a post-hoc analysis of the patient-level SAVVE trial data at study baseline.18 The same underlying risk was initially applied to both treatment arms to reflect the shared disease severity at model entry; subsequently, ulcer outcomes differed according to treatment-specific rVCSS transitions and ulcer-related event probabilities.

For the VenoValve arm, the probability of experiencing ulcer improvement, a fully healed ulcer, a partially healed ulcer, and a recurrent ulcer were sourced from the SAVVE study.9,14,15 For the SOC arm, the probability of experiencing ulcer improvement and a recurrent ulcer were derived from the VenUS IV study conducted in the UK, which evaluated compression-based conservative management of venous leg ulcers.19 Importantly, the VenUS IV study data were used only to inform ulcer-specific transition parameters among patients with active ulcers and were not used to define baseline disease severity or rVCSS distributions in the SOC arm. The probability of experiencing a fully healed ulcer and partially healed ulcer were conservatively assumed to be equivalent to the VenoValve arm given a lack of available evidence on the efficacy of wound care or compression therapies on full or partial ulcer healing.

Given differences in study design and the absence of patient-level comparator data, ulcer outcomes between studies were not formally compared statistically and were instead incorporated as study-specific transition probabilities within the model.

The risk of experiencing an infected ulcer was sourced from a retrospective case-control study of patients with venous leg ulcers and reflects the proportion of C6 patients with a venous leg ulcer with at least one inpatient admission for an infection related to their ulcer during the 1-year study period.12 The model assumed the same risk of infected ulceration for both treatment arms.

All ulceration-related probabilities were applied uniformly to each rVCSS health state.

Surgical site infections and surgical pocket hematomas

Within the SAVVE study, a proportion of patients who received the VenoValve experienced surgical site infections, and surgical pocket hematomas (ie, bleeding in the surgical cavity), which were assumed to be associated with the implantation procedure. Thus, the model applied a probability for these events in the first model cycle, which were derived from the SAVVE trial.9,14,15

Mortality

The age-dependent baseline risk of mortality was assessed based on life tables from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.38 In addition, an excess mortality burden was applied for CVI and venous leg ulcers, which were derived from large, retrospective cohort studies.23,24 Furthermore, the model assumed that the excess mortality burden was multiplicative.

Background and excess mortality risks were applied to both arms of the model. Any modeled mortality differences between treatment arms were indirectly driven by differences in ulcer burden and healing over time, based on published associations between venous ulceration and excess mortality.

Costs

Ulceration, DVTs, PEs, surgical site infection, and surgical cavity bleeding events were all associated with acute event costs, which were applied in the cycle in which the event occurred.27 The per-cycle cost associated with an ulcer (partially healed or not healed) was estimated using a treatment algorithm for chronic venous leg ulcers and is described further in the appendix (Appendix 1, online only; Supplementary Table I, online only).25 For fully healed ulcers, the model applied the cost of a compression stocking given this is part of standard treatment guidelines. The cost associated with an infected ulcer was sourced from the same retrospective case-control study as the probability of experiencing an infected ulcer.12

The cost of the VenoValve device was assumed to be $30,000 USD, which is a placeholder given the lack of an established price. Given that the device is surgically implanted during a hospital outpatient procedure, it was assumed that 100% of patients would receive the device in the hospital outpatient setting, which is associated with a procedure cost of $10,786.28

All costs were adjusted to 2023 US dollars using the medical care component of the US Bureau of Labor Statistics consumer price index.39

Health state utilities

The base health state utility value for each rVCSS health state was estimated via a post-hoc regression analysis using patient-level data from the SAVVE study.18 Ulceration, DVT events, and PE events were all associated with disutilities sourced from the literature.29,30 Additional details on the derivation of health state utilities are presented in the appendix (Appendix 2, online only).

Model outcomes

Model outcomes were expressed in terms of average per-patient total and incremental costs, number of ulcers avoided with VenoValve, and the cost-savings per ulcer avoided with VenoValve. Additional model outcomes included life-years (LYs), quality-adjusted LYs (QALYs), and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), which was calculated as the difference in total costs divided by the difference in QALYs.

When considering the US payer perspective and a 5-year time horizon, the total average costs per-patient associated with VenoValve were $111,136 and $143,289 for SOC, resulting in cost savings of $32,153. Total average LYs per-patient with VenoValve were 4.12 and 4.05 for SOC, resulting in an incremental gain of 0.06 LYs. The total average QALYs per-patient for VenoValve were 3.74 and 3.42 for SOC, resulting in a gain of 0.33 incremental QALYs per-patient. These results indicate that VenoValve was dominant (ie, lower costs, higher QALYs) vs SOC.

In addition, treatment with VenoValve resulted in 2.2 ulcers avoided per-patient on average. These clinical benefits translated into cost savings of $14,779 per ulcer avoided and a cost savings per rVCSS point change of $4065. Furthermore, the model indicated that VenoValve achieved a negative incremental cost (ie, breakeven) between years 2 and 3; prior to breaking even, VenoValve achieved cost-effectiveness at a willingness-to-pay threshold of $150,000 per QALY in year 2 (ICER = $98,085).16

Discussion

Based on clinical data from the SAVVE pivotal study, the findings of this analysis indicate that the VenoValve is a dominant treatment option compared to SOC. Treatment with the VenoValve results in fewer ulcers and PE events, yielding better health outcomes and lower costs relative to wound care and compression therapies. In particular, the ability of the VenoValve to reduce disease severity (ie, transition patients from a higher rVCSS to a lower rVCSS), irrespective of CEAP classification, is a key driver of model results. Moreover, the high burden of venous ulcers, including significant wound care costs, excess mortality, and disutility, is a driver of model results, albeit to a lesser extent. The model indicates that by avoiding costly events, the VenoValve can be a cost-effective option over the modeled time horizon.

While prior modeling frameworks have been published to assess the cost and quality of life implications of wound care and compression bandaging for ulceration, this is the first analysis to quantify the deep vein CVI patient pathway via rVCSS health states. While the health states utilized are based on rVCSS groupings from the SAVVE study, this analysis is largely consistent with previously published studies, which also account for active, healed, and recurrent ulcers in addition to costs of compression, bandaging, debridement, and excision associated with wound care.25 Importantly, ulcer-related outcomes were modeled explicitly and independently of rVCSS health states; therefore, the clinical and economic impact of venous ulcers was not determined solely by their contribution to the rVCSS score.

A cost-effectiveness study evaluating interventional outpatient procedures for superficial venous reflux in patients with chronic venous ulceration found that these procedures were a dominant treatment strategy compared with compression therapy alone.41 Similarly, a cost-effectiveness analysis comparing stenting to SOC for deep venous outflow obstruction and leg ulcers found that stenting was also a dominant strategy.42 The EVRA trialists report that endovenous ablation, compared with compression with deferred endovenous treatment of superficial venous reflux, was also a cost-effective treatment strategy for patients with venous leg ulceration.43 These findings align with the present analysis, further supporting the economic and clinical benefits of targeted venous interventions.

Therapies limited to the superficial venous system have demonstrated effectiveness in improving outcomes for patients with venous leg ulcers. The findings from previous cost-effectiveness studies support the broader principle that addressing venous pathology through targeted interventions can provide both clinical and economic benefits. Unlike treatments focused solely on the superficial system, the VenoValve directly addresses the underlying deep vein reflux and has the potential to provide sustained benefits beyond what has been observed with superficial interventions alone.

The rVCSS structure offers a comprehensive assessment of deep venous CVI and allows for a more granular understanding of ulceration and quality of life at different rVCSS scores. Furthermore, the rVCSS framework can serve as a valuable tool in future research, enabling more nuanced evaluations of treatment efficacy and cost-effectiveness across diverse patient populations.

Implications

The findings from this cost-effectiveness analysis suggest that, once approved, the VenoValve will likely provide substantial health benefits and cost savings compared to wound care and compression bandaging in patients with deep venous CVI. Across multiple scenarios, including both Commercial and Medicare populations, the VenoValve was consistently shown to dominate SOC, offering lower overall costs and higher QALYs due to its improvements in disease severity (rVCSS). Moreover, model results suggest that earlier intervention with the VenoValve, particularly when ulcers first present, significantly improves health outcomes and cost savings. This reinforces the need for timely access to the VenoValve, as delaying treatment may lead to worsened disease severity and higher long-term costs for payers. These results underscore the potential of the VenoValve as a valuable therapeutic option, particularly given there is currently no other approved treatment for venous reflux in the deep venous system.

The National Health Expenditure Accounts estimate that total healthcare expenditure in the US in 2022 was $4.5 trillion.44 As noted previously, CVI costs are estimated to be about 2% of the total healthcare budget, or $90 billion.10 If the annual per patient direct medical costs from this model were extrapolated to account for the 2.4 million US patients with severe CVI of the deep venous system, annual costs would equal $26.7 billion for patients receiving SOC; treatment with the VenoValve would result in cost savings of $5.9 billion. While severe CVI of the deep venous system constitutes only 1.5% of the entire CVI population in the US, the costs associated with managing more severe disease are equivalent to almost a third of all CVI-related costs.

Deep venous CVI is a complex and multifaceted health condition that extends beyond the development of venous ulcers. It involves impaired venous return, which can lead to venous hypertension and edema, contributing to further complications such as lymphatic dysfunction, skin pigmentation changes, pain, and in severe cases, DVT, PE, and even death.6 This interconnected impact on various bodily systems underscores the need for a more holistic approach to the disease modeling of deep venous CVI. The de novo model structure presented here accounts for these complexities via the rVCSS health states, given that rVCSS includes pain, venous edema, skin pigmentation, inflammation, induration, ulceration, and the use of compression therapy.45 Furthermore, these post-hoc analyses of the SAVVE trial data are the first to illustrate the relationship between rVCSS and health state utility (EQ-5D).18 Both of these elements contribute to a more accurate and relevant cost-effectiveness analysis than a model structure solely based on ulceration.

Limitations

As previously mentioned, the SAVVE study was a single arm trial; while patients in the SAVVE study were required to fail at least 3 months of SOC (ie, compression bandaging) and demonstrate no superficial reflux prior to enrollment, for the purposes of this economic analysis, the trial design and patient population criteria differed from those in support of compression bandaging.9,14 In addition, patient-level trial data was not available within compression bandaging trials, which would have been required to estimate the baseline, 6-month, and 12-month distributions for the rVCSS groupings included in this model. Furthermore, external SOC data sources such as VenUS IV did not report detailed information regarding post-thrombotic disease, anticoagulation use, or deep venous reflux severity, limiting direct assessment of population comparability with the SAVVE cohort. Thus, an indirect treatment comparison was not feasible.

Long-term durability of the VenoValve beyond currently available follow-up remains uncertain. The base case assumed persistence of the 12-month treatment effect over the modeled time horizon, informed in part by 3-year follow-up data from the first-in-human VenoValve study demonstrating sustained improvement in rVCSS through year 3. To further evaluate this uncertainty, an additional scenario analysis incorporating gradual waning of treatment effect was conducted and demonstrated directionally consistent findings. Future long-term follow-up data will be important to further validate these modeled projections.

Anticoagulation-related bleeding complications were not explicitly modeled; it is anticipated that inclusion of these events would increase the absolute cost burden equally across treatment arms. Similarly, although avoiding PTS could potentially lead to healthcare cost savings, the modeled population reflects patients with post-thrombotic disease, so PTS costs were not explicitly assessed.

The model did not account for the excess mortality associated with PE and DVT, as the available mortality data for CVI already included these factors. Although the VenoValve was associated with a higher DVT rate, excluding DVT- and PE-related excess mortality was not expected to materially affect the model results. In addition, DVT event definitions differed between the SAVVE study and literature sources used for the SOC arm, as SAVVE primarily reported ipsilateral target vein thrombosis related to the implanted VenoValve. This difference in event definitions may introduce uncertainty when comparing thrombotic event rates between treatment arms. Nevertheless, these data represent the best currently available evidence regarding thrombotic outcomes following VenoValve implantation and were considered appropriate for informing the present analysis.

Costs within the model are based entirely on wound care and compression therapies related to ulceration, DVT events, and PE events. Wound care practices and utilization of advanced wound care products may have evolved since completion of the VenUS IV trial. However, compression therapy remains the cornerstone of conservative management for venous leg ulcers, and advanced wound care products are generally reserved for refractory ulcers that fail standard compression-based therapy. In addition, there may be additional costs borne by payers and patients for other complications of deep venous CVI, such as venous edema, skin pigmentation, inflammation, induration, and mental health events. To the authors' knowledge, there are no real-world evidence studies quantifying healthcare costs (direct or indirect) by rVCSS. Future studies could build and improve upon this analysis by quantifying the direct and indirect costs by rVCSS, establishing an even more robust modeling framework for deep vein CVI.

Conclusions

Treatment with the VenoValve would result in potential cost savings and extended life, over a 5-year time horizon, compared to SOC treatment with wound care and compression therapies. The results from this economic analysis indicate that VenoValve may be a cost-saving treatment option for patients with deep vein CVI in the US.
