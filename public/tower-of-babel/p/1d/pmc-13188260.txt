Adjunctive strategies in multiple sclerosis treatment: non-pharmacological approaches and their potential clinical implications
Authors: Yuxin Wang, Weiheng Yan, Lan Yao, Wanyi Liu, Zegang Yin, Lan Xiao, Jianqin Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory demyelinating disease affecting the central nervous system (CNS), and represents the most common non-traumatic cause of disability among young adults. Although current immunomodulatory therapies have demonstrated beneficial effects in mitigating disease progression, their associated side effects and limitations render MS a condition without a definitive cure. A major obstacle in achieving effective treatment and recovery is the pathological microenvironment within the MS-affected CNS, which severely impairs endogenous tissue repair and diminishes the efficacy of existing interventions.Main bodyRecently, non-pharmacological approaches have gained increasing attention for their potential to treat MS-related pathology. In this review, we summarize how non-pharmacotherapeutic interventions, including physical exercise, dietary adjustments, cognitive training, social participation, and mixed rehabilitation programs, can affect key pathological elements of neuroinflammation in MS by elucidating the molecular mechanisms, such as modulation of neuroinflammation, suppression of glial cell activation, promotion of remyelination, and enhancement of neuroprotective processes. By integrating preclinical experimental data from animals with clinical evidence from MS patients, we also illustrate how various non-pharmacological strategies can dynamically modulate the disease microenvironment.ConclusionsWe emphasize the capacity of non-pharmacological approaches to enhance endogenous repair by fostering a more supportive CNS microenvironment, highlighting their potential to transform disease management.Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, Neurodegenerative disease, Non-pharmacotherapeutic interventions, Neuroinflammation, Glia, Remyelination

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory neurodegenerative disease of the central nervous system (CNS), and one of the most common causes of non-traumatic disability in young adults aged 18 to 40 years, affecting approximately 2.8 million people worldwide [1]. According to clinical course descriptors categorized by the International Advisory Committee on Clinical Trials, MS is classified into three clinical courses: relapsing-remitting (RRMS), characterized by acute attacks followed by recovery and accounting for approximately 80% of cases; primary progressive (PPMS), marked by gradual worsening from onset and comprising 15% of cases; and secondary progressive (SPMS), which initially follows a relapsing-remitting course but gradually worsens later in the disease progression, representing 5% of cases [2]. Among these clinical courses, the presentations of MS are highly heterogeneous, and involve multi-system neurological impairments that encompass visual, motor, sensory, cognitive, and autonomic functions [3]. These neurological deficits exert profound impacts on both physical and psychological well-being, significantly impairing social functioning, quality of life, and placing increasing demands on public health systems [4, 5].

Although the etiology of MS is not yet fully understood, accumulating evidence suggests that its onset and progression are driven by an intricate interplay between genetic predisposition and environmental factors [6–8]. Insights from MS patient samples, clinical trials, and pre-clinical studies have established the crucial role of peripheral leukocytes and further delineated four key pathophysiological elements [9]: (1) activation of autoreactive lymphocytes, (2) immune invasion into the CNS, (3) immunomodulation, and (4) progressive neurodegeneration. These interconnected pathogenic mechanisms drive inflammatory infiltration, demyelination, gliosis, and axonal degeneration in MS progression [10]. Based on that, recent advances in disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) of MS, particularly the B-cell depletion therapies like ocrelizumab [11], have shown efficacy in mitigating disease activity and managing relapse-associated symptoms by targeting peripheral immunity (via immunoregulatory, anti-migratory, or depleting mechanisms) [12]. However, majority of current therapies still face significant challenges [13]. First, systemic immunosuppression weakens the immune system of MS patients, leading to increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections and malignancies [14]. Second, while existing drugs effectively manage relapses, they fail to halt progressive disability caused by axonal and neuronal damages during the progressive phase [15, 16], particularly in progression independent of relapse activity (PIRA), the primary driver of disability accumulation in MS [17].

To address these limitations, emerging studies have explored novel pathogenic mechanisms and therapeutic strategies, revealing that systemic functional dysregulation and localized abnormalities in the neural microenvironment collectively fuel disease progression [16, 18]. In line with this, it has been well-accepted that oligodendroglial recruitment, differentiation, and axonal contact are disrupted in MS. These limitations arise not only from the intrinsic regenerative deficits of oligodendroglia but also from dysregulated crosstalk with other cell types in local lesions [19–23]. As a result, promotion of remyelination has been recognized as a promising therapeutic strategy of MS, with several agents under clinical investigations [24]. For instance, clemastine [25, 26] shortened P100 latency on full-field, pattern-reversal, visual-evoked potentials by 1.7 ms/eye in RRMS patients with chronic demyelinating optic neuropathy in a crossover clinical trial, indicating remyelination potential [27]. However, recent findings also indicate that clemastine fumarate facilitates prolonged activation of P2RX7 channel in oligodendrocytes, thereby may exacerbate pyroptosis and potentially worsen progressive MS disability [28]. Thus, the needs for novel MS therapeutic strategies persists, motivating further basic and clinical research.

Recently, non-pharmacological interventions have gained significant attention due to their multifaceted therapeutic potentials [29–33]. These approaches offer a more comprehensive beneficial effect in a systemically modulatory manner and through multiple mechanisms, including the regulation of immune responses and inflammation, activation of microglia and astrocytes, regeneration of oligodendroglia and remyelination, as well as modulation of neuronal activity and neurotrophic factors [34]. Growing evidence suggests that non-pharmacological interventions not only complement pharmacotherapies by improving the MS microenvironment to facilitate repair, but may also provide stand-alone benefits while avoiding drug-related side effects [35, 36]. Importantly, their flexibility supports patient-centered and individualized precision medicine therapy for symptom management [29–33]. Therefore, in this review we aim to evaluate neuroprotective and microenvironment-modulating effects of non-pharmacological interventions in both MS patients and experimental models. By synthesizing current evidence, we aim to present a narrative framework that serves as an instructive guide for understanding the fundamental cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying these approaches, and we call for greater attention from both neuroscientists and clinicians toward future research on non-pharmacological interventions for MS.

Potential therapeutic effects of non-pharmacotherapeutic interventions for MS patients

Non-pharmacological therapeutic strategies encompass various approaches, including physical exercise, dietary supplies, cognitive training, social participation, and mixed rehabilitation programs (Fig. 1). Current research on MS has indicated that these interventions can be beneficial in improving physiological function, cognitive performance, mental health and the overall quality of life of MS patients [33, 37–39]. The present review examines a variety of non-pharmacological interventions for MS, focusing on their therapeutic effects (Table 1) and the underlying mechanisms. It should be noted that certain symptom-specific modalities (such as electrical neuromuscular stimulation, acupuncture, and transcranial magnetic stimulation, etc.) have been comprehensively reviewed elsewhere [29–31], and therefore fall beyond the scope of this review.

Physical exercise

Exercise, defined as a planned, structured, and repetitive form of physical activity [40], has undergone a significant paradigm shift in its therapeutic application for patients with MS. Although exercise was once discouraged due to isolated reports of transient side effects (e.g., exercise-induced visual blurring in a small minority of patients) [41], systematic reviews and meta-analyses have confirmed that exercise is now a well-established intervention for managing symptoms and improving various aspects of health, including physical fitness, walking mobility, balance, fatigue, cognitive function, depressive symptoms, and quality of life [42–44]. Resistance exercise training has led to mild improvements in lower-extremity muscle strength [45], while aerobic exercise training led to moderate improvements in cardiorespiratory function [45, 46]. Both resistance and aerobic exercise training have produced mild but significant beneficial effects for walking speed and endurance [47]. Targeted balance training can improve composite scores and mobility test performance in MS patients with an EDSS score below 7.5 [48]. A multi-component program that integrates aerobic and resistance exercise has been shown to be the most effective intervention for enhancing overall cognitive function [49], and an optimal dose of 180 min per week (delivered over 8–10 weeks, with sessions not exceeding 60 min) yields the greatest cognitive benefit [50]. In addition, endurance exercise, mixed training, and other forms of trainings (such as yoga) may help reduce self-reported fatigue [51]. Recent analyses suggest that engaging in at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise per week, or alternatively two sessions of 10 to 30 min of moderate-intensity aerobic training combined with two sessions of resistance training, can significantly alleviate the severity and impact of fatigue [52]. Furthermore, exercises like treadmill exercise, stretching, yoga, and swimming have also demonstrated positive effects on improving depressive symptoms in MS patients [53]. Therefore, exercise has been proposed as a viable adjunctive approach for managing symptoms in MS.

In the past decade, research emphasis has shifted from symptom control to the modulation of disease activity and progression. These effects have been evaluated using conventional clinical and paraclinical metrics, such as relapse frequency, disability accrual, and neuroimaging parameters; however, the accumulated evidence remains inconclusive [44, 54]. Data from a systematic review and meta-analysis of 40 randomized clinical trials (n = 1780) showed no significant difference in the relapse rates between exercise and non-exercise control groups in MS patients [55]. Additionally, another systematic review encompassing 43 studies found that although exercise training could not lead to a measurable change in the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score, it was likely associated with improved Multiple Sclerosis Function Composite (MSFC) score [56].

It is worth noting that a recent scoping review highlighted several challenges in studying exercise interventions for MS [57]. For instance, the absence of a consistent neuroimaging biomarker, a unified sensor-based metric, or a reproducible pattern of neural adaptation to exercise in MS, hampers the ability to draw definitive conclusions about its effects. Moreover, methodological limitations, such as small sample sizes, a narrow range of outcome measures, and insufficient follow-up durations, contribute to the pronounced outcome heterogeneity across studies.

Dietary supplies

Nutritional intake represents a compelling environmental factor for MS treatment. A growing number of patients are incorporating dietary modifications into their management strategy [58]. Evidence has confirmed the potential roles of diet on clinical outcomes for patients with MS [59], and indicated that specific dietary components and patterns can yield beneficial effects in MS treatment [60–64].

On one hand, certain dietary components appear to play a key role in the prevention of MS onset. A large prospective cohort study established a link between higher dietary alpha-linolenic acid (ALA, a kind of polyunsaturated fatty acid) intake and a reduced MS risk; in contrast, no such association was found for eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) or docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), suggesting a specific protective role of ALA against MS [65]. This finding was further supported by a recent study showing that higher serum ALA levels were associated with a lower risk of clinically definite MS, fewer relapses, and less disability progression [66]. Notably, these inverse associations persisted over 11 years of follow-up period [66]. The results for other fatty acids were either non-significant or inconsistent [66]. Similar associations have been observed with vitamin D, indicating that the lower serum levels during MS relapses (compared to remission) were correlated with increased disability. However, a meta-analysis of 12 randomized controlled trials involving 933 MS patients found that vitamin D supplementation was not associated with improved outcomes [67]. Regarding probiotics, a meta-analysis that included three randomized controlled trials with a total of 178 MS patients demonstrated that probiotic supplementation is beneficial for mental health, though the certainty of this evidence is still very low [68]. Additionally, supplementation with the polyphenol epigallocatechin-3-gallate significantly improved both metabolic health markers (such as fat oxidation and muscle mass, and positive shifts in lipid metabolism) and functional performance (including gait speed and balance), particularly when administered in combination with coconut oil [69]. Furthermore, a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of observational studies revealed that fruit and vegetable consumption is inversely associated with the risk of MS [70]. These findings suggest that certain dietary components may play a significant role in both the management of symptoms and the prevention of MS onset.

On the other hand, certain dietary pattern/style is also involved in the prevention of MS. A systematic review with network meta-analysis, which included 12 randomized trials comparing eight dietary interventions (such as low-fat, mediterranean, ketogenic, anti-inflammatory, paleolithic, fasting, calorie restriction, and regular), revealed that paleolithic, low-fat, and mediterranean diets were associated with reduced fatigue. Both paleolithic and mediterranean diets were shown to improve physical and mental quality of life [71]. Given the limitations inherent in this analysis, which may stem from the overall low quality of the included trials, these results necessitate validation in future high-quality randomized controlled trials. A recent systematic review of 6 studies suggested that a ketogenic diet may improve clinical outcomes in MS, including fatigue, depression, sleep quality, and quality of life, although its conclusions are limited by small sample sizes and methodological variability [72]. Moreover, another systematic analysis that included 5 high-quality studies supported the potential of intermittent fasting to alleviate MS-related symptoms and enhance quality of life [73]. In addition, several randomized clinical trials have reported the benefits of low-fat [74, 75], anti-inflammatory [76–78], and calorie restriction [79, 80] diets in alleviating MS symptoms.

Nevertheless, it is also worth noting that contradictory systematic reviews and meta-analyses have indicated that dietary components failed to show a significant impact on MS-related outcomes, such as relapse rate or disability progression [67, 81–83]. We speculate that this may be partly due to methodological shortcomings in these trials. Also, studies of dietary patterns were often too diverse to allow for comprehensive analyses [81]. For instance, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis, which included 7 studies with a total of 1118 RRMS participants, suggested that the mediterranean diet combined with dimethyl fumarate therapy is beneficial in reducing relapse rates and the intensity of disability symptoms in MS patients [84]; however, several methodological and interpretive issues in this analysis warrant serious consideration [85]. Well-designed, large-scale clinical trials focused on diet may provide valuable complementary therapeutic advantages for individuals with MS.

Cognitive training

Cognitive training refers to the utilization of various cognitive tasks to enhance cognitive function. Current studies have identified attention, learning and working memory, processing speed, and executive function as practical and sensitive indicators for evaluating cognitive performance. In recent years, computer-assisted cognitive training has also emerged as a recommended approach in MS-related cognitive management. Several interventions and programs have already received the support from practice standards and practice guidelines [86–91].

A meta-analysis of 19 studies (725 MS patients) demonstrated that computer-based cognitive training is effective for improving information processing speed and memory [92]; however, no significant benefit was observed for attention or executive function [92]. A dose-response relationship for cognitive improvement was also identified [92]. Based on this analysis, for patients with a disease duration of 10 years or more, memory improvement was best achieved through task-based programs administered for at least 8 weeks (3–4 sessions/week, 46–60 min/session). For patients with a disease duration of less than 10 years, information processing speed was most effectively enhanced using RehaCom software (1–3 sessions/week, 46–60 min/session) [92] (RehaCom is recognized as Europe’s leading software for cognitive rehabilitation, and its effectiveness has been confirmed, http://www.Schuhfried.at [93]).

Additionally, evidence also indicates that cognitive behavioral therapy, whose therapeutic benefits are highly dependent on the treatment protocol, is effective in improving psychological and social functioning and lifestyle outcomes [94], as well as reducing fatigue [95] in MS patients. Fatigue education, at least in the short term, can be effective in reducing fatigue in MS patients [96]. Psychological interventions have proven effective across a broad spectrum of outcome domains in MS patients, including mental health, cognition, physical function, and quality of life, thereby providing robust evidence to support their integration into clinical care [97, 98]. Moreover, virtual reality has emerged as an effective therapeutic modality for MS, contributing to significant improvements in balance, quality of life, and emotional well-being [99–102].

Collectively, accumulating evidence supports the notion that the incorporation of cognitive training into MS management might be a viable and effective adjunctive approach.

Social participation

MS patients often experience significant impairments in social engagement following their diagnosis, a situation that is closely linked to progressive functional decline and the psychosocial burden of disease-related stigma. This social withdrawal can lead to increased social isolation [103–105] and even a heightened risk of suicide [106], underscoring the critical need for social support interventions, which have recently emerged as a therapeutic focus in MS care [107].

A growing body of evidence indicates that strong social support systems not only exert significant positive impacts on health-related quality of life but also effectively alleviate depressive symptoms in MS patients [108, 109]. A study involving 150 MS patients confirmed that emotional social support contributes to an improvement in MS-related depressive symptoms [110]. Additionally, the Patient Support Programs that incorporate webinars, home care, nursing follow-ups, peer experience sharing, and peer support, have significantly enhanced treatment adherence, self-management, and overall health [111]. Furthermore, higher levels of social capital, refers to the many resources derived from the social interactions between individuals and groups, are associated with lower physical and psychological impacts of MS [112].

Mixed rehabilitation programs

Although there is a lack of large-scale multicenter randomized controlled trials, current evidence strongly implies that combined rehabilitation programs offer significant synergistic benefits in MS management [113]. This aligns with the emerging model of the MS-specialized ward, which is designed to address the comprehensive, multidisciplinary treatment needs of MS patients by integrating care from neurologists, physical therapists, psychologists, and other specialists [114].

Compared to neuromotor rehabilitation alone, MS patients who received integrated cognitive and neuromotor training, administered through ERICA software (Esercizi di Riabilitazione Cognitiva, http://www.erica.giuntios.it/it/) and paper–pencil tasks, and complemented by traditional neuromotor rehabilitation, exhibited superior therapeutic outcomes in emotional, motor, and cognitive aspects [115]. Moreover, existing evidence indicated that RRMS patients who participated in an intensive computer-based cognitive rehabilitation program using RehaCom software, in addition to conventional physical therapy, demonstrated significantly greater reductions in fatigue and greater improvements in cognitive abilities compared to those who received only conventional physical therapy [116]. Additionally, a combined cognitive-motor approach appeared to be more beneficial than administering cognitive or motor rehabilitation separately [117]. This is further supported by a systematic review and meta-analysis of 6 studies (n = 186 MS patients), which specifically demonstrated that dual-task training significantly improves executive function, as measured by the Frontal Assessment Battery [118]. Research involving both RRMS and progressive MS patients revealed that a 6-week integrated protocol combining aerobic exercise with cognitive training improved cognitive performance more effectively than aerobic exercise alone [119]. However, a CogEx trial enrolling 104 progressive MS patients across four sites failed to show that combined cognitive rehabilitation and aerobic exercise treatments offered additional cognitive benefits compared to treatments administered in isolation or sham treatments [120]. Similarly, a multicentered randomized trial, focusing exclusively on progressive MS patients, found combined cognitive rehabilitation plus exercise did not improve processing speed in these patients obviously, underscoring the importance of tailored rehabilitation strategies that consider the specific characteristics of the disease courses [121].

Cellular and molecular mechanisms of non-pharmacological treatment approaches in patients and animal models of MS

Thanks to advancements in molecular biology and neuroimaging technologies, we now have a deeper understanding of non-pharmacological interventions. Understanding the mechanisms behind non-pharmacological interventions in clinical practice (Table 2) can provide further valuable insights into the pathophysiology of MS, particularly regarding how they eliminate pathogenic factors and enhance tissue repair mechanisms (Table 3); these insights may, in turn, facilitate the development of new therapeutic approaches. In brief, the present review illuminated the roles of non-pharmacological therapies in regulating immune and inflammatory responses, inhibiting microglial and astrocytic activations, promoting oligodendroglial regeneration and remyelination, as well as enhancing neuroprotective effects (Fig. 2).

Modulation of inflammatory and immune reponses

Physical exercise

Numerous clinical studies have shown the beneficial effects of exercise for MS patients [42–44]; however, the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying these benefits remain unclear, primarily due to challenges in obtaining CNS tissue samples from MS patients. As a result, current human studies predominantly rely on peripheral blood biomarkers to infer the mechanisms of exercise [122]. In this scenario, some of clinical findings have not been consistently aligned with preclinical study results. For instance, there is significant evidence of exercise-induced changes in circulating inflammatory markers (e.g., IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, IL-17, IL-4, IL-12, adipokines) in MS patients [123, 124]. We speculate that these inconsistencies may arise from limited sample sizes, the heterogeneous MS subtypes enrolled in studies, and confounding factors such as sex differences. These discrepancies underscore critical gaps in translating preclinical observations to human populations suffering from MS.

In regard to preclinical animal studies, a substantial amount of evidence has demonstrated that physical exercise can significantly influence the progression of MS by modulating both innate and adaptive immune responses in peripheral and central nervous systems [125]. Generally, physical exercise can be categorized into forced exercise and voluntary exercise. Forced exercise has documented immunomodulatory effects on experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) models, which serve as an animal model for MS [126, 127]. For instance, a 5-week preconditioning exercise training (spanning 3 weeks before immunization to 2 weeks post immunization), including strength training through weighted ladder climbing and endurance training using treadmills, significantly reduced clinical scores in adult female EAE model mice and decreased expressions of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IFN-γ, IL-17, and IL-1β in the spinal cord [128]. Similar result has also been observed with weighted swimming training [129], and further indicated that the long-term benefits of preconditioning swimming training may be achieved through reduced infiltration of CD4+, CD8 + T cells and B cells in the spinal cord [130]. Additionally, studies using proteolipid protein (PLP)-induced transfer EAE model showed that 6 weeks of motorized treadmill running in 6 ~ 7-week-old female SJL/JCrHsd mice markedly improved EAE performance by inhibiting immune system responses to an auto-antigen rather than broadly suppressing the immune system functions [131].

On the other hand, voluntary exercise, which requires no external motivation and more closely resembles natural activity patterns, is particularly suitable for long-term studies [132]. Recent research indicated that providing access to running wheel from the day of immunization significantly ameliorated motor disability, and reduced IL-1β levels in the hippocampal CA1 region of 11-week-old female C57BL/6 mice, when compared to those mice in standard cages [133]. Similar findings were also observed in male C57BL/6J mice, where EAE model mice with unrestricted access to running wheels exhibited less severe neurological impairment and a later onset of disease than those mice with a locked wheel, along with significantly reduced immune cell infiltration in the ventral white matter tracts of the lumbar spinal cord [134]. Notably, even daily access to a running wheel just for 1 h significantly delayed the onset of clinical signs of the disease, and reduced the number of infiltrating CD3 + T cells in the spinal cord dorsal horn [135].

Moreover, a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis has revealed that exercise intensity and protocols significantly impact symptoms of MS patients [136], which has been corroborated in preclinical animal models, and linked to immunomodulatory mechanisms [125–127]. An animal study involving 6 ~ 7-week-old female SJL/JCrHsd mice confirmed that high-intensity continuous treadmill training improved clinical severity in PLP-induced transfer EAE model mice, decreasing the microglial release of IL-6 and MCP-1 by 32% and 25% respectively [137], thereby significantly inhibiting the propagation of autoimmune neuroinflammation. High-intensity swimming (swimming with a weight load of 4% body weight) also showed the similar outcomes, significantly increasing Treg cell numbers and decreasing Th1 and Th17 populations in the spinal cord compared to moderate-intensity exercise (swimming without weight load). This variation led to increases of anti-inflammatory factors IL-10 and TGF-β, while reduced of pro-inflammatory factors IFN-γ and IL-17 [138].

Collectively, these preclinical findings suggest that physical exercise promotes an overall anti-inflammatory shift in the pathological microenvironment of MS, although the precise mechanisms underlying immune regulation by physical exercise require further systematic investigation.

Dietary supplies

Dietary supplies have emerged as a critical determinant of inflammatory pathophysiology and disease trajectory in MS [60–63]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials indicated that multiple dietary interventions mitigate systemic inflammation in MS patients, with the therapeutic effect becoming more pronounced over longer durations [139]. Mechanistic studies have revealed that both specific nutritional components and dietary patterns exert profound immunomodulatory effects in MS pathogenesis [64]. A systematic review including 7 studies indicated that fish oil/omega-3 supplementation (a recommendation for daily supplementation with 4 gm of omega-3/fish oil) may improve quality of life in MS patients, potentially through modulations in inflammatory markers, glutathione reductase activity, and the omega-6 to omega-3 ratio [140]. Another meta-analysis, including 3 studies and 178 MS patients, revealed a beneficial effect of prebiotics on mental health, with the proposed mechanism involving the reduction of insulin resistance, inflammation, and oxidative stress [68].

In regard to preclinical animal studies, daily administration of 100 mg/kg body weight of dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), an endogenous omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid metabolite, significantly attenuated disease severity in relapse-remitting experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (RR-EAE) mice model by reducing the polarization of naïve T-cells toward pro-inflammatory Th1 and Th17 phenotypes [141]. Additionally, another experiment using spontaneous opticospinal encephalomyelitis (OSE) model confirmed that dietary supplementation with non-fermentable fiber (26% cellulose formulation) during early adulthood (3 ~ 4-week-old, post-weaning) conferred durable neuroprotection through gut microbiota-mediated immunoregulation, effectively rebalancing the Th1/Th2 axis and associated inflammatory mediators [142].

In the dietary pattern aspect, systematic reviews and meta-analyses have established that the ketogenic diet favorably impacts the inflammation in MS [72, 143]. A 6-month phase II trial involving 65 patients with RRMS demonstrated that a ketogenic diet led to improvements in fatigue, depression, and quality of life, alongside decreased leptin and increased adiponectin levels [144]. In addition, data from a randomized controlled trial indicated that intermittent calorie restriction is associated with a reduction in memory T cells [145] and an increase in CD45RO+ regulatory T cells [80].

In preclinical animal studies, a ketogenic diet has been shown to significantly alleviate EAE clinical severity in a gut microbiota-dependent manner, which was associated with decreased Th17 activation [146]. Periodic 3-day cycles of a fasting mimicking diet (FMD) effectively ameliorated symptoms in EAE mice with a remarkable 75% reduction in spinal cord CD11b+ cell infiltration by day 14 after the first sign of EAE. Similar results were observed in autoreactive T cells, where CD4 + T cell displayed a 4-fold decrease by day 3, and sustained a low level until day 14 [147]. Additionally, intermittent fasting has been demonstrated to improve clinical performance by reshaping gut bacteria diversity and enhancing anti-oxidative microbial metabolic pathway, leading to a decrease in IL-17-producing T cells and an increase in regulatory T cells, which is further supported by a pilot study involving 16 RRMS patients [148].

Overall, studies on mechanisms demonstrate that the immunological microenvironment in MS animal models reacts to particular nutrients and dietary patterns. Nevertheless, the application of these findings is constrained by methodological variability in study designs and insufficient robust evidence regarding disease-modifying effectiveness. This shortcoming highlights the need for well-designed, prospective clinical trials to thoroughly assess the therapeutic potential of dietary supplies in MS.

Social support and interaction

Current research on the immunomodulatory effects of social support and interaction in MS animal models still remains limited. Available evidence from studies using Theiler’s virus-induced MS models showed that social disruption stress applied before infection in male BALB/CJ mice resulted in glucocorticoid resistance and a more severe disease progression through the mediation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 [149, 150], suggesting that negative social experiences may dysregulate fundamental immune-homeostatic mechanisms in MS pathogenesis. In addition, in patients with both MS and major depressive disorder, social support appeared to mitigate the impact of changes in depression on the production of IFN-γ, a cytokine playing a crucial role in MS pathogenesis [151]. This study revealed that over the 16 weeks of treatment, there was a noticeable reduction in both depression and IFN-γ production [151].

However, significant knowledge gaps remain regarding the translational relevance of these findings to human MS. Future research should adopt integrated translational approaches that combine carefully designed preclinical models with clinical studies [107]. This would systematically evaluate how psychosocial interventions could positively influence disease course and treatment responsiveness in MS patients. Such investigations could yield novel therapeutic strategies that incorporate psychosocial modulation as an essential component of comprehensive MS care.

Mixed rehabilitation programs

Research on multidimensional interventions in MS has shown a significant modulation of inflammatory and immune response in its early pathological stages [152, 153]. Evidence indicates that for MS patients, the integration of computer-based cognitive training within a standard physical therapy program effectively improved the disease condition by reducing the serum levels of TNF-α and IFN-γ [116].

From a preclinical aspect, animal models offer valuable insights into the neurobiological mechanisms that underpin combined rehabilitation programs. Environmental enrichment (EE), an experimental paradigm that incorporates physical exercise, dietary supplies, cognitive training, and social participation through enhanced housing conditions (such as larger cages with more toys, better food and nutrition, and moderately increased mouse density per cage), acts as strong preclinical evidence for combined rehabilitation programs. Notably, this paradigm has consistently shown disease-modifying effects in MS animal models, suggesting a promising potential for clinical translation [152, 153].

Although current preclinical evidence is limited, it has shown that EE offers beneficial immunomodulatory effects through multiple mechanisms. For instance, studies using EAE mouse model indicated that EE alleviates disease symptoms by preventing EAE-induced disturbances in the thymus [154]. Mechanistically, EE inhibited neuroinflammation through glucocorticoid receptor-mediated modulation of the Th1 cell response, a process that depends on hypothalamic brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)-induced activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis [154]. Additionally, another study revealed that EE exerts neuroprotective actions within the CNS microenvironment by improving synaptic function and preserving glutamate transmission [155]. Furthermore, across various MS models, EE has been shown to downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and TNF-α) and upregulate anti-inflammatory factors (e.g., TGF-β), thereby resulting in a multifaceted suppression of neuroinflammatory processes [120]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that combinatorial rehabilitation approaches can profoundly reshape the neuroimmune landscape in MS. The multimodal effects of EE, spanning from systemic immunomodulation to synaptic protection, highlight its potential as a therapeutic strategy deserving of further clinical exploration [152, 153]. Future research should aim to clarify the temporal dynamics of these interventions, and to develop standardized protocols for their potential application in MS management.

Critical summary and recapitulation

A substantial amount of preclinical research employing MS animal models has established that physical exercise, dietary supplies, psychosocial support and their various combinations can differentially regulate inflammation and immune responses, consistently promoting a shift toward anti-inflammatory homeostasis [60–64, 125–127, 149, 150, 152, 153]. Despite these promising preclinical findings and observed clinical correlations with symptom improvement in MS patients, significant knowledge gaps remain concerning the precise inflammatory mechanisms underlying these therapeutic effects. The existing evidence base lacks both consistency and mechanistic depth in its connection between intervention-induced immunomodulation and clinical outcomes [60–64, 125–127, 149, 150, 152, 153]. This highlights the critical need for well-designed, large-scale prospective clinical studies that can simultaneously: (1) validate the therapeutic efficacy of these non-pharmacological approaches in MS patients, and (2) elucidate their fundamental biological mechanisms through integrated multi-faceted analyses. Such comprehensive investigations eventually would lay the essential scientific groundwork for developing precision rehabilitation protocols and optimizing clinical management strategies tailored to individual MS patients.

Suppression of microglial and astrocytic activations

Physical exercise

Microglia, the resident immune cells of the CNS, play pivotal roles in maintaining homeostasis through various functions, including antigen presentation, pathogen clearance, synaptic pruning, and cytokine secretion [156]. In the context of MS pathogenesis, microglia exhibit a dynamic phenotypic transition, progressively shifting from pro-inflammatory stage during acute phases to anti-inflammatory stage in chronic disease stages [157]. Compelling preclinical evidence indicates that physical exercise induces significant, stage-dependent modulation of microglial activity in MS [36, 133, 135, 158]. For instance, in EAE mice models, physical exercise exerted an early and long-lasting mitigating effect on microgliosis, and preceded its neuroprotective action. Specifically, voluntary wheel running significantly reduced microgliosis and microglial TNF release, resulting in decreased neuroinflammation [133]. Even daily access to a running wheel for just 1 h significantly delayed disease onset, partly by reducing pro-inflammatory microglia in the spinal cord dorsal horn [135]. Advanced analyses in lysolecithin-induced focal demyelination models, where Iba1 labeled cells were classified into CD206 anti-inflammatory or CD16/32 pro-inflammatory subsets, revealed that physical exercise preferentially promoted the polarization of microglia toward a protective phenotype [36]. Similarly, the therapeutic benefits of voluntary wheel running have been observed in cuprizone-induced demyelination models, where improvements in neuromuscular function and motor coordination were associated with attenuated microglia activation in the corpus callosum and striatum [158].

Furthermore, training intensity determined microglial responses, as evidenced by high-intensity continuous training producing a 61% reduction in Iba1+/iNOS+ (pro-inflammatory stage) microglial population without affecting Iba1+/Arg-1+ (anti-inflammatory stage) population during the acute phase of EAE. This suggests that the neuroprotective effects of high-intensity continuous training may arise from the selective suppression of neurotoxic microglial activation [137]. While recent evidence from cuprizone-induced demyelination models showed that low-intensity forced wheel-running exercise reduced hippocampal microglial activation and conferred a beneficial neuroinflammatory effect, the concomitant elevation in TNF-α implies an incomplete suppression of microglial pro-inflammatory activity [159]. Collectively, these findings across diverse MS models suggest that: (1) the dynamic phenotypic transition of microglia is a key mechanism through which physical exercise affects MS progression; (2) physical exercise consistently attenuates abnormal microglia activation, thereby creating a favorable microenvironment for neural repair and functional recovery.

Astrocyte, the predominant glia cell in the CNS, serves critical functions in maintaining homeostasis and providing metabolic support to neurons [160]. In the context of MS pathology, astrocyte exhibits dualistic effects; their activation state can either exacerbate or ameliorate disease processes [161, 162]. On one hand, reactive astrocytes, as identified by glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), Vimentin, C3, disrupted blood-brain barrier integrity, facilitating the infiltration of peripheral immune cells, which led to neuroinflammation and demyelination [161–164]. Conversely, astrocytes can exert neuroprotective effects by secreting matrix metalloproteinases, which help for remodeling extracellular matrix components and supporting remyelination [161, 162]. Experimental evidence from cuprizone-induced MS models demonstrated that 6 weeks of voluntary wheel running resulted in a marked reduction of GFAP protein levels within the corpus callosum, suggesting that exercise mitigates pathological astrogliosis [158]. Although the precise mechanisms underlying this phenomenon require further elucidation, these findings align with emerging evidence that positions astrocytes as a key mediator of exercise-drug synergies in MS treatment. Notably, a compelling study in EAE models showed that regular treadmill exercise prior to pharmacological treatment can modify the effects of the treatment administered after the first relapse, which is likely due to decreased astrocytic response influenced by exercise [165], indicating that astrocytes might also be a crucial target for enhancing the efficacy of exercise-induced therapies.

Mixed rehabilitation programs

Current evidence regarding the effects of mixed rehabilitation programs on the activation of microglia and astrocyte in MS remains limited. Insufficient evidence from adult rat cortical models with chronic IL-1β overexpression, which was designed to mimic key aspects of MS neuropathology, showed that EE interventions can effectively improve cognitive impairment and reduce anxiety-like behaviors. These improvements appear to correlate with measurable decreases in both microgliosis and astrogliosis within affected brain regions [120]. However, the fundamental mechanisms underlying these observed effects remain uncharacterized and require further investigation.

Critical summary and recapitulation

In the context of neuroinflammation, resident microglia and astrocyte progressively lose their homeostatic maintenance functions, adopting instead reactive configurations marked by significant alterations in gene expression profiles, cellular morphology, and functional states. These changes collectively reshape the disease microenvironment, critically influencing tissue repair and disease course [160]. Both physical exercise and mixed rehabilitation strategies have shown the ability to facilitate the polarization of microglia toward anti-inflammatory stage, while simultaneously attenuating pathological astrogliosis. Nonetheless, current research has yet to fully elucidate the mechanistic underpinnings of how non-pharmacological therapies regulate glial cell behavior in MS, highlighting an urgent need for more comprehensive investigations aimed at understanding the permissive effects of microglia and astrocyte for neuroprotection and repair.

Promotion of oligodendroglial regeneration and remyelination

Physical exercise

The progressive nature of MS results in chronic susceptibility of oligodendroglia within the inflammatory CNS microenvironment. Although these myelin-producing cells exhibit remarkable regenerative capacities in the early phase of the disease, their reparative functions gradually decline over time [166, 167]. Recent studies have underscored the positive effects of exercise interventions on the dynamics of oligodendroglial lineage in conditions characterized by demyelination [36, 168]. Increasing evidence from carefully executed clinical-pathological and neuroscience research, although constrained by limited sample sizes, consistently indicated a strong correlation between the capacity of endogenous remyelination and favorable clinical outcomes in MS [169, 170]. Exercise-based interventions in MS patients showed a potential for remyelination; meanwhile, patients who exhibited more remyelination were often associated with significantly lower rates of disability progression [169, 170]. A systematic review involving 4 articles indicated that constraint induced movement therapy, a well-established neurorehabilitation approach, is beneficial for enhancing white matter integrity [171]. This constraint-induced movement therapy led to significant enhancement in limb usage among patients with hemiparetic MS, which coincided with enhanced structural integrity in white matter regions, such as the contralateral corpus callosum, ipsilateral superior occipital gyrus, ipsilateral superior temporal gyrus, and contralateral corticospinal tracts [172]. Furthermore, high-intensity, task-oriented, visual feedback training using a video game balanced board system resulted in microstructural changes in the superior cerebellar peduncles in MS patients, as evidenced by fractional anisotropy and radial diffusivity [173]; additionally, facilitative physiotherapy, which includes somatosensory stimuli, significantly impacted brain microstructure as assessed by diffusion tensor imaging [174]. These techniques are valuable for assessing myelination-related changes in humans [172–174].

Preclinical animal studies provide additional support for the positive influence of exercise on the improvement of myelination. In lysolecithin-induced focal demyelination models using 8 ~ 12-week-old female C57BL/6 mice, voluntary running wheel exercise significantly promoted oligodendrogenesis. Quantitative analyses indicated a 32% increase in oligodendrocyte lineage cell density at lesion sites on day 7 post-injury, and a 30% increase on day 14. This regenerative effect was attributed to exercise, which prolonged the proliferation of oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) in demyelinated areas, thereby supporting subsequent oligodendrocyte differentiation and maturation [36]. Additionally, comparative studies using cuprizone-induced demyelination models demonstrated varying therapeutic outcomes based on exercise parameters. Both high-intensity interval training and low-intensity continuous training effectively prevented cuprizone-induced abnormal neurological movements, but high-intensity interval training was superior in restoring oligodendroglial populations, suggesting a greater neuroprotective potential [168]. These findings collectively emphasize the role of exercise-induced oligodendrogenesis as a promising therapeutic target for demyelinating conditions, while also underscoring the importance of optimizing exercise parameters for maximizing reparative effects in MS.

The CNS utilizes endogenous remyelination mechanisms to restore functional integrity after demyelination [175]. Emerging evidence suggests that physical exercise may positively influence this reparative process [176]; however, a fundamental mechanistic question remains about whether exercise primarily enhances de novo remyelination or merely reduces ongoing myelin damage. Studies examining interventions post-demyelination offer critical insights into this temporal relationship. One study using EAE models tested this hypothesis by immunizing 10-week-old female C57BL/6 mice, which revealed significant memory impairment alongside hippocampal demyelination. Following treadmill training (30 min/day, 5 times/week, for 4 weeks) starting on day 20 post-immunization, these pathological changes were partially reversed [177]. Further research has clarified the cellular origins of activity-dependent remyelination [36, 178]. In a study of lysolecithin-induced focal demyelination, voluntary wheel running notably enhanced oligodendrogenesis, the rate of remyelination, and the proportion of remyelinated axons, suggesting that newly generated oligodendrocytes are a primary source for reforming myelin sheaths [36]. However, a study using cuprizone-mediated demyelination observed through two-photon microscopy indicated that motor learning after partial remyelination not only enhanced oligodendrogenesis and subsequent axonal remyelination, but also promoted the ability of surviving oligodendrocytes to form new myelin sheaths [178]. Collectively, these findings across various preclinical models have established exercise as a significant modulator of oligodendroglial biology, fostering a microenvironment conducive to both oligodendroglial regeneration and remyelination, even as the underlying mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated.

Mixed rehabilitation programs

Our current understanding of how mixed rehabilitation programs influence oligodendroglial biology and remyelination in MS is still evolving [152, 153]. However, accumulating preclinical evidence indicates that EE exerts robust effects on oligodendroglial recruitment and differentiation during myelin repair [179, 180]. In both lysolecithin-induced focal demyelination model and EAE model, EE markedly promoted cell proliferation in the subventricular zone (SVZ) and enhanced migration of SVZ-derived progenitor cells to the demyelinated lesions. Specifically, in the EAE mice model, EE facilitated the differentiation of SVZ-derived cells into oligodendroglial lineage within the lesioned areas, suggesting that EE creates a supportive condition that boosts endogenous repair mechanisms [179].

At the molecular level, further mechanistic insights come from studies using acute demyelination models, which revealed that serum-derived exosomes from rats housed in an EE-condition are enriched with miR-219, a key regulator of oligodendroglial differentiation, promoting the proliferation and differentiation of OPCs by targeting the expression of NeuroD1, PDGFRα, and ELOVL7 [180]. Hence, these findings illustrate that EE orchestrates both cellular recruitment and molecular reprogramming to enhance repair mechanisms in MS.

Critical summary and recapitulation

Both physical exercise and mixed rehabilitation approaches have demonstrated beneficial effects on oligodendroglial regeneration and remyelination across various preclinical MS models. These interventions facilitate myelin repair through complementary mechanisms: (1) reactivation of surviving oligodendroglia, allowing them to extend new processes and re-ensheathe denuded axons, and (2) enhanced differentiation of OPCs into functionally competent myelinating oligodendrocytes, which establish new myelin segments. Notably, comparative functional analyses reveal that physical exercise can induce remyelination comparable to certain pro-myelinating pharmacological agents, with synergistic enhancement observed in combination therapies [36]. These findings underscore the importance of identifying the precise molecular pathways through which exercise and other non-pharmacological interventions exert their effects on oligodendroglial lineage.

Enhancement of neuroprotective processes

Physical exercise

The disability of MS results from neuronal damage within the CNS [10]; and numerous studies have shown that exercise has neuroprotective effects [181]. Neuroimaging evidence supported these neuroprotective effects of physical exercise in MS patients. In pediatric MS patients, higher levels of strenuous physical activity were correlated with lower relapse rates and lower T2 lesion volumes [182]. Furthermore, a 12-week community-based “start-to-run” program demonstrated improvements in various aspects, including aerobic capacity, functional mobility, visuospatial memory, fatigue, and overall quality of life, which may be associated with an increase in pallidum volume [183]. Additionally, task-oriented training, such as upper limb motor training [184], action observation training [185], and visuomotor task [186], has further confirmed the neuroprotective effects of exercise, although current imaging limitations prevent definitive exclusion of white matter-related confounders [187].

The neuroprotective effects of exercise in MS may also involve neurotrophic signaling pathways, which include BDNF, glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), nerve growth factor (NGF), and ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF). Among these pathways, BDNF is particularly important for promoting the survival of neurons and oligodendrocytes, modulating neurotransmitters and ions, and enhancing neuroplasticity [188]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses that analyzed peripheral biomarkers have demonstrated exercise-induced alterations in circulating BDNF levels among individuals with MS [189, 190]. An 8-week combined exercise training program improved physical performance and elevated serum BDNF levels [191]. Similarly, 24 weeks of endurance and resistance training resulted in a comparable increase in BDNF levels in RRMS patients [192]. Notably, the responses of neurotrophic factors exhibited variability depending on the exercise protocols [189]. For instance, low-level aerobic training improved coordinative function, physical fitness and quality of life without affecting BDNF or NGF levels [193]; whereas, aquatic endurance training (unlike overland training) activated BDNF signaling pathways after just 3 weeks [194].

Preclinical animal studies indicated that voluntary wheel running, initiated on the day of immunization and sustained throughout the disease course, significantly improved motor function in EAE mice. Notably, during the acute phase (days 21–28 post immunization), exercise promoted the survival of parvalbumin-positive interneurons and prevented abnormalities of synaptic plasticity in CA1 region [133]. Additional studies further documented an exercise-induced preservation of neuronal viability and function across diverse exercise protocols [130, 134]. However, it is important to note that elucidating the direct neuroprotective effects of exercise in MS remains methodologically challenging due to the confounding interactions with inflammatory processes and glial responses [181, 195, 196]. Limited evidence attempted to clarify the regulatory mechanisms of exercise affecting neurons [197]. One potential pathway involves the cannabinoid receptor CB1R. Data showed that exercise can activate the endocannabinoid system, the stimulation of CB1R promoted synaptic plasticity in both rodents and humans [198]. Conversely, EAE mice lacking CB1R exhibited a more severe clinical progression and greater synaptotoxicity [199]. Furthermore, access to a running wheel restored the diminished sensitivity of striatal GABA synapses to CB1R stimulation in EAE mice [197], suggesting a key role of CB1R in exercise-induced neuroprotection.

Evidence from preclinical animal studies revealed that consistent swimming exercise prior to the modeling (30 min/day, 5 days/week, for 6 weeks) significantly upregulated BDNF expression while improving clinical outcomes in EAE mice [129]. Similar findings were observed in cuprizone-induced demyelination models [168]. However, variations in exercise protocols, animal models, and MS types have led to differing results. For instance, in 8-week-old female Lewis rats, a 10-day forced treadmill running failed to induce differential BDNF expression between EAE rats and controls [200]. Collectively, these findings establish exercise as a powerful intervention for neuroprotection in preclinical MS models, while highlighting the necessity for further studies to clarify the mechanistic differences induced by exercise.

Therefore, current evidence demonstrated that moderate-to-high intensity exercise fosters multidimensional neurorestorative effects in MS through enhanced neuroplasticity and neurotrophic support, collectively creating a pro-regenerative microenvironment in the CNS [182–186, 191–194]. Future therapeutic strategies could incorporate advanced neuroimaging techniques and peripheral biomarkers to optimize personalized exercise prescriptions that consider disease heterogeneity while maximizing clinical outcomes.

Cognitive training

Cognitive rehabilitation strategies for MS patients currently focus on computer-assisted training that targets attention, working memory, episodic memory, and mental visual imagery (specifically designed to restore autobiographic memory) [89]. Advanced functional neuroimaging techniques, particularly functional magnetic resonance imaging, have clarified how training induces neuroplastic changes in both patterns of regional brain activation and functional connectivity within targeted neural networks [201]. Additionally, specific adaptations have been consistently documented in key brain regions, notably the precuneus, prefrontal cortical regions, and cingulate cortex following cognitive training in MS patients [202–204].

Despite these advancements, the challenges of interpreting mechanisms from human brain tissue samples and applying the cognitive rehabilitation strategies to preclinical animal models leave the neuropathological mechanisms associated with cognitive training’s impact on MS unclear. To address this, in the future, researchers should prioritize the development of optimized preclinical animal models that can both respond to and accurately detect the effects of cognitive training interventions. Such models would provide critical mechanistic insights into: (1) how cognitive rehabilitation modulates the CNS microenvironment in MS at cellular and molecular levels, and (2) the neurobiological basis for its clinical efficacy.

Mixed rehabilitation programs

Emerging evidence from animal studies revealed that EE induces significant neuroplastic adaptations, including modulation of neuronal activity and neurotrophic factor expression [205–207]. Mechanistic preclinical studies further identified that EE exerts neuroprotective effects in EAE models through BDNF-mediated pathways, specifically by upregulating hypothalamic BDNF and subsequently normalizing HPA axis dysfunction [154]. This, in turn, led to attenuated thymic disturbance and improved clinical outcomes. These findings strongly implicate BDNF signaling as a key mediator of EE’s therapeutic effects in MS pathogenesis. While clinical studies have documented the neuroprotective benefits of mixed rehabilitation approaches in MS patients [115, 119, 208], as previously reviewed, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying these observed clinical improvements remain to be fully characterized.

Critical summary and recapitulation

Accumulating preclinical evidence indicates that exercise-based interventions exert profound neuromodulatory effects in MS, including the functional optimization of neuronal networks and dynamic regulation of neurotrophic factors in the CNS microenvironment, which collectively contribute to the modification of the disease course. However, data demonstrating the potential neurobiological benefits of cognitive training and mixed rehabilitation programs on neuronal circuitry are still limited, with poorly characterized pathophysiological mechanisms.

Conclusion and future perspective

Current non-pharmacological interventions are beneficial for the treatment of MS. In this review, we comprehensively analyze various non-pharmacological approaches, including physical exercise, dietary supplies, cognitive training, social participation, and mixed rehabilitation programs, with a focus on their effects on immune and inflammatory regulation, microglial and astrocytic activation, oligodendroglial regeneration and remyelination, and neuroprotective effects in preclinical MS animal models and MS patients.

Indeed, while current non-pharmacological therapies cannot replace DMTs of MS, their significance is increasingly being acknowledged. This recognition is evident in several aspects: (1) compared to the lengthy timelines and high costs associated with novel drug development, the integration of non-pharmacological approaches with established first-line drugs shows considerable promise, as demonstrated by preclinical studies [36, 165, 209]. (2) non-pharmacological therapies seem to operate through independent mechanisms, enabling compromised cells to respond simultaneously to both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions, thereby promoting tissue regeneration [35]. (3) a deeper understanding of the mechanisms behind non-pharmacological therapies could help to customize a joint approach to facilitate more effective drug action and enhances therapeutic synergy.

Regarding treatment intensity, high-quality prospective data from 329 MS centers across 42 countries confirm that early intensive treatment in children with MS can significantly delay the progression of disability and lessen the risk of developing severe disability by about 60%, thereby maximizing the preservation of neural reserve in children with RRMS [210]. Additionally, multiple clinical trials have also revealed preliminary success in stem cell transplantations, including hematopoietic stem cells [211], mesenchymal stem cells [212, 213], and neural stem cells [214, 215]. Although some issues remain unresolved, the recent advancements in MS research instill optimism that may improve function or symptoms and potentially complement pharmacological therapies for MS.

Despite the promising applications for non-pharmacological interventions, several issues still require further exploration. (1) the molecular pathways through which these non-pharmacological interventions affect the MS microenvironment need to be elucidated. For example, how does exercise regulate inflammation? Is there a potential physiological pathway, such as the muscle-brain axis? (2) how can individualized treatment plans be developed based on the pathological microenvironment to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes? What constitutes the ideal rehabilitation strategy for each patient, and when is the best time to implement it? (3) most existing studies tend to focus on the impact of single factors on MS, with insufficient exploration of the synergistic effects that different intervention strategies might have. This overlooked aspect may be more significant than previously recognized [113]. Therefore, understanding how various non-pharmacological interventions can be combined and, more broadly, how to optimize the integration of drug and non-drug treatments under specific conditions to clarify the changes within the pathological microenvironment may be crucial in addressing these challenges. (4) last but not the least, there are also technical limitations in human studies, particularly concerning anatomical sampling, assessments of synaptic function, and investigations into intervention-induced neuroplasticity changes [152]. As a result, human studies often remain observational and correlational, hindering the establishment of causal relationships and limiting deeper exploration of potential cellular and molecular foundations. In contrast, while animal models offer advantages like controllability and reproducibility, they cannot always accurately predict how humans will respond to treatment [152].

We must acknowledge that there are still limitations in this field of research. Currently approved DMTs have not demonstrated efficacy in the progressive forms of MS [216]. The impact of exercise on inflammatory markers lacks a unified conclusion, and a clear dose-response correlation has yet to be established. Further research is required to conduct meta-analyses on specific types of exercise to clarify this dose-effect correlation. These examples also indicate that studies on non-pharmacological approaches for MS are still in their early stages. The available evidence, along with mechanistic studies, is insufficient to create a ‘translation checklist’ for implementing non-pharmacological programs in clinical care. Therefore, continued research into the effects and underlying mechanisms of these approaches remains essential and holds promising prospects.

In this review, our main aim is to draw more attention to future studies on non-pharmacological approaches for MS treatment. We provide a narrative that serves as an instructive guide for considering effective outcomes and fundamental cellular and molecular mechanisms of these approaches, rather than imposing a restrictive framework. It is worth noting that this review does not systematically detail or compare classifications based on standard MS clinical endpoints, such as relapse metrics (e.g., annual relapse rate), disability progression (EDSS), walking function (25-Foot Walk), upper-limb function (9-Hole Peg Test), fatigue, depression, quality of life scores, and MRI criteria (T2 lesion load, brain atrophy or remyelination imaging).

Finally, we would like to reiterate that non-pharmacological interventions play significant therapeutic roles in improving the pathological microenvironment of MS, which may foster a brighter future for clinical MS management.
