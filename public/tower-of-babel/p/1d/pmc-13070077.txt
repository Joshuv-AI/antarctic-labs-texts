Targeting Ferroptosis and Necroptosis to Treat Stroke
Authors: Bautista-Perez Sandra Monserrat, Silva-Islas Carlos Alfredo, Sánchez-Thomas Rosina, Figueroa Alejandra, Barrera-Oviedo Diana, Maldonado Perla D
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIschemic stroke is a leading cause of death and long-term disability worldwide. It results from cerebral blood flow obstruction (ischemia) and, in some cases, the restoration of cerebral blood flow (reperfusion), triggering a cascade of pathophysiological events collectively known as the ischemic cascade and reperfusion injury, which leads to the activation of different regulated cell death types, and this review focuses on necroptosis and ferroptosis. Both pathways are closely linked to oxidative stress and contribute significantly to neuronal death and inflammation following ischemic stroke. Dysregulation of redox homeostasis, iron dyshomeostasis, glutathione depletion, and mitochondrial dysfunction are key events in neuronal damage. Understanding the interplay between oxidative stress and these pathways is crucial for developing effective neuroprotective therapies. This review highlights recent advances in understanding necroptosis and ferroptosis in ischemic stroke, proposing redox-targeted interventions as promising strategies to mitigate brain injury and improve outcomes in patients affected by this condition.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Necroptosis, Ferroptosis, Oxidative stress

Methods of Literature Search

Database and Search Terms

We conducted a systematic search across major electronic databases, including MEDLINE (PubMed). The search period was restricted to articles published between January 2005 and December 2025 related to necroptosis and ferroptosis in ischemic stroke.

In addition, a combination of keywords related to the aim of this review was employed. The primary search string was constructed using Boolean operators as follows:

("ischemic stroke" OR "cerebral ischemia" OR “brain ischemia") AND ("necroptosis" OR "ferroptosis") AND ("oxidative stress" OR "reactive oxygen species").

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following predefined criteria: 1) peer-reviewed original research; 2) studies conducted on cells, animals, and human subjects; and 3) articles published in English.

Exclusion criteria included case reports, conference abstracts, and editorial letters.

Data Extraction

Initial screening was performed by two independent reviewers based on titles and abstracts. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus or consultation with a third senior researcher.

Stroke

Stroke is a clinical syndrome characterized by the sudden onset of focal neurological deficits resulting from underlying cerebrovascular disease, being the second cause of death worldwide, only after ischemic heart disease, and it is expected to remain so in 2030. In addition, it is the leading neurological disease-causing disability in the world population [1, 2].

There are two types of strokes: ischemic and hemorrhagic [3]. Ischemic stroke is the most common type of stroke, accounting for 65.3% of all new cases globally. Each year, there are more than 7.8 million new ischemic strokes and approximately 3.6 million deaths, while nearly 70 million people worldwide are currently living with the effects of this condition. While the proportion of ischemic stroke is higher in high-income countries (74.9%) compared to low-to-middle-income countries (63.4%), the impact on younger populations is substantial, with over 49% of new cases and 59% of prevalent cases occurring in individuals under the age of 70 [4]. The most recent Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021, discussed by Feigin et al. [4], estimates that stroke is the third leading cause of death and disability combined, expressed as disability-adjusted life-years lost (DALYs) in America (11.4 million), Europe (18.7 million), Asia (112.8 million), and Africa (17.3 million).

According to this high incidence, prevalence, and mortality, this review focuses on ischemic stroke, which is caused by the interruption of blood supply to some areas of the brain, leading to a lack of oxygen and glucose, and subsequent loss of function and cell death [1]. The clinical manifestations will depend on the specific area affected. The abrupt disruption of blood supply to the brain can be caused by large-artery atherosclerosis, cardioembolism, small-vessel occlusion, or a determined or undetermined etiology, according to the Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) trial [5].

All patients with suspected acute ischemic stroke should undergo a brain imaging study, such as a noncontrast computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), to exclude intracerebral hemorrhage before thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy [6].

The goal of acute treatment for ischemic stroke is to optimize hemodynamic status and achieve early tissue reperfusion. Hemodynamic status is optimized by controlling fluid volume, blood pressure, and cardiovascular function [1]. Tissue reperfusion is achieved with intravenous (i.v.) thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy [7]. Recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA) is the treatment approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for acute ischemic stroke. rt-PA binds fibrin and converts plasminogen to plasmin, which degrades fibrin and dissolves the clot [8]. Alteplase is the oldest approved plasminogen activator used for ischemic stroke (1996). It is i.v. administered within 3 h after ischemic stroke symptoms onset with a dose of 0.9 mg/Kg (maximum 90 mg) over 60 min with an initial bolus of 10% [6]. Moreover, alteplase could be administered in patients until 4.5 h with the same dose, but considering the following exclusion criteria: a) older than 80 years old, b) with a National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score > 25, c) who take oral anticoagulants, d) with diabetes mellitus, or e) who have had a previous stroke [7, 9]. Additionally, alteplase could be administered to patients who awake with stroke symptoms and have a diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (DW-MRI) lesion smaller than ⅓ of the middle cerebral artery territory, with no change on T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) [6]. It can also be used in patients with focal neurological deficits without a differential diagnosis of stroke type. Moreover, alteplase is strictly contraindicated in patients with hemorrhagic stroke, tumors, abscesses, or any vascular malformation [7, 9].

In addition, tenecteplase, a derivative of alteplase with 3 amino acid substitutions, has several advantages over alteplase. Possess a prolonged half-life, major fibrin specificity, and resistance to the plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1). It was approved by the FDA in 2025 for acute ischemic stroke within 3 h after ischemic stroke symptoms onset with a dose of 15–25 mg according to patient weight (< 60 kg to ≥ 90 kg) in a single i.v. bolus; however, some clinical trials evaluated it within 4.5 h, with similar efficacy and safety to alteplase. Moreover, some studies suggest that tenecteplase is more effective than alteplase at lysing clots in large vessels [8, 10].

Mechanical thrombectomy is a procedure that removes a clot (thrombus) that obstructs cerebral blood flow in large vessels using stent-retrieval or aspiration. It is indicated in patients within 6 h after ischemic stroke symptoms onset, with a prestroke modified Rankin scale (mRS) score of 0 to 1, occlusion of the internal carotid or middle cerebral artery segment 1 (M1), age ≥ 18, NIHSS score ≥ 6, and Alberta stroke program early computed tomography score (ASPECTS) ≥ 6. Mechanical thrombectomy using stent-retrieval could be used in some patients with middle cerebral artery occlusion at segments 2 and 3 (M2 and M3). Additionally, mechanical thrombectomy is recommended for patients with large-vessel occlusion in the anterior circulation, with symptom onset within 6–24 h and DAWN eligibility criteria or 6–16 h and DEFUSE3 eligibility criteria [6].

Although rt-PA is the main pharmacological therapy in ischemic stroke, some adverse effects have been reported when it is administered after its therapeutic window. Experimental and clinical studies indicate that rt-PA may disrupt the blood–brain barrier [11], promote ischemic-to-hemorrhagic stroke transformation [12], enhance excitotoxicity [13], and amplify inflammatory [14] and proteolytic responses [15]. These secondary effects suggest that rt-PA can contribute to neurovascular injury during reperfusion and support the need to better understand its interactions with regulated cell death pathways, such as ferroptosis and necroptosis.

Importantly, patients who survive an ischemic stroke have an increased risk of recurrent events. Prevention of a new event involves antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy, as well as control of causal risk factors, with a focus on the underlying mechanisms [16].

Pathophysiology of Ischemic Stroke

Under physiological conditions, cerebral blood flow (50–60 mL/100 g tissue/min) supplies the oxygen and glucose necessary for normal brain function [17]. In neurons, ATP is primarily produced via the citric acid cycle, which is essential for maintaining the membrane potential and generating action potentials, thereby enabling neuronal communication [18]. An action potential induces the opening of voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCC), resulting in Ca2+ influx and promoting synaptic vesicle fusion with the plasma membrane, thereby releasing neurotransmitters such as glutamate [19]. Regulation of Ca2+ in the presynaptic neurons is conducted by Na+/Ca2+ exchanger (NCX), which pumps out Ca2+ with the cytoplasmic influx of Na+, a mechanism that depends on the Na+/K+-ATPase. Moreover, Ca2+ levels are regulated by the activity of the plasma membrane Ca2+-ATPase (PMCA) in an ATP-dependent mechanism. Glutamate release into the synaptic cleft promotes activation of N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (NMDAr), thereby allowing Ca2+ influx into the postsynaptic neuron, which is regulated by NCX, PMCA, and mitochondrial storage via the mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter (MCU) [20]. To prevent overactivation of NMDAr, astrocytes remove glutamate from the synaptic cleft via the excitatory amino acid transporter (EAAT), which co-transport Na+. The Na+ gradient in the astrocytes is maintained by the Na+/K+-ATPase [21] (Fig. 1).

A reduction in cerebral blood flow to 10–15% (4.8 to 8.4 mL/100 g of tissue/min) generates an area known as the ischemic core [22], where cells die within a few minutes of the reduction in cerebral blood flow, and the tissue is permanently damaged. Whereas, reductions of 28–70% (14.1 to 35.0 mL/100 g of tissue/min) result in the penumbra zone [22]. This region is reversibly injured and is considered a therapeutic target for reducing permanent tissue damage [23].

The reduction in cerebral blood flow activates mechanisms known as the ischemic cascade [24], resulting in cellular injury and death, ultimately leads to brain tissue damage. The mechanisms involved in the ischemic cascade include cellular bioenergetic failure, excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell death [25], affecting neurons, astrocytes, and microglia (Fig. 2).

A reduction in blood flow decreases oxygen and glucose delivery to the brain, leading to a switch from aerobic to anaerobic metabolism. Then, immediately decreases phosphocreatine content and the pH due to increased lactate production from anaerobic metabolism. The reduction in ATP levels is delayed by approximately 2 min, likely due to the consumption of phosphocreatine and glycogen stores [26]. ATP depletion results in an imbalance of ion gradients due to Na+/K+-ATPase dysfunction, leading to increased extracellular K+ and intracellular Na+ [27, 28], thereby depolarizing the neuron [29]. Elevated intracellular Na+ levels reverse the NCX ion transport, increasing intracellular Ca2+ levels [30]. Additionally, ATP depletion blocks Ca2+ efflux from the cytoplasm via the PMCA, thereby increasing cytoplasmic Ca2+ levels [31]. Severe ATP depletion during the ischemic phase promotes rapid necrosis in the ischemic core, thereby eliciting an initial inflammatory peak.

On the other hand, membrane depolarization (via increased extracellular K+ levels) activates VGCC, thereby increasing cytoplasmic Ca2+ levels [32]. Membrane depolarization and elevated intracellular Ca2+ levels contribute to glutamate release, thereby increasing its concentration in the synaptic cleft [33]. Furthermore, energy failure compromises glutamate clearance from the synaptic space via astrocytic EAAT [34, 35]. Glutamate accumulation in the synaptic space overactivates NMDAr, α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolpropionic acid (AMPA), and kainate receptors [33]. Specifically, excitotoxicity results from the overactivation of NMDAr (primarily the NR2B subunit), which induces excessive Ca2+ influx into the cytoplasm and triggers signaling pathways that ultimately lead to cell death [33]. A first peak of Ca2+ occurs immediately after cerebral blood flow reduction and lasts around 26 min, followed by a second and gradual increase in Ca2+ maintained for at least 3 h. The intracellular Ca2+ level is associated with the extent of cerebral damage [36]. This overload of intracellular Ca2+ activates Ca2+-dependent enzymes, such as lipases, proteases, phosphatases, endonucleases, and neuronal nitric oxide synthase, leading to cellular damage and death [33, 37]. Furthermore, mitochondria are susceptible to Ca2+ overload, which leads to dysfunction, increased ROS production, decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, release of cytochrome c into the cytoplasm, and subsequent activation of apoptosis [38–40]. Additionally, the interaction between the transient receptor potential melastatin 4 (TRPM4), an impermeable Ca2+ channel, and the NMDAr (NR2B subunit) can exacerbate excitotoxicity [41].

ROS are oxygen-derived molecules partially reduced and highly reactive [42], produced during normal metabolism by mitochondria, NADPH oxidase, xanthine oxidase, cytochrome P450, peroxisomes, endoplasmic reticulum [43], and other enzymes (around 50 different sources) [44]. Physiologically, ROS play a crucial role in cellular signaling, cell differentiation and proliferation, gene expression, epigenetic modifications, migration, angiogenesis, and regulation of vascular tone [44, 45]. However, an imbalance between ROS production and antioxidant defenses generates oxidative stress, in which lipids (lipoperoxidation, as indicated by malondialdehyde (MDA) and 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (4-HNE) levels), proteins (nitrotyrosine and carbonyl group levels), and DNA (8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine level) are oxidized. These oxidative modifications result in cellular damage and death (Fig. 2) [42].

ROS production is region-specific in the brain. According to the results of Vinokurov et al. [46], the brainstem and cerebellum exhibit the highest levels of ROS under resting conditions, which are associated with glial cells. Stimulation with ATP or glutamate leads to the highest ROS production in the midbrain, whereas lipoperoxidation is highest in the hippocampus and the midbrain [46]. The permanent reduction in cerebral blood flow increases ROS production for up to 100 min; however, ROS production increases at the onset of reperfusion, after 60 min of ischemia [47]. On the other hand, in hippocampal and cortical neuron cultures subjected to oxygen–glucose deprivation, ROS production occurs through three mechanisms. The first rise of ROS is generated by mitochondria at 3–7 min, followed by a second increase caused by xanthine oxidase. Finally, the third rise of ROS occurs after reoxygenation and glucose restitution (reperfusion), and mitochondria and NADPH oxidase are responsible for this last increase [48]. In particular, superoxide radical anion increases immediately after ischemia and reaches a steady state at 180 min [49]. In contrast, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) production is maintained for up to 48 h after the ischemic insult [50] (Figs. 2 and 3). Restoration of cerebral blood flow (reperfusion) increases ROS production, thereby activating cell death pathways, including apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis in the penumbra zone (Fig. 3).

As oxidative stress is an important mechanism in the pathophysiology of ischemic stroke, some formulations with antioxidant and neuroprotective properties have been explored. Among them, edaravone, approved in Japan in 2001, and ginkgo mihuan, approved in China in 2012, have been used in specific clinical settings, supporting the idea that compounds capable of scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) may help mitigate brain damage after ischemic stroke [51]. However, no neuroprotective therapy has yet achieved broad worldwide approval for the treatment of ischemic stroke.

In addition, the microglia activation and proliferation are early events in ischemic stroke. Microglial cells increase at 12 h after the ischemic insult, reaching a maximum level by 3 days [52] and remaining elevated until day 21. These immune cells could represent up to 80% of cells in the ischemic core at day 14 [53]. Moreover, peripheral immune cells are recruited to the affected brain region, followed by microglial proliferation. Macrophages begin to infiltrate the brain 24 h after the ischemic insult, and their levels peak after 3 days.

In contrast, neutrophils increase on day 3 and remain elevated until 7 days post-injury [52]. The damage caused by microglia and peripheral immune cells to the brain parenchyma results from their proliferation and the production and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which activate cell death pathways in nearby neurons [54]. Additionally, microglial cells could phagocytize damaged but viable cells, such as stressed neurons, increasing neuronal loss and brain damage [55].

Oxidative Stress and Cell Death in Ischemic Stroke

ATP depletion, excitotoxicity, cytoplasmic Ca2+ elevation, and oxidative stress lead to necrosis in the ischemic core. This type of cell death occurs a few minutes after the cerebral blood flow interruption [56, 57]. On the other hand, several death pathways are observed in the penumbra zone (e.g., apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, autophagy, parthanatos, PANoptosis) (Table 1). However, in this review, we discuss the crosstalk between oxidative stress and two cell death mechanisms, necroptosis and ferroptosis [58, 59] during ischemic stroke.

Necroptosis

Necroptosis is a form of regulated cell death characterized by membrane rupture and the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs, endogenous danger signals), cytokines, and chemokines that promote inflammation [69–71].

In ischemic stroke, necroptosis initiates with the binding of some molecules, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα), tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL), interferon (IFN), and Fas ligand (FasL) to their receptors; however, the better-studied signaling pathway involves TNFα/tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1). TNFα binds to TNFR1, which recruits adaptor proteins that form the membrane-bound Complex I, including TNFR1-associated death domain protein (TRADD), TNF receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2), receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIP1), cellular inhibitors of apoptosis proteins 1 and 2 (cIAP1/2), and the linear ubiquitin chain assembly complex (LUBAC). In this complex, cIAP and LUBAC mediate the ubiquitination of RIP1 at Lys63 (Fig. 3), providing docking platforms for downstream kinases such as transforming growth factor-β–activated kinase 1 (TAK1), TAK1-binding proteins 2 and 3 (TAB2/3), and the inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) kinase IKKα/IKKβ and NF-κB essential modulator (NEMO). This signaling cascade activates the NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways, ultimately promoting the transcription of genes involved in cell survival and inflammation [69–71].

The signaling shifts from pro-survival to pro-death, occurring when RIP1 ubiquitination is disrupted by enzymatic deubiquitination, the receptor is internalized, or cIAP is inhibited. Under these conditions, TRADD and RIP1 dissociate from the membrane, assembling in the cytosol with Fas-associated via death domain (FADD) and procaspase-8 to form Complex IIa or IIb (without TRADD), which induces apoptosis by activating caspase-8 (Casp-8). However, if Casp-8 is inhibited (either pharmacologically or genetically) or if RIP3 is overexpressed, this apoptotic complex becomes the necrosome, also known as Complex IIc (Fig. 3). The necrosome is characterized by a RIP homotypic interaction motif (RHIM)-dependent oligomerization of RIP1/RIP1, RIP1/RIP3, and RIP3/RIP3, leading to their auto- and cross-phosphorylation, and the formation of amyloid-like filaments that serve as a structural scaffold [69–72].

A key modification in RIP3 is autophosphorylation at Ser227, which facilitates recruitment of the mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL). Additionally, RIP3 undergoes Lys5 ubiquitination, which is essential for maintaining necrosome integrity. Upon recruitment, MLKL is phosphorylated by RIP3, inducing a conformational change that exposes its four-helix bundle (4HB) domain. This structural rearrangement enables MLKL oligomerization and subsequent translocation to the plasma membrane, where it forms pore-like clusters, disrupts tight junctions, or alters ion flux, leading to osmotic imbalance, cell swelling, and eventual membrane rupture (Fig. 3) [69–71].

In specific cellular contexts, necroptosis can occur via mechanisms that do not require complete necrosome assembly. For instance, the phosphorylation of a single RIP3 molecule within a dimer may be sufficient to trigger MLKL activation and execute necroptosis. Overall, necroptosis represents a highly orchestrated and regulated mode of cell death that serves both defensive and pathological roles, particularly in inflammatory diseases, neurodegeneration, and ischemic injury [69–71].

Necroptosis and Oxidative Stress

RIP3 activation leads to cell death via necroptosis. RIP3 knockout animals subjected to ischemia (24 h) by electrocauterization exhibit less motor deficit compared to wild-type animals undergoing the same surgery. Moreover, in RIP3-knockout animals, inhibition of necroptosis increases apoptosis and reduces histological damage, highlighting necroptosis as a potential therapeutic target in stroke [73].

Evidence below suggests that ROS production can induce necroptosis, although the link remains poorly understood (Fig. 4).

Arginase 2 (Arg2) is a mitochondrial enzyme expressed primarily in extrahepatic tissues; it has been linked to diseases such as hypertension and neurovascular degeneration. Shosha et al. reported that necroptosis (RIP3) and Arg2 levels increase in retinal neurons subjected to ischemia–reperfusion injury. Moreover, inhibiting this enzyme reduces protein nitration, gliosis, and necroptosis. The results suggest that ROS production induces Arg2 activation, which, in turn, triggers necroptosis [74].

Additionally, histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6), an enzyme primarily localized in the cytoplasm, deacetylates peroxiredoxin-1 and peroxiredoxin-2 (antioxidant enzymes), thereby increasing ROS levels and activating necroptosis, as evidenced by increased levels of RIP1, RIP3, and pMLKL in cortical neurons subjected to oxygen and glucose deprivation [75].

Isolated mitochondria from diabetic mice or U937 monocytes showed increased ROS production, which induced oxidative modifications and RIP1 oligomerization, together with elevated levels of RIP3 and MLKL, thereby increasing necroptosis. Concurrently, a reduction in the activation of Casp-3, Casp-6, and Casp-7 was reported, leading to the attenuation of apoptotic cell death [76].

The evidence presented above suggests that increased ROS production can activate necroptosis. In contrast, the following section demonstrates that activation of necroptosis further enhances ROS generation, establishing a pathogenic feed-forward loop (Fig. 4).

Mitochondrial serine/threonine-protein phosphatase member family 5 (PGAM5) acts as a critical cytoprotective factor against necroptosis by facilitating phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN)-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)-dependent mitophagy. In the absence of PGAM5, dysfunctional mitochondria accumulate and overproduce ROS, which in turn exacerbate the necroptotic pathway, increasing RIP1 and RIP3 levels [77].

RIP3 phosphorylates Ca2⁺/calmodulin-stimulated protein kinase II (CaMKII), a serine/threonine kinase involved in Ca2⁺-dependent signaling and homeostasis of intracellular Ca2⁺. pCaMKII subsequently phosphorylates proline-rich tyrosine kinase 2 (Pyk2), a Ca2⁺-dependent tyrosine kinase that is translocated to the mitochondrial matrix, where it promotes mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload and enhances ROS generation. This kinase cascade (RIP3/CaMKII/Pyk2) directly impairs mitochondrial function, leading to intracellular and mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload, marked increase in ROS production, and loss of mitochondrial membrane potential. Ultimately, activation of this pathway converges on the execution of necroptosis [73].

Necroptosis is linked to ROS production and to activation of the NLR family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome. If apoptosis is inhibited, necroptosis is activated, and necrosome formation promotes the release of DAMPs that increase mitochondrial ROS production and promote NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, culminating in the maturation and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18 [78].

NLRP3 inflammasome activation can be regulated by molecules such as nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), an endogenous negative regulator. In response to oxidative stress, Nrf2 translocates to the nucleus, where it induces the expression of antioxidant genes, thereby reducing ROS generated during necroptosis. Thus, ROS-dependent NLRP3 inflammasome activation is blocked, thereby preventing neuroinflammation and secondary tissue injury following ischemic insult [79].

In addition, molecules and pharmacological strategies proposed as potential therapeutic interventions targeting the interplay between necroptosis and oxidative stress will be discussed (Table 2).

Antioxidant molecules could directly affect proteins involved in necroptosis. Under hyperglycemic conditions, N-acetylcysteine (NAC) has been reported to inhibit necroptosis by preventing the oxidation and subsequent phosphorylation of RIP1, thereby blocking the formation of the necrosome (RIP1/RIP3/MLKL). Consistently, NAC treatment preserves the activity of executioner caspases (caspases-3, -6, and -7), counteracting their downregulation under severe oxidative stress and preventing the shift in cell death signaling from apoptosis to necroptosis. In addition, by markedly reducing intracellular ROS levels, NAC suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby decreasing the production and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β. NAC is also recognized as an effective cysteine precursor for glutathione (GSH) synthesis, contributing to the restoration of cellular redox homeostasis, and has been described as a promising neuroprotective agent in animal models of perinatal brain injury and cerebral ischemia [76].

Recombinant human thioredoxin-1 (rh-Trx), an antioxidant, inhibits the increase of RIP1, RIP3, and MLKL in microglia after ischemia–reperfusion, reducing necroptosis. Additionally, it reduces NLRP3 inflammasome activation, decreases IL-1β release, and promotes a M2 microglial phenotype. Finally, rhTrx-1 decreases the RIP1/RIP3 complex, thereby preventing the overexpression of pyruvate dehydrogenase, which promotes excessive ROS production [78].

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide hydrate (NAD+) and NADPH are essential for signaling pathways and enzymatic reactions, and their levels are depleted after ischemia–reperfusion injury. NAD+ (50 mg/kg), NADPH (7.5 mg/kg), or NAD + /NADPH treatment at reperfusion onset in mice subjected to ischemia (2 h) and reperfusion (16 h) resulted in a diminished RIP1 level. In contrast, NADPH and NAD+/NADPH attenuate RIP1 and RIP3 levels. At the same time, the administration of NAD+/NADPH maintained higher levels of reduced glutathione (GSH), and decreased ROS levels and oxidative damage in lipid (4-HNE levels) and protein (nitrotyrosine levels) [80].

Progranulin is a secreted protein expressed in tissues, including microglial cells and neurons. This protein exerts neurotrophic effects by promoting cell proliferation, microvascular development, and wound healing, while also regulating the inflammatory response. Progranulin-knockdown animals subjected to ischemia (1 h) and reperfusion (72 h) are more susceptible to ischemia-induced damage. They exhibit greater blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption and higher levels of oxidative stress markers (MDA and NOX subunits such as p47phox, and gp91phox) and lower levels of antioxidant enzymes (superoxide dismutase and hemeoxygenase-1) compared to wild-type animals. In addition, progranulin overexpression or necrostatin-1 (a RIP1 inhibitor) administration counteract the effects of ischemia, indicating that progranulin mitigates ischemia-induced oxidative stress and inhibits necroptosis by regulating RIP1 [81].

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is a recently described form of cell death characterized by its intracellular iron dependence and morphological distinctiveness from necrosis and apoptosis [82]. This cell death results from increased lipoperoxidation, which depends on elevated levels of labile Fe2+ and lipoxygenase (LOX) activity [83] (Fig. 5).

Ferroptosis and Oxidative Stress

Ferroptosis can be activated by two mechanisms: one involving distinct transporters (extrinsic) and the other involving enzyme inhibition (intrinsic). In particular, the extrinsic pathway is involved in GSH and iron metabolism, inhibiting GSH transporters and activating ferroportin, transferrin, and lactotransferrin. In contrast, the intrinsic pathway is associated with inhibition of antioxidant enzymes, such as glutathione peroxidase-4 (GPX4) [84].

One feature of ferroptosis is iron-dependent lipoperoxidation, which preferentially affects polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) [84]. Iron is a cofactor indispensable for oxidative metabolism, myelination, and neurotransmitter synthesis, but its metabolic dysregulation triggers ferroptosis due to its participation in hydroxyl radical (HO•) production by the Fenton reaction. In this reaction, H2O2 is converted to a more oxidizing species, the HO•, in a Fe2+-dependent reaction. HO• can oxidize PUFAs in the plasma membrane during lipoperoxidation, generating degradation products such as MDA and 4-HNE [85].

Iron cell content depends on its import and export by different mechanisms. Transferrin (Tf) binds Fe3+, then interacts with the Tf receptor (TfR) on endothelial cells of the BBB and neurons, promoting its endocytosis [86]. In the endosome, the metalloreductase six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of prostate 3 (STEAP3) reduces Fe3+ to Fe2+, which is then transported from the endosome into the cytosol by divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1) [87]. Cytosolic Fe2+ binds to ferritin heavy chain (FTH) and ferritin light chain (FTL), or is extracellularly exported by ferroportin (FPN), preventing Fe2+-induced oxidative stress [88, 89].

Additionally, PUFAs are substrates for LOX, generating unstable hydroperoxides that can react with proteins via Michael reactions [90] and may be the unstable compounds that initiate ferroptosis [91]. Arachidonic acid (AA) and adrenic acid esterified to phosphatidylethanolamine are the main PUFAs that are doubly- and triply-oxygenated by LOX, leading to ferroptosis. Importantly, AA esterified to phosphatidylethanolamine rather than free AA or other PUFAs promotes ferroptosis [92].

Two enzymes are involved in the insertion of AA into cell membranes: the Acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4, a biomarker of ferroptosis) and lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3) [93]. ACSL4 is an essential regulator of PUFAs metabolism, mainly AA, converting them into acyl-CoA, followed by its insertion in the plasma membrane by LPCAT3 [94]. ACSL4 is involved in ferroptosis progression; its downregulation reduces the end products of lipoperoxidation, such as MDA, but does not affect intracellular iron content [94]. Moreover, this enzyme is phosphorylated and activated by protein kinase C beta II (PKCβII), thereby increasing lipoperoxidation and ultimately leading to ferroptosis [95]. In contrast, knockdown of LPCAT3 decreases ferroptosis [92].

GPXs are a family of enzymes involved in the reduction of H2O2 and hydroperoxides (-OOH) to H2O or reduced hydroperoxides (-OH), respectively. In particular, GPX4 contains a selenocysteine in its catalytic center. This enzyme reduces H2O2 and hydroperoxides present in phospholipids and cholesterol inserted into cell membranes, using GSH [96]. Due to its role in hydroperoxide removal, inactivation or decreased activity of this enzyme leads to ferroptosis [97]. During ischemic stroke, GPX4 levels and activity are reduced, thereby promoting lipoperoxidation and increasing MDA levels [98–101]. GPX4 requires GSH as a reductant agent, and its activity is related to the availability of GSH; thus, the depletion of GSH promotes ferroptosis (Fig. 5) [102].

GSH is composed of glycine, cysteine, and glutamate; it is synthesized by ɣ-glutamyl cysteine synthetase and GSH synthetase [103], and cysteine is the limiting amino acid for its synthesis [104]. Cysteine levels are, in part, regulated by the cystine cell transport system, known as system Xc- [103]. Specifically, the solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), a protein that forms part of the system Xc-, is crucial for cell redox homeostasis because it imports one molecule of cystine into the cytoplasm and exports one molecule of glutamate into the extracellular space. Cystine is essential for redox homeostasis because its reduction generates cysteine, which subsequently is used in the synthesis of GSH [105]. Hence, SLC7A11 regulation is involved directly in ferroptosis. Its p53-dependent downregulation is associated with increased lipoperoxidation, leading to ferroptosis. p53 represses SLC7A11 expression at the transcriptional level by binding to its promoter region, and the downregulation of p53 induces cell resistance to ferroptosis [106].

Ferroptosis is a cell death mechanism observed in different neurological disorders such as ischemic stroke, Alzheimer’s disease, and Parkinson’s disease [88, 107]. In ischemic stroke, iron and chromium levels increase in the affected tissue [63, 108, 109], possibly due to disruption of the BBB [110]. Particularly, stroke increases labile Fe2+ levels through the increase in Tf and TfR [102], as well as a decrease in ferritin levels, by a selective autophagy called ferritinophagy [102, 111, 112]. Ferritin degradation increases HO• production via the Fenton reaction, leading to lipoperoxidation and ferroptosis [111, 112]. Moreover, ferritin degradation is mediated by the autophagy receptor nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4), whose levels increase during ischemic stroke through deubiquitination by the deubiquitinating enzyme ubiquitin-specific peptidase 14 (USP14) [111].

In addition to iron dysregulation, ischemic stroke reduces GPX4 activity and GSH levels, thereby increasing ROS production and lipoperoxidation, ultimately leading to ferroptosis [102, 109, 111]. This diminution in GPX4 activity may be associated with decreased SLC7A11 expression, resulting from GSH depletion [113–115]. Additionally, selenium brain content decreases in ischemic stroke [108], which may also be associated with reduced GPX4. However, selenium administration protects brain tissue after ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke by increasing GPX transcription, which is regulated by the Sp1 transcription factor, thereby reducing ferroptosis [64].

Other proteins are also affected by ischemic stroke. ACSL4 and 15-LOX2 increase their levels, leading to lipoperoxidation and elevated MDA and 4-HNE levels [108, 109, 111]. The overexpression of ACSL4 in ischemic stroke increases lipoperoxidation and ferroptosis, resulting in more brain damage and inflammation [59]; however, the treatment with rosiglitazone, an inhibitor of ACSL4, decreases the infarct volume, prevents the decrease in GSH and GPX levels, and reduces ROS, lipoperoxidation, MDA levels, and ferroptosis [109].

Finally, other compounds, such as liproxstatin-1 and ferrostatin-1 [63], two specific inhibitors of ferroptosis, as well as cottonseed oil [98], have been shown to decrease the damage and neurological impairment induced by ischemic stroke, suggesting the importance of ferroptosis in cell death during ischemic stroke (Table 3).

Crosstalk Between Necroptosis and Ferroptosis

Du et al. reported a bidirectional inhibition between these two cell death pathways. Liproxstatin-1 (a classic ferroptosis inhibitor) and necrostatin-1 (a classic necroptosis inhibitor) prevent disruption of redox balance and inhibit both cell death pathways in a middle cerebral artery occlusion model. Additionally, a similar effect was observed using deferoxamine, an iron chelator [117]. Furthermore, a compensatory response has been observed in acute kidney ischemia. ACSL4 (protein-involved ferroptosis) deletion activates the necroptosis pathway, whereas MLKL (protein-involved necroptosis) deletion activates the ferroptosis pathway [118]. Collectively, these observations suggest that selective blockade of a single cell death pathway may be insufficient and, in certain contexts, could shift vulnerability toward alternative forms of regulated cell death. Thus, therapeutic strategies targeting shared upstream mechanisms, such as redox balance, could reduce activation of both pathways in the penumbra zone and improve translational relevance.

Therapeutic Opportunities and Translational Challenges

Current evidence suggests that reperfusion therapies must include cell death pathways as therapeutic targets. Although rt-PA remains an essential treatment for ischemic stroke, evidence indicates that some of its adverse effects during reperfusion may be associated with ferroptosis [119]. This correlation is supported by Geng et al. (2024), who showed that liproxstatin-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor, attenuates rt-PA-associated brain damage. Likewise, antioxidant and neuroprotective approaches may partially overlap with ferroptosis modulation; edaravone dexborneol has been reported to protect the blood–brain barrier after cerebral ischemia/reperfusion by inhibiting ferroptosis through activation of the Nrf2/HO-1/GPX4 axis [120]. In addition, emerging clinical evidence indicates that ferroptosis-related biomarkers (HSP70, ferritin, and ACSL4) are associated with severity and functional outcomes in patients undergoing endovascular thrombectomy [121]. In contrast, the relationship between current stroke therapies and necroptosis has not been studied. Together, these findings suggest that the efficacy and complications of reperfusion therapies may be influenced, at least in part, by ferroptosis-associated mechanisms, and that the co-administration of anti-ferroptosis agents with rt-PA warrants further investigation.

Oxidative stress is a central mechanism in the activation of necroptosis and ferroptosis in the penumbra after ischemic stroke. Although numerous antioxidant approaches and pathway-specific modulators have shown neuroprotective effects in preclinical models, their clinical translation has been limited. Currently, only two clinical trials are using NAC; however, the primary aim is not to quantify specific markers of cell death pathways.

Additionally, most preclinical models used young and healthy animals, whereas ischemic stroke predominantly affects aged individuals with comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease. These conditions profoundly alter redox balance, inflammatory signaling, and vulnerability to specific cell death mechanisms, potentially modifying therapeutic responses. Furthermore, challenges include dose translation, safety, and the lack of reliable clinical biomarkers to monitor pathway activation and target engagement. Due to this, studies must be performed using older animals with these comorbidities and provide an integrated neurovascular unit perspective, including neurons, astrocytes, microglia, endothelial cells, pericytes, and infiltrating immune cells.

Conclusions

Necroptosis and ferroptosis are two regulated cell death pathways implicated in neuronal damage after ischemic stroke, and growing experimental evidence supports their relevance as potential therapeutic targets.

Both cell death pathways are activated by oxidative stress. Also, they can increase ROS production, thereby forming a vicious cycle that exacerbates cellular damage and death, suggesting that antioxidant molecules could be beneficial tools for reducing the resulting damage. Therefore, exploring their therapeutic potential might provide a promising approach for decreasing disability and mortality associated with ischemic stroke.

The available evidence suggests that effective stroke treatment may require multi-targeted therapeutic strategies that combine reperfusion with interventions to limit oxidative stress, neurovascular injury, inflammation, and regulated cell death pathways, such as ferroptosis and necroptosis.
