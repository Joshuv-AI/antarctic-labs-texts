Role of bacterial dysbiosis in infertility: the BIOME study protocol for a Danish multicentre prospective observational cohort study
Authors: Charlotte Storck-Thy, Line Buur Dessing, Betina Hebbelstrup Jensen, Birgitte Oxlund-Mariegaard, Karen Angeliki Krogfelt, Rie Jønsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMicrobiome composition across multiple body sites, including the vagina, gut, urinary tract and semen, has been implicated in reproductive health and infertility. Vaginal dysbiosis and reduced Lactobacillus abundance have previously been associated with poorer outcomes in assisted reproductive technologies, yet few studies have simultaneously characterised microbiomes of both partners or compared infertile couples with healthy population controls. The BIOME study, a prospective cohort study, aims to characterise multisite microbiomes in infertile and healthy Danish couples and examine their association with long-term fertility outcomes.Methods and analysisThe BIOME study is a multicentre, prospective observational cohort study including 100 couples undergoing fertility treatment following ≥12 months of unsuccessful pregnancy attempts and 100 healthy control couples. Participants provide biological samples from multiple body sites (vaginal swab or semen, faeces, urine and blood) prior to initiation of fertility treatment. Samples will undergo microbial sequencing, quantitative PCR, culture-based analyses, proteomics, metabolomics and microRNA profiling. Questionnaire data, clinical information and electronic medical record data will be collected. Participants will be followed for 5 years after sample collection to assess fertility outcomes. No intervention, randomisation or blinding is performed. Recruitment is planned over 24 months.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the Danish Ethics Committee (#SJ-1033), and all participants provide written informed consent. Data are processed according to the General Data Protection Regulation and the Danish Data Protection Act, with pseudonymisation and restricted-access procedures. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and scientific conferences. Sequencing data will be deposited in a public repository (eg, European Nucleotide Archive) with personal identifiers removed, and curated metadata will be available on reasonable request.

Introduction

Infertility affects approximately 17% of couples worldwide and represents a growing public health concern.1 The use of assisted reproductive technologies has increased substantially in recent decades, reflecting both delayed childbearing and persistent challenges in achieving pregnancy. Despite advances in fertility treatment, a large proportion of infertility cases remain unexplained, placing a considerable mental and financial burden on affected individuals and couples.2

Reproductive health has historically focused primarily on female factors; however, infertility is increasingly recognised as a condition involving both partners.3 In parallel, women’s health has gained more broad attention as an underexplored area in biomedical research, particularly with respect to biological mechanisms influencing reproductive outcomes.4 Emerging evidence suggests that microbial communities may play an important role in reproductive health, with associations reported between dysbiosis and adverse fertility outcomes.5

Most microbiome research in infertility has focused on the female reproductive tract, while the potential contribution of the male microbiome, including the semen microbiota, remains poorly understood.6 Given that successful reproduction depends on the interaction between both partners, there is a critical need for well-characterised cohorts that allow integrated investigation of microbial factors in both women and men. This cohort was therefore established to explore the role of microbial communities as potential contributors to infertility, with the aim of advancing biological understanding and identifying factors relevant to reproductive health and fertility treatment outcomes.

Methods and analysis

Study design

The BIOME study is a Danish multicentre prospective observational cohort study of couples undergoing fertility treatment as well as healthy controls (figure 1). The participants from the infertile group are recruited from the public Fertility Clinic at Zealand University Hospital, Denmark. The clinic provides infertility evaluation and treatment to couples and women from Region Zealand. The fertility clinic performs a substantial number of fertility treatments annually, including intrauterine insemination (IUI) with partner or donor sperm, fresh in vitro fertilisation/intracytoplasmic sperm injection (IVF/ICSI) cycles and frozen embryo transfer (FET). In 2025, the clinic performed fertility treatments for a total of 376 insemination (homologous and heterologous), 664 IVF/ICSI treatments and 748 FET. The women were aged ≤39 years.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study. Patients are recruited as part of the routine clinical practice on inclusion at the fertility clinic at Zealand University Hospital, Køge. Both patients and participants from the public receive written and oral information about the study, including details on sample collection, data use and storage of biological material, in accordance with the approvals granted by the Danish Ethics Committee. All participants are informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequences for their clinical care.

Study population and recruitment

The patient population from the fertility clinic reflects a broad socioeconomic distribution comparable to that of the general population. The patients undergoing treatment represent a wide range of infertility aetiologies and clinical characteristics, including both primary and secondary infertility.

As the study is taking place in the routine clinical practice, participants are recruited in connection with standard infertility investigation or fertility treatment, which sets the frame for the study.

The healthy control population is recruited through the Department of Public Health, Section of General Practice, University of Copenhagen. Controls are drawn from the general population and recruited independently of fertility clinics or hospital-based reproductive care. The healthy controls do not have a known history of infertility, reproductive disorders or ongoing fertility treatment at the time of inclusion. The control group is screened for basic reproductive health characteristics such as regular menstrual cycles and absence of known gynaecological conditions. Controls are selected to broadly match the study population in age and sex distribution. Information on socio-demographic and lifestyle factors, including age, employment, body mass index (BMI) and smoking status, will be considered to ensure comparability.

For patients, clinical data are obtained as part of the initial assessment before starting fertility treatment. For healthy controls, corresponding information on reproductive history, previous infections (including sexually transmitted infections) and gynaecological conditions are collected through structured questionnaires. This approach enables comparable characterisation of key reproductive and health-related variables across study groups.

All controls are recruited within the Danish public healthcare system, as a result, the control population reflects a broad socioeconomic distribution comparable to that of the general Danish population. The inclusion of population-based healthy controls enables comparisons between individuals undergoing fertility treatment and individuals from the general population, facilitating identification of microbiological and biological factors associated with infertility.

Eligibility criteria

Both groups are eligible for inclusion if they are adult men or women of reproductive age (18–40) and belong to one of the following groups: (1) patients undergoing fertility investigation or fertility treatment at a public fertility clinic or (2) healthy men or women from the general population serving as controls. Healthy controls have no known history of infertility at the time of inclusion.

Participants are excluded if they are receiving ongoing antibiotic treatment or have been diagnosed with a sexually transmitted infection within the 3 months prior to inclusion.

Study questionnaires

All participants must complete a set of questionnaires giving information on demographic, medical, reproductive and lifestyle factors, including one of the questionnaires that has been developed for this study. For the patients undergoing fertility treatment, women and their partners complete a questionnaire once referred to the clinic. Healthy control participants complete the same questionnaires.

The questionnaires cover information about the participants’ reproductive history, current and past medical conditions, use of medications and supplements (eg, antibiotics and probiotics), allergies, family history of hereditary diseases, previous surgeries, lifestyle factors such as smoking, exercise and alcohol and drug use. The questionnaires also cover information about their menstrual cycle and their fertility-related history, such as past infections in the reproductive organs.

The questionnaire is based on standard clinical and epidemiological questions routinely used in the fertility assessment, ensuring consistent and comparable data collection across the two groups.

Specimen collection and laboratory analyses

For both groups, we are creating a biobank, and all samples are collected before fertility treatment to avoid any influence of hormonal stimulation. Each participant provides a blood sample, urine, a faecal sample and either a vaginal swab or a semen sample. For female participants, vaginal swabs are collected at a single time point. Information on menstrual cycle phase at the time of sampling will be recorded and included in the analyses to account for known temporal variation in the vaginal microbiome.7

Semen samples represent a low microbial biomass sample type, and therefore particular attention will be paid to contamination control. Negative controls will be included throughout DNA extraction and sequencing workflows. Positive controls, such as mock communities or samples spiked with known bacterial strains, will be included to assess DNA extraction efficiency and sequencing performance. Although abstinence period is not specifically controlled for the samples for microbiome analyses, semen samples are collected as part of routine clinical procedures following standardised instructions prior to collection at the clinic.

All samples are stored in aliquots at –80°C at Roskilde University until analysis.

The biological material will undergo several laboratory analyses. DNA will be extracted for microbial sequencing, using either amplicon-based or shotgun approaches, depending on sample yield and bacterial abundance will be quantified by quantitative PCR. Viable bacteria will be characterised through culture-based methods, and selected isolates will undergo whole-genome sequencing to achieve strain-level resolution and to assess whether partners within a couple share identical or closely-related bacterial strains. In addition, proteomic and metabolomic analyses will be performed to identify biomarkers related to inflammation and host-microbe interactions. MicroRNAs will be isolated for downstream molecular assays, and inflammatory responses will be evaluated using ELISA-based quantification of selected biomarkers identified through the proteomic analyses.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study is the difference in the prevalence of a Lactobacillus-dominated vaginal microbiome between infertile women and healthy control women at baseline. This outcome aligns with established evidence indicating that the relative abundance of Lactobacillus species is a key marker of vaginal health and of microbiome stability in fertility-related contexts. Secondary outcomes include the characterisation of microbial diversity and taxonomic profiles across all sampled body sites. Vagina or semen, faeces and urine as the identification of cross-site microbial associations, such as potential gut-vagina, vagina-bladder and vagina-semen axes. Strain-level relatedness between partners will be explored using whole-genome sequencing of cultured isolates to determine whether couples share identical or closely-related bacterial strains. Additional secondary outcomes include biomarker profiling based on proteomic, metabolomic, microRNA and ELISA-based measures to evaluate host inflammatory and antimicrobial responses. Long-term reproductive outcomes, including time to pregnancy, clinical pregnancy and live birth, will be assessed through electronic medical records and registry linkage up to 5 years after inclusion. Exploratory multi-omics analyses will integrate microbial, proteomic, metabolomic and molecular data to generate hypotheses regarding mechanistic pathways linking microbiome configurations to fertility outcomes.

Sample size

The planned enrolment period spans 24 months, during which couples are seen once at baseline for sample collection. Based on current experience, 101 patients have been invited, with an acceptance rate of 22%, underscoring the sensitivity of the population being recruited.

Based on large population-based studies, approximately 70–80% of reproductive-age women in the general population have a Lactobacillus-dominated vaginal microbiome.8 9 In contrast, a limited number of studies have found that infertile and subfertile women are more likely to present with a disrupted vaginal microbiome, characterised by reduced Lactobacillus abundance and a higher prevalence of Community State Type (CST) IV.10 11

However, these studies vary considerably in population characteristics, infertility diagnosis and methodological approaches, and the exact prevalence of Lactobacillus dominance in infertile populations therefore remains uncertain. Accordingly, for sample size estimation, we conservatively assume a difference of approximately 40 percentage points (70% vs 30%), representing a clinically meaningful and plausible effect size rather than a fixed population estimate.

Detecting this anticipated difference of approximately 40 percentage points between infertile women and healthy controls, with a two-sided α of 0.05 and a power of 80%, requires approximately 40–50 participants per group.

To ensure adequate power in the presence of potential drop-out, non-viable samples or insufficient sequencing depth and to enable secondary and exploratory analyses, we aim to recruit approximately 100 women in each group. This expanded sample size provides robust power (>80%) to detect smaller differences than initially assumed (down to approximately 20–25 percentage points) and enables well-adjusted multivariable analyses.

Infertility diagnosis is obtained from routine clinical assessment, and exploratory analyses will evaluate whether microbiome profiles differ between diagnostic groups (eg, stratified analysis according to infertility diagnosis).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will be conducted using GraphPad Prism and R. Group differences between infertile couples and healthy controls will be analysed using appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests, depending on the data distribution. Microbiome sequencing data will be processed using standard bioinformatic pipelines, with evaluation of diversity measures and community-level differences. Differences in microbial composition will be assessed using established methods for compositional microbiome data.

Associations between microbial features and infertility will be examined using multivariable regression models adjusting for key confounders, including age, BMI, smoking, menstrual cycle phase at sampling and recent antibiotic use. For male participants, the characteristics of the semen microbiome will be analysed in relation to conventional semen quality parameters.

Long-term reproductive outcomes will be evaluated using time-to-event and logistic regression models, depending on the endpoint. Missing data will be handled using appropriate imputation methods. Whole-genome sequencing data from bacterial isolates will be analysed to determine strain-level relatedness within and between couples. As both partners within couples are included, statistical analyses will account for within-couple correlation using appropriate methods, such as mixed-effects models or clustered SEs.

Ethics and dissemination

The study has been approved by the Danish Research Ethics Committee (#SJ-1033). All participants will be provided with a written informed consent form prior to inclusion.

Participants will be provided with both written and oral information about the study objectives, procedures, potential risks and their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. In the event of withdrawal, all associated data and biological samples will be deleted in accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

Participant confidentiality is ensured through pseudonymisation using coded identifiers, and access to identifiable data are restricted to authorised personnel. Biological samples are collected using minimally invasive procedures, and potential risks are minimised through standardised protocols and trained personnel.

Special consideration is given to participants undergoing fertility treatment, with recruitment and communication conducted in a respectful and sensitive manner to minimise participant burden.

All personal data will be processed in accordance with the GDPR and the Danish Data Protection Act. The biobank has been registered with the Danish Data Protection Agency, and a data processing agreement has been signed by all collaborators.

Results

Results from this study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and presentations at national and international conferences. Sequencing data will be deposited in a public repository, such as the European Nucleotide Archive, with all personal identifiers removed in accordance with GDPR. Curated metadata and processed, non-identifiable datasets will be stored in compliance with FAIR principles and will be made available to other researchers on reasonable request to ensure transparency and reproducibility.
