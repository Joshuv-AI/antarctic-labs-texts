Perception and Attitude of undergraduate medical students toward mentor–mentee program
Authors: Anita Kumari, Sudha Rani, Vinita Kumari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Mentor–mentee programs in medical colleges offer vital support for undergraduate students’ academic, professional, and personal growth. In the demanding world of medical education, they provide structured guidance to navigate complex curricula, clinical training, and career planning.Method:This study, conducted at Sheikh Bhikhari Medical College (SBMC) in Hazaribag, Jharkhand, from February to July 2025, evaluates the perceptions and attitudes of undergraduate medical students toward the mentor–mentee program. The study involved 248 MBBS students from Phases I, II, and III, selected via simple random sampling.Result:A significant majority (76.9%) agreed or strongly agreed that the program supports personal development, while 74.6% believed it enhances academic performance. Additionally, 79.1% valued mentors’ guidance in navigating medical college challenges, and 71.7% recognized the program’s role in aiding career decisions. However, attitudes toward the program were moderately positive, with only 55.2% feeling comfortable approaching mentors and 48.6% actively seeking engagement, indicating room for improvement in fostering proactive participation. Challenges included time constraints (34.3%), unclear guidelines (32.1%), and mentor unavailability (19.4%). Students suggested regular meetings, better mentor access, and matching by shared interests.Conclusion:Mentor–mentee program is useful for personal, academic, and career growth, but limited interaction, time constraints, and unclear guidelines hinder effectiveness, requiring structured meetings, better accessibility, improved matching, and further research to enhance impact.

Introduction

Numerous definitions of mentoring can be found in the literature, but the most frequently referenced one describes it as a process where a knowledgeable, respected, and compassionate individual (the mentor) supports a typically younger person (the mentee) in the process of development and the re-examination of their ideas, learning, and personal or professional development.[1] Mentoring started in medieval times in Greece,[2] but formal programs were developed in America for medical students in the late 1990.[3] Mentoring programs for medical students aim at one side to accompany mentees successfully through their studies, thereby providing them with a greater degree of satisfaction with their academic experience; on the other side, they also help students pursue their specific goals, for example, a successful clinician, medical teacher or researcher.[4] Medical students value mentorship to such an extent as indicated in surveys, where the majority of them have rated mentoring as very important and shown great interest in developing a mentor–mentee relationship.[5]

The mentor–mentee program in medical education has gained significant traction globally, particularly among undergraduate medical students, due to its potential to foster academic success, personal growth, and career development. The perception and attitude of undergraduate medical students toward these programs are critical, as their engagement and receptiveness determine the program’s efficacy. Mentoring provides a structured framework where students can receive guidance on navigating the complexities of medical education, including academic challenges, clinical exposure, and career planning. It also fosters a supportive environment that enhances students’ confidence, resilience, and professional identity formation. The mentor, often a faculty member or senior clinician, serves as a role model, offering insights into clinical practice, research opportunities, and ethical decision-making, which are essential for shaping competent and compassionate future physicians.[6]

Students’ perceptions of mentoring programs are generally positive, with many appreciating the personalized support that helps them address individual academic and professional needs. Studies have shown that medical students view mentorship as a vital component of their education, particularly in managing stress, improving academic performance, and clarifying career goals.[7] However, the success of these programs depends on factors such as the mentor’s availability, compatibility with the mentee, and the program’s structure. Some students report challenges, including mismatched expectations or limited mentor engagement, which can negatively influence their attitudes.[8] Understanding these perceptions is crucial for designing effective mentoring programs that align with students’ needs and expectations.

Mentor–mentee programs in medical education benefit not only undergraduate students but also practitioners and patients. For practitioners, these programs produce graduates who are academically stronger, more confident, and better prepared for clinical responsibilities, thereby easing the transition into professional practice and reducing the burden on senior doctors. For patients, the indirect benefits are significant: mentored students tend to become empathetic, resilient, and patient-centered physicians, which enhances communication, ensures safer clinical practice, and improves overall quality of care. Effective mentorship also guides students in making thoughtful career choices, ultimately strengthening healthcare delivery in underserved areas.

Research questions: What are the expectations and different challenges of undergraduate students that may hinder the effectiveness of mentor–mentee program?

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting: This was an observational questionnaire-based study conducted at Sheikh Bhikhari Medical College (SBMC), Hazaribag. This is a government medical college established in 2019 and situated in the northern part of Jharkhand.

Study duration: February 2025 to July 2025

Study population and sample size: MBBS Students of Phase I, II and Phase III

Inclusion criteria: students willing to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria: Students not willing to participate in the study.

Sampling procedure and data collection: We included 100 students each from Phases I, II, and III by a simple random sampling method. A predesigned and pretested questionnaire was given to them to record their perception regarding the mentor–mentee program. The questionnaire was designed based on general guidelines from a literature review. Three independent researchers created it and resolved any differences through discussion. Professors and associate professors from the Department of Community Medicine and Physiology of Sheikh Bhikhari Medical College, Hazaribag, then revised the survey questionnaire. A pilot study with 20 medical students was conducted to test its functionality and usefulness. The questionnaire was divided into five parts. The first part consists of demographic details of students, and the second part consists of their perception of the mentor–mentee program. The third part shows their attitude regarding the program. The fourth part consists of their view on the hurdles they faced during the mentor–mentee program (the questions of the second to third parts are based on the Likert scale), and the fifth part shows qualitative insight through open-ended questions. The data collected were analyzed using Microsoft Excel and the software R. The data were represented as mean and SD. The median score for individual items of the feedback questions was obtained.

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee, letter no.IEC/SBMC-H/05/2025; dated May 20, 2025.

Observation and Results

Out of 300 students, 248 responded to the Google questionnaire. The study population was 27.01% aged 18–20 years, 43.1% aged 21–22 years, 27.8% aged 23–25 years, and only 2.01% over 25 years. Gender distribution was nearly balanced, with 52.5% female and 47.5% male students. Students were evenly distributed across MBBS phases: 33.1% in Phase I, 34.6% in Phase II, and 32.3% in Phase III [Figure 1]. A significant majority (91.5%) knew who their mentor was, while 8.5% did not. However, engagement with mentors was limited, as 45.9% of students never met their mentor, 20.9% met once, 9.6% met twice, 6.04% met three times, and 17.3% met several times. Regarding initiative, 29.8% of students used a combination of self-initiative and waiting for their mentor to reach out, while 15.8% solely waited for their mentor, and 8.4% took self-initiative. One hundred fourteen (45.9%) who never met with a mentor were excluded from the study [Table 1].

Perception of the mentor–mentee program

Students generally viewed the mentor–mentee program positively. A combined 76.9% either agreed (39.6%) or strongly agreed (37.3%) that the program is important for personal development. Similarly, 74.6% agreed (45.5%) or strongly agreed (29.1%) that it could improve academic performance. A strong majority (79.1%) believed mentors provide valuable guidance in navigating medical college challenges, with 42.5% agreeing and 36.6% strongly agreeing. The program was also seen as enhancing confidence and motivation, with 72.4% agreeing (38.8%) or strongly agreeing (33.6%). Additionally, 71.7% felt the program aids in making better career decisions, such as speciality choice, with 36.6% agreeing and 35.1% strongly agreeing. However, 38.1% disagreed (26.9%) or strongly disagreed (11.9%) with the notion that the program is only useful for academically struggling students, indicating a broader perceived utility [Table 2, Figure 2].

Attitude toward the mentor–mentee program

Attitudes were moderately positive but showed possibility off improvement. A combined 55.2% felt comfortable approaching their mentor for advice or support (32.1% agreed, 23.1% strongly agreed), though 35.8% were neutral. Only 48.6% actively sought opportunities to engage with their mentor (29.9% agreed, 18.7% strongly agreed), with 32.1% neutral and 19.4% disagreeing or strongly disagreeing. A majority (61.9%) believed mentors genuinely care about their development (40.3% agreed, 21.6% strongly agreed), but 30.6% remained neutral. The program received strong endorsement, with 78.3% recommending it to other students (45.5% agreed, 32.8% strongly agreed). Opinions on whether mentors should be faculty members rather than senior students were mixed, with 57.4% agreeing (26.1%) or strongly agreeing (31.3%), but 29.1% neutral and 13.4% disagreeing or strongly disagreeing [Table 3, Figure 3].

Challenges in the mentor–mentee program

Several challenges were identified. Time constraints were notable, with 34.3% agreeing (26.1%) or strongly agreeing (8.2%) that finding time to interact with mentors was difficult, though 37.3% disagreed or strongly disagreed. A lack of clear guidelines was reported by 32.1% (23.9% agreed, 8.2% strongly agreed), with 35.8% neutral. Mentor unavailability was a concern for 19.4% (14.9% agreed, 4.5% strongly agreed), but 41.8% disagreed or strongly disagreed. Mismatch in personality or interests was less prevalent, with 50.8% disagreeing (25.4%) or strongly disagreeing (25.4%), though 35.8% were neutral [Table 4, Figure 4].

Qualitative insights

Most valuable aspect of the mentor–mentee program

The most frequently cited value was the guidance provided by mentors, which encompassed academic support, career advice, and personal development. Many appreciated the opportunity to build relationships and improve communication through interactions with their mentors. The program was also noted for boosting confidence and enhancing personality development. Other benefits included improved study strategies, better planning, and mental health support. However, some respondents expressed dissatisfaction, stating that they found “nothing” valuable in the program, often due to limited engagement or lack of meaningful interaction.

Specific challenges faced in the mentor–mentee program

Participants reported several challenges. Time-related issues were prominent, including scheduling conflicts, busy schedules, and a general lack of time to engage with mentors. A significant barrier was the lack of interaction or communication with mentors, with some respondents noting they had never met their mentor or were unaware of who their mentor was. Hesitation in sharing personal or academic problems with mentors was another challenge. Additional issues included mentor unavailability, irregular meetings, and a lack of clear guidance. Some respondents, however, reported experiencing “no challenges,” indicating varied experiences within the program.

Suggestions for improving the mentor–mentee program

To enhance the program, respondents suggested implementing regular and scheduled meetings, such as monthly or weekly sessions, to ensure consistent interaction. Increased communication and accessibility of mentors were also emphasized, along with the need for fixed time slots to address scheduling conflicts. A more structured program with clear expectations, better mentor–mentee matching, and ongoing support was recommended. Additional ideas included allowing students to choose their mentors, providing training for mentors to improve their effectiveness, and encouraging active participation from both parties to strengthen the program.

Positive experiences with mentors

Respondents who had positive experiences highlighted the value of academic and career guidance, particularly in developing effective study strategies and making informed career decisions. Mentors were praised for building confidence, supporting personality development, and acting as approachable role models who inspired and motivated students. Specific examples included mentors helping resolve issues such as hostel problems or academic challenges. However, many respondents reported no positive experiences, often because they had never met their mentor or had limited interaction, underscoring gaps in program engagement [Table 5].

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate a generally positive perception among undergraduate medical students toward the mentor–mentee program, with 76.9% agreeing or strongly agreeing that it supports personal development and 74.6% acknowledging its role in enhancing academic performance. These results are consistent with previous research, such as the work of Frei et al.,[1] which demonstrated that mentoring programs foster academic success, build confidence, and assist in career planning among medical students.

Similarly, Karuna Sree et al. (2018)[9] observed that structured mentorship improved confidence, study strategies, and career clarity among MBBS students in Telangana.

Our study also aligns with international evidence. Pölczman et al. (2024)[10] demonstrated that near-peer mentoring programs in Hungary contributed positively to medical students’ mental health and resilience. Wu and Olagunju (2024)[11] emphasized that both official and unofficial mentorship programs play a transformative role in shaping competent physicians.

Subsequent studies reinforced these observations. Udhayakumar et al. (2023)[12] found that mentorship promoted both personal and professional growth among undergraduate medical students, though they noted challenges related to emotional support and mentor availability. Jiwan and Oberoi (2025) evaluated mentorship programs in Indian medical colleges qualitatively, highlighting the role of the National Medical Commission in mandating such initiatives and pointing out gaps in implementation.[13]

However, this study differs from previous research by identifying structural barriers that limit program effectiveness. Unlike earlier studies that focused primarily on mentor–mentee compatibility or emotional support, our findings highlight limited mentor interaction (45.9% never met their mentor), time constraints (34.3%), and unclear guidelines (32.1%) as major challenges. Jiwan and Oberoi (2025) noted the role of the National Medical Commission in mandating mentorship programs in India, but their evaluation was largely qualitative.[13] In contrast, our study provides quantitative evidence from a newly established institution, offering fresh insights into implementation challenges in emerging medical colleges.

Students’ attitudes toward the mentor–mentee program in this study were moderately positive, with 55.2% reporting comfort in approaching mentors and 61.9% believing that mentors genuinely cared about their development. However, the relatively high proportion of neutral responses (e.g. 35.8% regarding comfort in approaching mentors) and the low level of proactive engagement (only 48.6% actively sought mentor interaction) indicate that while students value the program, its practical implementation may not fully meet their expectations. Similar challenges have been noted in previous studies, where limited mentor accessibility and unclear program structures reduced student participation.[38] The preference for faculty mentors over senior students (57.4%) reflects a desire for experienced guidance, consistent with earlier literature that emphasizes the mentor’s role as a professional role model.[6]

Qualitative insights from this study further revealed that students highly valued academic, career, and personal guidance, as well as confidence-building and relationship development. Nevertheless, dissatisfaction among some students—who reported “nothing” valuable due to lack of interaction—underscores the need for structured frameworks to ensure consistent mentor–mentee engagement. Suggestions for improvement, such as enhanced mentor accessibility and more structured programs, align with recommendations from prior studies that advocate for clear guidelines and regular interactions to maximize the benefits of mentorship.[14]

Limitations of this study include its single-institution setting, which may limit generalizability to other medical colleges with different mentoring structures or student demographics. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported data via questionnaires may introduce response bias. Future research should explore longitudinal outcomes of mentoring programs and incorporate perspectives from mentors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of program dynamics. Comparative studies across multiple institutions could also identify best practices for optimizing mentor–mentee interactions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that undergraduate medical students at SBMC value the mentor–mentee program for its contributions to personal, academic, and career development. However, challenges such as limited mentor interaction, time constraints, and unclear guidelines significantly hinder its effectiveness. To address these issues, institutions should implement structured programs with regular, scheduled meetings, improved mentor accessibility, and better mentor–mentee matching. These enhancements could maximize the program’s impact, fostering greater student engagement and satisfaction. Future research should focus on longitudinal evaluations and multi-institutional studies to refine mentoring frameworks and ensure their alignment with student needs.
