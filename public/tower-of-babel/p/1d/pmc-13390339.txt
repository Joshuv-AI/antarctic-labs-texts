Yellow nail syndrome: a syndrome with prominent manifest involvement of the respiratory system
Authors: Ying Wang, Juan Ren, Congzheng Mao, Huaiming Qiu, Beibei Meng, Xiaoyu Cai, Bo Yang, Haichao Liu, Zhenhong Hu, Yao Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundYellow Nail Syndrome (YNS) is a rare disorder characterized by the classic triad of yellow nails, lymphedema, and chronic respiratory involvement. Case PresentationWe report a 42-year-old male with YNS and childhood-onset symptoms lasting for over three decades. He presented with refractory, recurrent chylothorax that progressed to organized pleural effusion and pleural calcification, complicated by respiratory failure and pericardial effusion. Systemic lymphangiography confirmed severe lymphatic reflux and obstruction.ConclusionThis case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of YNS, which often leads to underdiagnosis. Introduction

Yellow Nail Syndrome (YNS) is a rare disorder defined by the triad of yellow nails, lymphedema, and chronic respiratory manifestations. Its pathogenesis is hypothesized to involve impaired lymphatic development. First described in 1964 [1], fewer than 500 cases have been documented worldwide. YNS predominantly affects individuals over 50 years of age, although pediatric-onset cases—though uncommon—are increasingly reported in the literature [2, 3]. Its precise etiology and underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated. We present a male patient with childhood-onset YNS and a clinical course exceeding three decades. His manifestations extended beyond the classic triad: yellow nails complicated by onychomycosis; refractory lymphedema; and rare, severe systemic complications—including organized pleural effusion, pleural calcification, progressive respiratory failure, and symptomatic pericardial effusion. This case delineates disease progression from localized to systemic complications, providing valuable insights into YNS natural history and pathological evolution.

Case presentation

A 42-year-old male was admitted for recurrent exertional chest tightness and wheezing of 16-year duration, worsening over the past 20 days. His disease followed a progressive, indolent, and staged course spanning three decades. At age 9 years, he developed bilateral, nonpitting lower-limb edema, initially misdiagnosed as erysipelas and unresponsive to antimicrobial therapy. At age 13, he was diagnosed with bilateral lower-limb lymphedema of undetermined etiology at a local hospital. By age 22, he exhibited yellowish-brown discoloration of the fingernails and toenails, accompanied by nail plate thickening. At age 26, the patient underwent debridement for lymphatic and soft tissue lesions in the left lower extremity, with suboptimal efficacy; consequently, right lower extremity surgery was forgone. Recurrent chylous pleural effusion ensued, presenting with intermittent chest tightness and dyspnea; multiple thoracenteses were performed. Biochemical testing revealed pleural fluid triglyceride levels of 4.85 mmol/L (right side) and 2.68 mmol/L (left side), with lymphocytic predominance (57% on the right, 63% on the left) on cell differential analysis. Bacterial, mycobacterial, and fungal cultures, as well as malignant cytology, were all negative. At age 31, scrotoplasty was performed for scrotal edema. Due to financial constraints and poor therapeutic response, no thoracentesis or pleural biopsy was performed over the subsequent 5 years, and the patient was ultimately admitted for worsening chest tightness and dyspnea.

On admission, physical examination revealed lethargy, scattered bilateral lung moist rales, occasional wheezes, abdominal distension, bilateral lower limb non-pitting edema (left limb surgical scar, Fig. 1B), and yellowish-brown nails with thickening and longitudinal ridging (Fig. 1C, D). Arterial blood gas analysis indicated type II respiratory failure: partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO₂) 63 mmHg, partial pressure of oxygen (PaO₂) 54 mmHg, base excess (BE) 12.1 mmol/L, total CO₂ 41.8 mmol/L. Laboratory tests showed elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR, 16 mm/h), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA, 4.39 ng/mL), interleukin-6 (IL-6, 18.8 pg/mL), serum creatinine (Scr, 118 µmol/L), uric acid (UA, 725 µmol/L), and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP, 867 pg/mL). Chest CT demonstrated bilateral encapsulated organized pleural effusions, pleural calcification/fibrotic thickening, and mild pericardial effusion (Fig. 1E). Inguinal lymphangiography showed lower limb lymphatic stasis (visible vessels) but no opacification of inguinal and above lymphatic pathways, indicating proximal obstruction (Fig. 1F). Nail fungal microscopy confirmed Trichophyton infection. Whole-exome sequencing of peripheral blood identified no pathogenic mutations linked to the phenotype, supporting sporadic YNS and excluding known hereditary lymphedema.

YNS was diagnosed based on the classic triad (yellow nails, progressive lymphedema, and recurrent pleural effusion), chylothorax, lymphangiographic evidence of impaired lymphatic drainage, chest CT findings, and exclusion of differential diagnoses—including tobacco-stained nails, filarial lymphedema, autoimmune diseases, malignancies, and tuberculous pleurisy. Multidisciplinary consultation confirmed the diagnosis. Treatment included oral itraconazole (400 mg/day, 1 week per month for 3–4 months) for onychomycosis and vitamin E (800 IU/day for 3–4 months). The patient remained stable after discharge with ongoing follow-up.

Discussion and conclusions

YNS diagnosis relies on the classic triad, although only 27%–60% of patients present with all three features, and symptom onset may be separated by years, leading to underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis [4]. Our patient had a 33-year delay from symptom onset at age 9 to diagnosis at age 42, with sequential manifestations of limb edema, nail changes, and pleural effusion—reflecting YNS’s characteristic staggered symptom emergence, which hinders early diagnosis. Pediatric YNS may present with nail or pulmonary lesions in infancy and delayed lymphedema, with no fixed symptom sequence [4]. Thus, the presence of any two components of the triad should raise suspicion for YNS. YNS’s most distinctive feature is nail changes—yellowish-green discoloration, thickening, longitudinal ridging, hardening, slow growth, and obscured lunula due to hyperkeratosis [1, 5]. Etiologies include impaired lymphatic drainage, secondary obstruction [6], and lipofuscin deposition [7]. Concurrent onychomycosis, as seen in this case, has been reported [5]; clinicians should not attribute nail changes solely to fungal infection but should consider YNS in patients with lymphedema or pleural effusion. The nail alterations observed here likely result from a combination of YNS-related dystrophy and fungal infection, with the individual contributions remaining unclear.

Lymphedema (with an incidence of 29%–80%) is the most common YNS manifestation, initial in one-third of patients [2, 8, 9], typically presenting as bilateral below-knee non-pitting edema, indistinguishable from primary lymphedema [10]. Pleural effusion occurs in 36% of patients (68% bilateral), and is initial in 30% [11]. Most (95%) are exudative, 92% lymphocyte-predominant, and 22% chylous [11]—consistent with our patient, who developed the rare complication of calcified pleural effusion, respiratory failure, and pericardial effusion. These complications stemmed from 17 years of untreated chylothorax, with chronic stasis and inflammation inducing pleural fibrosis and calcification—emphasizing the need for early intervention to prevent disease progression. Moreover, YNS is frequently complicated by pulmonary comorbidities including bronchiectasis and chronic bronchitis [8]. Current evidence links its clinical manifestations closely to tissue edema and abnormal fibrous tissue proliferation secondary to lymphatic dysfunction [12]. Laboratory abnormalities reflected systemic involvement: elevated CEA and IL-6 indicated chronic inflammation due to lymphatic stasis; increased UA suggested tissue hypoxia and metabolic impairment; elevated BNP confirmed right ventricular dysfunction secondary to pleural effusion and cardiac failure. These biomarkers support a pathological cascade initiated by lymphatic obstruction, leading to inflammation, metabolic disturbances, and end-organ damage. Alternative mechanisms, such as microvascular protein leakage, have been proposed [13]. Most YNS cases are sporadic [12], a finding supported by negative whole-exome sequencing in our patient.

YNS lacks a standardized treatment, with management focused on symptomatic care. Oral vitamin E may improve nail abnormalities via antioxidative effects [14]; zinc sulfate benefits nails and lymphedema but not respiratory symptoms [15]. Spontaneous nail remission occurs in 30% of patients, possibly linked to improved respiratory status—though the underlying mechanism remains unclear [2]. Lymphedema requires compression therapy, skin care, exercise, and, occasionally, surgery [16]. Recurrent pleural effusions require thoracentesis; however, non-adherence (as in this case) leads to effusion organization and calcification, increasing surgical risks. Our patient showed mild nail improvement with itraconazole and vitamin E, with no adverse effects. In conclusion, this case’s multisystem manifestations confirm impaired systemic lymphatic circulation as YNS’s core pathophysiology. Clinicians should maintain high suspicion for YNS in patients with unexplained lymphedema, pleural effusion, or nail changes to enable early intervention and improve outcomes.
