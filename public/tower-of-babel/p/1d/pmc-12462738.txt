Green Fluorescent Protein SELEX: Immobilization Chemistry and His‐Tag Epitope Bias
Authors: Stefen Stangherlin, Tyler Malloch, Anthony J. Clarke, Juewen Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite numerous DNA aptamers for proteins having been reported, a model system allowing the use of cost‐effective proteins, unmodified DNA, and convenient homogeneous assays is still lacking, which has in turn limited not only fundamental studies of aptamers but also their translation to practical applications. Herein, three separate green fluorescent protein (GFP) selections were carried out using both non‐tagged and His‐tagged GFP immobilized on either NHS‐activated resin or Co2+ affinity resin. Only the GFP/NHS system resulted in aptamers that consistently bind to unmodified GFP, whereas the His‐tagged GFP yielded aptamers biased toward the His‐tag epitope. Sequence alignment and fluorescence polarization assays indicate that many previously published aptamers bound to the His‐tag instead of the intended protein. This work not only obtained a model aptamer for proteins but also revealed critical information on bias toward His‐tags during aptamer selections.

Proteins are integral to many aspects of life, including disease progression and immune responses. Consequently, their detection and targeting are essential for disease diagnostics and therapeutics.[ 1 ] Currently, antibodies are the gold standard for enabling the rapid, accurate, point‐of‐care detection of proteins.[ 2 ] However, antibodies have a complex, high cost of production, high batch‐to‐batch variability, and short shelf life.[ 3 ]

The development of SELEX (Systematic Evolution of Ligands by EXponential enrichment) has enabled the isolation of aptamers (short, single‐stranded DNA oligonucleotides).[ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 ] DNA aptamers for a range of protein targets have been reported, including for free human proteins, viral proteins, as well as membrane proteins.[ 10 , 11 ] Despite this progress, their translation to clinical and commercial use has been limited. Although some chemically modified aptamers, such as SOMAmers have been commercialized,[ 12 ] using unmodified aptamers is more attractive due to their lower cost and broader accessibility.

Quite strikingly, a good model protein‐binding aptamer is still lacking, which has limited fundamental studies. The most commonly used thrombin aptamers have G‐quadruplex structures that can bind to many other molecules.[ 13 ] In addition, human thrombin is very expensive (∼$1000 US mg−1). Lysozyme is another widely studied aptamer target. Although lysozyme is cost‐effective, it is highly positively charged and can non‐specifically bind to DNA. Given that the original lysozyme aptamer is an RNA,[ 14 ] most subsequent studies used the DNA version of it with various poorly verified truncations.[ 15 , 16 , 17 ] For other popular protein targets, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and platelet‐derived growth factor, their costs are even higher than for thrombin. In contrast, for small‐molecule binding aptamers,[ 4 , 18 ] many classical model systems are available, such as those for ATP,[ 19 ] cocaine/quinine,[ 20 ] theophylline,[ 21 ] and OTA.[ 22 ] Here, we hope to isolate a good model aptamer for proteins.

The binding assay is another challenging area for studying protein‐binding aptamers. The gold standard assay relies on surface plasmon resonance (SPR) spectroscopy, which not only requires an expensive instrument but also expensive gold surfaces for the immobilization of thiolated aptamers.[ 23 ] We prefer to have a homogeneous label‐free assay free of potential artifacts brought by surfaces. In this regard, green fluorescent protein (GFP) is a good choice, which allows the use of unmodified aptamers for highly sensitive fluorescence polarization assays. In addition, GFP is highly stable, available, has many prior structural studies, and most importantly, is fluorescent. GFP can be easily expressed in large quantities using standard E. coli lab strains. Most universities probably have a plasmid for it, and it is used in many teaching labs. To date, only an RNA aptamer has been reported for GFP,[ 24 ] while no DNA aptamers are known.

It is also important to note that the same protein name can represent different molecules. A typical example is whether a His‐tag is involved, which is often designed to facilitate protein purification. Herein, we systematically selected aptamers for GFP to gain two critical insights. First, we sought to investigate various immobilization methods. For this, we used two constructs of mGreen‐Lantern GFP with or without a C‐terminal hexa‐histidine tag (GFP‐His6).[ 25 ] Additionally, we used two methods of target immobilization, including Co2+ metal affinity resin (with GFP‐His6) and NHS‐activated agarose resin (with both GFP and GFP‐His6) for a total of three distinct selection strategies. With this systematic SELEX experiment, we observed a strong bias toward evolving aptamers for the His‐tag epitope, not only for GFP, but also for other proteins. Finally, we proposed a simple and general GFP‐based assay for testing nonspecific His‐tag binding by aptamers.

His‐Tag and Bioconjugation Strongly Affect SELEX

The GFP and His‐tagged GFP (GFP‐His6) used for SELEX were expressed in E. coli and purified (Figure S1). The isoelectric point of GFP was 6.5, and thus it is overall negatively charged in our selection buffer at pH 7.4. To immobilize GFP for aptamer selection, we utilized two types of resin materials. First, we, respectively, covalently bound GFP and GFP‐His6 to NHS‐activated resin (Figure 1a,b) via a surface lysine residue of GFP. In addition, we attached GFP‐His6 to Co2+ affinity resin (Figure 1c).

Schematic representation of immobilization strategies used for aptamer selection of GFP. NHS‐activated agarose resin was used to covalently link a) untagged and b) His‐tagged GFP, as well as c) using NTA‐Co2+ affinity resin with His‐tagged GFP. d) The structure of an NTA–Co2+–His6 complex.

We utilized a DNA library comprising 30 random nucleotides (N30) flanked by two constant primer regions. The two primer regions have complementarity, which ensures the formation of a 10‐base‐pair stem; consequently, target binding is restricted to the N30 region, which reduces the complexity of secondary structure analysis. The selection of GFP‐binding aptamers used a process as depicted in Figure S2. To start, 500 pmol of ssDNA library was incubated with GFP‐bound resin to allow potential aptamers to bind. After washing away non‐binding DNA, the resulting aptamer‐bound resin was resuspended and directly used for PCR amplification to generate the library for the next round of selection. To increase stringency, the incubation time and the amount of GFP‐bound resin were gradually lowered. A total of 12 rounds were completed for each of the three immobilized GFPs, after which the libraries were deep sequenced.

Figure 2a–c shows the top ten most abundant sequences from each selection. To our surprise, all of the selections resulted in different sequences, despite all using GFP as the target. This result indicates that even a minor His‐tag modification and different immobilization methods can lead to distinct SELEX outcomes. Overall, all three selections showed good enrichment, with the top ten sequences accounting for > 40% of the final pool (Figure 2d). Using Co2+ metal affinity resin with GFP‐His6 (Figure 1c), we noticed a significant enrichment of two sequences (GFP‐His6‐Co‐1 and GFP‐His6‐Co‐2 in Figure 2c). These sequences were not found in the other two selections. Similarly, using GFP‐His6 conjugated NHS resin (Figure 1b), there was significant enrichment of a single sequence (GFP‐His6‐1, Figure 2b), as well as very high conservation of its corresponding family. Using GFP conjugated NHS resin (Figure 1a), there was a more equal abundance of enriched sequences (its most enriched sequence was named GFP‐1, Figure 2a). For the two selections done with NHS resin, there are some sequence similarities, although still quite low. The high‐abundance GFP‐1 aptamer was found in the NHS–GFP‐His6 selection at 1.6%, whereas the GFP‐His6‐1 aptamer was found in the NHS–GFP selection at only 0.06%. We also looked for conserved motif regions from the top aptamer of each selection and found no significant similarities to other libraries as well (Table S1). These results indicated quite distinct sequence evolution of these three SELEX experiments.

High‐Affinity GFP Aptamers

To test the binding affinity of the enriched sequences, we used fluorescence polarization (FP) assays (485 nm excitation and 525 nm emission). FP uses the inherent fluorescence of GFP to achieve a label‐free and homogeneous assay, avoiding potential non‐specific interactions with gold or other surfaces. We tested the top three most abundant sequences of each selection (GFP‐1, 2, 3; GFP‐His6‐1, 2, 3; and GFP‐His6‐Co‐1, 2, 3) for binding to both GFP and GFP‐His6. For the NHS‐GFP selection, its top three aptamers all induced an approximately 30 mP increase in FP with K d values between 12 and 27 nM (Figure 3a), whereas a control sequence of the same length showed no FP change, confirming specific binding of the aptamers. Similarly, this group of aptamers can also bind GFP‐His6 with similar affinities (Figure 3d). Thus, the NHS‐GFP selection yielded low nanomolar affinity aptamers to both GFP and GFP‐His6, suggesting the binding events occurred away from the His‐tag.

Fluorescence polarization binding curves of the top three aptamers from each selection, along with a non‐binding control aptamer. Binding curves are displayed for the NHS‐GFP selected aptamers (a,d), the NHS‐GFP‐His6 selected aptamers (b,c), and the Co2+‐resin selected aptamers (c,f), using GFP (a–c) or GFP‐His6 (d–f) as the target. The K d values are supplied. n.d. represents “not determined.” Error bars are standard deviations from three replicates.

In contrast, the NHS–GFP‐His6‐derived aptamers did not bind to GFP apart from GFP‐His6‐1 (Figure 3b), while all aptamers bound to GFP–His6 (Figure 3e). Therefore, the use of His‐tag GFP produced aptamers biased toward the His‐tag epitope. Furthermore, the Co2+–GFP‐His6‐selected aptamers failed to bind either construct of GFP apart from GFP‐His6‐Co‐2 (Figure 3c,f), which bound to GFP‐His6 with only a modest FP change of 10 mP. Thus, the enrichment of these aptamers was influenced not only by the His‐tag but also by the Co2+ resin. Although GFP is a relatively large molecule (MW 27 kDa), binding of an aptamer (MW 14 kDa) still induced a significant increase in FP (up to 30mP). Although this increase is lower than what is achievable by labeling the aptamer (up to 200 mP),[ 26 , 27 , 28 ] the use of non‐labeled, cost‐effective aptamers is highly attractive for binding assays.

To confirm binding to the His‐tag, we mixed 10 nM GFP with 1 µM MltF‐His6, and then added aptamers. MltF is a 54 kDa protein with a predicted pI of 6.34 that bears no structural resemblance to GFP. The binding of GFP by the GFP‐1 aptamer was not influenced by the 100‐fold excess MltF‐His6 protein (Figure 4a). However, the binding of the GFP‐His6‐2, GFP‐His6‐3, and GFP‐His6‐Co‐2 aptamers was all decreased (Figure 4b–d), indicating that these three aptamers indeed interact with the His‐tag moiety of the proteins. Using the GFP‐His6‐2 aptamer as an example, the presence of 100‐fold excess MltF‐His6 protein has increased the apparent K d to GFP from 13 to 112 nM (a 9‐fold change). Therefore, the resulting K d for MltF‐His6 was estimated to be 131 nM. Based on this competitive binding assay, the GFP‐His6‐Co‐2 aptamer has the strongest affinity to the His‐tag, whereas the GFP‐1 aptamer has no affinity to the His‐tag.

Binding curves of a) GFP‐1, b) GFP‐His6‐2, c) GFP‐His6‐3, and d) GFP‐His6‐Co‐2 aptamers to 10 nM GFP‐His6 (red lines), and 10 nM GFP‐His6 mixed with 1 µM of MltF‐His6 for 10 min before adding aptamer (green lines). e) Secondary structure of the GFP‐1 aptamer predicted by mFold. In a mutant, the circled guanine at position 11 is deleted. Error bars are standard deviations from three replicates.

Based on the selection results, GFP‐1 is the best aptamer for binding to GFP for several reasons: i) it binds with low nanomolar affinity to GFP, ii) its binding is not influenced by the His‐tag, and iii) it has the highest abundance. Using mFold, we predicted the secondary structure of GFP‐1 (Figure 4e). To probe this structure, we deleted the bulged nucleotide G11 from GFP‐1 and this mutant failed to bind to GFP (Figure S3). This mutant thus provides strong evidence to confirm specific binding of the GFP‐1 aptamer. Accordingly, in the binding structure, the three base pairs close to the large loop (5′‐CTG…CAG‐3′) are likely to be open and participate in GFP binding.

His‐Tag Biased SELEX

Since numerous aptamers were selected using His‐tagged proteins, our results prompted us to examine a broader range of aptamers. Actually, His‐tag as a peptide was previously used as a target in a few aptamer selections. We first aligned the sequence of GFP‐His6‐2 against six His‐tag peptide binding aptamers from the literature (Figure 5a).[ 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 ] Interestingly, the 20S aptamer shows a stretch of 16 identical nucleotides, whereas the other sequences also comprise a fairly conserved region of GGT(C/T)GGGTTTGG. This result further confirmed that our GFP‐His6‐2 aptamer is a His‐tag binding aptamer.

a) The His‐tag binding GFP‐His6‐2 aptamer sequence aligned with published His‐tag binding aptamers and His‐tagged‐protein binding aptamers. The conserved region of GGT(C/T)GGGTTTGG is highlighted in red. Binding curves of b) CoV2‐6C3, c) MSA1‐T3, and d) tNSP3 protein‐binding aptamers with 10 nM GFP (red lines) or 10 nM GFP‐His6 (green lines). Error bars are standard deviations from three replicates.

We then turned our attention to previously reported aptamers selected using His‐tagged proteins. Given the vast number of such aptamers, we randomly picked a few His‐tagged Covid proteins and a few other proteins (Table S2).[ 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 ] Alignment of these sequences with GFP‐His6‐2 also revealed a well‐conserved region (Figure 5a). To test their affinity towards the His‐tag, we picked three aptamers: CoV2‐6C3, MSA1‐T3, and tNSP3, and monitored their binding to GFP and GFP‐His6 (Figure 5b–d). Notably, Cov2‐6C3 bound GFP‐His6 with low nanomolar affinity but had no affinity to GFP (Figure 5b). Although an affinity could not be reasonably fitted for tNSP3, it was clearly influenced by the His‐tag at higher concentrations (Figure 5c). MSA1‐T3 showed no binding to either GFP or GFP‐His6 (Figure 5d). Therefore, out of the three aptamers reported for Covid proteins, two can bind to GFP‐His6. This result demonstrates that many previously reported aptamers might be enriched to bind to the His‐tag epitope instead of the intended target protein. Since histidine can interact with DNA via both pi–pi stacking and hydrogen bonding, it is not surprising that a poly‐histidine fragment can dominate aptamer selection. Therefore, we stress that for the selection of aptamers for proteins, His‐tagged proteins should be avoided, or the selected aptamers need to be carefully examined for potential His‐tag binding.

GFP is an important tool in chemical biology. For example, GFP and its derivatives have been used as a fusion tag to monitor protein localization and dynamic cellular events,[ 43 ] as a reporter gene to characterize the strength of promotors,[ 44 ] and as a FRET reporter to study protein‐protein interactions in vivo.[ 45 ] While the GFP‐binding aptamers can find great utility for fundamental aptamer binding studies, there is also potential for their use together with GFP in chemical biology, such as replacing anti‐GFP antibodies. Additionally, a quencher‐labeled aptamer may act to modulate GFP fluorescence. To further test its practical applicability, we determined the binding affinity of GFP‐1 for GFP in 10% bacterial lysate, where its binding affinity was not affected (Figure S4).

In this study, we systematically investigated the effect of immobilization methods for the enrichment of DNA aptamers for GFP, and have reached the following conclusions. First, for immobilization of target proteins, using NHS resin is recommended, since our Co2+‐resin‐based SELEX failed to yield aptamers for GFP‐His6. Second, using His‐tagged proteins might enrich aptamers preferentially binding to the His‐tag epitope, and thus, non‐tagged proteins are recommended for SELEX. Third, some previously selected aptamers that used His‐tagged proteins might bind to the His‐tag instead of the intended protein. We reported here a simple method for using GFP to identify such aptamers. Finally, we report the first GFP‐binding DNA aptamers with low nanomolar affinities. These aptamers offer a convenient method for the study of aptamer binding to a model protein that will be useful for fundamental aptamer binding studies, biosensor development, and chemical biology applications.
