Analysis of factors associated with poor R-wave progression in precordial leads in patients with acute pulmonary thromboembolism: a retrospective study
Authors: Xiaomei Wu, Yun Guo, Panpan Liang, Honghong Ma, Peng Jiang, Bing Zhuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute pulmonary thromboembolism (APE) presents with nonspecific clinical manifestations, and the classic electrocardiogram (ECG) abnormalities associated with it have limited sensitivity. Poor R-wave progression (PRWP) is a common ECG finding linked to various cardiovascular diseases; however, the factors associated with PRWP and its clinical significance in patients with APE remain unclear. This study aims to investigate the factors related to PRWP in APE patients, providing ECG-based evidence to enhance clinical assessment.ObjectiveExplore the factors associated with PRWP in the precordial leads of patients with APE.MethodsThis retrospective analysis included 170 patients diagnosed with APE by computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) at the People's Hospital of Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, Ningxia Medical University, from January 2022 to December 2025. Patients were divided into two groups based on the presence of PRWP on the ECG within 24 h of admission: the PRWP group (n = 49) and the non-PRWP group (n = 121). Clinical characteristics, laboratory findings, and echocardiographic parameters were collected. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent factors associated with PRWP. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsPatients with PRWP had significantly higher N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide(NT-proBNP) (1270 vs. 430 pg/mL, P = 0.020) and tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TVR) (3.10 vs. 2.68 m/s, P = 0.017). Multivariate analysis identified TVR as the only independent correlate of PRWP (OR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.08–2.58, P = 0.021). Subgroup analysis demonstrated that elevated NT-proBNP (≥ 200 pg/mL) was significantly associated with PRWP (OR = 3.31, 95% CI: 1.37–7.99, P = 0.008). No significant associations were observed for D-dimer, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), or cardiac chamber dimensions.ConclusionTVR is independently associated with PRWP in patients with APE, and elevated NT-proBNP (≥ 200 pg/mL) also predict PRWP in subgroup analyses. The absence of chamber enlargement suggests that PRWP may reflect pressure overload before structural changes become apparent on echocardiography, indicating its potential as an early marker of right ventricular strain. Pending prospective validation, PRWP could serve as a simple ECG marker to complement existing risk stratification methods in APE patients.Keywords: Pulmonary thromboembolism, Electrocardiogram, Poor R-wave progression, NT-proBNP, Risk factors, Tricuspid regurgitation velocity

Introduction

Acute pulmonary thromboembolism (APE) is a clinical syndrome characterized by the obstruction of the pulmonary artery or its branches by endogenous or exogenous emboli, leading to acute pulmonary circulatory disorders or right heart dysfunction [1, 2]. It is a common cardiovascular disease with high mortality. Its clinical manifestations are diverse and nonspecific; some patients may be asymptomatic, while others may experience sudden cardiac death. This wide spectrum increases the risk of missed or incorrect diagnoses in clinical practice. In recent years, the widespread use of CTPA has significantly improved the diagnostic rate of APE. However, in emergency departments, primary care settings, and bedside evaluations, the use of simpler, non-invasive methods for rapid preliminary screening and risk assessment remains a crucial step in optimizing the diagnosis and treatment of APE.

ECG, a simple, non-invasive, and readily accessible diagnostic tool, plays a crucial role in the diagnosis and assessment of APE. Classic signs such as the SIQIIITIII pattern, right bundle branch block (RBBB), and T-wave inversion in the precordial leads are well-established indicators of increased right ventricular load. These signs provide significant reference value for disease diagnosis, risk stratification, and prognosis evaluation [3]. However, ECG manifestations in APE patients are diverse and often non-specific, with typical signs demonstrating limited sensitivity. Therefore, an in-depth exploration of potential ECG indicators is essential to enhance clinical diagnostic accuracy and improve risk stratification in APE.

Poor R-wave progression (PRWP) in the precordial leads is a common electrocardiographic finding. According to the diagnostic criteria proposed by Zema et al. [4], PRWP is primarily defined as the absence of a normal progressive increase in R-wave amplitude across the precordial leads V1 to V3, characterized by an R-wave amplitude in V3 (RV3) ≤ 3 mm and RV2 ≤ RV3. Previous studies have predominantly associated PRWP with anterior myocardial infarction, where the pathophysiological basis is a weakened ventricular depolarization vector caused by myocardial ischemia or fibrosis in the anterior wall, resulting in abnormal R-wave progression [5, 6]. Recent studies have shown that PRWP can also be observed in CHD, myocarditis, pulmonary diseases such as pneumothorax [7], dilated cardiomyopathy [8], and even in a small number of healthy individuals. Moreover, existing studies have indicated that PRWP is associated with poor prognosis in the general patient population and an increased risk of sudden cardiac death of cardiac origin in patients with coronary heart disease [9]. However, research on the clinical significance, specific causes, and correlation with cardiac structural and functional parameters of PRWP in APE patients remains relatively scarce.

Therefore, this study retrospectively analyzed the clinical data, laboratory results, and imaging findings of 170 patients with APE to investigate the correlation between the presence of a PRWP and various clinical indicators. The objective was to provide a reference for more accurate clinical assessment of the underlying health status and cardiac function in patients with APE.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This was a single-center retrospective study. We enrolled 170 inpatients diagnosed with APE by CTPA at the People's Hospital of Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, Ningxia Medical University from January 2022 to December 2025, excluding cases of repeated admission. This study was performed in compliance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the People's Hospital of Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, Ningxia Medical University.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: 1)Patients with a first diagnosed APE in accordance with the 2019 ESC Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of Acute Pulmonary Embolism; 2) completion of ECG, echocardiography, and relevant laboratory examinations within 24 h of admission; 3) availability of complete clinical data for review.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) Presence of advanced malignant tumors; 2) History of acute myocardial infarction, complete left bundle branch block, or cardiac pacemaker implantation; 3) Mental disorders or cognitive dysfunction; 4) Incomplete clinical data or repeated hospital admissions; 5) Severe chronic underlying diseases and systemic comorbidities, including: ① Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), hypertension, coronary heart disease (CHD) and other ischemic cardiomyopathy (ICMP), which were excluded to reduce confounding bias; ② Other severe systemic diseases, such as severe hepatic and renal failure, coagulation disorders, and autoimmune diseases (Fig. 1).

Diagnostic criteria and grouping method

PRWP Diagnostic Criteria: We adopted the classic diagnostic criteria proposed by Zema et al. [4]. PRWP was diagnosed when both of the following conditions were met: (1) R-wave amplitude in lead V3 ≤ 3 mm (0.3 mV); (2) R-wave amplitude in lead V2 ≤ R-wave amplitude in lead V3. Patients with pathological Q waves in leads V1 or V2, or with abnormal lead placement, were excluded from PRWP assessment.

Grouping method: Based on ECG results obtained within 24 h of admission, patients were divided into two groups: the PRWP group (patients meeting the PRWP diagnostic criteria) and the non-PRWP group (patients not meeting the PRWP diagnostic criteria).

Thrombus Burden Grading Criteria (Qanadli Score): Based on the admission CTPA report, the thrombus burden was quantified using the Qanadli score [10]. The formula is: CT Obstruction Index = (Σ(n × d)/40) × 100, where n represents the number of segmental branches distal to the thrombus [1–20], and d indicates the degree of obstruction (1 = partial, 2 = complete). Classification: Low (< 20%), Moderate (20–40%), High (> 40%).

Statistical analysis

We performed all statistical analyses using SPSS version 27.0 and R software (version 4.5.1). Categorical data were expressed as n (%), and comparisons between groups were conducted using the chi-square (χ2) test. Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (øverline x ± s) and compared using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous data were expressed as median (interquartile range) [M (P25, P75)] and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Multivariate logistic stepwise regression analysis was applied to identify independent factors associated with PRWP. univariate logistic regression RCS curves with three preset nodes were used to explore the dose–response relationship, and the Wald χ2 test was employed for interaction analysis in subgroup studies. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of clinical data between the two groups

A total of 404 patients clinically diagnosed with APE were identified from the electronic medical record system, of whom 151 cases (37.6%) met the PRWP criteria. After excluding 234 cases based on predefined criteria, ultimately, this study included a total of 170 patients with APE, with 49 (28.8%) in the PRWP group and 121 (71.2%) in the non-PRWP group. Regarding past medical history, the proportions of patients with Diabetes mellitus (DM) (12.2% vs. 4.1%, χ2 = 2.571, P = 0.109) and in the in-hospital death rate (12.2% vs. 5.8%, χ2 = 1.248, P = 0.264) were significantly higher in the PRWP group than in the non-PRWP group, but there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P > 0.05). The proportion of patients in the non-PRWP group with a history of trauma or surgery was higher than that in the PRWP group (19.8% vs. 6.1%, χ2 = 4.908, P = 0.027), and no significant differences were found in other baseline characteristics. Laboratory examination results showed that the NT-proBNP and partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2) levels in the PRWP group was significantly higher than that in the non-PRWP group (NT-proBNP: 1270 pg/mL vs. 430 pg/mL, U = 2292.5, P = 0.020; pCO2: 39.0 mmHg vs. 36.0 mmHg, U = 2336.5, P = 0.031). In contrast, Ca2+ level in the non-PRWP group was higher than that in the PRWP group (2.18 mmol/L vs. 2.13 mmol/L, U = 2392.0, P = 0.049). No statistically significant differences were observed in other laboratory indicators—including white blood cell count (WBC), Hs-CRP, myocardial enzymes, coagulation function—between the two groups (P > 0.05). Echocardiographic analysis revealed that pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) (45.0 mmHg vs. 33.0 mmHg, U = 2319.5, P = 0.026), pressure gradient (PG) (38.3 mmHg vs. 28.2 mmHg, U = 2267.0, P = 0.016) and TVR (3.10 m/s vs. 2.68 m/s, U = 2271.0, P = 0.017) were significantly higher in the PRWP group than in the non-PRWP group (P < 0.05). Main pulmonary artery diameter(MPA), right ventricular end-diastolic diameter (RVIDd), right atrial transverse diameter(RATD), right atrial antero-posterior diameter(RAAPD) and left ventricular end-diastolic diameter(LVEDD) were approximately equal in the PRWP group compared to the non-PRWP group, meanwhile these differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). The positive rate of RBBB was 21.5% (26/121) in the non-PRWP group and 18.4% (9/49) in the PRWP group (χ2 = 0.208, P = 0.649). Detailed results are presented in Table 1 and Fig. 2.

Univariate logistic regression analysis of PRWP occurrence

Taking the presence or absence of PRWP in APE patients (PRWP group = 1, non-PRWP group = 0) as the dependent variable, factors with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) in the univariate analysis—including History of trauma or surgery, pCO2, NT-proBNP, TNT-HS, Ca2+, TVR, PASP and PG—were included in the univariate logistic regression analysis. In contrast, pCO2, NT-proBNP, TNT-HS and PASP showed no significant predictive effect (P > 0.05). Details are shown in Table 2.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between baseline demographic and clinical characteristics and the risk of PRWP in patients with APE. In univariate logistic regression, elevated NT-proBNP (≥ 200 pg/mL) was the only statistically significant predictor of PRWP (OR = 3.31, 95% CI: 1.37–7.99, P = 0.008). Diabetes mellitus showed a borderline association with increased PRWP risk, although it did not reach the conventional threshold for statistical significance (OR = 3.24, 95% CI: 0.94–11.16, P = 0.063). No significant associations were found for sex, age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, D-dimer, or hs-CRP (all P > 0.05). These findings suggest that NT-proBNP is a strong risk marker for PRWP, while the potential role of diabetes warrants confirmation in larger prospective studies. Discussion

The study group consisted of 404 patients clinically diagnosed with APE. After excluding 234 cases based on predefined criteria—228 with hypertension and/or coronary heart disease and 6 with other conditions—170 APE patients with complete initial diagnostic data were ultimately included. These patients were stratified into a PRWP group (n = 49) and a non-PRWP group (n = 121). For the diagnosis of PRWP, the study adopted the classic Zema criteria [4] proposed in 1982, which is one of the most widely recognized and validated standards for PRWP diagnosis in clinical and cardiovascular research. Currently, multiple diagnostic criteria for PRWP exist in the academic community, including the Marquette, Depace, and Warner criteria, which differ in their defined amplitude thresholds and rising patterns of R waves in precordial leads [5]. The Zema criteria were selected for this study due to their simplicity, high inter-observer reproducibility, extensive application in previous cardiopulmonary clinical studies, and good comparability with existing published evidence. The use of a single diagnostic criterion may limit the generalizability of our findings to studies employing other PRWP definitions, which is acknowledged as a limitation of this study.

We identified two primary signals. First, TVR was independently associated with PRWP after adjusting for other variables. Second, elevated NT-proBNP (≥ 200 pg/mL) emerged as a strong predictor in subgroup analysis. Neither D-dimer nor hs-CRP showed any significant association. Additionally, cardiac chamber dimensions did not differ between the two groups. TVR was the strongest correlate of PRWP in our data. Each 1 m/s increase in TVR was associated with an approximately 67% higher adjusted odds of PRWP. The confidence interval (1.08–2.58) indicates moderate precision, although the wide range also reflects our limited sample size. In APE, Vascular occlusion caused by thrombi in the pulmonary circulation can lead to increased pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), decreased pulmonary arterial compliance, and ultimately elevated right ventricular afterload [1, 11].

Because the right ventricular wall is thinner and more compliant than that of the left ventricle, an acute increase in afterload cannot be adequately compensated. This leads to right ventricular dilation, septal displacement toward the left ventricle, and tricuspid regurgitation [10, 12, 17–21]. A notable finding of this study is that there were no significant differences in right or left ventricular dimensions between the two patient groups. The median RVIDd was 20.9 mm in the non-PRWP group and 20.4 mm in the PRWP group (P = 0.686). Both groups exhibited nearly identical RAAPD values, each measuring 47.0 mm. These results further suggest that PRWP may reflect a state of pressure overload preceding ventricular enlargement. If confirmed, PRWP could serve as an early indicator of right ventricular overload that may not be detectable by echocardiography. However, these findings are preliminary and require further validation through prospective studies.

The PRWP group exhibited significantly higher NT-proBNP levels—approximately three times greater than those in the non-PRWP group (1270 vs. 430 pg/mL). Subgroup analysis confirmed this association: an NT-proBNP level of 200 pg/mL or higher corresponded to an OR of 3.31. These findings align with the established role of NT-proBNP as a marker of right ventricular strain in APE [1, 13, 14, 18]. However, when TVR was included in the multivariate model, NT-proBNP was no longer a significant predictor. This likely reflects collinearity between the two variables, as both capture overlapping aspects of right heart pressure overload [1, 5, 13, 14, 22]. Mechanistically, an acute increase in afterload elevates right ventricular wall stress, triggering gene transcription within ventricular myocytes that promotes BNP precursor synthesis and cleavage, ultimately releasing active BNP into the circulation [22]. Concurrently, right ventricular dilation may shift the interventricular septum leftward, impairing left ventricular filling and raising left ventricular end-diastolic pressure, which further amplifies cardiac stress and synergistically drives BNP release and tricuspid regurgitation [12, 14, 22]. The association between PRWP and elevated NT-proBNP, along with its borderline correlation with TVR, raises the question of whether PRWP might serve as an early electrocardiographic indicator of right ventricular dysfunction and a potential predictor of right-sided heart failure in patients with APE. The finding that PRWP correlates with NT-proBNP elevation suggests that PRWP may reflect similar pathophysiological processes—namely, increased right ventricular wall stress and neurohormonal activation—even before overt chamber enlargement becomes detectable on echocardiography [1, 22]. This interpretation is supported by our observation that cardiac chamber dimensions did not differ significantly between the two groups. If confirmed by prospective studies, PRWP could serve as a simple, non-invasive, and widely available ECG marker for identifying APE patients at risk of developing right-sided heart failure, particularly in resource-limited settings where echocardiography may not be immediately accessible. However, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and NT-proBNP is not specific to right-sided heart failure. For now, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating. Future prospective studies with longitudinal follow-up are needed to clarify the prognostic significance of PRWP in APE patients. Collectively, these findings suggest that PRWP may reflect right heart pressure overload rather than acute thrombotic burden or inflammation and may have potential value as an early indicator of right ventricular strain—though this requires prospective validation.

The prevalence of diabetes was three times higher in the PRWP group (12.2% vs. 4.1%), but this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.109). In the multivariate analysis, the OR was 3.40, with a 95% CI of 0.96 to 12.00, the lower bound just below 1.0. The subgroup analysis yielded a similar estimate (OR = 3.24, P = 0.063). A clinical study by Bildirici [15] demonstrated that chronic hyperglycemia in diabetes can lead to the deposition of myocardial glycation end products, myocardial microvascular pathology, autonomic dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis, and electrophysiological conduction abnormalities [16]. These changes may manifest as impaired R-wave progression on resting ECG, reflecting left ventricular diastolic impairment and an elevated Tei index in patients with diabetic cardiomyopathy [16]. Similarly, Bildirici [15] argues that rapid identification of the PRWP phenomenon on an electrocardiogram, after excluding all other conditions that may contribute to poor R-wave progression, can serve as an early indicator of diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCMP).

We also examined the relationship between PRWP and classic ECG findings of APE (S1Q3T3, RBBB, and precordial T-wave inversion). The results showed no significant differences in the prevalence of these classic findings between the PRWP and non-PRWP groups. According to ESC guidelines [1], these classic ECG changes are generally attributed to increased right ventricular load and impaired right heart function. Therefore, PRWP and classic ECG findings may provide complementary rather than overlapping information. The combined use of these two types of ECG indicators may help to more comprehensively assess cardiac involvement in patients with APE.

So clinically, PRWP can serve as a straightforward auxiliary indicator for risk stratification and clinical decision-making in the management of APE. Patients presenting with PRWP often exhibit increased right ventricular strain and require intensified monitoring and targeted treatment, whereas routine care is typically sufficient for those without this ECG abnormality. This marker is especially valuable for rapid screening in emergency departments and primary care settings.

Study limitations

First, the single-center retrospective design and modest sample size (n = 170, including 49 PRWP cases) limited statistical power and resulted in wide confidence intervals. Second, the absence of a non-APE control group precluded the assessment of PRWP specificity. Third, the timing of ECG and laboratory tests was not standardized relative to symptom onset, and quantitative severity measures (e.g., RV/LV diameter ratio) were not consistently available for adjustment. Fourth, we did not perform serial ECG follow-up or external validation of the prediction model, which limits the assessment of PRWP reversibility and generalizability.

Future directions

Future multicenter prospective studies with larger sample sizes are necessary to validate these findings. Such studies would benefit from including non-APE control subjects, standardizing the timing of data collection relative to symptom onset, and incorporating quantitative severity parameters (e.g., RV/LV diameter ratio, pulmonary artery obstruction index) into multivariate models. Serial ECG follow-up after treatment would help determine whether PRWP is reversible and if it has long-term prognostic significance. Additionally, direct comparisons of different PRWP diagnostic criteria and further investigations into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying PRWP across patient subgroups are warranted.

Conclusion

TVR is independently associated with PRWP in patients with APE, and elevated NT-proBNP levels (≥ 200 pg/mL) also predict PRWP in subgroup analyses. The absence of chamber enlargement suggests that PRWP may reflect pressure overload before structural changes become apparent on echocardiography, indicating its potential as an early marker of right ventricular strain. Pending prospective validation, PRWP could serve as a simple ECG marker to complement existing risk stratification methods in APE patients.
