Enhancing the solubility of celecoxib via lyophilized solid dispersions using computational and experimental approaches
Authors: Fatima Rasool, Ayesha Naseem, Huma Javaid, Zobia Afzal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCelecoxib (CLX), a selective COX-2 inhibitor and BCS Class II drug, is widely used for osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis but having poor aqueous solubility (4.2 µg/mL) and limited dissolution, restricting its oral bioavailability. This study aimed to enhance CLX solubility and dissolution using integrated computational and experimental approaches. Simulated crystal morphology revealed a dominant (1 0 0) face, indicating a plate-like crystal habit that may hinder dissolution. Molecular dynamics simulations further identified hydroxypropyl β-cyclodextrin (HP-βCD) as the most compatible polymer, exhibiting the lowest interaction parameter (χ = − 5.86) and mixing energy (Emix = − 3.47 kcal/mol). Physical mixtures (PM1–PM4) were prepared at a 1:1 drug-to-polymer ratio and evaluated for solubility and recrystallization inhibition. PM1 (CLX–HP-βCD) showed the highest solubility (64.18 µg/mL) and longest induction time (140 s), indicating improved supersaturation stability. Lyophilized solid dispersions (SD1–SD4) were then developed and characterized using solubility studies, FTIR spectroscopy, microscopy, and in vitro dissolution testing. SD1 exhibited the greatest solubility enhancement (645 µg/mL), over 150-fold higher than pure CLX. FTIR and microscopy confirmed molecular dispersion and amorphization within the hydrophilic matrix. In vitro dissolution testing showed SD1 achieved 78.5% drug release in 3 h, with release kinetics best described by first-order (R2 = 0.9661) and Korsmeyer–Peppas (n = 0.879) models. These dispersions were compressed into tablets (T1–T4) and assessed for mechanical properties and drug release. T1 demonstrated the highest release (99.88% in 3 h), with release kinetics fitting first-order (R2 = 0.8996) and Hixson–Crowell (R2 = 0.9351) models. Introduction

For many decades, limited aqueous solubility is a major difficulty affecting 40% of marketed and 60% of newly reported drug candidates1,2. This limitation not only hinders the drug absorption but also compromises its therapeutic efficacy1. Many scientists have attempted to improve the poor solubility by using various strategies, i.e., salt formation3, pH modification4, polymeric micelles5, prodrug design6, self-emulsifying drug delivery systems (SMEDDs), Co-crystallization7, cyclodextrin complexation5, particle size reduction8, and dispersion systems9.

Among these techniques, the solid dispersion (SD) technique has emerged as a promising and versatile strategy for improving the solubility and dissolution of BCS class-II (poorly water soluble) drugs10. This approach is an effective simple method, involves a uniform molecular dispersion of the drug in the amorphous state within a hydrophilic carrier matrix, which not only increases the wettability of pure drug and disrupts its crystalline nature of pure drug but also causes particle size reduction, increases surface area, and ultimately leads to an improvement in bioavailability11.

Celecoxib (Cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitor) commonly prescribed for the long-term treatment of inflammation, pain and arthritis, belongs to category of BCS-II12. Its limited water solubility (3–7 µg/mL) and lower dissolution rate hinders its oral bioavailability (22–44%) and ultimately affects its therapeutic efficacy13. Among all the solubility enhancement techniques14–19, solid dispersion is an effective and simple strategy to resolve solubility related problem of BCS-II drugs20–22. Solid dispersions are made using a variety of methods, such as solvent evaporation23, hot-melt extrusion24, spray drying24 and electrospinning25. However, strong drug–polymer interactions are essential for forming stable solid dispersions, as they prevent phase separation or recrystallization during storage26. These interactions help maintain the amorphous state of the drug, enhancing its solubility and bioavailability27.

Drug–polymer miscibility issues in solid dispersions have been addressed by researchers using a variety of experimental approaches, including employing Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM), solid-state Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction data analysis, Raman mapping, and Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)28. However, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, an essential computational technique in the pharmaceutical industry, provides insights into molecular interactions, conformational changes, and structural stability to forecast medication solubility and formulation performance. In recent years, these computational studies have gained attention for their ability to predict drug–polymer interactions at the atomic level29. For example, Larson and colleagues used all-atom MD simulations to study how poorly soluble drugs like phenytoin interact with polymers such as HPMC and HPMCAS in aqueous solutions30. Cyclodextrin-based inclusion complexes (especially β-CD and its derivative, HP- β-CD) widely employed to improve aqueous solubility, chemical stability and oral bioavailability of poorly water soluble drug by host-guest complexation and formation of ternary system31. Siva R. Munnangi et al. study demonstrated a solvent-free hot melt extrusion (HME) approach to prepare hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin inclusion complexes of ibuprofen, using PVP VA-64 as an auxiliary polymer, showed amorphous drug conversion and rapid dissolution (85% in 30 min), highlighting HME as a green and effective method for enhancing solubility32. By studying erlotinib–SBE-β-CD interactions using in silico methods, Devasari et al. confirmed the development of inclusion complexes and better solubility, which helped with formulation design for increased efficacy and bioavailability33. B. Li et al. used molecular dynamics simulations to investigate excipient compatibility, bonding energies, and crystal surfaces in order to avoid crystallization in supersaturated solutions of Roxadustat34.

There is limited research on integrating the computational and experimental approaches for predicting drug–polymer compatibility poses a significant barrier to developing an effective solid dispersions for enhancing the solubility of poorly water-soluble drugs like celecoxib. To address this gap, we combined molecular simulations with the best formulation prediction and characterization of physical mixtures, freeze-dried solid dispersions, and tablets compressed from these dispersions to enhance solubility, dissolution rate and therapeutic effectiveness of celecoxib.

Materials and methods

Materials

Celecoxib (CLX) was kindly provided by Genetics Pharmaceuticals. Hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) and hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HP-βCD) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (via local distributor). Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and starch were generously supplied by Daejung Chemicals. For in silico analyses, we utilized PubChem databases, ChemSketch, and the Material Studio Software Package (Accelrys Software Inc., USA). DD-Solver and Microsoft Excel were employed for the evaluation of release kinetics.

Methods

Computational predictions (molecular simulation)

For analyzing the crystal form of celecoxib, the Morphology module in BIOVIA Material Studio (Accelrys Software Inc., USA) was employed. The crystallographic information file (CIF) of monoclinic celecoxib (a = 10.6 Å, b = 9.8 Å, c = 12.5 Å; α = 90°, β = 113°, γ = 90°) was imported. For analyzing the crystal form of celecoxib, the Morphology module in BIOVIA Material Studio (Accelrys Software Inc., USA) was employed. This information was included in the imported crystallographic information file (CIF): a = 10.6 Å, b = 9.8 Å, c = 12.5 Å; α = 90°, β = 113°, and γ = 90°. The atom-based method was used to calculate van der Waals forces with ultrafine accuracy, and the Ewald summation method was used to calculate electrostatic interactions. The attachment energy (AE) was used to predict the shape of crystal growth. A vacuum slab model was constructed to investigate equilibrium crystal habit, growth rates, and surface interaction features based on Miller indices and face-specific surface areas34.

The Blends tool in Materials Studio was used to evaluate celecoxib compatibility with selected polymers. The Forcite module with the COMPASS force field was used to construct the geometry of the 3D structures of celecoxib and polymer monomers (HP-βCD, HPMC, PVP, and starch). Using a 1:1 molar ratio, binary drug–polymer systems were built at 298 K with periodic boundary conditions under the NVT ensemble. Approximately 10,000 equilibration and 20,000–50,000 manufacturing steps were included in Monte Carlo simulations. Interaction parameter (χ), average binding energy (Ebs avg), and mixing energy (Emix) were the thermodynamic parameters that were computed to assess the compatibility of celecoxib with each selected polymer35.

These physical mixtures for preliminary screening of solubility and recrystallization inhibition. At this stage, small-scale mixing was sufficient to generate uniform physical mixtures and evaluate feasibility prior to scale-up.

Lyophilized solid dispersion (SDs)

Lyophilized solid dispersions (SDs) were prepared using a 1:1 (w/w) ratio of celecoxib and polymer as shown in Table 2. Accurately weighed amounts of each polymer (HP-βCD, HPMC, PVP, and starch) were dissolved in 20 mL of distilled water. An equivalent amount of celecoxib was then dispersed into the polymer solution. The mixtures were stirred at 500 rpm for 30 min and maintained at 30 °C for four hours. Afterwards, the dispersions were lyophilized to obtain the solid dispersions. The resulting products were labeled as SD1, SD2, SD3, and SD4, corresponding to celecoxib combined with HP-βCD, HPMC, PVP, and starch, respectively. The lyophilized powders were stored in a desiccator for further analysis37. Resultant solid dispersions were evaluated using solubility studies, FTIR spectroscopy, microscopy and in vitro dissolution and release kinetics profile.

Tablets from solid dispersions

Celecoxib (CLX) tablets were prepared by direct compression of the lyophilized solid dispersions (SDs) using a single-punch tablet press. The resulting tablets were designated as T1, T2, T3, and T4, corresponding to formulations containing SD1, SD2, SD3, and SD4, respectively. Each tablet had an approximate weight of 500 mg and contained 100 mg of celecoxib (CLX) as the active ingredient. The remaining composition consisted of maize starch (26% w/w), croscarmellose sodium (5% w/w), lactose (61% w/w), colloidal silicon dioxide (1% w/w), sodium starch glycolate (6% w/w), and magnesium stearate (0.2% w/w). The blended powder was compressed using a single-punch machine under a compression force of 7–8 kg/cm2. These tablets were characterized by hardness, diameter and thickness and in vitro dissolution and release kinetics38.

Characterization of physical mixtures

Solubility studies

Solubility studies were carried out using the shake flask method, to assess the effect of polymers on the solubilization of celecoxib. Each sample of physical mixtures was accurately weighed 10 mg of each sample and added to 10 mL of distilled water in Falcon tubes. These tubes were agitated to achieve through mixing and then placed in a water bath, which was set to a constant temperature at 37 °C. The aliquots were withdrawn, filtered and analyzed using UV-spectrophotometry at 254 nm39.

Nuclear induction time

An amount equivalent to 2 mg/mL of celecoxib from each physical mixture was added to 10 mL of methanol (with or without polymer) in a 15 mL test tube, equilibrated at 37 °C. The time from addition to the onset of turbidity was recorded as the induction time, indicating nucleation initiation34.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectra were used to analyze the characteristic functional group peaks of pure celecoxib, polymers (HP-βCD, HPMC, PVP, and starch), physical mixtures, and solid dispersions, and to detect any spectral changes. Small sample amounts were pressed onto the holder, and spectra were recorded after exposure to infrared radiation20.

Morphology study

The Zoom Stereo Microscope with a digital camera was used to analyze the surface morphology of the lyophilized solid dispersions and the raw drug. Samples were put on sanitized glass slides, and pictures were taken in ideal lighting circumstances at different magnifications40. Furthermore, the pure drug and SD1 morphology was analyzed using scanning electron microscope (SEM) (EVO LS 10, Zeiss Germany) by placing small amount of the sample on a clean metal stub, which was then mounted in the SEM chamber and voltage was adjusted at 1500 kV. The magnification was set between 50x and 1,000x, and high-resolution images were captured best resolution image for detailed analysis41.

SDs dissolution testing

The dissolution behavior of pure celecoxib (CLX) and lyophilized solid dispersions (SD1–SD4) was assessed using the powder dissolution method. Samples equivalent to 100 mg of CLX were dispersed in 100 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 6.8), maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C, and stirred with a magnetic stirrer at 100 rpm. For a 100 mg dose of celecoxib (solubility ≈ 4–7 µg/mL), true sink conditions would require volumes > 14 L, which is impractical. Therefore, 100 mL of phosphate buffer was selected as a biorelevant compromise consistent with literature and USP/EMA guidance, while the presence of hydrophilic carriers (HP-βCD, HPMC, PVP) enhanced apparent solubility, bringing the system closer to sink conditions. At predetermined intervals (5, 10, 15, 30, 45, 60, 120, and 180 min), 1 mL aliquots were withdrawn, filtered, and analyzed spectrophotometrically at 254 nm. Dissolution profiles were further evaluated using DD-Solver software34,40.

Characterization of tablets

Physical testing of tablets

Tablets were measured for thickness, diameter, and hardness in order to assess mechanical strength and uniformity. The hardness was measured using a digital tester, and the results of three different tests were expressed in kg/cm2. The dimensions were measured using a digital Vernier caliper, and the average values were assessed in accordance with USP Pharmacopoeial standards42.

Tablet dissolution testing

In vitro drug release from compressed CLX tablets (T1–T4) was assessed using USP Apparatus I (Basket). The basket was rotated at the designated speed while the study was carried out at 37 ± 0.5 °C in phosphate buffer (pH 6.8). To ascertain the celecoxib release profile, samples were taken out at regular intervals up to 3 h, filtered, and examined with a UV-Vis spectrophotometer at 254 nm and celecoxib release behavior was evaluated using DD-solver42.

Results and discussions

Computational predictions

Simulated morphology analysis

The simulated crystal morphology of celecoxib showed a significant differences in attachment energies (Eatt) across various crystallographic faces, indicating diverse growth behaviors and contributions to the final crystal habit. The (1 0 0) face exhibited the least negative attachment energy of -8.31 kcal/mol, signifying the slowest-growing face and hence the most dominant in the crystal morphology, covering approximately 65.68% of the total facet area. The intermolecular interactions in this plane were primarily driven by van der Waals forces, with minimal contribution from electrostatic interactions and no hydrogen bonding observed. In contrast, the (0 1 1) face showed a significantly more negative Eatt of -32.71 kcal/mol, indicating a faster-growing surface, contributed 32.47% to the total crystal surface area as shown in Table 3. This suggests that while it grows more rapidly than (1 0 0), it remains moderately exposed due to balanced growth kinetics. Electrostatic interactions played a major role in stabilizing this face, accounting for over 40% of the total interaction energy. Other high-energy faces such as (1 1 −1), (1 1 0), and especially (1 0–2) as shown in Fig. 1, with Eatt values of -33.74, -35.36, and − 59.88 kcal/mol respectively, demonstrated extremely fast growth potential. These faces appeared minimally or were not prominently expressed in the final morphology. The rapid growth rates lead to their diminished presence in the external shape of the crystal.

Overall, van der Waals forces were the primary contributors to the crystal packing in all faces, with no significant hydrogen bonding observed. The dominance of the (1 0 0) face suggests a plate-like morphology for celecoxib, which can influence its dissolution behavior and potential interaction with excipients in solid dosage formulations. This morphology may enhance or hinder wetting and surface dissolution, affect agglomeration tendencies, and alter contact angles with hydrophilic or hydrophobic excipients, thereby impacting drug-excipient interactions and overall tablet performance. According to this study, plate-shaped APIs typically have a large surface area and particular shape traits that affect downstream features including compaction, flowability, and dissolution43. This study emphasizes the crucial role that surface anisotropy and crystal structure play an important role in controlling wettability, dissolution, and drug–excipient interactions through surface functionalization and morphological tailoring44.

Using the Chi (χ) values at 298 K, the kind of solid dispersion system can be identified. A solid suspension and phase separation are reflected in higher χ values, while improved drug-polymer miscibility and the formation of a solid solution are frequently indicated by lower χ values. For CLX with HPMC, Starch, PVP, and HP-βCD, the corresponding χ values were 2.490945, 6.418598, 5.727446, and − 5.86401, respectively, as shown in Table 4. For CLX with HP-βCD, a negative χ value (-5.86401) denotes remarkable miscibility and the potential for solid solution formation as shown in Table 4.

By in silico drug–polymer compatibility analysis, the interaction parameter (χ), mixing energy (Emix) and average binding energies (Ebs) provided insight into the thermodynamic stability and miscibility of each binary system. Among the tested polymers, HP-βCD showed the most favorable interaction with celecoxib, exhibiting a highly negative χ value of -5.86 and Emix of -3.47 kcal/mol, indicating excellent miscibility and strong thermodynamic compatibility. Additionally, the average binding energy between celecoxib and HP-βCD (Ebs = -8.71 kcal/mol) was the lowest among all polymers, suggesting a strong drug–polymer interaction that is ideal for maintaining the drug in its amorphous form. HPMC demonstrated moderate compatibility with celecoxib, with a χ value of 2.49 and Emix of 1.48 kcal/mol. Although the drug–polymer interaction (Ebs = -6.40 kcal/mol) was fairly strong, the positive χ and Emix values indicated limited miscibility. Starch and PVP, on the other hand, showed poor compatibility with celecoxib. PVP exhibited a χ value of 5.73 and Emix of 3.39 kcal/mol, along with a weak Ebs of -2.33 kcal/mol, suggesting weaker drug–polymer interaction. Starch displayed the highest χ value (6.42) and mixing energy (3.80 kcal/mol), indicating poor thermodynamic favorability despite a relatively stronger Ebs of -6.93 kcal/mol.

Overall, the compatibility ranking based on interaction parameter, binding energy, and mixing energy is: HP-βCD > HPMC > Starch > PVP. These findings indicated the selection of HP-βCD as the most promising polymeric carrier for celecoxib in solid dispersion formulations. These findings align with the work of Katja et al., who demonstrated that the Flory–Huggins interaction parameter (χ) is a reliable predictor of phase separation behavior in co-amorphous systems. Their predictions were substantiated through experimental techniques such as differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), confirming the utility of χ as a robust tool for assessing drug–polymer miscibility and formulation stability45. Similarly, the study on roxadustat highlights that polymers inhibit the recrystallization and maintain its supersaturation in the solution moreover, simulations conducted using Material Studio revealed a strong positive correlation between the compatibility and binding energy of polymers with the drug roxadustat (RST), and their overall effectiveness in formulation34.

Experimental analysis

Physical mixtures

Physical mixtures (PMs) were formulated and characterized through solubility studies to evaluate the effect of the polymers on drug solubilization, and nuclear induction time to determine the inhibitory effect of polymer on recrystallization of celecoxib.

Solubility analysis of physical mixtures (PMs)

The solubility of pure celecoxib (CLX) in water was found to be 4.2 µg/mL. All physical mixtures (PM1–PM4) showed improved solubility compared to pure CLX. PM1 exhibited the highest solubility at 64.18 µg/mL, followed by PM2 (54.18 µg/mL), PM3 (40.55 µg/mL), and PM4 (36.91 µg/mL) as shown in Fig. 2.

Comparative analysis of intrinsic solubility of celecoxib in pure form and with its physical mixtures using shake flask method.

The significant improvement observed with HP-βCD can be attributed to its ability to form inclusion complexes with lipophilic drugs, enhance wettability, and increase aqueous solubility through host-guest interactions46, HPMC and PVP are hydrophilic polymers that reduce interfacial tension and improve surface wettability to prevent crystallization and improve drug dispersion. Starch, though less effective, contributes via partial solubilization and matrix formation but lacks the strong inclusion or hydrogen bonding potential seen in the other polymers47.

Nuclear induction time

The induction time reflects the duration a drug remains in solution before the onset of precipitation; a longer induction time suggests improved solubility and prolonged supersaturation. Pure CLX exhibited the shortest nucleation time (45 s), indicating rapid crystallization due to its poor aqueous solubility and limited stability in solution. In CLX-polymers mixture, the nucleation time increased, indicating a delay in crystallization. At the 1:1 ratio, PM1 demonstrated the longest nucleation time (140 s), followed by PM2 (135 s), PM3 (120 s), and PM4 (90 s) as shown in Table 5. These findings indicate that the physical mixtures, particularly PM1, effectively prolonged the nucleation time and improved the supersaturation of drug in the aqueous solution.

Solid dispersion formulations can be used to increase the solubility of drugs that are poorly soluble in water. Baicalein solid dispersion with Pluronic F68 using spray-freeze drying resulted a 2.3-fold rise in bioavailability as compared to pure drug22. In another study, leflunomide solid dispersions prepared by kneading, grinding, and freeze-drying with PVP, PEG, and HPMC showed better solubility with PVP due to amorphization and highlighted favorable drug-polymer interactions by formulation characterization. M. Wang et al. demonstrated that solid dispersion of Roxadustat with HPMC, PVP, and PVPVA increased solubility as compared to its pure form34. These studies supported that solid dispersions significantly increase drugs solubility and bioavailability.

The reported reduction in crystallinity of the solid dispersion formulations is consistent with previous studies. Nguyen et al. demonstrated that curcumin and HP-βCD formed an inclusion complex through hydrogen bonding, which reduced crystallinity51. Similarly, the presence of characteristic FTIR peaks that match celecoxib in the present study, including NH stretching (~ 3300 cm−1), aromatic ring vibrations, and sulfonamide peaks (~ 1150–1350 cm−1), suggests that the drug was effectively dispersed throughout the HPMC matrix20. These spectrum changes are consistent with the crystalline structure of celecoxib breaking down. According to Pandya et al. (2009), celecoxib also decreased crystallinity through the formation of hydrogen-bonded inclusion complexes with PVP-K3020.

Morphology study

All solid dispersions (SD1–SD4) and pure celecoxib were initially examined using zoom stereomicroscopy to provide a non-destructive comparative visualization of surface morphology. These images clearly demonstrated morphological differences among the formulations in Figure S3. For higher-resolution analysis, SEM was selectively performed on the raw drug and the best-performing solid dispersion (SD1), chosen on the basis of solubility and dissolution data. SEM of pure celecoxib revealed a highly organized crystalline structure with well-defined rod-like particles Fig. 5, whereas SD1 displayed irregularly shaped particles with loss of distinct crystalline boundaries Fig. 6. This morphology analysis highlighted the transformation of celecoxib into a more uniformly dispersed, amorphous form within the polymeric matrix. In the solid dispersion system, molecular-level dispersion is supported by the absence of crystalline features, which indicates that celecoxib was successfully transformed into an amorphous state similar to Mukesh et al., proposed the similar results that amorphous salt solid dispersion of celecoxib enhanced its solubility and dissolution rate52.

Solid dispersion dissolution testing

The dissolution data demonstrate increased solubility and dissolution rate of celecoxib (CLX) when formulated as solid dispersions (SD1–SD4), compared to pure CLX as shown in Fig. 7. Pure CLX showed minimal dissolution over 3 h, reaching only 21.87% at the end, indicating its poor aqueous solubility. All solid dispersions exhibited significantly improved drug release. SD1 (CLX-HP-βCD) showed a steady increase in dissolution, reaching 78.51% at 180 min, the highest among all formulations. SD2 (CLX-HPMC) and SD3 (CLX-PVP) also displayed marked improvements, reaching 73.51% and 66.42%, respectively. These results suggest that hydrophilic polymers like HP-βCD, HPMC, and PVP enhanced drug wetting and solubilization, likely due to molecular dispersion and amorphization. SD4 (CLX-starch), while better than pure CLX, demonstrated the lowest enhancement among solid dispersions (50.33%), indicating less efficient solubilizing effect. The dissolution profiles support that solid dispersion, particularly with HP-βCD, is a promising strategy for improving dissolution rate of pure celecoxib supported by the previous studies7,20,50,51.

Tablet dissolution testing

The dissolution behavior of celecoxib from tablet formulations T1 to T4 was evaluated over 3 h, as represented in Fig. 8. The results revealed marked differences in drug release rates among the formulations, reflecting the influence of the polymer used. T1 showed a gradual but consistent increase in drug release, reaching 86.44% in 1 h and almost complete release (99.88%) in 3 h. This indicates a rapid and efficient release profile, likely due to enhanced solubility and optimized matrix composition. T2 also exhibited a high release profile, achieving 96.48% release at 120 min and reaching a plateau at 96.74% by 3 h. Although slightly slower than T1 in early time points, T2 demonstrated sustained release with excellent overall efficiency. T3 displayed a moderate release rate, reaching 90.41% in 2 h minutes and 91.07% at 3 h. The initial release (e.g., 16.3% at 15 min) was slower than T1 and T2, suggesting a more delayed onset but still acceptable overall performance. T4 had the slowest release, with only 4.15% at 15 min, gradually increasing to 83.99% in 3 h. The lower early-phase release indicates a more compact matrix and possibly slower erosion or wetting rate, which prolonged drug dissolution.

The comprehensive evaluation of drug release kinetics revealed that solid dispersion formulations significantly improved the solubility and dissolution behavior of celecoxib compared to the pure drug. Among all formulations, the HP-β-CD-based solid dispersion (SD1) demonstrated highest rate constants and best model fit in both first-order and Hixson–Crowell models. This indicates a concentration-dependent and erosion-influenced release pattern. Additionally, strong correlation with the Korsmeyer–Peppas and Higuchi models suggested that the drug release was governed by a combination of diffusion and matrix relaxation mechanisms. When compressed into tablet form, the HP-β-CD formulation retained its enhanced dissolution profile, further confirming its potential to overcome the poor aqueous solubility of celecoxib. These findings support the use of HP-β-CD as an effective carrier in solid dispersion systems for developing improved oral delivery of celecoxib.

This comparative assessment provides insight into how different formulation strategies influence the solubility and release characteristics of celecoxib, a drug known for its poor aqueous solubility. At the 15-minute interval (Q15), CLX demonstrated very limited release (4.87%), consistent with its poor aqueous solubility. In contrast, solid dispersion formulations exhibited significant improvements, with SD1, SD2, and SD3 releasing 12–14% of drug content. This corresponded to 2.5–2.9-fold higher RDR values relative to CLX, indicating enhanced wettability and dispersion in hydrophilic carriers. The tablet formulations displayed strikingly higher early release compared to both CLX and solid dispersions. T1 released 25.42% (RDR_15 = 77.67), while T2 (20.35%, RDR_15 = 62.18) and T3 (16.3%, RDR_15 = 49.81) also showed remarkable improvements. Even T4 (4.15%, RDR_15 = 12.68) showed better relative dissolution rate.

These findings are in line with reports on ibuprofen–HP-β-CD inclusion complexes prepared by solvent-free hot melt extrusion, where ternary complexes achieved ~ 85% release within 30 min in both acidic and neutral media, supported by amorphous conversion and improved wettability32. Both studies highlight that polymer-assisted cyclodextrin complexation and dispersion-based strategies significantly improve dissolution performance of BCS class II drugs, with HME providing a green, scalable alternative to conventional methods. Together, this supports the critical role of formulation design—whether via solid dispersions, tablets, or CD-based extrusion complexes—in overcoming solubility barriers and ensuring bioavailability.

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrated that lyophilized solid dispersions, guided by computational prediction and experimentally validated has significantly enhanced the solubility and dissolution of celecoxib (CLX). Molecular dynamics simulations and simulated morphology not only revealed plate-like crystal structure of drug, which contributes to poor dissolution, but also identified hydroxypropyl β-cyclodextrin (HP-βCD) as the most compatible polymer for solid dispersion formulation. Experimental validation confirmed that SD1 (CLX–HP-βCD) achieved a 150-fold increase in solubility and superior drug release in both solid dispersions and tablet form. FTIR, microscopy, and dissolution kinetics. These findings strongly support the use of computational tools in formulation design and establish HP-βCD-based lyophilized solid dispersions as a promising system for improving CLX oral bioavailability.
