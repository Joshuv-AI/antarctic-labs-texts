Nurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients: a hybrid concept analysis
Authors: Meng Yue, Chunjuan Yao, Qi Zhang, Dengfen Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients plays a crucial role in determining the effectiveness of these interactions and is central to the nurse-patient relationship. However, a standardized definition of nurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients is required. This study aims to theoretically define nurses' attitudes towards communication with patients, providing a theoretical foundation for developing measurement tools and nurse-patient communication training programs.MethodsA mixed method design was applied in the concept analysis to explore the meaning of nurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients in clinical settings. The analysis followed the three-phase procedure of the hybrid concept analysis approach. In the theoretical phase, systematic Literature searches were performed across Chinese and English databases, and the included Literature was reviewed to identify the attributes, antecedents, and consequences of the concept. For the fieldwork phase, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 18 nurses, and the data were analyzed using content analysis with Nvivo software. During the final analytic phase, the results of the two previous phases were integrated. The reporting of qualitative findings was guided by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist.ResultsFive conceptual attributes were identified: based on professional values and norms, emotional responses and evaluations, expression through language and expression, polarity, and predictive of communicative behaviors. Nurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients are influenced by factors related to the nurses themselves, their work environment, and the patients. It impacts patients' psychological status and health outcomes, nursing service quality, nurses' professional fulfillment, career advancement and personal well-being, while also influencing the evolution of nurse-patient relationships.ConclusionNurses' attitudes towards communication with patients exhibit a three-dimensional attribute characteristic. These attitudes are influenced by factors related to nurses themselves, patients, and the environment, while simultaneously impacting patients, nurses, nursing practice, and nurse-patient relationships. The findings of this study provide a theoretical basis for the development of measurement tools assessing nurses' attitudes towards communication with patients, as well as guidance for nurse-patient communication training.Keywords: Communication, Attitude, Nurses, Patients, Concept analysis

Background

Communication constitutes a behavioral process mediated by specific carriers, through which individuals exchange information, shape perceptions, and achieve shared objectives [1]. In clinical settings, nurse-patient communication represents a specialized form of this process, involving dynamic exchange of information and interaction between nursing professionals and patients. Its scope covers not only illness and rehabilitation-related information but also the emotions, concerns, and expectations from both nurses and patients [2]. As an integral component of inpatient medical care, nurse-patient communication serves as a fundamental element of quality nursing practice and lies at the heart of patient-centered care [3–5].

Nurses' attitudes towards communication with patients reflect their humanistic values of respecting patients and their commitment to patient-centered service [6]. Several studies have explored nurses' views and understanding of nurse-patient communication. In a semi-structured interview study conducted in Belgium, nurses emphasized the significance of communication in providing quality palliative care and highlighted the significant role of attitude in effective communication [7]. A qualitative interview study [8] conducted in Botswana with 20 ICU nurses showed that some nurses may have a negative attitude towards nurse-patient communication in the intensive care unit (ICU), but encouragement and motivation from fellow nurses and nurse managers can improve this situation.

Effective nurse-patient communication is significantly enhanced when nurses demonstrate positive, open attitudes characterized by respect and empathy, thereby facilitating stronger therapeutic alliances and more productive information exchange [9]. On the contrary, if the nurse's attitude is negative, closed or disrespectful towards the patient, it may hinder effective communication and may lead to misunderstanding and conflict [6]. Therefore, a positive nurse-patient communication attitude is an important prerequisite for establishing effective communication.

Concept analysis provides a theoretical basis for the development of measurement tools by operationalizing conceptual dimensions and attributes into the tool's dimensions and items. However, there is no unified definition of the concept, which limits in-depth exploration of nurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients.

Peng [10] believes that the service attitude of healthcare professionalsis a comprehensive reflection of their professional cognition, emotions, and behavioral intentions toward patients. This definition aligns with the Tripartite model of Attitudes, proposed by Eagly and Chaiken [11], which posits that attitudes are composed of three dimensions: cognitive, affective, and behavioral tendency. The cognitive aspect pertains to the beliefs and knowledge related to the object of the attitude and approaches toward it. The affective element deals with emotional responses triggered by the attitude object, encompassing emotional experiences and expressions in particular situations or in response to specific stimuli. The behavioral tendency component involves the individual's behavioral intentions or tendencies toward the attitude object, reflecting potential actions the individual may undertake.

Huang [12] defines the attitudes in nursing service as “the demeanor nurses exhibit toward patients through language and behavior during nursing activities”. While this interpretation captures observable manifestations, it narrowly confines the construct to superficial behavioral displays. Feng [13] noted that healthcare professionals' attitudes towards patients encompass a tripartite framework of cognitive evaluations, emotional responses, and behavioral tendencies during clinical interactions. Nonetheless, existing studies [11–13] have predominantly conceptualized nurses' attitudes within general medical service contexts, neglecting the specific domain of nurse-patient communication attitudes. Giménez-Espert [14] adopted the Tripartite model of Attitudes [11] to characterize nurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients across cognitive, affective, and behavioral dimensions. However, the study failed to utilize standardized conceptual analysis methods to formulate the concept, merely presenting its three constituent dimensions without rigorous conceptual development.

To address this gap, we employed a concept analysis approach to delineate the conceptual boundaries and attributes of nurses' attitudes towards communication with patients, thereby providing a theoretical basis for developing validated assessment tools. Furthermore, this analysis aims to establish foundational knowledge for future research on the formation mechanisms and intervention strategies targeting nurses' attitudes towards communication with patients. Such efforts are critical for improving communication efficacy, operationalizing humanistic care principles, and elevating overall nursing service quality.

Methods

The questions underpinning the concept analysis were: What constitutes nurses' attitudes towards communication with patients? What attributes define nurses' attitudes towards communication with patients? What are the antecedents and consequences of nurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients?

Concepts represent the rational cognition of objective phenomena, including their essential attributes and fundamental nature [15]. Concept analysis serves as a methodological approach to elucidate ambiguous disciplinary constructs, so as to achieve conceptual clarity. This process holds significant implications for theoretical development, research advancement, and the enhancement of nursing practice standards [16]. The hybrid concept analysis method, initially proposed by Schwartz-Barcott and Kim [17] with subsequent refinements in 2000 [18], combines traditional literature analysis with empirical field research techniques including interviews and observational studies. The fieldwork phase enables in-depth investigation of conceptual manifestations within practical contexts. In contrast to classical and evolutionary concept analysis methodologies, the hybrid approach synthesizes theoretical review with empirical data collection, yielding a more robust and comprehensive conceptual framework [17, 18]. Theoretical phase

In the theoretical phase, we reviewed the literature on nurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients. The English databases ScienceDirect, PubMed, CINAHL, ProQuest, Scopus, and Web of Science, and the Chinese databases Wanfang,VIP and CNKI databases were searched. The literature search covered the period from the databases’ inception to February 2024. A medical reference librarian assisted in formulating the search terms by combining subject terms with keywords. We systematically searched for “nurse attitudes” and “nurse-patient communication” along with their respective synonyms, and subsequently performed a combined search. Keyword searches were limited to title and abstract for the search terms included “nurse patient communication”, “communication with patients”, “communication with the patient”, “interpersonal communication”, “nurses’ attitude”, “attitude”, “nurses’ attitudes”, “nurses’ opinion”, “nurses’ opinions”, “nurses’ sentiment” and “nurses’ sentiments”. Boolean operators of “OR” and “AND” were applied to the search terms.

Studies were included if they focused on nurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients and addressed the conceptual attributes, antecedents, or consequences. Studies were eligible if they were published in Chinese or English and employed qualitative, quantitative, mixed-method, review, or instrument development designs. The exclusion criteria were duplicate entries or inaccessible full-text articles. EndNote software was used to manage the search literature.

The first and second authors independently conducted preliminary screening of titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies. Subsequently, the second and third authors performed full-text evaluations to determine final study inclusion. During the literature review process, textual segments explicitly describing the essential characteristics of nurses' attitudes towards communication with patients (e.g., "manifested as…", "its features include…") were extracted. Guided by the three dimensions of the Tripartite Model of Attitudes [11], the researchers classified the extracted materials. A table was created to present both the characteristics of included studies and extracted data, encompassing conceptual attributes, antecedents, and consequences. Furthermore, the researchers extracted and documented attributions, antecedents and consequences using a pre-designed table. Any discrepancies during the screening process were resolved through consensus-based discussions with the corresponding author.

Fieldwork phase

To ensure methodological rigor, meticulous attention must be given to both the environmental context and participant characteristics that are conceptually relevant during the selection process [18]. This study employed a combination of purposive sampling and snowballing method to recruit nurses from two hospitals in Beijing and Tianjin, China. Following official approval, potential participants were recruited through poster advertisements displayed within the hospital premises, which outlined the study’s objectives, significance, and methodology. We employed maximum variation sampling to ensure diversity among interviewees in terms of gender, age, years of experience, educational background, and working department, thereby obtaining more comprehensive interview data. The inclusion criteria were registered nurses with at least one year of work experience. The exclusion criteria included nurses in training or internship positions and non-frontline nurses working full-time in teaching, research, or management roles. Prior to obtaining informed consent, researchers reiterated the study’s purpose, scientific rationale, and methodological approach to all participants, while emphasizing the principles of voluntariness and confidentiality.

The sample size was determined based on the principle of coding saturation, which was considered reached when no new codes were identified [19]. The interview guide was developed based on the five-step process proposed by Kallio [20], which includes: (1) determining the research question; (2) searching for and utilizing existing knowledge; (3) drafting a preliminary interview guide; (4) pilot testing the guide; (5) finalizing the formal interview guide. The initial drafted interview outline was pilot tested. Five nurses were selected for field testing to assess the comprehensibility and practicality of the questions, ensuring that the questions could elicit the respondents' genuine experiences and perspectives.

In the formal interview phase, data collection occurred between September 1 and October 31, 2024. Following interviews with 15 nurses, coding saturation was attained. To confirm saturation, three additional participants were interviewed, with no new codes identified, thereby definitively establishing data saturation.

The interview location was selected in a relatively quiet place such as the duty room or conference room, and each interview was conducted within 30 to 60 min. With the permission of the interviewees, all interviews were recorded. The interview was conducted by the first author who had received qualitative research training. The interview outline included the following questions: (1) What do you believe typifies nurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients, (2) How would you describe a nurse’s positive attitude towards communication with patients, (3) Which personal experiences or external factors have shaped your views on nurse-patient communication, (4) In your opinion, what effects does a nurse’s communication attitude have, (5) Could you recall a particular nurse-patient communication you’ve had? What emotions or impressions did you take away from that experience?

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee on Scientific Research of XXX Hospital (NO.2024XXXXXXXX). All participants were asked to sign a written consent form before the interview, and the interviewees had the right to withdraw from the study at any time. Content analysis [21] was used to extract the attributes, influencing factors and results of nurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients. The transcribed text was coded, classified and analyzed using Nvivo 12.0 software. Two researchers who had received qualitative research methodology training independently coded the textual data. To ensure consistency, a random sample of 20 transcript pages was coded by both researchers, and inter-rater reliability was assessed using category agreement (CA) [22]. The CA values ranged between 0.74 and 0.95, well within the acceptable threshold.

The researchers maintained reflective journals and analytical memos throughout the study. These methods were systematically employed to critically examine their positionality and its potential influence on the results. This reflexive approach fostered an open-minded analytical stance while mitigating biases stemming from personal assumptions, prior experiences, or preconceived notions that could affect the findings [23]. To enhance the credibility of the results, member checking was conducted by returning the synthesized findings to participants for verification.

Final analytical phase

In the final analytical phase, the researchers integrated insights from both the theoretical phase and fieldwork phase through a systematic comparison of conceptual attributes, antecedents, and consequences across the two datasets. This comparative approach produced a nuanced and comprehensive definition, which not only aligns with existing literature but also authentically incorporates the perspectives of interview participants.

Results

Theoretical phase of hybrid concept analysis

Literature search results

A total of 2149 articles were initially screened. After excluding duplicate articles and reading the title abstracts, 102 articles remained. After reading the full text, 36 articles were included, including 26 English articles. Concept attributes in the theoretical phase

No clear or consistent definition of nurses' attitude towards nurse-patient communication was identified in the included literature. The analysis of included studies revealed three key attributes that characterize nurses' attitudes towards communication with patients: (1) Rooted in professional values and norms (cognitive dimension); (2) Shaped by emotional responses and evaluations (affective dimension); (3) Predictive of communicative behaviors (behavioral dimension). These attributes align with the Tripartite Model of Attitudes [11], which comprise cognitive, affective, and behavioral tendency components.

Antecedents in the theoretical phase

Nurse-related factors

The theoretical analysis reveals that nurses' attitudes towards nurse-patient communication are shaped by various intrinsic factors, which can be categorized into sociodemographic characteristics and psychological dimensions. Regarding sociodemographic influences, research indicates that a nurse's gender, age, educational background, economic status, and professional specialization significantly predict their communication attitudes [24–28].

Psychological factors exert equally important influences [29–33]. Nurses' emotional states, particularly when characterized by positive affect and adaptive functioning, correlate with more constructive communication attitudes [29]. The emotional climate within the work environment, including the affect displayed by colleagues and nurse managers, further modulates these attitudes [30]. Findings from an investigative study suggests that empathy serves as a more robust predictor of communication attitudes than transient emotional states alone [31]. Research by Radtke [33] documented that nurses frequently exhibit communication avoidance with patients suffering from stigmatized conditions such as HIV/AIDS or diabetic foot gangrene, primarily due to safety concerns or personal discomfort; Meanwhile, some nurses employ communication avoidance strategies as a coping mechanism to regulate emotional investment, which may result in perceived clinical detachment. Similar avoidance behaviors have been observed towards patients from disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds, revealing underlying disease-related and status-based biases that influence communication dynamics [32].

Patient-related factors

Research findings indicate that patient characteristics significantly shape nurses' communication approaches. Specifically, patients' educational attainment and health literacy levels were identified as key external determinants [32, 34]. Nurses demonstrated more composed and engaged communication styles when interacting with patients possessing stronger educational backgrounds or greater health literacy; Meanwhile, patient's health status also influenced communication intentions: nurses adopted notably more cautious and measured communication approaches when dealing with patients presenting serious health conditions [34]. Furthermore, social stratification effects were evident in nurse-patient interactions. Empirical observations revealed that nurses consistently exhibited more positive and proactive communication attitudes when engaging with patients occupying higher socioeconomic positions [32].

Social/organizational factors

Fristly, nurses' professional status within society exerts considerable influence [35]. Studies [9, 36] consistently report that in regions where nursing enjoys higher social standing, practitioners demonstrate more positive and patient-centered communication approaches. Cross-cultural dynamics also emerge as significant determinants. Researches [35, 36] documented that language barriers and cultural differences, particularly when caring for non-local patients, those with differing religious backgrounds, or non-native language speakers, substantially alter nurses' attitudes towards communication with patients. Secondly, workload factors prove particularly consequential. The reviewed literatures [9, 34, 37, 38] identified nursing workload as the most decisive organizational factor, with excessive patient loads, staffing shortages, mandatory overtime, and extended shifts all contributing to demonstrably poorer communication attitudes among nursing staff. Hospital environments—including administrative systems and workplace culture—show statistically significant correlations with communication quality [39]. Organizations implementing rational policies and fostering collaborative atmospheres cultivate more constructive attitudes [39]. Finally, structured communication training initiatives yield measurable improvements [32]. Healthcare institutions that implement evidence-based communication programs observe marked enhancements in their staff's communicative attitudes [32].

Consequences in the theoretical phase

Impact on patients

Studies have shown that a positive communication attitude significantly alleviates patients' anxiety and stress [9]. Particularly in critical care settings, patients consistently identify nurses' communication attitudes as pivotal to their perceived safety [40]. Furthermore, when nurses exhibit genuine caring and compassion, patients report enhanced feelings of personal dignity and worth [41].

Impact on nurses and nursing work

The quality of nurses' communicative approaches bears substantial professional consequences. A qualitative evaluation of communication training programs revealed that attitude fundamentally shapes end-of-life care delivery [8]. Additional studies [42, 43] establish that communication attitudes directly manifest in corresponding behaviors, ultimately determining both care quality and measurable patient health outcomes.

Impact on nurse-patient relationship

Communication attitudes critically influence therapeutic relationships in three key dimensions. First, need responsiveness. Positive attitudes correlate with heightened sensitivity to patient concerns and more effective need fulfillment, yielding superior satisfaction rates [36, 38, 44]. Second, shared understanding. The attitude foundation determines whether nurses and patients achieve mutual comprehension of medical conditions [45]. Third, therapeutic efficacy. Appropriate communication attitudes foster productive clinical dialogues, strengthening the therapeutic communication [32, 46].

Feasible definition of the theoretical phase

Nurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients refers to subjective assessment and position that nurses hold regarding nurse-patient communication. Shaped by nursing professional values and norms, these attitudes mirror the emotional experiences, reactions, and compassionate approaches of nurses, ultimately serving as indicators for their communication practices.

Fieldwork phase of hybird concept analysis

In this phase, participants comprised 13 women and 5 men, with ages ranging from 25 to 50 years. Among them, 15 participants (83.33%) held a bachelor’s degree, while three had obtained postgraduate qualifications. The participants represented diverse clinical departments, including internal medicine, psychiatry, surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, and pediatrics.

As a result of analyzing the collected interview data, we identified three attributes: expression through language and actions, polarity, and behavioral tendency. The antecedents were categorized into three domains: organizational environment factors, patient-related factors and nurse-related factors. Regarding consequences, four primary impacts were identified: psychological effects on patients, implications for patient safety and prognosis, effects on nurses themselves, and impact on nurse-patient relationship. In summary, we defined nurses' attitude towards communication with patients as their positive or negative evaluations and responses toward patients and their needs, rooted in personal values and social norms, manifested through verbal and nonverbal expressions, and predictive of their actual communication behaviors. Nurses' attitudes towards communication with patients is influenced by nurse-related factors, patient-related factors, and workplace environments. Simultaneously, NACO carries a ripple effect, impacting the nurses themselves, patients, nurse-patient relationships, and nursing care.

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrate that environmental factors—including nursing workload, workplace conditions, and communication training—significantly influence nurses' communication attitudes towards patients. Pérez-Francisco’s study [47] indicated that excessive workload contributes to occupational burnout, manifesting as physical exhaustion (e.g., headaches) and psychological strain (e.g., irritability), which collectively impair nurses' capacity to maintain constructive communication. These observations align with Astuti's [48] research demonstrating that workplace environments critically impact nurses' professional engagement, where supportive settings enhance work commitment—a conclusion corroborated by this study.

Existing literature [49, 50] substantiates that communication training programs yield multifaceted benefits: they not only improve communicative competence and self-efficacy but also cultivate positive emotional states and mitigate psychological burdens. Our analysis further identifies patient-related factors—particularly variations in educational background, information needs, and comprehension abilities—as additional determinants of nurses' communication approaches, consistent with prior research [51].

Our findings indicate that nurses' communication attitudes are influenced by their personality traits, emotions, moral ethics, empathy, professional identity, and professional knowledge. Prior research [52] had demonstrated that certain personality traits are associated with a more open and mature demeanor, linked to high emotional stability and enhanced mental well-being. Individuals with such traits tend to possess positive coping strategies for managing work-related stress and exhibit favorable work attitudes [52], aligning with the conclusions drawn from this study.

Emotions are also an important psychological factor affecting nurses' communication attitudes. A survey of newly employed nurses showed that nurses with better emotional management skills had higher levels of happiness, which may be related to their ability to communicate with patients in a positive manner and successfully resolve conflicts with patients [53]. Fumis’s study [54] showed that nurses with moral distress or moral dilemmas interacted less with patients or had a negative attitude during interactions, resulting in a decline in nursing quality and an increase in complaints. Nurses with high empathy are more optimistic and positive in nurse-patient communication, exhibit altruistic and prosocial behaviors, maintain more harmonious interpersonal relationships, and report higher communication satisfaction [55]. At the same time, professional knowledge is the foundation of nurses' practice, and it plays an important role in building nursing values and shaping professional identity [56].

This study found that a positive nurses’ attitude towards communication with patients is conducive to ensuring patient safety, improving patients' psychological prognosis, promoting nurses' professional development, and enhancing nurse-patient relationships. Previous studies have shown that sufficient and effective communication can prevent information transmission errors, thus ensuring patient safety [40, 57]. Effective nurse-patient communication can alleviate patients’ negative emotions, enhance treatment compliance, improve disease prognosis, ensure the quality and safety of medical care, reduce the occurrence of medical errors and disputes, boost patients’ satisfaction with nursing staff, and strengthen nurses’ sense of professional achievement [4, 58, 59].

The findings of the conceptual analysis offer a theoretical basis for defining dimensions and developing item pools in measurement tool development. Specifically, conceptual attributes are directly translated into scale items, thereby improving the instrument's content validity. The Tripartite Model of Attitudes [11] delineates the three dimensions of the measurement tool: cognitive, affective, and behavioral tendencies, thereby ensuring the tool's structural validity. Currently, the Nurses' Attitudes towards Communication with Patients Scale is the only specific instrument for assessing nurses' attitudinal dispositions towards patient communication. Developed by Professor Giménez-Espert [14] through a rigorous methodology incorporating literature analysis and expert consultation based on the Tripartite Model of Attitudes [11], this 25-item scale demonstrates satisfactory psychometric properties. Although theoretically anchored in the Tripartite Model of Attitudes [11], the scale primarily operationalizes two attributes — emotional responses and evaluative; predictive of their actual communication behaviors— while failing to fully capture the complete conceptual spectrum of nurses' attitudes towards communication with patients.

By clarifying the key attributes of nurses’ communication attitudes, this conceptual analysis provides a theoretical foundation for nursing curriculum design, helping educators focus on core communication competencies in nurse-patient interactions. Building on the antecedents and consequences identified in the conceptual analysis, simulated clinical scenarios can be designed to enhance case-based, context-aware teaching methods.

While providing important insights, the study presents certain limitations that warrant consideration. Because few publications explicitly addressed the attributes, antecedents, and consequences of nurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients, the terms “attributes”, “antecedents” and “consequences” were excluded from the search strategy to maximize retrieval of relevant studies. This broad search strategy, however, resulted in broader search outputs and increased difficulty in screening. The exclusive focus on Chinese nurse samples may affect cross-cultural generalizability due to potential homogeneity in professional values and culture-specific communication patterns. Additionally, while this conceptual analysis employed a mixed-methods approach, the lack of a standardized data integration framework may lead to subjective bias in the interpretation of results.

Conclusions

Through systematic literature review and empirical field research, this study has conceptualized nurses’ attitudes towards communication with patients as a multidimensional construct with distinct cognitive, affective, and behavioral tendency dimensions. The research demonstrates that these it is shaped by a tripartite system of influences: nurses-related characteristics, patients-related factors, and environmental conditions. Simultaneously, it exerts significant effects across four critical domains: patient outcomes, nurse professional development, quality of nursing practice, and nurse-patint relationship. The findings of this study provide a theoretical basis for the development of measurement tools assessing nurses' attitudes towards communication with patients. The results have important implications for nursing education and training programs, where focused attention should be directed toward cultivating and reinforcing appropriate communication attitudes through evidence-based pedagogical approaches. Based on the identified influencing factors, several practical strategies emerge for improving nurses' communication attitudes, such as strategic workforce planning through recruitment of adequately trained nursing staff to optimize workload distribution, and implementation of workload management systems to prevent burnout and maintain communication quality.
