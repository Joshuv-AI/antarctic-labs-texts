Co-creation and implementation of a healthy lunch at Dutch primary schools in disadvantaged neighbourhoods: design of a cluster randomised cross-over effectiveness study
Authors: Annemieke Wargers, Katerina Polychronakis, Daan Nieboer, Hein Raat, Wilma Jansen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe majority of children fails to have a healthy diet. Providing a healthy lunch at school is a way to target all children, regardless of ethnic and socioeconomic background. This study aims to investigate the effectivity and feasibility of a co-created healthy school lunch in disadvantaged neighbourhoods of the city of Rotterdam, the Netherlands.Methods and analysisThis is a cluster randomised cross-over trial with five participating primary schools. Schools will be randomised to have an 18 week healthy school lunch the first or second half of the academic year. The other half of the academic year, children will bring their own lunch from home. A tailored lunch concept for each school will be co-created with school staff, parents, children and key-stakeholders.Primary outcome is the dietary content of children’s lunches, assessed by observations for all children. Secondary outcomes include healthy lunch-related parameters, like general dietary behaviour, perceived health, taste preferences, concentration in class, and some feasibility outcomes, like satisfaction, implementation at school and affordability for parents. Secondary outcomes will be assessed by questionnaires for children (grades 5–8), parents and teachers. The observations and questionnaires for children and parents will be completed at baseline (September 2023), half way the academic year (January–February 2024) and at the end of the academic year (June–July 2024). Teachers will complete a monthly questionnaire starting in September 2023.Ethics and disseminationThis study obtained ethical approval from the Institutional research Review Board Erasmus MC of Rotterdam, the Netherlands. Informed consent will be collected from all participants (parents, teachers and children 12 years or older) and/or their parents (for children under the age of 16). The findings will be disseminated by conference presentations and publications in scientific peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration numberNCT06058325.Keywords: Schools, NUTRITION & DIETETICS, PUBLIC HEALTH, Community child health, Community-Based Participatory Research

Background

The majority of children fails to have a healthy diet. Only 42.5% of European children eats fruit on a daily basis, and even only 22.6% consumes vegetables every day.1 According to a Dutch public health survey administered in 2022, respectively 8% and 22% of children from 4 to 11 years old in Rotterdam, the Netherlands, eat fruit and vegetables less than 5 days a week.2 Considering that eating behaviours of children track into adult life,3 promoting healthy lifestyle behaviours already at a young age is of great importance to counteract the health consequences later in life. Healthy dietary behaviours decrease the risk of non-communicable diseases, but also obesity,4 which nowadays, constitutes a major public health problem, in children too.5

To promote healthier dietary behaviours, guidelines have been established. The WHO and the Dutch Health Council recommend to eat 400 g, equivalent to five servings, of fruit and vegetables every day.4 6 The Dutch national nutrition centre provides dietary recommendations per age: children 4–12 years old should eat, depending on their age, between 100 and 200 g of vegetables and 1.5–2 portions of fruit each day.7

Various factors influence dietary behaviour of children.8 Among others, socioeconomic (SES) differences exist for dietary intake.8 9 Children from a relatively high SES background or with relatively higher educated parents are more likely to have a higher fruit and vegetable intake.10 11 Therefore, children from high SES backgrounds show relatively lower Body Mass Index (BMI) Z-scores10 and children with a low SES background are more likely to be overweight when not eating enough vegetables.9 Those inequalities in obesity also exist in the city of Rotterdam, with a higher prevalence of obesity in lower SES areas.12 In addition, in Rotterdam children from families with a migration background are generally more likely to be overweight or obese than children in families without a migration background.12 Declining those gaps among different SES groups is a huge public health challenge.8

To reduce those inequalities, a recommended strategy is to target all children.13 Schools can be a good starting point for this, as children spend a lot of their time at school,14 especially around lunch time. Yamaguchi et al showed that a universal school lunch programme contributes to a reduction of SES inequalities for fruit and vegetable consumption.13 Already many European countries provide school lunches.15 For example in Sweden, where the provided school lunches positively contribute to dietary behaviour of children, but also decrease social inequalities.16 However, in the Netherlands, most primary schools do not provide a (healthy) school lunch. Generally, children at primary schools either eat at home or stay at school during lunch break, but then bring all their food and drinks from home and do not have the possibility to buy this at school. Food policies, like only drinking water or bringing healthy food, are apparent in some schools, but are not obliged nationally. A typical Dutch lunch mostly contains sandwiches with a drink.17

Previously, two research projects in the Netherlands investigated the effects of a healthy lunch provided at school: the Healthy School Lunch project and the Healthy Primary School of the Future. The research on the Healthy School Lunch project shows that a 6-month healthy school lunch improves the healthy food intake, but this is not maintained at follow-up, 6 months after the healthy school lunch ended.18 Children, parents and teachers were satisfied with the healthy school lunch and would like to continue with it.18 The Healthy Primary School of the Future targets the whole school with both a healthy school lunch and more physical activity. Significant favourable effects were shown on school water consumption and lunch intake of vegetables and dairy products, for the intervention group compared with the control group following a regular curriculum.19 After 4 years of intervention, children’s body composition (like BMI, waist circumference) remained stable in the intervention schools, whereas this significantly increased in the control group.20

Although the results of these studies seem promising, it is uncertain whether these effects will also occur in a large diverse city like Rotterdam,20 where many people live in more disadvantaged neighbourhoods and have diverse ethnic backgrounds: more than half of the population in Rotterdam is from non-Dutch origin.21 Due to this great variety, dietary behaviour, preferences and requirements among children in Rotterdam might differ.22 23 This can be reflected in the foods and drinks they bring to school and it might influence the size of the effects as some groups of children could benefit more from a healthy lunch at school. It can also complicate the offer of an acceptable lunch for all children. Therefore, this study should investigate whether children in Rotterdam will benefit in the same way as children in other places in the Netherlands and if school lunches are feasible and acceptable for them.

Furthermore, financial restraints of parents may contribute to unhealthier dietary behaviours,24 and might also influence the food and drinks children take to school. On the contrary, school lunches might provide additional benefits for parents experiencing financial constraints. Free school lunches may reduce these financial constraints and offer room for more healthy foods provided at home.

It is known that various factors are influencing a successful implementation of interventions and policies. The systematic review of Ronto et al shows that support from school staff, for example, enables successful implementation of food policies.25 Moreover, poor acceptance of healthy food in the school community or lack of support from parents are perceived as barriers for successful implementation.25 Furthermore, top-down designed policies are experienced by school stakeholders to be barriers for successful food policy implementation and compliance.25 Therefore, in this study, co-creation will be used to develop a school lunch concept that is feasible to implement and supported by those directly involved. Co-creation is an important method of consulting those directly involved: school staff, parents, children and key-stakeholders.

Within the current study, we want to investigate the effects of the implementation of healthy school lunches in disadvantaged neighbourhoods of the city of Rotterdam, the Netherlands. The co-created school lunches will be provided at primary schools in Rotterdam, the Netherlands, for a duration of half an academic year (18 weeks excluding school holidays) at each school. The study aims to look at both effectivity and feasibility of the healthy school lunch.

Methods

Study design and timeline

This study has a cross-over design, meaning all participating schools have both a control and intervention period during academic year 2023–2024. Schools will be paired according to the size of the school. Within their pair, schools will be randomly assigned, using ResearchRandomizer,26 to group A or B, either starting in September 2023 with the healthy school lunch or in February 2024. During the period that the school joins the ‘intervention condition’, schools will implement a healthy school lunch for all children 4 days a week during half an academic year (18 weeks excluding school holidays). The other half of the academic year, the school will join the ‘control condition’; in this period, the children will bring their own lunch box. A period of 18 weeks between two measurements seems reasonable based on earlier research in the Netherlands.18 This study will not be blinded. The costs of the healthy school lunches in this study will be covered by the Municipality of Rotterdam. The maximum budget for a healthy lunch per individual is €3,80 per day.

During academic year 2023–2024, there will be three measurement moments to collect data of children and parents, as shown in figure 1. Observations and questionnaires for children and parents will take place at the start (September 2023 = T1), middle (January 2024 = T2) and end (June 2024 = T3) of the academic year. Data from teachers will be collected on a monthly basis during academic year 2023–2024. Data will be collected from the same participants at each time point.

Recruitment

Recruitment of schools

Primary schools in Rotterdam, the Netherlands, participating in the municipal obesity prevention programme ‘LekkerFit!’ (translation: ‘Enjoy being fit!’) were invited to participate in this study based on deprivation scores and percentage of children with overweight/obesity. The ‘LekkerFit!’ programme has been implemented in 2005 to combat overweight and lack of physical activity among children and young people in Rotterdam. It focuses on nutrition, exercise and making healthy choices, but so far (healthy) school lunches are not part of this programme. About 90 schools within Rotterdam participate in the ‘LekkerFit!’ programme, and each school has a yearly BMI measurement for all children. Schools with a percentage of overweight/obesity among children of 20% or more were invited for this study. Schools were ranked on deprivation scores of Statistics Netherlands, with a higher deprivation score, representing a lower SES of the school. Selected primary schools were primarily contacted by their ‘LekkerFit!’ project leader. When interested, schools received more information by e-mail and a face-to-face meeting was planned with the research team. Schools were eligible when having a continuous time schedule, where children have lunch at school. We aimed to include approximately six schools, depending on the size of schools, as there was a limit on finances for the number of children that could participate in the project. In the end, five primary schools in Rotterdam agreed to participate in the study. Recruitment of schools was completed at the end of April 2023.

In this study, all children can participate in the observations, but only children in grades 5–8 will complete questionnaires.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was given by the Institutional research Review Board Erasmus MC of Rotterdam, the Netherlands (permission ID: MEC-2023-0336). Informed consent will be signed by all participants in this study before data collection. An example of the information letter and informed consent form is presented in online supplemental appendix A. All children, parents and teachers at participating primary schools will be invited to participate in this study. Informed consent for children will be signed by their parents and by children when they are 12 years or older at data collection, in accordance with the Dutch guidelines, legislation and the Medical Research Act in the Netherlands. An information letter will be provided on paper to children in grades 5–8 and when they are 12 years or older they will also receive an informed consent form on paper. Information will be explained verbally by teachers to children from grades 1 to 4. Schools will inform parents in general terms about the implementation of the healthy school lunch through their usual communication channels. All parents will receive an information letter and informed consent form on paper by the research team. In agreement with schools, an additional information meeting for parents and/or teachers can be provided, researchers will attend existing information meetings at school to present the research and answer any questions and/or information material will be translated in other languages than Dutch. Parents can indicate whether both themselves and their child(ren) will participate in the study or either only the parent or only their child(ren). Parents who complete the questionnaire in January–February (T2) and/or June (T3) will receive a gift card of €10. All teachers will receive an information letter and informed consent form on paper by the research team.

The healthy school lunch will be offered to all children in participating schools. If parents do not want their child to eat the lunch at school, they will communicate this to the school and children will bring their own lunch box. Also, if children eat the healthy lunch offered at school, they are not obliged to participate in observations or questionnaires.

Co-creation and intervention

Co-creation is an important method of consulting those directly involved.27 28 Therefore, the concept of the healthy school lunch will be co-created for each school separately in collaboration with the school team, parents, children and key-stakeholders, for example a school dietician. Thus, the concept can be different for each participating school. One co-creation session will be organised per school and is facilitated by the research team. A co-creation session has a duration of approximately 3 hours and takes place at school. Schools in groups A and B (see figure 1) will respectively have their co-creation sessions in May 2023 or October–November 2023.

The co-creation session starts with an introduction of participants and some background of the healthy school lunch project. Two topics will be discussed plenary to shape the lunch concept. First, a choice is made between a self-made lunch by children or a catered, prepacked lunch. Second, a choice is made between internal and external organisation, where respectively either the school itself (e.g., staff and/or parents) or an out-sourced organisation is responsible for preparation of the healthy lunch. A combination of concepts is also possible. During those plenary discussions, all participants will be evenly divided into two groups and each group will come up with arguments for one concept and against the other. For example, one group will come up with arguments for internal organisation of the lunch and against external organisation. Whereas the other group will come up with arguments for external organisation and against internal organisation. By using this method, both pros and cons for each concept will be weighed against each other.

Afterwards, participants will be evenly divided into two subgroups to discuss more elaborately the content and organisation of the lunch. In the first round, a subgroup will focus on either content or organisation. In the second round, they change topic and add to the ideas from the previous group. The co-creation session will end with a plenary summary on discussed topics and next steps will be presented by the research team. Children will only join the co-creation session for 1 hour and will form its own subgroup to discuss questions on content and organisation. Observation of content of children’s lunch and snack at school

We will use observation forms to objectively score on 1 day what snack and lunch children bring to or get at school, this is the primary outcome. Observations will be conducted by trained researchers on 1 day during each of three measurement moments in the academic year 2023–2024. Depending on the school size, approximately 2–6 researchers will observe during 1 day. The observation form is (partly) adapted from the studies of van de Gaar et al34 and van Rongen et al35 and includes items on different types of bread, toppings, drinks, fruit, vegetables and other types of food. Researchers will be trained how to use the observation form. Ambiguities will be discussed and cleared. Data will be presented as number of portions within food groups: bread (brown, white and other), toppings (sweet, savoury and other), drinks (water, milk, sweet beverages and other), fruit, vegetables, snacks and (hot) main dishes (like soup or a pasta meal). When children did not have any food or drinks during lunch or snack break, this was scored as ‘nothing’. Any ambiguities in choosing the right food group will be discussed with at least two researchers in order to reach agreement.

When children bring their own snack and lunch box from home, the teacher will ask children to show the content of their snack and lunchboxes to the researchers. When children get a healthy lunch at school, we will observe the content of this lunch. However, researchers will also note when children bring their packed lunch from home instead of eating the provided lunch at school. The scoring of type of snack and lunch will take ca 1–2 min per child and will be done before the break in which children eat their snack. Depending on the lunch concept the school has chosen, the content of the lunch is the same for all children or varies per child. The content of the lunch can be predetermined or decided during the lunch break. In the latter case, we will score the content of the lunch before the children start eating at the beginning of the lunch break.

Questionnaire for children

Questionnaires for children will focus on secondary outcomes, namely general dietary behaviour (consumption on number of days per week), taste preferences of fruit and vegetables (3-point scale of tastiness or ‘I don’t know’), perceived health (5-point or 7-point Likert scale), atmosphere in the class during lessons and during lunch (5-point Likert scale) and the satisfaction with the healthy school lunch (5-point Likert scale or multiple choice). Children are included into the project to complete questionnaires if they are in grades 5–8 of a participating school during academic year 2023–2024. All children in those classes will be invited to complete the questionnaire during all three measurements. Questions regarding satisfaction with the healthy lunch are only asked during the intervention period. One question is also focused on how often children eat the lunch provided at school, to indicate whether they have eaten it regularly or not. Completing the questionnaire will take ca 20–25 min and children can do this on paper during class at school.

Questionnaire for parents

Questionnaires for parents will also focus on secondary outcomes, like general dietary behaviour of their child (consumption on number of days per week), taste preferences of fruit and vegetables (3-point scale of tastiness or ‘I don’t know’), perceived health of their child (5-point or 7-point Likert scale), financial situation (3-point Likert scale or multiple choice), satisfaction with the healthy school lunch (5-point Likert scale or multiple choice) and affordability of a healthy school lunch (multiple choice and open question). Parents will complete the questionnaire about their youngest child if they have multiple children attending the same school. Only one parent has to complete the questionnaire. Questions regarding satisfaction with the healthy lunch are only asked during the intervention period. One question is also focused on how often their child(ren) eat the lunch provided at school, to indicate whether they have eaten it regularly or not. Completing the questionnaire will take ca 15–20 min and parents can do this on paper at home.

Questionnaire for teachers

Questionnaires for teachers will focus on atmosphere in their class during lessons and during lunch (7-point Likert scale), as well as implementation, feasibility and satisfaction with the healthy school lunch (7-point Likert scale, multiple choice and open questions). Questions regarding implementation of the healthy lunch are only asked during the intervention period and can also be used to improve the school lunch along the way. Completing the questionnaire will take 5–10 min and can be done online via LimeSurvey every month. We will send a web link for the LimeSurvey questionnaire to teachers’ e-mail address of school, which they provided on their informed consent form.

Since randomisation is performed at the level of the school, the power calculation depends on the within-period intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), cluster autocorrelation (CAC) and individual autocorrelation (IAC).37 The within-period ICC measures the degree to which measurements within a cluster in a period are correlated, the CAC measures the correlation between cluster-specific means at both periods and the IAC measures the correlation within participants across periods. As estimates of these quantities are unknown, we assumed conservative values for the IAC (0) and a value of 0.8 for the CAC as previously suggested.38 39 Based on these numbers, we would need to include 20 children in each of the five schools to be able to detect an increase from 18%19 to 40% (medium effect size for Cohen g) in percentage of children eating vegetables for lunch at school across within-period ICC values up to 0.2. Assuming a response of 60%,33 we will need to invite at least 33 children/parents per school.

Statistical analyses

Baseline descriptive statistics of all schools will be presented. Continuous data that follow normal distribution will be expressed as mean±SD, not-normally distributed as median (IQR), while categorical data as n (%).

In order to estimate the impact of the intervention, generalised linear mixed models will be used. In these models, fixed effects include the intervention and period. Additionally random intercepts for school, school-period and school-children will be included. Since the number of clusters randomised is below 40, we will use t-test to assess statistical significance of the treatment effect where the df will be estimated using the Kenward–Roger and Satterthwaite approximations. Possible carry over effects will be assessed through interaction tests between period and intervention. Analysis will be adjusted for relevant sociodemographic characteristics. Analysis will be done for all lunch concepts combined as well as for separate lunch concepts. Additional exploratory analysis will be performed on sociodemographic characteristics, like grade, age, gender and migration background of children (based on country of birth of both child and their parents). Missing values in adjustment variables will be imputed, if needed, following common procedures.

Descriptives on feasibility data will be presented. Differences in feasibility between lunch concepts will be tested using χ2 and analysis of variance or non-parametric tests depending on the type of outcome variable.

Qualitative data from open answer categories in the questionnaires will be described and summarised or synthesised.

Patient and public involvement

Participants and/or the public were not involved in obtaining funding or creating the protocol. In this project, participants will play a key role in co-creating the healthy school lunch concept for their school. Members of the school team, parents, external stakeholders and children will be involved in a co-creation session to develop the healthy school lunch concept for their school by discussing predefined topics. During this session, participants can also provide input on other school lunch-related topics important to them. The healthy school lunch concept can differ per school. The final concept will be discussed with the school team to assure a feasible implementation.

Discussion

This study will assess the effectiveness and feasibility of a co-created healthy lunch at primary schools in socioeconomically disadvantaged neighbourhoods of Rotterdam, the Netherlands. A cluster randomised cross-over design will be used and various outcomes regarding effectivity and feasibility will be assessed, as outlined in box 2. For each school, a healthy school lunch concept will be co-created with school staff, parents, children and key-stakeholders.

The WHO reports that the majority of children have unhealthy dietary behaviours.1 A healthy lunch at school can target all children equally, regardless of ethnic and SES background. Previous reviews show that school food policies, school meals and nutrition interventions at school can improve dietary behaviour.40,42 Although results remain limited, as there is still a great variety in the provision of (healthy) school lunches between countries.43 Furthermore, van Rongen et al found that perceptions of children, parents and school staff on implementation of school lunches in the Netherlands are mixed.44 They highlight the need for more research on the organisational aspects of a school provided lunch, like agreement and support among those involved, affordability of parents and logistics within school.44 Those topics will be addressed in this study, both in the co-creation process and in the outcomes measured.

A novelty of this study is the use of co-creation to design a tailored healthy school lunch concept for each school with those directly involved: school staff, parents, children and key-stakeholders. By using co-creation as a method, we aim to advance feasibility of the healthy lunch at school, like support, satisfaction, implementation and sustainability.27 28 Green et al suggest to engage with those directly involved to enhance implementation in practice. Motivation and willingness to implement are key for better programmes and for improving sustainability and continuation on the long-term.45

Another strength of this study is the use of a cluster randomised cross-over design.46 This allows all participating schools to receive a healthy lunch for half an academic year. Furthermore, this design enables to compare the same school during both an intervention and control period. A period of 18 weeks between two measurements seems reasonable based on previous research to avoid spillover effects.18 46 A third strength of this study is the great variety of outcome measures, which assures a multidisciplinary view on the benefits of a healthy school lunch. This contributes to new insights and helps decision-making in policy.

One limitation of this study is the lack of long-term effect measurements. Due to the cross-over design, half of the schools will start with the intervention and will bring their own lunch during the second half of the academic year. However, the short duration of just one academic year limits the measurement of long-term effects. Furthermore, seasonal variation in lunch provided might influence the comparability of results between schools starting in September or in February. Although there are overlapping seasons in both periods and we will analyse lunch concepts separately. Moreover, we expect the seasonal effects to be low as in every season there will be supply in the specified food groups. Another limitation is the use of self-reported and non-validated questionnaires. Although we gained some input from the other Dutch studies evaluating healthy school lunches,31,3547 the questionnaires and observation form that will be used are (partly) adapted or developed for this study and have not been pilot tested. Whereas the primary outcome, dietary content of food and drinks at school will be observed by researchers, the questionnaires will be self-reported by children, parents and teachers. Although there are some alternative approaches to measure dietary content, like food photography or food diaries, we have chosen to use an observation form in this study. We wanted to have one way of measuring in all age groups, and this objective measure was the most feasible, also for the youngest age groups. Observation forms take little time, are easy to score and are considered to be an accurate and reliable option for assessing lunch content and portion sizes.48

A practical challenge when performing this study will be the inclusion of enough children with a young age (4–12 years old) and parents from low SES neighbourhoods who provide written informed consent. We purposely decided which outcome measures to include in order to keep the questionnaire feasible for all participants and to maximise the willingness from parents to consent and therefore increase the response rate. We will closely work together with schools to use appropriate communication strategies for all participants.

Overall, this study contributes to the field of knowledge regarding health of children. Results will provide information for implementation and feasibility in other Dutch schools. Several feasibility questions are already included in the questionnaires, but more feasibility research, elaborated on different topics and with more schools in Rotterdam, will be performed and reported separately. Finally, the outcomes of this study can guide policy making regarding healthy meals at schools.
