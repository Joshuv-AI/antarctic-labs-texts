STAT3 S727 phosphorylation drives pathogenic Th17 differentiation and neuroinflammation in autoimmune disease
Authors: Douglas S. Prado, Achyudhan R. Kutuva, Richard T. Cattley, Andreza Buzolin Sonego, Parth Sutariya, Elena Im, Shuxian Wu, Mijoon Lee, Rachel L. Cumberland, Isha Mehta, Greg M. Delgoffe, William C. Boggess, James R. Faeder, Mark J. Shlomchik, William F. Hawse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
T helper 17 (Th17) cells are a heterogeneous subset of CD4⁺ T cells that mediate host defense and drive autoimmune pathology. While phosphorylation of STAT3 at tyrosine 705 (Y705) is essential for Th17 differentiation, the functional role of serine 727 (S727) phosphorylation remains unresolved. Here, we show that STAT3 S727 phosphorylation is selectively required for the differentiation and effector function of pathogenic Th17 (pTh17) cells. Mechanistically, S727 phosphorylation facilitates STAT3 interactions with transcriptional co-regulators and chromatin remodeling complexes, establishing a transcriptional and epigenetic landscape that reinforces pTh17 identity. A STAT3 S727A point mutation disrupts these interactions, resulting in aberrant STAT1 activation, elevated IFN-γ production, impaired glycolytic metabolism, and destabilization of the pTh17 program. Metabolomic and extracellular flux analyses revealed a marked reduction in glycolytic capacity and spare respiratory reserve in STAT3S727A Th17 cells, indicating that S727 phosphorylation is required for optimal bioenergetic fitness and effector programming. In vivo, CD4⁺ T cells expressing STAT3S727A have reduced pathological capacity in the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), conferring protection in a murine model of multiple sclerosis. These findings uncover a noncanonical STAT3 signaling axis that integrates transcriptional and metabolic control of Th17-mediated neuroinflammation and suggest that selective targeting of S727 phosphorylation may offer therapeutic benefit in MS by disrupting both effector function and glycolytic support. STAT3 S727 phosphorylation is essential for assembling transcriptional and metabolic programs that drive pathogenic Th17 differentiation and autoimmune neuroinflammation.

Introduction

T helper 17 (Th17) cells are a subset of CD4⁺ T cells that play a central role in mucosal immunity, particularly in defense against extracellular bacteria and fungi [1–4]. Th17 cells secrete IL-17 family cytokines, which stimulate epithelial and stromal cells to produce chemokines such as IL-8 and GM-CSF, thereby recruiting and activating neutrophils to eliminate pathogens [5, 6]. The importance of Th17 cells in host defense is underscored by clinical observations: patients with autosomal dominant hyper-IgE syndrome, caused by mutations in STAT3, exhibit impaired Th17 differentiation and suffer from recurrent bacterial and fungal infections [7]. Similarly, HIV-mediated depletion of Th17 cells contributes to susceptibility to opportunistic infections [8, 9].

While Th17 cells are essential for immune homeostasis, dysregulated Th17 responses are implicated in the pathogenesis of autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis and multiple sclerosis [10–13]. Th17 cells are functionally heterogeneous. Nonpathogenic Th17 (npTh17) cells, generated in the presence of IL-6 and TGF-β1, produce IL-17 but induce minimal inflammation [14]. In contrast, pathogenic Th17 (pTh17) cells, induced by IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-23 or TGF-β3, drive robust inflammatory responses and autoimmune pathology [14, 15].

STAT3 is a critical transcription factor downstream of IL-6 signaling and is indispensable for both npTh17 and pTh17 differentiation [16, 17]. Canonical activation of STAT3 involves phosphorylation at tyrosine 705 (Y705) [18] which promotes dimerization, nuclear translocation, and transcriptional activation of Th17-associated genes [19, 20]. However, STAT3 also undergoes phosphorylation at serine 727 (S727). Our previous work identified PIKFYVE as a lipid kinase that promotes STAT3 S727 phosphorylation during npTh17 differentiation [21]. While studies in other cell types suggest that S727 phosphorylation can regulate a subset of STAT3 target genes independently of Y705 [22, 23], the molecular mechanisms linking S727 phosphorylation to Th17 effector function and pathogenicity have not been elucidated.

Here, we demonstrate that STAT3 S727 phosphorylation is selectively required for the differentiation and function of pTh17 cells. We show that S727 phosphorylation promotes STAT3 interactions with transcriptional cofactors and chromatin remodeling complexes, enabling the establishment of a transcriptional and metabolic program that supports Th17 pathogenicity. Loss of S727 phosphorylation leads to aberrant STAT1 activation, increased IFN-γ production, impaired glycolysis, and reduced Th17-mediated neuroinflammation in vivo. These findings define a previously unrecognized role for STAT3 S727 phosphorylation in enforcing Th17 lineage commitment and function, with implications for targeting Th17-driven autoimmune disease.

To assess the functional relevance of S727 phosphorylation, we used CD4⁺ T cells from mice harboring a germline serine-to-alanine mutation at position 727 of STAT3 (STAT3S727A). This mutation did not affect Th1, Th2, or iTreg differentiation (Fig. S1A–F). However, under Th17-polarizing conditions, STAT3S727AT cells exhibited a significant reduction in IL-17–producing cells compared to wild-type controls, with a more pronounced defect under pTh17 conditions (Fig.1E–H).

We next sought to identify the upstream signals responsible for STAT3 S727 phosphorylation. Combinatorial stimulation of naïve CD4⁺ T cells revealed that TCR/CD28 and IL-6 signaling were sufficient to induce S727 phosphorylation, which was further enhanced by TGF-β or IL-1β (Fig. S1G, H). To identify the kinase responsible, we performed a pharmacologic screen targeting candidate kinases previously implicated in S727 phosphorylation. Additionally, some of these kinases including mTORC1 and CDK are critical for Th17 differentiation[24, 25]. Inhibition of mTORC1 or CDK had no effect, whereas JNK inhibition significantly reduced STAT3 S727 phosphorylation to baseline levels (Fig. 1I, J). Functionally, JNK inhibition impaired pTh17 differentiation, as evidenced by reduced IL-17 production (Fig. 1K, L). Prior work by Tan and Lam demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of JNK reduced IL-17 production and that the JNK pathway likely plays a broader role in Th17 differentiation beyond its upstream regulation of STAT3 S727 phosphorylation[26]. These findings identify JNK as a key upstream kinase that phosphorylates STAT3 at S727 in response to IL-6 signaling and demonstrate that this modification is essential for pTh17 lineage commitment.

STAT3 S727 phosphorylation orchestrates transcription factor and coactivator interactions in Th17 programming

Phosphorylation of STAT3 at tyrosine 705 (Y705) is required for its dimerization, nuclear translocation, and transcriptional activation [19, 20]. To determine whether serine 727 (S727) phosphorylation influences these canonical STAT3 functions, we cultured naïve CD4⁺ T cells from wild-type and STAT3S727A mice under pTh17-polarizing conditions. Immunoblotting revealed comparable levels of STAT3 Y705 phosphorylation in both genotypes, indicating that the S727A mutation does not impair STAT3 activation (Fig. S1I). Moreover, nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation demonstrated equivalent nuclear accumulation of STAT3 in wild-type and STAT3S727A T cells (Fig. S1J, K), suggesting that S727 phosphorylation is dispensable for STAT3 nuclear translocation.

We next investigated whether S727 phosphorylation regulates STAT3’s interaction with transcriptional cofactors (Fig. 2A). Using immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (IP-MS), we profiled STAT3-interacting proteins in wild-type and STAT3S727A CD4⁺ T cells cultured under Th17 conditions. Notably, components of the SWI/SNF (BAF) chromatin remodeling complex—including SMCA4, SMCA2, and SNF5—were significantly reduced in STAT3S727A immunoprecipitates (Fig. 2E–G). Given that S727 phosphorylation modulates STAT3’s interactions with transcription factors and chromatin remodeling complexes, we hypothesized that this modification is essential for establishing the chromatin architecture required for pTh17 differentiation. To test this, we performed ATAC-seq (Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing) on wild-type and STAT3S727A CD4⁺ T cells cultured under pTh17-polarizing conditions. This analysis revealed that a subset of genomic regions exhibited increased accessibility in wild-type cells, while others were more accessible in STAT3S727A cells (Fig. 3C).

Motif enrichment analysis of differentially accessible regions identified transcription factor binding motifs enriched in wild-type cells, including STAT, NFAT, ETS, and interferon regulatory motifs. In contrast, chromatin regions more accessible in STAT3S727A cells were enriched for Fos and Jun motifs (Fig. 3D). These findings suggest that STAT3 S727 phosphorylation promotes a chromatin landscape that favors Th17 lineage-specific transcriptional programming.

To determine whether these chromatin changes translated into transcriptional differences, we performed bulk RNA-seq on wild-type and STAT3S727ACD4⁺ T cells polarized under pTh17 conditions. While the transcriptomes of naïve CD4⁺ T cells were largely unaffected by the S727A mutation (Fig. S4B), polarization under pTh17 conditions resulted in over 3,000 differentially expressed genes between genotypes (Fig. 3E). Notably, IL-17 transcripts were significantly reduced in STAT3S727A cells, whereas Th1-associated transcripts, including IFN-γ, were upregulated.

To integrate chromatin accessibility with gene expression, we performed a combined analysis of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq datasets. Chromatin accessibility was often correlated with transcript abundance. For example, the IL-17 locus exhibited reduced chromatin accessibility and transcription in STAT3S727A cells, while the IFN-γ locus showed increased accessibility and expression (Fig. 3F). These data indicate that STAT3 S727 phosphorylation-dependent chromatin remodeling is critical for activating the transcriptional program that defines pathogenic Th17 cells.

To further explore the transcriptional consequences of S727 phosphorylation, we performed pathway enrichment analysis on the RNA-seq data. The STAT3 S727A mutation significantly downregulated genes involved in Th17 differentiation and cytokine signaling, including IL22, IL17a, RORC, RORA, and IL23r (Fig. 4A, B). These findings were validated by qPCR, which confirmed reduced expression of Rorc and Il23r in STAT3S727A cells (Fig. 4C, D). RORγt expression was further confirmed at the protein level, with WT cells exhibiting higher RORγt levels compared to STAT3S727A cells (Fig. S1L). This reduction was consistently observed irrespective of whether the analysis was performed on total CD4⁺ T cells, IL-17⁺ cells, or IL-17⁻ cells. In line, since IL-6 is responsible for inducing STAT3 phosphorylation and RORγt expression[18, 27], we also investigated whether the STAT3S727A point mutation could modulate the expression of its receptor. Conversely, the STAT3 S727A mutation upregulated genes associated with Th1 differentiation, including Tbx21 (T-bet) and IFN-γ (Fig. 4E–G). Flow cytometry and immunoblotting confirmed increased protein levels of T-bet and IFN-γ in STAT3S727A CD4⁺ T cells under pTh17 conditions (Fig. 4H–J, S2A–B). This Th1-like skewing was also observed under alternative pTh17-polarizing conditions using TGF-β3 (Fig. S2C–F) and was phenocopied by CD4-specific deletion of STAT3 (Fig. S2I–J).

To determine whether JNK-mediated phosphorylation of STAT3 at S727 regulates this transcriptional balance, we treated CD4⁺ T cells with a JNK inhibitor during pTh17 polarization. JNK inhibition increased IFN-γ and T-bet expression (Fig. 4K–N), mirroring the effects of the STAT3 S727A mutation. These results suggested that STAT3 S727 phosphorylation enforces the Th17 transcriptional program by repressing Th1-associated gene expression and promoting chromatin accessibility at Th17-specific loci.

STAT3 S727 phosphorylation represses STAT1 activation and IFN-γ signaling to maintain Th17 identity

To investigate how STAT3 S727 phosphorylation influences Th17 lineage fidelity, we examined the activation of STAT1, a transcription factor known to antagonize Th17 differentiation[12]. RNA-seq analysis revealed that IFN-γ–regulated genes were significantly upregulated in STAT3S727A CD4⁺ T cells cultured under pTh17 conditions (Fig. 4E, F).

To determine whether this STAT1 activation was driven by autocrine IFN-γ signaling, we cultured wild-type and STAT3S727A CD4⁺ T cells under pTh17 conditions in the presence or absence of an IFN-γ–neutralizing antibody. Blocking IFN-γ reduced STAT1 activation and T-bet expression in both genotypes, with a more pronounced effect in STAT3S727A cells (Fig. 5E–G). Correspondingly, IL-17 production increased upon IFN-γ blockade, although it remained lower in STAT3S727A cells compared to wild-type (Fig. S2G–H). Conversely, addition of exogenous IFN-γ suppressed IL-17 production in both genotypes to similar levels (Fig. S3), confirming that IFN-γ signaling antagonizes Th17 differentiation.

These findings suggest that STAT3 S727 phosphorylation suppresses STAT1 activation and IFN-γ production, thereby reinforcing Th17 lineage commitment. In the absence of this phosphorylation, STAT1 becomes hyperactivated, promoting a Th1-like transcriptional program that destabilizes the Th17 phenotype.

STAT3 S727 phosphorylation coordinates proteomic remodeling to sustain Th17 pathogenic metabolism

Given the transcriptional reprogramming observed in STAT3S727A CD4⁺ T cells, we hypothesized that S727 phosphorylation is also required for establishing the proteomic landscape that supports Th17 pathogenicity. To test this, we performed quantitative proteomics on wild-type and STAT3S727A CD4⁺ T cells polarized under pTh17 conditions for four days.

Using a 1.41-fold cutoff, we identified 128 proteins upregulated in wild-type cells and 142 proteins upregulated in STAT3S727A cells (Fig. 6A). Among the proteins upregulated in wild-type cells were Themis, IKZF1 (IKAROS), and NFAT2 (Fig. 6D)—factors known to support Th17 differentiation[28]. Immunoblotting confirmed reduced NFAT2 protein levels in STAT3S727A cells (Fig. 6E), consistent with impaired Rorc transcription observed earlier. In contrast, STAT3S727A cells showed increased expression of proteins such as ITK and granzyme B, which are associated with cytotoxic and Th1-like programs [29–31]

We next examined whether STAT3 S727 phosphorylation influences metabolic programming. Several key glycolytic enzymes—including aldolase, PGK1, and G6PI—were more abundant in wild-type cells (Fig. 6F). Despite this, STAT3S727A cells exhibited comparable proliferation (Fig. 6G) and glucose uptake (Fig.6H), suggesting that glucose transport was unaffected.

However, Seahorse extracellular flux analysis revealed that STAT3S727A cells had significantly reduced glycolytic activity, as measured by extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), and diminished glycolytic reserve following oligomycin treatment (Fig. 6I–K). These defects were specific to pTh17 conditions, as no differences were observed under npTh17 polarization (Fig. S4A).

To assess the functional consequences of impaired glycolysis, we cultured cells in media with varying glucose concentrations. IL-17 production in wild-type cells increased with glucose availability, whereas STAT3S727A cells were less responsive to glucose supplementation (Fig. 6L). These findings indicate that STAT3 S727 phosphorylation is essential for upregulating glycolytic enzymes and sustaining the metabolic demands of IL-17 production in pathogenic Th17 cells.

To isolate the T cell–intrinsic role of STAT3S727, we employed an adoptive transfer approach, allowing us to specifically assess how the mutation affects T cell behavior in vivo. This strategy provides a more precise framework for dissecting the contribution of pSTAT3S727 to T cell–mediated neuroinflammation. Thus, we adoptively transferred MOG-reactive CD4⁺ T cells from wild-type or STAT3S727A mice into RAG1⁻/⁻ recipients. Mice receiving STAT3S727A CD4⁺ T cells developed significantly milder EAE, with reduced clinical scores throughout the disease course (Fig. 7D, Fig. S4C-D). Importantly, the S727A mutation does not alter Th17 cell homeostasis, as no significant differences in IL-17 production were observed between WT and STAT3S727A mice (Fig. S4E).

At peak disease (day 15), flow cytometry of spinal cord-infiltrating cells revealed a lower frequency of IL-17⁺ CD4⁺ T cells and GM-CSF⁺ CD4⁺ T cells in the STAT3S727A group (Fig. 7E, F, H, I). In contrast, T-bet expression was elevated in STAT3S727A CD4⁺ T cells (Fig. 7G), while IFN-γ production remained comparable between genotypes (Fig. 7J), suggesting that other cell types may contribute to CNS IFN-γ levels.

Histological analysis at day 30 confirmed reduced neuroinflammation and demyelination in mice receiving STAT3S727A CD4⁺ T cells (Fig. 7K). These findings demonstrate that STAT3 S727 phosphorylation is critical for the generation of encephalitogenic Th17 cells and the development of autoimmune neuroinflammation.

Discussion

Herein, we discovered that STAT3 S727 phosphorylation is crucial for the differentiation of pathogenic Th17 cells. Mechanistically, we found that S727 phosphorylation is necessary for STAT3 to form complexes with transcription factors and coactivators, which in turn promotes alterations in chromatin structure to support the Th17 transcriptional program. For instance, S727A mutation disrupted a complex containing STAT3 and STAT1, leading to increased STAT1 activation. Consequently, elevated STAT1 transcription resulted in the destabilization of the pTh17 phenotype. In addition to transcriptional regulation, we discovered that STAT3 S727 phosphorylation was required to activate metabolic pathways including glycolysis to sustain Th17 effector function.

STAT3 contains multiple phosphorylation sites, including Y705 and S727. In addition to these, other post-translational modifications (PTMs) like lysine acetylation also occur on STAT3. PTMs enhance protein functionality and introduce various regulatory mechanisms. PTM crosstalk refers to the interactive and combinatorial effects of multiple PTMs on a single protein, enabling more sophisticated regulation [32, 33]. Moreover, PTM crosstalk allows STAT3 to integrate multiple signaling inputs. Y705 phosphorylation is catalyzed by JAKs, acting as a proximal signal from the IL-6 receptor [34]. mTORC1 and JNK are kinases more downstream from proximal signaling that phosphorylate STAT3 at S727 [35, 36]. Our findings suggest that JNK is the kinase responsible for phosphorylating STAT3 at serine 727 during pTh17 differentiation, and that its inhibition attenuates the differentiation of pathogenic Th17 cells. In line, a JNK inhibitor (SP600125) was found to inhibit IL-17 production under nonpathogenic conditions, but no increase in IFNγ production was observed, as was seen under pathogenic-skewing conditions, probably because nonpathogenic conditions have a more prominent effect on blocking the production of this cytokine [26]. Functionally, Y705 phosphorylation alone is sufficient for STAT3 nuclear localization, while S727 phosphorylation is dispensable [37, 38]. Moreover, STAT3 S727 is essential for keratinocyte and embryonic stem cells proliferation [23, 39], but our data demonstrate that it has a minor effect on cell proliferation during Th17 differentiation. However, STAT3 S727 phosphorylation is required for forming transcriptional complexes that establish chromatin structure and transcriptional networks supporting Th17s. In line with recent reports, it has been demonstrated that NFAT regulates RORγt expression by binding to the RORC locus and promoting a permissive chromatin conformation [28]. In our study, the STAT3S727A point mutation reduced NFATC2 expression as well as its chromatin accessibility, which could modulate RORγt transcriptional activity—critical for Th17 differentiation. However, the underlying mechanism by which STAT3 phosphorylation on S727 regulates NFATC2 remains to be elucidated.

Our data provides evidence of PTM crosstalk between Y705 and S727 phosphorylation on STAT3 that acts as a checkpoint to enforce the Th17 lineage. While STAT3 is the major STAT phosphorylated downstream of the IL-6 receptor in Th17 cells, we observed a small amount of STAT1 phosphorylation. In wild-type cells, STAT3 formed a complex with STAT1, which correlated with reduced STAT1 phosphorylation, nuclear translocation, and expression of STAT1-controlled genes. The formation of STAT3-STAT1 complexes required STAT3 S727 phosphorylation, as the STAT3 S727A mutation diminished STAT1 binding. The STAT3 S727A mutation also resulted in elevated STAT1 phosphorylation, increased expression of STAT1-regulated genes, and increased secretion of IFN-γ. Th17 differentiation is negatively regulated by the IFNγ–STAT1–T-bet pathway, as deficiencies in these components lead to increased IL-17 production [11, 12]. Similarly, IFNγ- and STAT1-deficient mice develop more severe EAE compared to wild-type animals, which is associated with elevated IL-17 levels and increased leukocyte infiltration [40, 41]. Our finding that IFNγ neutralization partially reverses IL-17 production in STAT3S727A cells suggests that the STAT3 point mutation affects Th17 differentiation, potentially due to exacerbated IFNγ production. Consistently, we observed reduced IL-17 and GM-CSF production during EAE, both of which play an important role for disease progression [11, 42]. However, we did not detect differences in IFNγ production during EAE, which may be attributed to the contribution of other cell types to IFNγ expression. We hypothesize that the difference in IFNγ production between in vivo and in vitro settings reflects the presence of additional regulatory inputs in vivo—such as Tregs, dendritic cells, macrophages, and B cells—that modulate cytokine output. In contrast, our in vitro culture conditions were designed to promote Th17 differentiation, favoring IL-17 production and creating an environment that is suboptimal for IFNγ induction. IFNγ expression may instead be driven within the CNS, where cytokines like IL-12 and IL-2 are present and can support its production [43, 44]. Thus, we acknowledge that the in vitro system does not fully recapitulate the complexity of the CNS inflammatory milieu. These data suggest that in Th17 cells, phosphorylated STAT3 binds to STAT1 and acts as a molecular fail safe to limit STAT1 activation.

T cell activation, Th17 cytokine generation and their pathogenicity are supported by cellular metabolism [45–47]. Pathogenic Th17 cells upregulate glycolysis [48]. Our data demonstrate that STAT3 S727 phosphorylation is required for pathogenic Th17 cells to upregulate the abundance of enzymes in glycolysis. Furthermore, STAT3 S727 phosphorylation is required to upregulate glycolytic activity in pathogenic activity, which was not observed in nonpathogenic Th17s. Functionally, we found that STAT3 S727 phosphorylation is required to upregulate glycolysis to support IL-17 production. These data suggest that STAT3 S727 functions in the metabolic programming of pTh17 cells.

Our research identified that STAT3 S727 phosphorylation is a critical regulator of Th17 differentiation. Because pathogenic Th17 cells were more sensitive to blocking STAT3 S727, targeting STAT3 S727 phosphorylation therapeutically could help blunt pathogenic Th17 cells, which may be clinically relevant to multiple inflammatory diseases. While we focused our analysis on the regulation of STAT1 by STAT3 S727 phosphorylation, our analysis suggests that STAT3 S727 phosphorylation is required for the formation of many other transcription complexes that are likely critical for Th17 cells, which will be the focus of future work.

Materials and methods

Murine strains

C57BL/6J (#000664) mice were purchased from Jackson laboratories. STAT3S727A mice were used as homozygotes and were a gift from Dr. David E. Levy from New York University [49]. Mice were housed at the University of Pittsburgh in a pathogen-free facility and handled under Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee–approved guidelines. RAG1−/− (C57BL/6J-Rag1em10Lutzy/J; strain: 034159), STAT3f/f (B6.129S1-Stat3tm1Xyfu/J) and CD4cre (B6.Cg-Tg(Cd4-cre)1Cwi/BfluJ; strain: 022071) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory.

Immunoblotting

PAGE was performed using BioRad precast Protein TGX gels. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes using a BioRad Trans-Blot Turbo transfer system with the preprogrammed mixed molecular weight setting. Antibodies used for Western blotting purchased from Cell Signaling Technology included: pSTAT3 S727 (CAS#9134), pSTAT3 Y705 (CAS#9145), STAT3 (CAS#12640), pSTAT1 Y701 (CAS#9167), STAT1 (CAS#9172), T-bet (CAS#97135), NFAT (CAS#8032), NPM (CAS#3542), β-Tubulin (CAS#5346), β-actin (CAS#5125).

For the cytoplasmatic and nuclear extraction, cells were cultured for 4 days and the fractions were extracted according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Scientific; CAS: 78833).

All primary antibodies utilized were rabbit. An anti-Rabbit IgG-HRP antibody (Cell Signaling Technology 7074) was used with the SuperSignal West Femto chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Scientific) for detection on a BioRad ChemiDoc system. Densitometry quantitation was performed with the ImageJ software package or ImageLab 6.0.

Murine CD4+ T cell differentiation assays

Naive CD4+CD25−CD44low T cells were purified from lymph nodes and spleens of wild-type C57BL/6 or STAT3S727A mice using a CD4+ T cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Purified cells were activated with plate coated anti-CD3 and soluble anti-CD28 (anti-CD3: 4 µg/mL, Clone 145-2C11 BD Biosciences; anti-CD28: 2 µg/ml, Clone 37.51 BD Biosciences) on flat-bottom plates (1 × 105/well) for Th17 cells or coated with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 (both 2 µg/ml; BD Biosciences) on flat-bottom plates (1 × 105/well) for Th1, Th2 or Treg cells. Skewing conditions were as follows: npTh17: 2.5 ng/ml rhTGF-β1 (Biolegend) plus 20 ng/ml rmIL-6 (BIolegend); Th1: 20 ng/ml rmIL-12 plus 20 ng/ml rmIL-2; Th2: 20 ng/ml rmIL-4, 20 ng/ml rmIL-2 plus 10 µg/ml anti-IFNγ; iTreg polarization: 3 ng/ml rhTGF-β1 (Biolegend); pTh17: 40 ng/ml rmIL-6, rmIL-23 and IL-1β (BIolegend). CDK (Dinaciclib), mTOR (Rapamycin) and JNK (JNK-IN-8) inhibitors (MedChemExpress) were used in vitro as indicated.

Intracellular staining and flow cytometry

The cells were cultured under Th1, Th2, Th17 or Treg conditions for 4 days. For the intracellular staining, cells were stimulated with PMA (Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate), ionomycin (Sigma-Aldrich), and Golgi stop (BD Biosciences) for 4 h and then stained for viability, CD4, IL-4, T-bet, IFNγ, IL-17 A and/or FoxP3 with transcription factor staining buffer set (CAS# 00–5523−00, ThermoScientific). The cells were analyzed on a LSR II cell analyzer (BD Biosciences) and data was processed using FlowJo v10.8.1 (BD Biosciences).

IP-MS analysis of STAT3 binding proteins during Th17 activation

Four million activated cells were lysed in a buffer containing 0.1% Triton X-100, 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris HCl pH 7.4, and 1x HALT Protease and Phosphatase inhibitor (ThermoFisher Scientific). Biotinylated anti-STAT3 rabbit mAb (Cell Signaling Technology #14801) at a ratio of 1:100 and incubated overnight with rotation at 4 °C. The next morning, added 20 ul of streptavidin magnetic bead conjugate (Cell Signaling Technology #5947) to each sample and incubated with rotation for 1.5 h at room temperature. After washing beads, bound proteins were eluted at 95° C in 5% SDS, 100 mM TEAB (Sigma Aldrich), and 100 mM DTT. Performed alkylation with 50 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma Adlrich) for 30 min at room temperature. Proteins were acidified by adding 12% phosphoric acid to a final concentration of 1.2%. The acidified proteins were diluted 1:7 in a binding buffer of 90% methanol in 100 mM TEAB. After dilution, the proteins were added to an S-trap micro column (ProtiFi) and washed three times with 90% methanol in 100 mM TEAB. Samples were digested using 3 ug of trypsin (Promega) in 50 mM TEAB at 55 °C for 1.5 h. Peptides were eluted sequentially in 3 steps with 50mM TEAB, 0.2% formic acid in water, and 50% acetonitrile. Eluents were combined and desalted using C18 columns (Supelco) on a vacuum manifold. Resulting peptides were dried using a vacuum centrifuge, followed by resuspension in 0.1% formic acid. Peptides analyzed with liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry using a nanoACQUITY HPLC (Waters) and Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Briefly, solvent A (0.1% Formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% Formic acid in acetonitrile) were used for mobile phase. Peptides were eluted onto a capillary column (75 μm ID x 360 μm OD x 15 cm length; Polymicro Technologies) containing C-18 stationary phase (5 μm particle size, 125 Å pore size; Phenomenex). Resolved using a 100 min gradient with a flow rate of 200 nl per minute (3–33% B for 90 min, 33–80% B for 2 min, constant at 80% B for 6 min, and then 80–0% B for 2 min to equilibrate the column). Data was collected in positive mode and PEAKS studio X (Bioinformatics Solutions) was used to database search against the UniProt murine database.

ATAC-seq library generation and sequencing

ATAC-seq was completed following the Omni ATAC protocol [50]. Briefly, 50k cells were transposed with Illumina Tagment DNA Enzyme (TDE1, 20034198). Library amplification was completed with 2X Phusion High Fidelity Mastermix (Thermo Scientific, F531L) with Illumina RNA/DNA UD indexes. Library quantification and assessment was done using a Qubit FLEX fluorometer and an Agilent TapeStation 4150. Libraries were normalized and pooled to 2 nM by calculating the concentration based off the fragment size (base pairs) and the concentration (ng/µl) of the libraries. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 2000, using a P2 100 flow cell. The pooled library was loaded at 750 pM and sequencing was carried out with read lengths of 2 × 58 bp, with a target of 50 million reads per sample.

RNA sequencing library generation and sequencing

RNA was assessed for quality using an Agilent TapeStation 4150/Fragment Analyzer 5300 and RNA concentration was quantified on a Qubit FLEX fluorometer. Libraries were generated with the Illumina Stranded mRNA Library Prep kit (Illumina: 20040534) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 500 ng of input RNA was used for each sample. Following adapter ligation, 11 cycles of indexing PCR were completed, Illumina RNA UD indexes. Library quantification and assessment was done using a Qubit FLEX fluorometer and an Agilent TapeStation 4150. Libraries were normalized and pooled to 2 nM by calculating the concentration based off the fragment size (base pairs) and the concentration (ng/µl) of the libraries. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 2000, using a P4 100 flow cell. The pooled library was loaded at 750 pM and sequencing was carried out with read lengths of 2 × 58 bp, with a target of 50 million reads per sample.

RNA-Seq data analysis

After initial QC and adapter trimming, the sequence data was quantified using Kallisto [51], to obtain transcript level abundances using mm10 (UCSC) as reference. Post quantification differentially expressed genes and transcripts between the WT and STAT3 S727A mice were identified using Sleuth [52]. Significant differentially expressed transcripts were defined using a q-value (Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value) threshold of < 0.2. Over-representation (ORA) and Gene Set Enrichment analysis (GSEA) [53, 54] were performed using the clusterprofiler [55, 56] tool to identify enrichment of the genes in specific pathways described in Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [57]and Reactome [58] databases.

Total RNA extraction was performed using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, the RNA was quantified and converted to cDNA using High-Capacity cDNA kit (Thermo Scientific). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Themo Scientific) using a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR machine (Thermo Scientific). Gene expression was determined relative to Gapdh and fold change calculated by using the 2−ΔΔCT threshold cycle method. Unstimulated wild type cells were used as a calibrator for analysis.

Metabolism assays

For glucose uptake assay, Th17 cells were incubated with 30 µM 2-NBDG (Cayman Chemical) in 10% FBS-containing media for 30 min at 37 °C. Cells were surface stained with cell viability dye and αCD4 and then were acquired by FACS.

T cells were plated on PDL coated Seahorse culture plates (150,000 CD4 T cells/well) in assay media consisting of minimal, unbuffered DMEM supplemented with 1% BSA and 25 mM glucose, 1 µM pyruvate, and 2 mM glutamine and analyzed using a Seahorse.

XFe96 (Agilent). Basal extracellular acidification rate was taken for 20 min. Cells were stimulated with Glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1 µM) and 2-deoxyglucose (50 mM). Glucose-induced ECAR is measured as the difference between ECAR glucose average and ECAR basal average. Induction and assessment of EAE

Wild type or STAT3S727A mice were immunized with subcutaneously injection of an emulsion containing 200 µg of myelin oligondedrocyte peptide (MOG35−55, Celtek Peptides) in CFA (5 mg/ml of Mycobacterium tuberculosis) plus pertussis toxin treatment on days 0 and 2 after immunization. On day 7 after the immunization, the draining lymph nodes were harvested and the cells were restimulated in vitro with MOG35−55 (50 µg/ml), IL-6, IL-1β and IL-23 (40 ng/ml) during 96 h at 37 °C. Then, the cells were stained with αCD3, αCD4, viability probe, αIL-17 A, αIFNγ for flow cytometry analysis. Total CD4 T purification was performed with Miltenyi kit (130-104−454) and then 1 × 106 cells were injected into RAG1−/− mice. At peak of disease (around 15 days after EAE induction), the mice were anesthetized and perfused with PBS and the spinal cords were harvested. They were cut and digested with collagenase D and DNAse I (2.5 mg/ml and 1 mg/ml, respectively, Sigma Aldrich) during 30 min at 37 °C. Next, mononuclear cells were isolated by a 37/70% percoll gradient (GE Healthcare). Finally, the cells were stimulated and stained with viability probe, αCD3, αCD4, αCD45, αIL-17, αFoxp3, αIFNγ, αT-bet or αGM-CSF and the samples were analyzed by flow cytometry. Clinical score was measured as the following description: 0: no disease, 0.5 partial limp tail, 1: completely limp tail, 1.5: slightly walking, 2: hind limb weakness, 2.5: one hind limb paralyzed, 3: completely hind limb paralysis, 3.5: weakness in forelimbs, 4: one forelimb paralyzed, 4.5: bore forelimbs paralyzed and 5: Severe paralysis/dead [59].

Histology

After 30 days of EAE induction, mice were anesthetized and perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Then, spinal cords were harvested, post-fixed in PFA for 24 h and dehydrated in 30% sucrose solution. The tissues were embedded in OCT compound (Tissue-Tek, Fisher healthcare) and frozen on dry ice. Spinal cords were cut using a cryostat (Leica) into 10 μm slices, mounted on glass slides and stained for hematoxylin and eosin (HE) or Luxol Fast Blue, which was counterstained with PAS (Periodic acid-Schiff). The images were acquired using a fluorescence microscopy (Olympus).

Data analysis and statistics

The legend of each figure describes the statistical tests used to determine P values to analyze data. Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Multiple-group comparisons were performed by Student’s T test, one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc tests as noted in the figure legends. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The results are expressed as mean ± SEM as noted in the figure legends.
