Ketamine for comorbid treatment-resistant depression and substance use disorders: balancing risks and opportunities
Authors: Benjamin D Brody, Dora Kanellopoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent retrospective analyses have shown that individuals with comorbid Substance Use Disorders (SUDs) are overrepresented among responders to ketamine for Treatment Resistant Depression (TRD). Ketamine is an effective treatment for depression and also a potential substance of misuse. The authors contrast a mechanistic pathway with a possible expectancy effect to account for these findings, and urge careful consideration of risks and benefits when considering ketamine for individuals with TRD and SUDs.Keywords: Ketamine, Treatment-resistant depression, Substance use disorders, Electroconvulsive therapy, Transcranial magnetic stimulation

To the Editor,

Walaszek et al.’s brief report [1], highlighting that individuals with treatment-resistant depression (TRD) and comorbid substance use disorders (SUDs) were over-represented among responders to ketamine therapy, replicates a trend [2] in our similar inpatient population. The discussion highlights ketamine’s multiple mechanistic pathways, and the authors’ hypothesis that “ketamine’s capacity to modulate glutamatergic dysregulation and normalize activity in the prefrontal and mesolimbic pathways central to SUD pathophysiology” may provide a neurobiological rationale for their findings. Recent work has demonstrated a shared underlying mechanism for the antidepressant effects of ketamine and electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), and emerging clinical evidence suggests the effectiveness of ECT and other neuromodulation techniques such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) in dual-diagnosis patients [3, 4]. If ECT and TMS are also effective in this population, these therapeutic options are important alternatives to ketamine, which is also a substance of misuse with attendant risks such as overdose, ketamine-induced cystitis, and neurotoxic effects.

Walaszek and colleagues also note an important limitation to their findings: that the greater prevalence of SUD comorbidity in ketamine responders “might also…reflect a sampling bias in patient recruitment.” We presume that they are referring to the possibility that TRD patients with a comorbid history of SUDs may be familiar with ketamine’s recreational use and biased towards substances with psychoactive properties. This self-selection bias could exert an expectancy effect that positively moderates the ketamine response. Randomized non-inferiority trials of ketamine versus other neuromodulation techniques in individuals with TRD and SUDs could disentangle the authors’ mechanistic hypothesis from the possible self-selection bias in retrospective cohort studies–but not from differential expectancy effects, as blinding subjects to the intervention remains unattainable.

We wish to highlight that careful patient selection and monitored ketamine administration are paramount to ensure that patients with comorbid TRD and SUDs receive safe treatment. Similar to experiences in Europe and Asia cited by Walaszek and colleagues, there are concerning trends around ketamine misuse in the United States (US). Although overdose deaths involving ketamine remain rare in the US, they doubled in a recent four-year period, from 47 deaths in 2019 (0.3% of all overdose deaths) to 107 deaths in 2023 (0.5% of all overdose deaths) [5]. Unlike esketamine, which has regulatory requirements for in-person administration and monitoring by a healthcare professional in the US and Europe, racemic ketamine can be prescribed directly to consumers and has no monitoring requirement. Thus, unsupervised ketamine treatment may present unique risks for populations with comorbid TRD and SUD: self-dose escalation, overdose risk, diversion, and potential for development of ketamine use disorder.

Given the global burden of disability caused by TRD and SUDs, there is a need for non-inferiority trials with safety outcomes comparing ketamine with other neuromodulation techniques. In the meantime, we urge careful evaluation of SUD remission status and in-person administration and monitoring for ketamine therapy to balance risks and opportunities in this vulnerable population.
