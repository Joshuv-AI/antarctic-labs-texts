Spectrum of Findings on Compressive Duplex Ultrasonography of the Lower Limbs in a Tertiary Hospital in Ghana: A Prospective Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Mary Kordei Oblitey, Jared Nii Oblitey, Kwame Yeboah, Samuel Asiamah, Yaw Boateng Mensah, Augustina Badu‐Peprah, Mark Tettey, Klenam Dzefi‐Tettey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective and AimsDeep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a potentially fatal disease that can be diagnosed with compression duplex ultrasound (CDUS). This study investigated the prevalence, anatomical distribution, and ultrasound characteristics of lower extremity DVT in suspected patients at a Ghanaian tertiary hospital.MethodsIn a prospective cross‐sectional study, CDUS was performed in the leg veins of 138 patients suspected of DVT. A structured questionnaire was used to collect data on socio‐demographics and possible risk factors.ResultsDVT was confirmed in 49 (35.5%) of 138 patients, with most thrombi (65.3%) located in the common femoral vein. Acute DVT was found in 19 (38.8%) patients and was more common among females than males (50% vs. 23.8%, p = 0.03), while chronic DVT was seen in 25 (51%) patients. In DVT patients, vessel abnormalities such as non‐compressibility were found in 41 (83.7%) patients, incomplete colour fill‐in in 43 (97.7%), loss of respiratory phasicity in 42 (95.5%) and absent augmentation in 42 (95.5%) patients. Previous history of DVT [OR (95% CI); 5.65 (3.53–16.8), p < 0.001] and varicose veins [3.89 (1.33–11.4), p = 0.028] were major risk factors associated with DVT diagnosis.ConclusionThe prevalence of DVT in our study population was 35.5% with no gender predilection. DVT was mostly chronic, and thrombus was mostly found within the common femoral vein. Varicose veins and a history of previous DVT increased the likelihood of DVT diagnosis. Comprehensive ultrasound screening, centred on proximal veins, especially in patients with varicose veins or a previous DVT, may be required for the diagnosis of DVT.Keywords: compression duplex ultrasound, deep vein thrombosis, lower extremities, thromboembolism, veins

Introduction

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is the presence of coagulated blood in any of the deep venous conduits that return blood to the heart [1]. The term DVT is often applied to thrombi in the lower limb veins, but the condition may also occur in the upper limb and neck veins. DVT, along with its associated complication, pulmonary embolism, comprise the third most common cause of cardiovascular disease (CVDs) globally [2]. DVT is associated with a high mortality rate, substantial healthcare costs, and a high rate of recurrence [3]. Accurate and early diagnosis of DVT is very critical for good patient management and survival. Radiological imaging methods used to confirm DVT include Doppler ultrasound, conventional venography, computed tomography venography and magnetic Resonance venography. A duplex ultrasound, the principal imaging modality for evaluating DVT, is very accurate, easy to perform, relatively inexpensive and safe. Ultrasonography assesses vessel compressibility, colour flow patterns, and spectral waveform patterns with respiratory phasicity and employs provocative manoeuvres such as calf compression and Valsalva [4].

Few studies have reported on DVT in sub‐Saharan African populations. In Ghana, only three studies have reported on DVT: young ambulant patients in a surgical unit [5] and two retrospective studies involving patients with pulmonary embolism in a cardiothoracic unit [6] and patients with unilateral lower limb swelling [7]. In this study, we investigated the prevalence and duplex ultrasound characteristics of patients suspected of DVT, as well as their associated risk factors.

Methods

Site, Design and Participants

The study was a cross‐sectional design conducted at the Ultrasound Unit of the Radiology Department, Korle Bu Teaching Hospital (KBTH), Accra, over 2 months. The KBTH has over 2000 beds and 17 clinical and diagnostic Departments. It has an average daily OPD attendance of 1500 patients and about 250 admissions. It serves as a tertiary centre for all the regions of Ghana as well as neighbouring African countries. Systematic sampling was used to recruit every 4th consenting patient who was referred to the Radiology Department for a Doppler ultrasound of the lower limb. Ethical approval for the study was given by the KBTH Institutional Review Board (KBTH‐IRB/00004/2019) and the West African College of Surgeons (EXM/PR/RAD/VOL.18/048). All the participants provided voluntary informed consent before being enroled in the study. Patients with hypercoagulable diseases such as sickle cell disease and connective tissue disorders, those already diagnosed and being treated for DVT and severely ill patients who could not be interviewed or provide voluntary consent were excluded from the study.

Covariate Measurement

A structured questionnaire was used to document the patient's age, level of education, employment, smoking and alcohol status. In addition, the following conditions were extracted from the referral notes or asked directly from the patients: previous history of DVT, recent surgery, malignancy, chronic renal disease, varicose veins, diabetes, hypertension, myocardial infarction (heart disease), immobility and history of hormone replacement therapy and oral contraceptives.

The weight and height of all ambulant patients were measured using a stadiometer, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the ratio between weight (kg) and height (m) squared. Obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. Blood pressure (BP) was measured using a semi‐automatic digital blood pressure monitor, and hypertension was described as BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg or the use of antihypertension medication.

Compression Duplex Ultrasound

Complete compression duplex ultrasound (CDUS) studies of the lower extremities were performed by a newly qualified radiologist under the supervision of three consultant radiologists with 10–15 years of experience. All examinations were performed using the 7.5‐MHz linear probe (Toshiba Medical Systems, Otawara, Tochigi, Japan) on a Toshiba Diagnostic Ultrasound System (Applio 300/TUSA300, 2013). The assessment was done from the inguinal ligament to the ankle (including posterior tibial and peroneal veins in the calf). Images of the right and left common femoral venous spectral Doppler waveforms (to evaluate symmetry), popliteal spectral Doppler and colour Doppler were analysed and documented at 2‐cm intervals.

B‐mode imaging was used with the ultrasound probe compressing the entire length of the lower extremity deep veins for direct visualisation of the intraluminal thrombus (Figure 1). A patent, thrombus‐free vein will demonstrate complete vein wall coaptation with compression by the transducer. Loss of compressibility of the vein was a reliable indicator of the presence of a thrombus within the vein. In addition to the loss of compressibility, acutely thrombosed veins were commonly dilated with a diameter greater than that of the adjacent artery (Figure 2). Intraluminal echoes consistent with thrombus were imaged. Colour‐flow Doppler was used to assess for residual flow within a thrombosed venous segment in nonocclusive DVT and to confirm the patency of venous segments that were not accessible for compression manoeuvres. Also, a pulsed Doppler spectral waveform from a normal, widely patent lower extremity vein demonstrated spontaneous and respirophasic flow. Alteration of this expected waveform raised suspicion of venous obstruction proximal to the level of interrogation (Figure 3). In addition to the assessment of respirophasicity, distal augmentation manoeuvres such as calf compression were performed during spectral Doppler evaluation to further demonstrate patency of the veins. A sharp “spike” of augmented anterograde venous flow was expected for distal augmentation for normal veins, with blunted or absent flow in augmentation indicating venous obstruction distal to the level being interrogated.

Results

A total of 138 suspected cases of DVT, comprising 56 (40.6%) males and 82 (59.4%) females, were recruited for this study. There was no significant difference in male and female participants in terms of age, weight, BMI, systolic and diastolic blood pressures, educational level, retirement status, employment status and household incomes. Male participants were generally taller and took more alcohol than females (Table 1). Out of the 138 participants evaluated for possible DVT, 35.5% (49 cases) were confirmed by CDUS with a similar proportion among male and female patients (χ 2 = 0.163, p = 0.686) (Figure 4).

Most of the patients diagnosed with DVT had thrombi located in the proximal leg veins. The commonest locations of thrombi were the common femoral vein, followed by the superficial femoral vein and popliteal vein. For all locations apart from the deep calf veins, thrombi were seen more on the left leg veins than on the right leg veins (Table 2). Also, thrombi were identified in a single vein in 11 (22.4%) patients, two veins in 14 (28.6%) patients, three veins in 3 (6.1%) patients, four veins in 18 (36.7%) patients and five veins in 3 (6.1%) patients.

The majority of thrombi detected by CDUS were hyperechoic, with 9 patients having calcified thrombi. The affected vessels were non‐compressible in 41 out of the 49 patients diagnosed with DVT. The veins were dilated in 20 (45.5%) participants, incomplete colour fill‐in on Doppler scans in 43 (87.7%) participants, loss of respiratory phasicity in 42 (85.7%) participants and absent augmentation in 42 (85.7%). Finally, there was non‐cessation of flow on Valsalva in 43 (87.7%) participants with DVT; 19 (44.2%) males and 24 (55.8%) females (Table 3). Based on the Doppler characteristics described above, patients with DVT were categorised into acute, chronic and indeterminate DVT. The acuity of the DVT was determined by the echogenicity of the thrombus on B‐mode imaging, vein lumen size, vein wall appearance, venous compressibility and function of the venous valves. Out of the 49 patients with DVT, 19 (38.8%) had acute DVT with higher female representation compared to males (23.8% vs. 50.0%, p = 0.03), 25 (51%) had chronic DVT with higher male representation compared to female participants (66.7% vs. 39.3%, p = 0.04) and 5 (10.2%) were indeterminate (Figure 5).

Discussion

The prevalence of DVT in this study was 35.5%, similar in male and female patients. This prevalence is slightly lower than that of a previous study by Olowoyeye et al. [8] in Nigeria, who reported a prevalence of 42.3% but similar to the findings of Baroncini et al. [9], who had a prevalence of 36.4% in Brazilian patients. Both studies had a similar design to the current study, a cross‐sectional hospital‐based design conducted in suspected patients, making the findings comparable. However, other studies conducted in Africa with a different design and patients reported a low prevalence of DVT. In Sudan, for example, a study conducted in 1974 reported a 9.6% prevalence of DVT, using 125I‐fibrinogen scanning, ultrasound and venography in postoperative prostatectomy patients [10]. Another study from Uganda reported a 5% prevalence of ultrasound‐diagnosed DVT among emergency and general surgical wards patients with a high female preponderance [11]. The prevalence of VTE in patients who underwent neurosurgical procedures in a study in Nigeria was documented as 2.4% [11]. The reason for low DVT prevalence in these studies could be that only one category of high‐risk patients was included, while the current study had patients with various risk factors who had already been triaged by their attending physicians and had an index of suspicion for DVT. In addition, some steps such as early mobilisation, anticoagulation, stocking and others may have been taken to prevent DVT.

Most DVTs diagnosed in this study showed signs of chronicity, such as hyperechoic thrombi and calcifications similar to the findings of Vululi et al., who reported a high prevalence of chronic DVT in Ugandan HIV patients (42.8% vs. 31.1%) [12]. In contrast with the findings, Olowoyeye et al [8] in Nigeria documented a higher prevalence of acute than chronic DVT (43.7% vs. 15.7%). This difference may be attributed to the lower sample size in that previous study, and likely because their patients sought earlier medical treatment.

In this current study, vessel wall non‐compressibility was recorded in 83.7% of all patients diagnosed with DVT, incomplete colour fill‐in in Doppler scans in 87.7% of patients, loss of respiratory phasicity in 85.7% of patients, lack of augmentation in 85.7% of patients, and non‐cessation of flow on Valsalva in 87.7% patients. The findings of this index study support the findings in the literature that vessel non‐compressibility, incomplete colour fill‐in, loss of respiratory phasicity, lack of augmentation and non‐cessation of flow on Valsalva are important clues to the diagnosis of DVT [13, 14, 15].

The current study found DVTs to be commonly involved in the CFV (65.3%), followed by the SFV (63.3%) and popliteal veins (59.2%). This is similar to results from other studies in Ghanaian [5, 6], Ugandan [12], Nigerian [16], and Brazilian [9] patients that reported thrombi to be mostly located within proximal lower limb veins; the common femoral and popliteal veins. Such results mean that sonographers must particularly focus on these locations in their search for DVT. It has been further shown that thrombi often begin intraoperatively, as confirmed by Doppler ultrasound scanning and venographic studies [17]. Thus, aggressive thrombolytic therapy shortly after surgery may help prevent complications. Unfortunately, such proximal DVTs are the ones most likely to cause PE, as shown in a study by Righini and Bounameaux (2008) in Swiss patients. In that study, four out of nine patients who developed proximal DVT detected by Doppler ultrasound scans had a subsequent PE diagnosed clinically, compared to none in 31 patients whose DVTs were diagnosed in the calf and either resolved or remained confined to the calf [18].

Varicose veins and a previous history of DVT were the significant risk factors among the patients evaluated in this study. Patients with varicose veins have been reported to have a threefold increase in the likelihood of developing DVT compared to those without varicose veins [19, 20]. These findings agree with recent large epidemiological studies, which found varicose veins as an important risk factor for thrombotic disorders [21]. Other studies including a cross‐sectional study on 2357 patients with varicose veins in Germany reported a sevenfold increase, another with 401 patients with DVT and 431 control patients aged 70 years or older reported a 1.6‐ to 10.5‐fold increase and a longitudinal study involving more than 420,000 participants reported 5.3 fold higher prevalence of DVT all pointing to the importance of varicose veins as a risk factor for DVT [19, 22, 23]. In this study, patients with a previous history of DVT also had a sevenfold increase in the proportion of DVT compared with those without a previous history of DVT. This is supported by Heit et al. [24], who reported a recurrence rate of around 30% within 10 years in patients in the United States. They thus suggested that VTE is a chronic disease with episodic recurrence.

Clinical and Public Health Implications

This study provides pilot data that could have both clinical and public health implications for DVT management in Ghana and similar settings [25]. Clinically, there is a need for more aggressive and comprehensive ultrasound screening, centred on proximal veins, especially in patients with varicose veins or a previous DVT. In addition, this study underscores the need to improve public and professional awareness, strengthen diagnostic capacity, implement effective hospital prophylaxis, and foster a context‐specific research agenda to combat the significant burden of DVT [26].

Limitations of the Study

This study is a cross‐sectional design and, hence, cannot be used to infer causality. Hence, a longitudinal study design may identify risk factors associated with DVT in Ghanaians. Also, the study was conducted in a single tertiary healthcare facility, and the conclusion from this study cannot be generalised to other healthcare facilities in Ghana. It would be interesting to know the prevalence of DVT in other healthcare strata in the Ghanaian community as well. The exclusion of hypercoagulable patients (e.g., sickle cell disease, HIV) might have led to DVT prevalence underestimation. Interobserver variability and reproducibility measures were not investigated. In addition, we did not measure biomarkers of coagulation, d‐dimers, which might have identified more patients with venous thrombosis. The odds ratio from the logistic regression analysis had wide confidence intervals for some variables, suggesting a small effect size. All the same, this study has shed insight into the prevalence, anatomical distribution and ultrasound characteristics of DVT in suspected patients in Ghana.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the prevalence of DVT in suspected patients in our study population is 35.5% with no gender difference in prevalence; most of the disease was chronic and in the large veins of the lower limbs. Comprehensive ultrasound screening, centred on proximal veins, especially in patients with varicose veins or a previous DVT, may be required for the diagnosis of DVT.
