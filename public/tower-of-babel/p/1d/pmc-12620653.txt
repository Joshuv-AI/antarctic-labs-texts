Strong selection maintains a narrow, stable tension zone between grosbeak species in the Great Plains
Authors: Devon A DeRaad, Jonah D Alderson, Claire O Geiman, Lucas H DeCicco, Robert G Moyle, Garth M Spellman, Stepfanie M Aguillon, Catherine Linnen, Tim Connallon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhen genetically divergent and geographically isolated lineages come back into contact, their interactions allow us to observe reproductive isolating barriers in action. The avian contact zone between Pheucticus melanocephalus and P. ludovicianus in the North American Great Plains has been studied for more than 60 years, but never with the aid of genomic data. Here, we generate reduced-representation genome sequence data from a transect in South Dakota spanning pure P. melanocephalus to pure P. ludovicianus. While the presence of recent-generation hybrids and backcrosses confirms the ongoing production of fertile F1 offspring, the narrow (48–61 km) and concordant geographic transitions in nuclear ancestry, mitochondrial ancestry, and male plumage phenotypes are hallmarks of a “tension zone,” where selection against hybrids is balanced by immigration of parental individuals. Additionally, the bimodal distribution of genomic ancestry in the heart of the contact zone and the highly significant association between genomic and mitochondrial ancestry both suggest a role for intrinsic genetic incompatibilities in the maintenance of this species boundary. Overall, we infer that strong selection maintains a narrow region of geographic contact between P. melanocephalus and P. ludovicianus in the Great Plains despite ongoing hybridization and introgression.Keywords: hybrid zone, contact zone, Great Plains, speciation, reproductive isolation

Introduction

Studying geographic regions where species tend to come into secondary contact following initial divergence in allopatry—“suture zones” (Wait & Peñalba, 2025)—continues to reveal the nature and strength of reproductive isolating mechanisms acting in natural systems (Barton & Hewitt, 1989; Harrison, 1993). It is critical to understand how reproductive isolation evolves between natural populations, because reproductive isolation allows for the emergence and persistence of morphological and ecological differentiation (Lenormand, 2002; Slatkin, 1987). Therefore, the evolution of barriers to reproduction between populations is arguably the most crucial step in the process of global biodiversity accumulation. Because of this global importance, evolutionary biologists leverage suture zones to study how naturally evolved reproductive isolating barriers maintain the boundaries between species (Arnold, 1993; Barton & Hewitt, 1985; Brumfield et al., 2001; Toews et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2020). And with the emergence and widespread adoption of massively parallel genomic sequencing over the last decade, researchers can now broadly survey the genomes of organisms across contact zones, offering unprecedented levels of detail and insight (Delmore et al., 2024; Peñalba et al., 2024). Today, these genomic datasets can be integrated with detailed phenotypic and organellar (e.g., mitochondrial or chloroplast) data, providing a powerful framework for understanding the dynamics of reproductive isolation, i.e., a window into the evolutionary mechanisms that maintain species boundaries in nature (Harrison, 1990; Taylor & Larson, 2019).

Studies of geographic contact between divergent lineages (i.e., contact zones) has an especially rich history in the North American Great Plains, which has long been recognized as a suture zone (Wait & Peñalba, 2025), where many previously isolated lineages come into secondary contact (Remington, 1968). Classic studies of avian hybridization in this region (e.g., Anderson, 1971; Lanyon, 1979; Mengel, 1970; Sibley & Short, 1959, 1964) laid the foundation for evolutionary biologists to understand the geographic and phenotypic dynamics of secondary contact, as reviewed in detail by Rising (1983). Additionally, these studies began to build toward our modern understanding that incomplete reproductive isolation and ongoing hybridization between divergent vertebrate species is common in nature (Harrison & Larson, 2014; Ottenburghs, 2023). More recently, many of these classic Great Plains contact zones have been revisited using modern genomic approaches, revealing widely varying levels of hybridization and genetic introgression between contacting lineages (Aguillon et al., 2021; Anderson et al., 2025; Dougherty & Carling, 2024; Semenov et al., 2023; Walsh et al., 2020). Yet, our understanding of how reproductive isolating barriers can vary among lineages despite a shared environmental context is still limited (Wait & Peñalba, 2025), highlighting the need for additional genomic case studies of secondary contact in the Great Plains.

One classic Great Plains avian contact zone occurs between sister species of grosbeaks, Pheucticus melanocephalus and P. ludovicianus (Barker et al., 2015; Pulgarín-R et al., 2013; Scott, 2022; estimated divergence time 1.5–2.5 MYA). These migratory species breed parapatrically across North America (Figure 1A), with an east–west contact zone where birds with intermediate phenotype can be found spanning from North Dakota through Nebraska (Anderson & Daugherty, 1974; Kroodsma, 1974; West, 1962). Recently, Mettler & Spellman (2009) used morphology and sequence data from the mitochondria and four nuclear genes to characterize this region as a tension zone, where selection against hybrids is balanced by immigration of parental individuals (Barton & Hewitt, 1989). Mettler & Spellman (2009) also found that this contact zone has remained geographically stable since at least the 1970s (and potentially as far back as the 1850s), suggesting strong selective pressures. Yet, the lack of genome-wide information left many questions unanswered. For instance, the nuclear genes sequenced by Mettler & Spellman (2009) were not strongly differentiated between the species, making comparisons of cline centers and widths across the contact zone challenging. Thus, important questions remain about the dynamics of genomic introgression and the role of natural selection in maintaining the Great Plains contact zone between these grosbeak species.

Here, we address some of these unresolved questions using a reduced-representation genomic sequencing approach on the same specimen-vouchered samples from Mettler & Spellman (2009) from a transect spanning the putative contact zone between P. melanocephalus and P. ludovicianus in South Dakota. Specifically, we tested whether geographic clines for ancestry and phenotype were narrow and concordant, as expected in a tension zone where natural selection acts against hybrid ancestry (Barton & Hewitt, 1985, 1989). We also tested for a bimodal distribution of genomic ancestry (Gay et al., 2008; Jiggins & Mallet, 2000; Semenov et al., 2017), which is expected only when selection against hybrids is strong or assortative mating is complete (Irwin, 2020). We also assessed the degree of concordance within individuals between genomic ancestry, mitochondrial ancestry, and phenotype. This concordance reveals whether natural selection maintains linkage between aspects of genotype and phenotype (Delmore et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2019), or whether novel combinations of traits are allowed to drift or even be favored by selection in the contact zone (Barton & Hewitt, 1989). Finally, we tested for hybrid dysgenesis (Martinez-Bengochea et al., 2022) in a key sexual feature (male testis size) across the transect. In sum, our integrative investigation of this North American Pheucticus hybrid zone provides a case study into the dynamics of reproductive isolation, which will serve as a building block in growing our understanding of the evolutionary mechanisms that maintain species boundaries.

Methods

Distribution and sample mapping

We used the R package ebirdst v3.2022.3 (Fink et al., 2023) to download the breeding occurrence data for P. melanocephalus and P. ludovicianus. We subset this data to include only breeding season occurrence data as determined by package developers (June 21, 2022–July 5, 2022 for P. melanocephalus, and June 14, 2022–August 2, 2022 for P. ludovicianus). We then visualized the occurrence density during the breeding period for both species and overlaid our genomic sampling localities to generate a single cohesive plot using R v4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2023). Note that the occurrence density shown in the map is based on direct observations, but is extrapolated using a variety of bioclimatic variables.

Phenotypic quantification

For a complete, detailed description of phenotypic quantification please see Mettler & Spellman (2009). To summarize, adult bird mass was measured in the field, while four morphological measurements: bill length, bill width, wing cord, and tarsus length, were taken in the lab. We then used the hybrid index scoring system of Anderson & Daugherty (1974) to classify five discrete plumage characters (nape, flank, back, rump, and breast) in each sample. Male phenotype was quantified for each individual by summing these five scores and dividing by the maximum possible score to generate a scaled 0–1 score, where a pure P. melanocephalus male would have a score of 1, while a pure P. ludovicianus male would have a score of 0. Second-year males were included with adults. Females were classified similarly but excluded from further phenotypic analysis due to small sample sizes and high variability.

Generating genomic data

We extracted genomic DNA using a magnetic bead-based protocol (Rohland & Reich, 2012) from 156 total samples collected from a South Dakota transect that spans a major contact zone between P. melanocephalus and P. ludovicianus and separate reference populations (Figure 1A, Table S1). 136 out of 138 samples from the transect (sites 1–11) were collected during a single field season, May 10, 2007–June 18, 2007 by GMS and collaborators (with a bias toward males, 129/138 samples). The samples from this transect are exclusively adult (i.e., after hatch year) birds on breeding territories, as evidenced by fully ossified skulls and territorial response to song playback. Additionally, we included populations geographically separate from the contact zone to ensure that we had pure parental “reference” genotypes: P. melanocephalus collected from throughout the state of Colorado (n = 9, site 0) and P. ludovicianus collected from the state of Kansas (n = 9, site 12; Table S1).

To generate genomic sequence data from these samples, each extract was normalized to a concentration of 10 ng/ul and used as input for restriction-enzyme associated DNA sequencing (RADseq) library preparation, performed at the University of Kansas Genomic Sequencing Core. RAD fragments were isolated following the protocol outlined in previous publications (DeRaad et al., 2023b, 2024). Briefly, genomic DNA extracts were digested with the enzyme NdeI, individually barcoded, and size selected to retain only fragments between 495 and 605 base-pairs. These libraries were then amplified via Polymerase Chain Reaction, bead-purified, and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 using P2 sequencing chemistry to generate ∼400 million single-end 100 base-pair reads. The mitochondrial haplotype assignments presented here come directly from Mettler & Spellman (2009), who generated mtDNA haplotype assignments for 135 out of the 138 samples from the South Dakota transect used in this project.

SNP calling and filtering

We used the Stacks v2.60 (Rochette et al., 2019) module “Process_Radtags” to demultiplex raw reads according to the unique sample barcodes. We then used fastp v0.23.4 (Chen et al., 2018) to assess data quality and trim the leading five base-pairs of each read to remove all remaining barcode and adapter sequence. We then mapped these trimmed reads to a P. melanocephalus reference genome assembly (Feng et al., 2020; GenBank assembly ID: GCA_013398405.1) using the command “mem” from the program bwa v0.7.17 (Li & Durbin, 2009). Preliminary analysis using an outgroup reference assembly for read mapping showed qualitatively similar results, so we performed all downstream analyses using reads mapped to the more closely related P. melanocephalus reference genome. We used SamTools v1.15 (Li et al., 2009) to sort the mapped reads, which were used as input for the Stacks modules “Gstacks” and “Populations,” which were executed with default parameters, generating an unfiltered SNP dataset containing 156 samples and 407,520 SNPs with 67.7% overall missing genotypes.

We filtered this Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) dataset using the R package SNPfiltR v1.0.2 (DeRaad, 2022) to remove potential technical artifacts and increase the overall signal to noise ratio of this dataset. We converted genotypes supported by less than three reads to missing and did the same for genotypes with a Phred-scaled quality score less than 25. We converted heterozygous genotypes with an allele balance outside of the range 0.15–0.85 to missing (Puritz et al., 2014). Next, SNPs with an average depth >250 were removed from the dataset (0.19% of the dataset; mean depth across all SNPs = 27.8), which are likely the result of overmapping paralagous reads onto a single genomic region. We then removed 17 samples that were missing genotype calls at >82% of all remaining SNPs, plus a single additional sample whose missing data proportion remained an outlier following per-SNP completeness filtering, leaving us with 138 total samples passing genomic filtering protocols. After testing various per-SNP completeness thresholds, we determined that a 90% per-SNP completeness threshold optimized the cutoff between data amount and quality, and visually confirmed that missing data was not driving patterns of sample clustering at this threshold. This left us with a filtered SNP dataset including 138 unique individuals, 51,595 SNPs, 2.99% overall missing data, and no individual missing genotype calls for >21.5% of all SNPs. Finally, we filtered this dataset for physical linkage, removing any SNPs separated by less than 1,000 base-pairs, to create an unlinked, filtered SNP dataset including 138 individuals, 3,584 SNPs, and 3.40% overall missing data.

Unsupervised approaches for assessing genetic relatedness

We used the filtered SNP dataset (51,595 SNPs) to construct an unrooted phylogenetic network displaying patterns of genomic relatedness among all sequenced samples. To do this, we converted the vcf file into a “genlight” object using the R package adegenet v2.1.1 (Jombart, 2008), and used the R package StAMPP v1.6.3 (Pembleton et al., 2013) to calculate Nei’s Genetic Distance (Nei, 1972) in a pairwise manner between all 138 samples. We visualized these pairwise distances as an unrooted phylogenetic network by applying the default NeighborNet method in SplitsTree v4.19.2 (Huson & Bryant, 2006). We also used our filtered SNP dataset as input for StAMPP, to calculate pairwise FST (following the formula of Weir & Cockerham, 1984) between the allopatric sampling localities (0 vs. 12).

We used the unlinked, filtered SNP dataset (3,584 SNPs) as input for the program ADMIXTURE v1.3.0 (Alexander et al., 2009), which takes an unsupervised approach to generating genomic ancestry assignments for each sample. The same analysis was repeated with singleton SNPs removed (2,464 SNPs remained), which has been shown to increase the signal to noise ratio in ancestry assignment exercises (Linck & Battey, 2019). In both of these iterations, we allowed the number of discrete ancestry bins (K) to vary from 1 to 10. Both iterations had an optimal K = 2, based on the lowest cross-validation error, and generated highly correlated results (Pearson’s r > 0.99 at K = 2). Here, we present the results from the dataset with singletons removed. To visualize the distribution of genomic ancestry, we used R to generate ancestry (i.e., q-value from ADMIXTURE) histograms, both for the entire sampling transect and for the core of the contact zone where maximum admixture could occur (sites 8–9). For each of these distributions, we tested whether we could reject a null hypothesis of unimodality using Hartigan’s Dip Tests of Unimodality (Hartigan & Hartigan, 1985) implemented via the R package diptest v0.77–1 (Martin, 2013), with p-value computed via linear interpolation.

Generating geographic clines

To assess the geographic transition between our two focal species across the contact zone in South Dakota, we fit sigmoidal clines to a linearized version of our sampling transect. To do this, we followed the approach outlined in detail by Aguillon & Rohwer (2022). In brief, we began by creating a linearized transect using the mean latitude of sites 1–11. We then designated site 1 as 0 km (i.e., the beginning of the linear transect) and estimated the linear distance along the transect by calculating the distance between this point and the mean longitude of all samples included at each of the subsequent sampling sites (2–11), using the function “distm” from the geosphere v1.5–20 R package (Hijmans et al., 2017). We fit equilibrium sigmoidal cline models (Gay et al., 2008; Szymura & Barton, 1986) using the mean value of all samples at a given sampling locality (1–11) as input for the “nls” R function, following the formula outlined by Aguillon & Rohwer (2022). We repeated this procedure for mitochondrial ancestry, nuclear ancestry, and phenotype, each time calculating 95% confidence intervals for the estimated model parameters of cline center (c) and cline width (w), using the R function “confint.”

Assessing the relationship between mitochondrial ancestry, nuclear ancestry, and phenotype

Because ancestry assignments alone (e.g., from ADMIXTURE) cannot distinguish between isolation by distance and hybridization (Wiens & Colella, 2025), we also assessed heterozygosity at SNP genotypes putatively fixed for different alleles between the two parental species. This heterozygosity information can reveal the presence of specific hybrid classes (e.g., F1, backcross) and give general insights into the dynamics (i.e., general timing) of hybridization for a given group. We used the R package triangulaR v0.0.1 (Wiens et al., 2025) to identify 266 ancestry informative SNPs that were fixed between the allopatric sampling localities (sites 0 and 12) from our filtered SNP dataset (51,595 total SNPs). We then used these 266 fixed SNP genotypes to construct a triangle plot and color-coded each sample according to its assigned mitochondrial haplotype from Mettler & Spellman (2009).

To analyze the relationship between genotype and phenotype on a per-sample basis, we constructed a linear model using the R function “lm.” As input, we used the q-values from ADMIXTURE as the independent variable and the total male phenotype score (normalized to a 0–1 scale) as the response variable. We visualized the line of best fit on top of a scatterplot for these two variables using the R package ggplot2 v3.5.2 (Wickham et al., 2020) and used the output p-value to test the null hypothesis that the true slope of this line = 0. To test for an association between mitochondrial ancestry and major parent ancestry (i.e., the species assigned > 0.5 q-value at K = 2), we constructed a 2 × 2 contingency table and performed a Pearson’s Chi-squared test using the R function “chisq.test.”

Because testicular development is a crucial aspect of fitness and fertility (Barone et al., 1994; Penfold et al., 2022), we used this sampling transect to test for an association between hybrid ancestry and male testis size. For each adult male bird collected, the length and width of the left and right testis were measured to the nearest millimeter using a standard ruler at the time of specimen preparation (soon after collection). We then multiplied the length × width to get an estimated area for each testis and added the area of the left and right testis to calculate “testis area” for each individual. To test if collection date (a potential confounding variable) could explain the variation in testis area, we constructed a linear model with collection date as the independent variable and testis area as the response variable. Because there was no significant linear relationship between these variables (Figure S1), we then fit a linear model to test whether minor parent ancestry (i.e., the extent of genomic admixture measured from a minimum of 0 to a maximum of 0.5) was associated with testis area.

Results

We first constructed maps of the breeding distributions of P. melanocephalus and P. ludovicianus using occurrence data from the community science platform eBird (Figure 1A). The projected ranges revealed little apparent overlap in breeding distributions and a sharp transition in central South Dakota between the two species, which is spanned by our sampling transect (Figure 1A inset). To assess overall patterns of nuclear relatedness across this transect, we constructed an unrooted phylogenetic network using all 51,595 SNPs that passed per-SNP quality filtering (Figure 1B). This network revealed two moderately divergent sample clusters corresponding to the two focal species (FST between allopatric sites = 0.26), with a handful of intermediate samples emerging directly from the internal branch connecting the two species. These intermediate samples were derived exclusively from central South Dakota, mostly from the apparent heart of the contact zone (sites 6–9). Mitochondrial ancestry assignments from Mettler & Spellman (2009) were almost perfectly correlated with major parent nuclear ancestry, based on sample placement in the phylogenetic network.

We used the program ADMIXTURE to assign ancestry proportions to all 138 samples (Figure 2A) based on the optimal number of ancestry bins (K = 2; Figure S2). As expected, no samples from the allopatric sampling sites (site 0 and site 12) showed evidence of shared genomic ancestry. Samples with intermediate ancestry were found almost exclusively at sites 6–9 (Figure 2A), in the heart of our South Dakota transect, confirming results from the phylogenetic network. Histograms of nuclear ancestry proportion among all samples (Figure 2B) and from the center of the hybrid zone (sites 8–9, Figure 2C; sites 7–9, Figure S3) both revealed significantly bimodal ancestry distributions (p < 0.0001). These bimodal distributions suggest that adult male hybrids are rare, even in areas where these taxa co-occur with ample opportunity for interbreeding.

To understand the geographic dynamics of this contact zone, we linearized our sampling transect across South Dakota and fit sigmoidal clines for nuclear ancestry, mitochondrial ancestry, and male phenotype. The resulting clines revealed that all three of these traits transition rapidly from predominantly P. melanocephalus to predominantly P. ludovicianus between sampling sites 8 and 9 (Figure 3) and cannot be statistically differentiated in center or width.

Next, we generated a triangle plot (Figure 4A) to visualize the assignment of hybrid samples relative to the theoretical expectations of various hybrid classes (e.g., F1, F2, backcross, etc.). In this plot we identified nine individuals (6.5% of included samples) with heterozygosity >0.25 across diagnostic SNPs, suggesting their ancestry is derived from hybridization within the last three generations. We also observed a large number of individuals displaying a small proportion of admixed ancestry shared via introgression following many generations of directional backcrossing. A contingency table demonstrates that the strong concordance between major parent ancestry and mitochondrial haplotype visible in the triangle plot is also highly significant (p < 0.0001; Figure 4B). We also identified a highly significant relationship between nuclear ancestry and male plumage phenotype (p < 0.0001; Figure 4C). Finally, we tested for evidence of hybrid dysgenesis by searching for an association between male testis size and genomic ancestry. This revealed no significant association (p = 0.7956; Figure 4D), suggesting that male individuals we sampled developed similarly sized testes regardless of their degree of genome-wide admixture.

Discussion

Here we build on foundational work in the classic Great Plains contact zone between P. melanocephalus and P. ludovicianus (Anderson & Daugherty, 1974; Kroodsma, 1974; Mettler & Spellman, 2009; West, 1962) by integrating mitochondrial, genomic, and phenotypic data for the first time to reveal the dynamics of secondary contact. These dynamics have important implications for understanding how species boundaries (and therefore biodiversity at large) are maintained despite secondary contact (Harrison, 1993). Between these divergent species, we revealed the existence of recent-generation hybrids and backcrossed individuals, confirming the ongoing production of fertile F1 hybrid offspring. Yet, narrow geographic clines indicate rapid and concordant transitions in mitochondrial ancestry, nuclear ancestry, and male phenotype; hallmarks of a classic tension zone (Barton & Hewitt, 1985, 1989). Further, we identified a bimodal distribution of ancestry in the heart of the contact zone, suggesting high dispersal and strong selection against hybrids (Gay et al., 2008; Harrison & Bogdanowicz, 1997; Jiggins & Mallet, 2000), because even strong but imperfect assortative mating alone cannot maintain a bimodal ancestry distribution over evolutionary timespans (Irwin, 2020). Importantly, this bimodal distribution refutes the “bounded superiority hypothesis” (Barton & Hewitt, 1989), which predicts a unimodal distribution of intermediate ancestry in the center of the contact zone when hybrid genotypes are favored by natural selection. Here, the lack of particular hybrid classes, including late generation hybrids (i.e., individuals in the center of the triangle in Figure 4A) and individuals with mismatched ancestry between their nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, suggests strong selective mechanisms preventing their creation and/or development (e.g., Moran et al., 2024). Yet, the nonsignificant relationship between hybrid ancestry and testis size among adult males suggests that infertility of hybrid males is not the cause of these “missing classes” of hybrids. Here, we identify plausible explanations for the genetic and phenotypic patterns we observe across this hybrid transect and use these patterns to infer the mechanisms of natural selection acting to maintain species boundaries between these naturally hybridizing taxa.

Evaluating the evidence for reproductive isolation and selection against hybrids

Foundational research based on the careful examination of hundreds of museum specimens, suggested that contact between P. melanocephalus and P. ludovicianus was moderate in Nebraska (West, 1962), limited in South Dakota (Anderson & Daugherty, 1974), and nearly nonexistent in North Dakota (Kroodsma, 1974). This latitudinal variation in contact zone width suggests that habitat availability may be an important factor determining the extent of geographic overlap (and therefore mixing) between these species. Yet, all three of these studies concurred that ecology alone could not explain how geographically narrow the zone of contact is between these continentally distributed species. This nascent concept was later formalized as the “tension zone model” (Barton & Hewitt, 1985; Key, 1968), which suggests that contact zones in nature can reach stable equilibria because of a balance between migration and selection. This balance occurs because while migration into the zone by pure parental individuals promotes mixture, natural selection against hybrid offspring maintains isolation between the lineages and prevents secondary contact zones from continuously expanding as expected under a model of pure neutrality (Barton & Hewitt, 1989).

Here, we tested the key empirical prediction of the tension zone hypothesis that geographic clines should be narrow and concordant. We first confirmed the results of Mettler & Spellman (2009), who used a handful of genetic markers to reveal that contact between P. melanocephalus and P. ludovicianus was both geographically restricted and temporally stable. We furthered these results by confirming that the transition in nuclear ancestry occurs in just ∼61 km (95% CI: 37–91 km), representing <1.5% of the >4,000 km wide combined ranges of these species across North America. Direct dispersal distance estimates are not available for these species, but with breeding and wintering grounds separated by up to 4,000 km, these annual migrants are undoubtedly capable of traversing this ∼61 km transition zone with ease. Therefore, this temporally stable (Mettler & Spellman, 2009) and narrow transition zone cannot be explained by a neutral model, which predicts continuous hybrid zone expansion in highly dispersive organisms (Barton & Hewitt, 1989). Further, the concordance between geographic clines from nuclear ancestry, mitochondrial ancestry, and male phenotype suggest that a strong selective mechanism is acting to maintain the coinheritance of these traits (Lopez et al., 2021) and prevent geographic displacement (e.g., Del-Rio et al., 2022; DeRaad et al., 2023a; Dufresnes et al., 2024). Next, we discuss whether patterns of coinheritance between the nuclear genome, mitochondrial genome, and phenotype across this contact zone are concordant with selective mechanisms identified in well-studied contact zones from other taxa.

Evidence for intrinsic incompatibilities maintaining this tension zone

Although the contact zone between P. melanocephalus and P. ludovicianus has not yet been studied in enough detail to generate direct estimates of fitness from the field (e.g., Chen et al., 2016) or perform crossing experiments (e.g., Lanyon, 1979), patterns of coinheritance between mitochondrial and nuclear loci alone can provide powerful insights into the nature of reproductive isolating mechanisms (e.g., Lopez et al., 2021). For instance, here we observe a distinct lack of late generation (i.e., F2 and beyond) hybrid individuals. One potential explanation for this pattern is that mating between F1 hybrids is rare. This could be due to disassortative mating (i.e., F1 hybrid preference for pure partners), or because F1 individuals occur at such low density that it is simply unlikely for two F1s to form a pair. However, disassortative mating seems unlikely considering that the only information we have on mate choice in the hybrid zone suggests weakly assortative mating based on phenotypic characteristics (Anderson & Daugherty, 1974). An alternative (potentially complementary) explanation for these missing late-generation hybrids is that intrinsic genetic incompatibilities between P. melanocephalus and P. ludovicianus manifest in the F2 generation and beyond (e.g., Moran et al., 2024), as expected under a multilocus incompatibility model (Dobzhansky, 1982; Muller, 1942). Multilocus genetic incompatibilities resulting in developmental or fertility issues in late generation hybrids have now been documented in a growing suite of natural systems (Aguillon et al., 2025; Arce-Valdés et al., 2025; Dufresnes et al., 2021; Yuzon et al., 2023), and would provide a plausible explanation for the missing class of hybrids observed here.

In addition to this missing class of later generation hybrids, we also observed a significant deficit of mitonuclear mismatch across the sampling transect (Figure 4B). This pattern, when observed in previous studies (Lopez et al., 2021; Moran et al., 2024; Pritchard & Edmands, 2013), has been interpreted as evidence for the role of mitonuclear incompatibilities in maintaining tension zones. Both theory (Burton, 2022; Orr, 1997; Toews & Brelsford, 2012) and data from natural systems (Hill, 2017; Nikelski et al., 2023) have suggested that mitochondria and sex chromosomes are hotspots for multilocus genetic incompatibilities because deleterious variants are more likely to be expressed compared with regions of the genome that are always inherited in a diploid state. Here, we identify patterns within the natural contact zone between P. melanocephalus and P. ludovicianus concordant with the inviability of both late generation hybrids and “mitodiscordant” (McDiarmid et al., 2024) backcrosses. These patterns could be parsimoniously explained by multilocus genetic incompatibilities involving the mitochondria and/or sex chromosomes, but further work will be required to validate this hypothesis and pinpoint the causal loci involved in this putative incompatibility.

Conclusions

Here, we studied the incomplete, but highly effective, reproductive isolating barriers between P. melanocephalus and P. ludovicianus across an instance of contact in the North American Great Plains. We identified individuals from the contact zone with recent hybrid ancestry, but confirmed that hybrids are rare and restricted to a narrow geographic area, conforming to the expectations of a tension zone model with strong selection against hybrid offspring (Barton & Hewitt, 1989). Patterns of ancestry within the contact zone hint at a role for multilocus genetic incompatibilities involving the mitochondria as a plausible mechanism for maintaining the geographically narrow zone of contact between these interfertile species. These genomic patterns support previous specimen-based studies, which found evidence that phenotypically intermediate grosbeaks experienced lower annual survivorship than phenotypically pure individuals from either parental species, potentially due to unfavorable intermediate migratory routes (Rohwer et al., 2023). Future field-based studies will be necessary to disentangle the relative roles of ecological versus intrinsic genetic mechanisms in generating the apparently strong natural selection pressure against hybrid grosbeaks that we infer here based on patterns of genomic ancestry. Ultimately, our genomic investigation of this grosbeak hybrid zone contributes to the growing recognition of the role postmating reproductive isolating barriers play in maintaining species boundaries and shaping patterns of global biodiversity.

Data availability

All analyses presented here are also presented in a thoroughly documented GitHub repository (https://github.com/DevonDeRaad/grosbeak.rad.hybridtransect). Readers are encouraged to use the links on the homepage of the repository to view html documents detailing the exact step by step processes used in all analyses including additional visualizations of intermediate steps and reproducible code chunks. The unfiltered, filtered, and filtered unlinked vcf files, as well as the phenotypic scores and mitochondrial haplotype information can all be downloaded here: https://github.com/DevonDeRaad/grosbeak.rad.hybridtransect/tree/main/data, meaning that the results presented in this manuscript can be reproduced and validated by readers without constraint. This entire GitHub repository is also archived with a permanent DOI, via Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.16990209). The raw DNA sequence data are available through the NCBI BioProject PRJNA1298969 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/PRJNA1298969).
