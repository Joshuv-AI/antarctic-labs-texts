Asymmetrical polyimide membranes with programmable polymer chain architectures for liquid hydrocarbon fractionation
Authors: Weilin Feng, Fupeng Li, Jiaqi Li, Zhiyi Li, Lu Xu, Hukang Guo, Nanwen Li, Xinzhong Cao, Chuanjie Fang, Baoku Zhu, Liping Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Conventional fractionation of liquid hydrocarbons relies on energy-intensive distillation. While organic solvent reverse osmosis provides an energy-efficient alternative, the challenge lies in engineering membranes with accurately tailored molecular differentiation for complex hydrocarbons. Here, we develop diverse fluorinated polyimide membranes featuring programmable polymer chain architectures for efficient hydrocarbon separation. By stoichiometry-controlled polycondensation, the chain packing and microporosity of synthesized polyimides are finely regulated, verified by molecular simulations. The corresponding asymmetrical membranes with defect-free thin layers of 100 to 250 nanometers are prepared via solution casting and thermal annealing steps. Such programmed membranes enable tunable permselectivity for hydrocarbons with less than 40 carbon atoms. The fractionation of kerosene-paraffin mixture in toluene is demonstrated through a two-stage process containing the optimized membranes. The cascade process remarkably enriches the C10-C13 hydrocarbons from 50% up to 97%. The demonstrated polyimide membranes with on-demand molecular discrimination capability provide a potential candidate for the membrane-based hydrocarbon fractionation.

INTRODUCTION

Hydrocarbon fractionation serves as the cornerstone of modern petrochemical industries, transforming multicomponent feedstocks into mission-critical products spanning energy carriers (e.g., diesel or kerosene) and basic chemicals for polymer synthesis (1, 2). Yet, this foundational process is overwhelmingly relied on energy-intensive distillation processes, consuming more than 3.9 × 1018 joule annually (equivalent to 1% of the world’s energy usage) (3). Developing the next-generation separation techniques with energy-saving advantages has emerged as an urgent need, empowering sustainable refinement of these hydrocarbon mixtures into high-value derivatives.

Membrane technology, refined through over a century of development, is considered an attractive alternative to the thermal-driven separation processes. Its phase change–free nature permits up to 90% energy savings compared to conventional methods (4). As a specialized subset targeting organic systems, organic solvent reverse osmosis (OSRO) separates nonaqueous liquids through molecular sizes and interaction disparities, potentially addressing the fractionation of liquid hydrocarbons (5–7). However, separating viscous and complex hydrocarbon mixtures requires on-demand solvent-resistant membranes. The rapid liquid permeation and precisely adjustable molecular selectivity are core indicators of membranes to effectively partition the multicomponent hydrocarbons into valuable narrow-cut fractions, like light kerosene and heavy paraffin (8). These requirements fundamentally rely on constructing microporous channels with subnanometer precision customization.

Recently, OSRO technologies have achieved partial success in hydrocarbon separation through the utilization of solution-processable polymers, including polytriazoles, spirocyclic polymers, and fluorinated polyamides (9–13). However, the precise modulation of membrane selectivity has still been ameliorated, where concise and chemically intuitive regulation strategies rooted in fundamental molecular architectures remain critically underdeveloped.

Here, we report a molecular engineering strategy enabling precise control over microporous architectures of polyimide membranes through manipulating structural unit ratios. The polyimides were synthesized from 4,4′-(hexafluoroisopropylidene) diphthalic anhydride (6FDA) with varied diamine ratios of 3,5-diaminobenzoic acid (DABA) and 2,4,6-trimethyl-1,3-diaminobenzene (DAM). The asymmetrical polyimide membranes were fabricated via an optimized nonsolvent-induced phase separation (NIPS) protocol coupled with controllable thermal treatment (Fig. 1A) (14–16). The engineered membranes featuring thin fluorinated selective layers demonstrated exceptional permeability toward nonpolar solvents (e.g., toluene and hexane). Substantially, these polyimide membranes exhibited tunable molecular selectivity within the molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) ranging from 350 to 530 Da. This programmable structural regulation and scalability reveal the possibility of integration of membranes in industrial hydrocarbon fractionation processes.

RESULTS

The 6FDA-DABA/DAM polyimides were synthesized via a two-step condensation protocol following established methodologies (fig. S1) (17, 18). Systematic control of copolymer composition was achieved by varying the feed molar ratio of DABA to DAM diamines (4:0, 3:1, 2:2, 1:3, and 0:4). The resulting polyimides were designated as AXMY, where A and M denoted DABA and DAM, while X and Y represented their molar ratios, respectively. Comprehensive characterizations confirmed the successful synthesis and chemical structures of polyimides. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) revealed high molecular weights for the series, with weight-average molecular weights (Mw) ranging from 82 to 125 kDa and number-average molecular weights (Mn) exceeding 42 kDa (table S1). The Mn supports near-quantitative monomer conversion (~99%) analyzed by Carothers equation. The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy observed the disappearance of amide absorption peaks (1650 and 1550 cm−1) and the emergence of characteristic carbonyl stretch peaks of imide groups at 1728 and 1786 cm−1, demonstrating the complete imidization of polyamic acid (PAA; fig. S2A). 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis further validated specific structures. The spectra showed three chemical shifts at 7.32, 2.13, and 1.91 parts per million (ppm), corresponding to the aromatic (labeled a) and benzyl protons (labeled b and c) in DAM units, respectively. The broad peaks between 12 and 14 ppm confirmed the carboxyl protons (labeled d) in DABA units (Fig. 1B and fig. S3). The systematic enhancement of a-c peaks and attenuation of d peaks correlated with increasing DAM content, providing spectroscopic validation of composition control.

The dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) uncovered a composition-dependent glass transition temperature (Tg), which increased linearly from 328°C (A4M0) to 409°C (A0M4) with DAM content (Fig. 1C). This trend is attributed to the restricted backbone mobility induced by DAM units with sterically hindered methyl groups (19, 20). Collectively, these results confirm the stoichiometric engineering of polymer chains, which substantially governs the rheological modulation of the polyimide series.

Asymmetrical membranes were fabricated from the synthesized polyimides (A4M0, A3M1, A2M2, and A1M3) using the NIPS method. N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF) solutions of these polyimides were cast onto glass plates and subsequently immersed in a coagulation bath (70 vol % deionized water and 30 vol % DMF) to induce phase inversion. Notably, different polyimides effortlessly generated porous membranes (designated AXMY-Pre), confirming the excellent processability across the DABA:DAM ratio spectrum (Fig. 1D and fig. S4). To endow the defect-free selective layers, the nascent membranes were thermally treated under vacuum conditions, yielding modified membranes labeled AXMY-M. The thermal treatment was performed at temperatures approaching the Tg of polyimides, consistent with our prior methodology (14–16). The corresponding scanning electron microscopy images revealed distinct morphological evolution. The membranes containing lower DAM content (A4M0, A3M1, and A2M2) underwent complete surface reconstruction at 330°C, generating pinhole-free selective layers (Fig. 1D and figs. S5 to S7). For DAM-rich A1M3 with the Tg up to 396°C, the annealing temperature was moderately elevated to 360°C to effectively eradicate the residual surface defects (fig. S8).

Positron Doppler broadened energy spectroscopy (DBES) examined the pore features in the thermally reconstructed skin layer (21, 22). Positron implantation depth was controlled by the incident energy between 0 and 30 keV. In DBES, the counts between 364.2 and 496.2 keV originate from 3γ annihilation in macropores (nanometer to micrometer scale), while the 504.35 to 517.65 keV region reflects 2γ annihilation in free-volume elements (angstrom to nanometer scale). Consequently, the 3γ annihilation ratio (i.e., R parameter) probes the macrovoid distribution (21). As shown in Fig. 1E, the nascent asymmetrical A4M0-Pre membranes showed an R parameter of ~0.47 at positron energy below 2.00 keV (corresponding to detected depth <84 nm), whereas both thermal-restructured A4M0-M membranes and solution-cast A4M0 films demonstrated congruent R parameter trends, further decreasing to ~0.46. This indicates complete densification of the thermally restructured skin layer, structurally analogous to nonporous polymer matrices. The R parameter of A4M0-M membranes stayed at 0.46 up to 3.00 keV before gradually rising to 0.47, confirming a dense layer (~160 nm thick) overlying the porous sublayer.

The cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy observation further verified the formation of defect-free selective layers with approximate thickness varying from 111 to 238 nm (Fig. 1F and fig. S9). The blurry boundaries suggested the interpenetrating macrovoid substructures, facilitating the mechanical strength of membranes under high operation pressure. The structural evolution of membranes arises from spatially varied chain mobility. The bulk regions with Tg exceeding annealing temperatures maintain porous sponge structures through restricted chain motion, while surface-adjacent chains exhibit enhanced mobility and creep for the pore closure in the region of the skin layer. Such regulation of asymmetric structures coordinates molecular sieving capability with enhanced liquid permeation, overcoming the trade-off effect in membrane-based separations.

Although macroscopically indistinguishable, these membranes exhibited microporous structure divergences resulting from the molecular architectures of 6FDA-DABA/DAM polyimides and, more basically, the diametrically opposed effects of DABA and DAM moieties on aggregation states. The simulation calculation was applied to elucidate the relationship between chemical structures and micropores. First, the quantum chemical potential energy scans were carried out to explain the monomer difference in the benzoimide-phenylene torsional angles θ (Fig. 2A) (23–25). For the 6FDA-DAM segment, the ortho/para-methyl groups adjacent to the N-phenylene bond imposed severe steric hindrance, showing torsional energy barriers of ~30 kcal mol−1, over 12 times greater than that of substituent-free 6FDA-DABA segment (<2.5 kcal mol−1) (Fig. 2B). To illustrate this molecular-scale constraint with chain rigidity, we coquantified the torsional angle θ and distance between two bridging nitrogens on the terminal dianhydrides d (Fig. 2A) (26–28). The spatial distribution density maps of θ and d revealed that the 6FDA-DAM fragment exhibited a concentrated distribution between 17 and 22 Å, indicative of locked conformation architectures (Fig. 2C). Whereas 6FDA-DABA fragments span 16 to 25 Å with multimodal peaks, indicating their higher flexibility from a lower energy barrier. The distinct flexibility contrast between 6FDA-DABA and 6FDA-DAM establishes DAM moieties as rigid molecular skeletons, while DABA counterparts enhance conformational adaptability, positioning the stoichiometric ratio as a molecular design lever to program the chain stacking. Computational modeling of dry-state polyimides implied that methyl-induced steric hindrance in DAM moieties confines the imide linkage dihedrals predominantly to 60° (figs. S10 to S13) (29–35). This imposes geometric constraints and locks chains into staggered conformations through restricted torsional relaxation. Consequently, the increase of DAM fractions reduced the polyimide density from 1.47 (A4M0) to 1.33 g cm−3 (A1M3) while expanding the pore gate size, defined as the diameter of the biggest sphere that can percolate through the models, from 1.88 Å (A4M0) to 1.96 Å (A1M3) (figs. S14 and S15) (36).

The void analysis with probe diameters of 1.7, 3.3, and 3.64 Å visualized the ultramicropores in polyimide models (37, 38). The A4M0 model contained isolated ultramicroporous voids with restricted accessibility, contrasting with the interconnected pores in A1M3 (above in Fig. 2D and figs. S16 to S18). The calculated pore size distributions confirmed this modulation of DAM moieties on the pore architectures. A4M0 displayed a narrower pore distribution with the most common peak at 3.0 Å. The DAM incorporation broadened the pore distribution to 12 Å and shifted the dominant pore size to 3.7 Å of A1M3 (Fig. 2E and figs. S19 and S20). Toluene swelling simulations further elucidated the aggregation states of polymers in solvent (below in Fig. 2D) (39). All polyimide models exhibited pronounced swelling behavior with reduced density (fig. S21). The pore size distributions of swollen polyimides broadened substantially to 1 to 15 Å (Fig. 2E). Nevertheless, the ultramicropores below 7 Å persisted prominently, constituting 39 to 58% of total porosity in different swelled polyimides. Crucially, the compositionally programmed polymer chains persistently governed the swollen porosity. As the DAM moieties increased, the pore size distributions systematically shifted toward larger dimensions, concurrently with the enhanced pore connectivity (Fig. 2, D and E, and figs. S22 to S25). Moreover, deviations between dry-state and swollen-state pore dimensions intensified with decreasing DABA moieties. For the swollen A4M0 model, the pore gate size expanded to 4.0 Å, whereas A1M3 exhibited a more marked swelling-induced expansion to 6.7 Å (fig. S26). It is suggested that the 6FDA-DABA units with carboxyl groups promote strong interchain cohesion in the swelled state, attributing to the preservation of ultramicroporous voids. The minimal mass change of A4M0-Pre and A4M0-M membranes in toluene immersion tests validated these simulation results (fig. S27) (40).

The microporosity of polyimide membranes was further investigated through gas sorption and x-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements. The membrane with a higher DAM content demonstrates an enhanced CO2 sorption capacity, indicative of a higher microporosity (Fig. 2F). The density functional theory (DFT) calculations quantitatively revealed a linear correlation between the surface areas and DAM ratios, with values increasing from 89.1 m2 g−1 (A4M0-M) to 131.3 m2 g−1 (A1M3-M). XRD spectra exhibited broad amorphous halos for the polyimide membranes, with calculated d-spacing indirectly reflecting the chain packing density in polyimide matrix. Critically, the rigid DAM moieties induced a monotonic d-spacing expansion from 5.4 to 6.5 Å, suggesting a systematic correlation between chemical composition and interchain spacing (Fig. 2H) (17, 18). The combination of theoretical and experimental investigation conclusively demonstrates that the ratio adjustment of DABA and DAM achieves the programmable microporosity, benefiting in precisely tailoring membrane selectivity for molecular separation.

The thermal behaviors of polyimide membranes were characterized. The membranes exhibited partial decarboxylation and interchain covalent crosslinking during thermal treatment due to the DABA moieties with carboxyl groups. Infrared spectroscopy confirmed this transformation via attenuated intensity at 1400 cm−1 assigned to in-plane hydroxyl deformation in carboxyl acid dimers. Complementary evidence from thermogravimetric analysis showed reduced mass loss between 400° and 500°C, indicative of diminished decarboxylation (figs. S2 and S28) (18, 41, 42). The crosslinked networks consequently produced membranes with substantial gel fractions (fig. S29). The membranes displayed negligible variations in CO2 adsorption profiles and XRD patterns before and after thermal treatment, giving evidence of the reservation and consistency of micropore architectures (figs. S30 to S32). This structural robustness originates from the exceptional thermal stability of the polyimide backbones, which restricts chemical transformations primarily to the pendant carboxyl groups.

The programmed polyimide membranes feature thermally reconstructed dense layers and the molecularly engineered micropores, enabling precise molecular selectivity coupled with rapid liquid transport for hydrocarbon separations. To validate membrane integrity under the frequently used chemical separation medium, the separation performance was evaluated through filtration in typical solvents like ethanol and toluene. As shown in Fig. 3A, the membranes exhibit substantial solvent permeance (e.g., 12.9 liters m−2 hour−1 bar−1 for methanol in A1M3-M membranes). The permeance values range with DAM content, following the order of A4M0-M < A3M1-M < A2M2-M < A1M3-M. Critically, for toluene transport in different membranes, the thickness-normalized permeances exhibited a positive correlation with the swollen surface area, demonstrating that DAM-induced higher microvoid concentration intrinsically enhances the polyimide permeability (fig. S34).

The mechanistic analysis further correlates solvent permeance with solvent viscosity (η), Hansen solubility parameter (δ), and molecular diameter (dm), as plotted in Fig. 3B, fig. S35, and table S2. All membranes deviated notably from the phenomenological transport model, as permeance showed negligible dependence on δ η−1 dm−2 (43, 44). Instead, solvent transport predominantly followed the pore-flow mechanism, evidenced by the inverse correlation between permeance and viscosity for the A3M1-M, A2M2-M, and A1M3-M membranes (9, 45–49). In addition, A4M0-M membranes exhibited a deviation from this inverse viscosity trend that methanol permeated 3.8-fold faster than toluene despite their similar viscosities (0.54 mPa·s versus 0.56 mPa·s). This exception stems from its most size-restricted channels, favoring the transport of smaller methanol molecules (dm = 0.51 nm) over the toluene (dm = 0.70 nm) (48). Crucially, this pore-flow–dominated transport underpins the high permeance for nonpolar solvents like toluene and hexane, in sharp contrast with traditional polyamide membranes (43, 50).

The rapid transport of nonpolar solvents ensures membrane feasibility in nonpolar separation systems. To validate this, we systematically assessed the separation performance of these polyimide membranes in binary nonpolar mixtures, exemplified by toluene-based hydrocarbon solutions. As shown in Fig. 3C, all membranes retained hexaphenylbenzene (HPB; 534.7 Da) with >80% rejection despite enhanced solution permeance at higher DAM ratios. This demonstrates defect-free skin layers with molecular sieving capability in the typical nanofiltration range (200 to 1000 Da) (51). The separation of 1,3,5-triisopropylbenzene (TIPB; 204.4 Da) and toluene, an established surrogate solute for OSRO, further revealed the molecule sieving capacity of membranes. Strategically modulated polyimide composition governs the microporosity, thereby regulating molecular passability through the polymer matrix. This structural control fundamentally dictates membrane selectivity, that as DAM content increased, the toluene/TIPB separation factor progressively decreased from 7.2 (A4M0-M) to 1.7 (A1M3-M) (Fig. 3D). Phenomenologically, the selectivity reduction correlates with enlarged pore gate size in the swollen state, demonstrating a composition-based customization over membrane structure and performance (fig. S36A). This trend also exists in ethanol, albeit solute physical properties dominated selectivity in polar systems (fig. S37 and table S3) (52). The 6FDA-DABA/DAM asymmetrical membranes emerged from state-of-the-art membranes, surpassing the experimental OSRO upper bound concerning solvent permeance and toluene/TIPB selectivity (Fig. 3D and table S4) (10–12, 53, 54). This excellent superiority was ascribed to their distinctive thin selective skin and inherent compatibility with nonpolar solvents. The stable filtration performance in toluene over 72 hours confirms membrane durability under harsh solvent conditions (fig. S38) (10).

The MWCO (corresponding to 90% solute rejection) of polyimide membranes was carefully evaluated using linear alkane markers in toluene. The alkane concentrations were analyzed by gas chromatography (GC). As shown in Fig. 3E and fig. S39, the A4M0-M membranes exhibited efficient exclusion of short-chain alkanes larger than 24 carbons, with an MWCO of ~350 Da. A1M3-M membranes facilitated faster permeation of larger solutes consistent with the enlarged pore gate size, thus elevating the MWCO to ~530 Da (fig. S36B). The separation performance was further investigated using a hydrocarbon series between 100 and 300 Da, spanning OSRO to tight nanofiltration (Fig. 3F and fig. S40). Beyond the DAM-modulated tunability, the hydrocarbon rejection demonstrated not only molecular weight dependence but also an inverse correlation with aromaticity (quantified as the aromatic carbon proportion; fig. S41). This possibly arises from high affinity and hydrophobic interactions between the aromatics and polyimide (10). These results highlight that the precision differentiation of solutes is achieved through synergistic size and chemical affinity effects, underscoring the nuanced selectivity mechanisms in molecular separations. Consequently, the molecularly engineered polyimide membranes establish a versatile platform for tunable hydrocarbon separations.

This programmable selectivity and ultrafast nonpolar solvent permeation render the developed polyimide membranes uniquely suitable for the fractionation of hydrocarbon feedstocks. To evaluate the potential in practical separation systems, a hydrocarbon fractionation model containing 5 wt % kerosene and 5 wt % liquid paraffin in toluene was devised as a simulation of industrial refinery streams (fig. S42, A and B). To ascertain the hydrocarbon content, a series of C7-C40 normal alkane calibrations was used to correlate carbon numbers with retention times in GC analysis (fig. S43). On the basis of alkane calibrations, kerosene predominantly comprised C10-C13 hydrocarbons, whereas paraffin consisted of C13-C40. Their GC signals were distinctly resolved, enabling quantitative analysis (fig. S42, C and D, and table S5). According to the aforementioned MWCO of membranes, the dense A4M0-M and the relatively loose A1M3-M membranes were selected as cascade separation systems for hydrocarbon fractionation. The first stage prioritizes rapid removal of heavy alkanes, while the second stage selectively fractionates the kerosene component to a high purity (Fig. 4A and fig. S44). Initial evaluation of A4M0-M and A1M3-M membranes revealed distinct differences in the solvent permeance and hydrocarbon rejection toward the feed mixture (figs. S45 and S46). The A1M3-M membrane exhibited exceptionally high permeance of 1.93 liters m−2 hour−1 bar−1, selectively rejecting 63.7% of paraffin components (especially the heavy fraction between C30 and C40 according to the alkane calibrations) and permitting kerosene permeation. This performance motivated its deployment for the primary removal of heavy alkane.

For the long-term filtration in stage 1, the A1M3-M membrane processed 400 ml of the feed solution and yielded 280 ml of the permeate solution. The membrane performed a gradual decline of the permeance from 2.0 to 1.3 liters m−2 hour−1 bar−1 with prolonged operation time (Fig. 4C). This was attributed to the progressively hindered mass transport due to the enrichment of heavy alkanes in the retentate. Notably, despite this acceptable flux attenuation, the membrane allowed passage of >80% kerosene fraction while intercept of >60% paraffin, thus giving rise to the relative component of kerosene increasing from 50 to 70.3% in the permeate (Fig. 4, B and E, and fig. S47). The high prefractionated efficiency toward heavy hydrocarbons made it possible for a secondary enrichment of kerosene.

On the basis of the coarse separation of stage 1, stage 2 targeted refined purification of the residual paraffin through precision sieving, which is critical for optimizing the fuel combustion efficiency of light alkanes. The dense A4M0-M membrane, designed for precision molecular sieving, removed around 90% of the residual paraffin components (C13-C40), outperforming the A1M3-M membrane in separation precision (fig. S48). When processing 280 ml of primarily permeating hydrocarbons collected in stage 1, the system delivered 200 ml of the final permeate. Similarly, the membrane permeance decreased moderately from 0.57 to 0.40 liters m−2 hour−1 bar−1 during a 70-hour operation due to the concentrated hydrocarbon solutes (Fig. 4C). The A4M0-M membrane maintained uncompromised selectivity throughout extended operation, persistently blocking larger than 90% of paraffin while permeating over than 60% of kerosene (Fig. 4B and fig. S49). Ultimately, the cascade separation process successfully lightened the hydrocarbon mixture by efficiently permeating kerosene-range fraction (e.g., n-C11H24 and n-C12H26) while rejecting heavier paraffin fractions like n-C17H36 (Fig. 4D). The kerosene fraction was enriched up to 92.6% within the C10-C40 range in the final permeate (Fig. 4E and fig. S49C). This highly efficient separation highlights the transformative potential of molecularly programmed selective membranes for the fractionation of complex hydrocarbon streams.

DISCUSSION

The 6FDA-DABA/DAM polyimide membranes are designed with programmable polymer chain architectures for gradient separation of hydrocarbon mixtures. The regulation between flexible DABA moieties and rigid DAM moieties creates variable micropores for molecular sieving. The solvent-casted and thermally post-treated membranes enable scalable manufacturing and deterministic tuning of microporous architectures. This resulted in subnanoscale precision in selectivity control toward hydrocarbon mixtures with different sizes. The cascade separation of the representative kerosene and paraffin mixture demonstrates unprecedented operational durability and distinct selectivity in multistage cuts. By establishing quantitative structure-transport relationships between diamine stoichiometry and separation metrics, this work unlocks a materials genome strategy for membrane design, facilitating sustainable fractionation technologies across petrochemical refining, biofuel upgrading, and beyond.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

6FDA (99%), DAM (98%), N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 99.9%), 3-methylpyridine (99%), and acetic anhydride (98.5%) were purchased from Aladdin Chemistry Co. Ltd. (China). DABA (98%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. DMF (99.5%), hexane (97.0%), heptane (98.5%), toluene (99.5%), methanol (99.5%), and ethanol (99.7%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (China). C7-C40 saturated alkane standard was obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co. Ltd. (China). All other reagents were obtained from Aladdin Chemistry Co. Ltd.

6FDA and DAM were sublimated, and DABA was dried under vacuum at 130°C for 12 hours before use. All other chemicals were used as received.

Polymer synthesis

6FDA-DABA/DAM polyimides with the DABA:DAM molar ratio of 4:0, 3:1, 2:2, 1:3, or 0:4 were synthesized by a two-step polycondensation reaction of the 6FDA dianhydride with DABA and DAM diamines, as shown in fig. S1. First, 30.00 mmol of diamines, consisting of DABA and DAM diamines with a desired molar ratio, was dissolved in 80 ml of NMP under a N2 atmosphere. After the diamines were completely dissolved, the solution temperature was cooled to ~0°C by submerging the reactor in an ice bath, and 30.00 mmol of 6FDA dianhydride was added into the reactor. The solution temperature was maintained at ~0°C until the dianhydride was dissolved. After 2 hours, the reactor was removed from the ice bath, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 hours to form a viscous PAA solution. Subsequently, 30 mmol of 3-methylpyridine and 300 mmol of acetic anhydride were added into the reactor for chemical imidization. The solution was stirred for another 24 hours at room temperature to convert PAA to polyimide. Last, the synthesized polyimide was precipitated in methanol, washed with ethanol five times, and dried at 150°C under vacuum for 24 hours.

The synthesized 6FDA-DABA/DAM polyimides were referred to as A4M0, A3M1, A2M2, A1M3, and A0M4, corresponding to the DABA:DAM ratio. In the abbreviated name, A stands for DABA diamines, and M stands for DAM diamines. For example, A3M1 represents that the molar ratio of DABA:DAM during the polymerization is 3: 1.

Membrane preparation

The polyimide membranes were prepared by NIPS and thermal treatment. In the NIPS process, 18 wt % polyimide solutions in DMF were cast on a glass plate using a doctor blade with a 250-μm gap. The glass plate was immersed into a distillate water bath at 30°C and left to stand for 10 min to ensure complete phase inversion of polymer solution. The obtained membranes were washed with ethanol for 12 hours and dried in a cool place for 24 hours.

In the thermal treatment of membranes, a tube furnace (OTF-1200X-5S, China) was used. An aluminum oxide ceramic mold with 1.5-mm gaps was used to load membranes into the tube furnace. The tube was evacuated with a vacuum pump for 1 hour before heating. The membranes were thermally treated under vacuum, and the heating rate was set at 5°C/min before 300°C and 1°C/min from 300°C to the target temperature. The membranes consisting of A4M0, A3M1, or A2M2 polyimides were lastly treated at 330°C for 20 hours, and those consisting of A1M3 polyimide were treated at 360°C for 10 hours. After thermal treatment, the membranes were activated by immersing in a mixed solution containing 70 vol % ethanol and 30 vol % DMF at room temperature for 6 hours and lastly stored in ethanol.

The neat polyimide membranes from the NIPS process, as the precursors for thermal treatment, were referred to as -Pre depending on the used polyimides, and similarly, the heated membranes were referred to as -M. For example, A3M1-Pre is a membrane from the NIPS process of A3M1 polyimide, and A3M1-M is the A3M1-Pre membranes submitted to thermal treatment.

Polymer and membrane characterization

GPC (Agilent 1260 Infinity II) was used to analyze the molecular weights of the synthesized polyimides. Tetrahydrofuran was used as the mobile phase, and polystyrene standards were used to obtain the calibration curve of molecular weight.

1H NMR spectra of the 6FDA-DABA/DAM polyimides were recorded using spectrometers (Bruker Avance III 500) operating at 500-MHz resonance frequencies. The polyimides were vacuum dried at 150°C for 12 hours and dissolved in deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide for detection.

Attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared spectra of the polyimides and membranes were detected using spectrometers (Nicolet 6700). The samples were vacuum dried at 150°C for 12 hours before testing.

The weight of polyimide and membranes versus temperature was recorded using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TA-Q500). Before testing, the samples were vacuum dried at 150°C for 12 hours to remove the trace solvent. The samples were heated from 50° to 700°C at 10°C/min under a nitrogen purge rate of 60 ml/min.

A dynamic mechanical analyzer (DMA850) was used to find the viscoelastic property of polyimides at different temperature. The solvent-casted polymer strips (40 mm by 5 mm by 0.3 mm) were used, and the test was carried out in tensile mode under nitrogen atmosphere. During the testing, the strips were first heated from room temperature to 150°C at a rate of 10°C/min without stress. Then, the samples were heated to 450°C at 5°C/min and subjected to a periodic oscillating stress at a frequency of 1 Hz. The strains of the samples were maintained at 1 per mil. The glass transition temperature of polyimide was determined from the peak value of tangent of the loss angle (i.e., the ratio of loss modulus to storage modulus) versus temperature.

Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SU-8100) was used to observe the morphology of membrane surfaces and cross sections. Before observation, the samples were metal-sprayed using the MC1000 ion sputter with a platinum target.

Transmission electron microscopy (HT-7700) was used to observe the membrane cross sections. The membranes were resin-embedded and sliced at room temperature before observation.

The DBES were measured as a function of positron implantation energy at the Institute of High Energy Physics of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. A 22Na radioactive source was used as the positron source, with continuously tunable incident positron energy between 0 and 20 keV. The positron implantation depth was calculated asD=(40ρ)E1.6where D is the implantation depth (nanometers), ρ is the material density (grams per cubic centimeter), and E is the positron energy (kilo–electron volts) (21, 22). The acquired DBES were characterized using the R parameter, defined as the ratio of 3γ to 2γ annihilation events. The 3γ annihilation event corresponds to counts between 364 and 496.2 keV, and the 2γ annihilation event corresponds to that between 504.35 and 517.65 keV.

Sorption of CO2 at 273 K was measured using a gas sorption analyzer (Micromeritics 3Flex) at relative pressures ranging from 1 × 10−5 to 0.030. The polyimide membranes were ground into powders and degassed at 200°C under vacuum for 8 hours before analysis. The pore size distribution of the membrane was obtained by analyzing the gas adsorption curve based on the DFT.

XRD (D8 Advance) was operated to evaluate the microporous properties of membranes. XRD tests were carried out using the Cu Kα1 radiation with a wavelength of 1.54 Å, and the membranes were scanned in a 2θ range of 10° to 40°. The average d-spacing of polyimides was calculated according to Bragg’s lawλ=2d⋅sinθ

Gel fractions of membranes were determined by the residual mass of membranes after being immersed in DMF. The weighted A4M0-M, A3M1-M, A2M2-M, and A1M3-M membranes were submerged in DMF at ambient temperature (25°C) for 1 week. After carefully removing the solvents, the membranes were washed with ethanol and totally dried under vacuum at 150°C overnight. The residual membranes were reweighted. Each measurement was performed twice using different pieces of membranes to improve accuracy. The gel fraction was calculated asGel fraction (%)=mresidualminitial⋅100where minitial and mresidual are the weights of initial and residual polyimide membranes, respectively.

To measure the toluene uptakes of polyimides, dense films of polyimide series were prepared by casting an 18 wt % DMF solution of each polyimide on a glass plate. The cast polymer solution was allowed to stand at 60°C for 6 hours in a blast drying oven, and the obtained films were then further dried under vacuum at 150°C overnight. The degree of sorption in toluene was measured by submerging weighted fragments of the films in toluene at ambient temperature (25°C) for 1 week. The solvated films were reweighted after wiping the surface dry. Each measurement was performed twice using different fragments of polyimide films to improve accuracy. The solvent uptake at unit activity was calculated asMass change(%)=mswellen−mdrymdry⋅100where mswellen and mdry are the weights of swelling and dry polyimide films, respectively.

Membrane performance

The membrane performances were measured with a custom-built cross flow system (fig. S33) pressurized by a high-performance liquid chromatography pump (LC-3060B, China), using a membrane area of 7.1 cm2 and at a pressure between 4 and 8 bar. The permeance measurements of different solvents were evaluated at room temperature using three different membranes. Before testing, the membrane samples were soaked in the corresponding solvent for 24 hours, then loaded in the filtration cell with a nonwoven fabric support, and prepressed at 5 bar for 30 min. The solvent permeance (P) was evaluated asP=VA⋅t⋅Δpwhere V is the permeation volume (liters), A is the effective filtration area (square meters), t is the testing time (hours), and Δp is the pressure (bar). The testing solvents include methanol, ethanol, toluene, hexane, and heptane.

The rejection in ethanol was investigated by filtrating a series of solutes, including isatin (147.13 Da), methyl orange (327.33 Da), food yellow 3 (452.36 Da), congo red (696.68 Da), brilliant blue G (854.02 Da), rose bengal (1017.64 Da), and vitamin B12 (1355.38 Da). The concentration of the solutes in ethanol was 20 ppm. The rejection experiments were performed at 4 bar, discarding at least 5 ml before collecting the samples for analysis. The rejection (R) was calculated asR(%)=(1−CpCf)⋅100where Cp is the solute concentration in the permeates, and Cf is the solute concentration in the feed. An ultraviolet (UV) spectrometer (UV-1900i, Japan) was used to detect the concentration of the solutes.

The hydrocarbon rejection was investigated by dissolving HPB (534.7 Da) in toluene at a concentration of 100 ppm. A UV spectrometer was used to evaluate the separation. The filtration was performed under 4 bar for 2 to 4 hours. For measuring rejection toward components with smaller molecular weight, isooctane (114.22 Da), propylbenzene (120.20 Da), tetralin (132.20 Da), n-butylcyclohexane (140.27 Da), 1-methylnaphthalene (142.20 Da), dodecane (170.33 Da), TIPB (204.36 Da), i-hexadecane (226.44 Da), dodecylbenzene (246.43 Da), and pristane (268.51 Da) were dissolved individually in toluene in a ratio of 1:99 mol % of hydrocarbon/toluene. GC (9790Plus, China) was used to test the separation performance. The filtration was performed under 8 bar, and the permeate was taken until the permeance and rejection were stabilized for 24 hours. In particular, the separation factor for toluene-TIPB was calculated asToluene/TIPB separation factor=CToluene,pCToluene,r⋅CTIPB,rCTIPB,pwhere CToluene,p and CToluene,r are the permeate and retentate compositions of toluene, and CTIPB,p and CTIPB,r are the permeate and retentate compositions of TIPB.

MWCO was determined using a seven-component mixture containing 0.3 wt % each of decane (C10, 142.28 Da), pentadecane (C15, 212.42 Da), eicosane (C20, 282.56 Da), tetracosane (C24, 338.65 Da), triacontane (C30, 422.81 Da), and tetracontane (C40, 563.08 Da) in toluene. All membranes were tested in cross flow at 8 bar. The filtration system was operated for 24 hours, and the permeate was collected until the rejection and permeance were steady. The concentrations of alkanes were detected by GC (Agilent 6890, loaded with an Agilent 19091J-413 chromatography column). The MWCO was defined as the smallest molecular weight corresponding to a 90% rejection.

Hydrocarbon fractionation using A4M0-M and A1M3-M membranes

A4M0-M and A1M3-M membranes were loaded simultaneously into the cross-flow system (active membrane area = 7.1 cm2) (fig. S33). Kerosene and light liquid paraffin were dissolved in toluene at a concentration of 5 wt % each to obtain a hydrocarbon feed mixture. The initial feed mixture was 300 ml and fractionated in a cascade process containing two stages. In the first stage, the permeate of A1M3-M membranes was collected while that of A4M0-M membranes was reflowed into the feeding tank. We continuously operated the system under 8 bar at ambient temperature, and 280 ml of permeating solutions was collected in the first stage. For the second stage, the collected permeate in the first stage was used as feed, and we permeated 200 ml out of this using A4M0-M membranes under 8 bar (at the same time, the permeate of A1M3 membrane was reflowed).

We analyzed the components of hydrocarbon solution using the same GC as the above MWCO experiment. To identify the components in the permeates, a standardized C7-C40 saturated alkanes solution in hexane was used. The initial feed and collected permeates in each stage were further characterized using a GC–mass spectrometry system (Agilent 7890B/7000C). Some representative hydrocarbons with high matching fidelity (match factor, >80%) were identified in the spectra.
