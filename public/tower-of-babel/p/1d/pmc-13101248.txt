Targeted radio-thermo dual sensitizer magnetic gold core-shells nanoparticles enhance radioresistant glioblastoma via promoting apoptosis and ROS generation
Authors: Sakine Shirvalilou, Samideh Khoei, Sepideh Khoee, Mohammad Reza Karimi, Bahareh Haji Ali, Elahe Sadri, Mohammad Hossein Parsaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Main objectiveThis study aims to evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeted Fe3O4@Au/FA core-shell nanoparticles as dual sensitizers in enhancing radiotherapy and hyperthermia for C6 glioma cancer cells, minimizing off-target effects. The investigation focuses on the efficacy of this combination therapy in promoting apoptosis and generating reactive oxygen species (ROS).MethodsThe Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles were synthesized and characterized using dynamic light scattering (DLS), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to confirm their size, zeta potential, and morphology. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to analyze the functional groups on the nanoparticle surface. The cytotoxicity of the nanoparticles was assessed through the MTT assay on OLN-93 and C6 glioma cells, and cellular uptake was quantified via inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). Ex vivo biocompatibility was evaluated with hemolysis tests and measurements of liver enzymes, urea, and creatinine. Heat generation was monitored during exposure to alternating magnetic fields (AMF) to calculate cumulative equivalent minutes (CEM). The therapeutic effects were evaluated using colony formation assays (CFA) for plating efficiency (PE) and survival fraction (SF) calculation. Flow cytometry was utilized to quantify apoptosis and necrosis, while intracellular ROS levels were measured with the DCFH-DA assay following combined treatments with varying doses of ionizing radiation (0–8 Gy).ResultsCharacterization confirmed the nanoparticles’ spherical morphology, with an average size of 40.8 ± 2.8 nm and a negative zeta potential of -19.93 mV. Ex vivo biocompatibility tests demonstrated that the nanoparticles exhibit good compatibility, suggesting minimal adverse effects on biological systems. ICP-OES confirmed effective cellular uptake. CFA revealed significant decreases in PE and SF for combined therapies. Flow cytometry showed enhanced apoptosis and necrosis in combined treatment groups (p < 0.01). Furthermore, ROS levels increased significantly with the maximum radiation dose in the combined treatment (p < 0.0001), highlighting the nanoparticles’ effectiveness in promoting therapeutic responses against glioma cells.ConclusionOur magnetic gold core-shell nanoparticles demonstrate good biocompatibility and effective tumor uptake, enhancing radiotherapy and hyperthermia for C6 glioma treatment. These findings highlight their potential for improving treatment outcomes through enhanced apoptosis and ROS generation.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Multifunctional theranostic metal nanoparticles (MTMNPs) serve as innovative platforms that seamlessly integrate diagnostic and therapeutic functions [1]. These versatile NPs are widely utilized in clinical practice for various applications, including drug delivery, overcoming drug resistance, radiation dose enhancement, photosensitizers, hyperthermia induction, immunotherapy enhancers, and imaging contrast agents [2]. The multifunctionality of NPs offers a promising solution to the challenges posed by cancer treatment, especially for treatment-resistant cancers like brain tumors, opening new avenues for improving patient outcomes in modern medicine [3]. At the nanoscale, the properties of these particles undergo significant changes that enhance their interactions with cell surfaces and intercellular structures. It is well established that nanosystems ranging from 20 to 200 nm exhibit superior biological characteristics, including increased internalization within tumor cells and the ability to effectively cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) via two primary endocytosis pathways: clathrin-mediated and caveolin-mediated endocytosis [4, 5]. According to the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) theory, nanosystems can easily navigate through the fenestrated blood vessels formed during angiogenesis and accumulate within tumor tissues, largely due to the impaired lymphatic drainage system surrounding the tumor [6]. Some studies have indicated that the EPR effect in glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is less effective compared to other cancer types, primarily due to the presence of the blood-brain tumor barrier (BBTB) [7]. To address this limitation in efficient drug delivery nanomedicine, researchers have modified the surfaces of nanosystems with targeting ligands that can selectively bind to specific or overexpressed receptors on tumor cells (Ligand-based active targeting strategy), such as folate receptors, lectin, transferrin, interleukin, neurokinin-1, and integrin v3 [7, 8].

Recent advances in nanomedicine have created new avenues for sensitizing resistant cells [9]. Metal-based nanosensitizers (MBS) allow for the precise focusing of dose gradients, as well as the focus of light or heat to the surface of the target tissue, enhancing treatment efficacy and overcoming cellular resistance [10]. Radiation therapy is the standard treatment for glioblastoma (GBM), but its efficacy is often hampered by the development of radioresistance [11]. In conventional glioma treatment, whole-brain radiation typically employs linear accelerators, which can inadvertently damage healthy brain tissue and alter the radiation dose delivered to the tumor [12]. To enhance the efficacy of radiation therapy, inhibit tumor growth, and safeguard surrounding healthy tissue, advancements in radiotherapy technology have shifted from broad-spectrum whole-brain irradiation to more localized approaches by MBS [10, 13]. Furthermore, combining these methods with adjunctive therapies such as hyperthermia has demonstrated promising results in overcoming existing limitations and improving overall treatment outcomes [14].

One particularly promising agent in enhancing radiation therapy is gold nanoparticles (AuNPs). These NPs are gaining traction for their excellent biocompatibility, intrinsic radiosensitivity, and ability to penetrate and persist in tumor tissues [15]. AuNPs can effectively target tumors through both the passive EPR effect, as well as by evading the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) through active targeting strategies [16]. Given that GBM is characterized by its radioresistant nature and that most recurrences tend to occur within the radiation field, enhancing tumor radiosensitivity is crucial for improving outcomes in GBM patients. AuNPs radio-enhancers possess a high atomic number (Z = 79) and exhibit a superior photoelectric absorption cross-section compared to the surrounding medium [17]. This unique property allows AuNPs to provide significant intratumoral dose-enhancing effects. The primary mechanism of action involves physical enhancement, where AuNPs are activated by incoming photons, resulting in the production of high linear energy transfer (LET) radiation, including Auger electrons, photoelectrons, and Compton scattering [16]. These photoelectric products contribute to a markedly increased biological effectiveness in tumoral cell killing. Following this physical enhancement, the energy absorbed by AuNPs leads to the generation of a cascade of reactive oxygen species (ROS), further intensifying the therapeutic impact and damaging cancerous cells [15, 18].

Combination therapies that integrate NPs with radiation and hyperthermia represent an innovative and potentially effective approach to combat cancer [19]. When MTM nanoparticles are employed alongside radiation therapy, they can significantly enhance both the targeted delivery of radiation dose and the overall efficacy of the hyperthermia [20]. These NPs can be engineered to absorb radiofrequency waves and convert this energy into heat, a strategy known as magnetic hyperthermia (MH) [21]. By elevating the temperature at the tumor site, this method can improve the destruction of cancer cells. Among various options, superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) stand out as the most promising thermal sensitizers when subjected to an alternating magnetic field (AMF), thereby optimizing treatment outcomes and enhancing the effectiveness of radiation therapy [22].

Therefore, in the present study, we synthesized and characterized dual radio and thermosensitizer Fe3O4@Au core/shell NPs conjugated with folic acid to investigate its treatment effect on glioma cells. For this purpose, the therapeutic effect of the Fe3O4@Au/FA NPs in combination with 6 MV X-ray and AMF coil (13.56 MHz) was investigated on rat C6 glioma cell line. In order to accurately assess the effectiveness of new cancer treatments, since necrosis and apoptosis have distinct biological consequences, accurate determination of apoptosis induction is necessary to evaluate any new cancer treatment strategy. Accordingly, we performed flow cytometry analysis to determine the amount of apoptosis induction and ROS measurement the amount of free oxygen produced, and colony formation assay to evaluate survival fraction following different combination treatments using NPs, MH, and RT.

Materials and methods

Materials

In the synthesis section, FeCl2·5H2O, FeCl3·6H2O, ammonia, ethanol, HAuCl4·3H2O, sodium citrate, mercaptopropionic acid, N, N-Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO), N-(3-imethylaminopropyl) N’-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride, N-hydroxysuccinimide and folic acid were used, all purchased from Merck. For in vitro experiments, the brain cancer and healthy cells (C6 and OLN-93, respectively) were purchased from the Pasteur Institute of Iran. Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), and Ham’s F12 were purchased from Gibco (Invitrogen, USA). FBS (fetal bovine serum), penicillin/streptomycin, and Trypsin were produced by Biowest in France. 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) and Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were bought from Sigma-Aldrich (MO, USA). The Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit was purchased from eBioscience (San Diego, USA). All chemicals, materials, and reagents required for the synthesis of NPs were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.

Methods

Synthesis of Superparamagnetic Iron Oxide Nanoparticles (SPIONs)

Chemical coprecipitation method was used for the synthesis of SPIONs [23]. Aqueous solutions of iron(II) chloride pentahydrate (FeCl2·5H2O, 1 g, 0.0046 mol) and iron(III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, 2.6 g, 0.0096 mol) were prepared in a round-bottom flask equipped with a nitrogen gas inlet and a magnetic stirrer. The reaction mixture was heated to 70 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere, and then a solution of ammonia (10 mL) was gradually and slowly introduced into the reaction mixture through a drip method (pH ~ 9). Following the ammonia addition, the reaction was allowed to proceed for 2 h under a nitrogen atmosphere with continuous stirring at a speed of 400 rpm. After the reaction, the obtained product was washed sequentially with deionized water (DW) (3 × 20 mL) and ethanol (2 × 20 mL) to remove any impurities (pH > 5). The resulting NPs were then dried in a freeze-dryer (A-10, Tajhizat Sazan Pishtaz, Iran) at a temperature of −48 °C and a pressure of 1 mbar for 24 h.

Synthesis of Fe3O4@Au Core-Shell NPs

A 100 mL aqueous solution containing 15 mL of 2 mg/mL HAuCl4·3H2O was heated to reflux for 20 min, creating a homogeneous solution of gold(III) ions. Following this, 5 mL of freshly prepared iron nanoparticles (6 mg/mL) were rapidly introduced into the refluxing solution, facilitating the reduction of gold ions (pH ~ 6–7). Immediately after, 5 mL of an 80 mM sodium citrate solution was added to the reaction mixture, and the solution was boiled for an additional 5 min at high speed, ensuring thorough mixing and promoting the formation of Fe3O4@Au nanoparticles. After the reaction, the synthesized NPs were separated from the supernatant using a centrifuge, washed twice with DW to remove any impurities, and then magnetically collected using a neodymium magnet, resulting in the efficient recovery of the Fe3O4@Au nanoparticles.

Activating and Conjugating the Folic Acid Moiety

14 mg of N-(3-Dimethylaminopropyl)-N’-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) and 4 mg of N-Hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) were added to the DMSO solution containing the acid-functionalized NPs (pH ~ 5). The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 2 h to activate the carboxyl groups on the NPs. Then, 30 mg of folic acid was added to the solution, and stirring was continued for an additional 4 h, allowing the folic acid to conjugate to the NPs.

Nanostructure and surface properties of Fe3O4@Au/FA NPs

The physical and chemical properties of Fe3O4@Au/FA core-shell NPs were thoroughly characterized using various analytical techniques. DLS analysis was performed using a Nano ZS instrument (Malvern, UK) to determine the mean hydrodynamic size, size distribution, and zeta potential of the NPs in DW. Briefly, a small sample Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles (after magnetic washing and purification) aliquot was sonicated in distilled water (DW) for 10 min to ensure uniform particle distribution. The resulting suspension was then transferred to the DLS instrument for analysis. In addition, the long-term stability of the NPs was thoroughly evaluated. To assess their stability, the changes in size and zeta potential of the NPs were monitored over a period of one week using DLS analysis.

The morphological characteristics of the NPs were examined using TEM (Zeiss LEO906, Jena, Germany) and SEM. These techniques provided valuable insights into the size, shape, and surface morphology of the NPs. To confirm the conjugation of folic acid to the Fe3O4@Au nanoparticles, FTIR was performed using a Varian Inova instrument. These analytical techniques collectively provided a comprehensive understanding of the physical and chemical properties of the Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles. The magnetic properties of both bare Fe₃O₄ and Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles were evaluated using Vibrating Sample Magnetometry (VSM) at room temperature, with applied magnetic fields ranging from − 10,000 to + 10,000 Oe.

Determination of iron and gold Concentrations in Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles using ICP-OES

The concentration of iron (Fe) and gold (Au) in Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles was accurately quantified using ICP-OES analysis. To prepare the samples for analysis, 1 mg of Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles was dissolved in a strongly acidic solution consisting of a 1:3 mixture of 65% HNO3 and 37% HCl (2 mL). Notably, this acidic solution was identical to the medium employed in the synthesis of Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles, ensuring compatibility and accuracy of the results. The ICP-OES analysis was performed at Sharif University, where state-of-the-art equipment and expertise enabled precise measurement of the iron and gold concentrations in the samples.

The toxicity of NPs: in vitro and ex vivo investigations

Cancer and normal monolayer cell cultures and preparation

This study utilized two adherent cell types, C6 (rat glioma cancer) and OLN-93 (rat normal brain) cells, which were cultured at a density of 104 cells/cm2 and maintained for up to six passages. The cells were sequentially cultured in Ham’s F12 and DMEM high glucose mediums, respectively, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Upon reaching approximately 80% confluence, the cells were washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), trypsinized with 1 mL of a trypsin/EDTA solution, and then resuspended in the complete culture medium (2 mL).

In vitro cytotoxicity assessment of NPs on C6 and OLN-93 cells

The MTT assay was employed to evaluate the viability of C6 and OLN-93 cells in response to nanoparticle exposure. Control wells received 100 µL of medium without NPs. Following a further 24-hour incubation, the media was discarded, and wells were washed twice with cold PBS. After a 4-hour incubation with 100 µL of 0.5 mg/mL MTT solution, 200 µL DMSO was added. Ex vivo cytotoxicity assessment

To assess the toxicity of NPs under ex vivo conditions, we evaluated their effects on red blood cells and liver and kidney enzymes of healthy mice.

Cytotoxicity of NPs on liver and kidney function in mice

A total of nine mice were utilized to investigate the potential toxicity effects of NPs on liver and kidney function. The rats were randomly divided into three groups: a control group (treated with saline), a non-targeted treatment group (exposed to Fe3O4@Au), and a targeted treatment group (exposed to Fe3O4@Au/FA). Twenty-four hours post-exposure, the rats were anesthetized with a combination of ketamine, xylazine, and aspromazine. Blood samples were then collected from the heart via cardiac puncture and stored in coagulation tubes to prevent clotting. Blood samples were centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min at 3000 rpm to separate the blood plasma from the remaining components.

Liver function analysis

To assess liver function, serum levels of three key liver enzymes were determined using an automated chemistry analyzer (Biolis 24i, UK): alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP). Elevated levels of these enzymes are indicative of liver damage or inflammation.

Kidney function analysis

Similarly, kidney function was evaluated by analyzing serum urea and creatinine levels using commercially available kits. Elevated levels of these metabolites can indicate impaired renal function or kidney damage.

Enhancing cellular uptake of NPs via folate-assisted targeting: a comparative study

To investigate the impact of folic acid ligand on cellular internalization, we evaluated the uptake of Fe3O4@Au and Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles by two cancer and normal cell lines with high folate receptor expression (C6) and healthy cells with lower receptor expression (OLN-93). The cells were seeded in T-25 flasks and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with a concentration of IC10 concentrations (about 50 µg/ml) of each type of nanoparticle per cell line. Following treatment, unbound NPs were removed by washing with PBS buffer, and the cells were trypsinized and centrifuged for enumeration. To quantify the intracellular content of NPs, the cells were digested for 2 h with a mixture of HNO3 and HCl at 150 °C. The solutions were diluted to 2 mL with DW and analyzed for iron and gold concentrations using ICP-OES (VISTAPRO, Varian, Australia). Finally, the average iron and gold content per cell was calculated from the ICP-OES analysis, providing insights into the efficacy of folate-assisted targeting in enhancing nanoparticle uptake by cancer cells.

Calculation of heating profile and heat dose by applying RF coil

Magnetic hyperthermia is a cancer treatment that involves heating cancer cells to a temperature of 43 °C using magnetic NPs and an alternating magnetic field. To achieve this, a carefully designed time and temperature profile is essential. This protocol outlines the steps to calculate the heating profile and heat dose by applying an RF coil. A total of 25 × 104 C6 cells were cultured in a monolayer and incubated for overnight. After a 24-hour incubation, cells were treated with NPs for an additional 24 h. The medium was then removed, and cells were washed with PBS to remove any unbound NPs. Subsequently, 30 ml of fresh medium was added to the cells, and the thermometry flask was placed in the center of the RF coil. Under the alternating magnetic field of the coil (Basafan, 200 W), temperature changes were carefully monitored using an infrared camera (Testo 875-li, Germany). After analyzing the infrared image and taking into account the applied power of the device (70 W), the time required for samples containing NPs to reach a temperature of 43 °C was precisely evaluated. These steps were repeated for control (untreated) cells placed inside the coil only. Cell apoptosis/necrosis analysis

To investigate the mechanisms of cell death in C6 cells, the Annexin V-FITC I apoptosis detection kit (Mabtag, Germany) was employed for flow cytometry analysis. This assay allows for the detection of early apoptotic events by measuring the translocation of phosphatidylserine (PS) to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane, a hallmark of apoptosis. Cells were dissociated, and counted, and 1 × 10⁵ cells were resuspended in 100 µL of 1x binding buffer (manufacturer’s protocol). After a 15-minute incubation at room temperature in the dark, 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL of propidium iodide (PI) were added. Annexin V-FITC binds to PS, allowing for the detection of early apoptotic cells, while PI intercalates into the DNA of late apoptotic and necrotic cells, providing a measure of cell viability. After incubation, a washing step was performed, and the cells were resuspended in 400 µl of rebinding buffer. Fluorescent intensity was rapidly determined using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson). The data were plotted as FL-1 (Annexin V-FITC) versus FL-2 (PI) to distinguish between viable, early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and necrotic cell populations. Intracellular ROS measurement

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production was assessed using the 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) assay. Cells seeded in 24-well plates were treated with IC10 nanoparticles, radiation, or hyperthermia. Following treatment, the cell culture medium was replaced with a DCFH-DA solution, and the plates were incubated for 60 min at 37 °C in the dark to allow DCFH-DA uptake and intracellular esterase-mediated cleavage to the fluorescent DCF. After washing twice with PBS to remove excess DCFH-DA, DCF fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader (excitation 485 nm, emission 530 nm). Fluorescence intensity, normalized to the control group, was used to quantify relative ROS production and assess the effects of the various treatments.

Statistical analysis

Data, presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), represent triplicate experiments. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test (GraphPad Prism v13 and Python) was used for multiple group comparisons; statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Physical and chemical properties of Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles

Table 1 presents the results of the analysis, detailing the size, zeta potential, and elemental composition of the NPs. The data indicate that the hydrodynamic size of the Fe3O4@Au nanoparticles was initially 32.9 ± 4.1 nm, which increased to 40.8 ± 2.8 nm following conjugation with the folic acid ligand. Furthermore, the surface charge of the Fe3O4@Au nanoparticles shifted from − 24.57 mV to −19.93 mV upon conjugation, reflecting the impact of the ligand binding on the nanoparticle’s surface properties. Based on the results of the ICP-OES test, the amounts of iron and gold present in the Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles were 0.496 ± 0.05 mg and 0.138 ± 0.01 mg, respectively. Figure 1A and B display the TEM and SEM images of the NPs, respectively, which reveal the spherical-like structure of the core-shell Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles. Iron oxide nanoparticles are placed in the center and gold nanoparticles are placed on it in the form of a shell, and the folic acid coating is seen as irregularities on the surface of the nanoparticles. VSM analysis confirmed the superparamagnetic nature of the synthesized nanoparticles, with a noticeable decrease in saturation magnetization from ± 62 emu/g for bare Fe₃O₄ to ± 44 emu/g after gold coating, indicating successful formation of the Fe₃O₄@Au/FA core–shell structure (Fig. 1E). The FTIR spectra of Au@Fe₃O₄ and Au@Fe₃O₄/FA nanoparticles were analyzed to confirm the surface conjugation of folic acid (FA) (Fig. 2). Both samples exhibited a broad absorption band in the range of 480–700 cm⁻¹, which can be attributed to the stretching vibrations of Fe–O and Au–O bonds. However, significant spectral differences were observed upon functionalization with folic acid. In the Au@Fe₃O₄/FA spectrum, characteristic peaks appeared at 2900 cm⁻¹, 1720.192 cm⁻¹, 1640.161 cm⁻¹, and 1540.844 cm⁻¹, which were either absent or much less intense in the spectrum of bare Au@Fe₃O₄. These bands correspond to the stretching vibrations of the C–H bond of MPA, carboxylic C = O group, amide C = O bond, and N–H bending of the amine group, respectively. These findings confirm the successful conjugation of folic acid onto the surface of Au@Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles, thereby enabling potential targeted delivery applications.

In vitro cytotoxicity of NPs

Figure 2B illustrates the effect of varying concentrations of both non-targeted (Fe3O4@Au) and targeted (Fe3O4@Au/FA) nanoparticles on the viability of C6 and OLN-93 cell lines. As observed in this figure, the toxicity induced by the non-targeted NPs did not show a marked difference between cancerous (C6) and healthy (OLN-93) cells (p > 0.05). However, the targeted NPs exhibited significantly higher toxicity in C6 cells compared to OLN-93 cells at concentrations of 250 and 500 µg/ml (p < 0.05), with this difference reaching its maximum at a concentration of 1000 µg/ml (p < 0.01). Also, Table 2 summarizes the inhibitory concentrations (IC10 and IC50) for both C6 and OLN-93 cell lines following treatment with targeted and non-targeted NPs, using the MTT assay. The IC10 and IC50 values for the Fe3O4@Au nanoparticles in the C6 cell line were reported as 48.2 ± 2.1 µg/ml and 1127.5 ± 21.3 µg/ml, respectively. In the OLN-93 healthy cell line, the IC10 and IC50 values for the same NPs were 45.6 ± 3.3 µg/ml and 995 ± 11.4 µg/ml, with no notable difference observed between these two cell lines. In contrast, for the targeted Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles, the IC10 values in the OLN-93 cell line were 42.3 ± 1.9 µg/ml, and the IC50 value was 776 ± 18.6 µg/ml. However, in the C6 cell line, the IC10 and IC50 values were lower at 37.1 ± 1.1 µg/ml and 496 ± 9.7 µg/ml, respectively. Notably, the reduction in the IC50 value for the targeted NPs in the C6 cell line compared to the OLN-93 cell line was statistically meaningful (p < 0.05), indicating greater toxicity in cancer cells and highlighting the enhanced efficacy of the targeted treatment. The therapeutic ratio (Table 2), calculated from the relationship in Eq. 2, further supports these findings. For Fe3O4@Au nanoparticles, the therapeutic ratio was 0.84, indicating a similar impact on both cell types. In contrast, Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles exhibited a higher therapeutic ratio of 1.56, signifying more favorable selective toxicity towards cancerous cells relative to healthy cells. This demonstrates that nanoparticles with folic acid ligands cause greater toxicity in cancer cells than in healthy cells, because they are taken up more readily by cancer cells.

Ex vivo cytotoxicity of NPs

In this study, ex vivo toxicity was assessed by conducting experiments on red blood cells and measuring liver and kidney enzyme levels in healthy mice. In Fig. 3A, the results of the hemolysis test for Fe3O4@Au and Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles at various concentrations are shown. As illustrated in Fig. 3A, the hemolysis percentage rose as the concentration of NPs (0–2000 µg/mL) increased in the blood samples, which was observed for both types of NPs. At all tested concentrations, both Fe3O4@Au and Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles exhibited hemolysis levels below 5%, indicating their hemocompatibility. According to the ASTM E2524-08 standard, hemolysis values exceeding 5% suggest damage to erythrocytes, while values below this threshold confirm that the material is non-damaging to red blood cells [27]. Additionally, at each concentration tested, there was no statistically meaningful difference in hemolysis between the two types of NPs, indicating that neither nanoparticle induced greater hemolysis than the other at any given concentration. In this study, liver damage resulting from treatment with two types of NPs (Fe3O4@Au and Fe3O4@Au/FA) was assessed by measuring liver enzymes AST, ALT, and ALP, as illustrated in Fig. 3B. The results showed no meaningful differences in ALT and AST levels among the control, Fe3O4@Au, and Fe3O4@Au/FA groups. An increase in ALP levels was noted in the Fe3O4@Au group (p < 0.05), while an even greater increase was noted in the Fe3O4@Au/FA group (p < 0.01) compared to the control group. Additionally, kidney function was assessed by comparing urea and creatinine levels across the three groups, as shown in Fig. 3C. These markers were evaluated to investigate renal function following treatment with the NPs. The results indicated that creatinine levels did not show any noteworthy differences between the control, Fe3O4@Au, and Fe3O4@Au/FA groups. However, urea levels in the Fe3O4@Au/FA group were higher compared to the control group (p < 0.05). In contrast, no significant difference in urea levels was observed between the Fe3O4@Au group and the control.

Cellular uptake of targeted Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles

The cellular uptake of NPs was assessed using ICP-OES, and the results are shown in Fig. 3D. In C6 cancer cells, the uptake of both iron and gold from Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles was significantly higher compared to Fe3O4@Au nanoparticles (p < 0.001). In OLN-93 healthy cells, the comparison of iron and gold uptake between Fe3O4@Au and Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles revealed no considerable differences for gold (p > 0.05). This suggests that folic acid-assisted targeting effectively enhanced the internalization of NPs in cancer cells with higher folate receptor expression.

Calculation of heating profile and heat dose by applying RF Coil

As depicted in Fig. 4A, the thermal images captured by the IR camera vividly demonstrate the difference in heat distribution between the control group and the group treated with Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles. The heating profile of C6 cells, both treated and untreated, was monitored under the RF coil, and the temperature progression is shown in Fig. 4B. In the control group (without NPs), the temperature gradually rose, reaching 43 °C after approximately 11 min of RF exposure. In contrast, cells treated with Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles exhibited a much faster temperature increase, reaching 43 °C in just 5 min. A considerable temperature difference between the two groups was recorded at the 5-minute mark (p < 0.01), and this difference continued to be substantial at the 10-minute mark (p < 0.05). Additionally, the thermal dose was calculated from the heating profiles, as summarized in Fig. 4C. For the control group (RF coil only), the CEM 43 °C values at 2, 5, and 12 min were 0.0002, 0.0021, and 4.254, respectively. However, in the nanoparticle-treated group (NPs + RFC), these values increased markedly, reaching 0.0019, 6.2519, and 230.25 at the same time points. The markedly higher CEM 43 °C values in the nanoparticle group clearly demonstrate that the presence of NPs significantly enhanced heat generation under RF coil exposure, resulting in a faster and more pronounced thermal dose in the C6 cells.

Discussion

Radiotherapy and hyperthermia, while established cancer treatments, suffer limitations: radioresistance in aggressive tumors, risking damage to surrounding healthy tissues and imprecise temperature control in hyperthermia, respectively [28]. To address these challenges, our study aims to overcome these limitations by developing a targeted nanoparticle delivery system to enhance tumor radiosensitivity and create localized hyperthermia, thus improving treatment efficacy while minimizing damage to healthy tissue. Results of TEM and SEM images (Fig. 1A & B) demonstrate that the core-shell Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles possess a spherical morphology with a size of 40.8 ± 2.8 nm as determined by DLS (Table 1). Nanoparticle size profoundly influences tumor targeting efficiency and penetration. Studies show that smaller spherical particles (≤ 50 nm) exhibit better tumor penetration and accumulation and can more effectively cross the BBTB and diffuse throughout tumor tissue, while larger particles tend to remain near blood vessels [29, 30]. Recent research indicates that even NPs in the size range of 30–50 nm have a limited presence within tumors, primarily clustering around major blood vessels. This finding underscores the strong influence of the BBTB on the passive accumulation of NPs in brain tumors. Consequently, utilizing active targeting strategies is essential for effectively delivering NPs to GBM tumors [29]. Furthemore, The observed decrease in saturation magnetization (VSM test) from ± 62 emu/g for bare Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles to ± 44 emu/g after gold coating (Fig. 1E) is consistent with previous reports on core–shell magnetic nanostructures [31, 32]. This reduction is attributed to the non-magnetic nature of the Au shell, which partially shields the magnetic field of the Fe₃O₄ core and reduces the overall magnetic response [33].

In our study, we incorporated folic acid as a targeting ligand on our NPs, as confirmed by FTIR analysis (Fig. 2A). This approach leverages the high levels of folate receptors on tumor cells, facilitating enhanced selective targeting and accumulation of our NPs in GBMs, thereby overcoming the challenges of passive delivery and improving treatment outcomes [34, 35]. Also, the results showed that the Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles used in our study had a surface charge of −19.93 mV (Table 1). Studies show that negatively charged and neutral NPs are generally less phagocytosed and have longer circulation times in the bloodstream compared to positively charged NPs [36, 37]. The development of a protein corona (PC) in NPs when they are exposed to biological fluids greatly impacts their biological behavior [38–41]. This dynamic layer of attached proteins provides NPs with a new biological identity, impacting their cellular uptake, toxicity, biodistribution, and targeting efficiency [38, 41]. Positively charged surfaces tend to attract more proteins and cells than negatively charged surfaces, primarily because of electrostatic interactions [42, 43]. Therefore, the negatively charged NPs used in this study likely exhibit reduced PC formation, which minimizes alterations in particle size and enhances their ability to cross the BBB, making them suitable for in vivo studies as well. The cytotoxicity results 24 h after treating the cell lines with different NPs revealed that Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles exhibited considerably greater toxicity in cancerous C6 cells compared to non-cancerous OLN-93 cells, whereas this was not observed for the Fe3O4@Au nanoparticles (Fig. 2B). The reduction in the IC50 value for the targeted NPs in C6 cells compared to OLN-93 cells was statistically significant (p < 0.05), resulting in a therapeutic ratio of 1.56 (Table 2). This is due to the enhanced uptake of the targeted Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles by the cancer cells, as shown by the ICP-OES results (Fig. 3D). This finding is consistent with previous studies, which have shown that functionalizing Fe3O4@Au nanoparticles with folic acid increases their affinity for cancer cells that have an overexpression of folate receptors, enhancing their ability to specifically target and destroy cancer cells while reducing toxicity in healthy cells [44–46]. This supports their potential as a more effective and selective therapeutic option in cancer treatment. The hemolysis test demonstrated that NPs exhibited hemolysis levels below 5% across all tested concentrations, indicating their hemocompatibility and safety for potential use in clinical applications (Fig. 3A). This finding aligns with the ASTM E2524-08 standard, confirming that neither nanoparticle induces damage to erythrocytes, which is critical for applications involving intravenous administration [27]. In evaluating the liver toxicity of Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles, ALT and AST levels remained stable, but a rise in ALP was seen 24 h after administration (p < 0.01, Fig. 3B). This increase aligns with the findings of Tarin et al., where ALP levels also rose temporarily after gold-coated iron oxide NPs were injected, returning to normal after 48 h [47]. This suggests that the observed elevation in ALP in our study may be transient and not indicative of long-term toxicity. Also, the increase in urea levels following Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticle administration (p < 0.05) indicates a temporary effect on renal function, possibly from a mild stress or inflammatory response caused by the NPs (Fig. 3C). Urea, as a sensitive biomarker of kidney function, may increase in response to various physiological changes, including the temporary stress induced by nanomaterials [48, 49]. However, since creatinine levels remained unchanged, the kidneys’ filtration ability appears unaffected, suggesting that this increase is likely transient and does not point to long-term renal impairment. These results imply that our NPs did not cause severe or lasting damage to liver or kidney function. Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles play a crucial role as effective sensitizers for MH by rapidly increasing temperature during treatments (Fig. 4A). Thermal data indicate that cells treated with our magnetic NPs reached the critical hyperthermia threshold of 43 °C faster than the control group (Fig. 4B). This reduction in time is attributed to the NPs’ superior heat absorption and conduction abilities under RF exposure, which enhances localized heating. The heating mechanism involves Néel relaxation, where the NPs’ magnetic moments realign quickly with the alternating magnetic field, generating heat efficiently [50–52]. This rapid heating, alongside a higher CEM 43 °C (Fig. 4C), establishes these NPs as potent sensitizers, maximizing tumor cell death while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissue. The results from the CFA indicate that Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles substantially enhance the cytotoxicity of C6 cells when combined with radiation and hyperthermia (Fig. 5). The combined treatment of nanoparticles (NPs), radiation therapy, and hyperthermia significantly reduces cell survival and demonstrates a strong radiosensitizing effect. Increased radiation dose correlates directly with increased cytotoxicity. The combination of NPs and hyperthermia further enhances this effect, creating a more hostile environment for cancer cells and improving treatment outcomes. Statistical analysis supports the efficacy of this approach, suggesting that NPs enhance radiation’s impact on cellular mechanisms, likely by increasing DNA damage, and resulting in a decreased survival fraction (α/β ratio). The α factor corresponds to single-event damage caused by radiation, while β relates to damage from multiple radiation hits [53]. Irradiation in combination with Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles and RFC markedly decreased both α and β values (p < 0.0001). This suggests that the combination of NPs and hyperthermia sensitizes cells to radiation by enhancing both single-event and cumulative DNA damage pathways. NPs treatment, combined with hyperthermia, significantly enhances radiation efficacy, as evidenced by a DEF of 5.19 at 8 Gy (Table 3). A DEF > 1 indicates increased tumor cell susceptibility to radiation. This enhancement is attributed to the gold component’s radiosensitizing properties (increasing radiation absorption) and hyperthermia’s effects on tumor blood flow, oxygenation, and the induction of DNA damage [15, 54]. Additionally, hyperthermia enhances radiation therapy through mechanisms such as increased blood flow to the tumor and improved oxygenation, improved delivery of therapeutic agents like radiosensitizers, and direct induction of stress responses that lead to enhanced DNA damage [55, 56]. Furthermore, the synergistic effects of combining these treatments (RT + NPs+ RFC) are reflected in the significant increases in apoptosis and necrosis observed in C6 cells (Fig. 6). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that irradiation led to increased apoptosis (both early and late) but did not significantly induce cell death when nanoparticles were used alone compared to the untreated group. The combination of Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles with radiation and hyperthermia considerably increased apoptotic and necrotic cell death, reaching its maximum difference when the radiation dose was 8 Gy, compared to both the control and radiation-only groups (p < 0.0001, Fig. 6). This finding implies that the NPs amplify the harmful effects of irradiation and RFC, thereby boosting the apoptotic response. Katifelis et al. demonstrated that the Gold-coated iron oxide promotes apoptosis via the p53, bcl-2, and casp-3 pathways in various cancer cell lines [57, 58]. Shariati et al. further showed that Au-Fe3O4 nanoparticles, when exposed to electromagnetic fields and X-ray radiation, can eliminate up to 90% of cancer cells in vitro [59]. In addition, Emamgholizadeh Minaei et al. reported that in glioblastoma cells, folic acid-conjugated, temozolomide-loaded Fe3O4@Au nanoparticles improved the effects of hyperthermia by 1.94-fold and enhanced radiation efficacy with a dose enhancement factor of 1.65 [22]. The generation of ROS plays a crucial role in the induction of apoptosis and necrosis, as elevated ROS levels can trigger apoptotic pathways while also damaging cellular components, leading to necrosis in more severely affected cells [60–62]. The combination of Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles, radiation, and hyperthermia amplifies ROS generation, contributing to heightened oxidative stress within the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 7). This increased oxidative stress may facilitate cell death through both apoptosis and necrosis, as it overwhelms the cellular repair mechanisms. Our intracellular ROS measurement using the DCFH-DA assay revealed that higher radiation doses led to increased ROS generation (Fig. 7). Moreover, the use of Fe3O4@Au/FA nanoparticles at radiation doses of 4 Gy and 6 Gy resulted in significant increases in ROS levels compared to the radiation-only group (p < 0.05), which further intensified at the 8 Gy dose (p < 0.01). The combination of NPs, 8 Gy radiation, and hyperthermia maximized reactive oxygen species (ROS) production (P < 0.0001). This supports previous findings showing that Fe3O4@Au nanoparticles enhance ROS production in cancer cells exposed to radiation, particularly due to their negative surface charge promoting cellular uptake and hydroxyl radical/superoxide formation. Fe3O4@Au NPs demonstrated superior ROS generation compared to individual components, leading to enhanced cancer cell death while sparing healthy cells, indicating their potential as effective radiosensitizers [63, 64]. As a result, our study demonstrates a significant synergistic effect and enhancement of therapeutic efficacy through the combination of magnetic hyperthermia and radiotherapy in glioma cells. However, there are important limitations to consider. The experiments were primarily conducted in vitro using C6 glioma cells, which do not fully capture the complexity of the tumor microenvironment or the heterogeneity observed in vivo. Therefore, further in vivo investigations are essential to assess the biodistribution, long-term toxicity, and overall therapeutic effectiveness of these nanoparticles in animal models. Addressing these limitations will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the potential clinical applicability of this combined treatment approach.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the suitable morphology and size of the targeted Fe3O4@Au/FA core-shell NPs facilitate effective cellular uptake. These NPs also demonstrate excellent biocompatibility and favorable tumor uptake, attributed to the folic acid ligand, ensuring minimal off-target effects and reducing potential damage to normal tissues. Furthermore, the unique properties of gold enhance the effects of radiation, while the magnetic properties of iron oxide contribute to increased heat generation, thereby enhancing the efficacy of hyperthermia treatment. This combination allows the NPs to serve as dual sensitizers, effectively promoting apoptosis and increasing ROS generation in C6 glioma cancer cells. These findings highlight the promising application of targeted NPs in advancing treatment strategies for glioma.
