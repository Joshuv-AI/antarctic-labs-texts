Multiparametric MRI with DWI, SWI, and DSC-PWI for preoperative prediction of Ki-67, IDH, and ATRX in adult-type diffuse gliomas
Authors: Muhammet Arif Kursun, Begumhan Baysal, Gulnihal Ay, Fatih Calis, Mehmet Sabri Gurbuz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImaging-based assessment of proliferative and molecular features in adult-type diffuse gliomas may support preoperative tumor characterization. This study evaluated conventional MRI combined with diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), and dynamic susceptibility contrast perfusion-weighted imaging (DSC-PWI) for their associations with Ki-67 index, IDH status, and ATRX expression status.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study included 126 patients with adult-type diffuse gliomas who underwent conventional MRI, DWI, and SWI; rCBV from DSC-PWI was available in 59 patients. Quantitative ADC and rCBV measurements were independently performed by two readers. Histopathological and molecular data were obtained from pathology reports. Ki-67 expression was dichotomized as low (< 10%) and high (≥ 10%). ROC analysis assessed the diagnostic performance of MRI parameters for high Ki-67 expression and WHO grade ≥ 3 tumors; sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative Ki-67 thresholds of ≥ 15%, ≥ 20%, ≥ 25%, and ≥ 30%. Potential selection bias related to rCBV availability was assessed, and multivariable logistic regression was performed.ResultsAmong 126 patients, 84.1% had WHO grade 4 tumors. ADCmin, rADCmin, ADCmean, and rADCmean were significantly lower, whereas rCBVmax and ITSS grade were significantly higher, in tumors with high Ki-67 expression (all p < 0.001). High Ki-67 expression was also associated with necrosis, peritumoral edema, and heterogeneous enhancement. ADC-derived parameters differed significantly between IDH-mutant and IDH-wildtype tumors. rCBVmax was higher in tumors with retained ATRX expression (p = 0.016). rCBVmax showed the highest diagnostic performance for high Ki-67 expression (AUC = 0.967; 95% CI: 0.920–1.000). In multivariable analysis, lower ADCmin and rADCmin remained independently associated with high Ki-67 expression, whereas ITSS grade did not. Interobserver agreement was excellent for quantitative ADC and rCBV measurements.ConclusionMultiparametric MRI features, particularly diffusion-derived metrics and rCBVmax, were associated with proliferative activity in adult-type diffuse gliomas and may support preoperative noninvasive tumor characterization, but validation in larger and more balanced cohorts is required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02607-6.Keywords: Glioma, Ki-67 proliferation index, Magnetic resonance imaging, Diffusion-weighted imaging, Perfusion-weighted imaging, Susceptibility-weighted imaging.

Introduction

Adult-type diffuse gliomas are the most common primary malignant brain neoplasms. Integration of molecular markers such as isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutations and 1p/19q codeletion into the 2016 and 2021 WHO classifications has refined diagnosis and prognostication. According to the 2021 WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System, adult-type diffuse gliomas comprise three major entities: astrocytoma, IDH-mutant; oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted; and glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype [1–4].

IDH mutations, most often at codons R132 (IDH1) or R172 (IDH2), produce D-2-hydroxyglutarate, an oncometabolite that alters cell metabolism and promotes tumorigenesis [5–7]. IDH-mutant gliomas generally grow more slowly and respond better to therapy than IDH-wildtype glioblastomas [8]. ATRX (alpha-thalassemia/mental retardation syndrome X-linked) alterations frequently co-occur with IDH mutations, particularly in astrocytomas, leading to chromatin remodeling defects and telomere instability [9–11].

The Ki-67 index, a marker of cellular proliferation, correlates with tumor grade and prognosis but requires surgical tissue, limiting preoperative use [12]. Advanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques such as diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) mapping, susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), and dynamic susceptibility contrast (DSC) perfusion allow noninvasive assessment of tumor cellularity, microvascularity, and intratumoral susceptibility-related heterogeneity [13–16].

This study evaluated the associations of conventional and advanced MRI parameters with Ki-67 index, IDH status, and ATRX expression status in adult-type diffuse gliomas, with the aim of supporting preoperative noninvasive tumor characterization.

Materials and methods

This retrospective, non-interventional study was approved by the Istanbul Medipol University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee on August 29, 2024 (IRB approval No. 827). Because the institutional ethics committee of Istanbul Medeniyet University was temporarily unavailable during the study period, approval was obtained from an authorized external ethics committee. The study was conducted at Goztepe Prof. Dr. Suleyman Yalcin City Hospital/Istanbul Medeniyet University with institutional permission. The study protocol complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its later revisions governing ethical conduct in human research. The requirement for informed consent was waived.

Brain MRI scans of adult patients (≥ 18 years) obtained between January 2018 and September 2024 were reviewed. The inclusion criteria were as follows: histopathologically confirmed adult-type diffuse glioma classified according to the 2021 WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System; available molecular and proliferative marker data, including Ki-67 index, IDH status, and ATRX expression status; and pretreatment multiparametric MRI comprising conventional MRI, diffusion-weighted imaging/apparent diffusion coefficient (DWI/ADC), and susceptibility-weighted imaging/intratumoral susceptibility signals (SWI/ITSS). Dynamic susceptibility contrast perfusion-weighted imaging/relative cerebral blood volume (DSC-PWI/rCBV) data were analyzed when available.

Exclusion criteria included: history of another malignancy, lack of preoperative MRI, prior chemo/radiotherapy, poor image quality, missing molecular or pathology data, unclassified histology, or H3K27 mutation.

After applying these criteria, 126 of 184 patients were included. DSC perfusion data were available in 59 cases. Because of the retrospective design and the long study period, DSC-PWI was not uniformly included in all preoperative MRI examinations; therefore, rCBV analysis was limited to patients with available and technically evaluable DSC-PWI data. The remaining 58 were excluded for missing pathology (n = 54), absence of preoperative MRI (n = 1), lymphoma (n = 1), unclassified histology (n = 1), or H3K27 alteration (n = 1).

Imaging techniques

All patients underwent MRI on 1.5T scanners (OPTIMA MR450w and MR360, GE Healthcare). The protocol included axial and sagittal T2-weighted, axial and coronal T2-FLAIR, axial T1-weighted, DWI, SWI, and post-contrast axial, coronal, and sagittal T1-weighted or 3D T1 BRAVO sequences. Gadolinium-based contrast (gadoterate meglumine or gadobutrol) was administered at 0.1 mmol/kg.

DWI was performed using axial spin-echo echo-planar imaging with b-values of 0 and 1000 s/mm², and ADC maps were automatically generated.

For DSC perfusion, baseline images were acquired before contrast injection, followed by gadolinium administration at a rate of 5 mL/s and a 20-mL saline flush using an MR-compatible injector. CBV maps were generated using a single-compartment model with automated arterial input function selection.

Because this retrospective study used routine clinical examinations acquired over a prolonged study period, preload administration and separate leakage correction were not uniformly incorporated into the institutional DSC-PWI protocol; therefore, no preload dose was routinely administered and no separate leakage correction was applied.

Detailed acquisition parameters for the conventional MRI, DWI, SWI, and DSC-PWI sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Data processing

DWI-derived ADC measurements and DSC-PWI-derived rCBV measurements were independently reviewed on a dedicated workstation (AW VolumeShare 7, GE Healthcare) by a neuroradiologist (15 years’ experience) and a fifth-year resident, blinded to pathology and molecular data. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Conventional MRI features and ITSS grade were recorded by the resident after consensus review.

Assessed features included tumor location, size, necrosis, edema, cysts, enhancement pattern, margins, and multifocality. Necrosis was assessed on post-contrast T1-weighted images and categorized as absent, ≤ 50%, or > 50% according to the proportion of non-enhancing necrotic area relative to the total tumor area. Enhancement was categorized as absent/subtle, ring-like, or heterogeneous. Edema was graded on FLAIR as absent to mild (< 1 cm), mild–moderate (1–2 cm), or severe (> 2 cm). Tumor margins were classified as well- or poorly defined. Conventional MRI features were assessed using predefined qualitative criteria adapted from the VASARI feature set where applicable [17].

ADC and CBV measurements were performed by cross-referencing conventional MRI, post-contrast T1-weighted images, ADC maps, SWI, and CBV maps. ROIs were placed within solid tumor components while avoiding necrotic, cystic, hemorrhagic, calcified, edematous areas and large vessels. Hemorrhage and calcification were excluded by reviewing SWI/phase images and corresponding conventional MRI findings.

ADC and CBV maps were evaluated blindly. For ADCmin, three small regions of interest (ROIs) (0.1–0.2 cm²) were placed on the same axial slice within the visually identified solid tumor region showing the lowest ADC values. For ADCmean, a freehand ROI covered the largest solid tumor section.

A reference ROI was drawn in contralateral normal white matter to calculate relative values:

Molecular analysis

All tumors were classified or reclassified according to the 2021 WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System. Histopathological and molecular data, including Ki-67 index, IDH and ATRX expression status, and when available 1p/19q codeletion, were retrieved from pathology reports. Ki-67 expression was assessed immunohistochemically as the percentage of positively stained nuclei in the most active tumor regions. IDH mutation was identified by PCR and sequencing. ATRX expression status was determined by immunohistochemistry and 1p/19q codeletion by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). ATRX expression status was categorized as ATRX loss or retained ATRX expression.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (version 29.0). Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean ± SD, numbers, and percentages. Normality was tested with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests, and categorical variables with the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. For significant overall group comparisons, post hoc pairwise comparisons were evaluated using Bonferroni-adjusted p-values. Because of the limited number of 1p/19q-codeleted tumors, these cases were included within the overall IDH-mutant group for statistical analyses.

Interobserver agreement for quantitative ADC and rCBV measurements was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients. Agreement statistics were not calculated for conventional MRI features or ITSS grade because these variables were recorded by the resident and reviewed by consensus, rather than independently recorded by both readers.

Ki-67 expression was dichotomized as low (< 10%) and high (≥ 10%) using a prespecified literature-based threshold. ROC analysis with Youden’s index was used to determine optimal MRI parameter cutoffs for predicting high Ki-67 expression. Additional ROC analysis was performed to assess the cohort-specific ability of Ki-67 to discriminate WHO grade ≥ 3 tumors. Spearman’s correlation was used for associations between quantitative parameters and Ki-67. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative Ki-67 thresholds of ≥ 15%, ≥ 20%, ≥ 25%, and ≥ 30% to evaluate the robustness of imaging-based discrimination across different definitions of high proliferative activity.

To assess potential selection bias, baseline characteristics were compared between patients with and without available rCBV data. rCBV analyses were limited to patients with available DSC-PWI data, without imputation for missing perfusion parameters.

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate whether selected imaging parameters were independently associated with high Ki-67 expression (≥ 10%). ADCmin and rADCmin were evaluated in separate models because of their close correlation. To improve the interpretability of odds ratios, ADCmin was rescaled per 100 × 10⁻⁶ mm²/s increase and rADCmin per 0.1-unit increase. ITSS grade was also assessed in a separate model, with grade 0 used as the reference category.

All multivariable models were adjusted for age, IDH status, necrosis, edema, and contrast enhancement. For these models, necrosis, edema, and contrast enhancement were entered as binary variables: necrosis as absent versus present, edema as absent/not appreciable versus present and contrast enhancement as absent/subtle versus present. This dichotomization was performed to reduce model complexity and minimize unstable estimates due to small subgroup sizes.

WHO grade was not included in the multivariable models because of the highly imbalanced grade distribution, which resulted in unstable estimates. rCBVmax was not included in multivariable regression because DSC-PWI data were available in only 59 patients, and only five patients in this subgroup had Ki-67 < 10%, resulting in unstable model estimates.

Results

Study population

A total of 126 patients with adult-type diffuse gliomas were included. According to WHO grading, 84.1% (n = 106) were grade 4, 11.1% (n = 14) grade 2, and 4.8% (n = 6) grade 3. IDH-wildtype tumors comprised 70.6% (n = 89), and 29.4% (n = 37) were IDH-mutant. Among the 106 WHO grade 4 tumors, 89 were glioblastomas, IDH-wildtype, and 17 were astrocytomas, IDH-mutant, CNS WHO grade 4. The remaining cases were WHO grade 2 or 3 IDH-mutant diffuse gliomas, including 10 oligodendrogliomas, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted. The mean Ki-67 index was 26.8 ± 16.8%. Tumors with retained ATRX expression accounted for 85.7% (n = 108), while ATRX expression loss was present in 14.3% (n = 18) including 12 IDH-wildtype and 6 IDH-mutant tumors. ITSS grades were: 0 in 13.5%, 1 in 14.3%, 2 in 13.5%, and 3 in 58.7% of cases. Conventional MRI, histopathological, and molecular features are summarized in Table 1.

Association of tumor necrosis, edema, and cystic changes with molecular markers and Ki-67 proliferation index

Necrosis was significantly associated with the Ki-67 index (p < 0.001). Mean Ki-67 values were 8.2 ± 7.2% in non-necrotic tumors, 29.8 ± 17.1% in tumors with ≤ 50% necrosis, and 32.1 ± 13.5% in tumors with > 50% necrosis. Pairwise comparisons showed that Ki-67 values were significantly higher in both necrotic groups than in non-necrotic tumors (both p < 0.001), whereas no significant difference was observed between the ≤ 50% and > 50% necrosis groups. Tumors with necrosis more frequently exhibited IDH-wildtype status and retained ATRX expression (both p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 2).

Peritumoral edema correlated with higher Ki-67 (p = 0.035) and IDH-wildtype status (p = 0.013), but not with ATRX (p = 0.784) (Supplementary Table 3). Cystic changes showed no significant association with Ki-67, IDH, or ATRX (all p > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 4).

Contrast enhancement patterns

Contrast enhancement pattern was significantly associated with the Ki-67 index (p < 0.001). Mean Ki-67 values were 8.4 ± 8.9% in non-enhancing tumors, 28.5 ± 13.4% in ring-enhancing tumors, and 30.9 ± 16.0% in heterogeneously enhancing tumors. Pairwise comparisons showed that Ki-67 values were significantly higher in both ring-enhancing and heterogeneously enhancing tumors than in non-enhancing tumors (both p < 0.05). No significant difference was observed between ring-enhancing and heterogeneously enhancing tumors. IDH-wildtype was more frequent in enhancing tumors (ring 88.2%, heterogeneous 80.7%) than non-enhancing ones (14.3%, p < 0.001). Retained ATRX expression was also associated with contrast enhancement (p = 0.043) (Supplementary Table 5).

Compared to ring-enhancing tumors, heterogeneous lesions showed lower ADC and rADC values (all p < 0.001): ADCmin 849.8 ± 137.6 vs. 939.4 ± 104.2 × 10⁻⁶ mm²/s; ADCmean 954.3 ± 147.3 vs. 1065 ± 131.5 × 10⁻⁶ mm²/s; rADCmin 1.0653 ± 0.1649 vs. 1.2236 ± 0.1365; rADCmean 1.194 ± 0.171 vs. 1.384 ± 0.178.

Tumor margins and multifocality

Ki-67 was higher in poorly defined tumors than well-defined ones without statistical significance (p = 0.057). IDH status and ATRX expression status were not significantly associated with tumor margin (p = 0.115 and 0.116) (Supplementary Table 6).

Multifocal/multicentric tumors were more often IDH-wildtype, while Ki-67 and ATRX status showed no significant associations (p > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 7).

ITSS and molecular parameters

Higher ITSS grades were associated with elevated Ki-67 index (p < 0.001), increasing from 9.7 ± 8.8% (grade 0) to 30.5 ± 15.3% (grade 3). Grades 2 and 3 differed significantly from grade 0 (p < 0.05). However, ITSS grade was not independently associated with high Ki-67 expression after adjustment for age, IDH status, necrosis, edema, and contrast enhancement. The frequencies of IDH-wildtype status and retained ATRX expression also increased with higher ITSS grade (17.6% → 82.4% and 52.9% → 91.9%, both p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 8).

Quantitative parameters and IDH/ATRX status

IDH-wildtype tumors showed significantly lower ADCmin, rADCmin, ADCmean, and rADCmean values than IDH-mutant tumors (all p ≤ 0.001). Mean rCBVmax was higher in IDH-wildtype tumors but not significant (p = 0.066). Ki-67 was markedly higher in IDH-wildtype tumors (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Diagnostic performance of imaging for high Ki-67 prediction

All advanced MRI parameters showed significant discriminatory performance for high Ki-67 expression (≥ 10%) (all p < 0.001) (Table 3). Optimal thresholds were: ADCmin < 1029.4 × 10⁻⁶ mm²/s (AUC = 0.891), rADCmin < 1.223 (AUC = 0.907), ADCmean < 1107.1 × 10⁻⁶ mm²/s (AUC = 0.897), rADCmean < 1.378 (AUC = 0.897), and rCBVmax > 5.39 (AUC = 0.967). Diffusion parameters showed inverse correlations with Ki-67 (Supplementary Table 10).

Sensitivity analysis across alternative Ki-67 thresholds

Sensitivity analyses using alternative Ki-67 thresholds showed that the diagnostic performance of ADC-derived parameters decreased progressively as the Ki-67 threshold increased. ADC-derived metrics retained significant discriminatory performance at the ≥ 15%, ≥ 20%, and ≥ 25% thresholds, but showed weaker and non-significant performance at the ≥ 30% threshold. In contrast, rCBVmax maintained strong discriminatory performance across all alternative thresholds, including ≥ 30% (Supplementary Table 11).

Interobserver agreement

Interobserver agreement was excellent for quantitative ADC and rCBV measurements (all p < 0.001). ICC values were: ADCmin = 0.988, rADCmin = 0.986, rCBVmax = 0.988, ADCmean = 0.989, and rADCmean = 0.990. Mean values measured by both observers showed minimal differences (Table 5).

The associations between Ki-67, IDH, and ATRX status with necrosis, edema, enhancement, margins, multifocality, and ITSS grade are summarized in Supplementary Figs. 1, 2, and 3.

Ki-67 cut-off for predicting high-grade gliomas

Using the Ki-67 index, the optimal cut-off value for predicting high-grade adult-type diffuse gliomas (WHO ≥ 3) was determined to be ≥ 10%. At this threshold, the sensitivity was 95.5%, the specificity was 92.9%, and the area under the curve (AUC) was 0.971 (95% CI: 0.944–0.998), indicating statistically significant discriminatory performance (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 12).

Comparison of patients with and without available DSC-PWI/rCBV data

Patients with and without available DSC-PWI/rCBV data did not significantly differ in terms of age, sex, necrosis, edema, contrast enhancement pattern, IDH status, CNS WHO grade, ITSS grade, or high Ki-67 expression (Supplementary Table 13). However, selection bias related to unmeasured clinical or technical factors cannot be excluded.

Discussion

This retrospective study evaluated the relationship between MRI parameters and Ki-67, IDH, and ATRX status in adult-type diffuse gliomas. Among ADC metrics, rADCmin showed the highest diagnostic performance, whereas rCBVmax showed the highest overall univariable performance; both were associated with proliferative activity. All diffusion parameters correlated inversely with Ki-67, highlighting their potential to reflect tumor proliferation. SWI-derived ITSS and perfusion imaging offered additional insights into tumor vascularity. Quantitative metrics showed excellent interobserver agreement (ICC 0.986–0.990), supporting the reproducibility of these methods.

Our findings align with previous studies showing a significant association between necrosis and high Ki-67 levels, consistent with Öncü et al. [18], who identified necrosis as the most predictive MRI feature of tumor proliferation. Our findings suggest that the presence of necrosis may be more informative than its extent for reflecting proliferative activity, as Ki-67 values did not differ significantly between tumors with ≤ 50% and > 50% necrosis. This supports prior evidence indicating that necrotic volume proportion lacks predictive value for Ki-67 [19].

Peritumoral edema was associated with higher Ki-67 levels (p = 0.035), consistent with Yang et al., who reported greater edema in high Ki-67 IDH-mutant gliomas. However, our findings differ from those of Xing et al., who found no significant correlation between peritumoral edema and Ki-67 in glioblastomas. No significant difference was found between moderate and severe edema, likely due to the predominance of grade 4, IDH-wildtype tumors in both groups, reflecting similar aggressiveness.

Cystic changes showed no significant association with Ki-67, IDH, or ATRX status, consistent with Yang et al. [13]. Therefore, although cystic change did not appear to reflect proliferative activity in the present study, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the predominance of WHO grade 4 tumors. In contrast, both ring-enhancing and heterogeneously enhancing tumors showed significantly higher Ki-67 values than non-enhancing tumors. Heterogeneously enhancing tumors also showed lower ADC values, suggesting greater cellularity. However, Ki-67 did not differ significantly between ring-enhancing and heterogeneously enhancing tumors, possibly due to sample size imbalance or overlapping histologic patterns. Previous studies reported mixed findings: Yang et al. found enhancement pattern differences by Ki-67 level, while Xing et al. observed no such association [12, 13].

Tumor margin showed a borderline association with Ki-67 (p = 0.057), with higher values in poorly defined tumors. While Yang et al. linked ill-defined margins to high Ki-67, Xing et al. found better demarcation in proliferative glioblastomas, likely due to differing imaging sequences (T2/FLAIR vs. contrast-enhanced T1) [12, 13].

In our study, multifocal or multicentric tumors showed higher Ki-67 values than focal ones, though not significantly. This may reflect greater invasiveness rather than proliferation. Previous research found higher Ki-67 in bihemispheric glioblastomas, which may explain our non-significant findings due to mixed definitions of multifocality and multicentricity [20].

ITSS grade on SWI increased with Ki-67, consistent with Yang et al., who reported similar findings in IDH-mutant astrocytomas [13]. Higher ITSS grades were also associated with IDH-wildtype status and retained ATRX expression. These findings suggest that ITSS may reflect susceptibility-related tumor heterogeneity; however, its association with high Ki-67 should be interpreted cautiously because ITSS grade did not remain independently significant in multivariable analysis. ATRX-related imaging associations should also be considered exploratory, given the potential confounding effects of IDH status, 1p/19q codeletion status, and integrated histomolecular diagnosis.

All diffusion-based parameters showed inverse correlations with Ki-67, supporting their value as imaging markers of tumor cellularity. rADCmin showed the strongest correlation (rho = − 0.373), consistent with Ningfang et al. [21]. Slightly weaker correlations in our study may reflect methodological differences, including visually selected hotspot ROIs and freehand sampling of the largest solid tumor component, whereas Ki-67 was derived from localized histological sampling. Relative ADC metrics appeared to show better diagnostic performance than absolute ADC metrics, possibly by reducing interindividual variability.

No universal cutoff exists for distinguishing high versus low Ki-67 in adult-type diffuse gliomas. Yang et al. used 10% for IDH-mutant gliomas [13], whereas Xing et al. applied 20% in glioblastomas with methylation profiling [12]. In a large cohort of 703 glial tumors, Ki-67 was stratified as ≤ 10%, 10–30%, and ≥ 30%, with lower and intermediate levels linked to more favorable prognosis and clinical course. Given this heterogeneity and the lack of a standardized threshold, we used a prespecified literature-based cutoff of 10% to dichotomize Ki-67 expression as low (< 10%) and high (≥ 10%). Although ROC analysis in our cohort also identified 10% as the optimal cutoff for discriminating WHO grade ≥ 3 tumors, this cohort-specific result was reported as a supportive analysis rather than as the primary basis for dichotomization [22].

Sensitivity analyses using alternative Ki-67 thresholds showed that ADC-derived parameters were more threshold-dependent, with decreasing discriminatory performance at higher Ki-67 cutoffs, whereas rCBVmax maintained strong performance across thresholds. These findings support the robustness of the rCBVmax association and indicate that diffusion-derived performance should be interpreted in relation to the selected Ki-67 threshold.

In our study, rADCmin showed the highest diagnostic accuracy among diffusion parameters for predicting high Ki-67 (AUC = 0.907; sensitivity = 88.9%; cutoff = 1.223), outperforming ADCmean, rADCmean, and ADCmin. This suggests that the lowest ADC regions best reflect tumor cellularity and proliferation. Compared with prior studies (Ningfang et al., AUC 0.778; Yang et al., AUC 0.740), our study showed higher diagnostic performance, which may reflect methodological differences such as exclusion of grade 1 tumors, predominance of grade 4 cases, reader experience, ROI strategy, and Ki-67 cutoff selection [13, 21].

In our study, rCBVmax showed the highest univariable diagnostic performance for predicting high Ki-67 (threshold > 5.390; AUC = 0.967; sensitivity = 90.7%; specificity = 100%), outperforming other parameters and correlating most strongly with Ki-67 (rho = 0.493). Patients with and without available rCBV data did not significantly differ in terms of demographic, histopathologic, molecular, and conventional imaging characteristics, suggesting no evident selection bias based on the assessed variables. Our rCBV-based findings align with the literature, where a large meta-analysis reported strong diagnostic performance for DSC-derived rCBV (AUC ~ 0.91) in glioma grading [23]. These findings suggest that DSC-PWI-derived rCBV may provide useful information regarding tumor angiogenesis and proliferative activity. However, rCBV-related results should be interpreted cautiously because DSC-PWI data were available only in a subset of patients. Prior studies reported lower cutoffs (Ertan et al.: 2.0; Yang et al.: 2.355, AUC = 0.782) [13, 24].

Although rCBVmax showed strong univariable diagnostic performance, it was not included in multivariable regression because DSC-PWI data were available only in a subset of patients. Therefore, the multivariable findings primarily support the independent association of diffusion-derived metrics, particularly ADCmin and rADCmin, with high Ki-67 expression.

Differences in rCBV values across studies may result from variations in MR field strength, vendor specifications, and pulse sequence settings. Additional factors such as spatial/temporal resolution, contrast injection protocols, and post-processing methods (e.g., leakage correction, arterial input function, ROI placement) can also influence results. These technical and methodological inconsistencies significantly impact perfusion parameter comparability.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design limits the external validity of the findings. In addition, the cohort was predominantly composed of WHO grade 4 and IDH-wildtype tumors, which restricts the generalizability of the results to lower-grade adult-type diffuse gliomas. Subgroup analyses according to WHO grade were not feasible because of the small number of grade 2 and grade 3 tumors. The ROC-derived thresholds and diagnostic performance estimates were not validated in an independent cohort; therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory and require validation in larger, prospective, and more balanced cohorts. In addition, no internal cross-validation, such as 5-fold or 10-fold cross-validation, was performed, and the reported performance estimates should therefore be interpreted as preliminary.

Ki-67 was assessed on sampled histological sections and therefore may not fully reflect intratumoral heterogeneity, potentially weakening correlations between imaging features and proliferative activity. In addition, the lack of a universally standardized Ki-67 cutoff complicates comparisons with previous studies. Although a prespecified literature-based 10% threshold was used for the primary analysis, sensitivity analyses showed that the diagnostic performance of ADC-derived parameters varied across alternative Ki-67 thresholds, particularly at higher cutoff values. Therefore, imaging associations with high Ki-67 expression should be interpreted in relation to the selected Ki-67 threshold.

Furthermore, DSC-PWI data were available only in a subset of patients, and the absence of routine preload administration and separate leakage correction may have influenced rCBV estimates. Incomplete DSC-PWI availability also limited the construction of a robust combined multivariable model including rCBVmax. The use of visually selected hotspot ROIs may also have introduced reader-dependent measurement variability.

The small number of 1p/19q-codeleted tumors precluded analysis of 1p/19q codeletion as a separate imaging endpoint. ATRX-related findings should also be interpreted cautiously because of the small number of tumors with ATRX loss and the potential confounding effects of IDH status, 1p/19q codeletion status, and integrated histomolecular diagnosis. Finally, the semi-quantitative assessment of contrast enhancement may have introduced observer-related variability. Interobserver agreement for conventional MRI features and ITSS grade was not assessed because these variables were recorded through consensus review rather than independent paired readings.

Conclusions

Diffusion-derived metrics and rCBVmax were associated with proliferative activity in adult-type diffuse gliomas and may provide complementary information for preoperative tumor characterization. Given the retrospective single-center design, cohort imbalance, and incomplete DSC-PWI availability, these findings should be considered preliminary and require validation in larger, prospective, and more balanced cohorts.
